## 4. What the Storm Took

Ten years is a long time to rehearse a lie. I should know; I've told mine every day for a decade.

The version I tell the bees goes like this: *I was young, I was careless, the sky did a terrible thing and I happened to be standing under it.* The bees are generous listeners. They don't ask why the sky only does terrible things when I'm frightened, or angry, or — once, memorably — happy. They don't ask what my mother whispered to me before she vanished, because bees have never heard of my mother, and if they had, they would not approve of her.

But you're not a bee. So here is the true version, which I have never said aloud, mostly because there has been no one alive worth saying it to.

* * *

We lived in Hollowmere then, a village that earned its name honestly: a bowl of grey houses in the crook of two hills, famous for nothing except its apples, which were famously mediocre. My mother, Maren Dravenmoor, kept a candle-shop that sold almost no candles, and I spent my childhood wondering how we ate. Later I understood the candles weren't for selling. They were for marking. Every windowsill in our house held one, and every wick had been trimmed to a length only she knew, and when the flames leaned — even in still air, *especially* in still air — she would go quiet as a held breath and move us both to the back room.

"Storms lean toward certain doors," she told me when I was seven. "Ours more than most."

"Whose door do they lean toward?" I asked.

She trimmed the wick with her thumbnail. "Not yours," she said, which was the first outright lie she ever told me, and I want it noted that I loved her anyway.

Her other habit was whispering. She thought I slept through it. Nobody sleeps through a mother kneeling at the foot of their bed at midnight, murmuring into the floorboards like they owed her money. Most of it was in a language I didn't know — all consonants and low vowels, words that seemed to sink into the wood rather than bounce off it. But some phrases came through in plain Common, and those I remember better than I remember her face.

*"I know what's written. I know what's owed."*

*"The court beneath. The court beneath."*

*"You will not have him. You will not have his name."*

Him. That was me. She never said my name in the whispers — names, I would later learn, are currency down below, and my mother was many things but never careless with money.

I asked her about the Undercourt exactly once. I was nine. I'd heard a tinker use the phrase as a curse and recognized it from her midnight liturgy, and children are constitutionally incapable of leaving such things alone.

She set down the candle she was dipping. For a long moment she looked at me the way you look at a door you're deciding whether to bar.

"There's a place under the mountains," she said finally, "where everything you owe gets collected. Every promise. Every borrowed thing. Every gift." Her thumb traced a line across my palm, from heel to fingertip, and her hand was very cold. "And some of us were given gifts before we could agree to the terms."

"What kind of gifts?"

The candle behind her guttered, though no window was open.

"The loud kind," she said.

Then she smiled, made me dinner, and never spoke of it again. That was her way. My mother approached dangerous subjects the way a cat approaches water: acknowledging them fully, touching them never.

* * *

She vanished on the first frost of my fourteenth autumn. Not dead-vanished — I want to be clear about that, because for ten years I've had to live with people assuming grief when what I actually feel is something with far fewer edges. She went out for lamp oil and did not come home, and when I finally got the village elders to break down our door — I'd been too proud, or too frightened, to admit she was gone — they found the shop immaculate and every single candle burned down to a puddle. All of them. Dozens of wicks, all extinguished at nearly the same moment, wax frozen mid-drip like a room full of small white ghosts.

One candle remained standing. Mine. The one she'd placed on my sill the night I was born, according to a note I found later folded inside her ledger, along with three words in that same sinking language and one sentence in Common:

*If it leans, run.*

For six months I ran nothing. I stayed. I kept the shop. I told myself she'd gone to visit an aunt in Gravenholt, though neither of us had ever mentioned an aunt. I trimmed my candle's wick and watched it burn straight and refused to think about the fact that straight-burning candles were, in my mother's cosmology, the calm before something.

The something came in spring.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about calling a storm: it doesn't feel like calling. It feels like drowning in reverse — like the whole sky has been sitting on your chest your entire life and finally decides to exhale.

It started with the Rennick boy. Tam Rennick, fifteen, all elbows and cruelty, the sort of boy who makes other children's lives smaller for sport. He'd found my mother's ledger — I don't know how, I never learned how, the not-knowing has gnawed at me for a decade — and he stood in the square reading her whispers back to me in a falsetto. *"The court beneath. The court beneath."* He'd torn pages out and was folding them into boats to float in the trough, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me, that I wanted him to stop. Not that I wanted him hurt. Not that I wanted him gone. Just: *stop.*

And the sky, apparently, had been waiting ten years for someone to give it instructions.

The first thing that happened was the silence. Every sound in Hollowmere — the trough-water, the market noise, Tam's idiot voice — cut off at once, as if the village had been dropped underwater. Then my ears popped, and every hair on my arms rose, and the candle flame in the nearest window — mine, my mother's candle, the one I carried everywhere by then like the sentimental fool I am — leaned so hard sideways it went out.

*If it leans, run.*

I did not run. I stood in the square with my mouth open and felt something rise through me from the ground up, from deeper than the ground, from somewhere underneath the world's floorboards, and it came up my spine like a struck bell and out through my teeth, and I said — I remember this precisely, because it was so absurdly ordinary —

"*Stop it.*"

The cloud that formed above Hollowmere was not weather.

I've read every account since — the ones the magisters wrote, the ones the survivors whispered, the one ballad-writer who got nearly everything wrong and one thing chillingly right. None of them describe what I saw from the inside, which was: the sky opening like an eye that had always been there behind the blue, and looking down at Tam Rennick, and at the trough, and at the torn ledger-boats, and at me, with recognition. With *familiarity*. Whatever rose in me in that square, the storm knew it. Greeted it. Like a hound answering a whistle it had waited years to hear.

The thunder didn't crash. It *spoke* — one syllable, lower than hearing, a word in the consonant-and-vowel language my mother had whispered into my floorboards — and then Hollowmere ended.

I don't remember destruction. I remember light, and pressure, and the sensation of being at the bottom of a shout. When I came back to myself I was kneeling in the square untouched, unburned, unwet, in a perfect circle of standing grass, and around me the village was — the word the magisters used was *leveled*, which is tidy, and insufficient. Houses hadn't fallen. They'd been *unmade*, splintered into fragments small enough to sift. The great oak by the well had been turned inside out. Forty-three people lived in Hollowmere. Thirty-nine survived, because thirty-nine had been outside the square, and I have spent ten years refusing to do the arithmetic on whether that was mercy, aim, or accident.

Tam Rennick survived. He was found three fields over, alive, unharmed, and permanently mute. He looked at me when they carried him past. There wasn't hatred in his face. There was worse: there was understanding. He'd read my mother's ledger. He knew whose debt the storm had come to collect, and whose inheritance had answered.

* * *

They held the tribunal in Gravenholt, in a hall of grey stone with greyer men, and I sat through eleven hours of testimony about wind patterns and ward-failures and the structural integrity of market stalls, everyone circling the obvious the way villagers circle a well that's gone bad.

Because here was the thing none of them wanted to say: boys don't call storms. Hedge-witches can't unmake oaks. Whatever had happened in Hollowmere belonged to a lineage of happening, and everyone in that hall knew it, because everyone in that hall had heard the old stories, and the old stories had a name in them.

A magistrate presided — I remember him exactly: dry as August, spectacles half-mooned, a seal-ring of black iron that I stared at for hours because staring at it made the static behind my eyes go quiet. He read my mother's recovered ledger in full. He read the whispers. At one point he set the pages down, removed his spectacles, polished them longer than polishing required, and said, to the room but really to me:

"The Dravenmoors fled obligations. Obligations, however, are patient."

Nobody asked him what he meant. Everyone understood anyway. That was my first lesson in how power talks: it never explains itself where explaining might count as evidence.

My sentence was exile — dressed up, in the way sentences are, as mercy. A valley in the northern wastes, magic-dead, veiled, a place where nothing that owed anything could come collecting. "You will live out your years in stillness," the magistrate said, stamping the writ with the black-iron ring. "Consider it protection."

"Protection from whom?" I asked.

He looked at me over the spectacles, and for half a heartbeat something moved behind his face that was not bureaucratic at all. It was ancient, and it was amused, and it was *hungry*.

"From the alternative to protection," he said.

They put me on a cart with a year of supplies, a beekeeping manual chosen by someone with a sense of irony I've come to appreciate, and a guard who hummed hymns the entire six-week journey as though music could keep the sky off him. On the last morning, cresting the ridge above the veil, I looked back south toward the mountains, toward whatever court my mother had whispered about, and I felt — distinctly, unmistakably — *watched*. Not hostilely. Patiently. The way a creditor watches a debtor board a ship.

"Ten years," I said to the horizon. I don't know why I said it. It felt like signing something.

* * *

That's the story. Ten years of bees and candle-trimming and carefully never thinking about any of it, and it worked, mostly, right up until the morning a bleeding stranger crossed a veil that nothing crosses and died holding my hands.

I tell you all this now — I'm telling *myself* all this now, scratching it into the stable wall with a nail at three in the morning like a madwoman, because if I don't write it down I'm going to start pretending it isn't connected — because of what the map did tonight.

The courier's map, the blood-inked thing that won't burn and won't leave: it unfolded itself while I slept. I woke to find it pinned under my pack strap, spread flat across the stable floor, and in the margin — in handwriting I would know anywhere, handwriting I have spent ten years trying to forget — in my mother's hand, in ink the color of dried blood, three words:

*You're already listed.*

Below them, in smaller script, a date. My birth date.

I am sitting in the dark of a magic-dead valley where nothing supernatural is supposed to be able to follow, and my dead mother's handwriting is crawling slowly, patiently, toward the edge of the page like a line of ants. And beyond the treeline, where the veil hangs thin and grey against the stars, something is standing perfectly still.

Waiting for morning. Waiting for me.

*If it leans, run,* she wrote. But the candles are all leaning now, Mother. Every wick in the house.

There is nowhere left to run that isn't downhill.
