## 5. The Cartographer Who Deals in Doors

There are three rules to selling routes through impossible places, and I learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never draw a door you wouldn't walk through yourself. Two: never take payment in anything that breathes, weeps, or sings. Three: never, under any circumstance, do business in a valley where the magic has been scraped out like marrow from a bone—because a place with no magic is a place with no witnesses, and no witnesses means no one to stop you from doing something stupid.

I was breaking rule three when Wendell Dravenmoor walked into the trading post at Hollowmere with death folded up in their coat pocket.

I want it on record that I didn't know that yet. All I knew was: stranger, valley-born, hands stained with beeswax and something darker, moving through the post's narrow aisles like a man trying not to disturb his own funeral. I'd been in Hollowmere four days, which was three days longer than any sensible person stayed. The trading post was the only building in the valley that remembered what commerce looked like—a sagging timber hall smelling of tallow, dried fish, and the particular despair of goods nobody wanted—and I'd rented its back corner as an office because the owner asked no questions and charged almost nothing, both of which suited me fine.

My trade, if you're curious, is doors. Not carpentry. *Routes.* Ways through places that don't admit ways. A stair down into a city that drowned two hundred years ago. A gap between two Tuesdays. A road across the Cinder Waste that only exists if you walk it backwards and refuse, the entire time, to think about your mother. I sell these to people who need them, and people always need them, because the world is mostly walls and everyone is looking for a crack.

The trick of it—the part they don't teach—is knowing which cracks are real and which are teeth.

* * *

Wendell came in near dusk, when the light through the post's one grimy window went the color of old honey. I was hunched over my desk pretending to update a ledger that hadn't changed in a week, because a cartographer who looks busy looks trustworthy, and a cartographer who looks idle looks like she's plotting something. Which, to be fair, I usually am.

They stopped three feet from my desk. Stood there. Opened their mouth. Closed it.

"Buy something or leave," I said, without looking up. "This isn't a confessional."

"I—" They set a hand on the edge of my desk to steady themselves, and I noticed the tremor in it, and I noticed the paper-wrapped package beneath their other arm clutched like a newborn or a bomb. "You're the one who sells passages."

"I sell routes. Passages are free. It's getting through them alive that costs." I finally looked up, and got my first proper look at Wendell Dravenmoor, though I didn't yet have the name to hang on the face. Ordinary, was my first impression, and it was such a *deliberate* ordinary that my second impression arrived immediately behind it like a bill collector: *nobody is that ordinary by accident.*

Ten years in a dead valley will sand a person smooth, mind you. But this wasn't sanding. This was someone who had taken a chisel to themselves daily, carefully, removing every interesting feature one grain at a time. I've met fugitives before. Fugitives either flaunt or flatten. This one had flattened so thoroughly they'd nearly disappeared, and the only thing they couldn't erase was the way their eyes kept flicking toward the window—not checking the weather, but measuring it. Watching the treeline.

"Right," I said, and closed my ledger. "What do you need?"

"I need to know how to get out of the valley."

"That's not difficult. You walk to the edge and keep going. Most people manage it as toddlers."

"The veil," they said, lower. "Crossing the veil. Without it... noticing."

Ah. There it was. I sat back and gave them a longer look, and they bore it the way a fencepost bears rain. The veil around Hollowmere wasn't metaphor—it was a membrane, a scar of unmaking laid over the whole valley, and everything inside it was pressed flat and quiet like flowers in a book. Things could fall *in*. Almost nothing climbed *out*, because the veil didn't have a door. It had a wound, and wounds close.

"You want out of Hollowmere," I said slowly, "past a boundary that has swallowed better smugglers than you, carrying whatever that is—" I nodded at the package "—and you want it done quietly. And you came to me because I'm the only person within fifty miles who sells the impossible."

"Yes," said Wendell, with the desperate honesty of someone too tired to lie well.

"Now here's my problem," I said, leaning forward, and I watched them flinch slightly, because I imagine I have the kind of face that makes flinching reasonable. "People come to me wanting impossible things, and nine times in ten, the impossible thing is a symptom. The disease is whoever's chasing them. So before I quote you a price, I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to consider lying very carefully before you decide against it." I let the pause stretch. "Who is coming after you?"

And Wendell Dravenmoor—who I would learn was many things, cowardly and courageous in equal and confusing measure, guilt-ridden, stubborn, secretly one of the most dangerous people on the continent—looked me dead in the eye and said:

"No one. Yet."

*Yet.* Gods preserve me. That word should have sent me straight to my cot and a very long sleep. Instead I heard myself say, "Show me what's in the package."

* * *

Here is what I knew about blood-ink before that evening: it exists, it's obscene, and it's Undercourt work. Here is what I knew about the Undercourt: enough to stay clear of it, which is to say, nothing firsthand and several nightmares' worth of rumor. Debts collected in flesh and memory. A ledger with every name ever sold written in it. A magistrate who does not age because aging implies a future, and futures belong to him by contract.

Blood-ink is how the court writes. Not because they're dramatic—though they are—but because a soul leaves residue, and residue makes excellent binding agent. Ink mixed with what's left when a person sells themselves short. It doesn't dry. It *waits.*

So when Wendell unwrapped that package on my desk and the map inside seemed to lean toward my lamp like a plant toward sun, I felt the hair on my arms stand up and deliver a formal protest.

It was beautiful. That's the horror of these things—they're always beautiful. Fine vellum gone ivory with age, mountains inked in strokes fine as spider silk, rivers rendered in a blue that shifted when you weren't looking directly at it. And threaded through every line, underneath the geography, a second map: veins of dark red-brown script, dense as scripture, running beneath the roads and ranges like roots beneath grass.

"That's not ink," I said.

"It doesn't burn," Wendell said. "Or tear. Or stay thrown away. It came back into my pack twice while I was watching the fire instead of it."

"And you thought—" I laughed, and it came out harsher than I intended. "You thought you'd carry it into a valley with no magic and it would just... behave?"

"It has behaved. Mostly."

"Mostly." I turned the map over. The reverse was worse. The script there was denser, and some of it was fresh—the letters still wet-looking, glistening, as though the document were writing itself in its spare time. I bent close and read what I could, which wasn't much; the language was ledger-script, contractual, the sort of prose that exists to be enforced rather than understood. But I caught fragments. A name. A debt. A date.

A date ten days away. The winter solstice.

"Wendell," I said, and they started—I hadn't given them my knowledge of their name, but the valley's only trading post keeps its own ledger too, and I read it my first morning, because rule zero of my profession is *know your client's true name before they know yours.* "Where did this come from?"

"A man. Dying. He crossed into the valley somehow, which I'm told shouldn't be possible, and he put this in my hands and said one word and died." Their voice went flat in the way voices go when they're holding a lot of weight very still. "*Solstice.*"

"And you touched it. With your bare hands."

"I didn't know what it was!"

"No," I agreed. "You didn't. But it did. Look." I took their wrist—gently; they were strung tight enough to snap—and turned their palm to the lamplight. There, faint as a watermark: a mark on the skin, a small spiral of darkened lines, like a bruise learning calligraphy. "Blood-ink binds to the first hand that takes it willingly. Congratulations. You're not carrying this map anymore. You're *married* to it."

They pulled their hand back and stared at the mark, and I watched a decade of careful, sanded-down ordinariness crack right down the middle. What showed underneath was young. Scared. And furious about being scared, which is the most dangerous combination there is.

"So I burn it," they said.

"It won't burn."

"So I give it away."

"It'll come back. It always comes back to the hand that holds the debt." I tapped the fresh script on the reverse. "And whoever wrote this knows exactly where you are, because you're holding a piece of their stationery. That's not a map, Wendell. That's a leash with very good handwriting."

Silence, then. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out front, the post's owner argued with a farmer about the price of nails with the weary passion of men who have had this argument every week for years and intend to continue forever.

"They'll come for me," Wendell said at last. It wasn't a question.

"The solstice is in ten days. Something in that script gets sealed on the solstice—my guess is a debt gets called due, or a claim gets finalized. Either way, yes. Someone is coming. Someone patient, because patience is free when you own the clock." I studied their face. "Who died in your yard, Wendell? What did he owe?"

"I don't know. He said—" They stopped. Started again. "He said the mountain. Vhalen. He said his soul was *there*."

Every professional instinct I owned stood up, dusted itself off, and told me to walk away. Sell the poor fool a route to the coast and forget them. The Undercourt was not a market I traded in. The Undercourt was the reason my trade had a rule two: never take payment in anything that weeps. Because everything the court touches weeps eventually, and I had wept enough for one lifetime already, thank you, and I kept the rest of my tears in a locked box with the rest of my bad decisions.

But.

But there was a second map under the first, and I'd only pretended not to notice it. Beneath the courier's debts, beneath the script, there were markings in *my* tradition—cartographer's marks, surveyor's glyphs, the notation system my mother's guild used before the guild burned. Someone had charted the deep roads under Mount Vhalen. The ways *in.* The ways that even the court's own servants sometimes lost.

I had spent six years and one ruined reputation looking for those roads. Not for the court. For what the court kept.

"Half," I heard myself say.

Wendell blinked. "Half of what?"

"Whatever comes out of this. Whatever the Undercourt pays out—souls returned, debts dissolved, secrets unlocked, whatever currency this settles in—you give me half." I stood, pulled my travel case from under the desk, and began laying out instruments on the blotter: compasses that pointed at intentions rather than north, a pen that wrote only truths, a folded square of cloth that was, technically, a door. "In exchange, I get you out of this valley, down the Cinder Road, through Gravenholt, and to the mountain's throat before the solstice. Alive. Probably."

"That's not a price, that's a gamble."

"All prices are gambles. Mine just have better odds than most." I looked up at them. "Understand what you're buying, Wendell. I deal in doors. I don't deal in safety. The route I'll draw you exists, but it exists the way a knife exists—usefully, and only if you respect what it can do to you. And understand this too: once you cross that veil, the magic comes back. All of it. Whatever you were hiding from when you buried yourself in this place, it's going to wake up on the far side of that line, and it's going to be loud."

Something moved behind their eyes then—fear, yes, but underneath it, horribly, *hunger.* Like a starving man shown bread and told it might be poisoned. I filed that away. Filing things away is half my trade.

"When do we leave?" they said.

"Dawn. Not because dawn is lucky—dawn is a superstition for people who can't read maps—but because the veil is thinnest in the hour after sunrise, when the day hasn't decided what it is yet." I began packing. "Bring the map. Obviously. Bring nothing else you love."

"Why not?"

"Because things you love make demands, and on the road ahead you won't be able to afford any."

They gathered the map with hands that tried not to shake and mostly failed, and paused at the door of my corner, silhouetted against the last of the honeyed light.

"Why?" they asked. "Why help me? Half of nothing is nothing, if we die."

I smiled, and it was, I'm told, not a comforting smile. "Because I've spent six years looking for the roads under Mount Vhalen, Wendell, and you just carried a piece of them into my office wrapped in butcher's paper. As far as I'm concerned, you're not my client." I snapped the case shut. "You're my door."

They left. I sat alone with the lamp and my instruments and the fading ghost of blood-ink's presence, and I allowed myself, finally, to touch the locket at my throat—the one I never open, the one with a name inside it that the Undercourt holds, the name that made rule two necessary.

Ten days. One exiled storm-caller who thought they were nobody. One map that wrote itself. One mountain full of everything I'd sworn to steal back.

Outside, the valley darkened toward its long dead midnight, quiet as a held breath, and I began, by lamplight, to draw us a way out.
