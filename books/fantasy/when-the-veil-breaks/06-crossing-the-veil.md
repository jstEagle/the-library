## 6. Crossing the Veil

### Wendell

The map decided for me in the end. I'd like to claim it was courage, or grief, or some noble resolve about the dead man's last words, but the truth is the thing simply refused to stay put. I buried it under a stone by the well on Tuesday; Wednesday morning it was folded neatly inside my boot. I dropped it down the dry well shaft and heard it land—heard paper *land*, which should not make a sound at forty feet—and when I hauled up the bucket of water I'd drawn after, there it was, floating on top like a lily pad, ink still bright as an open wound.

"You're being ridiculous," Isolde said from my doorway. She'd appointed herself my conscience sometime around the third day, mostly, I suspect, because she was bored and because the trading post had run out of the good brandy. "You could burn it."

"It doesn't burn."

"Then throw it back over the treeline where you found its owner."

"And let whoever's waiting out there pick it up? The man in grey?" I turned the map over in my hands. It was warm. Maps are not supposed to be warm. "He can't come in here, Isolde. Whatever that veil is, he stands at the edge of it every night like a dog chained to a post. Which means the only safe place for this thing—the only place in the world—is exactly where I'm standing."

"Then we're agreed." She smiled with all the warmth of a ledger line. "You'll never leave."

"That was the plan," I said. "Ten years ago."

* * *

I want to explain what ten years in a magic-dead valley does to a person, because nobody explains it, they just assume exile is exile, prison is prison, and the shape of the cage doesn't matter.

Here is what it does: it teaches you to stop flinching.

Magic has a texture. Before the valley, I felt it the way you feel weather against your skin—a pressure, a hum, a low constant conversation between the world and whatever it is inside me that answers. When they marched me past the veil, that conversation cut off mid-sentence, and the silence was so total I screamed. They tell me I screamed for three days. I don't remember. What I remember is waking up on the fourth day feeling nothing at all, and thinking: *oh, so this is peace.*

It isn't peace. It's deafness. But I built a life in it anyway. Bees, mostly. Bees don't care that the valley eats enchantment; they care about clover and weather and whether your hands smell of smoke. Ten years of bees will convince you that quiet is a thing you chose.

And then a dying man bled a map into existence in front of me, and a shadow in grey livery started standing at the treeline every dusk like it was clocking in for a shift, and I remembered something important: quiet chosen is one thing. Quiet enforced by something that *waits* is another entirely.

So on the morning of the ninth day, I packed a bag I'd packed in my head a hundred times without ever letting my hands know about it, and I walked east through the bee-pastures with Isolde beside me and the map burning a hole through my coat like a coal with opinions.

The valley boundary wasn't marked. Of course it wasn't. The Magistrate—or whoever built this pen—didn't need fences. You could see the change from the outside, the travelers said, a shimmer like heat-haze, but from the inside it was just grass giving way to grass, and then the grass stopped being valley grass. It grew taller. Wilder. It had a smell to it, green and alive in a way that made my chest hurt, because I'd forgotten things were allowed to smell like that.

Isolde stopped walking. "This is where I'd normally give a speech," she said. "About how once you cross, there's no coming back. About how the collector will feel you the moment you do. About how everything you've built here ends today."

"Please skip it."

"No promises. Mostly I wanted to say—" She looked at me with those flat cartographer's eyes that saw terrain where other people saw scenery, and for a second something moved behind them, something almost soft. "I wanted to say I've sold routes to a hundred people running from a hundred things, and most of them die stupid deaths within the year. You might be different. You're annoying enough to survive."

"Isolde. That was nearly a compliment."

"Don't get used to it." She nodded at the treeline. "On three."

We didn't count. We didn't need to. I took one step, and then another, and the third step crossed a line no eye could find, and the world came back.

* * *

I have drowned before. Once, as a child, before everything—I fell through ice on a pond behind our house, and I remember the specific quality of that moment: the shock of cold so total it registered as light, as sound, as taste, every sense screaming at once and none of them saying anything useful.

Crossing the veil was drowning.

It hit me like a wall of water. Not gently, not gradually, not like a door opening—it came back all at once, ten years of it, owed and compounded, and the pressure of it drove me to my knees in the wet grass. Every hair on my arms stood up. My teeth ached. The air itself was *loud*, humming with currents and threads and old spells sunk into the land like nails into a ship's hull, and underneath all of it, rising fast, was the other thing.

The storm-thing. The inheritance. The part of me I'd spent a decade pretending was dead.

It woke like a animal that has been kicked too many times and remembers every kick. I felt it uncoil along my spine, tasting the open air, testing the vastness of the sky, and I heard myself laugh—a cracked, ugly, terrified sound—because gods help me, it felt *good*. Ten years of silence and now the whole symphony at once. My fingertips sparked blue-white. Somewhere above us, clouds that had been minding their own business began, very quietly, to gather.

"Wendell." Isolde's voice came from very far away, or maybe I was very far away. Her hand closed on my shoulder—she flinched at the static but held on, which I later decided was the bravest thing anyone did in this entire story—and she dragged me back into myself by sheer force of irritation. "*Wendell.* Breathe. You're glowing. People who glow get noticed."

"I know." My voice came out wrong, doubled, like two people speaking in unison. I swallowed hard and pushed the storm down, down, down, back into the cellar I'd built for it, and it went—but sullenly, the way a dog goes to its kennel, watching me the whole time. "I'm fine. I'm fine. That was—"

"Drowning?"

"Yeah." I knelt there shaking in the grass, half-sick, half-*singing*, and understood that I would never be able to describe to anyone what it was like, going deaf and getting your hearing back in the same heartbeat. Terror and joy, braided so tight you couldn't find the seam. "How far to the road?"

"Two days' walk to the Cinder Road. Three to Gravenholt if the weather holds." She glanced up at the gathering clouds. "Which it won't, apparently, thanks to you."

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize. Document." She pulled out her journal and began sketching the treeline, the haze, the exact spot where the world changed, because that was how Isolde loved things—by measuring them. "For the record: crossing confirmed possible from inside. Note the subject's dramatic collapse. Note the lightning hands."

"They weren't lightning hands."

"They were absolutely lightning hands."

I got up. My legs held, mostly. And that's when I felt it—not the magic, not the storm, but the *attention*. A point of cold between my shoulder blades, precise as a needle. Like being watched by something that had been waiting ten days for exactly this moment and intended to enjoy it.

I didn't turn around. Some instinct older than sense told me not to turn around. But I saw Isolde look past my shoulder, and I saw her face go carefully blank, which for Isolde is the equivalent of a scream.

"What is it," I said.

"Grey livery," she said quietly. "Ridge line, north. Standing very still." A pause. "It's raising its hand."

"Towards us?"

"No." Her jaw tightened. "Towards *you*. Drawing something. In the air."

I felt it then, whatever it drew—a hook settling into me, gentle as frost forming, invisible and unmistakable. A mark. A signature on a debt I hadn't signed, in a ledger I'd never seen. The cold point between my shoulders warmed slightly, and then pulsed once, slow and patient, like a heart learning my rhythm.

Like something taking attendance.

"Can you outrun it?" I asked.

Isolde gave me a look I'd come to think of as her honest face—the one she wore when the maps ran out and the truth had to do the navigating. "Nobody outruns the Undercourt. But," and here her mouth curved into something that was almost a smile, sharp at both ends, "nobody's ever walked into it carrying a stolen map, a ghost's unfinished business, and lightning hands, either. So. Congratulations, Wendell Dravenmoor. You've just become interesting."

Above the ridge, the figure in grey lowered its hand, satisfied, and was gone between one blink and the next.

The mark pulsed again. Patient. Certain.

And far away, in the direction of Mount Vhalen, something old and patient began, very softly, to laugh.

### Isolde

I've guided pilgrims across the Ashfall Wastes, smuggled a bishop through a war zone using nothing but goat tracks and confidence, and once talked my way past a troll by critiquing its bridge architecture until it got self-conscious. I am not easily rattled.

But I'll admit the crossing shook me. Not the magic—that part I expected, though watching Wendell drop like a felled tree and come up sparking was educational. No, what shook me was the ridge, and the figure on it, and the fact that when it drew its little sigil in the air, I recognized the style of the linework.

I'd seen it before. Weeks ago. In a room in Gravenholt with velvet walls and no windows, across a desk of black glass, while a voice like closing doors explained the terms of a route I'd been hired to sell.

*Delivery upon crossing,* the voice had said. *Confirmation upon marking. The rest, when she reaches the city.*

I told myself, as I always told myself, that it was fine. That debts were debts, and everyone paid someone, and the woman I was doing it for had a name written in the same black-glass ledger as Wendell Dravenmoor, and hers had a date next to it that was coming due soon. Very soon. Names in that ledger didn't wait forever.

Still. When the mark settled into him—when I watched the exact moment a person becomes *property*—my hand shook on the pen, and I smudged the treeline I was drawing, and for one long breath I couldn't tell whether the cold in my chest was the mountain wind or something else.

Wendell was laughing at something Corvin-less and grim, brushing grass off his knees, trying to look like a man who hadn't just been branded by hell's accountancy department. He looked young, suddenly. Young and stubborn and doomed, and walking straight into the one trap in the world built precisely his size.

"Well," I said, shouldering my pack and turning south toward the Cinder Road, toward Gravenholt, toward everything I'd set in motion. "Keep up. And try not to call any storms before breakfast."

Behind us, the veil shimmered once, like water settling after a stone.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, a page turned.
