## 7. The Ghost in the Margins

The dead man came back on the third night, and he did not have the decency to knock.

I should explain that by then I'd stopped sleeping properly. The map had seen to that. It sat in my pack like a cat that had decided the house was its house now, radiating a smug, patient weight, and every time I tried to leave it somewhere—the hearth, the well, once wedged under a fencepost where I told myself a badger might do something useful—it turned up again among my things with the quiet insolence of an object that has never once been lost.

So when I woke past midnight to a cold draft in a room with one shuttered window, I already knew what it was before I looked. That's the thing about dread. It stops surprising you. It just gets comfortable, pulls up a chair, and waits for you to notice it.

He was sitting on my table. On the table, not at it—perched cross-legged on the scarred oak like a gargoyle someone had commissioned from a sculptor who found gargoyles funny. He was translucent the way breath is translucent on a winter morning, grey-blue around the edges, and he was wearing the same road-stained courier's leathers he'd died in, complete with the arrow hole through his chest that I had personally failed to close in time.

"You snore," he said.

I did what any sensible person does when confronted with the ghost of a man whose blood is drying into their belongings. I threw the water pitcher at him. It passed through his head, shattered against the wall, and he winced sympathetically at the mess rather than at his own incorporeality, which told me something about how long he'd been dead.

"Rude," he said. "Also ineffective. Also, you're going to want to clean that up before morning or you'll cut your feet, and I've watched enough people die this month."

"Who are you?" My voice came out steadier than I felt, which was a small mercy. Inside, I was doing arithmetic I didn't like. A ghost in a magic-dead valley. A valley specifically chosen because nothing of the kind could exist here. Which meant either the veil over Hollowmere had cracked—and everything I'd spent ten years pretending wasn't true came pouring back in—or this thing had been inside me all along, folded up small in my pack, waiting.

"Corvin," he said, with a little bow that managed to be both courtly and mocking. "Corvin Ashvale, formerly of the Grey Route couriers, currently of nowhere in particular. And you're the Dravenmoor."

The name hit me like a hand flat against the chest. Ten years, and nobody had said it aloud. In the valley I was just Wendell, keeper of four hives and a stubborn vegetable patch, the quiet one who came down from the north slope years ago and never gave anyone cause to ask questions.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"Right." He examined his fingernails, which were also transparent, which must have made the examination unsatisfying. "And I don't know what it's like to be warm. We're both liars, but mine's at least more convincing." He unfolded himself from the table and drifted closer, and I pressed back against the headboard hard enough to make the frame complain. "Relax. If I wanted to hurt you, I'd have done it while you were drooling into your pillow. Believe me, the temptation was real. You drool *a lot*."

"What are you?"

"A guide." He said it the way a man says *prisoner*, and the two words sat on top of each other so neatly that for a moment I saw both shapes at once. "Bound to the map. Bound to whoever holds the map. Congratulations, by the way. Out of everyone in the world, the universe picked the one person who can't even look at a mirror without flinching."

I hadn't told anyone about the mirrors.

"How—" I started.

"The map knows things," Corvin said, waving a hand. "It's blood-inked. Blood remembers. Yours especially, apparently, but we'll get there, and honestly I'm dreading getting there, so let's talk about me first. I'm very interesting. I died dramatically. There was an arrow. There was mud. There was a speech I'd been rehearsing for weeks that got cut off halfway through the second word, which I'm still bitter about."

"You died bringing me that map."

"I died bringing that map to whoever would take it," he corrected, "and you were simply the nearest set of hands, so don't go building a monument. Although—" and here the flippancy snagged on something, a hitch in the translucency of him, like light bending wrong through dirty glass, "—although I'll admit I hoped it'd be someone like you. Stubborn. Guilty. The sort who takes responsibility for things even when it's stupid to. You get a lot of ghosts in my line of work. Well. You *get* one ghost, if you're unlucky, and he's stuck with you, and you learn to read people fast because otherwise eternity is very boring."

"Stuck with me," I repeated. "For how long?"

His grin went crooked. "Ah. Yes. The terms and conditions. I was hoping we'd bond first. Maybe share a campfire. Ghosts can't feel fire but we appreciate the gesture."

"*How long?*"

"Until your name's off the ledger or mine is," he said. "Whichever comes first. Probably neither. Probably we just walk together until one of us figures out how to be free, and the other one watches, and that's the whole story." He hopped off the table—he didn't quite land, more hovered an inch above the floorboards, which somehow made it worse—and spread his arms. "But hey! Free room and board. I don't eat. I don't sleep. I know every road between here and the mountain, including three that don't technically exist anymore. I make excellent conversation. I'm told my commentary during ambushes is 'unhelpful,' but those people are dead now, so who's to say?"

Outside, the wind moved along the treeline, and I thought of the figure in grey livery standing at the valley's edge, patient as frost. Three nights ago. It hadn't come back—or rather, it hadn't needed to. Things that wait are worse than things that chase.

"The solstice," I said.

Something shifted behind Corvin's eyes. For a moment all the performance dropped away, and what was underneath was young—younger than me, maybe twenty-five when the arrow found him—with the particular exhausted clarity of someone who has run out of road and knows it.

"Winter solstice," he said quietly. "Nineteen days. On the solstice, the Undercourt seals its ledger for the year. Anything still owed gets collected. Anything still held gets kept. Permanently." He tapped his own chest, right over the arrow hole. "My name's in that book. Written in blood-ink, same as the map. When I sold my route to them—I was broke, I was proud, it seemed like free money, it's always the same story—they didn't take payment up front. They took collateral. A soul makes wonderful collateral. It appreciates in value."

"And on the solstice—"

"On the solstice they stop accepting redemption. The debt converts. What's held becomes owned." His voice stayed level, and I recognized the effort of that, the way you recognize scaffolding on a building. I'd spent ten years holding my own voice level about other things. "There's a window. Before the solstice, a debt can be discharged. Stolen, bought, bargained, burned—depends on the creditor's mood. After? The page closes and the ink dries and that's that. Forever isn't a metaphor down there. It's an administrative state."

I sat with that. Nineteen days. To get out of a valley that nothing entered willingly, across country I hadn't seen in a decade, to a mountain I'd only ever heard named in my mother's whisper-voice—*Vhalen, little storm, Vhalen, where the debts live*—and steal something back from the dead.

"I can't," I said.

"Yeah," Corvin agreed cheerfully. "You can't. Definitely not. No chance at all." He drifted toward the window, and where he passed, the candleflame guttered and leaned as though bowing. "Here's what I know about you, Wendell Dravenmoor, in three days of observation from inside a folded piece of paper, which is more introspection than most people manage in a lifetime. One: you talk to your bees like they're colleagues, and the bees agree with you, which is unsettling. Two: you keep your mother's ring on a string under your shirt and you touch it when you think no one's watching, which is never, because I'm always watching, and let me tell you, it's a lot of watching. Three: you haven't left this valley in ten years, and you tell yourself it's because you can't. But I've stood at that boundary with you. Nothing's stopping you. Nothing except the story you need it to be."

"Shut up."

"Four," he continued, merciless, delighted, "you were relieved when I put that map in your hands. Terrified, yes, grieving, obviously—but underneath, way down deep in the cellar where you keep the good wine, some part of you heard the word *Undercourt* and thought, *oh. So that's where she went.*"

The words landed in the silence like stones into a well. I didn't remember deciding to stand. I was up, fists clenched, shaking—not at him, not exactly, but at the accuracy, and hate is just love with nowhere to go, and grief is just love with nowhere to be, and I had ten years of both packed into a body I'd kept very, very still.

"She vanished," I said. "When I was nine. She walked out one autumn morning and never came back, and everyone said the mountain took her, and I spent thirty years—ten years—" My voice cracked on the number. "Ten years being punished for a storm I never meant to call, and you want to tell me what I feel?"

"Yes," said Corvin, gently now, the mockery set down like a heavy bag. "That's literally my job. Bound guides tell holders uncomfortable truths. It's clause seven. I fought the wording in negotiations. I lost."

We looked at each other. Ghost and exile. Two people with no country between them.

"Nineteen days," I said finally.

"Eighteen and a half, if we count tonight, which I would prefer not to, because counting makes it real and I'm enjoying this stage where it's all banter."

"And the collector. The one in grey. If I step outside—"

"He marks you. Not kills—marks. You become *claimed property in transit*, and then every debt-thing between here and Mount Vhalen knows you're carrying something the Court wants delivered home." Corvin's grin returned, thinner. "Which raises the obvious question: why send a collector at all? Why not just let you rot in your valley? Why deliver the leash to the dog?" He shrugged, and the shrug had too many meanings in it. "Because the Court doesn't want the map back, Wendell. The map was always going to end up in your hands. That was the design. I was just the delivery mechanism, and I resent how well I worked."

"That doesn't answer anything."

"No," he agreed. "It really doesn't. Welcome to dealing with the Undercourt. Every answer opens onto a bigger question, like that nesting-doll nonsense rich people display. By the time you reach the smallest doll, you're already inside the mountain."

I looked at the map, lying innocently atop my pack. Up close, in candlelight, I could see the ink had moved again—the routes redrawn, the roads rearranged, a thin red line now running from my cottage door east and south, patient as a vein.

"If I do this," I said slowly, "if I walk out of here after ten years, I'm going to need help. Real help. Someone who knows the roads, the passes, the places where the world's worn thin. I'm a beekeeper. My combat experience is smoke and patience."

Corvin perked up with the shameless enthusiasm of the recently deceased presented with fresh material. "*Now* we're getting somewhere. And wouldn't you know it, there happens to be a person in this valley who deals in exactly that. Sells doors where there aren't any. Sharp tongue. Sharper knives. Eyes like she's auditing you for hidden fees." He leaned close, translucent and conspiratorial. "The cartographer. Isolde. She comes to the trading post day after tomorrow."

"You know her?"

"I know *of* her. Everyone on the Grey Routes knows of her. Half her maps are the only reason certain roads still work." He paused, and something flickered across his face—hesitation, quickly buried, but I was learning to read the burial. "One warning, holder to holder. People who sell impossible routes always owe somebody for the impossibility. Ask her who holds her paper before you trust her with yours."

The candle chose that moment to drown in its own wax, and in the sudden dark Corvin glowed faintly, grey-blue, like moonlight remembering itself.

"Get some sleep," he said. "You drool less when you're horizontal and unbothered. I'll be here. I'm always here. That's the whole tragedy of me."

I lay back down and did not sleep, and sometime before dawn I heard him humming—an old road song, tuneless and half-remembered, the kind a man hums to keep the silence from asking questions. It was, I realized, the first sound of home I'd heard in ten years that hadn't been my own.

Day after tomorrow, the trading post. Isolde. Eighteen days to a mountain full of debts.

I touched the ring on its string beneath my shirt, and outside, far off at the treeline, something in grey took one step closer and then, courteously, waited.
