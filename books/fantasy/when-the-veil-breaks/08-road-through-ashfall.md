## 8. Road Through Ashfall

I have crossed forty-one borders in my life, and I will tell you a secret about maps: they lie. Not in the way people think—north is north, rivers are where rivers are—but in the way that matters. A map says *here* and you believe it means safety, or passage, or home. It never tells you what the ground remembers.

The Cinder Road remembered fire.

We had been walking it for three days when the bandits found us, and in those three days I had learned three things about my traveling companions. First: Wendell Dravenmoor walked like a person trying to take up less space in the world, shoulders folded inward, as if apologizing to the air itself for occupying it. Second: Corvin's ghost could talk for six hours straight about the quality of his former boots without once mentioning how he'd died, which took a particular kind of cowardice I almost admired. And third—

Third I hadn't learned yet. But I was about to.

"Left at the fork," Corvin said, drifting ahead of us like a lantern with opinions. "The right one goes down into the slag fields. Very picturesque if you enjoy standing stones made of melted church bells."

"The left one goes through the pass," I said. "The pass has people in it."

"People," Wendell repeated, in the tone of someone encountering an unfamiliar species of insect.

"They're called bandits. They rob you." I checked the strap on my satchel out of habit, though everything worth stealing was stitched into the lining. "It's fine. I've been robbed eleven times. There's a technique."

"There's a *technique*," Wendell said flatly.

"You give them the obvious purse, you keep your hands visible, and you compliment something they're proud of. The knife, usually. Bandits love their knives." I shrugged. "Most robbery is just loneliness with better leverage."

Corvin laughed—the sound came out thin and crackly, like paper tearing politely. "Oh, I like her. I liked her before, but now there's documentation."

Wendell didn't laugh. They were staring up the pass at the haze of woodsmoke rising over the ridge, and their face had gone still in a way I was beginning to catalog as dangerous. Not afraid-still. *Listening*-still.

"We could go around," they said quietly.

"Around adds four days. We don't have four days." I didn't say *before the solstice*. Nobody said it anymore; we all carried the deadline like a stone in the shoe. "We go through."

"You're certain?"

"Wendell." I put a hand on my hip. "In eleven robberies, do you know how many times I've died?"

"...Zero, presumably."

"Presumably. See? Confidence. It's half the technique." I started up the switchback before they could argue, because arguing with Wendell Dravenmoor was like arguing with a held breath—you could feel the pressure building behind it and you knew, you *knew*, it would have to come out eventually.

I just didn't expect it to come out the way it did.

* * *

The bandit camp sat in a saddle of the pass, tucked against a cliff face that still bore scorch-marks from whatever ancient catastrophe had named this road. Eight men, maybe nine—a cookfire, two lean-tos, a rope strung across the path at ankle height with little brass bells tied to it, which was either a warning system or the worst wind chime ever conceived.

They saw us coming from a quarter mile off, which was the point of the bells, and by the time we reached them, five of them had arranged themselves across the road with the practiced looseness of men who did this daily. The leader was a big woman with a burn-scarred jaw and a crossbow she held the way other people hold teacups: casually, and with total confidence that everyone understood what it was for.

"Well," she said. "Travelers."

"Travelers," I agreed, hands already loose and open at my sides. First rule of the technique: be boring. Boring things get robbed and released. Interesting things get robbed and kept. "Just passing through to Gravenholt. Nothing worth your trouble."

"Everything's worth my trouble. That's the whole business model." She looked me over, then Wendell, then—this was unusual—she looked at Wendell *twice*. Something flickered across her scarred face. Recognition, or the cousin of it. "Your friend's quiet."

"My friend's shy."

"Shy." The word hung there. One of the men behind her shifted, and I caught the glint of a second crossbow in the lean-to's shadow. Nine, then. The math was getting unfriendly. "See, we had word come down the road yesterday. Courier from Vhalen way, dead of a wound nobody gave him, map missing from his effects. Word says whoever's carrying it—" she smiled, and the smile had cost her teeth, "—is worth more than everything in my camp. Worth more than the whole *road*."

Ah, I thought. So the Undercourt's reach extended past the veil after all. Someone had posted a notice. Somewhere between the tollhouse and here, our little funeral procession had acquired a price tag, and I felt the familiar cold weight settle into my stomach—not fear, exactly. Arithmetic.

"How much?" I asked, because I genuinely wanted to know, and because asking questions is the second-best distraction technique I own.

"More than you'd believe."

"I believe quite a lot. It's my profession."

"Enough to buy back every debt in Gravenholt twice over." Her eyes never left Wendell. "So here's how this goes. Your friend gives up the map, comes along quiet, and you two walk away rich. Everyone's happy. Well." The teacup-crossbow tilted. "Everyone who counts."

And here is where I should tell you what I did, because I'm honest in these recollections even when it makes me look bad: I considered it. For perhaps two full seconds, I ran the numbers. Wendell in their custody, my cut of nothing but my freedom and a head start, Isolde lives to bargain another day. The old arithmetic. The arithmetic I'd been doing since I was nineteen years old and first learned that every person is a sum and some sums are easier to spend than others.

Then I looked at Wendell.

They weren't looking at the crossbow. They weren't looking at the leader or the rope of bells or any of the nine directions death could come from. They were looking at the *ground*, at the grey ash underfoot, and their lips were moving slightly, silently, the way people move them when they're counting—or praying, or holding something down.

"No," I said.

"No?" The leader raised an eyebrow.

"Not the map, not the friend, not the quiet walk. No." And I heard myself add, with genuine surprise, "I have a contract."

"You have a *contract*."

"With terms. Look them up sometime." I stepped sideways, putting myself—foolishly, gloriously, against every rule of my profession—between the crossbow and Wendell. "Also, small note, free of charge: your man in the lean-to is holding that bow wrong. If he fires it like that it'll take his thumb off."

The man in the lean-to looked at his grip. This bought us exactly one heartbeat.

That was when the sky turned the color of a bruise.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because I've spent my life describing places and I have never described anything like this, and also because writing it down makes it slightly smaller, and I need it smaller.

There was no lightning. That's the thing no one believes later. Lightning comes *down*, out of a cloud, and this didn't come from anywhere—it came *up*, out of the ash itself, out of Wendell, a white-violet light that didn't illuminate so much as *insist*, and the air pressure changed so fast my ears popped and every hair on my body stood on end like the world had become a static-charged mirror of me. The cookfire went out—not blown out, *crushed* out, the flames pressed flat like flowers in a book. The bells on the rope all rang at once, though nothing had touched them, and the sound wasn't ringing anymore, it was screaming, high and thin and metallic.

Wendell was on their knees in the road with their arms wrapped around themselves, and the light poured off them like water off a fountain's crown, and their eyes—when they lifted their head I saw their eyes were full of storm-cloud, actual cloud, turning slowly, and I understood in one cold flash why the valley existed, why the veil, why ten years of exile, why mirrors and bees and the careful apology of their posture:

They weren't hiding from magic.

Magic was hiding inside them, and it had gotten out.

The leader fired. I'll grant her this: she reacted faster than most trained soldiers would have. The bolt flew true at Wendell's chest—and stopped. Midair. Hung there quivering in the charged air like a fly in amber, and then, gently, horribly, it turned around.

It didn't fire back. It simply *hung there*, pointed at the woman who'd loosed it, trembling with the effort of its own reversal, until she threw herself sideways and it buried itself in the cliff face to the fletching, close enough to her ear that I heard the whisper of it.

"GIVE IT BACK," Wendell said, and the voice was theirs and not theirs, doubled, harmonized with something vast underneath it, something that spoke in pressure and ozone. "GIVE IT BACK GIVE IT BACK GIVE IT BACK—"

Every bell on the rope exploded.

Not fell. Exploded, each one cracking into shrapnel of hot brass, and two of the bandits went down clutching their faces, and the cookfire's iron pot lifted six inches off the stones and hung there spinning, and the ash began to rise—all of it, the whole grey history of the Cinder Road lifting off the ground in a slow spiral around Wendell like the world's largest and most funereal maypole, and I realized with detached professional clarity that I was going to die here, on a road that remembered fire, killed by a storm wearing the body of a person who apologized to furniture.

"STOP," I shouted, and I didn't shout words so much as I shouted *myself*, threw my voice and my name and my whole stubborn cartographer's refusal to die unmapped into the word. "WENDELL. STOP. LOOK AT ME. YOU'RE GOING TO KILL EVERYONE INCLUDING THE ONES WHO DIDN'T DESERVE IT AND *INCLUDING YOURSELF*—"

Their head snapped toward me. The cloud-eyes found mine.

And I did the third-best technique I own, the one I've never had occasion to use before, the one I invented on the spot because I am a professional: I told the truth.

"This is what they exiled you for," I said, loud, over the roar. "This is what you've been sitting on for ten years. It's real, it's yours, and it is *magnificent*—and right now it is going to eat you alive unless you put it DOWN."

For a moment—one long, ringing moment—the spiral of ash hung motionless around them, thousands of grey feathers frozen mid-fall, the reversed bolt humming in the air, the pot spinning, the bandits flattened against the ground like pressed flowers themselves. The clouds in Wendell's eyes turned. Turned again.

Then they closed their eyes, and exhaled, and the world came down all at once—ash pattering to earth like grey snow, the pot clanging onto stone, the pressure releasing so suddenly that my knees buckled. The last of the violet light sank back beneath Wendell's skin and was gone, leaving only a young person on their knees in a dirty road, shaking, hollow-eyed, ordinary.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Nine bandits stared at us. Two were bleeding from brass shrapnel; the rest looked like men who had just watched their god lose an argument. The leader picked herself up off the road very slowly, keeping her hands where everyone could see them, and looked at Wendell for a long time.

"That bounty," she said hoarsely. "It's from the Undercourt, yeah?"

Nobody answered. She nodded as if we had.

"Keep your map," she said. "Keep your friend. Keep the whole cursed road." She backed away, signaling her people up, collecting the bleeding ones with a tenderness that surprised me. At the edge of the camp she paused, and spat into the ash, and said—to Wendell, directly, and I have thought about her saying it every day since:

"Whoever's hunting you isn't paying enough. Whatever you are."

Then they were gone, up the far side of the pass, and it was the three of us and the settling dust and a cookfire's dead coals smoking faintly in the wrecked air.

* * *

Corvin broke the silence first, naturally. He was hovering near the shattered bells, examining a fragment of brass that passed through his fingers when he tried to pick it up.

"So," he said. "Fun fact. When I signed on with the court's courier service, the orientation packet mentioned hazards. Bandits. Weather. Tolls." He looked at Wendell. "It did not mention that the package would occasionally *do that*."

"Don't." Wendell hadn't stood up yet. Their voice was scraped raw, sanded down to the grain. "Don't joke. Not right now."

"I'm a ghost, friend. Joking is load-bearing." But Corvin drifted closer, and his crackle softened into something almost gentle. "You pulled it back. You know that, yes? Half the wardens in the court's old stories couldn't do that. They called the storm and the storm kept them."

"I nearly killed Isolde." Wendell's hands were flat on the ash, pressing down, as if the ground might otherwise float away. "I nearly killed *everyone*. I could feel—I could feel how easy it would be. Like—" they struggled, and I watched them struggle, and something in my chest hurt in a way I resented, "—like a door that's always unlocked. All it wants is for me to stop holding it shut. And it's *tired*, Isolde. Ten years of holding it shut, and it's so tired, and it would be so *easy*—"

"Easy things usually cost the most." I crouched down to their level, because looming felt wrong and because my legs were still unsteady anyway. "Believe me. I've priced them."

They looked up at me, and their eyes were only eyes again—grey, exhausted, human—but for a moment I saw past them, saw the shape of the thing underneath, and I understood something I would carry up the rest of that mountain and beyond:

Wendell Dravenmoor was not afraid of the Undercourt. They were afraid of being *right*. Of the exile being justified. Of the Magistrate's ledger and the bounty notices and every whispered story about the Dravenmoor bloodline all pointing at the same terrible conclusion—that the storm wasn't something that happened to them.

It was something they *were*.

"You saw," they said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I saw."

"And?"

I should have lied. Lying is the fourth-best technique I own and the one I use most often. Instead I reached out and took their ash-covered hand, which surprised both of us, and said:

"And I'm updating the route. We avoid towns. We travel at night. And when we get to that court, Wendell—" I squeezed, ash and all, "—you are going to walk in there and show them exactly what they've been hunting. On your terms. Not theirs."

Something moved behind their eyes—gratitude, or terror, or the place where those two share a border. On a map, I would have drawn it as disputed territory.

We made camp that night in the lee of the cliff, no fire, cold rations. I took first watch, which meant I sat with my back to the rock and watched Wendell sleep—or fail to sleep, turning and turning, their fingers twitching against the bedroll like a pianist dreaming of keys. Once, deep in the smallest hours, the ash around our camp stirred, just slightly, all in one direction, toward them. Like the road leaning in to listen.

Like it recognized something.

I marked our position on the chart by memory, by starlight, and drew the line forward toward Mount Vhalen, and above the mountain I drew the little symbol I use for weather I can't predict.

Then, because no one was watching, I drew it again. Bigger.

Three days to Gravenholt. Four after that, if the passes held, to the mountain's throat. And somewhere behind us—or ahead of us, with the Undercourt you could never be sure which—a collector in grey livery was reading the same road signs I was, following the same trail of bent grass and frightened bandits, patient as interest accruing.

The solstice was eleven days away.

I did the arithmetic one more time, and for the first time in my life, I hated the answer.
