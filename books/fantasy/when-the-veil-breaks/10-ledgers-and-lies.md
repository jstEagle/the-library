## 10. Ledgers and Lies

The trouble with selling a route through an impossible place is that you have to walk it yourself to prove it exists.

That's what I told Wendell, anyway, when I suggested we overnight in Gravenholt instead of skirting the city as any sensible person would. "We need supplies," I said. "Rope, salt pork, a second lantern." All true, in the way that most good lies are built on a foundation of true things. We did need rope. We did need salt pork. What I actually needed was to stand in a specific cellar off Tanner's Row at the hour when the bells went wrong, and I could not exactly explain that to my traveling companions without explaining everything else, which would have meant explaining the ledger with my sister's name inked in it, which would have meant admitting what I'd done, and I am many things but I am not brave enough for confessions before breakfast.

Corvin, bless his translucent heart, wanted to see the city. Ghosts don't get much sightseeing. He drifted along beside me through the tannery stink and the fish-market shouting, making observations about architecture that no one asked for, while Wendell haggled over lantern oil two streets back with a merchant who smelled like he'd been pickled in it. I'd told them I'd meet them at the sign of the Gilded Compass by sundown. That gave me three hours. Three hours to do the thing, and then three hours to become the kind of person who could look Wendell Dravenmoor in the eye afterward and say nothing.

You'd think it would be harder than it was. It wasn't, and that's the part I keep turning over like a coin with a worn face.

* * *

Gravenholt wears its wealth the way old soldiers wear scars: prominently, and with stories attached. The city sits where three trade roads braid together, and every building taller than its neighbor is a monument to somebody's grandfather cheating somebody else's grandfather. The Undercourt loves it here. Not because of the money—money is just the skin of debt, the part you can see. The court loves Gravenholt because everyone in it owes someone something. A city like that is a orchard in late autumn, heavy and ripe, and the collectors come through with their grey livery and their soft voices and shake the branches.

I'd met mine twice before. The first time was eight years ago, in Vessic, when my sister Sera coughed blood into a handkerchief and the physicians' guild named a price for saving her that would have bought the house we grew up in, plus the street it stood on. I had a talent for maps even then—not the drawing of them, anyone can draw, but the *finding*. Doors where walls should be. Passages that shouldn't fit and did. I sold routes to people who needed to be somewhere they weren't welcome, and the coin came fast, and the coin was never enough, and one evening a person in grey sat down across from me at a tavern table I hadn't seen them approach and said, "Your sister's debt to the physicians can be purchased."

"Debts can be bought?"

"Everything can be bought," she said, in the tone of someone correcting a child who has asked whether the sea is wet. "The only question is what you offer in exchange."

What I offered was a route. One route, through a place I'd found and never told another living soul about—a door beneath the drowned chapel at Marrow's Reach that opened somewhere it had no business opening. She took it. Sera lived. And I learned the shape of the arrangement, which is this: the Undercourt doesn't want your soul, not usually. Souls are messy, resistant, prone to burning bright at inconvenient moments. What the court wants is *obligation*, cleanly rendered, compounding quarterly. They buy your debts from your creditors, and then they own the shape of your future. You spend years paying down a balance that never quite reaches zero, doing small errands, opening small doors, and each errand makes the next one easier to ask for.

Sera's cure cost me four routes over six years. Then the fifth request came, and it wasn't a route.

It was a name. *Wendell Dravenmoor.* And a map, drawn in ink I now recognized everywhere—the same rust-dark red that crawled across the courier's chart sleeping in my pack. Find the exile, the grey woman said. Get close. When the moment comes, you'll know it, and you'll do what's asked, and your sister's page will burn clean from the ledger.

I told myself I'd decide later. I've been telling myself that for eleven weeks.

* * *

Tanner's Row after dark smells like a wound. The tanneries shut down at dusk but the smell stays, soaked into the cobblestones, into the walls, into your clothes if you linger. The cellar door I wanted sat between a chandler's and a shuttered dye-house, unmarked except for a scratch in the wood shaped like a hook—which is not a symbol of anything, which is the point. Symbols attract attention. A scratch looks like damage.

Inside, stairs went down farther than the building above had any right to possess. I counted forty-one steps. On the last one, the sound of Gravenholt stopped entirely, cut off like a door closing, and I stood in a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse and didn't care for the company.

The room below was long and low, lit by lamps that burned without flickering, which bothered me more than the darkness would have. Fire is supposed to move. Along one wall ran shelves, floor to ceiling, and the shelves held books—but not books the way most people mean. Ledgers. Thousands of them, spines stamped in silver, arranged in an order that made my eyes slide off whenever I tried to find a pattern in it.

She was already there, of course. My collector. Grey wool, grey gloves, a face like a well-made piece of furniture: handsome, functional, impossible to describe five minutes after leaving the room. I've tried. I genuinely cannot tell you what she looked like beyond *grey*, and I suspect that's policy.

"You're early," she said.

"You're always early. Someone has to compensate."

Something that might have been amusement moved behind her eyes and decided against surfacing. She gestured to a table between us—plain oak, two chairs, a single sheet of paper laid square upon it. No pen. That absence told me more than anything else in the room. Whatever got written tonight wouldn't be written in ink I could buy.

"Sit, Mistress Vane."

I sat. "Isolde," I said. "If we're doing business, we're doing it under the name I travel under. Isolde Fairwind, cartographer. You know that. You chose that alias yourself."

"Aliases are useful," she agreed. "Yours especially. It keeps certain parties comfortable." She folded her gloved hands. "And how is our traveler? The quiet one. The bee-keeper."

"They're not a bee-keeper anymore. They're walking toward your master's mountain with a dead man's map stitched to their spine, so I'd say the bees are the least of their concerns."

"Mm." She turned the paper on the table so it faced me. It was blank. Of course it was blank. "You've been together nine days. Tell me what you've observed."

I'd prepared for this. I'd spent the whole road south preparing for this, composing answers in the dark while Wendell slept, cataloguing everything I'd noticed and deciding which parts were safe to sell. The storm-power, suppressed but leaking—no, I wouldn't give them that, or rather I'd give them the version they already had, because they'd exiled Wendell for it, they knew. The guilt. The stubbornness dressed up as humility. The way Wendell talked to Corvin like the ghost was a person and not a tool.

"The exile is cooperative," I said. "Motivated by the solstice deadline. Believes we're going to steal the courier's soul back out of the ledger. Doesn't suspect anything."

"And you find that easy? The deception?"

"I find it *necessary*." The words came out sharper than I intended, and I made myself soften them. This was the dance. I knew the steps. You don't snarl at the thing that holds your sister's leash. "There's a difference between lying to someone and lying *for* them, and I'm still deciding which one this is. Ask me again at the mountain."

"It will be too late to ask at the mountain. The asking happens now." She drew one glove off—slowly, deliberately, a conjuror's flourish—and touched her bare fingertip to the blank page. Where her skin met the paper, letters surfaced like bruises rising. Silver script, fine as spiderwork, and I read it upside down before I could stop myself.

*Isolde Vane. Balance outstanding: one delivery. Consideration held in trust: Seraphine Vane, aged 24, remitted.*

Remitted. Held in trust. Meaning Sera was alive, and free, and would stay that way exactly as long as I performed. The relief hit me first, warm and filthy, and the shame arrived half a breath behind it, and I sat there in the lamplight letting both of them use my body while my face did nothing at all. Ten years of trading in doors teaches you a certain stillness.

"The delivery," I said. My voice came out level. Small miracle. "Define it."

"When the moment comes—and it will come within the week—you will ensure the Dravenmoor heir arrives at the throne room unaccompanied by allies, unrestrained by fear, and *willing*." Her bare finger lifted from the page and the words sank back into the paper like stones into water. "Not dragged. Not tricked at the threshold. The coronation must be accepted. The court cannot force a crown onto an unwilling head; it can only arrange circumstances so persuasive that unwillingness becomes... impractical."

"So I'm not delivering a package. I'm delivering a yes."

"You are delivering the conditions under which yes becomes the only reasonable answer." She pulled the glove back on, joint by joint. "The Magistrate is patient. He has waited ten years and he can wait ten more, though he prefers not to. Your role is the final turn of the screw, Mistress Vane. Everything else—the map, the ghost, the deadline—is already in motion. You are merely the hand on the last door."

"And if I don't turn it?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled through forty-one steps and a city's worth of stone, the bells began the hour, and the notes came out wrong, flattened, as though the sound itself owed something and was being made to pay.

"If you don't," she said, "your sister's account reverts to its prior holders. The physicians' guild sold her debt to us at a loss; they'd be pleased to buy it back. The full price. With interest accrued across the years of our stewardship." She let that sit. "She is twenty-four, Isolde. The disease was slow the first time. Debts, unlike people, always come back."

I thought about Sera. Not the sickbed version—the real her, the one who used to steal my good pens and draw dragons on the margins of my charts, who laughed like a door coming off its hinges, who wrote to me twice a year from the sanatorium at Coldharbour in a round, careful hand that got steadier every letter. *The new treatment works,* the last one said. *I planted tulips. Come see them in spring.*

Come see them in spring.

"Yes," I heard myself say. "Understood. Conditions delivered. The heir arrives willing."

"Willing," she repeated, tasting the word, checking it for rot. "Good. One further instruction." She slid the blank-turned-blank-again paper aside and set down something else: a thin strip of grey cloth, a hand's width long, hemmed in silver thread. "When the moment comes, wear this at your wrist. It marks you as the court's hand to anything below that has eyes. There are things in the deep galleries that do not distinguish between guests and intruders, and the Magistrate would prefer his instrument survive the performance."

I picked up the ribbon. It weighed nothing. Cloth weighs nothing. That's the trick of it—that all the weight lives somewhere else, in a ledger, in a cellar, in the space between a promise and its keeping, and your hands stay light right up until they don't.

"One question," I said, standing. "The heir's own name. It's in the ledger—I've heard the ghost say the court keeps pages on everyone. If I deliver Wendell to that throne, and the crown goes on... what happens to the page with *Dravenmoor* written on it?"

For the first time in eight years of dealings, my collector hesitated. It lasted less than a heartbeat, a flicker like a lamp guttering, but I've spent my life reading margins and marginalia, and I caught it.

"That," she said, "is above your balance."

* * *

I walked back through Gravenholt with the ribbon hidden in my sleeve and the taste of the tannery in my throat, and I constructed the story I would live inside, the way I construct all my maps: start with true landmarks, connect them with lines that feel inevitable, and never show the reader the places where you invented the river.

Here is the map I drew for myself, walking those crooked streets:

*I haven't betrayed anyone yet. I've agreed to deliver conditions, and conditions are just geography—doors, timing, the placement of bodies in rooms. Wendell will walk into that throne room on their own feet, for their own reasons, to save a dead man's soul. If the Magistrate chooses that moment to spring his trap, that's the court's sin, not mine. I am a guide. Guides don't own what waits at the end of the road. And when it's done—when the crown is on and the ledger closes—Sera plants tulips, and I visit in spring, and someday, maybe, I find a route out from under all of it, the way I've found routes out from under everything else. There's always a door. Finding doors is the one thing I've ever been good at.*

It's a good map. Clean lines, plausible distances. Only one flaw, and I knew it as I climbed the stairs to the Gilded Compass and saw Wendell through the window, laughing at something Corvin was doing with a candle flame—actually laughing, the exile who'd spent ten years pretending to be ordinary, laughing like a person who'd forgotten to guard against it.

The flaw is this: on every other job, I never cared what happened to the person I was guiding. I sold them the route and wished them luck and forgot their faces by morning. Wendell Dravenmoor talks to ghosts like they matter. Wendell flinches at their own power like it's a wounded animal they're afraid of loving. Wendell is walking toward a trap I built the roadbed for, and they trust me, and trust, unlike debt, cannot be bought back once you've spent it.

I pushed open the door. Warmth, lamplight, the smell of bad stew.

"You're late," Wendell said, sliding a bowl toward me. "We ordered for you. Corvin says the stew here is a crime, but he also died in a ditch, so his standards may be compromised."

"My standards," Corvin said with dignity, "are eternal."

I sat down. I ate the criminal stew. I smiled, and the smile held, because I am very good at holding things, and I told myself—one more time, with feeling—that I hadn't yet done anything I couldn't undo.

Outside the window, at the far end of the street, something in grey stood beneath the eaves of the chandler's shop, and did not come in, and did not leave.

And Wendell, glancing past my shoulder, went very quiet, and said: "Isolde. Who is that?"
