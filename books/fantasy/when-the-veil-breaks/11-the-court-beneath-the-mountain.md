## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain swallowed us at dusk, and I mean that literally.

There was a door in the cliff face—not built, *grown*, if stone can be said to grow—and it opened for Isolde's knock like an old dog hearing its master's step. Beyond it, stairs. Thousands of them, spiraling down into a darkness that had texture, that had *weight*. The air coming up out of that dark smelled of candle smoke and old paper and something underneath both, something like the moment before lightning.

"Petitioners go down on foot," Isolde said, tying her hair back with the brisk efficiency of a woman preparing for surgery. "No one flies in the Undercourt. No one runs. You walk, you bow to nothing but the ledger, and you speak only when spoken to."

"And you?"

"I'm your advocate." She patted the satchel of maps at her hip. "Every petitioner needs someone who can prove they know where they're going. It's the only profession they respect down there."

Corvin drifted ahead of us down the first flight of stairs, translucent and glowing faintly, like a lantern made of grievance. He'd been insufferable since Gravenholt—since we'd learned what he really was, since I'd stopped speaking to him except through Isolde—but even his bitterness had gone quiet as we descended. Ghosts, it turns out, know when they're going home.

"You feel that?" he said finally, without turning around. "That hum in your teeth?"

I did. I'd been feeling it since the third hundred steps: a vibration low and constant, like standing too close to a hive. Like standing inside my own worst memory.

"That's the ledger," Corvin said. "Every soul in this mountain is written down somewhere below us, and writing has a sound, if you're part of what's written."

* * *

We walked for hours. Maybe longer. Time behaves badly underground, stretching like taffy, and by the time the stairs ended I had stopped counting my heartbeats and started counting the places where the wall-carvings showed people kneeling.

Because the walls were carved. That was the thing I hadn't been prepared for. Ten years in a magic-dead valley teaches you that power looks like absence—flat fields, silent air, mirrors that don't whisper. I'd forgotten that power can also look like *architecture*. The stairwell opened into galleries, and the galleries were lined with figures carved life-size into the living rock: petitioners of every age, every century, all of them bent, all of them holding out their hands as if waiting for something to be placed in them.

"What are they?" I whispered.

"Previous petitioners," Isolde said. "Or commemorations of them. Or warnings. Down here those three words mean roughly the same thing."

And then the last stair ended, and I saw the court itself, and I understood that everything above it had just been the porch.

* * *

The cavern was a cathedral the way a wolf is a dog.

It went up farther than my eyes wanted to follow, vaulted not by any architect's hand but by the slow patience of the mountain itself, ribbed with veins of pale stone that caught and held the light. And the light—the light was the horror of it, because the light came from *people*.

They hung from the ceiling in cages of black iron, hundreds of them, thousands, each cage barely bigger than a birdcage, and inside each one burned a soul. Not ghosts, not shades—*souls*, the raw article, glowing the way coals glow, some white-hot with rage or terror and others banked down to a dull orange ember of resignation. Their light fell through the iron bars in stripes and pooled on the cavern floor, so that walking across the court meant wading through other people's suffering, ankle-deep, everywhere.

I had spent ten years telling myself a story about what I'd lost when they exiled me. Community. Purpose. The right to be looked at without flinching. Standing under that ceiling, I learned what I had actually been protected from.

"They're the payments," Corvin said quietly. His voice had lost its edge; grief will do that even to a ghost. "When someone sells their soul to the Court, it doesn't stay with them. It gets brought here. Caged. Lit. A soul burns brighter than anything else in creation—that's why they use them instead of torches. You're looking at the Undercourt's chandeliers."

"How many?" Isolde asked, and her voice was steady, which is how I knew she was working hard at it.

"Last time anyone counted, eleven thousand," Corvin said. "But the count is never finished. That's sort of the point."

At the far end of the cavern rose the dais—a shelf of obsidian grown out of the floor like a tooth—and on the dais stood a desk. Not a throne. I want to be clear about that, because everything in me expected a throne, expected gold and skulls and some vast shadowed figure wreathed in smoke. Instead there was only a desk, long and plain and scarred by centuries of pen-scratches, and behind the desk sat a man.

He looked like a clerk. Grey robes, grey skin, spectacles perched on a nose like a knotted root, ink-stained fingers moving down a page with the mild attention of a tax assessor. If I had passed him in the market above, I would have taken him for someone's grandfather doing accounts.

"The Magistrate of Debts," Isolde murmured. "Don't let the face fool you. The face is the oldest trick down here."

* * *

The queue of petitioners was short. Three souls ahead of us, each escorted by a grey-livered servant—one of whom, I noticed with a cold drop in my stomach, wore the same livery as the shadow that had waited at the valley's edge. The collector hadn't given up. He'd simply come home.

We waited in line the way you wait anywhere: badly. Corvin hovered at my shoulder, invisible to everyone but me and, apparently, the occasional soul-cage overhead, several of which turned—*turned*, the flames bending like faces—to look at him as he passed beneath them.

"They remember me," he said, not quite steadily. "I delivered half of them. Little parcels of self, wrapped and signed for. 'Here's your soul, sir, mind the bars.'"

"You didn't know what happened to them after delivery," Isolde said.

"I knew." Corvin's laugh was a dry scrap of sound. "That's the thing nobody wants to hear about couriers. We always know. We just get very good at looking at the road instead of the destination." He drifted closer to me, and his glow guttered. "You're going to see him read your name off a page, Wendell Dravenmoor. When he does, don't react. Whatever he offers, whatever he says—the reaction is the product. He farms reactions."

Ahead of us, the current petitioner—a stooped old woman clutching a contract the size of a hymnal—finished her business. The Magistrate dipped his pen, spoke three words I couldn't hear, and two servants led her away toward a small door in the cavern wall, weeping quietly. Through the door, I glimpsed shelves. Endless shelves. Rows and rows of bound volumes receding into the dark like the ribs of some buried god.

The archive. Where souls were kept as inked names.

My hands were shaking. I pressed them flat against my thighs and thought about bees. About the hive-hum of a healthy colony, ten thousand wings in agreement. About the valley's flat, forgiving silence. *You came here to steal a page,* I told myself. *You are a petitioner, nothing more, no one special, the least remarkable person in this entire cavern.* The lie fit poorly. It always had.

"Next," said the Magistrate, without looking up.

Isolde stepped forward and bowed—shallow, precise, exactly the depth she'd drilled into me on the road. "Advocate Isolde Vane, licensed route-finder, presenting a petitioner under standard covenant."

The Magistrate's pen paused.

"Standard covenant," he repeated. His voice was dry and pleasant, like paper turning. "How novel. Most of your clients, Mistress Vane, arrive owing something." He looked up.

His eyes were ordinary. That was the worst part. Brown, tired, unremarkable—the eyes of a man who has read every book ever written and found none of it interesting. They moved past Isolde as if she were furniture and settled on me.

"And you are?"

"Wendell," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Wendell Marsh. Of the valley settlements."

Something ticked behind those tired eyes. Not recognition—I would have known recognition; I've spent ten years studying faces for it. Something smaller. The look of a man checking a list and finding, with faint pleasure, that a name is exactly where he left it.

"Marsh," he said. "Of course. Petitioner Marsh, state your business before the Court."

"I'm here to inquire after a debt," I said, following the script Isolde and I had built word by word on the Cinder Road. "A family matter. Old. I understand the Court keeps records of... outstanding obligations."

"Inquiries cost," the Magistrate said mildly. "Everything costs. That is the first law of this place and the last law of every place, though most people only learn it here. What will you pay for a search of the ledger?"

Isolde answered before I could, smooth as poured oil: "The petitioner offers service. One task, performed within the Court's jurisdiction, terms to be set by the Court upon completion of the inquiry."

"A laborer's bargain." The Magistrate smiled. It transformed his face completely and improved it not at all. "Very well. Names are cheap; labor is cheaper." He drew a fresh sheet toward himself, and the pen moved, and the scratch of it filled the whole cathedral the way a match fills a dark room.

"Give me the name you seek."

I opened my mouth to say it—some invented name, some safe stranger's name we'd prepared—and what came out instead was:

"Dravenmoor."

The word dropped into the cavern like a stone into a well. Every flame in the ceiling leaned toward me. Eleven thousand souls, caged and burning, all of them turning at once, and the light in the cavern shifted, brightened, took on a color I recognized from mirrors I wasn't supposed to look at—from static, from storms, from a village I had leveled with a word I didn't know I was saying.

The Magistrate set down his pen.

For a long moment he simply looked at me, and I felt that look the way you feel weather change. Then he folded his hands atop the ledger and said, gently, almost kindly:

"Now, that is a name I have not heard spoken aloud in this court for thirty years. And yet the ledger tells me, petitioner Marsh—" the smile again, patient as erosion "—that you already knew it would. Tell me: did you truly believe you could walk into my court wearing your grandmother's face and ask after your own bloodline as though it were a stranger's debt?"

Behind me, Isolde had gone very still. Corvin's glow had shrunk to a pinpoint. Somewhere far overhead, a soul began to sing—a thin, wavering note, high and hopeless—and one by one, the others took it up, until the whole cavern rang with the sound of eleven thousand voices that had no throats.

The Magistrate listened to the singing with the expression of a man enjoying distant music. Then he reached beneath his desk and produced a single sheet of paper, blank except for one line written in ink the color of dried blood:

**Wendell Dravenmoor — wardens' line — account active.**

"There is no inquiry to conduct," he said. "Your account has been open since before you could walk. Do sit down, child. We have a great deal to discuss—your inheritance, your exile, and the small matter of the crown that has been waiting thirty years for a head."
