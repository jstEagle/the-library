## 12. An Audience of Chains

They took my name at the door.

Not asked for it—took it, the way a toll-taker takes a coin, the way a pickpocket takes a purse. A clerk-thing with too many knuckles and a voice like wet paper unrolled itself from behind a desk of black stone and said, "State yourself," and when I opened my mouth to lie, Isolde's elbow found my ribs hard enough to rearrange my intentions.

"Petitioner," she said for me. "Come to bargain."

The clerk-thing's eyes—small, wet, numerous in a way I decided not to think about—slid over me once, twice, and then did something worse than linger. They *recognized*. I felt it the way you feel a draft under a door: not the thing itself, only its consequences.

"Wendell Dravenmoor," it said.

The name dropped into the cavern like a stone into a well. Around us, the caged souls dimmed—all of them, hundreds, thousands, the whole cathedral dark of them flickering like candles in a wind that hadn't started yet—and then brightened again, and the sound they made was almost a word.

I had spent ten years practicing how to be nobody. Turns out all it takes is one clerk with too many eyes and your whole performance comes apart like wet paper.

"That's not—" I began.

"It wrote itself," the clerk said mildly. "Names do, here. Yours has been on file rather longer than you've been alive to complain about it." It stamped something. The stamp made no sound, which somehow was worse than thunder. "The Magistrate will hear you now. He hears everyone eventually. That is rather the point of him."

* * *

I want to describe the throne room properly, because I've had ten years of quiet to get good at describing things, and because if I don't put it down in words I'll keep seeing it anyway, so it might as well be accurate.

Imagine a cathedral built by someone who had read about cathedrals but never been inside one—someone working from rumor and grievance. Pillars of basalt rising out of sight. Floors of polished obsidian that showed you your reflection, except the reflections were always half a second behind, as if even your mirror-image hesitated before following you down here. And light: everywhere light, cold and white and sourceless, and every lamp, every sconce, every chandelier was a cage, and in each cage burned a soul.

They weren't suffering, exactly. That would have been simpler. They were *employed*. Each one flickered with the particular rhythm of a person mid-thought, mid-hope, mid-regret, and the combined glow lit the Magistrate's court the way a hundred thousand lives light any empire: adequately, and at terrible cost.

Corvin hovered at my shoulder, and for once he wasn't talking. Ghosts, I was learning, have their own opinions about this place. His edges kept fraying toward the nearest cage and pulling back, fraying and pulling back, like a moth who'd done the math on flames.

"Don't," he said finally, very quietly. "Whatever he offers—and he'll offer, it's what he does, he's not a monster, monsters are honest—don't say yes fast. He counts on fast."

"Noted," I murmured.

"And Wendell? If he offers you me—" Corvin's laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Pretend I'm worth less than I am. Sentiment inflates the price."

The throne itself sat at the far end of the nave, and it was empty, which confused me until I understood that it wasn't. The seat was vacant; the *presence* was not. The Magistrate of Debts stood beside his own throne like a servant awaiting a master who never arrives, and I realized, with a lurch, that the vacancy was the point. A magistrate keeps accounts. A king spends them. He'd left the spending to someone else for a very long time.

He was tall in the way that architecture is tall, grey-robed, and his face—

His face was patient. That's all I can tell you. Every line of it arranged around the expression of a man who has waited out glaciers, who regards your entire mortal urgency as a rounding error. When he looked at me, I felt audited.

"Wendell Dravenmoor," he said. His voice was reasonable. That was the horror of it; I'd expected thunder and got bookkeeping. "You've come a long way to stand in a room you were born owing."

"I don't owe anything," I said. "That's why I live where I live. No debts follow past the veil."

Something moved through his face—not a smile, but the place where a smile would be filed. "No debts follow you, child, because the debt *is* you. You didn't flee the ledger. You're an entry in it, walking around loose, and we have all been terribly polite about it." He gestured, and a lectern rose from the obsidian floor between us, and upon it lay a book.

I knew the book. Of course I knew the book. Its cover was the same blood-dark shade as the map fused to my pack, and it fell open on its own to a page near the front, and there was my name in ink the color of old wounds.

*Wendell Dravenmoor. Entered:* —and the date was the day I was born.

"You see the difficulty," the Magistrate said gently. "You were collateralized before you drew breath. Your family saw to that. Or fled it. With Dravenmoors, the two acts are historically indistinguishable."

"My mother talked about this place," I heard myself say. "Before she vanished. She whispered about it like people whisper about weather coming."

"She would know the forecast," he said. "She grew up under it."

And then he told me.

* * *

Here is what the Magistrate told me, standing in a cathedral of caged souls while my ghost-friend pretended to study the ceiling and my cartographer stood very still in the way she does when she's memorizing a room:

The Undercourt was old when the mountains were young, and it needed wardens. Not jailers—the Magistrate was precise about this, and his precision had the ring of a man who has corrected this misunderstanding many times. Wardens. The storms that break against Mount Vhalen, the tempests that scour the high passes, the wild weather that mortals blame on gods—they are the court's excess, its overflow, its *breath*, and something must carry that breath or it carries itself, badly, into villages and valleys and graveyards full of people who never signed anything.

The first storm-wardens were Dravenmoors.

"They did not steal the power," the Magistrate said. "They were *built* for it, contracted for it, generation upon generation, and in exchange the court sheltered their line, kept their names off other ledgers, granted them long life and honored death. A fair arrangement. Then, some four generations ago, a Dravenmoor looked at her inheritance and called it slavery, and fled upward into the daylight, and her descendants have been running ever since—from a crown, child, not a chain. There is a difference, though I grant it is easier to feel than to explain."

"The village," I said. My voice came out flat. "Ten years ago. I leveled a village."

"You sneezed," he said, "after holding your breath for twenty-three years. The power doesn't die in exile. It ferments. What happened at Aldermoor was not a crime, Wendell. It was a *symptom*. And the treatment"—he spread his hands, grey sleeves falling like settled snow—"is what your ancestors abandoned. The warden-throne. Vacant these four generations. The weather has been unsupervised ever since. You may have noticed."

I thought of the storm I never meant to call. I thought of the map crawling back into my pack, patient as a dog. I thought of my mother's whispers and the static in mirrors and ten years of being so carefully, exhaustingly ordinary.

"If I refuse?"

"Then the solstice seals Corvin Ashgrave's soul into the permanent ledger, the collector collects whoever last touched the map—you, presently—and the storms go on breaking themselves against the world without a hand to gentle them. More Aldermoors. Larger ones. You've seen the arithmetic already; you simply haven't let yourself finish it."

"And if I accept?"

He gestured again, and this time it was a cage that descended—a small one, silver-barred, swinging from nothing on a chain of shadow—and inside it, dim but unmistakably himself, flickered Corvin's soul. The real one. The ghost at my shoulder went rigid.

"Your courier's page, released. Full pardon, struck from the ledger, free to move on to whatever waits past even our jurisdiction." The Magistrate's patient face inclined toward mine. "One soul, ransomed. In exchange for one heir, returned. Justice, Wendell, is mostly just arithmetic conducted slowly enough that no one checks it."

* * *

Isolde found me afterward in a side gallery, sitting on a step of black stone with my head in my hands, doing sums I didn't want finished.

"He's good," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't comfort. She lowered herself onto the step beside me, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, which from Isolde was practically an embrace. "The worst bargains come wrapped in fairness. I've sold enough of them to know the wrapping."

"He's not lying," I said. "That's the thing, Isolde. I checked. The way you check. Everything he said—it's true. The throne's real. The storms need a warden. Corvin's soul is right *there*, in a cage the size of a birdcage, and all I have to do is walk home and sit down."

"Sit down," she repeated. "On a throne in a court that writes babies into its ledger before they can cry. That's what sitting down means here. You know that. You've known it since the tollhouse took your mother's memory and you couldn't even grieve it properly because you couldn't remember what you'd lost." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, in her lap, had curled into fists. "Wendell. I have watched you flinch from your own reflection for the entire length of this road. Are you telling me you believe a crown fixes that?"

"I believe—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "I believe I'm tired of being a symptom. He's offering me a cure."

"No," said a third voice, frayed and bitter and closer than either of us wanted. "He's offering you a leash with better embroidery."

Corvin drifted through the gallery wall, and up close I could see what the throne room hadn't shown me: he was thinner. Thinner, for a ghost, translucent in new places, the cages downstairs pulling at him even from a distance. His own soul, hanging in silver bars a few floors below, and him up here pretending not to hear it calling.

"Pretend I'm worth less," I quoted back at him. "Sentiment inflates the price."

"Yeah, well." He flickered, something like a shrug. "New information. Turns out hearing your own soul rattling around in a birdcage does things to a fellow's pricing strategy." He settled across from us, insubstantial knees drawn up, and for a moment none of us spoke, three bound souls on a staircase in the dark.

"Here's what I know," Corvin said at last. "I was sent to find a Dravenmoor—I'll tell you the rest of that story later, and you're going to hate it, so savor the ignorance. But before all that, I ran messages past this mountain for eleven years, and I met wardens' ghosts, and deserters' ghosts, and one extremely chatty nun who turned out to be a demon, but that's another story. And every single one of them told me the same thing about that throne." He pointed downward, through stone, through souls, through everything. "It fits. That's the trick of it. It fits *perfectly*, the way a key fits a lock, and then you turn."

"Turns what?" Isolde asked.

"You," Corvin said. "It turns you. Slowly. First year, you're still you. Fifth year, you're mostly you. Tenth year, you're the throne wearing a person's memories like a coat, and you catch yourself signing entries into ledgers and calling it mercy." His flicker steadied, and his eyes—dead man's eyes, and still the most alive thing in this whole cavern—fixed on mine. "Your ancestor didn't flee because she was weak, Wendell. She fled because she was the only one strong enough to look at the job description and read the fine print. Don't you dare make her flight mean less by sitting down after all."

The gallery went quiet. Somewhere below us, a thousand caged souls hummed their almost-word, and for the first time I thought I caught it—the shape of what they were trying to say. Not *help*. Not *save us*.

*Remember.*

"I need time," I said.

"You have until the solstice," Corvin said. "Everyone always has until the solstice. It's practically the court's motto."

* * *

They gave me a cell. That's what I'm calling it—a chamber of smooth black stone with a bed, a basin, and no windows, lit by a single caged soul set into the wall, close enough that I could have touched the bars. Close enough that it could watch me.

I lay awake for a long time doing the Magistrate's arithmetic, because that's the trap of fair bargains: they stay fair while you turn them over. One soul freed, honestly freed, permanently. Against that: me, on a throne, gentling storms, sparing future Aldermoors. Weighed honestly, it was a good trade. Weighed honestly, refusing it was pride dressed up as principle, and I had ten years of experience dressing things up.

But then I thought about the ledger open on the lectern. My name, entered the day I was born. Before I'd done anything. Before I'd *been* anything. And I thought: what kind of justice needs to write you into its books before you exist?

What kind of arithmetic starts counting before you draw breath?

The caged soul in the wall flickered its slow, thinking flicker, and I got the strange, certain feeling it was watching me work through it, waiting, hopeful, the way you hope for a stranger.

"Tomorrow," I told it quietly. "I'll give him my answer tomorrow."

The soul flickered—once, twice—and if I didn't know better, I'd have sworn it sounded almost like laughter. Like it had heard that answer before, from better people than me, and knew exactly how the Magistrate counted *tomorrow*.

Down the corridor, boots were approaching. Two sets. Unhurried. Patient as glaciers.

They stopped outside my door, and a voice like wet paper said, through the stone:

"The Magistrate regrets to inform you that tomorrow has been cancelled."
