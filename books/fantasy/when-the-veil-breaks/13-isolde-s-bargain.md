## 13. Isolde's Bargain

There are three kinds of lies a cartographer tells.

The first is the small kind: *the pass is open*, when the pass has been closed for a season and the wolves have gotten comfortable. The second is the professional kind: every map I've ever sold contains at least one deliberate error, because a perfect map makes the buyer independent, and an independent buyer doesn't come back. And the third kind is the kind you tell yourself, which is the only kind with teeth.

I told myself I hadn't betrayed Wendell Dravenmoor.

I'd been telling myself that since Gravenholt, and I was getting very good at it. Practice makes fluent.

* * *

Three weeks before the dying man staggered into the valley — before any of it — I sat in the back room of a counting house in Gravenholt, across a desk from a thing wearing a lawyer's face.

The counting house was real enough on its surface: ledgers, inkwells, clerks scratching away in the front rooms, all of them human as far as anyone could prove. But the back room had no windows, and the candle burned without flickering, and the man across from me had been sitting perfectly still for so long that I'd begun to suspect breathing was something he did out of courtesy rather than necessity.

"Signora Vane," he said. His voice was dry paper. "You have a reputation."

"I have several," I said. "Which one are you buying?"

"The useful one. You deal in doors. Places where the world wears thin." He slid a sheet of vellum across the desk. On it, drawn in my own hand — a copy so exact it made my stomach turn — was the valley. The magic-dead valley. The one place on my charts marked not with a route but with three words: *do not sell this*.

"That's not for sale," I said.

"Everything is for sale. That is rather the point of this establishment." He turned the vellum around and set it down between us like a card played face-up. "We are prepared to offer a trade."

The Undercourt didn't pay in coin. Coin was for mortals who still believed money meant something. They paid in amendments.

He opened a ledger. Black binding, silver clasps, pages the color of old teeth. He turned to a page and let me look at it for exactly as long as it took me to stop breathing.

One line, written in ink that moved. Ink that *itched* to look at, like a word trying to crawl off the page and into your head.

**Vessa Vane. Debt: one soul, held in surety. Term: perpetual.**

My sister's name. My sister, who had been dead to the world for eleven years — not dead dead, though everyone in Ashfall said she was, though we'd buried a coffin full of stones — but walking, or standing, or whatever she did down there, in the dark under Mount Vhalen, her name inked in a book and her self folded up inside it like a letter never sent.

She'd sold herself at nineteen to buy our mother's medicine. She'd signed with a smile, because she was Vessa, because she never believed the fine print applied to her. Eleven years later I'd found the ledger's existence, and spent six years after that finding ways into rooms like this one.

"You want the Dravenmoor route," I said. My voice came out steady. I'm proud of that, in the way you're proud of not screaming when the bone breaks clean instead of messy. "You want me to chart a path out of the valley for the heir of the Undercourt's own bloodline, and deliver them walking along it."

"The courier will handle delivery," the lawyer-thing said. "You need only draw the door and leave it unlocked."

"And if I say no?"

He turned the page. Not to another entry — just turned it, so that Vessa's name slid out of sight, and the darkness where it had been felt louder than anything else in the room. "Then the term remains perpetual. As it would have remained, Signora Vane, whether you had ever walked through our door or not. We are not asking you to damn anyone. We are asking you to choose which names sit in this book." He folded his hands. "You may even tell yourself you had no choice. Most people do. It eases the sleep."

That was the third kind of lie, offered to me wholesale, gift-wrapped.

I took it.

* * *

Here is what I told myself on the road, while Wendell sharpened bees' wax off their hands and Corvin grumbled about the quality of his afterlife and the Cinder Road unspooled ahead of us like a fuse:

*I didn't kill anyone. I drew a line on paper. What they do with the door is the court's business.*

*I didn't know about the collector. I didn't know about the leash. If I'd known they meant to drag Wendell here by the neck—*

Except I had known. Not the details. The shape. Nobody sells the Undercourt a route to a hidden heir out of scholarly interest. You don't spend six years learning what that ledger smells like — hot iron and old honey, the smell of a thing being kept — and then pretend ignorance of what keeping means.

And here is the part I told almost no one, the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone:

It wasn't even a hard choice.

That's the confession. Not that I was tempted, not that I agonized, not that I wrestled through three sleepless nights. I looked at my sister's name and I looked at a stranger's freedom and the arithmetic took four seconds, and I have spent every day since trying to make those four seconds longer, more complicated, more forgivable, than they actually were.

Grief does that. Guilt does that. You commit the sin in a heartbeat and then spend years building a cathedral around it so it looks like deliberation.

* * *

Camp that night was in the lee of a shattered tollhouse, half a day below the mountain's shoulder. Wendell slept badly — they always slept badly now; the storm in them had started turning over in its sleep like a large animal — and Corvin had gone dim and quiet, hovering over the coals in the manner ghosts use to sulk.

I sat up with the maps.

That was the ritual. Every night since Gravenholt I took out my case and reviewed tomorrow's route, and every night I checked the same thing: the little margin mark I'd left at the valley's edge, a cartographer's shorthand meaning *sealed boundary, passage by prior arrangement*. Prior arrangement. As if it were a bridge toll. As if I'd arranged passage for a shipment of wool.

Wendell stirred. "Can't sleep either?"

I should have lied. Lying was my native language; I spoke it better than most people spoke their mother tongues. But the stone in my chest shifted, and what came out was: "Do you ever think about how much of a person is just the roads they've taken?"

Wendell blinked at me across the fire. "You're a cartographer. I assumed you thought about nothing else."

"There's a difference," I said, "between the roads you take and the ones someone draws for you."

They were quiet a moment. Firelight did things to their face — made the exhaustion visible, made the fear under the exhaustion visible. Ten years in a magic-dead valley had taught them to hide everything, but the mountain was already pulling it back out of them, layer by layer, like tide going out.

"Somebody drew mine," Wendell said finally. "The exile. The valley. I used to think it was punishment. Now—" They flexed their hands, and somewhere far above us, thunder answered, soft, like a dog hearing its name. "Now I think it was a fence."

Something in my ribs cracked along a seam I'd been pretending wasn't there.

"Yes," I said. "I expect it was."

I could have said more. This was the moment — I knew it even then, some honest corner of me marking it like a surveyor marks a fault line — this was the moment to say *I sold the route, they sent the courier, you were cargo before you were ever a companion.* Say it while the fire was low and the ghost was sulking and the truth couldn't run anywhere.

Instead I said, "Sleep. Tomorrow's climb is ugly," and watched them roll toward the fire, trusting, and hated myself with a thoroughness that felt almost like industry.

* * *

Because here's the other thing, the thing that keeps the cathedral standing:

Vessa is still down there.

Whatever Wendell decides — throne or no throne, storm or silence — my sister's name stays in that book until someone opens it. And the Magistrate of Debts does not amend ledgers out of charity. He amends them out of leverage. He had shown me the page the way a man shows you the well your child fell into and then mentions, casually, that he owns the rope.

So yes. I delivered Wendell Dravenmoor to the Undercourt. Yes, I drew the door and left it unlocked and watched them walk through it calling me *friend*. And yes, every mile of the Cinder Road, every bandit's blade Wendell's storm turned aside, every night they trusted me to take first watch — all of it happened inside a transaction, and I was the receipt.

But I have also watched them sleep. I've watched them give water to a stranger's mule without being asked. I've watched them flinch from their own power like it was a hand raised against them, and I've watched them use it anyway, twice now, to keep other people breathing. Whatever the court thinks it ordered in its crate, that is not what arrived.

And maybe — the third kind of lie, whispering its sweetest line — maybe that matters. Maybe a person delivered as cargo can arrive as something else. Maybe there's still time to stand in front of them and say the true thing before the court collects.

Maybe. Or maybe that's just the rope talking, and I'm holding it, and I always was.

* * *

Two days from the mountain's root, we stopped at a wayshrine to wait out a squall that wasn't weather. The air had been wrong since dawn — pressure behind the eyes, the taste of struck flint — and Corvin had gone from sulk to agitation, flickering around the shrine like a moth with opinions.

"It's the solstice pull," he said. "Court's winding its clock. Everything bound feels it. Even me." He held up a translucent finger. It trembled. "Especially me. Three days, cartographer. Then the ledger seals and everything in it goes permanent."

"Permanent how?" Wendell asked quietly.

"How do you think?" Corvin's grin was all edges. "Think of it as ink drying. Forever."

Wendell looked at me then. Not accusing — worse, *consulting*. Asking their cartographer, their ally, the woman who deals in doors, what the smart move was. Trusting my expertise.

"We go down fast," I heard myself say. "Tonight. There's a service entrance on the mountain's north flank — old, pre-court, probably unmapped by anything that breathes. I can get us to the archive level without passing a single toll."

Wendell stared. "How do you know the archive level?"

Because I studied the layout for two weeks before I ever met you. Because the lawyer-thing quizzed me on it. Because I can draw the Undercourt from memory the way I draw my own hands.

"Because I deal in doors," I said. "And doors are my whole religion."

Wendell smiled — tired, grateful, real — and went to check the gear.

And Corvin drifted close to me, close enough that his cold poured over my shoulder like water down a collar, and said, very softly, in a voice stripped of every joke:

"I know why the court's route-marks on your charts match its patrol routes, cartographer. I carried documents for forty years. I know what a delivery looks like." A pause. The fire guttered. "Tell them before the mountain does. It goes easier coming from you. Nothing down there comes easy at all."

Then he flickered away to bother Wendell, chattering again, and I stood alone in the wayshrine with the taste of flint in my mouth and my sister's name burning a hole through the inside of my skull, and I understood that I had run out of road to lie along.

Tomorrow we entered the court. Tomorrow, one way or another, the truth would be standing in the room with all three of us.

I only got to choose whether I'd be the one who said it out loud.
