## 15. Storms Remember Their Names

The mountain's throat was a vertical shaft of black stone, wide as a cathedral and twice as cold, and it had one virtue: nothing lived there to be hurt.

I'd chosen it for that reason. Corvin had chosen it because, as he put it, "the acoustics are fantastic," which told me everything about how seriously a dead man took my education.

"Again," he said, drifting three feet above the shaft floor in his grey courier's livery, translucent at the edges like paper held up to a lamp. "And this time, try not to look like you're passing a kidney stone."

"It's called concentration."

"It's called constipation. I've seen both. I died with excellent eyesight." He circled me lazily. "Wendell. Breathe. The storm isn't a thing you fetch from somewhere else. It's already in you. It's been in you since before you could walk. All you're doing is introducing yourself."

Introducing myself. As if the storm were a shy aunt at a funeral instead of the thing that had flattened Ashenmere and every soul in it ten years ago.

I closed my eyes. Found the place behind my sternum where the pressure lived—that familiar knot I'd spent a decade pretending was indigestion—and did what Corvin kept telling me to do. I stopped holding the door shut.

It came the way it always came now: not gradually but all at once, like a memory surfacing, like a name remembered mid-sentence. Static crawled up my arms. My hair lifted. Somewhere above us, deep in the mountain's gullet, thunder answered—a low, rolling *hello* that vibrated in my teeth.

"Good," Corvin said softly, and for once there was no joke riding on top of the word. "Now hold it. Don't spend it. Just... hold it. Feel where it wants to go."

Where it wanted to go was everywhere. That was the problem. The power didn't want a target; it wanted an *audience*. It pressed against the inside of my skin like light through a curtain, and beneath the pressure, faint but unmistakable, came something else—a hum. A chord. Dozens of voices, thin and far away, singing on a note just below hearing.

I let the door slam shut. The static died. The hum cut off like a lopped thread.

"You heard them again," Corvin said. Not a question.

"The souls." I wiped sweat from my brow with a shaking hand. "In the cages. I can hear them when I—when I open up."

"Of course you can. You're a Dravenmoor. Your family built half those cages." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Storm-wardens don't just command weather, Wendell. They're tuning forks. Every bound soul in this court resonates against your bloodline. That song you keep hearing? It's not ambient noise. It's addressed mail."

* * *

We trained for six days in the throat of the mountain while Isolde mapped the court above us and pretended not to watch.

I learned to call lightning down the shaft without burning myself. I learned to pull the wind into a spiral tight enough to crack stone. I learned to stand inside my own tempest and feel it as an extension of my body—the way Isolde said she felt her hands, the way I'd never once in ten years felt anything except afraid.

And every time, the hunger got louder.

That's the part nobody warns you about. Not the exhaustion—I expected exhaustion. It was the *appetite*. Each time I opened the door, closing it got harder. The power didn't want to go back in its box. It wanted more shafts, more storms, more sky. On the fourth day I caught myself, mid-exercise, reaching for the caged souls' song—not to free them, some noble instinct, but to *use* them, to drink their resonance and make the lightning brighter. I caught myself and nearly vomited.

On the fifth day I didn't catch myself until after.

"Tell me about the crown," I said that night. We'd made camp in a side gallery, Isolde asleep by the fire with her maps under her arm even unconscious, Corvin hovering over the coals like a man warming hands he no longer had.

"What about it?"

"When the Magistrate offered it—or when he offers it again, because he will—it's not just a job, is it. Warden-throne. What does wearing it actually do?"

Corvin was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "My grandmother had a saying," he finally said. "*Power is a coat that wears you back.* The warden's crown isn't a hat, Wendell. It's a marriage. The Undercourt's storms are made of debt—every unpaid vow, every broken oath, all of it condenses into weather down here. The crown lets you hold all of it. And all of it holds you right back. The last Dravenmoor who wore it—" he shrugged, ghostly and eloquent, "—well. Your mother used to whisper about that too, didn't she."

"Don't talk about my mother."

"Somebody has to. You won't."

I threw a pebble at him. It passed through his shoulder and clinked against the far wall, and he looked so delighted about it that I almost laughed, and almost laughing felt like losing, so I didn't.

* * *

On the sixth day, Isolde came down the shaft with her arms crossed and her mouth set in the particular line it got when she'd decided to be honest, which historically lasted about four minutes.

"You're getting stronger," she said.

"I'm getting hungrier. Those aren't the same thing."

"They're exactly the same thing, that's my point." She unrolled a fresh map on the stone—she drew maps the way other people prayed, quick and devotional—and tapped a point near the cavern's crown. "Solstice is tomorrow night. The sealing ceremony starts at moonrise. If we're going to move, we move tomorrow, and you need to be able to do one specific thing, not twenty impressive ones."

"One thing."

"One thing." She drew a line with her charcoal, sharp as a blade. "Break cages. Can you?"

I thought about the song. The dozens of voices, the hundreds maybe, threaded through the dark like veins of ore. When I opened the door, they sang *to* me. And part of me—the part that had grown a size every day this week—wanted to answer not with mercy but with appetite.

"I can try."

"Trying gets people killed. Can you?" She looked at me the way she must have looked at bridges she wasn't sure would hold. "Because here's what I've seen these six days, Wendell, and I'll say it plain since plainness is apparently my job: every time you use it, you come back a little less like someone who'd break the cages and a little more like someone who'd *keep* them. So I need to know which one shows up tomorrow."

The worst part was that I couldn't answer her. Because she was right. I could feel it—the erosion, grain by grain, the way water works on limestone. Ten years of being ordinary had kept me small and safe and starving. Six days of being powerful had fed me so well I could feel new rooms building themselves inside me, rooms I hadn't known I wanted, thrones-shaped spaces waiting for furniture.

"I'll show up," I said finally. "Which version, I can't promise."

Isolde studied me for a long moment. Then she rolled up her map and said, "That's the first honest thing anyone's said to me in this entire mountain," and went back up the shaft, and I genuinely could not tell if I'd passed or failed.

* * *

That last night, I went back into the throat alone.

I told myself it was practice. Final calibration. But the truth—which I'm only admitting now, in the privacy of my own head, where even dead couriers can't editorialize—is that I went because I wanted to hear the song.

I opened the door and let the storm fill me and stood in the middle of my own gathering lightning with my arms out, and the voices rose around me like water around a drowning woman. Hundreds of them. Caged souls, ledger-bound, each one a name written in ink that never dries. And they weren't singing randomly. Now that I listened—now that I let myself listen—they were singing *words*.

*Dravenmoor.*

Over and over. *Dravenmoor, Dravenmoor,* a litany worn smooth as river stones, generations of the bound calling the name of the family that chained them, because who else do prisoners pray to but their jailers?

And underneath the litany, one voice, clearer than the rest, close as my own pulse:

*Come home. Come home. Take the crown and come home.*

It sounded like my mother.

It probably wasn't. It was probably the hunger wearing her voice, the way the map had worn Corvin's desperation, the way this whole mountain wore the shape of salvation over the architecture of a trap. The Magistrate's patience made flesh in my own chest cavity.

But oh, it was beautiful. That's the confession. Standing in the storm with the souls singing my name, I understood—for one full, shameless minute—why my ancestors had fled rather than give it up, and why some of them, weaker or wearier, had come back. The belonging I'd craved my whole exile wasn't in the valley. It was *here*, in the thunder, in the litany, in a crown that would fit me perfectly because it had been measured against my skull the day I was born.

I closed the door.

It took longer than it ever had. My hands shook. The static clung to my skin like burrs, reluctant, and as it faded the silence rushed in so complete that I heard my own heart stumble.

"Wendell." Corvin's voice, from the shaft entrance. He'd followed me down; I hadn't noticed, which should have frightened me more than it did. He drifted closer, and his face had none of its usual comedy. "How much of that was you?"

"I don't know," I said. "That's the problem. Tomorrow, when it matters—I don't know how much of me will be left to choose."

He nodded slowly. Then he did something he'd never done: he reached out, and where his hand passed through mine, I felt it anyway—a cold spot, a pressure, a ghost's idea of a grip.

"Then here's what I know," he said. "I sold my soul to get that map to you. Worst plan I ever had, and I once tried to smuggle a wyvern through customs. But I chose it. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever the crown whispers, whatever the Magistrate promises—you were a person who chose, before any of this started. Hold onto that. It's the one thing down here they can't ink into a ledger."

Above us, muffled through miles of stone, the mountain began to ring. Bells, or something pretending to be bells. The solstice eve services, starting early, the whole Undercourt winding itself toward moonrise.

Corvin looked up toward the sound, then back at me.

"Moonrise is in eight hours," he said. "Time to go meet your inheritance."
