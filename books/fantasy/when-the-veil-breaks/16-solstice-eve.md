## 16. Solstice Eve

### Isolde

I had lied to better people than Wendell Dravenmoor, and I had lied to worse, and in neither case had the lying gotten easier. That was the trouble with conscience. It didn't wear smooth with practice. It just got more articulate.

We were in a storeroom off the pilgrims' gallery, three levels above the ledger archive, surrounded by crates of tallow candles stamped with the Undercourt's sigil—a crown made of lightning, which even by the standards of self-important subterranean monarchies was laying it on thick. Wendell sat on an overturned crate sharpening a knife they had no real plan to use. Corvin drifted through the wall and back again, practicing being solid, the way a man practices whistling when he's nervous.

Eight hours to moonrise. Seven and change, now.

I watched Wendell's hands move along the whetstone—steady, patient, wrong—and thought: *they trust me*. Not completely. Wendell wasn't a fool; ten years of exile teaches a person to count exits. But they trusted me the way you trust a rope bridge, because there was no other way across, and because I hadn't dropped anyone yet.

There was still time to be the person who didn't drop anyone.

There was also a ledger in Gravenholt with my sister's name inked into it, and a debt accruing interest at a rate set by things that considered mercy a bookkeeping error, and every hour that passed without me delivering Wendell Dravenmoor to the coronation chamber was an hour the Magistrate's clerks spent deciding what a cartographer's sister weighed against a runaway warden.

I'd run the arithmetic a hundred times. It never came out clean. It only came out whichever way I tilted the scale before I started.

"Wendell," I said.

They looked up. In the candlelight their eyes did that thing I'd been trying not to notice since the Cinder Road—the grey of sky deciding whether to rain.

"I need to show you something," I said. "And then I need to tell you something, and you're going to want to put that knife somewhere creative."

* * *

### Wendell

The map Isolde unrolled across two candle-crates was not the map we'd been following.

That was the first thing I understood. The second thing, arriving a heartbeat later like a delayed clap of thunder, was that she'd drawn it. Every line in her own hand, in her own ink—ordinary ink, honest ink, the kind that stayed where you put it. And every line was wrong compared to what Corvin had been guiding us through, or rather, every line was right, and everything Corvin had shown us had been arranged.

"Say it," Isolde said. Her voice was level the way ice is level. "Go on. I can see you doing the sums."

"The processional route," I said slowly. "The one we've been planning. Through the Warden's Gallery, up past the choir cages—it's a corridor. A funnel." I traced it with one finger. "It doesn't lead to the throne. It leads to the throne's *front door*, where a petitioner kneels, and there are six ways in from above for guards, and none out."

"Now the other route." She tapped the western passage. "Through the cisterns, under the choir. It's unmapped in every court record because I mapped it myself, eleven years ago, before I knew what the maps were for. It comes up inside the ledger room."

I stared at her. "Why are you showing me this?"

"Because in four hours I was going to walk you down the funnel," she said, "and stand at the top of it while they closed the doors behind you, and tell myself it was necessary, and hate myself in a new register I haven't invented yet." She rolled the map tight, but didn't take it back. She held it out to me instead. "So. Before that version of me gets a vote. Here."

Corvin floated through the crate beside me and let out a low, appreciative whistle. "Oh, this is *delicious*. Betrayal, repented of preemptively. Do you know how rare that is? Most people repent after. It's much less useful after."

"Shut up, Corvin," we both said, and for one absurd moment that shared sentence hung in the air between me and Isolde like a plank across a gap.

I took the map. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Somewhere beneath the steadiness, a storm I'd been sitting on for ten years shifted its weight.

"You sold them my route," I said. It wasn't a question. Chapter ten of my life, apparently, had a chapter heading I'd missed. "Weeks ago. Before the courier. The whole journey—the tollhouse, the road, all of it—you were walking a delivery."

"Yes."

"And your price?"

Isolde's jaw worked. When she spoke, each word cost her. "My sister. Maren. She sold a winter to the court when she was nineteen—cold, hungry, stupid with grief over our father. They bought it. Debts accrue, Wendell. Hers has been accruing for twenty years, and last spring the clerks sent me a statement." Her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, and I watched her hate it. "One soul, compounded. Payable in kind or in service. So I serviced."

The storeroom was very quiet. Above us the false bells rang on, patient as rot.

I should have been furious. Part of me was—a hot, small, animal part that wanted to break something. But underneath the anger sat something colder and worse: recognition. Because I knew what it was to be handed an impossible bill and told the payment was yourself. The Undercourt didn't corrupt people. It *invoiced* them.

"Maren's name is on a page in that ledger room," I said. "Along with Corvin's. Along with—" I stopped.

"Along with yours," Isolde said quietly. "Yes. I've seen the index. Dravenmoor. Dated the day you were born."

* * *

### Isolde

Here is what nobody tells you about confessing: the relief is real, and it is also worthless. You feel lighter, and the lightness means nothing, because the weight was never yours alone to carry—it was distributed among everyone you'd deceived, and confession doesn't recall it, it just changes the shipping address.

Wendell was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Corvin, mercifully, found something else to look at.

Then Wendell said, "Show me the cistern route again. Slowly."

Not forgiveness. I hadn't asked for forgiveness. I'd have taken fury, honestly—fury would have been cleaner, easier to carry, easier to deserve. What I got instead was something more frightening: Wendell folding my map open, memorizing it, treating my treachery-turned-loyalty as simply *intelligence*, as material, as one more thing between them and moonrise.

"We go through the cisterns," Wendell said. "We pull Corvin's page. We pull Maren's page, if we can. And then—"

"And then the Magistrate notices his delivery didn't arrive," I said, "and sends everyone he has."

"Everyone he has," Wendell repeated. There was something in their voice I didn't like. Something almost like relief, as if part of them had been waiting all journey for permission to stop holding the storm down.

"Wendell." I kept my voice low. "Whatever you're planning to do when they come—I've seen what you did on the Cinder Road, and that was you *scared*. What happens if you stop being scared? If you let it all the way out, down here, under a mountain full of caged souls?"

They looked at me then, and for a moment I saw straight down into the weather of them, ten years deep.

"Then I guess we find out what I was inherited *for*," they said.

* * *

### Wendell

We moved at the changing of the gallery watch, when the grey-livered clerks rotated and the candles burned lowest. Isolde led. Corvin scouted ahead through walls, reporting back in a whisper that carried anyway, because dead men don't really understand volume.

The cisterns smelled of cold stone and old water and something sweeter underneath—the smell of the choir cages, I realized. Souls don't rot, exactly. They *ferment*. They get richer and stranger the longer they're kept.

At the junction beneath the choir, we heard them singing.

Not the prisoners themselves—something older, a hymn worn into the stone by centuries of repetition, rising up through our boots. Isolde stopped. Even Corvin went quiet, and quiet, for Corvin, was a genuine sacrifice.

"That's the coronation hymn," Isolde breathed. "They're rehearsing it. They rehearse it every solstice eve, whether there's a coronation or not. Hope, maybe. Habit." She glanced at me. "This year there's one."

Above us, through forty feet of rock, the hymn swelled toward its final movement, and my chest did something it had no business doing. The storm in me rose to meet the music like a dog hearing its name. I could feel the cages up there, dozens of them, each one a small sun of stolen personhood, and I could feel how *easy* it would be to reach up and crack them open like eggs. All I'd have to do was stop holding on.

"Hold the map," I said, and my voice came out lower than I meant it to, rumbling faintly with something. "Both of you. Whatever I do in the next hour—if I start sounding like something that isn't me—hold the map. Make me look at it. It's ordinary ink. It's hers. It's the one true thing down here."

Isolde took the map from my pack and gripped it like a rail.

"One more thing," she said. "Before we climb. You said forgive or pretend. I'll take either. But I need you to hear the whole of it first—that I didn't defect tonight because I grew a heart. I defected because I ran the numbers and you were worth more to me free than delivered. I'm not a good person, Wendell. I'm a person who's good *at* this."

The hymn thundered overhead. The storm stirred in my ribs, tasting it.

"Good," I said. "Because I'm not asking you to be good. I'm asking you to hold the map."

And we climbed.

* * *

### Isolde

The last hatch opened onto darkness behind the ledger room's shelving, and the first thing I saw was the index—the great brass-bound catalogue where the court kept its names—and the second thing I saw was the clerk.

He sat at his desk with his grey sleeves rolled, pen scratching, utterly unbothered, as though intruders climbed out of his floor every solstice eve. Perhaps they did. Perhaps that was the point of the cistern route existing at all.

He looked up. His face was pleasant. His eyes were ledger-lines.

"Petitioner Dravenmoor," he said. "And the cartographer. Right on schedule. The Magistrate does admire punctuality." He set down his pen, delicately, the way you'd sheathe a knife. "Though I confess we expected you by the front door."

Behind me, Wendell's breathing had gone slow and deep, the way air goes before weather.

"Corvin's page," Wendell said. "Maren Voss's page. Now."

"Of course," the clerk said pleasantly, and reached beneath his desk, and the lamps guttered, and somewhere far above the bells stopped ringing all at once, which was somehow worse than any alarm—

—as the shelves around us began, softly, patiently, to fill with standing figures in grey livery, unfolding out of the dark like paper.

Wendell stepped forward, and the temperature of the room fell, and I tightened my grip on the map and thought, quite clearly:

*Hold.*
