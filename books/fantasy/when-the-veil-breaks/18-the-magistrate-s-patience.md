## 18. The Magistrate's Patience

I woke to the sound of my own name being read aloud.

Not shouted. Not whispered. *Read*—the way a clerk reads an entry in a ledger, flat and unhurried, as though I were a sum that had finally been tallied. The voice came from everywhere at once, which is the sort of acoustics you only get in places built by something that has never had to raise its voice.

"Wendell Dravenmoor," it said again. "Born under a waning storm. Invoiced at birth. Collected on schedule."

"Collected," I repeated, because my mouth has always run ahead of my judgment. "That's a generous word for whatever happened to me."

The dark around me peeled back like wet paper.

I was standing—had been standing, apparently, for some time—in a chamber that made the cathedral cavern above look like a village chapel's modest attempt at grandeur. Here there were no caged souls burning in their lanterns. Here there were shelves. Rows upon rows of them, climbing into darkness so far above that the ceiling might have been a rumor. And on every shelf, books. Ledgers, thousands of them, their spines stitched in ink that moved when I wasn't looking directly at it. Somewhere in those pages, I knew without being told, every name in the Undercourt sat waiting to be balanced.

Between me and the nearest shelf stood a desk of black stone, and behind the desk stood the Magistrate of Debts.

He looked almost disappointingly ordinary. A tall figure in grey robes that might have been livery once, before they outgrew the concept of service—the same grey as the collector who'd waited at the valley's edge, but older, finer, worn the way a general wears a uniform. His face was the problem. It kept rearranging itself, subtly, like a portrait trying several sitters before settling on none. When he smiled—and he was smiling now—it was with someone else's mouth.

"You're awake," he said. "Good. Patience is easier to practice on the conscious."

"I've heard you're very patient."

"I have waited ten years for this appointment." He gestured, and a chair slid across the floor toward me—not dragged, *offered*, which somehow felt worse. "Sit. This will take some time, and you'll want your legs under you for the parts that hurt."

"That's reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be." He sat opposite me, folding his hands, and for one disorienting moment his face settled into something almost kind—an old clerk's face, tired and reasonable. "Wendell. May I call you Wendell?"

"You may call me whatever gets this over with faster."

"Wendell, then. Do you know why you were exiled to Vhalen's shadow?"

"Because I called a storm that killed forty-one people."

"Because you called a storm," he corrected gently, "that the court spent nine years arranging for you to call. The village was incidental. Tragic, yes—we keep accounts of tragedy too; it's excellent collateral—but incidental. What mattered was the valley. You see, a Dravenmoor cannot be simply *taken*. Your bloodline fled our service six centuries ago, and flight leaves stains in a soul. Stains complicate collection. So we needed you somewhere the veil runs thin and the magic runs dead. Somewhere you would grow small. Contained. Practiced at not-being-what-you-are." He tilted his head. "Somewhere you would be grateful for the walls."

The chair was cold beneath me. Or perhaps the cold was coming from somewhere else entirely.

"The valley," I said slowly. "You're saying the valley was—"

"A pen," he agreed. "A comfortable one. Bees are a nice touch, incidentally. Very pastoral. We didn't arrange those, I'll admit—you did that all on your own, and I confess it charmed me. Even livestock builds its own rituals."

"And the courier. Corvin."

"A leash," the Magistrate said, with the satisfaction of a man laying down a winning card. "Blood-inked, bound to your touch, incapable of resting until it found the hand it was written for. Did you truly believe a dying stranger stumbled through a veil that nothing crosses willingly? Wendell. I chose the word *leash* with care. A leash doesn't drag. A leash lets the animal walk itself, right up to the hand that holds it."

My hands were shaking. I put them in my lap where I could pretend they weren't. "And Isolde."

"Sells routes," he said mildly. "We buy debts. You do the arithmetic; you strike me as someone who resents having it done for you."

I did the arithmetic. Every mile of the Cinder Road. Every tollhouse, every memory paid, every night Corvin's ghost grumbled at the fire while Isolde sharpened her knives and I pretended not to watch her watch the horizon. A delivery route with two marks on it, and one of them had been carrying the other the whole way.

"And the throne," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The exit you mentioned. Let me guess—it's the only one."

"Oh, *now* you're learning." The Magistrate leaned forward, and his face settled at last into a shape I recognized: my mother's. Younger, calmer, the way she'd looked in the one memory I still owned—the one the tollhouse hadn't eaten. "Here is the whole of it, Wendell, laid out plainly because you've earned plainness. Six hundred years ago, the first wardens of this court grew tired of wearing crowns of storm and walked out into the daylight. Their desertion left the throne vacant, and a vacant throne is a wound. The court has been bleeding authority ever since—every debt collected weaker, every seal set thinner, every winter solstice a little less final than the last. The crown cannot be worn by just anyone. It requires the blood that fled it. It requires *you*."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you remain here. Indefinitely. As does your cartographer, whose sister's page sits three shelves above us. As does Corvin, who is already ours and merely awaiting filing. As does—" he opened a ledger that had been waiting on the desk, and turned it toward me with terrible gentleness, "—your own name. Dated the day you were born. You saw it yourself, I'm told, in the archive. You must have wondered."

"I wondered if it was a forgery."

"We don't forge," he said, almost wounded. "Forgery implies uncertainty. We simply write early. Every soul in this room was invoiced long before it understood what it owed. That is not cruelty, Wendell. That is bookkeeping."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—a short, ugly sound that bounced off the shelves and came back sounding smaller. "Bookkeeping. Forty-one people died in Harrowfen because you wanted a *pen*, and you're going to sit there and call it bookkeeping."

"Forty-one people died," the Magistrate said, "because a girl raised on her mother's frightened whispers touched power she didn't understand, in a place we had arranged for her to be alone with it. Yes. Bookkeeping." He closed the ledger. "You may hate me for the framing. Most do, at first. But hatred requires believing things could have gone otherwise, and I promise you—they could not. Not for you. Not ever. That is what I most need you to understand before we proceed."

"Before we proceed," I echoed. "To the coronation."

"To the coronation," he confirmed, rising. "By force, I'm afraid, since you're making the face that suggests persuasion has failed. Though I'd ask you to reconsider one thing first." He came around the desk, and the shadows of the shelves seemed to lean in with him. "You have spent ten years starving. You know it—I watched you know it, every day, in that valley of yours. Every time you passed a mirror and felt the static gather. Every time you buried the wanting under bees and bread and called it contentment. The throne is not a cage, Wendell. The throne is the *meal*. Belonging, purpose, power enough to never be small again—and all we ask in return is that you wear it. That you come home."

"Home," I said. "You murdered my mother."

"We *collected* her," he said. "There's a difference, and someday you'll appreciate it."

Something in my chest went very quiet and very hot, the way the air goes before lightning. The Magistrate noticed—he noticed everything, that was rather his profession—and smiled with my mother's mouth one last time before letting the face fall away into nothing in particular.

"There it is," he said softly. "The inheritance. Feel it gathering. Don't fight it—fighting only makes the crowning rougher on both of us."

Chains came out of the floor.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had left: they didn't rise, they *arrived*, as though the stone had always contained them and had simply been asked, politely, to stop pretending otherwise. They took my wrists, my throat, my ankles, cold as ledger-iron, and they pulled me upright—not dragging me, never dragging, the leash philosophy apparently extending to restraints—and turned me to face the far wall, where the darkness had begun to open like a door.

Through it I could hear singing. Faint, vast, mournful. The caged souls above, feeling something change in the architecture of their prison.

"The coronation proceeds in three movements," the Magistrate said behind me, unhurried, sorting something—I could hear pages turning. "First, the claiming of the bloodline. Second, the acceptance of the crown. Third, the sealing at solstice, which begins tomorrow at dusk and cannot be interrupted by anything short of a miracle." A pause, delicately weighted. "Or an heir who refuses to sit. But we've covered refusal, haven't we. Refusal is how your family ended up here in the first place."

"You'll have to force me onto that throne."

"Yes," he said pleasantly. "That is rather the point of chains."

The first movement began.

It started as warmth at the base of my skull and spread downward like ink dropped in water. I felt it reading me—actually reading me, page by page, every year of the valley, every swallowed scream, every time I'd caught myself watching storms from the window and felt homesick for something that had destroyed me. The storm-calling rose in answer, uncoiling in my chest like it had only been waiting for permission, and for one horrifying half-second it felt *good*. It felt like the valley's dead air had been a held breath and this was the exhale, ten years long.

"That's it," the Magistrate murmured, close behind me now. "The court remembers you, Wendell. Let it."

"Don't," I managed.

"Don't what? Don't give you back what was always yours?" His hands settled on my shoulders—light, almost paternal. "Every cage needs a keeper. Your family understood that once. The throne doesn't corrupt you, child. The throne reveals what was always there. Ask your ghosts. Oh—wait." A soft chuckle. "One of them is already ours."

Corvin. Somewhere above, or beside, or inside the map that had started all of this—was the map even here? Had they taken my pack, or had it crawled here on its own, loyal to the end? I thought of his chattering, his bitterness, the grief he wrapped in jokes like I wrapped mine in silence, and something in me went hard and clean.

Because here is what the Magistrate had missed, in all his patient arithmetic: he had spent ten years teaching me to be small, and six centuries of his court had taught my family to flee, and neither lesson had ever stuck. Not really. The Dravenmoors ran, yes. But we ran *loudly*. Storms remember their names. He'd said so himself, in his way, when he named me and read me and invoiced me.

He thought he was crowning an heir.

He had chained himself to the weather.

The warmth reached my heart and paused there, waiting, and I let it wait, and I counted—one, two, three, the old rhythm from the valley, the beekeeper's patience, the patience he'd accidentally given me along with everything else—and above us, faint but unmistakable, the singing changed key.

The Magistrate's hands stilled on my shoulders.

"What," he said quietly, "did you just do?"

"Honestly?" I said. "I have no idea. But I think it's going to be loud."

Far above, through the opening dark, the first cage cracked.
