## 1. Salt on the Pass

The mule was named Nothing, which Odile considered the most honest name in the Thornfells.

"Nothing," Odile said that morning, scratching the animal's gray ears while the frost on the pack straps crackled like small applause, "today we are nobody going nowhere carrying no thing. As usual."

Nothing flicked one ear and did not dignify this with a response, which was why Odile trusted her more than any person left alive.

The pass above Hollowmere had three roads through it, and Odile took none of them. The Chancery road was for pilgrims and informants; the drover's road was for shepherds who counted strangers twice and reported them once; and the high trail was a rumor kept alive by goats. Odile took the goat track instead — the fourth road, the one that existed only when you refused to look at it directly. Fifteen years of practice had taught Odile that the mountains were like shy animals: they let you pass if you moved as though you had always been part of the scenery. Move like you belong to nothing, and nothing bothers to notice you.

It was good doctrine. It had kept Odile alive long past the point where being alive had stopped feeling like a victory.

The cargo today was salt, twelve stone of it, packed in oilcloth against the wet, because salt was boring and boring was safe. Salt crossed borders without questions. Nobody burned salt. Nobody wrote hymns about salt or declared it an abomination before the Assembly of Pure Speech. Salt was what remained when everything interesting had been boiled out of the world, and Odile had grown fond of it accordingly.

*No name spoken aloud.* That was the first rule, though Odile never said so aloud either, even in private. Rules spoken became habits, habits became signatures, signatures got people hanged. But the rules lived in the body now, worn smooth as river stones: *no name spoken aloud, no blade drawn, no Thornic sung.*

Three rules. Three nails holding down the lid of a coffin Odile had helped dig.

The morning climbed. Below, Hollowmere sat in its bowl of fog with its chimneys just beginning to smoke, a village of perhaps sixty souls who knew Odile only as *the tanner from up-valley*, a solitary sort who paid fair coin, smelled faintly of oak bark (a clever fiction; the smell covered other smells), and asked after no one's business. The villagers were not stupid. They simply understood, in the way rural people always understand, that some neighbors are doors best left shut. They nodded to Odile in the market with the particular courtesy reserved for people who might be dangerous and might merely be sad, and had decided it made no difference which.

Odile paused at the shoulder of the mountain where the track bent around a spine of black rock, and looked back — not at Hollowmere, but eastward, where the peaks stacked themselves against the pale sky like the ribs of something enormous sleeping under snow. Somewhere beyond those ribs, past the border stones and the survey markers and the careful blankness on every map printed since the Veil, there had been a country. It did not have a name anymore. Names could be burned too.

Fifteen years, and still the body turned east in the mornings like a compass needle that hadn't gotten the news.

"Well," Odile told the view, "you're very pretty. You're also dead. One of these things we can discuss."

Nothing snorted. The view said nothing. Odile adjusted the pack straps and went on.

* * *

By midday they had come down into the long fold of country called the Sighs — a valley so named because wind moved through its scree slopes all night making sounds like an enormous old man disappointed in everyone. Odile liked the Sighs. Nothing grew there worth taxing, nothing hid there worth hunting, and the echo distorted voices so thoroughly that even if Odile ever slipped and spoke a word of Thornic, the mountain would garble it beyond recognition. A practical sanctuary. Odile had chosen every campsite for fifteen years with the same care a priest chooses relics, and for much the same reason: because what remained mattered more than what was lost.

The buyer was waiting at the old shepherd's bothy below the second col, as arranged by a system of signs so old that half of them predated the Chancery itself. A cairn rebuilt crooked meant *trade, all clear*. A straight cairn meant *stay away*. An absent cairn meant *run*, and Odile had seen an absent cairn exactly once, and had run, and had later learned that a Chancery resonance-team had passed through the valley two days behind them, burning a farmhouse and the family's entire library, including a cookbook. Especially, someone said afterward, the cookbook, which contained recipes written partly in the old tongue, because grandmothers will insist on writing down the words their own grandmothers used, and the Chancery considered a grandmother's soup recipe to be a threat to imperial order.

Odile thought about the cookbook often. More often than the battles, honestly. The battles had been terrible, but they had at least been *about* something visible. The cookbook was just grief in its everyday clothes.

The buyer was Brannick — a fence with a face like a collapsed barn and the moral flexibility of weather, whom Odile had known for eleven years and trusted precisely as far as a knife can be thrown, which in Brannick's case was quite far. He sat outside the bothy on an overturned trough, whittling a piece of wood into what would eventually be either a spoon or an insult, depending on his mood.

"Tanner," he said, without looking up. This was the name he used. Everyone used it. It was a good name: common enough to be invisible, dull enough to be true.

"Fence."

"You're late."

"I'm scenic. There's a difference." Odile swung down off the mule and began unloading salt with the economy of long habit. "Your cairn was crooked. Very artistic. I nearly wept."

"The boy I pay to build cairns is an artiste. I keep telling him symmetry is for churchmen." Brannick finally looked up, and his eyes — small, quick, and older than the rest of him — did the thing they always did, the quick inventory: *Is she whole? Is she followed? Is anything wrong?* Eleven years of that look, and neither of them had ever once acknowledged it out loud, because acknowledging things out loud was how people got hurt, and Brannick was a professional. "Roads been quiet?"

"Quiet as a library during a book-burning." Odile heard herself say it and winced internally. Old joke. Dead order's joke. Brannick didn't blink; he'd heard worse from her over the years, little splinters of the before-times working loose like shrapnel. You couldn't dig all of it out. You just hoped the pieces came loose somewhere private.

He weighed a bag of salt in one hand, frowned at it professionally, then handed over a purse that clinked with the satisfying honesty of silver. "Fair weight," he lied, and Odile checked it anyway, and they both pretended not to notice, and this too was ritual, older than either of them.

They ate together — hard bread, hard cheese, softer conversation. Brannick talked about wool prices, a feud between two villages over a millrace, the Chancery's new toll station at Grey Ford ("staffed by children, basically, boys with badges and mothers' handwriting still on their letters home"). Odile listened, nodded, gave back nothing that wasn't already public knowledge. This was the dance. Eleven years, same steps.

It was only near the end, as Odile cinched the empty pack frames, that Brannick said, casually, in the voice of a man discussing wool prices:

"Heard the Chancery's posted new notices down in the lowlands. About language."

The wind moved through the scree. Somewhere far off, the Sighs sighed.

"What kind of language?" Odile asked, in the flat tone of a woman asking what kind of weather.

"Oh, you know. The usual kind. The kind that gets people excited." He shaved another curl from his spoon-or-insult. "Resonance teams moving north, they say. Two companies. Something about... contamination. Words turning up where words shouldn't be." He looked at her then, fully, and for one unguarded moment his face was not a fence's face at all but something older and wearier, a face from a battlefield Odile tried very hard never to visit in memory. "You'd know nothing about that, I expect."

"I'm a tanner, Brannick. My vocabulary is *hide*, *brine*, and *that'll cost extra*."

"Mm." He returned to his whittling. Then, quietly, without any of his usual theater: "Watch the passes, Tanner. And watch the weather. Storm coming down from the east, they're saying. Big one. Early for the season."

East again. Everything came from the east lately, or so it felt — the cold, the rumors, the compass-needle ache beneath the breastbone. Odile shouldered the packs.

"I don't mind storms," Odile said. "Storms wash the tracks clean."

"That's what worries me about you," Brannick said cheerfully, and went back to his spoon, and that was the whole of the goodbye.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, a spot Odile had used so many times that the ground had a permanent Odile-shaped depression in it, which was either a comfort or an indictment. Fire was small and screened — heat without light, warmth without testimony. Nothing cropped the thin grass. Above them the stars came out in the fierce, indifferent abundance that only mountains allow, and Odile lay back against the saddle and did the thing she did every night, the thing that was not prayer because prayer was forbidden and besides, the gods of the old country had all been sealed away along with the country, so who was listening?

She recited the rules. Silently. In order. Like a rosary.

*No name spoken aloud. No blade drawn. No Thornic sung.*

And then, because she was tired and the stars were loud and no one was watching except a mule, she allowed herself the third thing, the secret thing, the nightly treason: she thought of one word. Just one. She didn't speak it, didn't shape it with her mouth, didn't let it anywhere near her breath. She only held it in her mind the way you hold a coal in cupped hands on a winter march — carefully, briefly, knowing it could burn you and letting it warm you anyway.

The word meant *home*. In the old tongue it meant more than that — it meant the hearth-and-hill-and-harbor-of-it, the whole woven shape of a place that holds you — but *home* was close enough for a woman lying on frozen ground eating smuggler's bread.

Fifteen years ago there had been nine hundred swordspeakers of the Oathbound Order, and each of them could sing a blade awake, and each of them had sworn in that beautiful doomed language to stand between the Veil and whatever it devoured, and each of them had learned, in the space of one terrible season, what it means to swear an oath to something that stops existing. You cannot keep faith with ash. You can only bury your sword under your hearthstones and walk away and become a tanner with a dull name and a dull trade and a dull, durable grief.

Nine hundred swordspeakers. Odile had stopped counting survivors after the fifth year, when the count stopped changing.

"Tomorrow," she told the stars, "we sell the last of the salt, buy lamp oil, and go home to mend a roof. That is the entire plan. Plans are good. Plans are how you stay invisible."

Nothing, cropping grass nearby, made a low sound that might have been agreement and might have been commentary on the quality of the grass.

Odile slept, as she always slept: lightly, with one hand curled near the knife she never drew.

* * *

The storm caught them two days later, on the descent toward the cabin, and it was a bad one — the kind Brannick had promised, rolling down out of the eastern heights with a sound like a landslide learning to sing. Odile had beaten most of it home, but the last mile was white chaos, sleet driving horizontal, the world reduced to gray and roaring. She walked with her shoulder against Nothing's flank, both of them leaning into the wind like old comrades sharing a burden, and by the time the cabin's dark shape materialized out of the murk, Odile was soaked to the skin and thinking of nothing more ambitious than dry socks.

Which is why it took a moment to register what was wrong.

Smoke. Not from her chimney. From beside it — a small fire sheltered under the eave, fed with split kindling, burning with the neat competence of someone raised around hearths. Someone had taken wood from Odile's own stack. Someone had lit it against her wall.

Odile stopped. Her hand found the knife at her belt — found it, rested on it, did not draw, because *no blade drawn*, even now, especially now, especially soaked and exhausted with the wind covering all sound. Her eyes swept the yard. The woodpile. The lean-to. The trampled snow.

And there, between the smoke and the doorway, sitting on Odile's step as calmly as if the step had been built for her, was a child.

A girl, perhaps eight years old. Barefoot in the storm, though the eave's shelter reached her, though her feet were blue with cold and she did not seem to mind. Dark hair plastered flat by sleet. A smock that might once have been fine cloth, now ruined, hemmed with mud. She was not crying. She was not shivering, or not much. She was watching Odile approach with the grave, unhurried attention of someone waiting for a train they are certain will arrive.

Every instinct Odile owned stood up at once and began shouting contradictory orders. *Stranger. Danger. Child. Trap. Run. Help her. Doors shut, doors stay shut, fifteen years, you fool, you absolute—*

Then the girl opened her mouth, and out of it, into the teeth of the storm, in a voice high and clear and utterly unafraid, came words.

Not lowland speech. Not Chancery cant. Not the shepherd dialect or the toll-road slang or any of the hundred tongues Odile had spent fifteen years wrapping around herself like bandages.

Thornic. Pre-Imperial Thornic, the vowels round as bells, the consonants falling like rain on old stone. Odile's body understood it before her mind did — fifteen years of silence and still the language went straight through her like a blade finding the gap in armor — and the word the girl spoke was simple, a child's word, an offering, the first word anyone says at any door in any country:

"*Welcome,*" said the mute-marked child of a nation that did not exist, in the tongue of a kingdom erased from every map. "*You are expected. Come in from the storm.*"

Behind her, the little fire hissed in the sleet. The wind screamed down off the eastern peaks. And Odile Oyelaran, last of the oath-bound, keeper of three rules, stood in the falling white with her heart hammering out the oldest rhythm she knew, and heard herself think, with terrible clarity:

*The cairn was crooked. The storm came early. Nothing has been right since autumn.*

She had a very bad feeling that the quiet years were over.
