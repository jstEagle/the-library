## 2. The Child at the Threshold

The storm came down the valley the way debt comes to a gambler: suddenly, and with a great deal of noise.

Odile knew it was coming hours before the first thunderclap. The mules had gone quiet in their lean-to, which meant nothing good; animals always knew. Then the wind changed its mind twice in an hour, which in the Thornfells was as reliable an omen as anything written in the almanacs, and by dusk the sky had turned the color of a bruise healing badly.

They spent the evening doing what they always did before weather: checking the shutters, banking the fire, counting what could be counted. Salt in the crock—enough for another month if they were frugal. Dried fish wrapped in oilcloth. The tin of tea that was mostly twigs but pretended otherwise. A small life, inventoried nightly, kept deliberately small.

Fifteen years of small.

Odile had learned the arithmetic of invisibility early. A person who wanted too much got noticed. A person who spoke too much got remembered. So Odile wanted little, spoke less, and in the village below went by *the salt-trader*, no first name, no family name, a nod instead of a greeting, a shrug instead of a story. The villagers found them cold, which suited perfectly. Cold people were forgotten. Forgotten people lived.

It was, all things considered, a comfortable arrangement. Lonely, yes, in the way that an old scar is lonely—a thing you carried everywhere and had learned not to touch—but comfortable. Safe. The word *safe* had once meant something larger to Odile, something with banners and bells and a language full of vowels like open doors, but that meaning had been buried along with better days, and Odile did not dig it up.

Odile dug up nothing anymore.

The storm hit around midnight.

It arrived not gradually but all at once, as though someone had been holding the mountain's wind behind a door and finally let go. Rain came sideways. Thunder rolled down the valley floor like boulders being quarried by an impatient god. The cabin groaned on its stones, and Odile lay awake in the loft listening to it, cataloguing sounds out of fifteen years' habit: wind, water, timber, stone. Nothing else. All fine.

Then, over the storm, they heard something that did not belong to any catalogue.

A knock.

Three knocks. Small ones. Polite, almost—the kind of knock a neighbor gives when they know it's late and they're sorry about it. Which was absurd, because the nearest neighbor was four miles down-valley, and no one sane walked four miles in this. No one insane either. In thirty seconds of this rain a grown man would be soaked through and hypothermic within the hour. Whatever was knocking was either dead already or—

*Don't answer it.*

The thought arrived fully formed, the old cautionary voice, the one that had kept Odile alive when smarter, braver people died. It was a good voice. Odile had built half a life on listening to it.

But the knock came again. Three more. Smaller.

And underneath the wind, faintly, Odile heard something worse than knocking.

Humming.

A melody. Four notes, rising, then falling back to the first. Simple. Childish. The kind of tune a child makes up skipping stones, except that Odile knew it wasn't made up at all, because Odile had heard it before, long ago, sung by women drawing water in a country that no longer existed. It was a watering-song from the terraces above Bellharbor, where the green steps of the hillsides caught the rain and the bell-towers rang the hours in a language that predated every kingdom on the current maps.

Thornic.

Odile sat up so fast they cracked their head on the low beam and swore—in Trade Cant, safely, uselessly—and lay there with stars bursting behind their eyes while the humming continued below, patient, unhurried, utterly indifferent to the storm.

*No,* Odile told themselves. *You imagined it. Fifteen years alone will do that. You dreamed the old songs and now the wind is playing tricks.*

The humming stopped. And a voice said, clearly, through the door, in flawless, unaccented, pre-Imperial Thornic:

"Please. It is very cold."

---

There are fears, and then there are fears.

Odile had faced armed men on the pass roads without quickening their pulse much. They had stood over the graves of their entire order and felt only a numbness like deep snow. But hearing a living voice speak Thornic—that particular music, those particular vowels—went through Odile like a blade finding the gap between armor plates, and what it struck was not courage.

For a long moment Odile simply knelt in the loft, hand pressed flat against the floorboards, breathing.

*Dead,* they reminded themselves. *All of it dead. Sealed. Erased. There is no one left who speaks it. Therefore whoever is at your door is either a ghost, a trap, or a hallucination, and in two of those three cases the correct response is the same.*

Odile took down the iron poker. Not the sword—there was no sword, hadn't been for fifteen years, just a bundle of rusted pieces under the hearthstones that they did not think about, ever—but the poker would do against ghosts, traps, and hallucinations alike. It was a versatile tool. Odile respected it.

They went down the ladder quietly, which took some doing with old knees and a head still ringing, and crossed the main room with the poker held low. Through the shuttered window the storm threw white sheets of rain. The fire had burned down to orange coals, and in that light the cabin looked exactly as it should: small, poor, forgettable. A place where nobody important had ever lived.

"Who's there?" Odile called, in Trade Cant. Loud enough to be heard. Deliberately rough. *Just a smuggler,* the voice said. *Nothing here worth your trouble.*

The reply came in Thornic again, and this time, with the door between them muffling it less, Odile heard how young the voice was. How small.

"I don't know the words you're saying. Grandmother says I must learn new words. Please open the door. My feet hurt."

*Grandmother.* Odile closed their eyes. Every instinct they owned was screaming the same word: *no*. No names, no doors, no Thornic, no entanglement. Fifteen years of discipline distilled into a single syllable.

Their hand lifted the latch anyway.

Some part of Odile noted, distantly, that this was the moment everything ended—the quiet life, the careful arithmetic, the whole architecture of safety—and that part was already composing the epitaph: *Here lies the last oath-swordspeaker, undone by a polite knock.* It would have been funny, under other circumstances. Odile filed the joke away for later, if there was a later.

They opened the door.

The storm poured in. And standing on the threshold, barefoot on wet stone, soaked to the skin and shivering but not, apparently, frightened in the slightest, was a girl of perhaps eight years old.

She was small and thin, with dark hair plastered flat to her skull and brown skin gone gray-blue with cold, lips tinged purple. Her feet were a ruin of scrapes and blanches. She wore a shift of a make Odile didn't recognize—fine weave, odd cut, dyed a green that seemed too vivid for anything human hands had made—and she looked up at Odile with an expression of mild, weary expectation, as though Odile were a door that had taken an unreasonable amount of time to answer.

She said something in Thornic. It took Odile's reeling mind a moment to translate, because the girl's speech was not merely fluent—it was *old*. The phrasing was liturgical, the cadence of formal greeting, the kind of language used in ceremonies and court records, coming out of a mouth missing one of its milk teeth.

*"I have come a long way,"* she said. *"May I warm myself?"*

Odile did not answer. Odile stood in the doorway holding a poker and felt fifteen years of careful burial crack open like river ice in spring.

Because Odile understood her. Perfectly. Effortlessly. The way you understand your own heartbeat. And understanding meant answering, and answering meant speaking, and speaking Thornic was the one law Odile had never broken—not through the hunger years, not through the grief years, not once in fifteen years of dreams that smelled like rain on green terraces.

The girl tilted her head. She looked at the poker. She looked at Odile. And then, with the devastating practicality of children everywhere, she simply walked past them into the cabin.

---

What followed was, Odile reflected later, the least dignified quarter hour of their adult life.

The girl planted herself in front of the hearth with the authority of a duchess claiming a throne, dripping copiously onto the flagstones. Odile, moving as though in a dream, fetched the blanket off the chest—the good blanket, wool, traded for at cost of two weeks' salt-hauling—and dropped it around her shoulders. The girl pulled it tight and began, at last, to shake properly, the violent chattering shiver of a body remembering it had a temperature.

"You should not stand in rain," she informed Odile in Thornic, teeth chattering. "It is bad."

"No," Odile heard themselves say, "it is not."

And there it was. Out loud. Into the room. The first word of Thornic spoken aloud in fifteen years, and it tasted like—Odile searched for the comparison and found it immediately, horribly—like the first sip of water after a long thirst. Sweet. Dangerous. The sort of thing you couldn't unsay.

The girl's eyes widened. For the first time since arriving, she looked genuinely surprised, and something in her face opened like a window.

"You speak Old Words," she said.

"I speak nothing," Odile said automatically, also in Thornic, thereby ruining the statement entirely. They dragged a hand down their face. *Well done. Magnificent denial. Truly the discipline of a master.*

The girl considered this contradiction with the seriousness it deserved, decided it was beneath her, and returned to the fire. Odile busied themselves with survival, because survival was a language they still trusted: they set water to heat, crumbled in the twig-tea, found the heel of a barley loaf and the last of the honey. The girl ate like a small wolf, hunched over the bowl, and between bites she watched Odile with dark, unblinking attention.

"Your house is very small," she observed.

"Yes."

"There are no pictures on the walls."

"No."

"In my grandmother's house there are pictures of everyone we have ever been." She frowned slightly, as though consulting something far away. "That is a lot of pictures."

Odile's hands stilled on the teapot. *Everyone we have ever been.* Ancestor-galleries. That was a custom of the old country, the terraced valleys, the drowned bell-towers—a custom from a nation sealed out of existence before this child was born, described in books that had been burned, in a tongue that had been outlawed, on maps that no longer showed its shape.

"Where is your grandmother now?" Odile asked carefully.

The girl shrugged, a gesture so universally childish that it transcended language entirely. "Far. On the other side of the door."

"What door?"

"The door." She said it the way one says *the sky*, or *the ground*: a fact of the world, requiring no further specification. Then she yawned, enormously, a yawn that seemed to dislocate half her face, and burrowed deeper into the blanket. "I am tired. I will sleep now."

She slept. Instantly, completely, the way children do, curled on Odile's own bedroll with her scraped feet toward the fire, breathing slow and even while the storm hammered the mountains outside.

Odile sat across the room all night and did not sleep at all.

---

Toward the grey hour before dawn, with the rain easing to a steady drum, Odile gave up pretending and let the fear have its full weight.

Here was the truth they'd been dodging all night: the girl was impossible. Her clothes were from nowhere on any trade route Odile knew—and Odile knew them all. Her feet, though torn, were not frostbitten; she had not walked through these mountains in this season, because no one could have. She spoke a dead language with the ease of a native. She had appeared at the loneliest cabin in the loneliest valley in the range, on the worst possible night, humming a watering-song from a drowned country.

None of that was accident. None of it was survivable ignorance. Either the girl had been sent—which raised the question of by whom—or she had *come through* something, which raised questions Odile refused, absolutely refused, to begin asking.

And whichever it was, the Chancery would want to know too. The veil-burners had spent fifteen years making sure Thornic stayed dead. They burned books. They burned records. By rumor—and Odile tried very hard not to listen to rumors—they burned people whose mouths remembered too much. A living, breathing, fluent child would be, to them, the most dangerous object in the world. A seed. Contagion. Proof that erasure had failed.

Odile looked at the sleeping girl—at the rise and fall of the blanket, the smudge of ash on one cheek, the total defenseless trust of her—and felt the two halves of themselves grind against each other like millstones.

The sensible half said: *Take her down-valley tomorrow. Give her to someone else's care. Wash your hands. You survived this long by carrying nothing.*

The other half—the half that had sworn oaths once, in a language now illegal, before an altar now ash—said nothing at all. It just looked at the child, and waited, patient as a buried sword.

Outside, the storm moved east, dragging its thunder behind it. The coals settled. The girl murmured something in her sleep—a fragment of Thornic, soft as birdsong—and rolled over.

And beneath the hearthstones, so faint that Odile might have imagined it, might have invented it out of exhaustion and dread and fifteen years of not-listening, something hummed.

One note. Low. Long.

Like an old dog stirring in its sleep, hearing footsteps it remembered.

Odile sat very still until sunrise, and did not look at the floorboards, and knew—with the certainty of a person reading their own death warrant—that tomorrow they were going to dig.
