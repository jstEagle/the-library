## 3. Words That Predate Kingdoms

By morning the storm had spent itself against the mountains like a drunk against a locked door, and Odile woke with a plan that was really just a refusal wearing a plan's clothes: feed the girl, get her talking about something harmless, and under no circumstances answer in Thornic.

The first part went well. The second part was where things came apart.

Odile set out porridge — barley, goat milk, a spoonful of pine sugar hoarded for occasions that never seemed to arrive — and the child sat at the table as if she'd been sitting there all her life, legs swinging, hands folded around the bowl for warmth before she touched the spoon. She ate with the focused solemnity of someone who understood food as an event.

"Slowly," Odile said in Lowland trade-speech. "You'll make yourself sick."

"I know how to eat," the girl replied, also in trade-speech, and then ruined it completely by adding, in Thornic, *but thank you for worrying.*

Odile put down her own spoon.

"No," they said, to the room at large, to the rafters, to whatever god of bad luck had taken up residence above their hearth. "No."

The girl looked up, milk on her chin, entirely unbothered by being told no by a ceiling. "You said I should speak so you can hear me."

"I said— " Odile stopped. Recalculated. In fifteen years of careful, deliberate, joyless invisibility, they had developed a number of disciplines: never sleep facing the window, never buy from the same stall twice in one season, never hum. But no discipline had prepared them for a child who treated instructions like loose thread and pulled until the whole garment unraveled.

"Trade-speech only," Odile said. "This morning. Please."

"Why?"

Because every word of Thornic you say is a bell rung in a country that was murdered for ringing it. Because there are people whose whole profession is listening for bells. Because I have spent half my life burying that language in the yard behind my own heart, and you dig it up with your mouth open.

"Because I said so," Odile said, which was a coward's answer, and the child accepted it with the magnanimous shrug of someone who had simply filed the question away for later, along with all the others.

* * *

After breakfast, Odile tried reason.

They sat by the banked fire, and they laid it out plainly, the way Brannick laid out prices: this valley was watched; the Chancery kept men in the low towns whose ears were trained like hounds' noses; certain words were not merely forbidden but *hunted*; and if those words were spoken aloud in a pattern the listeners recognized, men would come up the pass with torches and instruments, and what happened after that Odile could not fix or mend.

The girl listened to all of this with grave attention, nodding at intervals, and when Odile finished she said:

"What are Chancery men?"

"They're—" Odile groped for a shape small enough for an eight-year-old. "Think of them as very rude neighbors. Rude neighbors who come into your house and take anything that reminds them of a song they don't like."

"Did they come to *your* house? Where your song used to live?"

And there it was again, that terrible precision, that way she had of walking straight through the middle of a sentence to the thing underneath it. Odile looked at her — the storm-tangled hair, the chapped bare feet now wrapped in wool socks three sizes too large, the eyes that were the particular grey-brown of wet slate, a color Odile knew, a color from home — and felt the old grief turn over in its sleep.

"Yes," Odile said. "Once."

"Then we should be quiet together," the girl said, as if proposing a game. "I'm good at quiet. I was quiet for a long time." A pause, and then, with the artlessness that made her most dangerous: "Before I walked here. It was very long, and I was quiet the whole way, because the door said to be."

"The door," Odile repeated.

"The door said many things. Most of them fell off me when I got cold." She frowned slightly, reaching for something. "But it said *carry the words carefully, they are heavier than they seem.* And it said—" Her face did something complicated, a child's version of an old man's sorrow. "It said *tell the sword.*"

Odile did not ask which sword. There was only one sword that mattered, and it was buried under the hearthstone in pieces, and Odile was not going to think about that today. Today they were going to do science. Badly.

* * *

Here is what Odile actually knew about the language they had sworn off, and it was more than they liked to admit even to themselves.

Thornic was old. Not kingdom-old — kingdom-old languages were common as gravel — but *old*-old, older than the Empire that had absorbed half the world's tongues and spat them back out reshaped, older than the maps. When Odile had been young and oath-bound and insufferably certain, their teacher had made them recite the lineage of the liturgies: how the spoken forms predated the written ones, how the written forms predated the *oaths*, and how the oaths themselves were fragments of something older still, a speech that had been old when the mountains were merely hills with ambitions.

Nobody spoke the oldest layer anymore. By the end — by the last year, the burning year — even the high speakers used the court forms, the polished imperial-adjacent Thornic that the Chancery's scholars catalogued so lovingly before they burned the catalogs. The deep forms survived only inside the oaths themselves, fossilized, the way a fly survives in amber: perfectly preserved, perfectly dead.

So that morning, while the girl stacked firewood with the methodical delight of someone who had apparently never been asked to do a chore in her life and found the concept charming, Odile sat at the table with a scrap of charcoal and did something they had not done in fifteen years.

They wrote down the opening of the Dawn Liturgy.

Their hand shook. Not from fear — though there was some of that, a fine cold vibration under the skin — but from the sheer strangeness of seeing the shapes come off their own fingers. The characters were angular, built on threes, each one a little fence holding a sound. Halfway through the second line Odile realized they were writing from memory of *sound*, not memory of sight; the letters were reconstructing themselves from the words, which meant the words were still alive somewhere in them, which meant—

Which meant nothing had been buried at all. It had only been waiting, patient as a seed.

"You're drawing fences," the girl observed, appearing at their elbow with the silence of her kind.

"It's writing."

"It's old writing." She leaned closer. "It's missing pieces."

"It is not missing— "

She took the charcoal from Odile's hand with the unselfconscious authority of a cat rearranging its territory, and she added two strokes to the third character. Then a fourth line Odile had never seen in any manuscript, any carving, any oath-stone.

"That's not a form I know," Odile said carefully.

"It's how you say it when you mean it," she said. "Your way is how you say it when you're reading. Those aren't the same."

Odile stared at the character. Spoke it aloud before they could stop themselves — the corrected form, the *meant* form — and felt it land somewhere below the ribs like a stone dropped down a well. The sound was the same sound. That was the horror and the wonder of it. Same syllable, same length, same pitch. But it *sat* differently in the air, the way a held note sits differently from a struck one. It had weight. It had direction. It was aimed.

At what?

* * *

Through the afternoon, they tested her. Odile told themselves it was caution — establish exactly what contagion they were harboring, assess the danger — but by midday the pretext had worn thin as old gloves, and both of them knew it. This wasn't triage. This was a starving person eating slowly so as not to weep.

Odile gave her lines of liturgy and asked her to finish them. She finished all of them, but never the way Odile expected.

The Harvest Benediction — Odile recited the opening, the version sung at every autumn festival of their childhood, the version whose ending every Thornic child knew: *and the field remembers the hand.*

"There's another ending," the girl said.

"Every version has that ending. I've heard it sung in four provinces."

"Not the festival version. The real one." She said it, and the words were in the deep register, the meant-register, and they were: *and the field remembers the hand, and the hand remembers the field, and neither lets go.*

"That's—" Odile stopped. Started again. "That's a vow-form. That's what you'd swear to the land if you were bound to work it for life. You've put a farmer's oath into a blessing."

"It was always there," she said, puzzled at Odile's puzzlement. "The short one is what it looks like from far away."

From far away. As if the festivals, the provinces, the entire living practice of a nation had been a distant view of something truer that children still saw up close.

Odile tried the Mourning Verses next, out of something between scholarship and self-harm. These were the verses Odile knew best, because Odile had sung them at more funerals than any person should attend by thirty: the lament for the dead of the border wars, the slow descent through grief toward the final consolation, *the river keeps what it is given.*

The girl listened to Odile's careful, rusty, fifteen-years-buried recitation with her head tilted, like a bird considering whether a scarecrow was worth the effort.

"You learned that one sad," she said finally.

"Everyone learns that one sad."

"No." She shook her head, frustrated, hunting for words in a language that kept offering her too many. "Sad is right. But yours is sad like—" She gestured vaguely. "Like the verse is already dead when you sing it. Like you're singing it at the verse." She took a breath and gave the consolation line in the deep form, and it was not *the river keeps what it is given.*

It was *the river keeps what it is given, and gives it back changed, and the change is also a gift.*

Odile sat very still.

Fifteen years ago, on the worst night of Odile's life, standing in ashfall while the sky over the capital turned the color of a healing wound, Odile had sung that consolation line over comrades who were past needing it. And even then, even with their voice cracking and the Veil coming down over everything they loved, some stubborn scholar-fragment in the back of their mind had thought: *this ending is wrong. It doesn't resolve. It stops too soon. Whoever wrote this down cut something.*

Now they knew what had been cut. Everything that hurt too much to keep. Every promise that the grief was not the end of the transaction.

The Chancery hadn't just erased the country. They had edited it. Burned the endings that pointed forward, kept the ones that closed like doors.

"You look strange," the girl said.

"I'm fine."

"You look like someone told you the last page of a story."

Odile laughed — an actual laugh, startled out of them, rusty as the liturgy. "Something like that."

* * *

By late afternoon the light through the windows had gone gold and low, and Odile had run out of pretenses. They got up, went to the chest at the foot of the bed, and took out the one object they owned that was neither smuggler's goods nor survival gear: a thin wooden teaching board, cracked with age, carved with the first twelve characters of the alphabet in a master's hand. Their teacher's hand. They had looted it from the academy ruins themselves, one night when looting was the only kind of love left available.

They set it on the table between them.

"Tell me what this says," Odile said. "Not the sounds. What it *says*. If you can."

The girl looked at it for a long time. Her finger hovered over the characters without touching, the way one hovers over a sleeping animal.

"It's a lesson," she said slowly. "For someone learning. The letters are the letters, but the order—" Her brow furrowed. "Whoever wrote it hid a word in the order. First letter of the first word, second letter of the second word... it spells..." She looked up. "It spells *the tongue is a lamp.*"

Odile had owned that board for fifteen years. Had carried it through checkpoints sewn into a coat lining. Had slept beside it, fled with it, nearly died twice for it — and had read it, all this time, as a simple primer. Twelve characters, a child's exercise, the alphabet in canonical order.

Except it wasn't canonical order. It had never been canonical order. Their teacher had hidden a message in the sequence, the way teachers hid messages everywhere in those last years, seeding the language with promises too small for censors to notice. *The tongue is a lamp.* A lamp is carried. A lamp is meant to be lit again.

Odile pressed both hands flat on the table and breathed.

"All right," they said. "One more test. The last one."

The girl waited, patient as stone, patient as mountains.

"The Oath of the Veiled Gate," Odile said. "The final oath. The one we swore when—" When the sky burned. When the maps were redrawn. When the last king's name became a crime to pronounce. "There are nine lines. No one alive knows all nine. We swore seven; the last two were lost with the high speakers who wrote them. Do you know them?"

The girl looked at Odile with an expression that was suddenly much older than eight — the expression of someone carrying something fragile across a long distance and finally setting it down.

"Yes," she said. "I know all nine. I've known them since before I had a name for knowing. They're not lost. They're *waiting*. That's different." She folded her small hands. "But I shouldn't say them here."

"Why not?" Odile's voice came out rougher than intended. "There's no one for forty miles. Say them."

"Because the ninth one isn't words yet," she said simply. "It needs the sword. Words that big need somewhere to live, or they just burn the mouth." She glanced toward the hearth — toward the hearthstone, specifically, with a child's unerring instinct for the exact spot where a broken oath-sword lay buried in oilcloth beneath six inches of flagstone — and then looked back at Odile, and smiled, and delivered the sentence that ended Odile's fifteen years of careful, deliberate, joyless invisibility:

"You keep it under the floor. It's been listening to us all day. It's very happy."

Outside, far down the valley, thin as a hair drawn across the dusk, a horn sounded. Once. Paused. Twice.

Chancery signal-calls. Odile knew their grammar the way they knew weather: two notes meant a search party regrouping. Three would mean found.

The girl tilted her head, listening. "What's that?"

"Neighbors," Odile said, already moving — banking the fire, shuttering the lamp, pulling the teaching board back into the chest with hands that were almost steady. "Rude ones. Stay away from the window."

And under the hearthstone, faint but unmistakable, in a voice like a struck tuning fork remembering its pitch, the sword began to hum.
