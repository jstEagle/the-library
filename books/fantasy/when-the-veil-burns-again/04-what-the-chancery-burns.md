## 4. What the Chancery Burns

The valley was called Merrow's Rest on the Chancery maps, which was a lie twice over: there had never been anyone named Merrow, and no one there rested. They farmed goats, argued about fences, and buried their grievances shallow enough to dig up every spring.

Maren Voss stood at the edge of it in the grey light of a wet morning and thought, not for the first time, that the Empire's cartographers should be required to visit the places they named. It might have made them honest. It would certainly have made them miserable, which she considered a fair trade.

"Beautiful country," said Corporal Ilex beside her, who was twenty-three and found everything beautiful that wasn't actively trying to kill him yet.

"It's mud," said Voss.

"Mud *with* mountains, Inquisitor."

She let him have that. There was no point arguing with a man who still thought the world was interesting. She remembered being that man. It had been a good decade, mostly.

Behind her, the rest of the detail was unloading the wagons: two veil-burners in their grey lacquered coats, a resonance-clerk named Pell who carried his instruments like a woman carrying sleeping cats, and six soldiers whose primary function was to look numerous. The Chancery's arithmetic of fear was simple — one inquisitor to think, one clerk to measure, one burner to destroy, and enough boots to make the thinking seem unnecessary.

Their warrant gave them authority over "linguistic contagion," which was a phrase invented by someone in the capital who had never heard a dying language and imagined words spread like fire through dry grass. In practice it meant this: somewhere in the Thornfell valleys, someone had reported hearing speech that predated the Empire. Speech from a country that did not exist. Speech that could not exist, because the country it belonged to had been removed from the world fifteen years ago, carefully, thoroughly, by people like her.

Voss adjusted the collar of her coat against the drizzle and began the walk down into Merrow's Rest.

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Goose, which at least had the virtue of honesty; the goose over the door had been drowned in paint so many times it resembled a lumpy cloud. Its common room smelled of peat smoke, boiled mutton, and the particular despair of beer brewed too close to winter.

Voss took a table near the hearth, ordered nothing, and set the Chancery seal on the wood where everyone could see it. That was usually enough. The seal did most of her work for her — a black circle stamped in lead, heavy as a small sin. People looked at it and began confessing to things she hadn't asked about.

The innkeeper lasted four minutes.

"We're honest folk here," he said, wiping a table that was already cleaner than his conscience. "Whatever you've heard—"

"I haven't heard anything," said Voss pleasantly. "That's why I'm asking."

He wiped harder. "Nobody here speaks anything but Common. Some of the old ones mutter, but that's just teeth falling out."

"Teeth," said Voss, "do not conjugate verbs."

The innkeeper stared at her. Behind him, a woman in a shawl got up very suddenly to check on a stew that was nowhere near done.

Pell set up his resonance-plates in the corner while the soldiers made themselves visible in the doorway. The plates were thin discs of treated slate that hummed when language touched the air — any language, but they were tuned, these, to a frequency the Chancery had spent fifteen years and an obscene amount of money trying to forget. When the old tongue was spoken within earshot of a tuned plate, the slate sang back. Quietly. Like something waking up under a stone.

Voss watched Pell work with the detached affection of long acquaintance. He was a careful technician and a terrible liar, which made him ideal Chancery staff. He believed in the mission the way a hammer believes in nails: completely, and without ever considering what it felt like to be the wall.

"Inquisitor." He beckoned her over. One of the plates was showing a faint ring of discoloration, like breath on cold glass. "Residual trace. Someone's spoken it in this room. Recently. Days, not weeks."

"You're certain it's not dialect? Mountain folk mangle Common beautifully."

"The harmonics are wrong for dialect. This is—" He paused, choosing the word with a technician's care. "This is *root* speech. Pre-Imperial inflection. I'd stake my pension."

"Your pension is safe. Your enthusiasm worries me more."

He blinked at her, uncertain whether he'd been complimented. Voss left him to it and went to question the village properly, which meant going door to door with the seal in her hand and her voice kept at the exact temperature of a well.

* * *

By evening she had learned the following:

That Widow Carrick's late husband had once sung a lullaby "in the old way," though the widow now insisted she'd never heard it and couldn't describe it and would thank the Inquisitor not to look at her like that.

That the miller's boy hummed tunes nobody recognized, but boys hummed nonsense, didn't they, all boys did.

That three separate households had recently burned books. Not banned books — the Chancery's index hadn't reached this valley in years — just books. Household ledgers, a book of herb-lore, one family Bible-equivalent of no particular sect. Burned in their own hearths, in the last month, unprompted.

That last item was the one that interested Voss. Fear travels ahead of the Chancery like weather. People who burned their own books were people who expected to be searched, which meant someone had told them to expect it, which meant word moved through these mountains faster than any official courier.

Someone had warned them.

She noted it in her journal, in the compressed shorthand the Chancery taught its inquisitors — *pre-burn activity, source unknown, likely smuggler network* — and then, because the room was dark and Pell was asleep and no one would ever see, added beneath it in plain script:

*Or someone kind.*

She looked at those two words for a long time before closing the book.

* * *

Here was the thing Maren Voss did not say aloud, had not said aloud in fifteen years, and arranged her life with great precision so as never to have to say:

She remembered the old country.

Not the way the Chancery remembered it — as a threat model, a containment success, a list of suppressed roots and forbidden phonemes. She remembered it the way you remember a person. Green terraces cut into hillsides like stairways for giants. Bell-towers with copper roofs gone soft green with age. A bread she had eaten once, in a market town whose name she was no longer permitted to know, warm enough to burn her fingers, and how she had laughed, and how Odile—

She stopped that thought the way you close a door on a draft.

Fifteen years ago, she and Odile Sandoval had knelt side by side in the rain at Ashfall and sworn the final oath of the swordspeakers' order: to carry the last of the old tongue in silence, to let it die in their keeping rather than let it be used, to bury blade and liturgy together. It had seemed clean, then. Merciful, even. The war was lost. The Veil was coming down whether anyone swore anything or not. All the oath bought was dignity — the dignity of surrendering on purpose.

Then the Chancery had come to the survivors with its offer: serve us, help us make sure it never comes back, and we will call your surrender wisdom instead of defeat.

Odile had walked into the mountains.

Maren had taken the black circle.

Some nights she still wasn't sure which of them had surrendered first.

* * *

The second day brought the checkpoint reports, and with them the machinery proper.

The Chancery maintained, across the whole spine of the Thornfells, a lattice of resonance-stations — little stone huts housing tuned plates and a bored clerk each, listening to the wind for the sound of a dead language. Most stations recorded nothing for months at a stretch. The plates were expensive, the clerks cheaper, and the entire apparatus existed chiefly to justify itself in annual reports.

But the eastern passes had been humming lately. Voss read the dispatches twice at the inn's scarred table while Ilex ate everything not nailed down.

"Three stations report trace harmonics in the past fortnight," Pell said, laying out his copies of the resonance-logs like a fortune-teller with unusually grim cards. "All clustered along the old salt road. And Inquisitor — the traces are getting *stronger*. Not repeated. Stronger. As if whatever's speaking is getting closer to the plates. Or louder."

"Smugglers sing on the road," Voss said. "It's a long walk."

"Not in root speech, they don't." Pell tapped the log. "Look at the decay pattern. Natural speech decays fast — consonants blur, the plate loses the tail of every phrase. These traces hold their shape. Every harmonic intact, end to end. That's not casual speech. That's *liturgical* delivery. Someone speaking it the way it was built to be spoken."

Voss sat back. Outside, the drizzle had thickened into honest rain, drumming on the Drowned Goose's roof like impatient fingers.

Liturgical delivery. Fifteen years ago, she had known a dozen people alive who could speak the old tongue that way — with the weight and cadence the words were forged for, the way you'd ring a bell rather than tap it. She could count the survivors on one hand, and she had personally accounted for several of the fingers.

One of them lived in these mountains. She'd known that for years, the way you know a scar is there without looking at it. The Chancery knew it too, in its slow bureaucratic way; there was a file, thin and dusty, marked *monitor only*, because even the Chancery hesitated to spend blood on a ghost who had kept faith with silence for a decade and a half.

*Monitor only.* Until something changed.

"And the third station?" she asked. "You said three."

Pell hesitated. "The third one's odd. Trace detected two nights ago, single utterance, very brief. But the clerk swears the plate rang *before* the sound arrived. A half-second early. He thinks his equipment's failing."

Voss looked at the log. The notation was there in the clerk's cramped hand: *anomalous lead-time, 0.5 sec, requesting recalibration.*

Equipment didn't fail that way. Plates didn't hear things before they happened. Unless the thing speaking wasn't entirely on this side of the air — unless the words were arriving from somewhere the plates were also tuned to, someplace adjacent, and the slate caught the echo of intent before the voice caught up.

She wrote that down too, then crossed it out, then wrote it again smaller.

"Inquisitor?" Ilex said. "You've got your thinking face on. The one that means bad news for somebody."

"It means bad news for my supper," Voss said, and closed the logs. "We move up-valley tomorrow. There's a cabin past the tree line where a hermit lives who knows more than he says."

"You know him?"

"I knew him," said Voss, "before he was a hermit."

Ilex, wisely, asked nothing further. Pell looked like he wanted to ask everything further, and she fixed him with a look that transferred the desire directly into his instrument case.

* * *

That night she dreamed of Ashfall, as she did perhaps once a season, always the same dream, always ending the same way.

Rain on the broken terraces. Smoke standing up straight in the windless grey. The Veil coming down over the valley like frost forming on glass — not violent, never violent, that was the horror of it, it was *gentle*, it simply made the country behind it stop being somewhere. The bells stopped mid-peal. Not silenced: unwound, as if they had never been cast. And she and Odile kneeling in the mud, saying the last oath, and Odile's hands shaking around the hilt of the shattered sword, and Odile looking up at her afterward with an expression she had spent fifteen years refusing to name, and the words — the actual words of the oath — already beginning to fade from her memory like footprints filling with snow.

In the dream she always tried to hold onto one word. Just one. Any word.

She always woke with her fist closed around nothing.

She lay awake after it this time, listening to the rain, and did the accounting she did after every such night. What the burning buys, she asked herself, using the phrasing of her old instructors. Safety. Containment. The Empire's maps stay simple. No one wakes a sleeping country.

And what does it cost?

A widow denying a lullaby. Families burning their own books out of fear of a knock that hadn't come yet. A clerk hearing bells before they rang. A valley full of people learning, year by year, to speak less.

It buys silence, she thought. We call the silence peace because we're the ones who imposed it.

She turned over. Tomorrow they went up-valley. Tomorrow, if the resonance-traces meant what she feared they meant, she would stand at a hermit's door with the black circle in her hand and ask the person she had once trusted with her life to prove he had kept faith with silence — or lose everything.

And beneath the professional dread, quiet as a coal under ash, another feeling she refused to examine:

Hope. The shameful, treacherous hope that the silence had finally broken. That somewhere in the high passes, the old words were being spoken again — spoken *well*, spoken liturgically, spoken by someone or something that made slate rings sing before sound arrived.

Because if the words were alive again, then the country might be.

And if the country might be, then everything she had burned, and everyone she had burned it for, might have been for nothing at all.

Or — and this was the thought that finally let her sleep, curled around it like a hot stone —

might have been worth exactly nothing, which meant it could be given back.

* * *

They broke camp at first light. The rain had cleared overnight into that hard washed brightness mountain mornings do, as if the sky had been scoured and hung out to dry. Voss rode at the head of the column with the resonance-logs in her satchel and the file marked *monitor only* weighted in her pocket like a stone she couldn't decide to keep or throw.

Halfway up the valley, Pell's portable plate — packed among the baggage, wrapped in felt, supposedly inert — began, very softly, to hum.

Pell nearly fell off the wagon unwrapping it. He held the slate up to the morning air, and Voss watched the discoloration bloom across it in a slow, perfect ring, like a ripple moving outward from a dropped stone.

"Trace," Pell called, puzzled. "Strong trace. But Inquisitor — we're miles from anywhere. There's nothing up here but the hermit's cabin and—"

He stopped. So did she.

The hum held its shape. Liturgical delivery, end to end, every harmonic intact. But it wasn't fading the way speech fades. It was repeating. Rhythmic. Rising and falling.

Like a bell.

Like a bell ringing somewhere high above the tree line, in a language that had no business being heard by anything in this world, calling across the water of some tarn no map bothered to name — and every tuned plate in the Chancery's lattice turning toward it, slow as flowers toward a sun that was supposed to be dead.

Voss closed her hand around the reins and gave the order that would set the next weeks burning:

"Up-valley," she said. "Now. And send word to the eastern stations — full resonance sweep. Whatever's singing, I want to know *where* before nightfall."
