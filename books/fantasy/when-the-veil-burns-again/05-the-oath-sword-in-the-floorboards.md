## 5. The Oath-Sword in the Floorboards

The goat was watching her again.

Odile had named him Tax, on the theory that anything you fed and sheltered and could never be rid of deserved an honest name, and Tax had developed, over the past four days, the fixed stare of a creature who knew exactly what she was about to do and intended to bear witness.

"Don't look at me like that," Odile said, kneeling at the hearth with a pry-bar in one hand and a lantern at her knee. "You eat moss off the north wall. Your opinions are void."

Tax chewed. It was not a supportive sound.

Outside, the morning was doing its best impression of innocence — thin sun on the frost, smoke rising straight from the chimney of the cabin as if this were any other household in Thornfell, keeping warm, minding its own business. The child sat on the doorstep with her knees drawn up, watching Odile through the open door with that same unblinking patience she brought to everything, as though she had crossed out of a dead country into this one and found it mildly disappointing but survivable.

Her name — the name she'd offered, third day, in a voice so quiet Odile had nearly missed it — was Sefa.

Odile had spent three days pretending that changed nothing. Three days of feeding her barley porridge and teaching her the smuggler's sign for *quiet* and *stay* and *if anyone asks, you're my sister's girl from down-valley*, three days of listening to that voice fill her cabin with a language she had buried deeper than any blade.

Three days was apparently her limit for cowardice. She'd always been able to go four at most, even in the war. On the fourth day something in her chest started knocking, and there was no use pretending she hadn't heard it.

Which was why the floorboards were coming up.

* * *

The hearthstone itself was a lie, and a good one. Fifteen years ago, a younger and considerably more frightened Odile had pried it loose, dug down through two feet of earth and ash, wrapped what remained of her life's one great work in oilcloth and sheep's wool, and laid it in the ground like something dead. Then she'd put the stone back, swept the seam with hearth-ash until no eye short of a Chancery truth-glass could have found it, and built forty-one fires on top of it, because the best way to hide a grave is to keep living on it.

She'd told herself it was a grave. She'd almost believed it.

The pry-bar bit under the stone's edge. Odile braced, breathed, and heaved, and the stone came up with a grinding complaint and a breath of cold air from underneath — air that smelled of old iron and older ash and, faintly, absurdly, of rain.

Tax made a low noise in his throat.

"I know," Odile muttered.

She lifted the lantern over the hole. Down in the dark, the oilcloth bundle lay where she'd left it, and even before her fingers closed on it she felt it through the wool — a tremor, fine as a struck tuning-fork, running up through her palm and into the bones of her wrist.

It had never done that before. Not once, not in fifteen years, not when she'd carried it over the pass wrapped in sacking between bolts of salted fish, not when she'd slept with it under her bedroll through two winters, not when grief had gotten so loud some nights that she'd taken it out and held it and begged it, silently, in the old tongue, to give her *anything* — a hum, a warmth, a single syllable of proof that the order she'd been last of wasn't just a story she told herself.

Silence, every time. She'd come to think of it as the sword's final courtesy. *You swore us both quiet,* it seemed to say. *Very well. Quiet we'll be.*

Now, four days after a barefoot child walked out of a snowstorm speaking pre-Imperial Thornic on Odile's own doorstep, the thing in the oilcloth was trembling like a hound that has caught a scent across a river.

"Well," said Odile, to the hole, to the goat, to nobody. "That's inconvenient."

* * *

She carried it to the table rather than unwrapping it in the pit, which felt too much like exhumation, though she supposed that was exactly what it was. The oilcloth came away in stiff folds. The sheep's wool beneath had gone yellow and flat.

The oath-sword lay there in pieces.

It had broken at Ashfall, at the end, when the Veil came down and every oath-bound blade in Thornic hands had shattered at the same instant — not snapped by force but *unmade*, the way a chord unmakes when you lift one finger off the strings. Odile had gathered the pieces from the ice afterward, because you do, because leaving them was unthinkable, though she couldn't then have said why.

Fifteen years later they looked like what they were: a foot and a half of dark steel split lengthwise into three shards, the guard cracked clean through, the grip leather gone hard as bark. A dead thing. She'd shown it to a fence in Lowmark once, drunk enough to be careless, and he'd laughed and said she was carrying scrap with sentimental value.

But now — she turned one shard toward the lantern, and the light ran along the fracture line and did something light should not do. It bent. Just slightly. Just the way water bends over a stone in a stream. Along the broken edge, where the metal had been torn apart at Ashfall, there was a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, and beneath the shimmer the steel was *moving*, a vibration too small to see and too large to ignore, thrumming up through the table, through the floor, through the soles of Odile's boots.

And it was keeping time.

That was the detail that stopped her breath. Not a random shiver — a rhythm. Slow, patient, regular as a sleeper's heart. And Odile knew rhythms the way a smuggler knows passes, and this one she knew better than any, because she'd spoken it ten thousand times in another life:

It was the cadence of the morning oath. The litany-count. The beat at which a swordspeaker's voice was supposed to fall.

Something outside was counting it.

* * *

"Sefa," Odile called, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "Come here a moment. Bring your porridge if you want. Don't bring the goat."

Sefa appeared in the doorway, bowl in hand, and padded across the room. Up close, in proper light, she was perhaps eight, all elbows and solemn eyes, hair the color of wet slate, a fading scratch along one cheek from the storm-brambles. She'd accepted the borrowed boots without comment and the borrowed coat with visible suspicion, as if charity were a foreign custom she was willing to study but not endorse.

She stopped at the table. She looked down at the shattered blade.

Odile watched her face the way she'd learned to watch mountain weather — for the small signs that preceded the large ones. What she saw was recognition, immediate and total, the way you recognize your own house. Sefa's lips parted. Her fingers curled around her bowl a little tighter.

Then she spoke.

Not to Odile. To the sword.

One word. Pre-Imperial Thornic, the deep register, the one that lived below meaning in the place where language stopped being a tool and became a force — Odile hadn't heard it spoken aloud in fifteen years and it went through her chest like a hand through water. The word was *senneth*. *Wake.* Or possibly *remember*. In the old tongue the two had never fully separated.

The sword answered.

There was no other way to describe it. The shards rose — not far, a finger's width, no more — trembling above the wool, and from somewhere inside the broken steel came a sound. Not a ring. Not yet. A hum, low and gathering, the sound of a bell heard through a hillside, of a voice remembered rather than heard, and the hum climbed the table legs and filled the floorboards and set the tin cups on the shelf buzzing against each other like nervous insects.

Tax bolted out the door. Odile could hardly blame him.

"Stop," Odile said, and then, because the old reflexes die hard and the old tongue was already halfway up her throat, "*senneth-dah*, rest—" and clamped her mouth shut on the rest of it, horrified at how easily it had come, how the words had been waiting behind her teeth all these years like soldiers waiting for an order that never came.

Sefa lowered the shards gently back onto the wool with one finger, the way you'd settle a bird. The humming sank, reluctantly, to a mutter. To a pulse. But it didn't stop. That was the thing. Fifteen years of silence, and now it would not stop.

"It knows me," Sefa said. In trade-tongue, oddly. She saved her own language for things that mattered, Odile was learning, and used the flat imperial words like coins she'd been given and didn't trust. "It was listening. Before. Through the ground." She frowned, trying to fit the thought into words. "Everything sealed is still there. Sealed isn't gone. Sealed is *holding its breath*."

"That's a comforting thought," Odile said, "and I don't believe it, and I'm going to go stand outside for a minute."

She did not go stand outside. She stood at the window instead, arms crossed, watching the treeline the way she hadn't watched it since the war — not for wolves, not for tax collectors, but for the specific silhouette of a Chancery rider, or worse, the empty air where a resonance-lattice plate might be turning.

Because that was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, and she was, whatever else, still good at arithmetic.

A humming oath-sword was a relic. Curious, sad, harmless — the kind of thing a fence laughed at. But a humming oath-sword *answering a child who spoke the dead tongue* was a signal. It was a lamp in a window. The Chancery's veil-burners didn't ride the passes looking for people; people were easy, people hid. They rode the passes listening for *language that predates permission*, and everything in Odile's cabin — the child, the sword, the woman standing between them — was suddenly, catastrophically audible.

She had spent fifteen years being no one. Four days. Four days, and she was the loudest thing in the valley.

Behind her, Sefa had picked up one shard of the blade and was turning it in her small hands, studying the shimmering fracture with the frown of a child doing sums.

"You broke it," she observed.

"The country breaking broke it. I just picked up the pieces."

"Why?"

Odile opened her mouth to say *habit*, or *guilt*, or some other small true lie — and found she didn't have one ready. She looked at the shard in the child's hands, at the faint bending light along the wound in the steel, at the pulse still moving through it like blood deciding whether to return to a numb limb.

"Because it wasn't finished," she said finally, and was surprised to find it was true.

Sefa nodded slowly, as though Odile had passed some examination whose questions had never been announced. She set the shard down with the others, aligned it carefully along the fracture line — and the edges *leaned toward each other*, a fraction, a whisper, like two halves of a door remembering they had been one door once.

"They can grow back together," Sefa said. Not asking. Telling. "If someone says the right words over it. Long words. I know some." A pause, and then, with the devastating pragmatism of the very young: "Do you know any, or only the ones for hiding?"

Odile laughed — a short, startled bark of a laugh, the first one in longer than she cared to count — and it hurt on the way out, the way laughter does when it's been stored too long.

"I know all of them, little one," she said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

She worked through the afternoon with the door barred and Tax stationed — voluntarily, smugly — in the corner, rewrapping the shards not in oilcloth this time but in an old campaign cloak, and burying them again, shallower now, under the loose stones behind the cold hearth where her hand could reach them in a heartbeat. If the sword was going to shout, it would at least do it indoors.

By dusk the pulse had settled to something almost bearable — a mutter, a murmur, a heartbeat in the walls. Almost. When Sefa sang herself to sleep on the cot — a fragment of a lullaby, terraces and bells, half-remembered, tuneless, utterly unmistakable — the shards behind the stones answered in kind, soft and low, two voices keeping each other company across a gap of fifteen years.

Odile sat up late with the lantern and did not sleep, and near midnight, through the frost-feathered window, she saw it.

Far down the valley, past the black teeth of the pines, a light moved where no light had any business being. Not a hunter's torch — too steady. Not a smuggler's lantern — too high, riding the ridgeline where only Chancery riders bothered to go. It traveled the old military road with the unhurried certainty of something following a map, and it paused, once, directly opposite her valley, and held there long enough for her heart to stop twice.

Listening.

The shards behind the hearth-stones pulsed once, twice — in time, Odile realized with a cold clarity that felt almost calm, with the rhythm of a sleeping child's breath — and went still.

Down in the dark, the light began to climb.
