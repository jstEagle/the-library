## 6. A Name Is a Debt

The woman with the grey in her braid said the word *bread* wrong again.

Not wrong exactly — she said it the way you say a thing when you learned it from a book that had been dropped in a river. *Braad.* The vowels flattened like dough someone had forgotten to knead. The girl — who had not yet decided whether to give this woman her name, because names were debts and debts were dangerous — chewed her bread and did not correct her. Not yet. Correction was a kind of gift, and gifts were also debts, and the girl was trying very hard to owe this woman nothing, even though the woman had given her soup and a blanket and a place by the fire where the floorboards were warmest, right over whatever was buried beneath them.

She could feel it through her bare feet. She had been barefoot since — since the water? Since the door? Her feet remembered cold stone and then wet grass and then the long terrible road of stones by the river, and now they remembered warmth, and the warmth had a pulse in it, faint as a second heartbeat under the hearth.

*Something is sleeping down there,* she thought. *Something with a voice.*

She kept her mouth shut about it. Keeping your mouth shut was the first rule of being alive, and she had been alive for eight years, or possibly for eighty, depending on how you counted the part before.

* * *

The part before came back to her in pieces, the way a dropped plate comes back: all the pieces present, none of them agreeing to be a plate again.

Green terraces, first. Always the terraces. They climbed the hillsides in steps, each one holding water the color of sky after rain, and old men walked the ridges between them with their hands behind their backs, arguing about weather in voices so loud the arguments could be heard three terraces down. She remembered the smell of the water in the channels — green and mineral, like crushed leaves and coins. She remembered that if you put your ear to an irrigation channel at dawn you could hear the whole valley talking to itself, every farm murmuring its little news downhill.

Then bell-towers. There had been eleven of them, one for each of the — she lost the word there, reached for it, found only a hole shaped like it. Eleven towers, anyway, and they did not all ring at once except on one day a year, and on that day the sound went into your chest and rearranged something, gently, the way a grandmother fixes your collar without asking permission. She could still feel the rearrangement. It sat behind her sternum like a swallowed coal that refused to go out.

And then — the door.

The door was the hardest piece. Every time she reached for it, it slid sideways in her mind like a fish in shallow water. She knew it was made of ash-grey stone. She knew it stood alone, which doors should never do; a door wants a wall, the way a word wants a sentence, and a door standing alone in a field of ice is either a miracle or a wound. She knew there had been words carved around it, and she knew the words, she could recite them, but when she tried to remember *reading* them her memory showed her only white, and cold, and the sensation of being poured from one place into another like water between cups.

She did not know how she had crossed over. This bothered her more than the hunger had, more than the bare feet, more than the storm. You are supposed to know how you arrive somewhere. Arriving without knowing how is just another way of saying *lost*, and she had decided, somewhere on the river road, that she was done being lost. Lost things get found by whoever finds them first, and she had seen who found things first out here: crows, and worse than crows.

So she would choose. That was the trick nobody told you. If you couldn't remember how you got somewhere, you picked a reason, and you held onto it, and eventually the reason grew roots and became true. She had chosen: *I came to find the last person who speaks properly.* And here was the woman, saying *braad*.

It was almost enough to make her laugh, and laughing was expensive, so she didn't.

* * *

"You're staring at my soup like it insulted you," the woman said, in accented Thornic.

"It's good soup," the girl said, which was true, and also a deflection, and the woman's eyebrows said she knew it was a deflection, which meant the woman was smarter than most adults, which was a problem.

"What do I call you?" the woman asked. "I can't keep calling you 'the child.' You'll grow out of it by spring."

The girl looked into her bowl. Here it was, then. The debt question, arriving on schedule, the way bills arrive.

In the old country — *her* country, the one with the terraces, the one that was apparently a secret now, which struck her as the single most ridiculous thing she had ever heard, because how do you lose a whole country, weren't people paying attention — in the old country, names worked differently than they did here. Here, the smugglers on the road had called each other by trade-names, half-words, nothing whole. But at home, a name was a thread. You gave someone your name and you were tied to them until one of you cut it, and cutting hurt both ends. Her mother had explained this while braiding her hair: *"A name is a debt, little bell. Lend it carefully."*

Little bell. That had been the pet name, not the real one. The real one was —

The real one was behind the same white coldness as the door.

She tested the shape of it in her mouth, silently, and found the shape there even when the sound wasn't, the way you can feel a tooth that isn't there anymore. It started with a sound like wind through a doorway. It ended with a sound like a small bell being struck once, politely.

"I'll tell you," she said, "when you say *bread* correctly."

The woman blinked. Then, to the girl's enormous surprise, she laughed — a short, rusty laugh, like a gate opening after years shut, and the girl felt the laugh land somewhere under her ribs next to the swallowed coal, warming it.

"Fair," the woman said. "*Bread.*" She said it correctly. Perfectly. The vowels rose and fell the way they were supposed to, the way the baker's wife in the terraces used to sing it across the square at dawn. Which meant the woman hadn't learned Thornic from a book at all. Which meant the woman had learned it from *a country*. From *before*.

The girl set down her spoon very slowly.

"You were there," she said. "Before the white."

The fire popped. Above the hearth, in the beams, something small moved — a mouse, probably, or the house digesting its own secrets. Under her feet, through the warm floorboards, the buried pulse beat once, twice, and — she would have sworn this on any of the eleven towers — *leaned toward her*, the way a plant leans at light.

The woman's face had gone careful, the way faces do when someone has stepped onto thin ice and is pretending it's a dance floor.

"Eat your soup," the woman said.

"You were there," the girl repeated, "and you *left*, and you buried your voice under the floor, and you think if you don't speak the words the white won't find you." She didn't know how she knew all that. She knew it the way she knew the terraces: completely, and without permission. Some knowledge arrived in her already finished, like the memories had been packed in her luggage by someone else. "But the white doesn't look for words. The white looks for *doors*. And you're sitting on one."

Silence. Long enough that the mouse in the beam gave up and left.

"That's a very large thing to say," the woman said finally, "for someone who won't tell me her name."

"A name is a debt," the girl said, with her mother's exact intonation, and watched the words hit the woman like thrown stones. Watched her flinch. Watched her hand drift, without her seeming to notice, toward the floorboards beside the hearth — toward the pulse, the sleeper, the humming thing under the house.

*Oh,* thought the girl. *You didn't bury a voice. You buried a sword. And it knows my name even though I don't.*

* * *

That night she slept in the loft, under a blanket that smelled of cedar and old smoke, and dreamed in fragments again, but kinder ones.

Green terraces, and an old man on the ridge calling down that the rain was coming from the west and everyone should stop pretending otherwise. Bell-towers, eleven of them, ringing not all at once but in a round, chasing each other across the valley like children playing a game whose rules she'd forgotten. Her mother's hands in her hair. A table with too much food on it, because in her country you set out extra food on the solstice for anyone who might arrive, even strangers, *especially* strangers, because a stranger at your table was luck wearing boots.

And the door. Just for a moment, at the bottom of the dream, the door: ash-grey stone, standing alone in the ice, patient as a held breath. Words around its frame. And beneath the words, worn nearly smooth by centuries of touching hands, a carving she had somehow never noticed before, though she must have seen it, must have touched it herself —

A sword, broken, laid flat. And growing up through the break, a small green shoot.

She woke before dawn with her heart going like a tower-bell and lay listening to the house. Wind at the shutters. The woman below, awake too — she could hear the particular silence of someone lying still and failing to sleep. And under everything, steady as a second dawn, the pulse in the floorboards.

It had changed in the night. She was sure of it. Before, it had hummed the way a struck string fades. Now it hummed the way a string hums when a player's hand is hovering above it, deciding.

The girl sat up in the dark, hugged her knees, and made a decision the way she had taught herself to make decisions: firmly, and without all the facts, because facts were heavy and she had walking to do.

Tomorrow — no, today — she would teach the woman the words. All of them. The liturgies, the lullabies, the rude songs the terrace-workers sang when the overseers were far away, the long rolling oath-words that made the air feel full of held breath. She would pour the language back into this house like water into dry channels, and the channels would remember what they were for.

Because the door was still closed. She felt that in the coal behind her sternum, in the rearranged place the bells had made. Closed, and waiting, and running out of patience the way a held breath runs out.

And something under these floorboards wanted to help open it.

Downstairs, very quietly, the woman began to speak — practicing, thinking herself unheard, testing each Thornic word like a man testing ice. And under the girl's bare feet, the buried thing answered, soft and unmistakable, in the old tongue, in the pre-dawn dark:

It said her name.

The one she hadn't been able to remember.

The one she had never told anyone here.
