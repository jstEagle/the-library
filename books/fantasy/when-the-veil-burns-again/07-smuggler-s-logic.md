## 7. Smuggler's Logic

There were three things Odile had learned in fifteen years of moving other people's goods through other people's mountains, and all three fit neatly on one hand if you were missing two fingers.

First: never take the road you'd want to take.

Second: every checkpoint is a habit wearing a uniform, and habits can be walked around, but only if you know where they sleep.

Third — and this was the one that kept you alive — never let anyone see what you actually love.

Odile stood at the window with a cup of barley tea going cold and thought about the third thing, which had just walked barefoot across the kitchen floor behind them, humming a lullaby in a dead language.

"Stop that," Odile said, without turning around.

The humming stopped. Then started again, quieter, which was worse, because it meant the girl had learned to be sneaky about it, and Odile had not taught her that. Nobody had. It came factory-installed in children, apparently, along with the ability to ask questions that felt like knives wrapped in wool.

"I said stop."

"You said *quiet*," the girl said. "I am being quiet."

"That's not—" Odile turned. The girl, whose name was still a wound in the shape of a name — she'd taken to answering to *Wren*, which was a lie they'd agreed on together and therefore almost honest — sat on the hearthstone with her knees drawn up, watching Odile with the particular patience of someone who has outlasted empires. Or so her eyes suggested. Her stomach, at that moment, suggested porridge.

"Sit down," Odile said. "Eat something. Don't sing."

"I'm not singing. I'm humming."

"Wren."

"Humming is how I think," Wren said, with dignity, and ate her porridge.

Odile drank the cold tea anyway and unrolled the map.

* * *

The map was a smuggler's map, which is to say it was drawn on oilcloth in charcoal and candle-grease, and half of it was lies. Not malicious lies. Useful ones. A good smuggler's map showed three countries: the one the Chancery believed in, the one that existed, and the one that could be gotten away across when the first two stopped agreeing.

Odile traced the middle country with a blunt thumbnail.

South through Thornfell: no. The Salt Pass was watched now — had been watched since the bell in the tarn rang, since Odile's own voice, or the sword's, or the child's, had written their position onto some clerk's resonance-map in letters of fire. There would be a checkpoint at the tollhouse by month's end. There might already be one. Veil-burners traveled fast and apologized slower.

West, over the Shoulder, down to the river towns: possible, except the ferry at Coldbridge had been Chancery-leased for six years, and the ferrymen counted passengers the way misers count coins, twice, aloud, in writing.

North: north was the glacier, and the glacier was winter, and winter was death with excellent posture.

And east—

East was burned already. Odile had watched the eastern passes go up two nights ago from this same window, a smear of orange under the stars like a wound in the sky's coat. Brannick had been east. Brannick, who owed Odile nothing and had nevertheless been holding the road open with his own stubborn carcass while they got clear.

The map didn't say whether Brannick had made it out. Maps never said the important things.

"You're making the face," Wren observed.

"What face."

"The face like the porridge is disappointed in you."

"The porridge *is* disappointing," Odile said. "It's you who makes it." Which wasn't fair, because the girl made it exactly as instructed, and Odile ate it exactly as served, and they both knew this ritual complaint for what it was: a small warm stone they carried between them, polished smooth by daily handling.

Wren leaned over the map, careful not to touch it, the way she was careful with everything that belonged to the world outside her own head. Her finger hovered over the eastern smudge.

"Is he there?" she asked. "The loud man?"

"He's not loud."

"He talks with his whole body. Like a bell."

That was Brannick exactly, and Odile hated how well the description fit, and hated more that the girl had known him four days and already understood him better than Odile had managed in twenty years of trading favors. "He may be there," Odile said. "He knows how to be elsewhere, too. That's the whole trade."

"But you're worried."

"I'm *planning*. Worry is for people with spare time."

"You're doing the worried planning face."

Odile put the tea down before it could become a projectile. "Here's what we know," they said, in the voice they used for inventory, flat and numbered, because numbers were load-bearing. "One: the Chancery has marked us. The bell did that; nothing to be done about it now. Two: they will come up the valley from the south, because that's the only approach their maps believe in, and their maps are wrong in useful ways. Three: whatever we're running toward — and we'll discuss what that is when I've finished being angry about it — it isn't here. So we need a road."

"There's always a road," Wren said. It wasn't childish optimism. It was said the way you'd say *there's always weather*, a fact of the world, mildly inconvenient.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. Roads are like rivers. They have to go somewhere, so they do." She shrugged. "In the old country we said the roads remember where they're going even when the people forget."

Something went cold and bright down Odile's spine. Pre-Imperial Thornic, the scholars had called her speech. Words that predated kingdoms. And here was a scrap of road-lore older than the Veil, offered up like a crust of bread, by an eight-year-old, over bad porridge.

"Say that again," Odile said quietly.

"Which part? The rivers part?"

"*The roads remember.*"

"In the old country," Wren recited, patient as a liturgy, "we said the roads remember where they're going even when the people forget. Grandmother said it. She said it about the pilgrim ways, mostly, when the priests wanted everyone to take the new straight roads and the old ones went wandering up into the hills like drunk shepherds." She paused. "You have a face again."

"It's a different face this time."

"It's the face like you remembered you left the stove on."

Odile looked down at the map. At the charcoal lines, the grease-shined crossings, the little x marks where caches lay buried — and then past all of it, at the fainter thing underneath: the paths those caches had been placed along. Paths Odile had never questioned, because the man who taught them had learned them from a woman who'd learned them from a man before her, back and back, a chain of smugglers each swearing they'd gotten the route from somebody reliable, nobody ever asking why a salt-trail would climb four hundred feet out of its way to pass beneath a shelf of rock shaped like a doorframe.

Why a certain cairn stood at a bend no traveler needed.

Why the old high road — the one every smuggler knew as *the Long Walk*, the one that avoided every checkpoint precisely because no Chancery cartographer had ever managed to find it — ran, in its middle miles, exactly along the line where the drowned kingdom's borders used to sit.

The roads remembered.

They'd been remembering for fifteen years, and Odile had been walking on them like a man sleeping on a fortune sewn into his mattress.

"We don't need to dodge the checkpoints," Odile heard themselves say slowly. "We need the Long Walk."

* * *

There were problems, of course. There are always problems; problems are how a road knows you're serious.

Problem the first: the Long Walk's southern entrance was a day's hike from Brannick's waystation, and nobody entered the Long Walk without Brannick's say-so, because Brannick held the only key worth having, which was knowledge of which section of the trail currently wanted to kill you. The mountain reorganized itself seasonally, landslides and meltwater and one memorable incident involving a bear with opinions. Going in blind was a coin flip with your life as the stake.

Problem the second: reaching Brannick meant crossing thirty miles of country the Chancery was currently setting on fire in alphabetical order.

Problem the third sat on the hearthstone licking porridge off a spoon, and weighed perhaps fifty pounds, and could not be left behind, and could not walk thirty miles fast enough to outrun a company of veil-burners, and hummed dead languages in her sleep loud enough — Odile had checked — to make the buried sword mutter in its grave like a drunk uncle at a funeral.

"So," Odile said to the room in general. The room declined to answer, as rooms do. "So. We need Brannick to come to us. Or we need a miracle. Or—"

"Or you send him a letter," Wren said.

"A letter. To a man who may be ash. Delivered by whom, exactly? The Chancery postal service? They're very prompt. They deliver you directly to the interrogation."

"Not the Chancery." Wren set down the spoon with the gravity of a magistrate. "The tinker."

Odile stared at her. "What tinker?"

"The one who came by. Before the storm. With the pots." She demonstrated: a small jingle of invisible bells, hands shaking. "He bought eggs."

"That was—" Odile cast back. Weeks ago. Months. A tinker had indeed come up the valley, a round grey man with a mule and a cart that sang, and had bought eggs, and had looked at Odile's cabin one beat too long, the way professionals do, and had paid too much for the eggs, which in Odile's experience was either an insult or a message. "That man is not a tinker."

"He had a mule."

"Everyone has a mule. Mules are cheap and opinionated." But Odile was already moving to the loose stone behind the flour bin, because fifteen years of smuggling teaches you to check the drop points even when you're sure they're cold, and sure enough, wedged in the dark, there was a twist of oilcloth that hadn't been there before the storm.

Inside: a strip of birchbark, charred at one end, bearing a message in a hand Odile knew like their own heartbeat.

*E. — Heard singing over the water. Heard bells that aren't bells. Am told the Chancery heard it too. If you're alive and stupid, as usual, come south by the sheep track before the new moon. Bring the cargo. B.*

Odile read it three times. *Bring the cargo.* Brannick didn't know what he was asking. He thought he was asking after contraband, after the usual desperate souls Odile ferried through the mountains, after anything with a price and a story.

He was asking for a child who was the last living key to a sealed nation.

"Well?" Wren demanded.

"Well what?"

"Is it good news? You have the stove face again."

"It's—" Odile folded the bark away. Sat down across from her. Here was where pragmatism earned its keep, and here was where pragmatism, for once in its grey little life, hesitated. Because there was a fourth problem, the real one, and it had been sitting in Odile's chest since the tarn: every plan that involved Wren walking toward safety involved Odile walking toward danger, and the arithmetic of that had changed somewhere in the last few weeks without permission. Fifteen years of careful invisibility, of never loving anything the Chancery could weigh and measure, and now the most visible object in the empire was a small person who stole the blankets.

"If we go," Odile said, "it will be dangerous. Genuinely. People die on these roads in good years. This is a bad year. I need you to understand that, because I won't lie to you about it. Liars are how children get killed."

Wren considered this with terrible seriousness. "I died once already," she said. "A little bit. When the door closed." She looked at her own hands. "It didn't stick."

"And I'd prefer it didn't stick a second time. Or at all. Preferably the not-at-all option."

"Then we should hurry," she said simply, "before it can start sticking," and began gathering her three possessions, which were a spoon, a river-stone, and a moth-eaten scarf, into a bundle with the brisk efficiency of someone who had fled places before and expected to flee places again.

Odile watched her pack and felt the protectiveness pull against the pragmatism like two oxen in opposite harness. The smart move — the smuggler's move, the move that had kept this skin intact for fifteen years — was to send word ahead, scout the road, wait for a clear night, move alone, and collect the girl later from some safe house. The smart move treated her as cargo.

But cargo didn't hum. Cargo didn't polish grief into small jokes about porridge. Cargo didn't look up at you with the eyes of a country that had trusted you once, when you wore a different name and swore a different oath, and had been sealed out of existence for its trouble.

"No," Odile said.

Wren froze mid-bundle. "No what?"

"No leaving you behind while I scout. We move together or not at all. Which means—" Odile pulled the oilcloth map close and began, with a stub of charcoal, drawing a line along the sheep track, south by southwest, avoiding every mark that mattered. "Which means we leave tomorrow, before dawn, and we pray the new moon keeps her mouth shut about it."

* * *

They left in the blue hour, when the mountains are neither day nor night but merely rumor, and the frost crunches like someone eating biscuits in the next room. Odile carried the pack, the reforged-and-still-muttering sword wrapped in sacking across their back like a scandalous relative, and a week's food. Wren carried the spoon, the stone, and the scarf, and insisted this constituted a full load.

The sword had opinions about the journey. It had begun, since the night it spoke her name, to emit a low vibration whenever Wren came within arm's reach, like a cat deciding whether to be affectionate. Odile had taken to hanging it on the far wall. Now, trussed in sacking, it hummed against Odile's spine with each step, a bone-deep thrum that said, with the patience of an object that had waited fifteen years under floorboards: *sooner or later, swordspeaker. Sooner or later.*

"Hush," Odile muttered.

"Talking to your sword again?" Wren asked, skipping over a frozen puddle.

"Sword doesn't talk. Sword hums. There's a difference, legally speaking."

"What's the difference?"

"Talking implies I'd have to listen."

She laughed — the bright, startling laugh that had no business surviving whatever she'd survived — and the sound went up the frozen valley and came back off the walls of Thornfell three times, and Odile flinched at the echo, and then, deliberately, didn't. Let the mountains hear. By tomorrow the mountains would be full of people listening harder than any echo.

The sheep track dropped and climbed, dropped and climbed, threading between the official world and the true one. Twice they passed cairns Odile had never marked on any map and now, with the girl's grandmother's proverb rattling in their skull, regarded with new suspicion — old stones, older than smuggling, placed by hands that remembered something. Once they crossed a stream on stepping-stones worn glassy by feet, and Wren stood a moment on the middle one with her eyes closed, swaying slightly, like a compass needle finding north.

"This way remembers," she reported. "It goes to the door-place eventually. All the old ways do. They're all spokes of one wheel."

"The door-place," Odile said carefully. "The Door of Ash."

Wren opened her eyes. "You know its name?"

"I know its name the way you know a scar. I was there the night it closed." Odile offered a hand across the water. "Come on. The stream doesn't care about our family history."

She took the hand and jumped, light as a bird, and for three steps afterward her fingers stayed curled in theirs, and Odile let them, and told themselves this was a tactical decision regarding footing on ice, which was the kind of lie smugglers told best because it was almost true.

They made the ridge above Brannick's waystation at dusk on the second day, and Odile's heart performed a complicated maneuver involving hope and dread sharing a single narrow staircase.

The waystation stood. Its chimney smoked. Its yard was not full of veiled soldiers. These were all excellent signs.

Its front door stood open, and leaning in the doorway, arms crossed, round grey face split by a grin containing roughly nine teeth and forty years of shared crimes, stood Brannick — alive, unburned, and visibly furious about it.

"You took your time," he bellowed, in a voice that could be heard in three counties and had probably been counted by the Chancery as a weapon. "Get down here before something sees you. And bring my cargo." His grin faltered as his gaze found the small figure beside Odile on the track. He squinted. He squinted harder.

"Brannick," Odile said, descending, "before you say anything—"

"That's not cargo," Brannick said. "Cargo doesn't skip."

"She does that."

"Cargo," Brannick repeated, with the dawning horror of a man watching his comfortable criminal worldview develop a crack, "doesn't *wave*."

Wren waved.

Brannick looked at Odile for a long moment, and whatever he saw in their face — the thing Odile had spent fifteen years never letting anyone see — made him sigh like a landslide starting.

"Oh, you great soft idiot," he said fondly. "Get inside. Both of you. We need to talk about the road." He stepped back to let them pass, and then, quietly, in a register Odile had heard him use maybe five times in twenty years, he added:

"And you'd best talk fast, swordspeaker. Because there's a lady asking after you down in the valley. Grey coat. Chancery boots. Carries herself like she knew you before you were nobody." He scratched his jaw. "Called herself Voss."
