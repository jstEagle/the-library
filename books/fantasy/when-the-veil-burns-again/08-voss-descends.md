## 8. Voss Descends

The valley had three names and none of them mattered.

The Chancery called it Survey District Nine-And-Twenty. The tax rolls called it Hollowmere. The people who lived there—sheep-farmers, a miller with wet lungs, a woman who sold eels out of a barrel by the bridge—called it nothing at all, because naming things invited attention, and attention in the Thornfells was like weather: it came when it pleased and ruined what it touched.

Maren Voss rode down into it on a gray mare with Chancery tack and a Chancery seal on her breast, and she watched the valley watch her back. Doors closed along the lane in a slow wave, one after another, like a held breath traveling downhill.

"Cheerful place," said her clerk, Tam Wicke, a round young man who had not yet learned that enthusiasm was a symptom of early death in this service.

"They're not unfriendly," Maren said. "They're careful. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. Unfriendly people slam doors. Careful people close them quietly and put the kettle on, so that when you knock they can say they were just having tea and knew nothing about anything." She swung down from the saddle in front of the first house, an inn whose sign had been repainted so many times its name was now a gray rectangle with a hook where letters used to hang. "Watch how it's done, Wicke. You'll want to know, if you survive me."

Inside, the innkeeper—a broad man with flour to his elbows—was indeed putting a kettle on. He did it without being asked, which told her everything.

"Inquisitor," he said, bowing the exact correct amount. Too deep would be mockery; too shallow, contempt. He'd done this before, or his father had, or his grandmother. Valleys like this remembered how to receive the Chancery the way riverbeds remembered floods.

"Mistress Voss," she corrected, out of old habit. Inquisitor made people stupid with fear, and stupid people lied badly. "I'm looking for a smuggler. Runs the high passes. Salt, mostly. Maybe other cargo lately."

The innkeeper poured tea for four, though there were only two of them, and set the extra cups upside down. A message or a habit; with mountain folk you could never tell, and often neither could they.

"Don't know him," he said.

"Of course you don't." She drank. The tea was terrible, stewed to leather, and she drank all of it, because refusing hospitality was a question and she wasn't asking questions yet. "I'm told there was a storm three nights past. A bad one. Trees down on the low road."

"There was a storm," the innkeeper agreed.

"And no one traveled in it."

"No one travels in storms here. We're not fools."

"No," Maren said pleasantly. "You're not. Fools don't last long in the Thornfells, do they? Fools sing songs. Fools keep words alive that ought to have burned fifteen years ago." She set down the cup. "Fools take in stray children."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked and then went very still, the way floorboards do when someone on them has stopped breathing.

"I don't know anything about any child," the innkeeper said.

"No. I believe you. That's the tragedy of this valley, innkeeper—I believe every word anyone says to me, and I still find out everything." She stood, dropped coins on the table—too many, deliberately, so the overpayment would sit in his chest like a stone—and smiled her Chancery smile, the one she'd practiced in mirrors until it stopped meaning anything. "Thank you for the tea."

Outside, Wicke was scribbling. "He lied about the child."

"He didn't lie about the child. He told us exactly where the child is by telling us nothing at all. Note the village. Note who wouldn't meet my eyes: the miller, the eel-woman, the boy sweeping the step who swept the same leaf eleven times." She mounted. "Fear draws maps, Wicke. You just have to read the country instead of the roads."

* * *

The cabin sat above the tree line where the smuggler's path forked toward the salt caves, and it was the loneliest habitation Maren had seen in fifteen years of lonely habitations. Stone walls, turf roof, a woodpile stacked with the fussy precision of someone who expected to die alone and wanted the corpse found tidy. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin, disciplined line, as if even the fire had been sworn to silence.

She dismounted a hundred paces out and walked the rest, because horses left prints and so did she, but hers she could choose.

The ground around the cabin was scoured by wind and rain—the storm three nights past had been a monster, the kind that rearranged valleys—but wind is a lazy cleaner. It takes the middle of things and forgets the edges. Maren circled wide, hands clasped behind her back like a woman admiring scenery, reading the margins.

Here: the ash pit, banked and cold. Here: a snare line for hares, freshly reset. Here, half-sheltered under the lip of the door's stone threshold, where the wind curled and gave up—

A bootprint.

Small. A child's boot, worn through at the outer heel the way boots wear when their owner walks on the inside of the foot, the way children walk when they are carrying something heavy that isn't visible. The print was pressed deep, deeper than a light child should press, as though the small foot had come down with the whole weight of a long journey behind it. Rain had softened the edges but not erased them. Two days old, maybe less.

Maren crouched beside it for a long time.

She thought about the report she would write tonight. *Possible unregistered minor at the residence of the subject known locally as* —and here the report would falter, because the Chancery's files listed the occupant as *no fixed identity, presumed deceased, file closed by order of the Third Veil Commission.* Fifteen years ago the Chancery had written Odile Sandoval off the world the same way it had written off a kingdom, and both entries had been lies, and Maren had signed one of them herself.

She took a casting kit from her satchel and then, after a moment, put it away. Plaster preserved evidence. Preserved evidence required action, and action meant veil-burners, and veil-burners meant—

"You're standing in my yard measuring nothing."

The voice came from the cabin door, and it was lower than Maren remembered, roughened by years of disuse or dissembling, but the cadence was unchanged: flat, unhurried, each word placed like a stone in a wall. Odile stood in the doorway with a fish knife in one hand and a look of profound domestic annoyance, as though the Chancery were a salesmanno one had ordered.

"You've aged," Maren said.

"You haven't. That's obscene. Don't they feed you down there, or is it something worse?" Odile leaned on the doorframe. Up close, the years showed anyway—not in lines but in stillness. The person Maren had known had been all motion, quick hands, quicker tongue. This version had learned economy. This version had practiced being invisible until invisibility became a body.

"The Chancery sent me to survey linguistic contamination in the district," Maren said.

"Linguistic contamination." Odile tasted the phrase and spat it out. "Fifteen years, and they've gotten worse with words. What does that even mean? Someone taught a goat to swear?"

"It means words that shouldn't exist anymore."

"Most words shouldn't exist anymore. Have you heard what merchants say about tolls?"

"Wick." Maren kept her voice level. "Have you heard it spoken."

And there it was—the pause. Not guilt. Guilt would have been easy, and Odile had never once in their life been easy. The pause was longer than denial and shorter than confession and full of something Maren couldn't catalogue, and it lasted exactly as long as a person deciding whether to protect a secret or protect the person asking.

"What child?" Odile said.

"I didn't say child."

"You said it with your face. Your face has always been illiterate, Mare, but I can read enough of it to know you came up the mountain expecting one."

Behind Maren's ribs, something old turned over. *Mare.* No one had called her that since Ashfall. No one alive had the right, except this exile with a fish knife, and hearing it felt like pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It did. It always did.

"I'm going to ask you plainly," Maren said, "because we're old friends, and because plainness is a courtesy I extend to very few people. If there is a child in that house speaking Thornic—if there is so much as a nursery rhyme in the old tongue within a league of this door—then what comes next won't be me. It will be a burn company out of Carhast with resonance-lanterns and orders I can't countermand twice. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand you're telling me while standing alone on my doorstep instead of reporting it," Odile said. "That's the interesting part."

"That's the part I'll deny under oath."

"Then we're both liars. Welcome home, shield-sister." Odile's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "There's no child here. There's a goat that swears, if you want to make an arrest. And there's tea, if you want to waste your own time instead of mine."

Maren looked past them, into the dim interior: a hearth, a table, two bowls drying on the rack. Two bowls. One large, one small, chipped at the rim.

"I'll take the tea another time," she said, and turned, and made herself walk down the hill at a surveyor's pace, unhurried, taking notes on geology that didn't interest her, while between her shoulder blades she felt the weight of the oldest gaze she knew.

In her report that night she wrote: *No evidence of unauthorized minors. Subject cooperative, if abrasive. Recommend continued passive observation.*

It was the second lie she'd ever signed for Odile Sandoval.

The first had been much larger.

* * *

Ashfall. Fifteen years ago, and the sky the color of a healing wound.

The Veil had gone up at dawn—a soundless unfolding, like the world blinking—and where the kingdom of Thorn had been there was now only horizon, clean and empty, as though the land itself had been a page torn out. The armies of the Chancery hadn't needed to fight. You cannot besiege a country that no longer occupies space. You can only stand at the edge of the absence and feel the wind come off it, smelling faintly of green terraces and bell-towers, and go mad by degrees.

Maren and Odile had stood at the edge together, in the ash that drifted down from a sky trying to remember what it had covered.

"Sword," Odile had said. They'd drawn it—the oath-sword, the blade that bound speaker to vow—and held it flat across both palms between them, the old way, the way it was done when there was no order left to swear to except the two of you.

"The words," Maren said. Her voice had already been breaking. "Say the words, Odile. Say them before they're gone."

So Odile had spoken Thornic into the dying air—three syllables, five, seven, the oath-forms rising like birds startled from a field—and the sword had drunk them, humming, glowing faintly blue along the fuller, the last living thing in a dead language. And Maren had repeated them after, clumsy-tongued, her Chancery-trained accent mangling vowels that predated kingdoms, and the sword had accepted her anyway, because oaths care about the heart's grammar, not the mouth's.

"*By the door and the bell,*" Odile had said. "*By the salt and the snow. Whatever burns, we carry it. Whoever falls, we follow. Until the Veil burns again—or until we do.*"

"Until the Veil burns again," Maren had echoed, "or until we do."

The blade sang once, softly, sealing it. Then Odile had driven it into the earth, and broken it there—one clean stroke of a whetstone hammer, snapping the oath-sword at the crossguard, because a weapon that powerful could not be allowed to fall into Chancery hands, and a broken oath could hide where a whole one could not. They'd buried the halves beneath a hearth in a cabin above the tree line, in a valley with three names and none that mattered.

"Why break it?" Maren had asked, watching the earth close over the pieces. "Why not keep it whole and hidden?"

"Because I mean to keep the oath," Odile had said. "And I don't trust myself to do it holding the proof."

Now, fifteen years later, Maren sat in her quarters at the Carhast way-station with her report finished, sealed, lying to her superiors in her best handwriting, and she took out the one object she carried against regulations: a strip of parchment on which she had copied, in her ugly Chancery script, the seven syllables of the final oath, transcribed that day in the ash. She had never spoken them aloud. Speaking them would register on instruments. But she read them, the way exiles read letters from countries that no longer existed.

*Whatever burns, we carry it.*

She had assumed the vow referred to the language. The words, the songs, the liturgies—the cargo of a murdered nation, to be smuggled through the years in the hold of memory. She had spent fifteen years assuming that.

But the child's bootprint under the threshold had been pressed deep, deep as a long journey, and Odile's face when asked had held something Maren had never seen there before, not even at Ashfall:

Terror. Not of the Chancery. Of something arriving.

Maren dipped her pen and added a single line to her private journal, beneath the syllables:

*If the old country isn't dead but sealed—and if something has found a key—then what did we swear to carry? The words? Or the door?*

Far to the north, in a drowned tarn below the glaciers, a bell that had waited fifteen years beneath black water shifted slightly on its rope.

It was almost ready to ring.
