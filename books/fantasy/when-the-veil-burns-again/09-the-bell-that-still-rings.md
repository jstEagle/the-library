## 9. The Bell That Still Rings

The child woke him before dawn, tugging his sleeve with the particular gravity she reserved for things that mattered, which was nearly everything.

"It's singing," she said in Thornic. "The water. It's been singing all night."

Odile had learned, over the past weeks, to translate her Thornic into sense before panic. *Singing water* ranked somewhere below *the fire is talking* and above *I dreamed about the door again*. He put a log on the coals and said, in careful Common, "Water sings when wind moves it. Go back to bed."

She didn't go back to bed. She never went back to bed. She stood there in her borrowed wool socks—two sizes too big, rolled at the ankle—and waited with the patience of someone who had once watched a whole country be patient and knew how it ended.

"The tarn," she said, switching to Common because she was kind that way. "Above the scree. The one shaped like a cupped hand. It's ringing, Odile. Not with wind." She pressed her small fist against her own sternum. "Here. It rings here."

And that, unfortunately, was the exact phrase he'd been dreading for six days, ever since the reforged sword had begun humming on its peg whenever she laughed.

He dressed without arguing. Arguing with her was like arguing with weather; you got wet either way.

* * *

They climbed out of the valley while the stars were still sharp, following the smuggler's track until it gave up and became goat-thought and then became nothing at all, just scree and lichen and the thin insistence of altitude. Odile carried the sword wrapped in oilcloth across his back, which was against every rule he'd lived by for fifteen years—*no blade drawn, no name spoken, no word sung*—but the rules had started dissolving the day she arrived, and he'd stopped pretending otherwise around the time he caught himself humming a Thornic cradle-song while gutting fish.

He'd told himself it was just muscle memory. A man hums. Men hum.

But the sword had hummed back.

"Left," the child said, though there was no path to be left of. "It's louder on the left."

"You can hear it from here?"

"I could hear it from your floor," she said. "You sleep very loudly. Like a bear falling down stairs. But under that—" she tapped her chest again— "this."

They came over the last shoulder of rock as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and there it was: the tarn, small and impossibly blue, lying in its bowl of stone like something set down carefully by hands that meant to come back for it. And standing in the middle of the water—

Odile's breath went out of him.

A bell. A bell taller than a man, green with age, half-drowned, tilted against a drowned steeple whose roof broke the surface beside it like the back of some sleeping animal. The metal was worked all over with script—Thornic, pre-Imperial, the deep old letters that predated kingdoms and maps and every law the Chancery had ever passed. He recognized the hand of it. Every swordspeaker did. It was the same hand that ran down the spine of an oath-blade.

"That's Saint Veyra's bell," he said, before he could stop himself, and the sound of his own voice saying those words aloud after fifteen years made his eyes sting stupidly. "That's—child, that bell was melted. I stood in the square at Ashfall and watched them melt it. I heard it stop."

"It wasn't melted here," she said simply. "It was rung here. The melting was somewhere else. Or we were." She frowned, working it through. "The old country isn't dead, Odile. I keep trying to tell you. It's *folded*. Like a letter. And this—" she pointed at the tarn, at the bell, at the impossible blue of it— "is where the fold touches through."

The bell rang.

No wind touched the water. No clapper moved—the bell's mouth was underwater, silted, sealed. But the tolling came anyway, up through the rock, through their boots, into the teeth and the breastbone, one slow bronze note that Odile felt rather than heard, and then the note *said* something. Not in meaning. In memory. It said *morning*, the way morning used to mean something in a country that no longer existed: bread ovens, bell-towers, children running cold streets, the smell of wet stone and woodsmoke, a language poured over everything like honey.

The child was crying without any fuss at all, tears simply present on her face, her attention elsewhere.

"What's it saying?" Odile asked, hoarse.

"Roll call," she said. "It's calling names. Listen." And then, because she was eight and made of terrible ideas, she cupped her hands around her mouth and called across the water, in flawless pre-Imperial Thornic:

*"Veyra! We're here!"*

The bell answered.

The second toll was different. Warmer. It went through Odile like a hand through a coat pocket, finding everything he'd hidden there—the oath he'd spoken at nineteen, the blade he'd broken himself rather than surrender it, fifteen years of silence stacked like cordwood—and it *read* him, top to bottom, the way a customs officer reads a manifest.

And then it kept ringing.

Not once more. Continually now, softly, a pulse in the bronze, steady as a heart, and Odile understood—with the cold professional clarity that had kept him alive through the fall of everything—that the bell wasn't greeting them.

It was *reporting* them.

"Get away from the water," he said quietly.

"Why? It knows us—"

"*Now*, little one."

* * *

They were halfway down the scree when he explained it, in the flat voice he used for prices and other unpleasant facts.

"The Chancery doesn't hunt people. People hide; people lie; people are inefficient. They hunt *resonance*. Every veil-burner company carries a resonance-map—a plate of treated slate that shows where the Veil is thin, where the old words are being spoken, where anything that predates the Empire is... active." He stepped off a ledge, turned, and lifted her down after him. Her weight barely registered anymore; he'd stopped noticing he was doing it, which frightened him more than the bell. "A drowned saint's bell ringing pre-Imperial liturgy on a mountaintop isn't activity. It's a bonfire."

"But it was already ringing," she protested. "Before us. All night. You said—"

"All night, faintly, in a basin surrounded by peaks that eat echo. That's a candle. You walked up to it and called it by name and it answered in *your* voice, with *me* standing beside it wearing a reforged oath-sword on my back." He crouched to her level, and gentled, because terror made him gentle, always had. "Somewhere in a Chancery garrison, a slate plate has just drawn a mark on these mountains that a clerk is going to look at twice. Then three times. Then he's going to fetch someone, and that someone will not be a clerk."

She absorbed this the way she absorbed everything: seriously, silently, and then sideways.

"So we shouldn't have come."

"We shouldn't have come," he agreed.

"But you came anyway. Because I asked."

"...Yes."

"And because you wanted to hear it too." She wasn't accusing him. That was the worst part. She was simply accurate, the way children and bells are accurate. "Your chest was doing the thing mine does."

Odile looked back up at the tarn, one last time. The bell had gone quiet—or quieter, a mutter beneath the hearing, a banked fire. The drowned steeple held its shape against the blue.

"Fifteen years," he said, mostly to himself. "I buried the language so deep I dreamt in Common. I broke my own sword so no oath could find me. And one bell rings my name and I climb a mountain before breakfast like a boy." He sighed. "We're going to need Brannick. And a new route. And probably a miracle, but start with the route."

"Brannick smells like onions," she said gravely. "Good onions, though."

"He does. Don't tell him."

* * *

Maren Voss stood in the garrison map room at Coldharbour station and looked at the resonance-plate the way she'd once looked at battlefields: reading it for what it wanted to hide.

The slate showed the Thornfells as a wash of grey—the Veil lay thick over the range, as it had for fifteen years, smooth as poured wax. Except.

There. High on the western shoulder of the massif. A bloom of light, faint, pulsing, regular as breathing. One of the junior clerks had circled it in chalk and written *probable instrument fault*.

"Instrument faults don't pulse in liturgical rhythm, Clerk Anselm," Voss said. "That's a four-count. Slow measure. Call-and-response cadence." She tapped the slate with one gloved finger, and her stomach did something complicated, because she knew that cadence. Every child raised within earshot of a certain drowned bell tower had known it. "That is Saint Veyra's morning roll call, and it hasn't sounded since Ashfall."

"You recognize it, Inquisitor?"

"I read," Voss said, which was true, and insufficient, and she let it stand.

She looked longer than she needed to. The bloom sat almost exactly where the trail reports had put a smuggler with no registered name who traded salt and secrets along the high roads—almost exactly where a bootprint in late snow, small, unshod or badly shod, had been found two valleys east. Almost exactly where her own life had forked, fifteen years ago, on a burning plain.

*Probable instrument fault.* If she wrote that, the matter died in this room. If she confirmed it, a veil-burner company would march within the week, and what they burned, they burned thoroughly: words first, then the mouths that held them.

Anselm waited, chalk hovering.

Voss thought of Odile—of what his name had been before names were contraband—standing somewhere in those mountains with a sword he'd sworn was destroyed and, if the plate was honest, a child.

"Confirmed," she said. "Log it. Priority escalation." Her voice did not waver; she had trained it not to for twenty years, and it obeyed. "I'll take the descent order myself."

Afterward she stood alone in the map room a long while, watching the little light pulse on the slate like a heartbeat, like a bell, like something folded and patient, waiting to be unfolded.

*Forgive me,* she thought, to no one in particular, to everyone. *Or don't. But hurry.*

Because the company would march in three days. And she intended to be on the mountain first.
