## 10. Burned Road

The fire came at dusk, and it came wrong.

Odile knew the eastern passes the way a shepherd knows his sheep—by smell, by sound, by the particular grumble each one made when the weather turned. So when the wind arrived from the east carrying smoke that tasted of pitch and something sharper underneath, something chemical and deliberate, they were on their feet before the kettle finished boiling.

"Burners," Odile said, and the word sat in the room like a stone dropped in still water.

The child—Wren, because she had finally given them a name she could bear to be called by, though her true one lived in Thornic where Odile could not safely reach it—looked up from the floorboards, where she had been tracing letters with a wet finger. Letters. Pre-Imperial script, older than any alphabet the Chancery's maps acknowledged, flowing out of her small finger like it was nothing at all.

"Is that bad?" she asked, in careful Common. She was learning Common the way other children learned skipping rhymes, piecemeal and delighted. But her eyes went to the reforged sword leaning against the wall, and Odile knew she already understood.

"It's bad," Odile said. "Pack the bread. Leave the rest."

They had talked about this. Not in so many words—Odile did not believe in saying frightening things aloud until frightened things required saying—but Wren had watched Odile fill the go-bag three nights ago without being asked, had helped fold the oilcloth around the sword, had asked only one question: *Will it fit through the low passes?* As if the problem were the sword's width and not what the sword meant.

Now the problem was neither. The problem was smoke rolling over the ridge line in a way no wildfire moved, too even, too patient, in three separate columns spaced like fingers closing into a fist.

* * *

Brannick was waiting for them at the fence line above the old tannery, which was itself a miracle of timing or a catastrophe of it, depending on how the night went. He looked worse than usual, which for Brannick meant he had shaved. His coat had new burn marks on the sleeves that had not been there at midsummer.

"You felt it too," he said. No greeting. Smugglers didn't waste greetings on nights like this.

"The whole valley will feel it by morning." Odile shifted the sword-sling on their back. "Tell me you have a road."

"I have a road." He said it the way men say *I have a plan* when the plan involves a bridge that might hold. "Old salt road, north side of the Kettle. Runs under the scree slope. Chancery can't burn what's underground."

"And the catch."

Brannick's jaw worked. He glanced at Wren, then back, and lowered his voice as though the girl couldn't hear him from four feet away, which was a courtesy Odile appreciated even knowing it was useless. "There's a checkpoint on the Kettle road. New since last month. Not veil-burners proper—contract men, the kind who take coin and don't ask questions. I can buy us past them. I've bought past them before."

"How much?"

"That's not—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with a hand rough as tree bark. "It's not money this time, Odile. They've been told to watch for a man matching my description traveling with a woman and a child. There's a price on descriptions now. On *words.*" His voice dropped further. "On a word in particular. They're calling it the root-tongue. Anyone heard speaking it gets taken for questioning, and nobody comes back from questioning."

Wren looked down at her boots. She had been wearing the ones Odile had cut down from an adult pair, laced twice around the ankle because her feet swam in them, and she had been very proud of them, and now she stared at them as though they had betrayed her by leaving prints.

"They won't hear anything from me," she said quietly, in Common, and the care she took with the consonants broke Odile's heart in a place they hadn't known was still soft.

"No," Odile said. "You won't speak at all tonight. Not a word in anything. Can you do that?"

She nodded. Then, because she was eight and honesty mattered more than comfort: "For how long?"

"For as long as it takes."

* * *

The salt road under the Kettle was exactly as advertised, which was to say dark, dripping, and old enough to predate the Chancery's grandfathers. Odile had run it twice in the early years, hauling brandy and pretending to be a deaf-mute quarry worker, and their body remembered the low places before their mind did—duck here, left shoulder to the wall, mind the seam of iron-stone that caught a lantern flame and threw it sideways.

They walked close together, Brannick ahead with the lantern hooded to a slit, Wren's hand tucked in Odile's. She hadn't spoken since the fence line. She hummed instead, barely, a thread of sound more breath than music, and Odile recognized the tune after a quarter mile and wished they hadn't. It was a threshing song. A harvest song from terraces that existed now only inside a child's head and underneath a Veil the size of a country's grief. The stone around them seemed to lean toward it. The sword, wrapped in oilcloth against Odile's spine, gave off a warmth like a sleeping animal.

"Don't," Odile murmured—not to Wren, but to the blade, and immediately felt foolish for it.

"She can't help it," Brannick said without turning. "People sing when they're scared. You used to."

"I used to do a lot of things."

"Aye." And then, softer, in the tone of a man setting down something heavy so he can pick up something heavier: "Odile. If it goes wrong up there—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong," he repeated, with the patience of the very stubborn, "you take the low fork and don't stop for anything. Not anything. I know how you get about debts."

"I don't get about debts."

"You absolutely do. You carried my ledger for two years after Vell's Landing because you said we were square and you knew damn well we weren't." He stopped walking. In the hooded light his face was all shadow except the wet gleam of his eyes, and he looked suddenly, terribly old—the age smugglers actually are, beneath the age they claim. "Promise me the fork."

"We'll discuss it if it goes wrong."

"There'll be no time to discuss." He exhaled through his nose. "Fine. Then I'll make it easy. If it goes wrong, I'm going to make noise. A lot of noise. The kind of noise contract men chase. And you're going to let me, because the alternative is explaining to a mute child why the nice fence man died for nothing." He lifted the lantern slightly. "Say yes, Odile."

Odile looked at him. Fifteen years of fences and favors and shared fires in leaky bothies, fifteen years of the closest thing to friendship a person could have while pretending not to have a name. Brannick had never once asked what Odile had buried under the hearthstones. He had only ever left an extra coil of rope outside the door, unremarked, the year after Ashfall.

"Yes," Odile said.

"Good." He started walking again. "Hate agreeing with me. Do it too often and I'll get ideas."

* * *

The checkpoint sat where the salt road climbed to meet the Kettle road, a ring of torches and a canvas shelter and six men in mismatched mail with the Chancery's gray sash tied at one arm. Contract soldiers. Hired spears. Men who would rather be anywhere else and knew it about each other.

Brannick walked out ahead alone, hands wide, doing what he did best: being enormous, cheerful, and impossible to arrest without paperwork. Odile caught fragments of it on the wind—*cargo manifests, tolls, my cousin in the customs house, surely we can settle this like gentlemen*—and used the cover of it to slip along the rock lip above the road with Wren pressed to their side.

It was working. That was the terrible thing. It was working beautifully. One soldier laughed. Another scratched under his mail and shifted his weight off the leg he'd clearly been favoring. Brannick produced a flask from somewhere inside his coat with the flourish of a stage magician, and the sergeant's suspicion visibly loosened its grip—

And then Wren sneezed.

Small. High. Nothing. A pebble of a sound in a canyon of noise.

But the youngest soldier turned, and his torch swung up the rock face, and the flame found the edge of Odile's hood, and the man's face did the thing faces always did when they saw Odile full-on: the flicker of *have I seen that one before?*, the slow reach for the paper every one of them carried now, the leaflets with the charcoal sketch of a tall figure, sharp-featured, silver-shot hair, *travels with a child; approaches with extreme caution.*

"Hoy!" the soldier called up. "Hoy, there! Come down!"

Everything after that happened very fast and also very slowly, the way disasters do.

Brannick's flask hit the sergeant in the teeth. Brannick's boot hit the nearest torch into the mud. Brannick's voice—gods, that voice, trained over thirty years of talking his way past gates—filled the whole Kettle like weather: *"That's her! That's the witch the Chancery wants! She's getting away, you idiots, AFTER HER—"*

And six contract men, whose entire understanding of their employment was *do what the loud man says*, believed him.

"Run," Odile breathed into Wren's hair, and they ran.

The low fork split from the high road a hundred yards on, a crack in the hillside barely wider than a person, and Odile took it with Wren's hand crushed in theirs and behind them the world turned orange. Torch-fire first. Then something else. Something with a roar to it, a wet roaring, the sound Odile had heard once before across a burning harbor and had spent fifteen years not hearing.

Veil-fire. They'd brought actual veil-burners, hidden upslope, waiting for a target.

"Brannick," Wren whispered—it escaped her before she could stop it, his name, in her bell-clear accent—and Odile squeezed her hand and did not look back, because looking back was the one thing the promise had forbidden.

He made good noise. He made magnificent noise. Even over the fire Odile could hear him bellowing directions, sending soldiers crashing west while the two fugitives went east, could hear the crackle of veil-fire catching scrub and pine, and then—abruptly, horribly—he could be heard no longer, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing Odile had ever stood inside.

* * *

They came out of the fork near midnight, on a shelf of granite above the tree line, and Odile finally allowed themselves to look back.

The eastern passes burned. All of them—a crown of fire laid along the ridges, precise as calligraphy, the Chancery erasing the roads the way they erased everything else. Somewhere in that orange chaos was a big man with a shaved jaw and a flask of rotgut who had never asked a single question he shouldn't have, who had kept an extra coil of rope by a door for fifteen years, who had promised nothing and delivered everything.

Odile sat down on the granite. It was the only part of them still taking orders.

Wren knelt beside them. She didn't say anything—she was better at keeping promises than Odile was—but she put her small cold hand on the back of Odile's neck, the way you steady something that is shaking loose, and together they watched a man's whole geography go up in smoke.

"He knew," Odile said finally. Their voice sounded borrowed. "He knew what the favor cost and he offered it anyway, the great idiot, he *haggled* with me about the fork—"

The shaking got worse. Odile discovered they were angry, which was better than grieving, and dangerous, and familiar. Fifteen years ago they had stood in a burning doorway feeling exactly this and had made a vow about it, and the vow had held, and the vow was currently dissolving in their chest like salt in rain.

Below them, downslope, a patrol torch bobbed along the switchbacks. Two points of light. Three. Hunting the fork they'd taken, methodical, patient. The columns of fire to the east were closing the high routes one by one; Brannick's underground road was behind them now, under the burn; and every remaining path to the Door of Ash ran through country that was, as of tonight, actively on fire.

The cost of survival, Odile thought. Made visible. Priced in fences.

Wren tugged their sleeve. She pointed—at the patrol, then at the sword on Odile's back, then at the patrol again. Her expression was grave and entirely unafraid, and it was the *un*-afraid part that undid the last of Odile's hesitation.

Because here was the arithmetic Brannick would have appreciated: a drawn blade meant a name. A name meant a trail. A trail meant the Chancery would never stop hunting them, not to the glacier, not past it. Silence had been Odile's armor for fifteen years, and armor only works if you never move.

But the alternative was explaining to a mute child why the nice fence man died for nothing.

Odile unwrapped the oilcloth. The reforged sword came into the starlight singing—that faint, hungry harmonic it had held since the day it drank Wren's voice—and the metal was warm as a living hand. Fifteen years of rust and refusal, and it woke like it had merely been resting.

The patrol rounded the bend below. Six torches now. Close enough to see faces.

Odile stood, set their feet the old way, left forward, weight low, the stance some dead oathmaster had drilled into a recruit a lifetime ago—and spoke one word of Thornic into the burning night. The first word. The oldest one. The word that means both *stand* and *remain*, the word oaths are built on:

"*Duren.*"

The sword answered. Light ran up the fuller like dawn hitting water, and the word went out of Odile bigger than they'd sent it, rolling down the mountainside, and every torch on the switchback guttered blue as if the night itself had flinched. The soldiers froze. One of them dropped his flame and ran. The others stood staring up at a figure on the rocks holding a blade full of morning, and whatever they had been told about caution and descriptions, none of it had prepared them for a language that made fire remember it was once a prayer.

Then the moment passed, as moments do, and the soldiers remembered their wages and their fear, and Odile scooped Wren onto their hip—she weighed almost nothing, she weighed a whole country—and ran west, away from the burned roads, toward the only route left: the long climb, the white country, the ice.

Behind them, far to the south, unseen in all that smoke, a rider in gray turned her horse from the checkpoint ruins and rode hard through the fire-lines, carrying news that would either save them or end them, and Odile would not learn which until the mountain told them both at once.
