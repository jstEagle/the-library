## 11. What I Swore at Ashfall

The Chancery archive beneath the Bastion of the Word kept its documents cold on purpose. Inquisitor Maren Voss had been told once—by a librarian with dust in his eyebrows and a voice like a closing ledger—that paper remembered better when it shivered. She had thought it a joke. Fifteen years in the Chancery had taught her that almost nothing said with a straight face was a joke.

She sat now in the restricted vault, third sublevel, where the air tasted of iron and old candle smoke, and read the file that was not supposed to exist.

It was thin. That was the first wrong thing about it. The fall of Thornmark—the Veiling, as the liturgy called it, the great merciful drawing of the curtain over a kingdom too wounded to save—had an entire wing of ceremony devoted to it. Hymns. Reliquaries. A stained-glass window in the Bastion's nave forty feet tall showing veiled hands lowering a sleeping country into rest. There were processions. There was a *holiday*.

And behind all of it, in a folder no wider than her hand, was the actual record.

*Ashfall*, the folder said, in a clerk's cramped hand. *Final disposition. Sealed by order of First Speaker Corvane.*

Maren turned the page and felt the cold of the vault move into her chest and settle there.

She had been twenty-three at Ashfall. Odile had been twenty-five, already famous for the way they could make a sword speak—literally speak, the oath-words running up the blade like light through water—and Maren had been their shield-sister, which meant she stood to their left and pretended not to be proud of it. She remembered the last night clearly, or she had believed she did. The sky the color of a bruise held up to a lamp. The bells of the capital ringing until the bells themselves cracked. Odile kneeling in the ash with their sword across their knees, saying the final oath while the Veil came down like snow made of silence.

*We hold the door,* Odile had sworn. *We keep the watch. We do not let the words die.*

That was what she remembered. That was what she had built fifteen years of service on: the idea that the Veil was mercy. That the Chancery had closed a wound. That somewhere behind the curtain, Thornmark slept, whole and waiting, and one day—

One day.

The file disagreed.

*Subject nation does not sleep,* the clerk had written, in handwriting gone brown and careful, the hand of someone recording something he did not want to be true. *Subject nation is withheld. The working designated "Veil" does not conceal; it excludes. Territorial integrity is maintained in name only. The land remains. The people are unmade from the record. No return is provided for. No return is possible.*

Maren read it three times. Then she did what any good inquisitor would do with a document that contradicted everything she believed: she looked around the empty vault, checked the door, and stole it.

Not the folder itself—that would be noticed within the hour; the librarians counted their silences the way misers counted coins—but she copied it, every word, into her own field book, in her own cramped hand, and then she sat back down as though nothing had happened, because her legs had briefly stopped being interested in holding her up.

*No return is possible.* Except someone had come back. A child, barefoot in the snow, speaking a language older than the Veil itself. Which meant either the file was wrong, or the file was right and the impossible had walked out of a sealed country and knocked on a smuggler's door.

Or both. Both was usually the answer. Both was how the Chancery liked its truths: doubled, so you could never quite hold one without the other slipping away.

* * *

"You look," said Brother Anselm, "like a woman who has found something in the soup."

Anselm was the archivist assigned to shadow her through the valley inquiry—a soft, round man with ink-stained fingers and the moral flexibility of a well-loved dog. He was also, Maren suspected, reporting on her to someone, but he was so cheerful about it that she'd mostly stopped minding. He set down two cups of the Bastion's terrible chicory substitute and lowered himself into the chair across from her with the sigh of a man who considered sitting a full day's work.

"I've been reading," Maren said.

"That's generally discouraged after ordination." He blew on his cup. "Anything interesting?"

"History."

"Ugh." He said it with real feeling. "Dead men arguing about taxes."

"Something like that." She wrapped her hands around the cup. It was too hot to hold and she held it anyway. "Anselm. When you took your vows. What were you told the Veil was?"

He blinked at her. "Protection. Obviously. The old country was—" he waved a hand, reciting, and she could hear the catechism under it, worn smooth as a stair stone, "—broken by the Sundering War, its people grieving past bearing, and the First Speakers drew the Veil so the land could heal unseen. Mercy, the liturgy says. The kindest act ever done by human hands."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's what I was told too."

"And?"

"And I'm wondering," she said slowly, "who told the First Speakers that the country wanted to be put to bed. Because I've been reading the disposition orders, and mercy has a strange way of sounding like a lock."

Anselm's cheerfulness flickered—not went out, never entirely went out, he was constitutionally incapable of total gloom—but it flickered, the way a candle does when someone opens a door somewhere else in the house.

"Maren," he said, gently, "I've known you six years. You have the best arrest record in the western chapter and the worst singing voice at any vigil I've attended. You don't ask questions like this unless something's bitten you. What did you find?"

She weighed him. She had been weighing people professionally for fifteen years and privately for longer, and Anselm came out better than most: a man who burned books only when ordered and apologized to them afterward.

"A word," she said finally. "In the margin of the disposition file. Someone wrote it small enough that a casual reader would take it for a smudge. It says *prison*. And underneath, in the same hand: *we were not saving them. We were saving ourselves from hearing them.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a bell rang the hour, indifferent and clean.

"That's treasonous," Anselm said at last, but quietly, the way you say a diagnosis.

"Yes."

"You're going to keep reading anyway."

"Yes."

He nodded, drank his chicory, and pulled her copy of the valley survey toward himself, all business again. But at the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said without turning around: "For what it's worth, my grandmother came from a village near the Thornfells. Before the Veiling. She used to sing something in the old tongue when she baked. We were told it was nonsense syllables. Comfort sounds." He rubbed his ink-stained thumb against his fingers, as if the words might still be there. "I've always wondered who decided they were nonsense."

Then he was gone, and Maren sat alone with a cooling cup and a stolen truth, and outside the window the mountains stood white and patient, keeping whatever secrets they kept.

* * *

The interrogation of the miller's wife should have taken ten minutes and instead took four hours, and by the end of it Maren had learned three things: that the woman was terrified, that she was lying badly, and that she was lying to protect a child.

"She came through the pass," the miller's wife said for the sixth time, wringing her apron into a rope. "A beggar girl, I told you. I gave her bread. That's charity, that's not a crime—"

"No one has accused you of a crime, mistress Aldse." Maren kept her voice at the register she thought of as *hearthside*, low and unhurried, the voice of a woman asking about weather. Terror made people stupid; kindness made them honest. Usually. "When did she come through? What season?"

"...Late autumn. Early storms."

"Alone?"

A hesitation. A whole biography lived inside that hesitation. "Yes."

"Where did she go afterward?"

"I don't know. Girls wander. I fed her and she went on."

Maren let the silence sit. In the corner, Brother Anselm scratched notes, and the scratch of his pen was the loudest thing in the room. Outside, the mill wheel turned its slow wet circles, counting time the way mills do, without opinion.

"The girl spoke oddly," Maren said at last. "You noticed."

The miller's wife flinched. It was small, a bird-flinch, but Maren had spent fifteen years learning to see birds startle. "Everyone speaks oddly. Mountain folk. Foreigners."

"In Thornic," Maren said.

The apron-roping stopped.

Here was the thing Maren had not told her superiors, the thing she carried in the same pocket as the stolen file: she knew Thornic. Not fluently—an inquisitor of the western chapter was given exactly enough of the forbidden tongue to recognize it, the way a customs officer is taught to recognize contraband without being taught to enjoy it. But she knew the sound of it. She had heard it sung over dying men at Ashfall. She had heard Odile swear in it, that last night, the words rising off the blade like heat-shimmer.

And she knew, with the certainty that was currently dismantling her faith plank by plank, that a language sealed out of existence did not reappear by accident. Languages did not knock on doors. Something had opened something.

"Tell me about the bootprints," she said.

"There weren't—I didn't—"

"Small prints," Maren said. "Bare feet, actually, or boots too large, going up the shepherd's track toward the high cabins. Toward one cabin in particular." She watched the woman's face do arithmetic it didn't want to finish. "Mistress Aldse. I am not going to burn your mill. I am not going to burn anything. I want to know if the child is safe, and I want to know who is keeping her, and then I want you to forget we had this conversation, and I will help you forget it too."

The miller's wife stared at her. "You're not like the others," she said finally, suspiciously, as though Maren might be a trap.

"No," Maren agreed, and heard the crack in her own voice, faint as a hairline in glaze. "I'm really not."

* * *

That night she dreamed of Ashfall, which was not unusual. What was unusual was that the dream refused to stay in its usual shape.

Normally the dream was liturgy-shaped: the bruise-colored sky, the cracked bells, Odile kneeling in the ash, the Veil descending like a blessing. She woke from it calm. That was the point of it. The Chancery had trained the memory into her so thoroughly that even her sleep performed its doctrine.

But tonight the dream kept going past the part where it was supposed to end.

The Veil came down, and instead of a sleeping country behind it there was—nothing. Not darkness. *Withholding.* The dream showed her a green terrace garden she had never seen, bell-towers she had never climbed, a door standing open in a hillside with warm light coming out of it, and then the Veil closed over all of it like a lid, and behind the lid she could hear—not screaming, that was the horror of it, no screaming at all—just the sound of a country continuing, muffled, patient, knocking.

Knocking. For fifteen years. The way a prisoner knocks on a wall, not hoping to be freed anymore, just wanting someone to know they're still there.

She woke with her hand on her sword hilt, in a cold room, in a cold bed, in a fortress built on a word that had just stopped meaning what she'd been told it meant.

*We hold the door,* Odile had sworn, fifteen years ago, ash in their hair. *We keep the watch. We do not let the words die.*

And she had reported them for it. She had signed the order that sent veil-burners after the last swordspeaker alive, because the oath Odile kept was the oath the Chancery had rewritten, and she had never once—never once in fifteen years—asked who had done the rewriting, or why the original needed changing, or what a merciful Veil needed with guards.

She got up. Lit the lamp. Opened her field book to the copied file and read the marginal note again, the small brown hand:

*We were not saving them. We were saving ourselves from hearing them.*

Below it, in the same hand, one more line she had missed before, or perhaps had seen and refused:

*If anyone reads this: the child-keys will come back. They always come back. When they do, choose differently than we did.*

Maren sat very still.

Somewhere north of her, up a shepherd's track, in a smuggler's cabin that smelled of woodsmoke and stubbornness, a mute-born child was teaching a broken sword to sing. And somewhere south of her, in the Bastion, her superiors were drafting resonance-maps and requisitioning burner companies, because a bell had rung in a high tarn and the maps had heard it.

She had maybe days. Perhaps less.

Maren Voss closed the field book, and for the first time since her ordination she prayed to no one in particular, in a dead language she was forbidden to love, using the only three words of Thornic the Chancery had left her—the recognition words, the ones stamped on every warrant:

*I hear you.*

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

She packed her kit, left a false report on Anselm's desk weighted with a stone—*pursuing lead south, return in ten days*—and walked out of the Bastion into the dark, heading north, toward the mountains, toward the cabin, toward whatever remained of the sister she had betrayed and the faith she was only now, at last, beginning to actually examine.

Behind her, the stained-glass window in the nave caught moonlight: forty feet of veiled hands lowering a sleeping country into rest.

From this angle, from outside, in the cold—it looked exactly like a lid coming down on something that was still awake.
