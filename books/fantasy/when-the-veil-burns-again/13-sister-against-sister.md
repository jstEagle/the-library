## 13. Sister Against Sister

* * *

The shrine had been a place of worship once, before the Chancery decided that worship required a permit.

Odile remembered it from the smuggling years—a hollow in the mountain's shoulder where the old road dipped beneath an overhang of granite, and someone, long ago, had carved a roof over the space and set three stone pillars inside like the ribs of some patient beast. Moss grew on everything. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the regularity of a metronome. In the back wall, half-eaten by lichen, there were letters.

Not smugglers' marks. Not Chancery tally-script.

Thornic.

The child—Wren, because Odile had finally broken down and given her a name, since "the child" made her flinch every time and "girl" made her scowl in a way that was frankly alarming for someone who came up to Odile's elbow—stood before the carved words with her hand flat against the stone.

"It says *the door remembers what the tongue forgets*," she said.

"That's comforting," Odile said, feeding dry moss to their small fire. "I'd hate for it to forget."

"You're making a joke."

"I'm told I do that when I'm terrified. It's a character flaw."

Wren looked back at them, and in the firelight her face did the thing it had been doing more and more often lately: softening. Three days in the cave shrine, three days of cold rations and whispered lessons, and she had stopped watching Odile like a stranger. She watched them now like something else. Like family, maybe. The word sat in Odile's chest like a swallowed coal—warm and dangerous in equal measure.

"Again," Wren said, turning back to the sword across her knees. "The third form. You keep flattening the vowel."

"I'm *not*—"

"*Ashen-vael.* The *a* opens. It's not a cough, it's a bell." She demonstrated, and the shattered oath-sword—reforged now, seven days of forge-work and one small miracle in Brannick's cousin's smithy—hummed against her knees like a contented cat. When Odile said it, the hum turned sulky.

"Bell," Odile repeated, flattening the vowel out of pure spite, and the sword gave a note so distinctly offended that Wren laughed—a bright, startled sound, the first true laugh Odile had heard from her—and the sound rang off the pillars and up into the mountain dark.

Odile would think about that later. About how loud it was. About how sound travels in empty places.

But that was later.

For now they ate, and slept in shifts, and the water dripped its patient count, and neither of them heard the footsteps on the old road until the fire had burned to coals and the night outside had gone utterly, professionally silent.

* * *

Maren Voss had learned long ago that mountains lie.

They lied with weather, with echo, with paths that doubled back on themselves like drunkards giving testimony. But they could not lie about resonance. The Chancery's maps had sung for two days now—one bright thread of Thornic moving through the high country, and then, three nights past, a single clear tolling from a tarn no map bothered to name, ringing in a dead language like a finger snapped in the face of the Empire.

She had followed the thread alone. That part she had not reported. Her superiors believed she was sweeping the eastern passes; her written report said as much, in her own hand, in ink she had chosen specifically because it was the ink of truth and she wanted, somewhere, to have told the truth about *something* this season.

Her report said: *contagion contained, subject tracked toward the lowlands.*

Her boots said: climbing.

She found the shrine by smell—woodsmoke, faint, hours old—and then by ear: the particular silence of people trying very hard to be quiet. Fifteen years of hunting had taught her that guilty silence has a texture. Innocent silence is accidental. This silence was *held*, the way you hold your breath when you hear a floorboard speak under someone else's weight.

Voss stood at the mouth of the shrine, snow settling on her shoulders, and looked at the fire's dying coals, and the girl asleep against a stone pillar with a sword across her knees like a doll she refused to put down, and Odile Sandoval sitting up straight as a drawn blade, eyes open, hand already at the hilt.

"Well," Voss said. "This is awkward."

"Hello, Maren." Odile's voice was level. Their eyes went past her, counting—how many, where—but the pass behind her was empty. "You came alone."

"You always did ask the important questions after checking for the knife."

"There's usually a knife."

"There is," Voss agreed, and drew it.

* * *

Here is what Maren Voss knew about killing Odile Sandoval: it would be easy, and it would be impossible, and both of these things were true in the same breath, which was the whole problem with her life.

Easy, because Odile was fifteen years out of practice and half-starved on smuggler rations, and Voss trained veil-burners twice a week and had never once let the forms go soft. Impossible, because the last time they had crossed blades—the last time, at Ashfall, with the sky burning and the Veil coming down like the lid of a coffin—neither of them had managed it either, and they'd been young and strong and actually trying.

Some fights are not about winning. Some fights are two people asking each other a question with steel, over and over, and neither wanting to hear the answer.

"Step away from the child," Voss said.

"No."

"Maren—"

"That's *Inquisitor* Voss to deserters."

"You're four days from any checkpoint. There's no one here to be impressed by the title but me, and I've seen you with a hangover. It didn't suit you either."

The girl woke. Voss saw it happen—the flicker of her eyes, the way she came awake without a sound, which was wrong, children don't wake like that, children wake screaming or not at all. She rose without putting her feet down loudly, and stood beside Odile, and looked at Voss with those eyes that had seen, apparently, an entire drowned country.

Then she spoke.

"Ashen-vael," she said—the third form, the bell-vowel, perfect—and laid her hand on Odile's shoulder, and the reforged sword sang.

Not hummed. *Sang.* A single sustained note that filled the shrine, that set the old carved letters glowing faintly green with lichen-light, that went through Voss like a lance of ice because she *knew* that note. She had heard it once, at Ashfall, when nine hundred swordspeakers spoke the final oath together and the world broke along the seams.

It was quieter now. One sword instead of nine hundred. One voice instead of a nation.

But it was the same song.

"Put it away," Voss said, and her voice cracked in the middle, which infuriated her. "Odile. Put it *away*. Do you know what happens if I report that sound? Do you know what comes down this mountain? Not me. Not a squad. A *company*. Burners with censer-lances and a resonator-cart, and they don't arrest, they *salt the ground*, and there is a *child*—"

"I know exactly what comes down this mountain," Odile said quietly. "That's why we're going to the Door of Ash."

And there it was. Said plainly, in the dripping dark, between two women who had sworn the same oath once and buried it in different graves. Voss felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped down a well—down, and down, and no bottom to the sound.

Because Voss had spent the last month reading sealed archives. She knew what the Veil was. She knew what Ashfall had actually been. And every night she wrote reports that said *contained* while her hands shook, because the Chancery hadn't imprisoned a threat.

It had imprisoned a *country*. Terraces. Bell-towers. Bread recipes. Lullabies. An eight-year-old girl's whole green memory of home.

"The Door doesn't open," Voss said. "That's doctrine."

"Doctrine also says the Veil protects us from them," Odile said. "Ask yourself, Maren. Protects us from *what*? From terraces? From bells? From a child who talks in her sleep in a language older than your Chancery?" They took one step forward, sword still singing low. "You read the sealed archives. I can see it in your face. You've known for weeks."

The silence stretched. Water dripped. Somewhere above, wind worried at the granite like a dog at a bone.

"Draw," Odile said gently. "If you're going to do it, draw and mean it. Because if you take us in, the girl burns, and if you let us walk, you burn instead, and either way one of us has to stop pretending this is duty. So draw, sister, and let's find out which of us is lying."

Voss drew.

* * *

Later, neither of them would be able to reconstruct the fight properly, and both would privately consider that a mercy.

What remained was fragments. Steel meeting steel in the narrow dark, sparks crawling up the wet stone walls like orange ivy. Odile fighting rustily but *honestly*, all economy and no flourish, the smuggler's habit of never wasting a motion married suddenly—jarringly—to the old oath-forms, the ones Voss herself had taught them, twenty years ago, on a training field under a green flag. The girl pressed against the far pillar with her hands over her ears, and the sword-note rising between the fighters like a third combatant, and Voss realizing, somewhere around the fourth exchange, that she was fighting the sword as much as the woman.

Every time she committed to a killing line, the blade *sang*, and the note bent her stroke a hair wide—not much, a finger's width, but a finger's width is the whole distance between a wound and a memory. Every time Odile committed to one, their stroke faltered too, pulling wide of her throat, her heart, her sword-arm's tendons. Twice they locked hilts, faces a span apart, breath fogging between them.

"You're pulling," Odile gasped.

"So are *you*."

"I know. It's infuriating."

"You always were a *terrible* liar—" Voss broke the lock, spun, cut; Odile caught it on the forte and the impact rang through the shrine like the bell in the tarn, and both of them staggered apart, and stood there, heaving, blades lowered, and neither could have said whose idea that was.

The girl took her hands off her ears.

"She can't kill us," Wren said—not to Odile, to Voss, directly, in accented but fluent Imperial. "And you can't kill us. So now everyone has to decide what they *can* do."

Voss stared at her. Eight years old. Barefoot in a shrine in winter, facing down an inquisitor with a drawn sword, and lecturing her on the arithmetic of the situation like a schoolmistress with a head cold.

"What's your name, child?"

"Wren."

"Wren." Voss sheathed her blade, slowly, and the sound of it sliding home was the loudest thing in the mountains. "Do you know what I am?"

"You're the one who used to braid Odile's hair before ceremonies and pretend you weren't crying."

A muscle jumped in Voss's jaw. "Who told you that?"

"The sword remembers things," Wren said simply. "People talk into swords. Everyone knows that."

"*Everyone* knows that," Odile muttered, "except the person whose hair it was," and got a look from Voss that could have curdled milk at forty paces, and deserved, entirely, the exhausted ghost of a smile it produced anyway.

* * *

They stood apart afterward, the three of them, in a triangle as old as any story: the fugitive, the hunter, the reason.

"If I walk away," Voss said, "and write my report, the company still comes. Maybe later. Maybe softer. But it comes. Unless my report gives them nothing to come *for*."

"So give them nothing."

"I intend to." She pulled out her field ledger—the real one, the one her handlers would read—and uncapped her pen with fingers that were steadier than she had expected them to be. She wrote while they watched: *Resonance source located and extinguished. Remnant speech-pattern confirmed as parroting mimicry, likely taught by deceased oath-keeper prior to death. Subject Sandoval not present in sector. Recommend downgrade to routine monitoring.*

"Lies," Odile said.

"Reports," Voss corrected, and blew the ink dry. "There's a difference. The difference is whether anyone can prove it, and they can't, because I'm the only resonator-trained officer within a hundred miles and I have just spent four days confirming my own findings. That's how the machinery works, Odile. It believes whatever the paperwork says. I used to find that monstrous." She pocketed the ledger. "Now I find it useful."

"And when they send someone else? Someone with their own instruments? The bell in the tarn rang, Maren. The whole eastern range heard it."

"Then I'll have bought you until solstice, and not one day more." She looked at Wren, and her voice did something complicated. "Solstice. The Door. You truly mean to try."

"We mean to succeed."

"No one succeeds at the Door. People *die* at the Door. I read the accounts of the sealing, Odile. Nine hundred swordspeakers went in and none—"

"—came back," Odile finished. "Yes. We've done our reading too." They paused. "Which brings us to the unpleasant part."

"The part where you ask me for help."

"The part where I don't have to ask, because you've already decided, and we both know it, and it's making you furious."

Voss laughed—one short, sharp bark that echoed off the pillars and sounded, to her own ears, like the last plank of a pier giving way. Fifteen years. Fifteen years of burning words and salting ground and telling herself the Veil was mercy, and it had taken one child and one stubborn, flat-vowelled deserter to walk her off the edge of her own doctrine.

"If they ever audit my route," she said quietly, "if they ever pull my resonance-logs against my reports, I will be tried for treason. You understand that. Not censured. *Tried*. And the Chancery does not lose those trials, because the Chancery writes the trials."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm not asking anything," Odile said. "I'm standing here remembering a training field and a green flag and a woman who braided my hair and cried about it, and I'm telling you that when the company comes—if it comes—I'd rather stand next to you than anywhere in this world. But the choice is yours. It was always yours. That's what the oath actually meant, Maren. Nobody ever understood that but us."

The water dripped. The coals ticked down to ash.

Voss looked at the child—Wren—who was looking back at her with an expression of terrible patience, the patience of someone who has already lost a whole country and knows precisely how much another one costs.

"Go north," Voss said at last, roughly. "Stay off the resonator roads. The glacier approach is swept weekly until solstice—after that, the garrison rotates and the sweep lapses for six days. Six days. That's your window, and it's thinner than paper." She turned toward the shrine mouth, toward the snow and the dark, and stopped. "And Odile. If I die for this—"

"I'll remember your name."

"Not in Thornic." A ghost of the old smile. "In Imperial. Say it plain, at my grave, where the censors can read it and choke."

Then she walked out into the falling snow, and they watched her go until the dark took her, and the mountain closed behind her like a book.

Wren tugged Odile's sleeve.

"She lied in her report," the girl said. "But she told the truth with her feet. The sword says so."

"The sword," Odile said, "is becoming a gossip," and sheathed it, and began packing for the north, and the glacier, and the six thin days of grace that were all that stood between them and the end of everything—or the beginning of it.

High above the shrine, unseen in the snowfall, a Chancery survey hawk wheeled once on the wind and turned south toward the lowlands, carrying in its brass throat a resonance-record of everything it had heard.

Including a name.
