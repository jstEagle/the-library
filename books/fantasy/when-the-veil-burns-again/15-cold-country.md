## 15. Cold Country

The glacier did not announce itself. It simply arrived one morning as a white wall across the world, so bright under the pale sun that Odile's eyes watered and the child laughed at them for it.

"You look like a startled goat," she said in Thornic, which was her favorite way to say anything insulting, because she believed insults were more honest in a dead language.

"I have been compared to worse animals," Odile said, tightening the last strap on their pack. "A mule. A fence post. Once, memorably, a damp sock."

"Who called you a damp sock?"

"Inquisitor Voss. The day we met." Odile shouldered the pack and did not let themself think about Maren Voss for longer than that single sentence, because thinking about Maren Voss out here, on the ice, with no fire and no cover and three days of white ahead of them, was a luxury their heart could not afford.

The child—Neyla, though she had only offered the name four nights ago, shyly, like a bird stepping onto an offered hand—looked up at the glacier the way other children looked up at festival fireworks. No fear in her at all. There never seemed to be. Odile had stopped asking whether this was courage or whether she simply didn't know enough yet to be afraid, because both answers frightened Odile equally.

"It hums," Neyla said.

"What does?"

"The ice. Can't you hear it?"

Odile could, actually. They had been hearing it since dawn and telling themself it was wind. A low, patient tone beneath the silence, like a bell heard through a wall, like a voice heard through years. The reforged sword rode against their back where it had ridden for two weeks now, wrapped in fleece they'd traded a whole winter's salt for, and it had been humming too, softly, insistently, ever since Brannick died and Odile spoke that first word aloud and the world decided, apparently, that there was no taking it back.

"Stay close," Odile said. "Rope stays taut between us. If I fall, don't come after me. Plant your feet, shout my name, and wait."

"What if shouting doesn't work?"

"Then shout louder."

"And if louder doesn't work?"

Odile crouched to her height. Her nose was chapped pink and her eyes were enormous and blue and entirely unafraid, and something in Odile's chest did its familiar painful folding thing. "Then I will climb back up myself," they said, "because I promised Brannick I'd get you there, and Brannick is the sort of dead man who holds grudges about broken promises."

Neyla considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Was he a good grudge-holder?"

"The best. He once refused to sell a man boots for eleven years over an unpaid debt of six coppers."

"Eleven *years*?"

"He said it was the principle."

She giggled—a small, startling sound, bright as a struck coin—and Odile stood and turned to face the ice and let the giggle settle somewhere inside them where it could be kept, the way you keep a coal in ash against the long cold.

They stepped onto the glacier together.

* * *

The first day was merely brutal. The second day was worse, because by then Odile had learned what whiteout meant.

It came down from the north like a lid closing. One moment there was sky and horizon and the distant gray teeth of peaks; the next there was nothing anywhere in any direction but white, white above and white below and white ahead, until walking felt less like moving than like being erased slowly, footfall by footfall. Odile roped themself to Neyla with the rope around their own waist rather than trusting either of them to hold it, and they walked by compass and by counting steps and by the faint, growing song of the sword, which had become the only direction left in the world.

"Tell me a story," Neyla shouted over the wind. "In Thornic."

"Why in Thornic?"

"So the ice can hear it. It likes stories. It's been alone a long time."

There were a hundred sensible reasons not to speak Thornic aloud on open ice. Every one of them was true and every one of them was useless, because the sword was already singing, already broadcasting their position to any resonance-map within fifty miles, and because the child had asked, and because Odile had spent fifteen years burying the language and had learned exactly how much good that had done anyone.

So they told her the story of the Miller Who Argued With Winter, the oldest story they knew, older than the Veil, maybe older than kingdoms, and they told it in the old tongue while the storm screamed and Neyla walked behind them laughing at the parts where the miller cheated, which was all the parts, because the miller cheated constantly and shamelessly and won every time, which was why the story had survived.

"—and so Winter said, *very well, old man, take your flour and go,* and that is why," Odile finished, "the first bread baked after the first frost is always a little sweet. Winter remembers being argued down, and he sulks into the grain."

"That's not true."

"Of course it isn't. But it's better than the truth, which is that someone a thousand years ago liked sweet bread and wanted an excuse."

The storm swallowed her laughter, but Odile felt it anyway, transmitted down the taut rope like warmth along a wire.

They made camp in the lee of a serac that groaned and settled all night like an old man in his sleep. Odile got the bivouac half up before their fingers stopped answering. That was the thing about cold that people who hadn't lived it didn't understand: it didn't hurt, at the end. It just quietly took things away. First feeling, then strength, then the ability to care that you'd lost them. Odile got the tent pegs in with their forearms and their elbows and their teeth, and then they pulled Neyla inside and got her hands under their own arms and held her there, shuddering, while the storm tried to decide whether tonight would be the night it finally buried them.

"Your fingers are gray," Neyla said, very calm, in the way of children delivering diagnoses.

"They're fashionable."

"They're *gray.*"

"Then I'm fashionable and gray. Some people manage neither."

She frowned at them, dug into her pack—the pack Odile carried most days, though she insisted on hauling it herself on principle, another thing she'd inherited from nobody living—and produced a lump of fat and a scrap of wool. And then, matter-of-factly, she took Odile's hands and began to rub warmth back into them, working the fat into the cracked skin, murmuring under her breath.

She was murmuring in Thornic. Not words Odile knew. Older words. Words that predated the liturgies, the ones she'd spoken in the cave shrine when the sword drank her voice like rain.

The pain came back into Odile's fingers all at once, hot and furious, and Odile nearly wept with gratitude and did not, because there were standards to maintain.

"What are you saying?" they asked.

"I don't know," Neyla said. "I just know it. Like I know how to breathe." She looked up. In the dark of the bivouac her eyes caught even the smallest light. "You know it too. You're just scared of it."

"I'm scared of lots of things. It's a full-time occupation."

"Not like that. Not scared-of-wolves scared. Scared like—" She searched for it. Eight years old, and searching for it. "Scared like a door that opens both ways."

Odile lay back against the pack and stared at the frost-feathers blooming on the fabric overhead and thought: *fifteen years old, that sentence, if it's a day.* Fifteen years of careful invisibility, and a child had put her finger on the exact shape of it in one evening, the way children do, the way they always do, because they haven't yet learned which doors everyone has agreed to walk past without touching.

"Sleep," Odile said. "We move at dawn."

* * *

The crevasse took them on the third morning, or rather, it took Odile, because Odile was in front and the snow bridge had looked exactly like all the snow that had held for two days and was, in fact, holding nothing at all.

There was no dramatic crack, no warning rumble. There was simply a lurch, a rush of cold air, and then rope, hard around Odile's waist, and the whole world swinging upside down into blue.

Blue, Odile discovered, hanging in space and rotating gently, was the color at the bottom of glaciers. Blue like deep water, like twilight, like the veins in a corpse. Far below, the crevasse floor glimmered with old ice and old dark. Above, a ragged circle of white showed where they'd punched through, and against it, silhouetted, small and steady, Neylia's head appeared.

"Odile!" she called. Her voice didn't shake. Of course it didn't. "Are you dead?"

"Working on it. Give me a moment."

"Brannick said you'd fall eventually. He said you walked like a man who thought the ground owed him money."

"Brannick," Odile said, testing the rope, finding it taut, finding her braced against—something, hopefully something solid—"was a wise and generous friend whom I miss terribly and will insult comprehensively once I'm back on top."

The rope had caught on the lip of the bridge, which was bad, because the lip was snow and would cut through under load. Odile had perhaps a minute before physics remembered it was in charge. They swung, kicked, drove a boot into the near wall, found a shelf of harder ice, climbed hand over hand with fingers that screamed, reached the edge, and lay there gasping while Neyla sat down heavily beside them and put one small hand on their heaving back.

"You're bleeding," she observed.

"Frostbite and blood. We're collecting the full set."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in Thornic, in the old cadence, she said something short and formal, and the sword—still strapped to Odile's back, still humming—went suddenly, shockingly silent, and then rang once, clear as a chapel bell, and went quiet again.

"What did you say?" Odile asked.

"A thank-you. For the rope. For—" she gestured vaguely at all of Odile, "—for carrying me. The old words for thanks aren't really thanks. They're more like a debt-notice. You say them when someone carries you somewhere, and it means you're bound now. Bound to carry them back, someday, however far."

Odile sat up. The wind had dropped; the sun had returned, thin and white and merciless. Somewhere ahead, under their feet, under a mile of ice, the Door of Ash waited, and the sword sang toward it, and Odile felt the debt-notice settling into their bones like cold.

*Neyla had been carried here,* they thought. *Across the Veil. By somebody. Bound to carry them back.*

"Neyla. When you crossed over. Do you remember hands? Anyone holding you?"

Her face did the thing it always did when she reached for the country she couldn't explain—green terraces, bell-towers, a door—the memory sliding away like fish through reeds. "I remember bells," she said at last. "And I remember falling upward. Isn't that funny? Falling upward." She smiled, and then her smile faded, because she was clever, far too clever, and she had watched Odile's face while she answered. "Why?"

"No reason. Let's move. Light's good and the ice is angry today."

But Odile thought about it all afternoon, walking, counting steps, following the song. About doors that opened both ways. About debts written in old words. About the terms of passage that must be carved somewhere on a threshold under a glacier, because thresholds like this one did not give passage freely—nothing that old gave anything freely—and someone, sometime, had paid.

By evening, when the sword's hum had grown so loud it was less a sound than a pressure, less a pressure than a presence, Odile understood—not fully, not in words, but the way you understand weather coming—that the Door was going to ask them something. That passage would have a shape, and the shape would fit one of them perfectly, and Odile already knew, with the sick certainty of a smuggler reading a contract, which one of them it was designed to fit.

Not the child. The key walks through; keys are meant to turn locks, not stay in them.

The opener stays.

"Well," Odile said aloud, to no one, to the ice, to the sword ringing against their spine like a heart learning to beat. "We'll see about that."

Ahead, the glacier rose into a final white shoulder, and beyond the shoulder, faint as breath on glass, something glowed.
