## 16. The Price Written Twice

The child had three names, and none of them worked anymore.

Her first name was the one her mother had whispered into her hair in a language that was not Thornic but older than it, the name of the terraced gardens and the smell of rain on warm stone. She had lost that name somewhere on the other side of the world, or perhaps it had been taken from her, folded up like a letter and posted to a country that no longer existed. Her second name was the one Odile used: *little bird*, *trouble*, *girl-who-eats-all-the-cheese*. Those were not names exactly. They were handles, the way a cup has a handle — useful for carrying, not part of what the cup is.

Her third name was her true one, the oath-name, and she kept it locked behind her teeth like a stone she was saving. She would need it later. She did not know for what yet, only that the sword went quiet and attentive whenever the shape of it rose in her throat, the way a dog goes still when its person picks up a coat.

So: the child. That would do for now.

* * *

They had been walking on ice for six days, and the child had learned that ice has moods.

It could be sulky, hiding its crevasses under bridges of snow so that you stepped where you thought was safe and found nothing under your foot at all. It could be theatrical, booming and cracking all night like an old man complaining about his joints. Once — this was her favorite — it had been playful, ringing under their boots with a note so pure and high that Odile had stopped dead and said, "Don't tell anyone I enjoyed that," which the child had immediately filed away as ammunition.

Odile walked ahead of her always, testing each step with the reforged sword held point-down like a blind man's cane. The sword sang when they were near the door. It had been singing for two days now, a thin thread of sound below hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears. The child sang back sometimes, small nonsense songs about cheese and smugglers and the specific way Odile snored, and the sword's song bent around hers, pleased, like a cat leaning into a hand.

"You're humming," Odile said without turning around.

"I am communicating with ancient forces."

"Ancient forces can carry the second pack."

That was the thing about Odile that the child had decided never to say out loud, because saying it might break it: Odile was funny. Not on purpose, mostly. But underneath the gray weariness and the fifteen years of practiced invisibility there lived a person who named their boots (*Left* and *Also Left*) and who, when the child had once asked what the mountains were called, said, "Depends who's asking," and then, after a pause, "The old name means *the shoulders that carried us*, which is either beautiful or self-important, and I haven't decided."

The child loved her with a fierceness that frightened her sometimes, the way a fire frightens you when you realize you are made partly of wood.

* * *

On the seventh day the glacier opened its hand.

They came around a shoulder of blue ice and there it was: a wall of black stone rising out of the ice field, taller than any bell-tower in her memories, older-looking than anything the Chancery had ever burned. And set into the wall — half-buried, half-revealed, as if the mountain were deciding slowly whether to keep it or give it back — stood a door.

Not a door like doors are. There was no wood, no iron. It was a slab of something dark and faintly warm-colored, like embers seen through ash, and carved across its whole face in lines deep enough to hold shadow even at noon: writing.

Thornic. Pre-Imperial. Older than the Veil.

The child stopped breathing for a moment, because she knew these letters. Not from books — there were no books left — but from somewhere underneath knowing. She had seen them before the way you have seen your own face: every day, without ever looking.

"Odile." Her voice came out strange, flattened by awe. "It's the door."

"I know." Odile had gone very still, the way she did before violence, except this was not that kind of stillness. This was the stillness of someone standing in front of a grave they had dug themselves, long ago, and only now reading the headstone. "I know what it is, little bird. I've known since the tarn rang."

She drew closer to the carvings. The child watched her lips move, translating, and watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"What does it say?"

Odile didn't answer. So the child read it herself.

* * *

The letters moved.

That should have terrified her and instead felt like being greeted. As she looked, the lines of the carving shifted and settled, rearranging themselves from the old formal script into something simpler, plainer, the way a stern face softens when it recognizes you. The door was speaking to her specifically. To her mouth-shape, her breath, her buried true name.

And this is what it said:

*Here stands the Door of Ash, which was shut by those who feared what it opened. One may pass freely through it: any child of the sealed country, any voice that carries the tongue, may go home.*

*But a door must be held open from both sides. The one who unseals it — the one whose voice wakes it — becomes its anchor: bound between worlds, belonging wholly to neither, able to see both and touch neither fully, for as long as the Door stands open. A year. A century. Until the last word of the tongue is spoken by a living mouth.*

*This price is written twice because it must be understood twice: once with the eyes, and once with the heart.*

*Choose with both.*

The child read it three times. On the third reading, her eyes did the thing eyes do, blurring the letters into water, and she wiped her face on her sleeve with great dignity and pretended it was the wind off the glacier, which was genuinely very cold, so it was barely even a lie.

Then she thought: *I can go home.*

And immediately, underneath, quieter and much colder: *someone has to stay.*

* * *

She did what she always did when her chest got too full: she went to find Odile.

Odile had retreated to the edge of the ice field and was doing the thing with the map that wasn't really a map — staring at the horizon and chewing the inside of her cheek, calculating routes that didn't exist yet. Smuggler's arithmetic, the child called it. *If we can't go through, we go around; if we can't go around, we go through anyway but angrier.*

"How long?" the child asked.

Odile glanced at her. "How long what?"

"The door stays open. If someone anchors it. How long?"

"As long as the words are spoken." Odile said it lightly, the way she said dangerous things when she didn't want them examined. "As long as somebody, somewhere, keeps talking Thornic. Could be forever. Forever's just a long line of days, and I've done long lines of days."

Something in the child went very quiet and very clear, the way the tarn had been clear the morning they'd pulled the bell-song out of it.

"Read me the terms again," she said. "All of them. Exactly."

"Why?"

"Because I want to hear you say it."

Odile looked at her for a long moment — the child had learned to read those looks; this one was *I see where you're going and I am going to pretend I don't* — and then recited it, flat and careful, smuggler's voice, no feeling in it at all: "One passes freely. The opener remains. Bound between worlds. Belonging to neither."

"There," the child said. "You said *one* passes freely. You didn't say *the child*."

"Little bird—"

"It doesn't say the child. It says *one*. And it says the opener is the one whose voice wakes it, and—" she took a breath, and here it was, the stone behind her teeth, the true name she had been saving, rising up her throat like a bird finally understanding the window is open— "and my voice woke it, Odile. In the cave shrine. At the tarn. When I sing, the sword drinks it. It's *my* door. It's been waiting for *me*. Which means it's mine to open, and mine to hold, and—"

"No."

"—and you said yourself forever is just a long line of days, and I'm eight, Odile, I have *so many more days than you*, statistically speaking, if you want to be practical about it, which you always say you are—"

"*No.*"

Odile almost never raised her voice. The glacier seemed to lean in to listen. The sword at her hip gave one low, mournful note, like a dog that knows a fight is coming.

The child planted her feet in the snow. She had learned stubbornness from a master; she intended to honor her teacher.

"You hid for fifteen years," she said, and her voice shook and she let it, because some things should shake. "You buried your sword under a floor and buried your name under that and buried the whole language under both, and you did it so that *no one else would have to burn*. That's who you are. You are the person who pays so others don't have to. Well." She pointed at the door, black and patient and warm-hearted as a hearth. "That's my job now. I'm the person who opens things. I crossed over once already and I don't even remember doing it — I've been a door my whole life, I just didn't know what I was a door *of*. Let me do the one thing I was made for."

"Your job," Odile said, and her voice had gone low and terrible and gentle all at once, "is to be eight years old. Your job is green terraces and bell-towers and learning what your mother's garden smelled like again. Your job is *home*, little bird, and I will carry you through that threshold myself if I have to, and you will hate me for a decade and then you will understand, because that's how it works, that's exactly how it works—"

"That's what your oath-brothers said to *you*." The words came out before she could weigh them, and she saw them land, saw Odile take the hit like a blade slipping past a guard. "When the Veil fell. Someone told you to hide instead of fight, and you've spent fifteen years being *obedient* to people who aren't even alive anymore. I heard the sword hum when you dug it up. Swords remember oaths, Odile. Maybe it's time somebody remembered yours."

For a moment the wind was the only sound in the world.

Then Odile laughed — a short, broken, astonished sound — and sat down right there in the snow, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and put her face in her gloved hands.

"You," she said, muffled, "are the worst child ever smuggled across a mountain range."

"I'm the only child ever smuggled across this mountain range."

"That's why you're the worst. No competition." She lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which the child had learned meant the crying was happening somewhere deeper down, in a room visitors weren't shown. "You think I don't want to give you this? You think there's a version of me that hears *bound between worlds forever* and thinks *yes, the child, perfect*?" She shook her head slowly. "I have done my time being nobody, little bird. Fifteen years of salt roads and false names and biting my tongue bloody every night so I wouldn't sing in my sleep. And I will tell you a secret, since you've earned it: I never minded. Not really. Because the order was dead and the country was gone and there was nothing left to belong to anyway. Being between worlds costs nothing when you've already lost the world you were born in."

She stood. She brushed off her knees with great ceremony, buying time, and the child let her have it, because she had also learned mercy from a master.

"But you," Odile went on, "have a whole country over there waiting to remember its own name. Terraces. Bells. Whatever your mother's garden smelled like. You get to go *be* that. And I get to stand here and watch the light of it come through the ice every morning for the rest of my life, which, I'll have you know, is a considerably better bargain than the one I had yesterday."

"It's not fair."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't. Doors never are. That's why they're doors and not windows — you have to choose to walk through them." She held out her hand. "Come look at the terms with me one more time. Properly. Together. And then we'll argue about it more on the walk, because I know you, and you've got at least four arguments left, and honestly one of them is pretty good."

The child took her hand. Her fingers were cold; Odile's were colder; between them they made almost one warm person.

They walked toward the door together, and the carvings brightened as they came, ember-warm, welcoming, patient as stone, patient as grief, patient as love.

And that was when the child saw, far down the white slope behind them, a single figure moving fast against the glare — running, stumbling, waving both arms, wearing colors that did not belong on a glacier and a face the child had only seen in Odile's worst silences.

Someone was coming. Someone was coming *fast*, and the door had just begun to glow.
