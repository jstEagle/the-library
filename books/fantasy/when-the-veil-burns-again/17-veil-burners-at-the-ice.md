## 17. Veil-Burners at the Ice

Maren Voss had burned a great many things in her fifteen years with the Chancery, and she had learned to read fire the way sailors read weather. A clean burn—paper, ink, a heretic's library—crackled bright and finished quickly, leaving ash you could sweep away by morning. A dirty burn—wet wood, green branches, a village's winter stores—smoldered sullenly and stank for days.

The order that arrived at her field camp on the glacier's southern shoulder was a dirty burn of a different kind. It came sealed in the Chancellor's own wax, which meant it had been written by someone who wanted it deniable, and it said, in the Chancery's favorite bloodless dialect:

*Inquisitor Voss is relieved of command pending review. Captain Aldery assumes operational authority. Subject O.S. to be taken alive if practicable. The child is not to be taken alive.*

Not *the subject*. Not even *the asset*. *The child.* Someone in the upper offices had stopped pretending this was about language.

Maren read it twice, standing in the wind with the paper trying to tear itself out of her hands, and felt something in her chest that she recognized from long ago—the specific cold clarity that came just before an oath broke.

She had been raised on oaths. Everyone in the old country had been; that was rather the point of them. You swore your blade to a hearth, your voice to a truth, and the words held you the way a riverbank holds water—not as a cage but as a shape. Then the Veil came down like a lid, and the Chancery taught a new kind of oath: swear to us, or be unmade quietly.

She had sworn it. She'd been twenty-two, bleeding, half-mad with grief, standing in the rain beside Odile Sandoval while the last bell of Thornfell drowned in its tarn behind them, and they had both put their hands on the Chancery's iron and promised.

Odile had broken that promise within a year. Maren had kept hers for fifteen, and it had rotted in her mouth so gradually she hadn't noticed the taste until now.

"Reading material, Inquisitor?" Captain Aldery stood at her shoulder without having walked there, which was his particular talent. He was a broad, pink, cheerful man with the eyes of a butcher counting stock. "Anything interesting?"

"The usual," Maren said. "Paperwork."

"Your paperwork has been very interesting lately." He smiled. "A shrine position reported three days ago. Empty when we arrived. Tracks leading north. Your report placed the fugitives two days south of that." He let the silence do its work. "Sloppy, Voss. Or generous. Hard to say."

"Hard terrain makes hard arithmetic," Maren said. "You'll find the passes lie more than the maps do."

"I don't doubt it." He turned to look up the glacier, white and vast and patient, the Door of Ash buried somewhere under all that ice like a coal under snow. "We have two hundred veil-burners, four resonance-casters, and a writ that lets me do whatever I decide is necessary. Whatever you did or didn't do, Inquisitor, I'd think carefully about whose side of the arithmetic you want to stand on."

He walked away whistling. That was the worst part. Aldery whistled while he worked, always something tuneless, and Maren thought of every village choir the Chancery had silenced because singing was how contagion spread, and wondered how he'd feel about being arrested for humming.

* * *

She spent the evening doing what she did best, which was reading people the way other inquisitors read documents.

Two hundred veil-burners sounded impressive around a campfire and looked considerably less impressive stretched along a glacier in a rising wind. They were mostly young. Mostly frightened, though they hid it under procedure. She moved through the camp with a cup of terrible tea, asking small questions, and the answers assembled themselves into a picture:

They believed the child was a weapon. That was the story handed down from the Chancery's upper floors, and it had the elegant self-sealing quality of all good propaganda: a child who spoke a dead tongue fluently could only be a vessel, a construct, a thing grown in some enemy's cellar. Nobody asked why a weapon would be eight years old and barefoot and, according to the one scout who'd glimpsed her before the shrine went empty, carrying a carved wooden bird like it was made of gold.

Nobody except, apparently, Maren herself, who had been asking it silently for weeks and had finally run out of places to put the question.

She found Sergeant Ilex at the picket line, checking a mule's hooves by lantern light. Ilex was fifty, gray, and had been burning books since before Maren was commissioned. He was also, she happened to know, the only man in the company who still hummed, very quietly, when he thought no one was listening—and the tune was old. Older than the Chancery. Old enough that it might once have had words.

"Sergeant," she said. "Walk with me."

They walked out past the tent lines, onto the blue ice where the wind took their voices and scattered them.

"You're going to ask me something you shouldn't," Ilex said.

"I'm going to tell you something first, and then you can decide whether to arrest me."

"That's a novel recruiting technique."

"It's not recruitment." Maren stopped and faced him. The lantern light caught the frost in his eyebrows. "It's confession. Three days ago I filed a false position report. I've been misdirecting this operation since the shrine. And in approximately six hours, when Aldery's vanguard reaches the Door, I intend to make that official."

Ilex was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere behind them, Aldery whistled his tuneless nothing.

"Why?" he asked. Not *how dare you*, not *do you know what they'll do to you*. Why. Which told her she'd chosen correctly.

"Because I've read the sealed archives," Maren said. "Fifteen years of clearance buys you access to the room nobody wants to visit. The Veil wasn't built to protect us from Thornic sorcery. It was built to seal a country *out of existence*—a whole nation, locked behind a door and struck from every map, because they refused to kneel. There were children inside when it closed, Ilex. Children who grew old inside. And now one child has come back out through a crack in the seal, and the Chancery's answer is a writ that says—" Her voice caught on it, even now. "*Not to be taken alive.*"

Ilex looked at the glacier. Then at her. Then, briefly, at his own hands, as if checking whether they still belonged to him.

"My grandmother used to sing," he said at last. "Three notes over and over. Said her mother taught her, and her mother said it was from over the mountains, from a place that didn't exist. We laughed at her. Thought the old woman was touched." He cleared his throat. "Last spring I caught myself humming it to my granddaughter. Didn't know where I'd got it from. Words come back funny that way. Like seeds in a boot heel."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's exactly what the Chancery is afraid of."

"And what are you afraid of?"

"That I'm too late," Maren said. "And that I'm not."

* * *

Defection, it turned out, was mostly logistics.

She had imagined it would feel like a door slamming—a grand renunciation, a sword thrown down. Instead it felt like packing a bag in the dark and lying to a supply clerk about rations, which was, she supposed, fitting. Treason as conscience was not a bonfire. It was a slow, careful theft of yourself from an institution that had never noticed you were in it.

By midnight she had: a map of Aldery's route (he kept it in his saddlebag and checked it obsessively, which made stealing it trivially easy during the changing of the watch); a resonance-caster's tuning fork, which would let her spoof the company's own signal codes; and, after a brief internal argument she lost, a borrowed mule named Turnip, on the grounds that the alternative was walking forty miles of ice and she was fifty-three years old and had nothing left to prove.

She left the map on Aldery's desk. Let him know exactly who had beaten him. Some part of her—the part that had once been a shield-sister, the part that remembered laughing—wanted him to understand that this wasn't betrayal. Betrayal implied the Chancery had ever been worthy of the word loyalty.

Then she rode north into the dark, and the wind came off the ice fields like a wall, and she bent over Turnip's neck and thought about Odile Sandoval.

Fifteen years since she'd seen them. Fifteen years since the rain and the iron and the oath. She had spent those years telling herself a story: that Odile was weak, that they'd run because they couldn't bear the discipline of the new world, that hiding in the mountains trading smuggler salt was a kind of moral failure. It had been a comfortable story. Comfortable stories usually are.

But she'd seen the bootprint outside the cabin—small, barefoot, pressed deep in the mud by the woodpile—and she'd stood there for ten full minutes in the rain, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do. A child, living with Odile Sandoval. Fed, shod eventually, taught. The last swordspeaker, who had every reason to hand the girl over and buy back their amnesty, instead building her a life one splintery kindness at a time.

And Maren had written her false report, and gone home, and sat by her fire, and understood that she had spent fifteen years calling Odile a coward for doing the single bravest thing she had ever witnessed.

"Hurry up, you stubborn woman," she muttered to the mule, or possibly to herself. "Both of you."

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold over the ice, and with it came the sound.

At first she thought it was the glacier settling—that deep groaning the ice made when it dreamed. But glaciers groaned downward, into the earth. This sound rose. It climbed the air in long silver threads, and Turnip stopped dead and laid his ears back, and Maren's resonance-fork, wrapped in cloth in her pack, began to buzz against the buckles like a trapped insect.

The bell. The drowned bell in the high tarn, or whatever remained of its echo, carried down the ice. Ringing in Thornic.

Maren sat in the saddle with frost forming on her eyelashes and listened to a dead language toll across a dead country's grave, and she laughed—actually laughed, a short cracked sound the wind stole immediately—because it was beautiful, and because she knew precisely what it meant.

Every resonance-map in the company had just lit up like a festival sky.

She spurred Turnip forward. Behind her, far to the south, horns began to sound—Aldery's vanguard, moving to the signal, two hundred veil-burners wheeling toward the Door like iron filings toward a magnet. Ahead of her, somewhere past the seracs and the crevasse fields, the bell rang again, and beneath it now, fainter, another sound: a sword being drawn for the second time in fifteen years.

She knew that sound too. Every oath-bound soldier did. It was the noise a promise makes when it stops being broken.

The ice field ahead split into a maze of blue ridges, and beyond them, impossibly far and impossibly close, she saw light—not sunrise, not reflection, but a vertical seam of gold burning up through the glacier like a crack in the floor of heaven.

The Door of Ash. Open. Or opening.

Maren Voss, traitor, inquisitor, shield-sister, drove her heels into a mule named Turnip and rode toward the battle that would decide whether any of it—the country, the child, the words, the whole buried shape of a people—burned again, or bloomed.

Behind her, gaining, came the horns. Ahead of her, thin and clear across the ice, a child's voice was singing her grandmother's grandmother's song.

"For once," Maren said out loud, to no one, to everyone, "let me be on time."
