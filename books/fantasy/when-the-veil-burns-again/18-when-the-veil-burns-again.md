## 18. When the Veil Burns Again

The cold on the glacier had a texture to it, Odile had learned over the past two days. Not one cold but many: the dry needle-cold of wind off the ice cap, the wet clinging cold that came up from the crevasses like breath from a grave, and now a third kind, rolling down the eastern flank in a grey tide—the cold of too many bodies moving fast in armor.

They were coming. Of course they were coming.

"Odile." The girl's voice cut through the wind, thin and bright as a bell wire. She stood at the threshold of the Door of Ash—actually *in* it, half of her flickering at the edges like a candle seen through smoked glass—and she had stopped singing only long enough to say Odile's name. Which was, itself, a small miracle. The song could not be interrupted; Odile had understood that somewhere around the second hour. It was less a song than a loom, and the child was both weaver and thread, singing green terraces and bell-towers back into the space where a country used to be. But she had bent the loom aside for one word. For a name.

"I'm here," Odile said.

"You're bleeding."

"It's mostly other people's."

"That's not comforting."

"No," Odile admitted, "I've never been good at comforting. I'm told I'm excellent at scowling, though. Watch this."

She turned east and scowled at the advancing company of the Chancery, forty veil-burners in ash-grey lacquer, their torch-staves guttering with stolen fire, and behind them the resonance-wagons with their brass throats pointed at the Door like cannons aimed at a hymn.

*Forty.* Odile had a reforged sword, an oath fifteen years out of practice, a shield-sister who might or might not arrive before the killing started, and a child who couldn't stop singing long enough to duck.

On balance, thought Odile Sandoval, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, the scowl was doing most of the work.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about speaking the full oath after fifteen years of silence: your mouth remembers the shape of the words better than your heart remembers why you abandoned them.

Odile had buried the language the way they'd buried the sword—carefully, in the dark, under the hearthstone of a life that wasn't really a life. And now the words came up out of them like water finding an old channel, worn smooth by centuries of other mouths, and every single one of them hurt.

Not the way a wound hurts. The way a home hurts when you come back and find the furniture exactly where you left it.

"*Ashen-vel,*" they said, and the sword woke.

It had been drinking the child's lessons for days now, there in the cave shrine, humming like a struck bowl whenever she corrected Odile's grammar—which was often, because Odile's Thornic had rust in it, and the girl was a merciless little tutor. ("No. *That* word means 'the rain that forgives,' not 'the rain that forgets.' They're completely different rains, Odile.") Now the blade took the first oath-word and turned it into light along its whole broken-and-mended length, gold running through the seams like sap through birch bark.

The nearest veil-burner—a big man, young under his visor, probably told he was burning contagion—stumbled to a halt and stared at the light as though he'd never seen anything honest in his life.

He probably hadn't, Odile reflected. That was rather the Chancery's whole business model.

They came anyway, because soldiers do, because orders are easier than questions, because the wagons behind them were chanting counterwords in dead Imperial cadence, trying to smother the child's song the way you'd smother a fire with wet blankets. Odile met the first spear with a parry that sang, met the second with a step their old swordmaster would have praised and then billed them for, and found—to their genuine surprise—that the body remembered this too.

Fifteen years of smuggler roads had taught Odile a hundred ways to avoid a fight. It turned out all of those ways made the fighting shorter.

"*Veth-kalai,*" said the second oath-word, which meant roughly *I stand between*, and between was where Odile stood, between the grey tide and the singing child, and the sword blazed so bright that shadows fled across the glacier like startled birds.

But forty is forty. And Odile was one, and fifty-three, and had been quietly expecting to die of bad tea and older knees in a cabin that smelled of pine smoke. The line bent. The line buckled. A torch-staff kissed Odile's shoulder through the coat and the world went white and smelling of scorched wool.

And then the horns arrived.

* * *

Later, Odile would try to describe what Maren Voss looked like riding out of the blowing snow at the head of absolutely no one, alone, having ridden her mule nearly to death, and would fail every time, because the true description was simply: *on time.*

"For once," Maren announced, dismounting onto the ice with the grace of a woman falling downstairs in slow motion, "I am punctual. Note it in the record. Someone note it in the record."

"There's no record," Odile said, back to back with her before either of them decided to be, blades up, breath steaming. "Records burn. That's sort of your whole thing."

"My *former* whole thing." Maren's inquisitor's blade—a Chancery issue, veined with suppression-silver—met the first attacker's stroke and threw it wide. "I've resigned. Verbally. To several people. Some of them are trying to kill me, which I choose to interpret as acceptance of my resignation."

"You're insane."

"Fifteen years late, but yes." Maren laughed—actually laughed, wild and cracked and more like crying than Odile had ever heard from her—and fought.

And oh, it was ugly, and oh, it was glorious. Two shield-sisters of a dead order holding a line that was not a line but a doorway, giving ground by inches, taking it back by inches, the sword and the silver blade crossing and uncrossing above the ice while the child's song climbed and climbed. Odile spoke the third word—"*selu-mar,* the debt remembered"—and felt it go through them like a bone-deep warmth, and beside them Maren, who had never been oath-bound, who had burned oaths professionally for a decade and a half, began without seeming to notice it herself to keep time with the song, her boots striking the ice on the beat.

"They can't smother it," Maren shouted over the clangor, nodding toward the wagons. "I checked the resonance maps. Her voice isn't *on* the frequencies anymore. It's *under* them. It's in the bedrock of everything. They're trying to blanket a mountain range with a tablecloth."

"Good," Odile gasped, and took a shaft of counterword-fire on the flat of the blazing sword, and felt the fire die against the gold like frost against a hearthstone. "*Then let her sing louder.*"

As if she heard—as if she had always heard, as if the loom and the line and the two battered swordspeakers before the door were all one weave now—the child's voice rose.

* * *

What she sang, Odile did not fully understand. It was pre-Imperial Thornic, the deep strata of it, the words that predated kingdoms, and it poured out of her in a flood: the names of rivers that had been unwritten, the bell-tolls of towers unmade, the green terraces climbing hillsides that no map had carried for fifteen years. And as she sang, the glacier *answered*. Light moved beneath the ice—not the grey flicker of the Door's edge, but color. Green. The impossible, outrageous green of growing things, spreading under the glacier like spring under a frozen lake, terraces ghosting up through sixty feet of ancient ice, and a bell-tower, far off, translucent as memory, and—

and a bell, ringing.

Every veil-burner on the ice heard it. Odile watched them hear it. Watched a company of forty trained instruments of erasure stand in the ringing shadow of everything they'd been sent to erase, and waver.

Some of them lowered their staves. One knelt. The big young man Odile had faced first simply sat down on the ice, visor up, tears freezing in his lashes, mouthing words he didn't know he knew—because nobody's blood forgets a country, that was the Chancery's secret shame, that was why they burned words instead of just forbidding them, because forbidden words sleep and burned words haunt.

The wagons chanted harder. The commander—Odile never learned his name, a tall grey figure with a voice like a filing cabinet—screamed the advance, screamed about contagion, screamed, and maybe twenty still obeyed him, and twenty was still more than two shield-sisters and a mule named Turnip.

So Odile did the thing they had sworn, fifteen years ago in the ashes, never to do again.

They spoke the whole oath.

All of it. Every word. From "*ashen-vel*" to the closing line that only three living mouths had ever finished, the vow that bound blade to speaker and speaker to the people the blade protected, the vow that had died with the old country and now, absurdly, impossibly, in the wind and the ringing and the green light under the ice, refused to stay dead. Odile's voice cracked on the high phrases and held on the low ones, and the sword drank each word like rain on drought-cracked earth, and by the end the blade was not gold but white, white as the first morning of a new country, and the oath hung in the air over the glacier complete and alive and *legal*, in the oldest sense of the word, binding.

The remaining veil-burners stopped as if they'd hit glass.

Because you cannot burn a lit oath. There are rules older than the Chancery. There have *always* been rules older than the Chancery; the Chancery's founding sin was believing otherwise.

"Right," said Maren, into the ringing silence, and spat blood, and grinned at Odile sideways with her split lip. "You always did show off at ceremonies."

"You should see the reception," Odile said.

* * *

Beyond the threshold, the child was finishing.

Odile turned—they couldn't help turning, no one could have helped it—and saw her silhouetted in the Door's opening, small and barefoot still, because she had crossed the passes barefoot and would evidently cross out of existence the same way, on principle. The grey flicker at the edges of her had resolved into something else: she was becoming *clearer*, not fainter, as if existence were finally focusing on her properly. Behind her, through the opening, there were hills. Terraces. A tower. Smoke rising gently from chimneys, or perhaps it was mist; perhaps it was the same thing now, in a country being sung back together.

The song ended the way a river ends—not stopping, arriving.

And the country breathed.

There was no other word for it. The light through the Door swelled, and somewhere beyond, faint but unmistakable, bells rang in towers that existed again, and Odile felt the anchor-pull begin in their own chest, a slow tidal hooking behind the ribs, the price written twice coming due, and knew—with the strange calm of a person standing at the exact center of their life—that the door wanted a keeper.

The child turned. Looked at Odile. And smiled, and opened her mouth to sing the last verse—the one that named the anchor, the one that would bind whoever it named forever between worlds—and Odile saw, with horror arriving like ice water, whose name was in her mouth.

"*No—*" Odile lunged for the threshold. "*Don't you dare—little one, don't you—*"

Behind them, Maren caught the first of the commander's loyalists rushing the Door, steel ringing, screaming something about traitors and miracles in the same breath. The horns sounded again, closer, confused, the company breaking apart into men kneeling and men charging and men simply weeping on the ice. The bell-tower beyond the Door swung into another peal.

And the child, eight years old, barefoot on the border of two worlds, drew the breath that would bind her to an eternity of standing in a doorway—and sang the first syllable of a name.

It was not hers.

It was Odile's.
