## 19. The Choosing

The wind stopped.

Not gentled—stopped, the way a held breath stops, and in that sudden hush Odile heard everything they had not been hearing for an hour: the creak of their own frozen leathers, the tick of cooling steel, and beneath it all, rising out of the Door of Ash like heat from a hearthstone, singing.

The Veil was burning again. Not with fire—with *language*. The old oath-words poured off the blade in Odile's hands and poured back through the threshold, and where they met, the air itself came apart in ribbons of gold and green that should not have existed above a glacier at the roof of the world.

The Chancery company lay broken across the ice. Not slaughtered—Odile had made sure of that, even now, even with the sword blazing in a way it had never blazed fifteen years ago, because the words the child had taught it were older than killing—but scattered, disarmed, kneeling in drifts of snow gone luminous with spilled enchantment. Their veil-burner engines had become so much expensive scrap metal. Somewhere among them, Maren Voss stood with her saber planted point-down in the ice like a pilgrim's staff, her breath ragged, her face turned toward the Door with an expression Odile had last seen on her the day the old country fell: awe fighting grief for control of the same mouth.

And the child—

The child stood in the threshold itself, one bare foot on the glacier's ice and one on whatever lay beyond, and she was *singing*, and the country behind her was coming back.

Odile could see it. That was the impossible thing, the thing they would spend the rest of however long this lasted trying to describe to Voss over bad tea: through the archway there was no longer void, no longer the grey sealing-mist the Chancery had poured over the Door when they erased Thornmark from every map and memory. There were terraces. Green ones, stepped down a hillside that could not possibly be on the other side of a wall of ice, terraces Odile remembered from before, from childhood visits with their mother, from liturgies sung in courtyards. A bell-tower. The bell-tower. Its bell swung without sound yet, waiting, patient as only bells and grandmothers are patient.

"Odile." The girl's voice came thin across the ice, worn down by an hour of unbroken song. She had been singing since the first word of the oath left Odile's lips, and she was eight years old, and she had not stopped, and Odile did not know how much more of her there was to give. "It's working. It's working, but—" A hitch in the melody. A wrong note. "But something's holding it. Something has to hold it."

Odile knew. They had known since the tarn, since the drowned bell rang and the resonance-maps of half the continent turned toward them like heads turning toward a scream. They had known since the carvings above the threshold, which the child had read aloud with growing confusion and then growing horror, her finger tracing terms in pre-Imperial Thornic while Odile translated the mathematics underneath: *one passes freely; one remains; the door requires a hand on either side, forever.*

An anchor. The Door needed an anchor. Someone bound between worlds, belonging wholly to neither, holding the passage open from this side while the country reassembled itself on the other.

The child had understood it before Odile did. Of course she had. It was written in her own tongue.

"No," Odile said now, crossing the ice, sword trailing light, boots crunching on frost that glittered like crushed glass. "Whatever you're about to propose, the answer is no."

"You haven't heard it."

"I heard you take a breath. I've raised smugglers. I know what a breath before a terrible proposal sounds like."

That got her—the flicker of a grin, there and gone, a small human thing in the middle of all this vastness. Fifteen years Odile had kept themselves empty of exactly this feeling, and now it arrived all at once, uninvited, enormous: the fierce, unreasonable tenderness that parents and fools call love and would defend to the death under either name.

"I have to stay," the girl said. "I'm the key. You said it yourself. The Door knows me. It opened for my voice, not yours."

"The Door opened for your voice *and* my sword," Odile said. "We're a matched set. And the anchor doesn't need to be the key. Read it again. Slowly. You're eight; you have an excuse. I don't."

"Odile—"

"It says *the opener may pass freely*." They said it gently, which cost them. Gentleness always had. "You opened it. You pass freely. Into your country. Where there are terraces, and bell-towers, and presumably someone who has been worrying about you for however long you were on the wrong side of the world, barefoot, in winter, knocking on the door of a grumpy stranger."

"But you'd be *alone*."

"I've been alone for fifteen years. I'm very good at it. I have a system. There's a schedule."

The girl laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh—and then her face crumpled, and she threw herself forward, and Odile caught her, sword and all, light flaring around them both like a struck match, and held on.

They stood like that for a long moment. Behind them, Voss turned her back with the elaborate courtesy of a woman who had once been a shield-sister and knew precisely what a shield-sister does not watch.

* * *

There was time. That was the strange mercy of it. The Door, once open, stayed open—not eagerly, but steadily, like a well-damped fire—and the country on the far side was rebuilding itself at the speed of a song being remembered, verse by verse. The girl had to finish the song. Odile had to hold the anchor-thread until the last verse settled. Neither of those things happened quickly, and so, absurdly, in the middle of a battlefield at the top of a glacier, they had time to talk.

They sat against the threshold stone, shoulder to shoulder, sharing a heel of black bread from Odile's pack because the child had insisted that no one crosses into a restored nation on an empty stomach, and also because she was eight and hungry, and restoring a kingdom is hungry work.

"Do you remember," she said, mouth full, "when you wouldn't even say hello to me in Thornic? First night? You said 'we do not speak that language in this house' and gave me soup?"

"I remember being extremely reasonable about a deeply alarming situation."

"You were scared."

"I was *prudent*."

"You were scared," she repeated, with the serene authority of a child who has never once lost an argument to an adult and does not intend to start at the end of the world. "Your face went all pinchy. Like Brannick's when someone asked him about his taxes."

Odile looked out over the ice. Brannick. Old fence, old friend, dead three days and forty miles south, buying their escape with a cartload of fireworks and a smuggler's grin. He'd have hated this view. Too cold, nothing to sell. He'd have loved what it was *for*. He'd have charged the Chancery double just for dying near it.

"He'd have liked you," Odile said. "He liked anyone who ate his bread without complaining."

"It was terrible bread."

"It was *provisioning*. Don't insult the fallen."

She giggled, and the sound carried strangely across the ice, and somewhere in the kneeling ranks of the broken company a soldier looked up at the noise—an ordinary soldier, young, frost-nipped, listening to a dead language spoken alive—and did not report it. Odile saw him decide not to. Saw him look away, deliberately, and pull his hood up. There would be many such decisions in the years ahead, Odile suspected. Words, once loosed, are contagious. The Chancery had been right about that all along. They'd simply been wrong about which side of it they wanted to stand on.

Voss came over as the light began to fail—which up here meant the light began to go gold and long and low across the ice, gilding the terraces beyond the Door until they looked painted. She moved stiffly. There was a burn along her jaw from the veilsword discharge, and her left hand was wrapped in a strip of her own cloak, and she walked like a woman who had recently committed treason and found it agreed with her less than expected.

"So," she said. "You're doing it. The anchor."

"I'm doing it."

"You understand what it means." Not a question; an inquisitor's framing. But her voice was careful, the way voices go careful around wounds. "Bound between worlds. Both and neither. Forever is a long contract, Odi. I've read some of them."

"Fifteen years ago I watched you swear one to an order that erased a country," Odile said. "Forever can't be worse than that."

Voss flinched. Then, to Odile's lasting surprise, she nodded, as if the blow had been fair and honestly struck. "No," she said quietly. "It can't." She crouched, wincing, to be level with the child. "And you. Little key. You'll look after her? Him. Them." A grimace. "Fifteen years and I still can't get the grammar right."

"They," said the girl solemnly, "and yes. I'll send letters. Through the door. When it's wide enough."

"And I'll read them," Voss said, "in the original, if I have to learn like a schoolboy. Which I will. Because somebody in the Chancery is going to want a full account of why their company broke, and I intend to give them an account so boring and so thorough that no one ever asks me anything again." She paused. "Also I'm staying in the valley. Permanently. As a witness. Officially to monitor residual linguistic contamination." Her mouth twitched. "Unofficially because someone has to guard a door, and I'm told the current management is otherwise engaged."

"Maren—"

"Don't," Voss said. "Don't make it a moment. I'll cry, and then you'll make it worse by pretending not to notice, and then the child will make it *even* worse by noticing loudly. We don't have time for our worst habits." She straightened, offered her wrapped hand, and Odile took it, and for a heartbeat they were two swordspeakers of a dead order gripping forearms over an open door to an un-dead one, and neither of them said anything at all, because some oaths renew themselves without words.

Then Voss walked away to see to the prisoners, because someone had to, and that had always been Maren's whole tragedy and whole glory: someone has to.

* * *

The last verse came at dusk.

The girl sang it standing in the threshold, both feet on the far side now, her voice steadying as the country firmed around her—as the terraces took on shadow and depth, as windows lit in the bell-tower one by one like lamps being answered down a street, as figures appeared, distant at first, then less distant, running down the hillside paths. People. Actual people, wearing actual coats, shouting things in Thornic that Odile had not heard shouted in joy since before the ashfall. A woman at the front of the running crowd had the girl's same dark eyes and the same stubborn set to her chin, and she was screaming one word over and over, and the word was the girl's name, and Odile finally learned it after months of calling her *sprout*, *small one*, and, once, memorably, *you, the barefoot hazard*.

It was a good name. Odile resolved to use it, later, through the door, in letters.

"Odile!" The girl spun at the threshold, the anchor-light already gathering around Odile's hands where they gripped the sword planted in the stone—warm, insistent, a leash and a lifeline both. "Take my hand! While you still—"

"I can't cross, sprout. Anchor, remember? One foot each side, forever. It's in the contract. Very clear. Terrible drafting."

"But—"

"Listen to me." Odile pitched their voice the way they had on the storm night, at the cabin door, when terror wore irritation's coat: steady, dry, immovable. "You are going to walk through that door, and you are going to eat something that isn't my terrible provisioning, and you are going to grow up in a country that speaks your name correctly, and every solstice you are going to come to this threshold, and I am going to be here, because I am *extremely* reliable and *terribly* stubborn, and you are going to tell me everything. Everything. Every terrace. Every bell. I want inventories. I want complaints about the food. I want to know if the king—does the old country still have a king? Never mind. I want a full constitutional report."

The girl was crying. So, in flagrant violation of several personal policies, was Odile.

"I don't want to leave you alone," she whispered.

"Small one," Odile said, "look behind you."

She looked. The crowd had reached the base of the hill. The woman was nearly there. Lanterns were coming out along the terraces, dozens of them, lighting the green country gold against the blue of evening, and the bell in the tower began—at last, at last—to ring, and the sound rolled through the Door and across the glacier and up into the bones of the mountain, and every soldier of the broken company rose to their feet without being ordered, because some sounds compel standing.

"That," Odile said, "is the opposite of alone. Go home. Be the living key. Grow up loud."

She crossed the last step. The woman caught her up and swung her around and the bell rang and rang, and the girl looked back over her mother's shoulder one last time, and mouthed something in Thornic that Odile felt rather than heard, the way you feel a struck tuning fork through your palm:

*I'll write. Write back.*

And then the light took her, gently, the way dusk takes a hillside, and the Door of Ash stood open between two worlds with a bell ringing through it, and Odile Sandoval—last of the oath-bound, first of the anchored—stood at the center of it with a blazing sword in their fists and frost in their beard and tears freezing cheerfully on their face.

"Well," they said to nobody, in Thornic, out loud, on purpose, for the second time in fifteen years and the first time of the rest of forever. "That's done, then."

The sword hummed agreement. Somewhere south, a former inquisitor was building a guard-post within sight of the glacier. Somewhere north, a country was relighting its lamps.

Somewhere inside Odile's chest, quiet as a banked coal, something long-frozen began, very slowly, to thaw.

The first letter arrived nine days later.
