## 20. Between Two Doors

The first thing I learned about being an anchor was that no one warns you about the cold.

Not the glacier's cold—that had ended, or rather it had stopped mattering, the way a toothache stops mattering once the tooth is gone. The cold I mean is the cold of standing in two places at once, of having your feet planted in one world while the rest of you keeps drifting toward the other like smoke toward a chimney draft.

I had expected pain. The threshold carvings had promised pain, written twice, in two hands, in two languages, as if the old country's scribes had wanted to be very sure no one could later claim they hadn't been told. *One may pass freely. The one who opens must remain.* I had read those words with a child's mittened hand gripping my sleeve and a company of veil-burners coming up the ice behind us, and I had thought: *pain, yes, I can budget for pain.* Fifteen years in the Thornfells teaches you to budget for pain the way merchants budget for salt.

What I had not budgeted for was the flickering.

* * *

It began that first night, while Maren Voss built a fire from the shattered remains of a Chancery supply sledge and the child—Aurel, her name was Aurel, she had told me at last on the ice, in a voice that cracked on the second syllable because she was crying too hard to say it properly—walked through the Door and did not come back.

I had expected to feel the severing. Instead I felt the opposite: a second heartbeat, arriving like a guest who had been standing outside in the snow for hours too polite to knock.

"Odile." Voss's voice, from across the fire. "You've gone strange around the eyes."

"I'm always strange around the eyes. You told me so in '41, after the Kellin bridge."

"I said you'd gone *stranger* around the eyes." She fed the fire a splintered sledge-runner. "Stranger than that."

She was right, of course. She was always right; it had made her insufferable at twenty and made her the only inquisitor in the Chancery's history to burn her own commission papers on a glacier, so I supposed it evened out. What she was seeing was this: I sat by the fire with my hands around a tin cup of snowmelt, and at the same time I did not sit by the fire at all. I stood in a green place. Terraces climbing a hillside in the old pattern, the seven-fold spiral the Thornic farmers cut into every slope because they believed water, like oaths, should be made to travel a long way before it arrived. Bell-towers. Gods, the bell-towers—there had been forty-one of them in the capital alone, and I could see three of them from where I stood in a world that was not standing anywhere, that was only light through water, the restored kingdom flickering at the edge of my vision like a lantern hung behind a curtain of ice.

Aurel had sung the country back. I had heard it happen—heard the sword drink the full oath out of my mouth and blaze until the glacier itself seemed to lean away, heard her voice rise past mine into a register no child should have been able to hold, and then the Door had opened, and she had gone through, and the country on the other side had *caught*, the way a fire catches, the way an oath catches.

And now I was its anchor, which meant I was its window, which meant that every few minutes, whether I wanted it or not, the veil between the worlds thinned and I saw.

"Describe it," Voss said. She had her notebook out—the Chancery-trained part of her that would never die, taking notes by firelight on a glacier while her former superiors presumably organized her execution. "The flickering. What do you see?"

"Terraces," I said. "Green. There's someone walking up the third terrace with a— I think it's a goat. Or a very ambitious dog."

"Useful. That narrows it down."

"Maren."

"Odile." She looked at me over the fire, and her face did the thing it had been doing since the battle, the thing where the inquisitor's mask kept sliding and she kept putting it back and it kept sliding, and finally, somewhere around the moment she had put her sword between me and a veil-burner's halberd, she had stopped putting it back at all. "If you're going to be a bridge between two worlds, someone has to take notes on the traffic."

I laughed. It surprised us both. I think it was the first time I'd laughed since the pass, since Brannick, since the word *Thornic* had come out of my mouth like a blade coming out of a wound—and it hurt, laughing, the way frostbitten fingers hurt when they warm, which is to say it hurt because something was coming back to life.

* * *

The flickering had a rhythm. I learned it the way you learn a smuggler's road: by walking it wrong until the wrongness teaches you.

It came strongest at the edges of sleep. I would close my eyes by the fire—or later, in the shrine-cave where we retreated from the wind, or later still in the half-collapsed waystation below the Door that Voss, with the cheerful ruthlessness of a woman who had spent a decade making people disappear, declared "defensible"—and I would be there. Not dreaming *of* it. Dreaming *in* it. The smell of the terraces after rain, which is a smell I had buried so deep in myself for fifteen years that finding it again was like digging up my own grave and discovering I'd been alive in there the whole time. The sound of the bells, all forty-one, ringing the hour in the old count that started at seven because the Thornic believed the day began with the seventh hour, when the light got honest.

And once—only once that first week—I heard her.

Aurel. Small and clear, somewhere up a hillside I couldn't climb because I had no hillside, I had a body anchored to a glacier on the wrong side of everything. She was singing. A counting song, the one smugglers' children sang on the Thornfell roads without knowing they were singing Thornic, the one about the magpie and the seven doors. I had heard human children mangle it in three languages. She sang it the way it was written, the way it had been sung before there were maps to erase, and the sound of it went through me like the sword going through me on the ice, and I sat up in the dark of the waystation with tears freezing on my face, and Voss, who slept like a cat and woke like a knife, said:

"She's all right, then."

"She's singing."

"Good." A pause. The fire ticked. "Is that good? You look like a woman who's been told good news by someone hitting her with it."

"It's good," I said. "It's—Maren, I can hear her. Across the Door. I can hear her and I will never touch her hand again, and both of those things are true at the same time, and I don't know where to put that."

Voss considered this for a long moment, in the way she considered everything, as though it were a lock she intended to pick.

"Put it in the ledger," she said finally. "Both columns. That's what a ledger's *for*." And she rolled over, and I sat with the flickering, green and gold and impossibly far, until sleep took me back through.

* * *

The loneliness, when it came, came in the gaps.

Not the big grief—that I had chosen, had chosen with my eyes open and my sword drawn, and a chosen grief sits differently in the chest than a stolen one. It was the small things. It was reaching for the Thornic word for *bread* and having it arrive instantly, perfectly, in a voice that was not mine, from a baker in the flickering country who was pulling loaves from an oven in a world I could see but never smell. It was knowing, always, exactly, the precise distance between myself and everything.

Voss noticed before I said anything, because of course she did.

"You flinch," she said, on the ninth day, when we were hauling water from the melt-pool. "Every time the flickering starts. You flinch like someone's thrown something at you."

"It's not thrown. It's offered."

"Same flinch."

I sat down on the waystation's broken step and looked at the Door. From below, at this distance, it was almost modest—a seam of gold light in the glacier's blue face, tall as a gate, quiet as a held breath. Somewhere on the other side of it, a country was relearning how to exist.

"When I swore the oath," I said, "fifteen years ago, the last oath, the one we swore as the Veil came down—I thought I was swearing to keep something alive. The words. The memory. I thought: I'll carry it, like contraband, like salt over the pass, and someday I'll deliver it somewhere." I turned my hands over. They were still a smuggler's hands, scarred and competent. "I didn't know I *was* the somewhere. I didn't know the delivery was me standing still."

Voss lowered herself onto the step beside me with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions about glaciers. For a while she said nothing, which from Voss was a speech.

"You know what I burned, in the Chancery?" she said at last. "Words. That was the job. Words, maps, songs, a family's whole library because the grandmother sang a lullaby in the old tongue to get a baby to sleep." She turned her hands over too, mirroring me, and her hands were worse than mine. "And every time, I told myself: this is mercy. A sealed country is a country at peace. A burned word can't start a war." She snorted. "And then I find a child, one child, barefoot in the snow, and the whole Veil comes down like a rotten roof. Fifteen years of burning, undone by an eight-year-old with a counting song."

"Nine," I said. "She's nine. She told me on the ice. She was very firm about it."

"Nine." Voss shook her head. "The Chancery's entire doctrine, outlived by someone who still loses teeth." She looked at me sideways. "You're not lonely because you're alone, Odile. I'm here, and you're stuck with me. You're lonely because you're *split*. Half of you is over there eating bread you can't taste. That's not a wound. That's a *job*. And jobs—" she stood, wincing, and offered me a hand up, "—jobs are easier with a witness. So I'm staying. I'm your witness. Write it in your ledger."

I took her hand. It was the first time in fifteen years I had let anyone pull me to my feet, and I found that I had missed it more than I had missed the sword.

* * *

The flickering changed as the weeks turned. That was the second thing I learned about being an anchor: the Door was not a wall with a hole in it. It was a lung. It breathed.

At first the other country came to me only in flashes—the terraces, the bells, the goat-or-dog. But as the solstice passed behind us and the Door settled into its new shape, the flashes lengthened. Whole minutes, sometimes, where the waystation walls went thin as vellum and I could see through them into a kitchen where a woman with Aurel's cheekbones was arguing cheerfully with a man about the correct way to fold dumplings. (She was right. I could tell she was right. Some things are true in every world.) Sometimes the flickering ran the other direction, and I would catch people on the other side *seeing me*—pausing in their fields, their markets, their bell-towers, staring at the air where I stood, and once, a very old woman had looked directly at me, pressed her hand to her heart in the old salute, the oath-speak gesture, *blade and word*, and I had returned it before I remembered I'd sworn never to use it again, and then decided, standing alone in a glacier waystation with my hand over my heart, that I un-swore it. Some oaths deserve to be broken. That had been the whole lesson, hadn't it. The Veil was an oath, and it had been a prison.

"They can see you," Voss said, the day I told her about the old woman. She was sharpening her sword on the step, a sound like a slow secret. "The flickering runs both ways."

"I think it always did. I think that's what the Chancery was really afraid of. Not the words. The *windows*."

"Then we should expect visitors."

"From which side?"

"Yes," said Voss.

She was right, as usual, and sooner than either of us guessed.

* * *

It happened on a morning of hard blue light, when the glacier creaked like an old ship and I was teaching my own hands, of all things, the first form of the oath-sequence, over and over, because the sword—reforged, renamed, hanging across my back like a sleeping animal—hummed when I did it, and the humming kept the flickering steady, and the steadiness felt like practice for something.

The flickering opened. And it did not close.

I stood in the waystation doorway, and the doorway stood in a green hillside, and the two doorways were the same doorway, and down the path between the terraces came three figures. One was small, quick, wearing a coat that fit her badly because it had been made for someone taller, and she was running.

"Odile!" Aurel's voice, not through the flickering this time but *through*, arriving in the ordinary air like a bird through a window someone had finally opened. "Odile, the Door held! It held all winter! Grandmother Sefa says the anchor-speech is *textbook*, she says whoever wrote the old carvings would weep with envy, she says—"

She reached the threshold and stopped, one foot in her world and one foot in mine, and the look on her face was the look of a child standing at the exact center of a see-saw, delighted and terrified in equal measure, and behind her the two adults—Sefa, broad and grey-braided, and a thin young man with a scribe's ink-stains—watched with the particular courage of people about to do something no one has done in fifteen years.

"Can I—" Aurel said. Her voice wobbled. "Just for a minute? Sefa says the Door will let me, now that it's breathing. She says the anchor can hold the frame."

I looked at Voss. Voss looked at me, and shrugged with her whole body, which from her was a benediction.

"It's your door," she said. "You're standing in it."

Aurel came through. Her hand found mine—small, warm, unbearably *there*, the hand I had carried across a glacier and let go of on the ice—and the flickering around us blazed gold for one heartbeat, two, and somewhere behind us, faint and far and unmistakable, a bell in a green country rang the seventh hour, and a bell in the blue country of the Thornfells answered it, or perhaps I answered it, or perhaps there was no longer any difference worth counting.

"Textbook," I said, when I could speak. "Grandmother Sefa said *textbook*?"

"She also says you fold dumplings wrong."

"That woman has never seen me fold a dumpling."

"She says she can see you *fine*."

And I laughed, again, the second time in a season, and the sound went through the open Door into a country that had been dead for fifteen years and was now, apparently, taking notes on my cooking.

Behind the three of them, down the green path, more figures were coming. A dozen. Then more than a dozen. Pilgrims, survivors, the scattered people of a scattered nation, walking toward the seam of light with their bundles and their children and their half-remembered words—and among them, at the back of the line, a figure in Chancery grey with her commission papers burned and her sword on her hip, walking toward me with the expression of a woman who had news, and the news had teeth.

"Aurel," I said, not letting go of her hand. "How many are behind her?"

The child squinted down the path. "All of them," she said. "Everyone. She told them the Door was open, and they *all* came."

Voss arrived at the threshold at a run, skidding on the last of the ice, and what she said was:

"Odile. We're going to need a bigger door."
