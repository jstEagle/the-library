## 21. Letters in a Living Tongue

### Odile — First Frost

The first letter came on the morning the tarn froze, and it arrived the way everything from that side of the Door arrived: impossibly.

I had gone down to the ice at dawn with my breath steaming and my hands in my pockets, as I did most mornings now, mostly to stand there and feel the hum of it against my teeth—the anchor's hum, low and constant, like a hearth-fire heard through a wall. The Door of Ash no longer looked like a door. It looked like a seam of gold light stitched into the glacier's face, and when I pressed my palm to it I could feel two worlds breathing out of rhythm, and my chest ached in that doubled way it always did now, half of me homesick for a place I was standing in.

That morning the seam flickered, and something slid out of it.

Not a person. A flat square of pale bark, thin as cloth, inscribed edge to edge with Thornic script in ink the color of crushed bilberries. It landed at my boots with a small, polite tap, as if it had been waiting its turn.

I knew the hand before I read a word. Round-bellied letters. The *thorn-rune* drawn like a little bird. A child's hand grown steadier over months but still tilting leftward, still rushing toward the ends of lines the way she rushed toward the ends of sentences.

*Dear Odile,* it began—and then, catching herself, crossing out nothing but adding above it in smaller script: *(that is not how we write to elders here. Dear Sword-Mother Odile. There. Better.)*

I sat down right there on the ice, which Voss would have said was asking for a cracked hip, and I read.

*The terraces are green again. I know you will say I am exaggerating because I exaggerated about the goats, but this time it is true, everyone says so, even Bellmaster Ilva who never agrees with anyone. The bell-tower on the western hill rang for the harvest and I could hear it from the school-house and I cried a little but only a little and Aurel says crying is allowed here, which is strange, but good.*

*I have been put in charge of the youngest class even though I am the youngest one in it. This is unfair and I told them so and they said "yes" and gave me the chalk anyway.*

*How is your winter? Is Maren still sleeping in the doorway like a dog? Tell her the bread recipe worked. Tell her she owes me a letter. She keeps saying her Thornic is too bad to write and I keep telling her that is what practice is FOR.*

I laughed out loud, alone on a glacier, which is the sort of thing a person should be embarrassed by and I was not.

Because here is what I had learned since solstice: the Door carried things both ways, but only small things, only true things. People passed—pilgrims first in ones and twos, then families, a trickle that grew each week into something like a road. But words written in Thornic passed more freely than anything else, sliding through the seam like water through cloth, and no one could explain why except perhaps that words were lighter than people, or truer, or that the Veil had never really known what to do with them.

So we wrote. She wrote; I answered. And between the letters, in the gaps where a whole country was remembering itself, I taught.

### The Child (Aurel) — Deep Winter

Here is what nobody tells you about having your country given back to you: it is a lot of work.

Sera had warned me before she went through. *It won't be the old country waiting for you,* she said. *It'll be the old country's bones, and someone has to put flesh on them.* I hadn't understood her then. I understood now. For every terrace that came back green, three were still grey scar. For every bell that rang, ten hung silent, their tongues taken or their ropes rotted. The restored Thornic kingdom rose out of the mist of itself slowly, like something surfacing from deep water, and it surfaced fastest wherever someone spoke to it.

That was the other thing nobody told me: I missed her. Not in the sharp way, not the way I'd missed my mother after the crossing, which was a wound. Missing Odile was more like missing my own shadow. I kept turning around to say something and finding no one there to scowl at me fondly.

So I wrote. And when writing wasn't enough, I taught.

The school-house had been a granary until the autumn. It had one window, a stove that smoked unless you fed it exactly right, and eleven students ranging from four years old to a shepherd named Doss who claimed to be sixty-two and learning letters "before it kills me." Every morning I wrote Odile's lessons on the slate board in my round-bellied hand, and every morning the room filled with the sound of Thornic spoken aloud—which is a sound I cannot describe to you if you have not heard it, except to say it is less like talking and more like weather coming home.

Doss was the worst student and the best one. He butchered every vowel. He called the oath-forms "those twisty prayers." And he had been a boy of nine when the Veil fell, standing on a terrace that vanished under his feet, and he had spent fifty-three years in a world with no word for what he'd lost. Now he sat on a child's stool with chalk dust on his beard and practiced the same syllable forty times, and when he finally got it right he didn't cheer. He just closed his eyes and breathed, like a man setting down something very heavy.

"That one," he said, hoarse. "Say that one again."

"*Veth-ira,*" I said. *Homecoming.*

"*Veth-ira,*" he repeated, and got it right, and outside the window, faintly, one of the dead terraces shivered and turned green at the edges.

We all pretended not to see it, because seeing it made Doss cry, and he'd told us firmly that he would not cry in front of children. But I saw. I always saw.

### Odile — Thaw

By thaw, I had a routine that would have horrified my younger self: the oath-bound swordspeaker of legend, scourge of the Chancery's maps, spending her mornings at a plank table outside the Door with a stack of bark-letters and a line of students.

They came down from the passes, the scattered ones. Survivors of the old country's diaspora, and their children, and a surprising number of people who weren't Thornic at all but had heard there was a door and a language and a woman who taught both, and wanted in on whatever that was. Voss ran the queue with her inquisitor's clipboard, which she had repurposed without any apparent irony, checking names, sorting beginners from returners, glaring at anyone who tried to skip ahead.

"You," she said to a nervous wool-trader on my third morning, "have been practicing. Don't lie, I can hear it in your vowels. Sit in the second row."

"Maren," I said, "you were literally paid to destroy this language."

"And now I'm unpaid to protect it." She flipped her clipboard. "The Chancery has excellent severance packages and terrible pensions. I'm diversifying."

The truth underneath her joke was this: the Chancery was in disarray. When the Door opened and the company sent to burn us broke against an oath-blade and a singing glacier and, crucially, the sight of an entire nation stepping back into existence, the doctrine of the Veil collapsed faster than anyone expected. Not everywhere—not cleanly, not without pockets of men in grey robes insisting up until the end that the whole thing was a resonance-hallucination—but enough. Enough that the veil-burner orders had been suspended. Enough that some of them had come to the mountain, sheepish as penitents, and stood in my second row holding chalk like it might bite them.

I let them. That was the part fifteen years ago me would not have believed. I let them sit among the survivors they'd helped orphan, and I taught them the words they'd burned, and I watched them get the vowels wrong and try again.

Repair, it turns out, sounds like this: *again. Again. Again.*

The letters came weekly now. I answered weekly. And once—only once—a letter came that was not from Aurel.

It was in a shaking, ancient hand I didn't recognize, and it said only:

*You do not know me. I held the western gate for six hours the night the Veil fell, so that others could run. I have waited fifty-three years to say this to someone who would understand: veth-ira, sister. Homecoming. Thank you for carrying the sword home.*

I read it four times. Then I walked out onto the tarn ice so no one would see my face, and stayed there a long while.

### The Child — High Summer

In high summer the Door widened enough for packages, and Odile sent the first one through with strict instructions that I open it alone, which of course meant everyone gathered around to watch.

Inside: a sword.

Not *the* sword—that one stayed anchored at her hip, humming its two-worlds hum. This was a copy, forged new, plain-hilted, and wrapped around it was a letter in her tight, slanted script:

*A swordspeaker is useless without students. You are teaching them words. Teach them the blade-forms too. Start with Still Water. Yes, I know they are farmers and shepherds and one extremely stubborn bell-master. So were we. —O.*

*(P.S. Do not let Doss near anything with an edge until his footwork stops being an insult to the concept of feet.)*

*(P.P.S. I miss you. That is not how we write to elders either. I don't care.)*

I read the last line aloud by accident, because I was reading fast, and the whole school-house heard it, and Doss said, "Well, obviously," in the tone of a man stating that water is wet.

So we drilled. In the mornings, words; in the afternoons, wooden staves cut from birch, and eleven students of wildly varying ages attempting Still Water in a cloud of dust and complaints. Doss's footwork remained a crime. A four-year-old named Pip had better form than anyone, possibly because she had no fear whatsoever, which is also a problem, but a later one.

And every evening I wrote back.

*Dear Sword-Mother Odile. Still Water achieved by nine of eleven. Doss achieved a related movement best described as Falling Barrel. We are proud of him anyway. The western tower rings again—I rang it myself, Ilva let me, my arms were noodles for two days. The grey-robed man in my class (the one who used to burn books, yes THAT one) got veth-ira right today and had to go stand outside. I let him. Everyone deserves the tarn ice sometimes.*

*Question: when I ring the bells, can you hear them? Aurel says yes but Aurel lies about goats.*

The answer came back the next week, short as her letters always were, long as her letters always were:

*Yes. Faintly. Like hearing rain through a roof. Keep ringing. —O.*

*(P.S. Goats. Explain the goats.)*

### Odile — Harvest

On the night before the harvest festival, Voss found me at the table sorting letters, and instead of her usual clipboard-shuffling she just stood there a moment, looking at the stacks.

"How many now?" she asked.

"Students on this side? Two hundred and change. On hers?" I tapped a bark-letter. "She claims forty. Which means thirty, which means it's probably actually fifty."

"And the language?"

I considered the honest answer. "There were maybe four hundred speakers left in the world when the Veil fell, counting us. There are over a thousand now. By next summer—" I shook my head. "Maren, you can't kill a language that lives in children. They tried for fifty-three years and they couldn't even kill it in *me*, and I was trying my hardest to help them."

Voss picked up the topmost letter—Aurel's latest, with its little bird-runes—and read it in silence. Her face did the thing it did, the slow thaw she'd deny under oath.

"She's teaching them the blade-forms," she said finally.

"God help the old country."

"She says Doss—"

"Falling Barrel. Yes."

Something between a laugh and a sigh went out of her. She set the letter down carefully, aligned its edges with the stack, the way she aligned everything, the way she'd once aligned interrogation transcripts and burning orders and, lately, chalk.

"I got a letter too," she said. "From her. Took me a week to work through it. My Thornic is still a crime scene." She paused. "She says she forgives me. For the hunting. For the—before." Her jaw worked. "Fifty-three years I thought the Veil was mercy, Odile. Mercy. And an eight-year-old writes me six lines of bark and takes it off my shoulders like it weighs nothing."

"It doesn't weigh nothing," I said. "But she's strong. She gets it from somewhere."

"Where? Certainly not from you, you're made of granite and guilt."

"Then from herself," I said, "which is the best place."

Outside, far off across the pass, faint as rain through a roof, a bell rang. Harvest eve. She'd promised she'd ring it. We stood in the doorway together and listened, the two of us, the anchor and the witness, granite and crime-scene, and neither of us said anything, because there was a language for what we felt and we were both fluent in it and it didn't need saying aloud.

### The Child — First Snow

First snow came early that year, the way it does in mountains, and with it came the strangest letter yet.

I knew before I unrolled it that something was different. It was heavier than her usual bark-squares, folded twice, sealed with wax—which Odile never used, wax being, in her words, "a way of making people break things to find out if they matter." I broke the seal with great ceremony and an audience of one (Doss, pretending not to watch).

The letter inside was not in her hand.

It was in many hands. Dozens. Scores. Bark after bark after bark, stitched together with cord into a scroll that unspooled past the edge of the table and kept going, and every section was different—round hands, crabbed hands, a child's wobbling capitals, one that was clearly written with the writer's non-dominant hand and proudly labeled as such.

At the top, in Odile's tight slant, was a single line:

*Everyone you ever taught, teaching everyone they ever met. Read it and weep, then wipe your face and get back to class. —O.*

And below her name, section after section, message after message, in a tongue that fifty-three years ago had exactly one living secret speaker hiding in the Thornfells, and now had more voices than I could count:

*Veth-ira,* said the first.
*Veth-ira,* said the second, in a different hand.
*Veth-ira,* said a hundred more, down the whole length of the scroll, farmers and shepherds and reformed book-burners and four-year-olds, all the way to the last entry, which was in Doss's terrible, beautiful handwriting, though he swore he hadn't sent it:

*VETH-IRA. TELL HER THE BARREL HAS LEARNED TO ROLL.*

I laughed until I couldn't breathe, sitting on the floor of the school-house with a nation's worth of greetings spilled around me, snow starting past the window, the stove ticking, the western bell getting ready for evening.

Then I sat down at my desk, took up my chalk-stub, and began the reply.

*Dear Sword-Mother Odile,* I wrote. *Enclosed: proof that you were right, which I know you will be unbearable about. Also enclosed: a question, and it is a big one, so save it for the tarn ice.*

*Next solstice, the Door widens widest. Ilva says the whole city wants to come through—not to leave. To visit. To see the mountain where the sword slept, and meet the woman who carried it, and say thank you in person, in the old words, all of them at once.*

*Can we come home for a day? Both directions at once?*

*Also, about the goats—*

I stopped. Smiled. Kept writing, and outside, the first snow of winter came down soft and thick over a country that remembered its own name, and somewhere on the other side of a seam of gold light, I already knew, a woman made of granite and guilt was sharpening her chalk and preparing to pretend she hadn't cried.
