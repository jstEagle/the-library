## 1. Salt on the Pass

There are exactly three things you need to smuggle salt through the Thornfells: a mule with more patience than sense, a coat that doesn't fit well enough to be memorable, and a road that the Crown insists does not exist.

I had all three, which is why I was alive, and why I was alone, and why on a grey morning in late autumn I was standing at the mouth of a pass called Hollowgate by no one, because officially it was not there to be called anything.

The mule was named Duchess, which she knew, and which she resented, because she felt the name implied a dignity her working conditions did not support. She stood hipshot in the scree while I checked the lashings on her panniers for the fourth time. Salt doesn't spoil. Salt doesn't care if you're late. But the man buying it in Coldmere cared very much about schedules, and men who care about schedules also care about what happens to people who miss them, so I cared too, in my way, from a comfortable distance.

"Two hours," I told her. She flicked an ear. Conversation with Duchess was mostly this: statements of intent met with editorial indifference.

The pass itself is not hidden, precisely. Hidden implies effort, and the Crown has never expended effort on the Thornfells beyond garrisoning the low towns and pretending the high country belongs to them. The truth is simpler and stranger: the roads up here are only where you know to look. Not invisible — *unremarked*. A traveler walking the main trail would pass within thirty yards of Hollowgate's entrance and remember afterward a wall of grey stone and nothing else. There's a word for what holds it that way. I don't say the word anymore. But I can walk through walls made of it, and I do, twice a season, hauling whatever the lowlands want and the law says they can't have.

Salt, this trip. Before that: ink, quinine, a crate of southern wine mislabeled as vinegar, and one sealed letter I hadn't opened and didn't intend to, because curiosity is how smugglers die. The letter rode in my coat's inner pocket, flat against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Everything else was honest cargo. Well. Honestly dishonest.

I led Duchess off the main trail at the split oak — the tree was older than the kingdom, or claimed to be; trees lie less than kingdoms but they still exaggerate — and put my hand against the stone face beside it. The rock looked like rock. It always looks like rock. That's the whole trick: it never stops looking like rock. You simply have to know that the looking is a courtesy extended to strangers, and that old roads keep their real faces for family.

The pass opened the way it always does: all at once, like a held breath released. Grey walls leaning close, a floor of packed shale, sky reduced to a bright seam overhead. Cold pooled down between the cliffs the way water does, patient and total. Duchess snorted and stepped in ahead of me, because even a mule knows a shortcut when the mountain offers one.

* * *

You'd think ten years in the high country would make a person talkative when company came. Mostly it does the opposite. I'd gotten so good at silence that silence had started to feel like a craft, something I could point to. Look, I'd built this. Four thousand mornings without saying a word that mattered.

The town below — Coldmere, six hundred souls, two taverns, one priest with a gambling problem — knew me as Hart, a trader out of nowhere in particular who appeared with goods priced just under what anyone could refuse. They knew I kept to myself. They knew I paid my tolls. They knew nothing true about me at all, and I had spent a decade making sure of it, and some mornings, scraping frost off a window with a thumbnail, I caught myself wondering whether being forgotten this thoroughly counted as survival or just a slower way of dying.

Then Duchess would step on my foot, or the kettle would boil over, and the question would resolve itself into the day's small work, which was the mercy of small work: it never asks you who you are.

Hollowgate ran narrow for the first mile, then widened into the long gallery the old maps — the ones that burned, along with everything else — had called the Choir, because wind moving through its fluted walls produced a sound like distant singing. Low notes. Patient ones. On bad days the sound got under my skin and stayed there, humming, like a hymn half-remembered by someone who'd sworn off congregations.

This was a bad day. I couldn't have told you why yet. The air had that particular stillness it gets before weather or worse, and Duchess kept stopping to plant her feet and swing her head back toward the way we'd come, ears flat, as though the pass were listening behind us.

"Nothing follows us," I told her. "That's the entire architecture of my life."

She did not believe me. Mules are honest that way. They believe what their noses tell them, and their noses are older than any lie I could construct, including mine.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually does, with someone else's incompetence.

A customs patrol should not have been anywhere near the upper trails in late autumn. The passes close with the first snow; the Crown's riders knew it; the Crown's riders had a warm barracks and a card game waiting. But either the seasons were turning strange this year — and they were, the frost had come weeks early, though I was doing my best not to think about why that might be — or somebody in Coldmere had finally grown ambitious, or bored. Boredom kills more smugglers than ambition ever has.

I heard them before I saw them: iron shoes on shale, carrying wrong. Shale is a liar's surface — it'll swallow a soft footstep whole — but a shod horse rings on it like a dropped pan, and once you've spent years listening to mountains you learn which echoes belong.

Four riders, maybe five, coming up the switchbacks below the Choir's far end. Which meant they were already inside the ground that wasn't supposed to admit them, which meant either they'd stumbled in blind luck through the outer seam, or —

No. Not that. Nobody outside remembered enough anymore to find the seams on purpose. I'd have known. I'd have *felt* it, the way you feel a draft under a door.

Blind luck, then. Or a map. And both of those possibilities were bad, so I did what I do instead of thinking when thinking gets expensive: I moved.

Off the trail, up a goat-path I'd found my third winter here, hauling Duchess by the lead — she complained, vocally, at length, in mule — until we were wedged in a fold of the cliff beneath an overhang furred with dead bracken. From there I could see the gallery floor forty feet below, and the riders when they came through.

Five of them. Customs livery under grey oilcloaks, which was wrong twice over: customs didn't ride in fives, and customs didn't wear grey. Customs wore the king's blue and looked miserable in it. Grey meant a different purse paying. Grey meant the kind of patrol whose reports went somewhere other than the provincial ledger.

They moved like soldiers, not tax collectors. Spread in a loose line. One of them, riding point, kept turning his head side to side — slowly, deliberately — the way a man does when he's been told to look for something he won't recognize until he sees it.

My hand had gone to my belt knife without asking me. I made it come back down.

They passed beneath us at a walk. Close enough that I could hear the creak of tack and one rider saying, low, "...says the anomaly's linguistic. Whatever that means. We're to note anything *spoken wrong*, apparently." A short laugh from another. "Half these hillfolk speak wrong."

"Not this far up," the first said. "That's the point. Nobody speaks anything this far up."

They rode on. The sound of them faded into the Choir's singing wind, and then faded under it, and then was gone, and I stayed under the bracken with my heart going like a smith's hammer and Duchess breathing warm against my shoulder, until the light in the gallery shifted a full hand's width along the far wall.

Anomaly. Linguistic.

Ten years, and the word arrived anyway, wearing grey coats and riding a road that didn't exist. Somebody in some cold office had noticed that language leaves traces — that certain tongues, spoken in certain places, behave less like speech and more like keys. I knew exactly which tongue. I had spent ten years not speaking it, not dreaming in it, not humming its lullabies even alone in the dark, and here came five men on horses paid to listen for its absence of echo.

I am a careful woman. Careful women survive by treating every coincidence as a bill come due. But I will confess, under the bracken, with the mule's flank against my back: for one ugly moment I wanted to laugh. All that silence, all those four thousand mornings of it, and the Crown had finally built itself an ear — and pointed it at the one sound I had left, which was none.

* * *

We made Coldmere by dark, taking the long way around, doubling back twice out of habit and a third time out of something that wasn't habit. The buyer — a cooper named Fenwick with forearms like hams and the soul of an accountant — counted his salt, frowned at the weight, paid anyway, and asked no questions, which is the highest compliment one tradesman pays another.

"You look tired, Hart," he said, which was as close to intimacy as our relationship permitted.

"It's the season," I said. "Mountains get heavy in autumn."

He grunted, unconvinced, unbothered. I took my coin and my leave and stopped at the Drowned Lamb long enough to drink one cup of cider and hear the news, because a smuggler who doesn't listen is just a courier with better margins.

The news was thin and it was bad. Early snows in the high passes — earlier than living memory, said the innkeeper, who had a living memory he inflated annually. Riders in grey asking questions at the toll bridges, polite ones, the dangerous kind. A priest down in Marrowdale who'd taken ill after talking to a traveler, forgot the whole conversation, couldn't even recall the man's face. The innkeeper told it as comedy. The room laughed. I turned my cup in my hands and did not.

A traveler whose conversation erased itself. Whose face wouldn't stay in a memory.

I finished the cider. I paid. I walked out into the street with the cold coming down off the peaks like a verdict, and stood a moment under the lamp outside the Lamb, watching my breath, and made myself say the thing plainly, because plainness is a discipline:

Someone is using the old art again. Someone is speaking Vessarine on this side of the mountains. And every year of my silence has been spent buying invisibility that dies the moment anyone else opens their mouth.

Which meant the decade was over. Not ended — over, the way a rope is over once it starts to fray. I could feel the strands going.

Duchess and I took the back lane out of Coldmere under a sky with no stars in it, cloud sitting on the ridgeline like a lid. My cabin sat a hard hour above the town, off a trail that existed, through a stand of pines that existed, at the end of a life that was supposed to. I lit no lamp. I unsaddled, fed, blanketed, banked the fire low. Sat down with the whetstone and the belt knife and worked the edge for no reason except that my hands wanted occupation while my mind ran its ledgers.

Options. There were always options; despair is just arithmetic done badly.

One: run. Deeper, higher, across into the principalities where the Crown's writ was a joke told at borders. But grey riders had been at the toll bridges, which meant the net was going out wide, and running now would look like exactly what it was.

Two: find whoever was speaking the old tongue and stop them, by persuasion or otherwise, before they pulled the whole buried past of the world up into the light with me under it.

Three: sit still and trust ten years of forgetting to hold.

I was still weighing three, because three required nothing of me, when Duchess screamed.

Not nickered — screamed, the full-throated alarm of an animal that has decided the world has broken its terms, and I was through the door with the knife before the sound finished, into the yard, into the wind, and the first flakes of the year's first snow were coming down around me, early, wrong, weeks early, and standing at the gate of my own fence, barefoot in it, was a child.

A girl. Eight years old, maybe. Thin as a winter branch, dark-eyed, unhuddled, unshivering, snow settling on her shoulders like it had been given permission. She looked at me the way nobody had looked at me in ten years: directly, without fear, without calculation, as though she had been sent.

And she spoke.

One sentence. Small voice, steady, pitched under the wind. Words in a tongue the maps had burned, that the priests had burned, that I had burned out of my own mouth one syllable at a time over ten years of silence — and they landed in me like stones dropped down a well, each one striking something deep before the splash.

I understood every word.

*I knocked at every door on the mountain,* the child said. *Yours is the only one that heard.*
