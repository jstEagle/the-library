## 3. Words Older Than Crowns

There are three kinds of trouble in the Thornfells: the kind that bites you, the kind that bleeds you, and the kind that knocks on your door barefoot in autumn. I'd survived the first two for ten years. It was the third that was going to kill me.

The girl—I still hadn't settled on a name for her, though *problem* had a certain accuracy—sat at my table the next morning eating porridge with the solemn concentration of someone who has decided food is a serious matter. She ate like my mother used to say Vessarine children ate, which is to say she treated every spoonful as if it might be her last and also as if it owed her an apology.

She hadn't spoken again since the night she arrived. That should have been a relief. Instead it sat in my chest like swallowed riverstone. Because when she *had* spoken—*Vessa ren i thala*, "I have come far," or possibly "the stars are falling," my grammar was rusted and my nerves were worse—she'd spoken the language of a country that did not exist.

Not *doesn't* exist. *Did not*, past tense, by royal decree. Twenty years ago the Crown's scribes had scraped Vessar off every map, its name out of every ledger, its tongue from every mouth that could be persuaded to forget it. Most mouths had cooperated eagerly. Forgetting is easy when remembering gets you burned.

I remembered anyway. That had been my punishment for surviving.

So there I stood, scraping the pot while a child who shouldn't exist spoke words that officially never had, and I did what any sensible smuggler does with contraband too dangerous to keep: I looked for somewhere to dump it.

* * *

Father Aldric kept the border shrine at Coldmere, four miles down the mountain where the pines gave way to scrub and the road remembered it was a road. He was the nearest thing the foothills had to honest clergy, which is to say he drank less than the others and had only once tried to report me to the customs house, and that had been over a dispute about candles rather than contraband. We understood each other the way two foxes understand each other across a henhouse wall.

More to the point, he ran the foundling rolls. Every child pulled out of the high passes went through his book before anyone thought to ask questions. If I could get the girl into Father Aldric's ledger, she would become a fact. Facts were safer than miracles. Miracles drew inquisitors the way honey draws wasps, and the girl was already the most miraculous thing I'd seen since a mountain learned to burn.

"You look terrible," Aldric said, opening the door before I knocked. He always knew. I suspected he smelled me coming, or perhaps he simply heard the sound of my conscience dragging its feet up his path. His eyes went past me to the child on my hip—the walk down had been long enough that she'd let me carry her, which told me either she trusted me or her feet had given formal notice—and something behind his face shifted, slow as weather.

"Found her on my doorstep," I said, which was true, and left out everything else, which was also true, in its way.

"Doorsteps are becoming fashionable." He held the door wider. Inside, the shrine smelled of tallow and cold stone, forty candles guttering along the walls in their little red glasses. Aldric liked candles the way some men liked drink—not wisely, but in quantity. "Bring her in, then. The fire's low but it's honest."

The girl looked around the shrine with an expression I couldn't read, and then she looked at the candles, and I swear on whatever oath I've got left that she looked *sorry* for them.

I didn't think anything of it at the time. Remember that. There will be a quiz later.

Aldric sat us at his table beneath the window, produced his foundling roll—a ledger fat enough to stop a knife—and dipped his pen. "Name?"

"She doesn't have one I know."

"They all have names. Some are just shy about lending them out." He leaned toward the child, arranging his face into the particular kindness priests practice in mirrors. "What shall we call you, little one? Have you a name your mother gave you?"

The girl considered him. Then she set down the crust of bread he'd given her, folded her hands in her lap like a small duchess, and answered.

She said six words.

I won't write them here. Not because they're forbidden—they're not; there's no one left with the authority to forbid them—but because writing them feels like picking a lock in front of witnesses. What matters is what they were: a full sentence of Vessarine, clean and unhesitating, in the accent of the river valleys south of the capital. My capital. The one that isn't on maps anymore.

Six words, spoken aloud in a room where no one had spoken them in twenty years.

Every candle in the shrine went out at once.

Forty flames, dead between one breath and the next, and the light through the window went grey as dishwater, and the cold came up through the floor stones like something rising from under ice. The girl watched the smoke curl off the wicks with an expression of mild disappointment, as if the candles had failed some test of theirs.

And Father Aldric forgot.

Not fainted. Forgot. I watched it happen, which is the part I still can't make peace with. One moment he was leaning forward with his pen raised, asking the child her name; the next he blinked, looked down at the blank line in his ledger, and frowned at it the way a man frowns at stairs he's climbed all his life.

"What—" He touched his temple. "Elodie, why am I holding my pen? Was I—did you come about the candles?" He glanced around at the dead flames and sighed with the weariness of a man whose congregation keeps stealing his tallow. "Third time this month. There's a draught in this building I swear was put there by God personally to spite me."

"Aldric." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "We were talking about the girl."

"What girl?"

I looked down. The girl was looking back at me, calm as a millpond, and I want to be very precise here: she was sitting at the table, in plain view, in a shaft of window-light, eating her bread. There was no mistaking her for furniture. But Aldric's eyes passed over her the way water passes over a stone in a stream—touching, unseeing—and moved on to the ledger, to the pen, to the draught, to anything else in the world.

"Elodie," he said gently, in the tone men use for women they suspect of drinking before noon, "you walked four miles down a mountain. Sit. Rest. I'll relight these and we'll talk about whatever's troubling you."

He got up and began going candle to candle with a taper, muttering. Each flame caught, burned steady, behaved itself completely. As if nothing had happened. As if the dark had been the natural state and the light the interruption.

I took the child's hand. Her fingers were cool and closed around mine with the certainty of someone being handed back a borrowed coat.

"We're leaving," I whispered—in Trade-tongue, mind you, the safe tongue, the tongue without teeth—and she nodded once, gravely, like a co-conspirator.

At the door Aldric called after me, warm and baffled: "Come Sunday, Elodie! And tell that man of yours the shrine takes donations of wax!"

I didn't have a man. I'd never had a man. He knew that. He'd teased me about it for years.

His voice followed me down the path, friendly and empty as a struck bell, and I did not look back, because I already knew what I'd see: a kind old priest waving at a woman walking alone.

* * *

I made it half a mile up the switchbacks before my legs informed me they'd prefer to be cowards sitting down, so we stopped on a flat rock above the tree line where I could see the whole valley and nothing in it could sneak up on us. The girl sat beside me, swinging her feet. Below, Coldmere's chimneys smoked peacefully into the afternoon.

"All right," I said to her. "Let's review."

She looked at me with the patience of the very young for the very foolish.

"When you speak—that language—you blow out candles. You erase old men. You make a shrine forget I exist." I rubbed my face. My hands smelled of woodsmoke and fear, a smell I knew intimately from another life. "Now, I've seen workings before. I carried enough sealed cargo through these passes to stock a war. Charms, wards, little gods in lacquered boxes. They cost coin and they cost blood and none of them, *none* of them, worked like that. So here's my question, small person, and I want you to think carefully."

She waited.

"Is it you? Or is it the words?"

Because those were different problems, and I needed to know which one I was carrying. A child who could unravel memory with a sentence was a weapon, and weapons had owners, and owners sent hunters. But a *language* that could do it—that was worse. That meant every word she'd ever say was a working whether she willed it or not. That meant the tongue itself was alive, or had been buried alive, and things buried alive don't stay quiet forever.

She tilted her head. Then, deliberately, she picked up a pebble, held it out on her palm, and said a word—one word, soft, almost gentle.

Nothing happened. The pebble stayed a pebble. The wind stayed wind.

Then she said it again, and this time I felt it. Not heard—*felt*, the way you feel thunder in your back teeth. The air over her palm shivered, bent, and for half a heartbeat I saw the pebble twice: once as stone, and once as something older, some grain of the world before the world agreed on its shapes. Then it was just a pebble again, and the girl closed her fist over it and looked up at me with an expression that said, plainly, in any tongue: *you see?*

"That's not possible," I told her, because someone had to maintain standards.

She put the pebble in my hand. It was warm, like a bird.

Here is what I knew, and what I had spent ten years refusing to know. When the Veil fell on Vessar—when the sky over the capital caught fire and the borders closed like a wound closing—we were told the country had been erased. Punished. Unmade. The Crown called it justice and the priests called it mercy and everyone sensible called it nothing at all, because calling it anything was fatal.

But erasure is a working too. And workings have prices.

I had assumed the price was paid by the dead. Sitting on that rock with a warm pebble in my palm and a child beside me who spoke the language of ghosts, I finally understood my error, and it went through me like cold water through a cracked hull:

Vessar hadn't been destroyed. It had been *sealed*. Folded away behind the Veil like a letter no one was allowed to open. And a seal, unlike a grave, can be broken. All it needs is a voice. All it needs is someone who remembers the words.

All it needs, apparently, is a barefoot girl who walks out of the mountains carrying ash-white tokens and sentences that put out fires and eat memories.

The token. I'd been avoiding thinking about the token since the night she arrived, the way you avoid probing a tooth. I turned it over in my pocket now: a disc of pale metal, no bigger than a coin, stamped with the sigil of the Swordspeakers' Oath—the blade crossed with the rising star. My sigil. The sigil of eleven women sworn to hold the Door of Ash, of whom exactly one had run away instead of dying.

How does a child in the Thornfell foothills come to carry a swordspeaker's seal?

There was one answer. I hated it. I carried it home anyway, along with the child, up through the pines as the light went long and gold, and every step I took I could feel the question sharpening itself against the inside of my skull.

Because if the seals were moving—if the Oath was reaching out, ten years late, collecting what it was owed—then it wasn't finished with me. And if the Veil was thin enough for a child to walk through...

Then somewhere east of everywhere, a door was opening.

Or worse: being forced.
