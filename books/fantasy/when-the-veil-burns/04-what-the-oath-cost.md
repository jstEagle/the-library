## 4. What the Oath Cost

The last morning of Vessar smelled like bread.

That's the detail that comes back to me, ten years and a mountain range later, when I let myself remember at all. Not the fire. Not the screaming. Bread. My mother had set two loaves on the windowsill of our house in the Lower Terraces to cool, because that was what she did every morning, because the world had always contained mornings, and I walked past them on my way to the citadel with my oath-blade newly strapped across my back and my whole life arranged in front of me like a banquet I'd already been invited to.

I was nineteen years old, and I thought the Veil was permanent.

Everyone did. The Veil wasn't a wall—you couldn't see it—but every child in Vessar learned its shape before they learned their letters. It sat over the kingdom like weather. It kept out the Crown's surveyors, the Crown's missionaries, the Crown's hunger, which was the same hunger wearing different coats. For four hundred years the swordspeakers had maintained it: one oath per speaker, spoken into the blade, renewed each solstice, woven together with all the others into something no army could cut through. We were not soldiers. We were stitches. There were ninety-one of us when I took my oath, and we called each other sisters because the old texts said an oath shared was blood shared, and none of us had ever needed to test whether that was poetry or fact.

Sera Vane stood beside me at my swearing-in. She'd taken hers three years earlier and had already earned the grey-trimmed sash that marked her as first among the juniors, and she stood there with her chin up and her eyes forward while I knelt on cold stone and spoke the words that would bind my voice to steel until one of us broke.

She held the blade for me. That was tradition—the oath-sister who sponsors you lays the flat of your sword across your shoulders like a yoke, like a blessing. I remember the weight of it. I remember thinking: *this is the heaviest thing I will ever carry.*

I want it noted, by whoever keeps accounts of such things, that for nine years I was right.

* * *

The trouble began, as most troubles do, with paperwork.

There was a census. The Crown had been demanding one for decades—names, holdings, tongues—and Vessar's council had refused for decades, because a census is how erasure begins: you count people so you can subtract them. But trade had gone sour, the passes were closing one by one, and the council voted, narrowly, to open the eastern villages to a partial survey. A gesture. An olive branch with a ledger attached.

Ninety-one swordspeakers were asked to extend the Veil's weave over the surveyed districts. More coverage, same number of stitches. The old speakers warned against it—they always warn against things, which is why nobody listens to them—but the arithmetic looked fine on paper, and Vessar had always trusted paper.

I was assigned to Merrowfen, a fishing village on the eastern marshes, with Sera and eleven others. We spent that autumn walking the dykes at dawn, speaking the renewal into our blades while the reeds went gold around us and the marsh birds rose in sheets. It was, I remember thinking, almost pleasant. The work of keeping a country alive looks, from the inside, like walking.

On the last night of autumn, the Veil tore.

Not everywhere. Just above Merrowfen—a seam, thin as a knife cut in silk, opening onto a sky that was not ours. And through it came the first of the Crown's inquisitors, who had not breached the Veil in four hundred years, and who now stepped through it the way you step through a door left unlocked by someone who assumed the house would always have someone home.

They came with words too. I learned that night that the Crown had its own speakers, its own oaths, its own blades, and that theirs were built for unmaking. Our Veil was a sentence four hundred years long, spoken by generations, and theirs was a knife slipped between the clauses.

We fought them on the dykes. Eleven of us, then eight, then five.

I killed my first man that night and found, to my lasting horror, that it was easy—that the oath had made it easy, that my body knew exactly what to do and my grief arrived late, politely, like a guest shown in after dinner had ended. Sera fought beside me the whole time. I know this because I checked. All through the burning of Merrowfen I kept finding her at the edge of my sight, grey sash dark with marsh water, still standing, still standing, and some animal part of me took comfort in that tally the way a drowning woman takes comfort in counting waves.

By dawn the seam had been sealed—or rather, cauterized. Sealing requires a full recitation from a living speaker standing where the breach occurred. Cauterizing only requires the speaker's blood on the blade and the will to hold it shut, and it costs what blood costs.

Maren of the Terraces did it. She was sixty-one years old and she held the seam closed for six hours while the village evacuated, and when they finally pried her sword from hands that had frozen around the grip, she was dead, and the seam was shut, and we counted ourselves lucky.

Lucky. That was the word used. I have hated that word ever since.

* * *

Winter came early that year, though I didn't yet know it was the Veil dying. Nobody did. We thought the tears were wounds; we treated them like wounds; we stitched and cauterized and buried our dead and told ourselves the arithmetic would hold.

It didn't. The Crown's speakers had learned something about our Veil that we had never thought to learn ourselves: it was a language, and languages can be translated. Every tear they opened taught them more of the grammar. By midwinter they weren't breaching the Veil anymore—they were *editing* it, opening doors where they wanted, closing ours behind us, cutting Vessar off from itself in pieces. The eastern marches went silent in January. The river country followed in February. Each time, the pattern was the same: a seam, a fight, a speaker spending herself to close it, and the closing costing more than the opening, because that is how erosion works—it only needs to be patient.

The order came down from the citadel in the spring of the last year: all remaining speakers to the capital. Full muster. Whatever was coming, we would meet it together, the way the Veil had been raised—together, one voice, ninety-one throats become one.

Eighty-two of us answered the muster. I'll let you do that arithmetic yourself. I did, many times, in the years after, usually at night, usually with a bottle.

The capital held. For a season it actually held—the Veil over the city blazing so bright that the inquisitors' seams wouldn't take, and for one absurd golden month I believed we might win, that the old texts were right and an oath truly shared was unbreakable, that all we had to do was stand.

Then the Crown stopped sending armies and sent an offer instead.

Terms of surrender. Amnesty for the speakers. Preservation of the archives, the temples, the royal line—everything but the name. The kingdom would remain, intact behind its mountains, simply... unnamed. Unmapped. Struck from every record, its language classified as extinct, its existence a rumor at best. The people would live. The country would die. That was the bargain, written in beautiful script on vellum that smelled of lavender, and I remember thinking, reading it in the citadel hall with eighty other exhausted women: *that's not surrender, that's burial with honors.*

The council debated for nine days. On the ninth day the Crown's patience ran out, and the inquisitors opened their largest seam yet—not to attack, but to show us what refusal would look like. They took a town none of us could reach in time. They took it completely. When the smoke cleared there was a hole in the world where Halveth had been, a circle of glassed earth, and the Crown's envoys said, very reasonably, that there were forty-seven more towns on their list.

The council capitulated. The erasure was signed.

But the signing required cooperation. The Veil couldn't just be torn down—it had to be *closed*, folded shut from the inside by the speakers themselves, sealed with a final recitation that would lock Vessar away beyond reach of even the Crown's knives. A sleep, the old texts called it. The country would not die; it would wait. And the sealing required every living speaker to speak her part, because a shared oath cannot be sealed by fewer than all who swore it.

Every living speaker. Eighty-two minus everyone the winter had taken. Sixty-one of us remained.

Fifty-nine spoke.

* * *

I need you to understand that I don't remember deciding.

People ask—well. No one asks. No one alive knows enough to ask. But in the version I rehearse at night, the version where I'm honest, people would ask: *how could you?* And the answer is that there was no moment. There was no dramatic refusal, no dropped blade, no scene. There was a courtyard, and snow falling into it from a sky that was already half-glassed over, and fifty-eight women standing in a ring with their swords point-down on the stones, and me.

I stood at the ring's edge. Sera stood across from me. We had been paired for the final recitation—oath-sisters face each other for the sealing, because the last thing each voice joins is the voice it was bound to. She was looking at me the way she always looked at me before a working: steady, certain, already trusting.

The provost called the first word.

Here is what I knew, standing there, that I have never told anyone: the sealing doesn't just lock the country away. It locks the *speakers' memories of it*. That's the price, the true one, the one the council hadn't put on the lavender-smelling terms. To seal Vessar is to forget Vessar—to give your name and your remembering into the Door, so that nothing carried in a speaker's mind can be used to find it again. Fifty-nine women were about to consent to stop existing as themselves, to walk out of that courtyard as strangers with borrowed lives, guarding a secret they could no longer know they kept.

And I—I had already lost Maren. I had lost the easterners, the river folk, Halveth, my mother's kitchen, the smell of bread on a windowsill. I had spent a year watching everything I loved get subtracted, line by line, and the sealing was asking me to volunteer for the subtraction myself. To be erased *by choice*, by my own voice, in front of the woman I—

Well. In front of Sera.

The second word was called. Fifty-eight voices took it up, and the sound went through me like cold water, and I felt my own name begin to loosen in my mouth like a tooth.

I have searched that moment a thousand times for the exact grain of sand that tipped me. Was it fear? Cowardice sits badly on me even now, and I'd like to believe it wasn't that. Was it grief refusing one more loss? Was it some flicker of foresight—the thought, unformed then, that a sealed country might someday need to be *found* again, that a door should have someone outside it who remembers there's a door?

I genuinely don't know. That may be the worst part. Ten years of exile and I still can't tell you whether I broke my oath to save something or to save myself. Both answers fit. Neither comforts.

What I know is this: the third word was called, and fifty-eight voices rose, and mine did not.

The recitation faltered. A shared oath missing one voice isn't weakened—it's *wounded*, and the Veil felt the wound the way a body feels a torn tendon. The sealing collapsed halfway through. Fifty-eight women caught the working before it unraveled entirely, improvising, holding what they could, and what they could hold was less than the full seal and more than nothing: a partial closure, a country locked but latched, sleeping behind a Door that was damaged and imperfect and, somewhere in its grammar, *unfinished*.

Because of me.

They finished what they could. Then the inquisitors came through the smoke to take custody of the survivors, and the fifty-eight—who could no longer remember why they were kneeling, only that they had sworn something enormous—went with them quietly, scattered to borrowed names in borrowed towns, and Sera...

Sera turned to look for me. I saw her turn. I saw the moment her eyes swept the courtyard and found my empty place, my sword lying point-down on the stones where I had set it, and I saw her understand—not the politics, maybe, not the mechanics, but the fact. Her oath-sister was gone, and the oath was broken, and the country was asleep behind a half-made door, and she was the one left standing in the snow with the tally.

I was already running. Through the postern gate, down the terraces, past the bakery where the ovens had gone cold because the bakers had already begun forgetting there was anything to bake. The last thing I heard from Vessar was not an explosion or a scream. It was silence—the particular silence of a place being carefully, thoroughly, administratively removed from the world.

I climbed north for nine days. On the tenth I crossed into the Thornfells, and I have been here since.

* * *

Ten years. You can bury a lot in ten years. I buried the language first—hardest burial, that one, because a tongue you grew up in doesn't stay dead, it waits under the floorboards of your thoughts and speaks to you in dreams. Then the blade, wrapped in oilcloth under the cabin floor. Then the name. Elodie Oyelaran, swordspeaker of Vessar, died in that courtyard along with fifty-eight braver women than me, and what climbed the Thornfell passes was nobody, a smuggler, a ghost with good balance and a talent for roads that officially don't exist.

It worked, mostly. Forgetting is a discipline. You practice it the way you practice scales, and after enough years the hand stops reaching for the strings.

Mostly.

Because here is what I learned up here alone with my guilt, the one tenant I never managed to evict: a broken oath doesn't dissolve. It hangs. Somewhere in the grammar of the world there is a sentence with my part missing, a Door with a latch that never quite caught, and every year the Veil's edges fray a little more, and every winter the mountains feel a little colder than winter has any right to make them, and I tell myself it's altitude.

And sometimes—rarely, maybe twice in ten years—something happens that I file under *coincidence* because the alternative is unbearable. A dream of bread on a windowsill so vivid I wake with the smell still in my nose. A word of Vessarine surfacing in my mouth mid-mutter, tasting like ash and home, gone before I can catch it.

A feeling, lately, growing all autumn like a bruise under the skin. The feeling that something is walking toward my door.

The first snowless night of the season, I banked my fire, barred the door, and lay listening to the wind come down off the high passes—which sounded, that night, faintly and impossibly, like someone practicing the second word of the sealing.

Someone small. Someone barefoot.

Knocking.
