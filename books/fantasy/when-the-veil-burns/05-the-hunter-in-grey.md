## 5. The Hunter in Grey

The Crown's paperwork called it a *linguistic anomaly*, which was the kind of phrase that sounded precise and meant nothing at all. I'd read the dispatch three times on the road south from Cael Harrow, by candle, by dawn, and once more out of pure spite, and every reading left me with the same conclusion: someone in the Archive had found a word they couldn't explain, and instead of doing the sensible thing—burning the paper and going to lunch—they had escalated.

The word was Vessarine. Of course it was.

There were four of us riding down out of the pass into the foothill country where Thornfell began its long grey argument with the sky: myself, two outriders whose names I made a point of never learning, and a junior archivist named Pell who talked the way water drips—steadily, tunelessly, until you wanted to build a wall around yourself. Pell had been assigned to me because he could transcribe six languages and because his superiors considered him expendable in the way that only useful men are considered expendable.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said, for perhaps the ninth time that morning, "the report specifies a child. A girl, eight years or thereabouts, seen at the market in Lowmarch speaking an unregistered tongue. The merchant who reported her said the words made his teeth ache."

"Did they?"

"I beg your pardon?"

"His teeth. Did they ache, or did he want them to ache? There's a bounty attached to anomalies now. Men have reported their own mothers for less than a toothache."

Pell's mouth opened and shut. He was twenty-three, devout, and honest in the way that made him terrible at his job and decent at everything else. I suspected he would not survive this posting, though I confess I did not yet suspect how much of that would be my fault.

"The report is credible," he managed.

"Reports are always credible. That's what makes them useless." I let the horse have its head a little, enough that we crested the rise and the foothills opened below us—the true ones, the old ones, where the land stopped pretending to be tame. Somewhere past those ridges, the Thornfells climbed into cloud and stayed there, holding whatever they held. "Who filed it originally? Before it reached the Archive."

"A customs officer. Stationed at Grey Ford."

Grey Ford. I looked at the name on the page and felt something cold settle behind my sternum, the way it always did when the world arranged itself into patterns I didn't like. Grey Ford sat at the mouth of a valley that officially did not exist. The roads through it appeared on no survey, answered to no magistrate, and were known only to people who had sworn oaths about them so binding that the oaths themselves had been classified as sedition ten years ago.

People like me. Formerly. In the sense that you can be formerly dead if the grave is shallow enough.

"Ride ahead," I told Pell. "Take the customs station, inventory anything the officer wrote down, and bring me his original notes. Not the fair copy. The notes."

He went, gratefully, glad to have orders instead of conversation. I watched him go and then I sat my horse on the ridge a while longer, looking east, because that was the direction I'd spent ten years refusing to look.

* * *

You should understand something about the fall of Vessar, since everyone in this story will eventually lie to you about it, including me.

The official history runs two sentences. *Vessar was consumed by sorcerous catastrophe; the Crown sealed the ruins for the safety of the realm.* Two sentences for a kingdom of a million souls. I know, because I helped write them. Not the ink part—I was nineteen and carrying a sword—but the enforcement part. After the Veil burned, after the Door closed, my sisters and I rode the borderlands for a year making certain that no one remembered too loudly. We collected books. We collected maps. On two occasions that I still count when I can't sleep, we collected people.

And then the others died—one in the north, one by her own hand, one simply vanished between one winter and the next—and I was offered a position, and I took it, because the alternative to serving the thing that killed your home is dying next to it, and I had done quite enough of that for one lifetime.

That is the version I tell myself. I tell it well. I've had practice.

The other version is shorter. It goes: *I survived, and she didn't forgive me for it.*

She being Elodie Oyelaran, last swordspeaker of the Vessarine oath, dead these ten years as far as any record shows, and alive these ten years as far as I knew otherwise, because I had been the one to find her body—or what was meant to pass for one—in a burn scar below the passes, and I had looked at the too-shallow grave and the wrong-angle break in the sword beside it, and I had understood exactly what my oath-sister had done, and I had knelt in the ash and said, aloud, to no one, *"Run far, then."*

And I had written *deceased* in the report.

Why? Ask me why I do most things. Because duty is a blade and mercy is the hand that sometimes turns it aside, and I have never in my life been able to tell which hand is mine. That night below the passes I told myself I was preserving the Crown's resources—a dead swordspeaker costs nothing to hunt. Ten years later I still tell myself that, and some mornings I almost believe it, and on the mornings I don't, I get up anyway and put on the grey coat and go back to work.

This morning, the work was a child who spoke a dead language at a customs post.

* * *

Pell met me at Grey Ford near dusk, and I knew before he spoke that something had gone sideways, because he was standing outside the customs station instead of inside it, and he was holding his transcription book against his chest the way a man holds a shield.

"It's gone," he said.

"What's gone."

"The officer's notes. The whole file. The station master says the officer requested transfer three days ago and left this morning. His quarters were cleared out. But—" Pell hesitated, and here is the thing about Pell: he hesitated honestly, weighing the truth like a man weighing coins he suspects are clipped. "But the station master showed me the officer's last log entry. Before he departed. He wrote that the child came to the station itself. Spoke to him directly. And afterward he wrote—" Pell turned the book toward me, and I read the transcription by lantern light, and felt the cold behind my sternum spread outward.

*She asked me for the woman in the mountains,* the entry ran. *When she spoke, I could not recall my own mother's face until she had gone. Requesting immediate reassignment. Some words should stay buried.*

*Some words should stay buried.* A customs officer, a functionary, a man who stamped forms for a living, had heard eight seconds of Vessarine and reached in one evening the understanding that the Archive took ten years and forty scholars to miss: the language wasn't a relic. It was a mechanism. Every spoken word did something—to memory, to matter, to the seam of the world. And somewhere in the Thornfells, a child was using it to ask directions to a smuggler.

To *the woman in the mountains.*

I stood there in the yard of that shabby station with the lantern guttering and the wind coming down off the high country smelling of pine and coming snow, and I did the arithmetic I had spent a decade refusing to do. If the language lived, the seal had thinned. If the seal had thinned, the Veil was failing. And if the Veil was failing, then everything my order had built its purpose upon—every erasure, every quiet grave, every sentence of the two-sentence history—was a dam holding back a flood that had already begun.

And Elodie Oyelaran was up there in the middle of it, alive, which meant that for ten years I had known, and knowing had changed nothing, and now it was about to change everything.

"Inquisitor?" Pell said carefully. "Your orders?"

My orders were to locate and neutralize the anomaly. Those were the words on the paper. *Neutralize.* A fine word. It means kill, when the anomaly has a pulse.

"We ride at first light," I said. "Into the mountains."

"And if we find the child?"

"You'll write down everything she says," I told him, "and I'll decide what survives in your transcription." Which was a cruel thing to say to an honest man, and true, and both of those facts sat in my chest all night while the fire burned low and I sharpened a blade I had no intention of using the way it was meant to be used.

* * *

Here is what I remember about Elodie, since memory is about to become the theme of this entire miserable enterprise.

Not the oath-day—everyone remembers oath-days, they're designed to be remembered, all candles and liturgy and the taste of silver on the tongue. What I remember is smaller. She was sixteen and I was seventeen, and we had stolen away from the cloister to swim in the Vessa river in the month of flowering, and she dared me to say the water-blessing underwater, and I did, and the river went still—not calm, *still*, the way glass is still—and for one heartbeat every fish hung motionless around us, listening. Then she laughed so hard she swallowed half the Vessa and nearly drowned, and I had to drag her out, and she lay on the bank coughing and laughing and saying, *you absolute fool, Sera, you blessed the whole river, they'll feel that in the capital—*

They did feel it in the capital. We were flogged for it. She took her lashes laughing.

I loved her the way you love the person who taught you that the world is larger and stranger and more breakable than the priests admit. I loved her the way you love a sister, which is to say with a violence that never needed naming because it was structural, load-bearing, part of the architecture of who I was. When the Veil burned I held her hand in the smoke and swore with her the broken half of an oath, and when I broke mine to live and she kept hers to die, something passed between us that neither of us has ever said aloud, and I have spent ten years hunting the shape of that unsaid thing across half a kingdom's worth of silence.

Now the silence was producing children who spoke fire.

We rode into the foothills as the first hard frost whitened the ditches. Three days we worked the valleys, and everywhere the same story repeated with variations: a girl seen at wells, at shrine-corners, at the edges of fields, barefoot in autumn, asking questions in a tongue that made grown men forget their mothers' faces. Always moving north. Always toward the passes. And always—this was the detail I kept turning over at night like a stone with something underneath it—always described as *unafraid*. A child alone in wolf-country, walking roads that didn't exist, and no fear in her anywhere. Whatever had sent her, it hadn't sent her to hide. It had sent her to knock.

On the fourth day we found the shepherd.

He was a boy, really, fifteen at most, and he'd been found by my outriders sitting at the edge of a burned waystation—the smugglers' rest at Hollowmere, gutted by fire two nights prior, though the ash was wrong somehow, too white, too complete, as if the fire had eaten even the smell of burning. He was unhurt. He was also unable to say who had pulled him clear of the blaze, because whoever it was had spoken to him, and the speaking had taken the memory with it, the way a tide takes footprints.

"He keeps describing the voice," Pell said quietly, crouched beside the boy with his transcription book. "A woman's voice. He says it was like—" the boy murmured something, and Pell's pen stopped. "Like hearing a door open in a house you thought was empty."

Yes, I thought. That is precisely what she sounds like. That has always been precisely what she sounds like.

I sent the outriders back down with the boy and a report that would take three weeks to reach the capital, and I took Pell up into the high country alone, because whatever was happening in the Thornfells, I wanted to reach it before the machinery of the Crown arrived and began grinding everything it touched into paperwork and ash. I told myself that too was efficiency. I was getting very good at telling myself things.

The snow line came early that year. We camped beneath it, and in the night I dreamed of the Vessa river going still, and woke with frost on my blanket shaped, unmistakably, like a door.

* * *

It was on the sixth day, in a border village whose name I never learned, that I finally heard the language myself.

A shrine-priest there—an old man, half-blind, tending a chapel the diocese had forgotten—had taken in a barefoot child two nights running. By the time we arrived she was gone, but the priest was standing in the middle of his own chapel with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a man trying to hold water in his hands. His candles had guttered, he said. Or been snuffed. Or perhaps he had snuffed them. He could not recall the conversation he'd had with the girl. He could not recall whether there had been a conversation. But he had written something down immediately, before the forgetting could finish its work, in the margin of his ledger, in letters he did not recognize as his own handwriting.

Pell copied them out with trembling hands. I looked at them over his shoulder and the world went very narrow and very bright, because I knew the letters. I had learned them at seven years old, scratched into slate with a wet finger, kneeling beside a girl who was better at everything.

Four words. *The door is waking.*

I stood in that shabby chapel while Pell transcribed and the old priest wandered back to his candles, already losing the thread of why strangers were in his church, and I understood that I was no longer hunting an anomaly. Anomalies are curiosities. This was a countdown. The Veil was failing, the sealed country was pressing its hands against the inside of the world, and a child had walked out of the crack ahead of the flood to find the one person alive who could open the Door properly—the last swordspeaker, the oath-breaker, the woman I had buried alive on paper and spared in fact.

Every road led to Elodie. They had always led to Elodie. I had simply spent ten years standing at the crossroads refusing to read the signs.

"We return to Grey Ford," I told Pell. "Send the full report. All of it. The child, the language, the priest's ledger, everything."

He stared at me. "Inquisitor—if the Archive confirms this is Vessarine working, they'll send the Order. Not investigators. The full rite. They'll burn the high country to the snowline just to be certain."

"Yes," I said. "They will."

"And the woman in the mountains? The one the child seeks? If the Order finds her—"

"They won't," I said, and heard the steel come into my own voice, and recognized it as belonging to a younger woman, one who had knelt in ash and said *run far, then.* "Because I'm going to find her first."

Pell looked at me for a long moment, this honest young man with his careful pen, and I watched him decide—correctly—not to ask what an inquisitor of the Crown intended to do with a fugitive she intended to reach before her own order did. He was learning. It pained me to watch.

We rode down out of the village in the last light, and behind us the Thornfells stood up into the dark with the new snow smoking off their shoulders in the wind, and somewhere up there, in a cabin with a stove and a decade of deliberate forgetting, Elodie Oyelaran was opening her door to a child who spoke the language of the dead.

*Run far,* I had told her, ten years ago, kneeling in ash.

Far, it turned out, had never been far enough. The world was folding the map closed again, and every road in it ended at her door—including mine. I put my heels to the horse and rode down into the dark, carrying my orders like a knife I hadn't decided yet whether to use, and behind me the mountain wind carried, faint and impossible, a sound I refused for six more days to identify:

Singing. High and thin and in a tongue older than crowns. A lullaby, drifting down out of the passes, from a cabin that officially did not exist, sung by a woman who was officially dead—to a child the Crown had already begun spelling *anomaly* in triplicate.

By morning, I knew the song.

I had fallen asleep to it, once, when we were girls and the world was whole.

I turned my horse east, and rode.
