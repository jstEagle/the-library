## 6. Smoke Over Thornfell

The waystation at Colder's Hollow had stood for forty years and smelled like it—tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular staleness of men who'd stopped expecting anything better from life. I'd slept there maybe a hundred times. It was the kind of place that existed in the gaps between maps: too small for the Crown to tax, too mean for bandits to bother robbing.

I was three miles below it when the sky went the wrong color.

You learn to read smoke up here the way farmers read clouds. Woodsmoke is grey and lazy. Peat is blue-white and sweet. This was black, greasy, climbing in a column so straight it looked drawn with a ruler, and underneath it rode a smell I hadn't encountered since Vessar burned: *oil of consecration*, the stuff the Church uses when it wants fire to be a sacrament instead of an accident.

"Damn," I said, conversationally, to the child on my back.

She didn't answer. She never answered. Eight days she'd been with me, eight days of that liquid, vowel-heavy silence, and I had gotten no further in placing her than I had on the first night. Except now I knew what her language was, and knowing had ruined everything, because you can't unknow Vessarine. You can't hear it and stay the person you were before you heard it.

I set her down behind a granite shoulder and climbed the rest alone, keeping low through the scrub pine, and came out on a ledge where I could see the hollow whole.

They'd taken the waystation apart with a thoroughness I almost admired. The outbuildings were already collapsed into their own foundations. The main house burned like a pyre. And in the yard, arranged with bureaucratic neatness, were the smugglers themselves—six of them, faces down in the mud, hands bound. Not dead. The inquisitors wanted them breathing for transport, which told me this wasn't a punitive raid. It was an interrogation sweep. Somebody upstairs had decided the Thornfells were hiding something worth asking questions about.

At the center of the yard stood a man in grey, unhurried, watching his work the way a carpenter watches joinery. Even at this distance I recognized the posture. Inquisitors don't stand like soldiers; they stand like men who have never once needed to hurry, because everyone else does the hurrying for them.

And then he turned his head—not toward the fire, not toward his prisoners, but toward my ledge, three miles off, as if he'd heard me thinking—and I stopped admiring anybody's thoroughness.

I went down that mountain faster than dignity allows.

* * *

"We're leaving," I told Wren, bursting back into our shelter, and she looked up at me with those grave dark eyes and said nothing, because of course she did, but she was already standing, already holding the little bundle of her things, and I want it noted for posterity that the child was ready before I was.

I threw together what mattered: the oath-blade wrapped in oilcloth, hard cheese, dried fish, the flint, my good rope. I left the rest. Leaving things used to hurt. Ten years of practice had worn that down to a dull scrape, like a stone in a boot you've learned to walk on.

"They're sweeping the passes," I told her, mostly because talking kept the fear at a manageable volume. "Which means the low roads are dead and the high roads are watched, which means—" I paused, because what it meant was the one thing I'd sworn never to use again. "Which means we take the old road."

Wren tilted her head. And then, softly, in that language that made candles gutter and priests forget their own names, she said something short and questioning.

"I know," I said. "I heard you. No." Because I could guess, more or less, what she'd asked—the way you start guessing after eight days, reading the music even when you can't bear the words. She'd asked if we were going home. If I knew the way.

"No," I said again, harder this time, and slung the pack. "We're going away. There's a difference. Learn it early and it'll keep you alive."

She considered me with the terrible patience children have, and followed.

* * *

Here is what most people never understand about the Thornfell mountains: they are not one range. They are several ranges, folded over each other like hands in prayer, and between them run valleys that do not appear on any survey, because they cannot be found by anyone who has not been taught to look. The Crown calls these phantom valleys surveying errors. The smugglers call them luck. Both are wrong.

They are the Oathroads. They were cut, or found—I was never sure which—by the swordspeakers of old Vessar, and they exist only along spoken memory. You cannot walk them. You can only *recall* them, step by step, word by word, and the mountain agrees to hold still under your feet for exactly as long as your recollection holds. Forget yourself mid-crossing, and there is no crossing. There is just air, and whatever's at the bottom of air.

I had not walked an Oathroad since the burning. Ten years, I'd kept that door shut, and I'd told myself it was discipline. Standing at the trailhead—a wall of unbroken scree that every instinct insisted was a wall of unbroken scree—I understood at last that it had been cowardice wearing discipline's coat.

Because to open the road, I would have to speak.

Not think. Not remember silently. Speak. The road doesn't listen to the inside of your head. It listens to your voice, to the vibration of true words given to the open air, and every word of Vessarine I gave to the mountains would ring like a bell struck in the night. Somewhere behind us, a man in grey was listening for exactly that sound.

"Well," I said to Wren. "Choices."

She sat down on a rock, folded her hands in her lap, and waited. It should have been funny. A child of eight, barefoot in October, waiting for a smuggler to make up her mind. Instead it landed somewhere beneath my ribs and stayed there.

"You understand what happens if I don't say them," I told her. "We go around. Low roads. Maybe we live, maybe we don't, and if they catch you—" I didn't finish that sentence. I couldn't finish that sentence. Every time I tried, an image rose of what the Church did with linguistic anomalies, and my throat closed like a fist.

"And if I do say them," I went on, quieter, "the road opens. And so does everything else. Every ear within thirty miles."

She reached out and took my hand.

It was such a small thing. Her fingers barely wrapped around two of mine, and they were cold, cold as stream stones, and she held on with the matter-of-fact grip of someone who has decided that this is simply how things are now. She looked at the wall of scree, then at me, and she spoke one word of Vessarine.

I won't write it here. Some words you keep.

But it meant *please*, in the oldest register, the one used between kin, and I am ashamed to report how little it took. One word. One small cold hand. Ten years of silence, and it fell to a child to spend the coin I'd been hoarding like a miser.

"All right," I whispered. "All right. But you hold on to me, and you don't let go no matter what the mountain shows you. It likes to show things. That's part of how it works."

She nodded solemnly, and climbed onto my back, and hooked her arms around my neck with the trust of someone who had never once been dropped, and I stood facing ten years of locked silence, and I unlocked it.

"*Ashen gate, ash-kept way,*" I said, and my voice came out rusty and cracked and sounding, to my own ears, like my mother's. "*By the word the sword remembers, by the blade the word obeys—open.*"

The mountain opened.

There's no other honest way to put it. The scree didn't move; it *reconsidered*. What had been solid stone became a path, became a valley, became a green thread of country threading up between peaks that had no business being there, lit by a light that came from nowhere and cast no shadows. The air smelled of juniper and cold iron. Somewhere far above, wind chimes rang in a wind that touched nothing else.

Wren's arms tightened around my neck. She made a small sound—wonder, I thought, not fear—and pressed her cheek against my shoulder.

"Yeah," I said thickly. "Yeah, I know."

I stepped onto the road, and the world behind me closed like a book.

* * *

Walking an Oathroad is a strange labor. You must keep speaking—not constantly, but steadily, a phrase every hundred paces, enough to remind the mountain what it's agreeing to. The phrases rise out of you half-remembered, and each one costs. It's like drawing water from a well you sealed yourself: the bucket comes up heavier every time.

And the road shows you things. That's the toll nobody warns you about. It runs on memory, so it pays itself in memory, dredging up what it needs.

A mile in, I passed a farmstead that hasn't existed for twenty years, standing whole and golden in its impossible light, and my mother was in the doorway with flour on her hands, saying my name—

I bit the inside of my cheek until I tasted copper and kept walking. Said the next phrase. The farmstead dissolved politely, like a guest taking a hint.

Another mile, and it was the training yard at Vel Sarath, and Sera was laughing, fifteen years old and gap-toothed, telling me I gripped the sword like a farmer gripping a shovel, and I laughed too, in the memory, and the sound of my own young laugh in my own ears nearly buckled me at the knees.

"Stop it," I muttered—to the road, to myself, to the dead. "Take the toll in something cheaper."

The road, being a road, ignored me.

Wren, bless her, slept through some of it, which seemed impossible and probably was. When she woke she didn't fuss, didn't squirm. She watched the phantoms with wide, unafraid eyes, and once, when a ghost of a market street bloomed around us—stalls, awnings, voices haggling in Vessarine—she pointed at a fruit-seller's ghost and said a word, clear and certain, and the phantom turned its head.

I did not wait to see what it did next. I said the walking-phrase very fast and very firmly and we moved on, and I decided not to think about why the road's memories responded to her, and I thought about nothing else for the next hour.

* * *

We camped that night in a hollow the road provided—dry ground, a spring that ran sweet, a ring of stones that might have been placed by swordspeakers or might have been a trick of the light. I got a fire going small, the kind of fire that apologizes for existing.

Wren ate her share of cheese and fish without complaint and then sat looking at me across the flames with that patient gravity, and finally, deliberately, she pointed at herself. Then at me. Then she raised her eyebrows.

"A name," I said. "You want a name. Or you want to give me one. Or you're asking if I have one, which is a longer conversation than either of us wants."

She repeated the gesture, slower, in case I was slow.

I looked at her for a long time. Naming a thing is a claim. I'd spent ten years refusing to claim anything—not land, not people, not even my own history—and here was this scrap of sealed country sitting in my firelight wanting to be claimed, and worse, wanting to claim me right back.

"You can't be 'the girl,'" I said. "That'll get confusing when I'm shouting at you, which will happen daily." I chewed on it. Outside the firelight, the phantom-light of the road pulsed faintly against the stones, like breath. "There was a bird," I said finally. "Back home. They nested in the eaves of the sanctuary, little brown things, and the masters said they were good omens because they sang before dawn, before there was any reason to sing. Stubborn about it. Sang whether anyone listened or not."

Her head tipped, listening.

"Wren," I said. "It suits you. Small, stubborn, sings anyway." I shrugged, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "It's yours if you want it."

She tried it. Her mouth shaped the foreign syllables carefully, tasting them, and then she said "*Wren*" aloud in her own tongue's accent, and something in my chest turned over and lay back down.

Then she pointed at me. Raised both eyebrows. Insistent, now.

"Oh no," I said. "No. I've got a name. It's a bad one, full of holes, but it's mine, and I'm keeping it buried where I put it."

She pointed again. And then she did the worst possible thing: she waited. Silent, patient, absolutely certain, the way only the very young and the very old can wait, as though time were a resource she had in abundance and I did not.

I fed the fire a stick rather than answer. The flames took it greedily.

"Hartwell," I said at last, giving her the alias, the false bottom of the barrel. "Elodie Hartwell. Smuggler. Bad-tempered. Keeps to herself."

She studied me for a long moment with those dark eyes that saw entirely too much, and then she shook her head—once, gently, the way you correct a child—and pointed at me again, and said a word in Vessarine. Just one. Soft.

I didn't ask what it meant. I was afraid I already knew, because the tone of it was the tone of the sanctuary registers, the formal ones, the ones reserved for oath-names, and if this child of sealed Vessar had looked at me across a smuggler's fire and pronounced my oath-name—the one I broke, the one that died with my sisters—then she knew things about me that I had told no living soul.

"Sleep," I said roughly. "We move at first light."

She lay down on my bedroll without argument, and was asleep in minutes, breathing small and even, trusting me with her unconscious body the way she trusted me on the road. I sat up watching the fire and turning that one soft word over and over, trying not to understand it, failing completely.

* * *

Near dawn, the road woke me.

Not gently. The chime-wind stopped all at once, and the phantom-light guttered, and the mountain under us went tense as a held breath. I was on my feet with the oilcloth off the blade before my eyes finished opening.

Down the valley, far below, where the Oathroad threaded back into real country, a light was moving. Not fire. Greyer than fire. A lantern with a shuttered lens, the kind inquisitors carry, sweeping side to side across the lower slopes with the patience of a man mowing a field.

They couldn't see the road. Nobody sees the road. But they could see where it *ended*, and they were watching the exits, all of them, in a cordon that had been laid while we slept.

"They're not chasing us," I murmured. "They're herding us."

Wren stirred, sat up, followed my gaze, and then did something that made the cold settle into my bones and make itself comfortable. She tugged my sleeve. Pointed east—not down, not toward the lanterns, but east, deeper into the high country, where the maps show nothing but ice, and she spoke a single urgent sentence of Vessarine.

I caught maybe one word in five. But the word I caught was a direction-word, a *toward*-word.

And the thing it pointed toward, if my rust-eaten grasp of the old tongue served at all, was the same word Ivo Marsh's stolen notes kept circling like a moth round a lamp.

The Door.

"Of course," I said to the dawn, to the child, to the mountain. "Of course that's where you're going. You were never lost. You were *traveling*."

Far below, the grey lantern swung, patient as tide.

Wren looked up at me, and for the first time in eight days, she smiled.
