## 7. The Cartographer's Debt

The town of Lowmark sat at the bottom of the world like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up. It smelled of tanneries and wet wool and the particular despair of a place that existed only because the Crown hadn't gotten around to dissolving it yet.

I left Wren with Marta at the ferry house, which was less a trust than an arithmetic. Marta owed me for three unasked questions about the crates I'd moved through her cellar last spring, and Wren, for her part, had looked at me with those grave dark eyes and pressed one small hand flat against my chest—the way you'd steady something you weren't sure would still be there when you came back—and then gone off to help Marta salt fish without a word of complaint. She never complained. That was somehow worse than if she had.

"You'll come back," she'd said. Not asked. Said. In Vessarine, because she only spoke Vessarine, and every word out of her mouth was a small act of treason against the maps of the world.

"Before the moon's full," I'd answered, in the same tongue, and felt the words sit strangely in my chest, like a key turning in a lock I'd bricked over years ago.

Now I walked into Lowmark with a stolen survey rolled in oilcloth against my spine and tried to look like a woman with nothing more interesting than bad knees and a grudge against the weather.

* * *

Ivo Marsh lived above his own shop, which was the way of men who had lost everything else worth living above. The sign said MARSH & SONS, SURVEYORS AND DRAUGHTSMEN, and had said it for twenty years despite there being no sons and precious little surveying. Underneath, in smaller letters some joker had added long ago: *and maps of places that don't exist.*

The joke was truer than its maker knew.

I knocked the pattern—three, pause, two—that smugglers use when they want to be let in fast and forgotten faster. From inside came the scrape of a chair, the shuffle of feet, and then Ivo's voice, thin and suspicious: "We're closed."

"It's raining," I said. "And I have something you've wanted to see for ten years."

A long silence. Then the bolt drew back, and there he was: Ivo Marsh, once the finest cartographic mind in the royal service, now a stooped man of sixty-odd with ink stains to his elbows and eyes like a man who'd spent decades looking for something on the horizon and finding only weather.

He looked at me for a long moment. Recognition moved across his face slowly, the way frost moves across a window—not all at once, but cell by cell.

"Swordspeaker," he said quietly.

"Not anymore."

"No," he agreed, stepping aside. "None of us are what we were. Come in before the rain does."

* * *

His rooms were what I expected and worse. Maps everywhere—hung, folded, stacked, pinned to ceilings. And beneath them, under canvases and false drawer-bottoms, the ones that didn't exist. He kept them the way misers keep coins and widows keep letters.

I set the oilcloth bundle on his table without ceremony. "Crown survey of the Thornfell high passes. Dated this spring. Every patrol route, every waystation, every bridge rated by load-bearing capacity." I let that settle. "Inquisitor engineering corps did the work. They're building roads up there, Ivo. Roads toward the Door."

His hands hovered over the bundle but didn't touch it. Old habit. A cartographer learns early that touching a thing is claiming it, and claiming things gets people erased.

"They can't find the Door," he said. "It doesn't permit itself to be found."

"It permitted itself to be *made*. And men with enough paper and enough patience can find anything that was ever made." I unwound the oilcloth myself, since he wouldn't. "Look at the western margin. See the notation?"

He bent over it. His breath went out of him slowly.

"*Recitation sites,*" he read. "They've marked recitation sites. They know the Door answers speech." He straightened, and for the first time I saw fear in him, real fear, the kind that lives in the bones. "Then they don't mean to seal it. They mean to open it—or burn it. Either way—" He stopped. Looked at me. "You have a child with you. Marta's letters are circumspect but Marta is not."

"I have a child with me."

"And she speaks it."

"She speaks it."

Ivo sat down heavily, as though the conversation had weight and he'd been carrying it too long already. "Then you need my notes."

"I need your notes."

* * *

Here is what the histories will tell you—I say *will*, because I refuse to believe the histories are finished—about the erasure of Vessar:

That the kingdom burned. That the Veil fell. That the maps were redrawn and the tongues forgot themselves and within a generation no document, no monument, no memory remained outside the sealed country itself.

What the histories do not tell you is that erasure is work. It takes hands. Someone had to draw the new maps with the old country's borders smoothed away like a scar healed over. Someone had to decide which mountain names survived and which became "the unnamed peaks." Someone had to stand in a room with the old charts of Vessar and feed them, page by patient page, into a fire hot enough to burn vellum.

Ivo Marsh was that someone. Or one of that someones. He had mapped Vessar in his youth—the last complete survey, rivers and roads and the Door of Ash itself, drawn from life—and then, when the order came down, he had been given the honor of unmapping it. He used to say, in the years before we stopped speaking, that it was the only commission he'd ever completed perfectly, and the only one he prayed nightly to fail.

"The notes aren't here," he said, when I asked.

"I didn't think they'd be anywhere so sensible."

He led me to the back room, past shelves of honest trade atlases, to a wall hung with a map of the southern coast. He lifted it down. Behind it, a cavity in the plaster, and in the cavity, wrapped in waxed linen, a packet thin as a psalter.

"Fragments," he warned, setting it on the table between us like a relic or an accusation. "When they ordered the destruction, I copied what I could from memory afterward. Memory is a poor draftsman. But the Door—" his finger traced a shape on the topmost sheet, a seam, a line like a crack in the world rendered in careful faded ink, "—the Door I drew twice. Once with my eyes and once with my heart. The heart's version has held better."

I looked at the drawing. Something in my chest turned over, slow and cold, like ice shifting in a spring lake.

I knew that seam. I had stood before it ten years ago, bleeding, oath-broken, while behind it a country screamed and then didn't.

"You were there," Ivo said softly. Watching my face. "At the fall. I heard rumors. A swordspeaker who walked out of the burning and lived."

"Rumors exaggerate."

"Do they? Then correct them."

I didn't. He waited, and I let him wait, and finally he sighed and turned back to his fragments, because we were both cowards in our own dialects and both fluent in each other's.

"The Crown's reach," he said instead, tapping the stolen survey. "This is what you came to learn, yes? Not just the road. The reach." He spread the sheets wider. "Inquisitor columns here, here, and here. Supply lines through Lowmark itself—they've requisitioned the granary. They're not hunting a smuggler, Elodie. They're provisioning an occupation. Of a mountain."

"How long?"

"At this rate? Before midwinter. They'll burn the Door rather than let it answer anyone. Fire is the only erasure they trust completely." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, a smile worn smooth by self-hatred. "I would know. I was their fire once."

* * *

We struck the bargain over his bad wine, which tasted like vinegar that had given up on ambition.

The survey was mine to give; the notes were his to lend. But Ivo, being Ivo, attached conditions the way barnacles attach to hulls.

"When you reach the Door," he said, "if you reach it—if it opens—there's a name I want spoken. My true name. Not 'Ivo Marsh,' which is the name they gave me to wear after, but the one I was born with, before the service took it and issued me a replacement like a uniform."

"That's a strange thing to ask."

"It's the only thing I have left to ask. Everything else I gave them willingly." He refilled my cup with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "Names are the one currency the Veil respects. Speak mine at the threshold, and whatever's left of me that was honest will know it was remembered."

I should have refused. I should have told him that names spoken at thresholds are how oaths get made, and broken oaths are how countries burn. Instead I looked at his ruined hands and his wall of beautiful lies and I said, "If the Door opens, I'll speak it."

Because I am, at bottom, a woman who cannot stop paying other people's debts.

"One more thing." He rolled the fragments carefully, reverently, and tied them with cord. "The child. When she speaks, does the world listen back?"

I thought of guttering candles. A priest forgetting his own sentence halfway through it. "Yes."

"Then understand what you're carrying." He met my eyes, and for a moment the disgraced drunkard fell away and the royal cartographer looked out, the man who had measured kingdoms. "The Veil isn't falling because it's weak. It's falling because something on the other side is pulling. And the Crown knows it. Why do you think they're moving now, after ten years of quiet? They've felt the pull too. Everyone with a chart and a conscience has felt it." He pressed the packet into my hands. "You're not running ahead of the storm, swordspeaker. You're running beside it. And storms don't leave witnesses."

* * *

I left Lowmark before dawn, taking the river road north with the fragments against my spine where the survey had been, trading one burden for another and calling it progress.

Behind me the town slept on, granary full of soldiers' bread, and I thought about Ivo standing alone in his shop of nonexistent maps, waiting for a name he might never hear spoken, and I decided that was a cruelty I'd examine later, when later seemed likely to happen.

The moon was nearly full. I'd promised Wren.

I put my head down and walked faster.
