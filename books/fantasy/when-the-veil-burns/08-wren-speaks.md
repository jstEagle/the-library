## 8. Wren Speaks

*(What follows is not what Wren said. Wren says nothing. What follows is what came through, the night she took Elodie's hand in her sleep and did not let go, set down as faithfully as a broken bell can ring. Some words are missing. Some words are missing on purpose.)*

---

I am in the house of sleep and the door does not knock itself.

In the waking place I have no mouth-words. In this place I have too many. They come out of me like water from a cracked jar and I cannot stop them and I do not want to stop them, because stopping is what I did for so long, walking and walking with my mouth sewn shut by silence that was not mine, and here —

Here the words just *go*.

The woman sleeps in the next room. The tall one. The sad one. She smells like cold iron and pine smoke and like a word someone said once over a grave. I know her smell. I knew it before I knocked on her door. That is why I knocked. Not because I was lost.

Because I was *sent*.

I sent myself. Both things are true. In Vessar both things can be true at once and nobody's head breaks about it.

I go to her room. My feet make no sound because in the house of sleep feet only make sound if you are afraid, and I am not afraid, I am the opposite of afraid, I am so full of not-afraid it hurts behind my eyes. I put my small hand on her big hand where it lies on the blanket near the knife she keeps there even asleep, especially asleep.

Her fingers close around mine.

Good, says her hand. Good. Come on then.

---

We fall together. Falling is how you travel here. You do not walk to Vessar. You fall toward it, the way rain falls toward the sea, because everything that came out of Vessar wants to go back, even the people who ran, even the woman whose face is doing the thing faces do when they are trying not to remember.

We fall past stars that are not stars. They are candles. Somebody lit ten thousand candles in the dark and then left, and the wax has never once dripped.

Then the falling stops being falling and starts being standing, and we are standing on a road I know, oh, I know it, I know every stone of it the way you know your own teeth —

and the road is on fire,

and the fire is not moving.

---

That is the first thing you must understand, if you are reading this with your waking ears. The fire does not move. The smoke does not rise. It hangs in the air like grey silk hung out to dry by giants, and through the silk the sun comes down orange and stopped, a sun caught halfway through setting on the worst evening of the world.

The houses burn without burning down. The flames stand in the windows like people looking out.

Elodie makes a sound. It is not a word. It is the sound a word makes when it dies before it gets born. Her hand tightens on mine until it almost hurts, and I hold on back, because that is the whole reason I brought her, isn't it. To hold on back.

*Where,* she tries to say. Her voice in the dream comes out wrong, all echo and rust, a bell dropped down a well. *Where is this. When is this.*

And I answer her the only way I can answer anything, which is completely:

**This is the last hour of Vessar. It is always the last hour of Vessar. It will be the last hour of Vessar until somebody says otherwise.**

---

We walk into the city.

I should say its name. I reach for its name and the name is there, whole, heavy, warm as bread — and then I watch Elodie's face when I begin to say it, and her face goes white as the token on its cord around my neck, and I understand that the name is a wound in her and I am pressing on it, so I fold the name up small and put it away, and I point instead. Pointing works. Pointing is a language too.

We walk the great street with the fountains. The fountains are frozen mid-splash, water hanging in the air like glass birds. There is a dog mid-leap over a low wall, four feet off the ground, ears flying, forever. There is a market square where the awnings smolder in colors — red, saffron, a green like river weed — and under the awnings stand the people.

They are asleep.

Not dead. Asleep. I need Elodie to know the difference so badly my chest cracks with it, so I take her sleeve and pull her to the nearest one, an old man with flour still on his hands, caught mid-turn, his mouth open around whatever he was saying when the Veil came down. His chest moves. Up. Down. Up. Slow as a season.

*Look,* I tell her. **He is breathing. They are all breathing. Ten years of breathing, slow as trees. Nobody died that day. Everybody just — stopped.**

Elodie kneels in the street of her burning country and looks up into the old man's floury, unfinished face, and she weeps without any noise at all, which I think must be something oath-speakers learn, crying quietly, saving noise for the important parts.

She reaches out. She stops her hand a finger-width from his cheek.

*Don't,* I say quickly. **If you touch them while the seal holds, you'll wake them into the burning. The fire is still real underneath. The Veil doesn't put fires out. It only pauses them. Wake a man inside a paused flame and he finishes dying.**

Her hand comes back. She folds both arms across her chest like she is holding herself shut, and I know — I don't know how I know, but I know — that she is holding in not tears now but a word. A big one. A sword-shaped one.

---

We go where I was going anyway. I do not get to choose much in the waking place, but in this place the choosing walks ahead of me and I follow it, and it leads us up the hill to the white house with the blue tiles, my house, the one with the lemon tree in the courtyard, except the lemons hang stopped and gold as little lanterns and the tree hums very faintly, the way a struck string hums after the music ends.

My mother is in the doorway.

She is kneeling. She was kneeling when it happened — reaching for me, I think, or reaching for the door to bar it, I have looked at this so many nights I have worn two stories smooth into one. Her hands are open. Her mouth is open around my name.

I have stood here every night since I learned to fall. Every night I stand in front of my mother and look at the shape my name makes in the air between us, unfinished, waiting, and every night I try to finish it for her, to say my name into her open mouth like feeding a bird, and every night nothing happens, because a name only counts if the speaker is heard, and the seal eats hearing, and —

And tonight is different.

Tonight I have a swordspeaker's hand in mine.

I put Elodie's hand against my shoulder blade, where the mark is. Under the skin. The little spiral, the one they gave me when I was born too soon and too quiet, the mark that means *this one carries*. She breathes in sharp through her teeth. She felt it. Even through cloth, through skin, through ten years and a burned kingdom, she felt it, because marks call to oaths the way moons call to tides.

*You're a carrier,* she whispers, and her whisper is half horror, which stings, and half awe, which heals the sting. **Yes. I carried the token out. I carried the words out. Someone had to be small enough to slip under the closing and strong enough to keep the words alive in their mouth. So they chose me. Or I volunteered. Both things are true. Nobody's head broke about it.**

*Who sent you?*

And here is the part I have never been able to show anyone, because there was no one to show, and I turn her around by the shoulders — she lets me, this iron woman, she lets a child turn her like a boat on water — and I point down the great street, past the frozen fountains, to the tower at the end of it, the Speaker's Spire, where the flame stands in the topmost window like a prisoner pacing a cell.

**Her,** I say. **The last one who stayed awake. She held the pause. She is still holding it. One old woman holding a whole country's breath the way you'd hold a door closed with your body. And she is tired, swordspeaker. She is so tired. Ten years on her feet inside one second.**

*Why me?* Elodie says, and her voice does the rust-bell thing again. *There were twelve of us. Eleven are — *

She doesn't finish. The dream flinches. Somewhere far off in the paused burning, a beam that has hung suspended for a decade creaks, one degree, and settles, and every sleeping face in the square turns a hair toward the sound, and I feel how thin the ice of the world is, how the whole sealed country is a held note that could crack.

**Eleven are gone,** I finish for her gently, because I am allowed to say it; it isn't my wound. **But oaths don't die with the mouths that spoke them. Oaths wait. Hers waited for you. She said — she made me memorize it, in the last minute before the seal closed, she pressed it into my mouth like a coin — she said:**

I stop. Because saying it means becoming it, and once I start I won't be able to stop until it's all out, and the words are enormous, they are stair-sized, they were built for grown throats and sworn hands, and I am eight years old in a dress with a burnt hem.

Elodie kneels down. She puts her forehead against mine. Iron and pine smoke, cold and warm.

*Say it,* she says. *I'll carry the weight of the hearing. You just carry the saying.*

So I open my mouth, and out comes the message, the whole impossible message, the one that has been sitting on my tongue for a hundred leagues and a hundred nights:

**The Door remembers the hands that broke it. The Door asks for the hands that broke it. Ash opens to ash. A name pays for a nation. Come home, sister. Bring your voice. We are running out of second.**

Silence. Even the humming lemon tree goes quiet.

When Elodie straightens up, something has changed in her face. Something has closed and something has opened, and I think — I am eight, but I am not stupid — I think I am watching a woman decide to stop hiding.

*Ten years,* she says softly. *Ten years I told myself breaking the oath was the worst thing I ever did. That surviving was the sin.* Her hand finds the hilt of the blade she hasn't drawn since before I was born, and rests there, not gripping. Just resting. Like touching a scar. *But an oath isn't broken while its words still exist somewhere. And you've been carrying them in your mouth, little bird, all this way, through snow and border stones and priests whose candles went out.*

She looks down at me and her eyes are doing the wet bright thing.

*You didn't come to me lost,* she says. *You came to collect a debt.*

**Both things are true,** I say, and grin at her, because in Vessar we can hold contradictions like two loaves of bread, one under each arm, and nobody's head breaks about it.

---

But dreams are honest, and honesty has a cost, and as the edges of the vision begin to soften — morning pulling at us, the candle-stars guttering, my mother's unfinished name folding itself away for another night — the dream shows me one thing more. Dreams always show one thing more. It's how they tattle.

At the edge of the city, where the road runs south toward the mountains and toward her, a line of figures stands in the paused smoke. Grey coats. Grey hoods. Faces like paper. They are not asleep and they are not of Vessar and they should not be here — the seal keeps out everything except carriers and debts — but there they are, patient as frost, and one of them is closer than the others, and this one is different. This one's face is not paper. This one's face is a face Elodie knows, a face from before the burning, a face the dream shows me turning slowly toward us across the frozen fire, lips forming a word that is not Vessarine and not Crown-speech either but something older, something that sounds like a key turning in a lock I didn't know our world had—

I woke up on the floor of the cabin with my hand still clenched around Elodie's, both of us gasping like surfacers, and outside, far down the valley, though it was barely dawn and no rider should have been abroad —

hoofbeats.

Many. Coming north.

Elodie was already moving, snatching the ash-white token from the table, her face gone grey as the dream-smoke. "Wren," she said, in her rough tongue, strapping on the blade she had not touched in ten years, "whatever you showed me in there — I think someone else saw it too."
