## 9. Sera's Bargain

The summons came at dusk, which I have always considered a coward's hour for bad news.

I was in the foothill garrison at Marrow's Rest, a place named for something unpleasant and built by people who wanted to be reminded of it daily. My quarters were a converted granary with a view of the latrines and a draft that whistled in a minor key. Ten years an inquisitor, and this was what the Crown thought I'd earned: a room that smelled of old barley and other men's ambitions.

The courier was young, sweating despite the cold, holding the dispatch case like it might hatch.

"Priority seal, Inquisitor Vane. From the Lord-Justiciar himself."

"I can read the wax," I said. "What I can't read is your face. You look like a man delivering a funeral notice to someone who doesn't know they've died."

He blinked. "It's... promotion business, I think, ma'am."

"Then he wants something." I took the case. "That's the only time anyone offers me anything."

He fled. Smart boy. He'd go far in the Inquisition.

I broke the seal alone, because whatever was inside, I already knew who would be in it. Not by name—the Crown had never once written her name where ink could be subpoenaed—but by shape. By the particular weight of paper when the thing being asked of you is unforgivable.

*Inquisitor Vane,* it began. *Your pursuit of the linguistic anomalies in the Thornfell range has been noted with approval.*

Anomalies. That was the word they'd chosen for a dead language walking around in a child's mouth. I'd sent four reports using the word *Vessarine* and every one had come back redacted, the word struck through so hard the pen had torn three sheets beneath it. The Crown did not deny Vessar existed. It simply refused, on principle, to permit the fact.

*His Excellency the Lord-Justiciar extends an offer. The successful conclusion of your current operation—specifically, the apprehension of the individual designated the Thornfell Speaker—will be rewarded with command of the Eastern Circuit. Full authority. Your own writ.*

Eastern Circuit. Four provinces. A salary that would make my mother weep. A seal of my own, so I'd never again stand in a room while some pimpled magistrate explained my own jurisdiction to me.

And then the second paragraph. There is always a second paragraph. In the Inquisition we called it the hook behind the honey, though officially we called it nothing, because naming things was how you ended up on the wrong side of a tribunal.

*Be advised: the Speaker is to be taken alive if practicable. If not practicable, the Justiciar will accept delivery of proof of death. The child, however, must be delivered alive and unharmed. His Excellency has particular interest in the asset.*

Particular interest in the asset.

I read that sentence six times. I poured wine over the first reading, because I'd guessed it was coming and still needed the ritual. I read it again standing, sitting, and once upside down out of pure spite, as though a change of angle might change the meaning.

They didn't want the child saved. They wanted the child *delivered*. Alive, unharmed, and in their hands—a distinction that sounds like mercy until you watch what the Inquisition does with things it finds interesting.

Somewhere up in those white mountains, Elodie Oyelaran was keeping that child warm.

*

Let me tell you about my sister-in-oath, since the official record won't.

We were fifteen when we swore, kneeling in the frost of the Vessarine high court with our palms cut open on the same blade, because that was the tradition—one sword, two oaths, blood mingling on steel so that neither could lie to the other without tasting it. She swore first. She always went first; she said someone had to check whether the ice would hold.

Elodie was the better swordswoman. I was the better liar, which in the end proved the more durable skill, and I have hated myself for that observation in eleven different languages.

When the Veil burned—when the sky over Vessar turned the color of a healing wound and the mountains folded shut around everything we had been—I survived because I ran toward the Crown instead of away from it. That is the honest version. The version I prefer involves strategy, intelligence-gathering, the long game. The truth is that I saw the fire come over the ridge and my legs chose a direction and my legs have never once apologized.

She survived too. I learned that years later, from a smuggler's manifest, of all things—a name, a route through Thornfell, a woman moving contraband with a swordspeaker's economy of motion. I sat with that manifest for a whole night. I could have burned it. Instead I filed it, and told no one, and told myself that was loyalty.

Ten years I have known where she is, and for ten years I have not gone. That is either love or cowardice or the thing that lives between them wearing both their clothes, and I have stopped trying to sort the laundry.

Now the Crown had noticed what I'd spent a decade not-noticing. Anomalies. A speaker. And somewhere above the snowline, Elodie had broken her silence badly enough that even the redactors couldn't keep up.

I wrote back accepting the meeting. What else was there? Refuse, and they'd send someone worse—someone who hadn't shared blood on a blade, who wouldn't hesitate at the ford or the door or the moment when hesitation was the only mercy left in the world.

At least this way, I told myself, I'm the one holding the knife.

I want you to notice that sentence. Hold onto it. We'll come back to it, and I'll be ashamed of it later, but shame is a tax I pay annually and I'd rather defer the installment.

*

The Justiciar received me in a room with no windows, which I understood to be a message: nothing said here would ever see daylight, including, possibly, me.

Corvane Dross was not the monster the pamphlets promised. That was the trouble with him. Monsters are easy; you can fight monsters. Corvane was a tidy man with kind eyes and the voice of a village priest, and he listened the way a well listens—deeply, patiently, and with the firm expectation that eventually you'll fall in.

"Inquisitor Vane." He gestured me into a chair that had been positioned to face him directly, no table between us. Intimacy as architecture. "Eleven years of service. Three commendations. Two reprimands—" he smiled, "—both of which I've read closely, and both of which suggest to me a woman who understands that rules are tools, not temples."

"I prefer to think of them as weapons," I said. "Tools build things. Weapons decide whose things survive."

His smile widened a fraction. He liked that. God help me, I knew he'd like that; I'd been performing competence for men like him since I was twenty-two, and the muscle memory holds even when the stomach turns.

"The Thornfell operation," he said. "Bring me the Speaker and the child, and the Eastern Circuit is yours. I say that plainly because I know you distrust ornament."

"And the child?" I kept my voice level. "My reports flagged concerns about handling protocols for—"

"For the asset. Yes." He folded his hands. "You may speak plainly, Inquisitor. This room forgets."

Rooms don't forget. Men do, when it's convenient, and Corvane had made forgetting a career. But I leaned forward anyway, because here was the door I'd ridden two days through sleet to reach.

"The language is a working," I said. "You know that. Every report I've filed says it. The words do things—to memory, to fire, to the minds of anyone listening who isn't protected. If your people try to interrogate that child, they'll get nothing, and they may get less than nothing. Some of your questioners will lose hours. Days." I let that sit. "And if the child dies in your care, the working dies with her, and whatever your Excellency hopes to open with it stays shut forever."

I watched him consider this. Not the ethics—he had none to consider, which simplified matters—but the arithmetic. Corvane did everything by arithmetic. It was why he frightened me more than any zealot I'd served under. Zealots can be starved of faith. You cannot starve a man of sums.

"You propose yourself as handler," he said.

"I propose myself as the only person in this organization who speaks enough of the language to manage the asset, and the only officer with a working relationship with the Speaker." Working relationship. Eleven years of tracking a ghost, and I called it a working relationship the way you call a scar a souvenir.

"And your terms?"

Here it was. The moment the chapter turns. I had rehearsed it on the road, in the inn, in my head at three in the morning staring at ceiling beams. I had rehearsed it until the words were smooth as river stones, and still, saying them aloud, I heard how thin they rang.

"The child comes to me within the hour of capture. No one else touches her. I conduct all interviews, I set all conditions, and I deliver her to your facility personally when—and only when—I judge her stable." A pause, the knife turning in my own hand. "And the Speaker is taken alive. If resistance forces the issue—if there's a fight, if she draws that blade of hers and someone panics—that's battlefield necessity, regrettable, investigated and cleared before the paperwork cools. But if it's my hand, my choice, my order? She lives. Bound, tried, imprisoned, whatever you need after—but alive."

"Why?" he asked, mildly, terribly. "She's a traitor to the Crown. She burned her oath. Why does her pulse matter to you?"

Because she taught me to hold a sword. Because she checked the ice first. Because there is a girl of fifteen in my chest who cut her palm on shared steel and has never stopped bleeding, and every year I wrap the wound in duty and every year it soaks through.

"She's worth more alive," I said instead. "A cooperating Speaker opens doors a corpse can't. Dead martyrs breed sympathizers. Live prisoners breed precedents." All true. None of it true. The best lies are ledgers—you can audit every line and the sum still comes out wrong.

Corvane studied me for a long moment, and I felt the well considering whether to fall in me.

"You're asking me to believe," he said, "that the Inquisition's coldest hound has gone soft over one traitor."

"I'm asking you to believe I'm thorough. Sentiment is sloppy. This is policy." I held his eyes. "Give me the clause, and I'll give you the Speaker and the child by spring thaw. Refuse, and I'll still get them for you—but slower, and with more fires, and you'll spend the difference in blood and gold wondering why you were proud."

Silence. Somewhere beyond the windowless walls, a bell rang the hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Alive," he said at last. "If she's taken cleanly. The clause goes in the private record—not the operational file. If she resists, Inquisitor, if she costs me a single rider, the clause burns and I will not mourn it." He extended his hand. "Do we understand each other?"

Perfectly, I thought. You've just bought my sister's life with my sister's freedom, and priced the child separately, and I've signed.

"We do," I said, and shook it, and his grip was dry and cool and certain, a man shaking hands with the future.

*

I rode out of the capital that night because the city felt like a coat that had shrunk.

Two days of sleet, then the foothills, then the long grey climb into country that pretended, as always, to be empty. I made camp alone below the tree line, and sat by the fire doing the thing I do instead of praying: reviewing the ledger.

Here is what I had bought. Elodie's life—conditional, revocable, contingent on her surrendering quietly, which anyone who knew her knew was about as likely as snow in July. The child in my custody, alive and unharmed, delivered to a man with kind eyes and no windows. Command of the Eastern Circuit, should I complete the exchange.

Here is what I had sold. I ran the inventory honestly, because dishonesty with myself is the one luxury I've never permitted—it's the flaw that gets everyone else killed, and I refuse to die of it.

The child. I had sold the child. Oh, I'd dressed it in handler clauses and stability assessments and personal custody, but underneath the tailoring, the transaction was simple: *give me Elodie's heartbeat, and you can have the girl.* I had looked at a mute eight-year-old I'd never met and calculated her against a woman I loved, and I'd done the sum in Corvane's own currency, and I'd shaken his dry cool hand.

Mercy, I told the fire. It's mercy. The alternative was refusing the meeting, losing control of the operation, letting some zealot captain put a bolt through them both at the first ford. At least this way Elodie breathes. At least this way I'm the one holding the knife.

There it was. The sentence from earlier, come home to roost. *I'm the one holding the knife.* As though proximity to the wound were a kindness. As though the difference between being stabbed and being handed, gently, into the dark were anything but a difference in etiquette.

The fire cracked. High above, past the black teeth of the ridgeline, Thornfell stood white under the stars, and somewhere up there a woman who had sworn beside me was teaching a child songs in a language the Crown had forbidden the world to remember. I wondered if she sang. She used to hum when she sharpened, off-key, unbothered, and I had mocked her for it and memorized every tune.

I took out my maps. I spread them by firelight, the smuggler routes, the water sources, the places a clever exile might run when the smoke started. I began planning the hunt the way I plan everything—thoroughly, coldly, leaving nothing to chance except the one thing that always ruins me.

Because here is the ledger's final line, the entry I write every night and strike through every morning: I am going to find her. I am going to offer her the bargain—her life for the child, my word on it, blood on it if she demands. And when she refuses, because she will refuse, because she is Elodie and she has never once let me check the ice first—

When she refuses, I will have to choose.

I fed the map-corner to the flames and watched it curl. Spring thaw, I'd promised. I intended to be up that mountain long before. Not out of eagerness.

Out of fear of what the waiting would make of me.

Far to the north, faint as a struck match, the horizon flickered—once, twice—like a veil beginning, very slowly, to burn.

I saddled my horse in the dark and started climbing.
