## 11. Ambush at Hollow Ford

### SERA

There is a particular silence that falls over a column of riders when they know they are close to their quarry. It isn't discipline. Discipline sounds like order. This sounds like held breath—thirty men and women who have spent nine days climbing into a mountain range that does not want them, all of them pretending they aren't afraid of the trees.

I let them hold their breath. Fear keeps inquisitors honest, or at least it keeps them quiet.

"Ford's ahead," said Corporal Dain, reining up beside me. He was young for the rank, promoted because everyone senior to him had either died or learned to avoid me. "Scout says the ice will take a horse."

"The scout says," I repeated. "The scout also said there were no linguistic anomalies in Thornfell, and now we're chasing one through a pass that appears on no survey I've ever signed off on."

He had the grace not to answer. Good. Dain was learning too.

The truth—the truth I kept folded up small beneath my breastplate, where it couldn't cut anyone—was that I knew exactly where Elodie Oyelaran would be. Not from the reports. Not from the burned waystation or the trail of a child's bare footprints that had haunted me since the foothills. From ten years of knowing her. If you cornered Elodie, she wouldn't run downhill toward the lowlands where help might be. She'd run uphill, along the spine of the world, toward the thing she was trying to protect. She had always been predictable in only one direction: forward, into the fire, carrying whatever needed carrying.

So when the scouts reported movement toward Hollow Ford—a crossing so minor the Crown's maps didn't bother naming it—I didn't need divination. I needed arithmetic. Two fugitives, one pass, one place shallow enough to cross with a child. She would be there by dusk.

Which meant I had until dusk to decide what I was going to do when I caught her.

The orders in my saddlebag were unambiguous. *Apprehend the swordspeaker. The child is to be taken alive and intact.* Alive and intact, as though Wren were a document. As though any of us believed the child would remain either for long once the archivists in the capital finished their questions. I had read enough sealed files to know what happened to anomalies after the questioning stopped.

And Elodie? The orders didn't say what to do with her if she resisted. They didn't have to. Everyone in the column understood the grammar of *swordspeaker* the way you understand the grammar of a funeral.

I had told myself, riding up out of the foothills, that I would find a third option. That I always did. That the woman I'd sworn half an oath beside—before the burning, before the breaking, before she vanished into these mountains like a word scrubbed from a page—deserved better than a file number and a shallow grave.

But here is what ten years as an inquisitor teaches you about third options: they exist exactly as long as it takes for your superiors to notice you looking for them.

The ford came into view through the pines just as the light went the color of old pewter. A narrow river, frozen at the edges, running black and quick down its center. Beyond it, the ground climbed into country where thirty riders would be twenty-nine too many.

And there—at the near bank, crouched behind a fallen pine with a child pressed against her side—was the reason I had stopped believing in third options years ago and kept looking anyway.

She looked older. Of course she did. Ten years will do that. But it wasn't the gray threading her braid or the lines around her eyes that struck me. It was that she stood like someone who had made peace with being hunted, the way other people make peace with weather. She'd heard us coming, obviously. She'd known for an hour. And still she hadn't crossed yet, because the ice wouldn't take them both before dark, and she would rather die on this bank than risk Wren going through the river.

*Fool*, I thought, with the tenderness I was permitted precisely nowhere else in my life.

"Hold here," I told Dain. "No one advances without my word."

"You think she'll surrender?"

I watched Elodie rise slowly from behind the log, one hand going to the wrapped bundle on her back that every report insisted was farming tools and I knew, with a certainty that felt like an old wound reopening, was not.

"No," I said. "I think she'll offer us a choice. She always did." I swung down from the saddle. "That's what makes her dangerous."

I walked to the bank alone. Some things you owe a person face to face.

"ELODIE OYELARAN." My voice carried across the water the way trained voices do, flat and official, and I hated how easily the office fit my mouth. "By authority of the Crown, surrender yourself and the child."

She looked at me across the black water, and for a moment neither of us was an inquisitor or a smuggler or anything but two women standing on opposite banks of a decade.

"Sera." Just my name. No title. She said it the way you'd set down something fragile. "You brought thirty people to a conversation."

"I brought thirty people to an arrest. The conversation's a courtesy."

"To me or to them?"

"To both of us." I kept my hands visible, away from my sword. A gesture. Maybe a true one; I genuinely didn't know anymore. "Walk away from the child, Elodie. Come with me. The orders are specific about her. They're less specific about you, and I am very good at writing reports that say less than they know."

Something moved through her face—not hope. Hope had left her face years ago, if it had ever lived there. Recognition, maybe. Grief wearing recognition's coat.

"And the girl goes home to her mother," Elodie said. "That's the price of my surrender. Say it."

"I can't say it."

"Then we don't have a conversation. We have a geography problem, and you're on the wrong side of it."

Behind me, Dain shifted his weight. Ahead of me, Elodie's hand rose to the wrappings on her back, and began, unhurried, almost gentle, to unwind them.

I had seen that blade once before—ten years ago, in the last hour of Vessar, when oaths were things you could still break rather than things that broke you. I had spent ten years telling myself I'd imagined the way it seemed to drink the light.

I hadn't imagined it.

"Elodie," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable like ice under a boot heel. "Don't. Whatever you're about to do—there are crossbows on the ridge, and I can't call them off twice."

"I know," she said. And then, softly, in a language that has no business sounding that much like a lullaby: "*Vessa en dorath.*"

The river answered.

### ELODIE

Here is what nobody tells you about the word of power: it doesn't feel powerful. It feels like taking off boots you've worn for ten years.

*Vessa en dorath.* Truth remembers. Two words, three if you count the way they lean on each other, and I had kept them locked behind my teeth for a decade the way you keep a key hidden from yourself. Every smuggled crate, every silent winter, every night I lay awake listening to the mountain breathe—all of it built on never saying those words aloud. Because words, in my old country, were not descriptions. They were instructions. And I had given myself one final instruction the day Vessar burned: *forget.*

Wren pressed against my leg, watching the riders with the terrible calm of a child who has already survived the worst thing. Her hand found mine. She didn't sign *run*. She signed something else—one of the old hand-words, the ones mothers used before speech, before the Veil, before everything.

*Stand,* she signed. *You stand.*

Children see straight through to the load-bearing walls of a person. I drew the oath-blade.

Ten years of rust came off it in a single sound—not a scrape but a note, low and clear, as though the steel had been holding its breath the whole time and finally exhaled. The light changed around it. Not brighter. Truer. The snow on the far bank turned the white of paper instead of the white of bone. Even the black water seemed to slow, considering itself.

Across the ford, Sera went very still.

Thirty crossbows on the ridge. Thirty men and women whose names I didn't know and couldn't afford to. One woman on the near bank whose name I knew better than my own heartbeat, and that was the whole problem, laid out in ice and iron.

They loosed.

I have no memory of deciding. That's the honest truth of it, and honesty was the whole point of the exercise. The word came up through me like water finding a crack—not chosen, remembered—and I spoke it into the river.

"*DORATH.*"

Remember.

The ice did not shatter so much as *obey*. Every pane of it from bank to bank stood up out of the water at once, a wall of frozen glass taller than two men, and the bolts hit it and stopped—not deflected, *stopped*, hanging in the ice like flies in amber. For one absurd heartbeat the whole battlefield was silent except for the river muttering underneath its own bones.

Then the wall came down.

It came down outward, away from us, a wave of shattered ice that swept the near bank and took the riders' horses off their feet in a tangle of screaming and steam. Men scattered. The ridge line dissolved into chaos. And through the middle of it, untouched, a corridor of clear ground opened between me and Sera Vane, as precise as if the river had read my heart and found it embarrassingly specific.

Sera picked herself up out of the slush, sword drawn, hair plastered to her face, and looked at me across fifteen feet of ruined ice.

Neither of us moved.

This was the moment. I want to be exact about it, because everything afterward depended on its exactness. She was open—sword line broken, balance gone, fifteen feet of treacherous footing between us, and I was holding a blade that had just parted a river. One stroke. The oath-blade sang in my hand with the memory of every vow it had ever kept, and every one of those vows said the same thing: *a swordspeaker ends threats.*

Sera Vane was a threat. She had ridden nine days through a hostile range to put an arrow in a child's future, and she would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, because that was what she was now. The blade knew it. The blade offered.

And I thought: *she held my hair back when I was sick with mountain fever, the first winter of our training, and told me the mountains were just hills that refused to lie down.*

I thought: *she lied to her commander to buy me an hour, the night Vessar burned, and has probably hated herself for it every day since.*

I thought: *if I kill her, I become the Crown's story about me. The last swordspeaker, mad and dangerous, rightly put down.*

I turned the blade aside.

Not a feint. Not a hesitation. I stepped inside her guard—which she was too good to leave open unless some part of her wanted me there—and put the flat of the oath-blade against the side of her neck, hard enough to stagger her, soft enough to leave a bruise and nothing more. Her sword arm hung. She could have taken the opening. We both noticed she didn't.

"Yield," I said.

"Elodie—" Her voice was wrecked. Ten years of inquisitor polish, gone in a word. "The ridge will regroup in minutes. You have to run. Take the girl and *run*—"

"That's not what happens next."

"It's exactly what happens next—"

"DORATH," I said again, quieter, and this time the word wasn't for the river. "Truth remembers, Sera. So tell me the truth. The orders. What do they say happens to the child?"

Her jaw worked. I watched her weigh it—duty against mercy, mercy against duty, the same scales she'd been carrying since the burning—and I watched the scales tip the way they always tipped for her, toward the person in front of her, and then get slammed back down by the institution in her blood.

"They want her alive," she whispered.

"For how long?"

Silence. The worst kind. The kind that answers.

I bound her wrists with the strap from my bedroll while the ridge was still shouting itself into order, and I did it gently, and she let me, and somewhere in the middle of it Wren appeared at my elbow and looked up at the famous inquisitor of the Crown with the frank appraisal of a cat inspecting a new chair.

"She's coming with us," Wren signed.

"She's what?"

"She knows the passes. She knows *you*." The child's eyes were ancient and eight years old at once. "And you didn't kill her. That means something. In the old country, that meant something."

In the old country, a great many things meant something. That was rather the point of the old country.

The ridge was reforming—Dain's voice cutting through the panic, getting the crossbows into lines—and I hauled Sera upright by her bound arms. Up close, she smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of the temple incense we'd both been sworn with a lifetime ago.

"You should have killed me," she said. There was no accusation in it. Something worse. Bewilderment.

"Probably," I agreed. "Get used to disappointment. It's been my profession for ten years."

I whistled once, high, and my pack mare came crashing down through the trees where I'd left her, because unlike the Crown's finest, my horse understands the assignment. I put Sera on her lead rope, put Wren up in front of me, and turned us all toward the high pass—the road that remembers, the road Ivo's notes promised would carry us to the Door of Ash, the road that would demand of me, mile by mile, every word of Vessarine I had sworn never to speak again.

Behind us, the first crossbow bolt hissed into the pines.

Ahead of us, the mountain waited, patient as a creditor.

And between my shoulder blades I felt Sera Vane's eyes, and understood that I hadn't captured an inquisitor at all.

I had just handed my past a horse and invited it to come along.
