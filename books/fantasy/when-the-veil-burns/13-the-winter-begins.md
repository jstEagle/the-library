## 13. The Winter Begins

The first snow came on the feast day of Saint Aldebrand, which meant nothing to me and everything to the lowland villages, because in thirty years of Thornfell winters the saint had never once failed to deliver snow by his own holiday. It was a small piece of theological bookkeeping, but mountain people keep such ledgers. They know when the sky is late.

This year the sky wasn't late. The sky was early, and it was wrong.

I knew it before I saw a single flake. I woke before dawn with my teeth aching—not cold-ache but something deeper, a hum in the jaw like biting foil—and found frost on the *inside* of the shutters. Wren was already awake, sitting cross-legged on her pallet, watching the window with the patient attention she gave everything. When I crossed the room, her breath came out white. So did mine.

"It's not cold enough for that," I said, mostly to hear a voice say something sane aloud.

She looked at me, then at the window, then back at me, and shrugged in the way children do when adults state the obvious. Then she raised one hand, palm up, and made a slow closing gesture—fingers curling into a fist, the whole motion deliberate, unmistakable even across the gap between our languages.

*Shutting.*

"Yes," I said. "Thank you. I'd gathered."

I had not gathered anything of the sort, actually. What I had gathered was a bad feeling, and I have learned over ten years to trust those the way other people trust maps. Bad feelings are how the body remembers what the mind has agreed to forget. Mine was telling me something I didn't want translated: that the cold outside wasn't weather. Weather doesn't arrive with intent.

We ate porridge with the last of the honey Ivo had traded us, and while Wren scraped her bowl with the concentration of a girl who understood scarcity even if she couldn't name its causes, I did sums I didn't like. Firewood: three cords stacked under the lean-to, enough for two people through a normal winter, maybe half that through a hard one. Flour, salt beans, dried apples, a haunch of venison Ivo had salted for us in October. If winter ran true, we were fine. If winter ran long—

I caught myself looking east, toward the high pass, toward the seam in the world Ivo's notes described, and stopped myself with some effort. You don't look at the thing you're afraid of. That's the first rule of hiding. You look at your hands, your work, the road behind you. You let the horizon stay vague.

But the horizon wasn't staying vague. The horizon was filling up with white.

* * *

By noon the snow was falling so thick the pines had become suggestions of themselves, and I decided—against every instinct I owned—that I needed to go down to Hallowmere. Not for supplies; I could stretch what we had. For news. Because there are only so many signs a person can ignore before the ignoring itself becomes more dangerous than knowing.

Wren wanted to come. She always wanted to come, and I usually said yes, because leaving her alone felt like leaving a lit candle in a hay barn—she wouldn't burn anything down, exactly, but you'd return to find the arrangement of the universe subtly altered. This time I crouched to her level, took her small cold hands in mine, and spoke slowly.

"I need you here. Guarding." I tapped my own chest. "Guarding this place. Can you do that?"

Her chin lifted. She pointed at herself, then at the cabin, then drew herself up with all eight years of gravity she possessed and nodded once, a general accepting a commission. Then, as I pulled the door open, she caught my sleeve and pressed something into my palm: a twist of wool, dyed ash-grey, knotted in the middle.

I knew what it was. Every swordspeaker child carried one—a knot-charm, tied by a mother's hands, meant to hold a word inside it until the speaker returned to untie it. Wren couldn't tell me what word hers held. But she looked up at me with an expression I recognized from ten years ago, from another child's face in another burning doorway, and I closed my fist around the wool until the knot bit into my skin.

"I'll be back before dark," I lied gently, and went out into the wrongness.

* * *

The road down to Hallowmere was a river of white. Snow fell without wind, straight down, as if the sky were lowering something rather than letting it drift. By the time I reached the tree line the drifts were past my knees, and I noticed—really noticed, with a smuggler's professional eye—the tracks.

Or rather, the absence of them. No deer. No hare. No fox prints stitching the margins of the trail. A forest this quiet in early winter isn't resting. It's holding its breath.

Hallowmere itself sat in its bowl of hills looking abandoned in broad daylight. Smoke rose from perhaps a third of the chimneys. The tavern was shuttered though it was barely midday. In the square, old Marta Kessin was loading a cart with the grim efficiency of a woman who has done this arithmetic before, decades ago, in some other winter she never talks about.

"Marta," I said, keeping my voice at the flat pitch I use in the village—lowlander vowels, no music in them. "Where's everyone gone?"

She glanced up, and for once she didn't perform suspicion. Fear had burned through all that. "Down valley. To Kettering, to cousins, to anyone with a roof lower than ours." She jerked her chin at the sky. "You felt it too, then."

"Felt what?"

"Don't play with me, Hartwell woman." My false name sounded strange in her mouth, like a coat worn too long. "The cold that comes *from* the mountain. Not down it—*out* of it. My grandmother told stories of the year the peaks breathed frost. Said it was the year before the war took Vessar off the maps." She tied down her load with sharp tugs. "Old wives' tales. Except now the well water freezes from the bottom up, and my hens won't roost in the coop—they press against the south wall all night like they're waiting for something to come over the ridge."

"Maybe something is," I said, before I could stop myself.

Marta paused. Looked at me a moment too long. "Aye," she said quietly. "That's what I think too." And then, because she was a practical woman standing in a failing village: "There'll be no trade this winter. Whatever you bring down the passes, bring it nowhere near here. And whatever you're carrying besides goods—" her eyes flicked, briefly, meaningfully, at the eastern ridge "—carry it higher. Away from folk."

It was kindness, of a kind. Exile wrapped in warning. I thanked her, bought nothing, and started back up before the light failed.

* * *

Ivo's tower sat in a fold of the mountains two hours beyond my cabin, reachable only by a path he'd erased from his own maps. He answered my knock before I finished knocking, which meant he'd been watching the road, which meant he was frightened. Ivo is never frightened. Ivo is professionally pessimistic, which is different, and requires better posture.

"You've seen it," he said instead of greeting me. His study smelled of ink and lamp oil and, beneath that, the faint char-smell I'd begun noticing everywhere lately, like the memory of smoke that wouldn't wash out. Maps covered every surface, weighted with stones, and on the largest of them—the survey of Thornfell we'd traded for—he'd been drawing lines. Red ones. Radiating outward from a point in the high pass that I refused to look at directly even on paper.

"The cold," I said. "The empty villages. Marta Kessin says the wells freeze from the bottom."

"They will everywhere, within the month." He turned to a shelf, pulled down a folio, and set it in front of me with the tenderness men reserve for dangerous things. "And it gets worse. Sit down, Rafaela."

Nobody called me that but Ivo. The name sat in the room between us like a third person.

"The Veil isn't just weakening," he said. "It's dying. There's a difference, and the difference is time. A weakening Veil leaks—a word escapes here, a dream slips out there, a child walks through where she shouldn't be able to." He didn't look at me when he said *child*, which was its own kind of courtesy. "A dying Veil thrashes. Everything it ever held gets shaken loose. The cold is the outer edge of it—the country behind the Veil was frozen mid-burning, remember? Its winter is bleeding through the seams. The whole range is going to freeze. Not the season. The range."

"For how long?"

"If the Veil dies on its own schedule?" He shook his head. "Years. Decades, maybe. Long enough for Thornfell to become what the lowlands already call it in their songs—a place where people go and don't come back. The Crown would lose the mines, the passes, three garrisons." He finally met my eyes. "Which brings me to why I sent for you."

He opened the folio. Inside were pages in his own cramped hand, interleaved with documents I recognized by their seals: Crown chancery. Inquisitorate. He still had friends in low places, or debts owed by them, and he spent them the way misers spend gold.

"Three weeks ago the Inquisitorate petitioned the Privy Council for authority to conduct 'a final remediation of the anomalous zone.'" His finger traced the words. "Do you understand what that means? Not the passes. Not the villages. The *zone*. The mountain above the tree line, the high basin, the—" he exhaled "—the Door."

"They can't burn stone."

"They don't intend to burn stone." He laid out a diagram, and my stomach dropped as I read it. "Fire-oil in quantities I've only seen specified for naval warfare. Packed into the basin above the Door, fired all at once. The heat-shock won't crack the Door—it'll do worse. The Door is a seam, and seams respond to violence by opening. All at once. Uncontrolled. With an army standing on one side and a dying Veil on the other."

"And on the far side," I said slowly, "a country full of sleeping people, mid-burning."

"Now you see it." Ivo sat down heavily across from me. For a moment he looked every one of his sixty-some years, and older. "They think they're cauterizing a wound. They're going to blow the lock off a house full of sleepers and pour fire through the doorframe. And the Crown will call it containment, and the histories will call it necessity, and no one alive will remember there was ever any other option." He tapped the folio. "Except us. Because I kept records I should have destroyed, and you—" he gestured at me, at all of me, the exile, the liar, the woman pretending to be someone named Rafaela "—you remember the language that opens doors."

"I'm not doing that."

"I haven't asked you to."

"You're sitting in a tower full of red lines pointing at a Door, asking me to."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile. "I'm telling you the schedule, Elodie. That's all. The remediation is provisioned for spring, when the passes clear enough for supply trains. Spring thaw. Which means—" he counted silently, lips moving "—if the winter comes early, and hard, and strange, the way it's coming... the Crown may move earlier. Before the roads close entirely. Before the snow makes fire-oil transport impossible. They'll want it done while they can still do it."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, the tower ticked and settled, and beneath both sounds I heard—or imagined I heard—the vast, slow groan of the mountain breathing frost.

"So the clock is running," I said.

"The clock was always running. Now it's loud." Ivo stood, went to the window, looked north toward the high pass with the expression of a man reading a map he wished he'd drawn differently. "Get ready. Both of you. Whatever getting ready means for someone like you. And Elodie—" he paused at the door, not turning around. "When the time comes, and you go up there. Speak my real name at the Door. That's all I ask. Not absolution. Just the name. Let something true stand in that place for once."

I didn't answer. Some promises are too heavy to pick up twice.

* * *

The walk home was dark, and the cold had teeth now, real teeth, the kind that find gaps in wool. Halfway back I stopped, struck by a silence so complete my ears rang. No wind. No owl. No creak of boughs under weight of snow. The forest around me stood white and still as a held breath, and in that stillness I heard—faintly, from the east, from above—from the direction I never look—

Singing.

Not voices. Not quite. A sound like a thousand people humming in their sleep, carried on no air, rising from no throat, threading up through the snow and the stone and the ten years of my buried life. Wren's country. Wren's people. Asleep behind the Veil, and dreaming loudly enough now that the waking world could hear them.

The cold coming down the mountain wasn't weather. It was breath. Their breath. The exhale of a sealed nation, seeping through the seams of its tomb.

I walked the rest of the way fast, not running, because running is a confession, but fast. The cabin's windows glowed amber, and Wren was waiting in the doorway with a lantern held high, having ignored every sensible instruction about staying away from windows, and when she saw me she didn't smile. She studied my face the way she studied everything—with that unnerving, total attention—and then she stepped aside to let me in, and pointed east, and made the closing gesture again. Fist. *Shutting.*

"No," I said, setting down my pack. "Something's opening."

She frowned. Tilted her head. Then, slowly, deliberately, she unclenched her fist, spread her fingers wide, and looked at me with an expression that said, in any language: *then hurry.*

Outside, the snow kept falling, straight down, silent as ash, and somewhere above us the mountain sang to its sleepers, and somewhere below us men were counting weeks until they could set the world on fire again, and I stood in my doorway between those two pressures like a pin in a hinge, understanding at last that hiding season was over.

Winter hadn't begun.

Winter had arrived to end things.
