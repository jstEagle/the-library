## 15. Sera Chooses

The orders arrived the way orders always did: sealed in grey wax, carried by a boy too young to grow the beard he was trying for, and stamped with the sigil of the Office of Linguistic Integrity—a closed mouth over an open eye, which had always struck me as the most honest heraldry in the Crown's service. We say nothing. We see everything. We are the state's tongue and its knife.

I broke the seal in my tent at Hollow Ford, alone, because I had learned by then that my face did things when I read certain words, and a woman in my position could not afford witnesses to her face.

*The child is to be taken alive,* the orders said. *Unharmed if possible. The swordspeaker is to be killed on sight—no parley, no capture, no exceptions. The mountain is to be razed. All settlements within the Thornfell highlands are to be put to the torch before first thaw. The Door is to be destroyed by any means necessary.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times I kept snagging on the same phrase—*the child is to be taken alive*—and assuming I had misread it. Surely they meant the child was to be taken alive *as well*. Surely there was some other clause, some provision, some—

There wasn't. I read the whole document again, front to back, every clause, every sub-clause, every footnote in the margins written in the small hand of some undersecretary who would never have to look at what his footnotes accomplished. *Taken alive* meant taken alive. Full stop. And then the sentence after it, the one that gave the order its true shape:

*The child is to be conveyed to the Citadel for formal interrogation regarding linguistic anomalies, after which disposition will be determined at ministerial discretion.*

*Disposition.* That word again. It came up more and more lately, in the documents that crossed my desk. Disposition of assets. Disposition of anomalies. Disposition of persons. It was such a tidy word for such an untidy thing. Nobody ever asked what happened to the disposed. They were disposed of, and then they were not, and then there was a receipt filed somewhere in triplicate, and the world moved on, lighter by one child.

I sat with the orders in my lap for a long time while the fire burned down and the boy with the almost-beard waited outside in the cold, because protocol required him to wait until I signed the acknowledgment, and protocol is a wonderful thing when you need time to think.

Here is what I thought about.

I thought about Wren. Eight years old, maybe seven—I had never gotten a good look at her teeth, which is apparently how you age a child properly, though I confess my expertise runs more toward aging corpses. Barefoot in the snow when Elodie found her. Unafraid of everything except, perhaps, being forgotten. Speaking a language that made candles gutter and priests forget their own names. Carrying an ash-white seal that meant something once, in a country that officially had never existed, and that now meant everything, because it was the last one left.

I thought about how she had looked at me across the ice at Hollow Ford, when Elodie shattered the ford and spared me—not failed to kill me, mind you, but *spared* me, deliberately, with a full killing stroke available and refused—and how I had stood in the freezing water afterward, soaked to the ribs, watching two fugitives walk away from me into the mountains, and felt something I still don't have a clean name for. Not defeat. Defeat has a taste, and this wasn't it. It was closer to recognition. As if for one moment the ice had cracked open between us too, and I had seen myself the way she saw me: a woman standing in a river, holding a sword, defending a kingdom that had burned a country out of existence and called it peace.

I thought about Elodie. Ten years I had hunted her ghost, or rather ten years I had hunted the idea of her, because hunting the actual woman would have required admitting she was still alive, and admitting that required explaining why I had never reported it, and explaining *that* required answering questions I could not answer without hanging. So I had let the file go stale. I had let the reports thin out. I had written, year after year, the same careful sentence: *No confirmed sighting of the subject; search suspended pending new intelligence.* Pending new intelligence. The great lie of bureaucracy—that anything is ever pending, that intelligence arrives on its own like weather instead of being made by people who decide what they want to find.

And I thought about the bargain I had struck. My private clause. My mercy. Elodie's life in exchange for the child. I had told myself it was mercy. I had told myself it so many times, so fluently, that I had almost believed it, the way you can believe anything if you rehearse it enough—the way you can believe a burning country is peace, or that a language is a crime, or that love is a debt you pay off by delivering the person you love to the people who will use her.

It was not mercy. I knew that now. I had known it for weeks, probably, in the place where I kept the things I didn't look at directly. It was a transaction. I had priced a child against a woman and found the exchange acceptable, and I had dressed the arithmetic in mercy's clothing because the alternative was to admit what I was doing, and I was not yet ready to admit what I was doing.

But the orders had changed the arithmetic. There was no longer any bargain to strike. There was no longer any clause to negotiate. The ministers had looked at my carefully constructed arrangement—Elodie traded for Wren, everyone alive, everyone accounted for, the anomaly contained, the past resealed—and they had discarded it as sentiment. They didn't want the trade. They wanted the child, whole, for questioning. They wanted the swordspeaker dead. They wanted the mountain burned down to the stone, every waystation, every homestead, every smuggler's cabin, every family that had ever traded with Elodie Oyelaran or sheltered her or sold her oats or simply failed to report her. Razed. Before first thaw.

Before first thaw. Which gave us—what? Six weeks? Five? The winter had come early and wrong this year, the Veil's death throes bleeding cold into the range, but even a wrong winter ends. And when it ended, the columns would come up the passes with fire and writs and the closed-mouth-open-eye banner flying, and everything that lived on that mountain would learn what the Crown's memory cost.

I signed the acknowledgment. Protocol required it. I wrote my name in the space provided, in my careful clerk's hand, the hand I had learned in the Citadel's scriptorium before anyone decided I was better suited to a sword than a quill, and I want to be clear about what that signature was: it was not agreement. It was a receipt. It said *I received these orders*, nothing more. A signature is not a soul. I have watched enough men sign things they did not believe to know the difference, and I clung to that difference the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, knowing all the while that driftwood is not a boat.

Then I dismissed the boy, told him the pass east was closed, and paid him double to take the long road back and lose a day doing it. He'd report the delay as weather. Weather was believable. Weather was always believable.

And then I sat down to write my resignation.

I got as far as *To the Minister of Linguistic Integrity, greetings and—*

And stopped.

Because here is the thing about resignations: they are addressed to someone. They assume an institution worth addressing. They assume that the institution will read them, weigh them, possibly regret them, possibly even—in the wildest fantasy of the resigned—learn something. And I no longer believed any of that. You cannot resign from a thing that does not notice you are part of it. You cannot politely withdraw your labor from a machine whose entire function is to continue regardless. The Office of Linguistic Integrity did not need my consent to burn Thornfell. It needed only my absence, and my absence was easily arranged, and my absence would be noted in a ledger, and the ledger would balance, and the fires would be lit on schedule.

A resignation was a courtesy extended to an institution that deserved none. So I didn't finish it. I folded the half-written page around the orders themselves, and I did something else instead.

I burned them both.

Not ceremonially. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the one currency I have left and I intend to spend all of it. I did not stand in the firelight making speeches to myself about freedom. I crouched over the brazier in my tent like a woman disposing of evidence, which is exactly what I was, and I fed the orders in corner by corner, and I watched the wax seal bubble and the ink lift off the page and float upward as smoke, and I felt—nothing. Or near enough to nothing that the difference didn't matter. No lightness. No liberation. Burning a piece of paper does not unmake what the paper ordered. Somewhere in the Citadel, the original still existed, countersigned, archived, scheduled. Somewhere a quartermaster was already tallying lamp oil by the barrel for the spring campaign. My little fire changed nothing except me.

But that was the point. That was the whole point. The paper was not the order. The order was real whether the paper existed or not, and the only thing my fire could destroy was my obedience to it. So that is what I burned. I burned my obedience, corner by corner, and I watched it go up, and when the last corner caught I held my hands over the coals until they stung, because I wanted the sting. I wanted proof that I was still capable of feeling something in those hands besides the weight of a sword hilt.

* * *

Defection, it turns out, is mostly logistics.

This disappointed me. I had imagined—vaguely, in the way you imagine things you're ashamed of imagining—that betrayal of an institution would feel like a door opening, like walking out of a dark room into air. Instead it felt like packing. Like inventory. Like counting.

I counted my riders first. Forty-one, when the rolls said forty-six. Five lost over the autumn: two to the cold, one to a fall on the scree, one to fever, and one—Corporal Dain—to a decision of his own, though his had gone the other direction. Dain had ridden to the Citadel in November to report that his commanding officer was soft on anomalies, and I had let him go, partly because stopping him would have raised questions, and partly because I understood him better than he knew. Dain believed. Belief like his is a kind of innocence, and I have never had the heart to kill innocence. I have killed plenty of other things.

Of the forty-one remaining, I could trust—this took a night of going through them in my head, rider by rider, the way you go through a purse you suspect has been shorting you—I could trust eleven. Twelve, counting myself, and I did count myself, because whatever else I was now, I was still the only person on that mountain who could hold forty-one armed men together long enough to keep thirty of them from doing something unforgivable.

Eleven. Ferro, my sergeant, who had followed me since the border wars and who had once told me, drunk, that he'd seen me spare a smuggler in '43 and had never mentioned it because "everyone deserves one thing they got away with." Marta, who could ride anything with legs. The twins, Osk and Oskana, who finished each other's sentences and shared one conscience between them, which was more than most. Little Petter, nineteen, who still wrote letters home to his mother describing the campaign as "mostly accounting work," bless him, and who was not entirely wrong. Six others whose names I'll keep, because names are the last thing you own when you defect, and I was down to owning very little.

The rest—twenty-nine men and women who had done nothing wrong except follow me—I could not take with me and could not leave behind without warning. So I did the only decent thing left: I lied to them magnificently.

I assembled them at dawn in the frozen mud of the camp at Hollow Ford and told them the campaign was being redrawn. That the high command had determined the Thornfell operation was to be consolidated under Colonel Ashe, a man I described in terms that were accurate in every particular except existence. That my detachment was being reassigned to the western passes for interdiction duty, effective immediately, and that the transfer orders would follow within the fortnight. And then I shook hands. I shook every single hand, forty-one of them, and I looked every single man and woman in the eyes, and I said some variation of *it's been an honor* to each one, and the terrible thing—the thing I will carry longer than the burns on my fingers—is that most of them believed me, and the ones who didn't believe me said nothing, and either way I walked away from twenty-nine people who trusted me, and I did it in daylight, with a straight spine, because the alternative was to tell them the truth and watch thirty of them choose the fire over their own consciences, and I had already spent my lifetime's ration of watching people choose wrongly.

Ferro knew. Of course Ferro knew. He'd been reading me for fifteen years the way I read documents, looking for the forgery underneath the fair hand, and when I gave the speech about consolidation and Colonel Ashe, he stood at the back with his arms folded and his face arranged into perfect neutrality, and when it was done he fell into step beside me and said, quietly, "Which way, Captain?"

"West," I said. "Then north."

"And the others?"

"They'll live. Ashe doesn't exist, so nobody's coming to consolidate anything. By the time the Citadel notices the detachment's gone quiet, we'll be ghosts."

"Ghosts," Ferro repeated, and spat, thoughtfully. "Been worse postings."

That was the whole eulogy for my career. Fifteen years in the Crown's service, from scriptorium to saddle, from ink to iron, and it ended with a sergeant saying *been worse postings* and spitting into the mud. I have attended funerals with more grief in them. But then, I have also attended funerals with less truth.

* * *

We rode north through the white silence of the high passes, twelve riders where forty-one had been, and the mountain did what the mountain always does, which is make you small and grateful for it.

I want to describe the ride honestly, because everything else in this chapter I've dressed or trimmed or outright lied about, and a person converting herself from an instrument into an agent ought to keep at least one accurate account. The ride was miserable. The cold had teeth this year—wrong cold, Veil-cold, the kind that got into the joints and stayed, the kind the old-timers in the foothill villages muttered about and blamed on everything from the stars to the tax man. Our horses labored in snow that reached the chest on the switchbacks. Petter's mount went lame on the second day and we turned him loose with a farmer who asked no questions because we paid in silver and silver asks no questions. Marta's frostbitten cheek went black at the edge and she refused, absolutely refused, to turn back, on the grounds that she had "come this far to desert, and deserting halfway is just getting captured." Osk and Oskana argued the entire route about whether loyalty to a person could outweigh loyalty to a crown, and concluded, somewhere around the fourth day, that it couldn't—but that a crown was an abstraction and a person was warm, and warmth wins in winter. This is, I have decided, the soundest political philosophy I have encountered in fifteen years of service, and I intend to adopt it formally.

On the fifth night we made camp in the ruins of one of Elodie's waystations—the one the outriders had burned in the autumn, its blackened beams jutting from the snowdrift like the ribs of something large and patient. I sat inside the burnt shell with my back against a charred wall and realized I was sheltering in the wreckage of my own side's work, and that this was, in miniature, the whole of my situation. Everything I had to offer Elodie now was wreckage. My rank: worthless, soon to be a warrant. My oath: broken, and broken in the worse direction—she had broken hers to survive, mine to betray, and there is a hierarchy in broken oaths, and I knew which rung I occupied. My knowledge: this was the one coin I had. I knew the disposition of the columns. I knew the timing. I knew the razing schedule, the supply lines, the names of the officers who would lead the burn, the routes the inquisitors would seal first. I knew where the Crown's memory ended and its blindness began. If Elodie and the child were going to reach the Door of Ash before first thaw, they would need every day I could buy them and every gap in the net I could name.

That was my offering. Not love—love was a luxury the mountain wouldn't carry. Knowledge. Timing. A bought distance. It seemed pitifully small, laid out against what I was asking: that she trust the woman who had hunted her for ten years, who had negotiated her life away like a line item, who had stood in the ice-water at Hollow Ford and been spared and had repaid the sparing by planning to sell her child.

But it was what I had. And I had learned, somewhere on that five-day ride, the lesson that should have come with my first commission: you do not get to choose what you bring to a reckoning. You bring what's in your hands. What's in my hands was a burned set of orders, eleven loyal ghosts, and the location of every trap on the mountain.

I woke before dawn on the sixth day and climbed alone above the camp to where the ridge broke clear of the trees, and I looked east, toward the deep passes, toward wherever Elodie and Wren were—warm, if the mountain allowed it; moving, if the clock allowed it; alive, if I had anything to say about it anymore, and I had finally made it so that I had something to say about it.

The sky over the eastern peaks was doing something wrong. I had watched mountain winters for fifteen years and I knew their moods the way I knew my own handwriting, and this was not a mood. The clouds stood still. The wind had died completely—not lulled, *died*, the way a voice dies—and the air had a pressure to it, a held-breath quality, as if the whole range were waiting for someone to speak. The Veil-cold again, but thicker, deliberate. The mountain itself seemed to be listening.

And I understood, standing there in the dead air with my burned hands and my eleven ghosts sleeping below me, why the Crown feared the language so much. Not because it was a weapon. Because it was a *voice*, and voices carry, and the mountain was leaning in to hear.

Somewhere east of me, I thought, she is speaking. She has to be. The road remembers only what is spoken aloud, Ivo Marsh's notes had said—or rather, the fragments of his notes I'd confiscated years ago and memorized and told no one about, because keeping useful secrets from your own office is the first skill they teach you and the last one you surrender. Every true word she forces herself to say makes the road clearer. And every word marks her.

She was trading safety for passage, word by word, spending her silence like coins, and every coin rang out across the snowfields like a bell, and every bell drew the hunters, and the hunters had my name on their rolls as the hunter-in-chief, and I was no longer hunting. I was the only person on this mountain positioned to know exactly where the next trap would spring and exactly how to un-spring it.

Twelve riders. Five days' hard ride ahead. A window measured in weeks, closing.

I turned back down toward the camp, and Ferro was already awake, already saddling, already watching the eastern sky with the expression of a man who has served one woman for fifteen years and has finally, finally figured out what he signed on for.

"Captain," he said. "Which way?"

"East," I said. "Fast."

He grinned like a man hearing good news in a burning house, and swung up into the saddle, and behind me I heard the twins laughing at something, and Petter asking Marta if deserts came with wages, and I stood there one moment longer in the dead listening air, looking east at the still, waiting sky.

Hold on, Elodie. Hold the child close. Speak your words and damn the bells.

I'm coming—and this time, I'm the warning.
