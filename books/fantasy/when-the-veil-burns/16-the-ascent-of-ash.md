## 16. The Ascent of Ash

The mountain stopped pretending to be a place and became an argument.

That was my first thought, somewhere above the tree line, when the whiteout swallowed the world whole. No sky, no ground, no east or west—just a whiteness so complete it felt less like weather than like erasure, the Crown's favorite trick performed at scale by the gods themselves. You could almost admire it. All those years I'd hidden in Thornfell because the mountains forgot things for you, and now the mountains had decided to forget everything at once, including which way up was.

"Elodie." Sera's voice came out of the white to my left, muffled by wool and wind. She'd tied a rope between us—her idea, delivered with the particular flatness she reserved for suggestions that were actually orders. "Still there?"

"Barely."

"Good. Keep being barely."

Behind her, Wren made a small sound—not a word, not quite a whimper—and my heart did the thing it had been doing since the foothills, that clench-and-drop, like a fist closing around something hot.

We'd been climbing since before dawn, if dawn was even a concept anymore. Three days since Sera burned her orders in the captain's tent and rode east through her own army's pickets with a stolen horse and a face like a woman settling accounts. Three days since Ivo's final coordinates had sent us toward the high col where the Door of Ash waited in its seam of black glass, patient as a held breath. And for three days the winter had come down on us like something with intentions.

Not natural cold. I'd grown up in these ranges; I knew their moods the way you know a bad-tempered horse. This was different. The Veil was dying—Ivo's letters had been clear enough about that, once we'd dug them out of his dead-drop in the burned surveyor's hut—and everything around Vessar was catching its death throes like sparks off a falling star. The frost came in patterns no frost should make: spirals on stone, feathering that followed the old oath-lines, ice that hummed faintly under your boots if you stood still long enough. Once, crossing a frozen stream, I'd looked down and seen the water beneath the ice still moving—but moving wrong, slow as honey, all flowing in one direction. Toward the mountain. Toward the Door.

Everything wanted to go home. The water. The wind. Me, if I were honest, though home for me was a country made of ash and guilt, and wanting it back felt like picking at a wound to see if it still bled.

It did. It always did.

* * *

We sheltered that night—or what passed for night—in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, backs against the stone, three bodies pressed close enough to share heat without shame. Sera built a fire the size of two cupped hands, using fuel we couldn't afford to burn, because Wren's lips had gone the color of slate and some costs stop being negotiable.

Wren slept between us, curled small, her breath fogging and fading, fogging and fading. Eight years old, maybe nine. Barefoot once, in autumn, unafraid at my door. Now she wore three layers of smuggled wool and boots cut down from Sera's spares, and still the cold found her, because the cold wasn't hunting evenly. It hunted her most of all. Whatever she carried—the seal, the blood, the language sleeping in her like a coal under snow—the Veil knew her name even if I didn't.

Sera watched me across the fire. Ten years of watching me from across fires, once. She'd gotten better at hiding what was behind her eyes, but exhaustion strips varnish, and what showed through now was older than her commission and colder than the pass.

"She shouldn't be here," Sera said quietly.

"She has nowhere else."

"There's always somewhere else. That's the whole architecture of exile." She fed a twig to the flames. "I've lived there twenty years."

"You lived there five," I said. "Then you got promoted into a house."

Something moved across her face—not quite a flinch, not quite a smile. "The house came with chains. You'd know. You broke yours loud enough to hear in the capital."

"I broke mine," I said, "because staying meant burying children."

The words came out harder than I meant them, and the silence after had edges. Across the hollow, the wind found a crack in the rock and played it like a flute—a thin, rising note that sounded uncomfortably like a bell.

Sera looked away first. "Sleep," she said. "You take first watch anyway. You always insisted."

"Some things survive erasure," I said, and settled the watch on my shoulders like an old coat, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning brought the columns.

I heard them before I saw anything—which is to say I heard nothing, a wrongness in the sound of the mountain, a hush where the wind should have been talking. Then, threading through the white, faint as memory: the creak of harness. Boots on scree. The bells.

Every inquisitor column carried bells. Silver-throated, consecrated, tuned to catch the frequencies of spoken workings—they'd rung them over Vessar while the Veil came down, ten years ago, a carillon to drown out every voice that tried to speak the country back together. I'd heard them from the ridge above the burning city. I'd heard them while I ran.

"Sera." I touched her shoulder. Her eyes opened the way they always opened—fully, instantly, no drift between sleep and war. She listened for three seconds. Then she was up, kicking snow over the fire, waking Wren with hands gentle as a surgeon's.

"How many?" she asked.

"Two bells. Maybe sixty men."

"They can't track us in this."

"They don't have to track us." I pulled the coordinates from memory—Ivo's numbers, inked into my mind in a hand that had trembled writing them. "They know where we're going. There's one route to the col that isn't suicide, and we're on it. They're not following our trail, Elodie-of-the-past. They're herding us."

Sera swore, low and creative, in the cadence of someone who'd spent a decade among soldiers learning exactly how profanity could be load-bearing. "Options?"

"One. We go off the route. Onto the road only oath-speakers can see."

She stared at me through the blowing white. "The memory-road. You told me yourself—it reshapes around spoken truth. Every word of Vessarine you say lights you up like a signal fire. They'll hear you from the valley floor."

"Yes."

"And if you don't speak?"

"The road stays dark. We wander this whiteout until the cold does their work for them, politely, without bells."

Wren tugged my sleeve. When I looked down, she was holding out her hand—the ash-white token resting on her palm, glowing faintly, the swordspeaker's seal that had started all of this. Her eyes were steady. They were always steady. Children who have crossed the end of the world learn to skip the parts where you're allowed to fall apart.

She said something in Vessarine. Small, urgent, four syllables.

And I understood her—of course I understood her, the language living in me like a splinter worked too deep ever to dig out—and what she said was: *Take the road. Say the words. I'll carry the cost with you.*

Eight years old, offering to share a price she couldn't possibly weigh.

"No," I said aloud, in Trade-Tongue, because saying it in hers would already be paying.

Then I closed my eyes, and I spoke.

* * *

*"Ammara veth, ammara sol—"*

The words came up out of me like water from a well nobody had drawn from in ten years, and they tasted of rust and milk and smoke. The road answered.

I can't describe the memory-road to anyone who hasn't walked it, and no one who walked it with me remembers it, so the description dies with me. But I will try, because trying is all this account is. It was a path laid through the whiteness like a vein through marble—visible only where true words touched it, each syllable a lamp lit along its length behind us and darkness ahead. It remembered feet. It remembered my sisters' feet, and my own younger ones, and it remembered the oaths spoken on it, and walking it was like walking through the halls of a house where every room was someone's voice.

Each word I spoke was a beacon. Somewhere below and behind, I knew, the bells would be turning toward us like flowers toward sun. I spoke anyway.

*"Ammara veth, ammara sol, kel doran issa vey—"*

Wren's small hand in mine. Sera at my back with her sword drawn, facing the whiteness we'd come from, guarding the light I was making. The slope steepened. The cold stopped being weather and became a presence with opinions, leaning on us, asking its question over and over: *why? why? why?*

By midday—if midday existed—Wren began to falter.

It started small. A stumble she caught. Then a stumble she didn't. Then she was walking with her eyes half-closed, dragged forward by my grip on her hand, her breath coming in little torn pieces, and I realized with a horror that arrived very calmly, the way the worst things do, that she had stopped shivering. Shivering is the body arguing for itself. Hers had conceded the debate.

"Wren." I knelt. "Wren, look at me."

Her eyelids lifted halfway. She smiled at me. It was the worst thing I saw that day, and I include what came later.

*Sera.* My voice cracked on the name. *She won't make it on her feet.*

Sera was beside us in a heartbeat, hands inside Wren's coat, checking, assessing, her soldier's mask firmly in place except for her mouth, which had gone tight and white. "Hypothermia. She needs warmth, movement, sugar—we have none of the last." A pause, the length of a blade's width. "Carry her."

"I can't climb and speak."

"Then choose."

There it was. The choice, laid out clean as a drawn line. Speak and carry her both impossible; drop the recitation and lose the road; leave her and—

I had left people before. That was the arithmetic of my entire adult life, the sum I'd been carrying since the Veil fell: what I saved against what I didn't, and the difference called survival.

I put my arms under Wren and stood up with her.

"Then I'll do both badly," I said, "and trust the road to forgive me."

* * *

I carried her on my back, her arms looped over my collarbone, her cheek against my neck, her breath a small failing furnace against my skin. And I climbed. And I sang.

Not sang—recited. Lullabies. The ones I had sworn on broken glass never to speak again, the ones my mother had sung in a kitchen that no longer existed in a country struck from every map, the ones my oath-sisters and I had traded back and forth on night watch until the words wore smooth as river stones. *Lirra vessa, lirra doe,* sleep, little ember, the mountain is your blanket, the stars are your keepers, no door is shut to the one who is loved—

Every verse was a beacon-flare. Every verse was a knife in the scar tissue of my chest. Every verse warmed the child on my back by a degree I could measure in the loosening of her grip.

Sera climbed ahead of me, breaking trail, turning back every thirty steps to check on us with a glance that lasted too long to be tactical. At one point, in a stretch where the wind dropped, I heard her humming. Low, barely there, harmonizing a third below the melody.

She knew the lullaby too. Of course she did. We'd learned it from the same mothers, in the same courtyards, before the Crown collected us both and taught us that everything we knew was contraband.

"Don't," I said, between verses, my lungs burning.

"It helps her," Sera said.

"It's a working. You know it's a working. Every note is a bell-toll pointing at us."

"Then let them come," Sera said, "and I'll hold the pass, and you'll sing, and she'll live, and we'll all be damned in good company."

I had no answer for that. I had a lullaby, so I gave her that instead.

* * *

Dusk—or dusk-shaped dark—brought the col into view.

It appeared the way things appear in whiteouts: not gradually but all at once, as if the mountain had been concealing it out of spite and finally relented. A saddle of black rock between two shoulders of ice, wind-scoured, bare, and at its head—still a thousand feet above us, up a slope that would take the last of everything we had—the faintest suggestion of a seam. A vertical line of darkness in the cliff face, too straight to be natural, too patient to be anything but a Door.

Below us, threading up through the passes we'd crossed, torchlight. Dozens of points, moving in disciplined order. The columns hadn't caught us. They'd simply stopped pretending they needed to. We were a funnel now, and funnels have one exit, and everyone in the funnel knows it.

Sera stood at the lip of the last ledge, sword out, looking down at the torches, and then up at the Door, and then at me—at us, the woman and the child tangled together on my back like one exhausted animal—and I watched her do the mathematics I'd seen her do a hundred times across a decade of separate wars. Casualties versus objective. Duty versus the thing duty exists to protect.

"We rest here one hour," she said finally. "No fire. Body heat only. Then the last push, and I go down and meet them at the narrows while you finish the climb."

"Sera—"

"That wasn't a request, Elodie. It's the plan. It was always going to be the plan. Some of us rehearse our sacrifices in private so they look spontaneous later." She sat down against the rock, drew Wren's boots onto her lap, and began chafing the child's feet with rough, competent hands. "Besides," she added, not looking up, her voice suddenly very quiet, "you'll need someone to hold the pass tomorrow. And I owe Vessar a debt I've been paying in the wrong currency for twenty years."

Above us, the seam of black glass caught the last grey light and held it, like a pupil dilating in the face of the mountain.

And far below, faint but unmistakable, the first of the bells began to ring.
