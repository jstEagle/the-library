## 18. Two Oaths, One Blade

### Sera

I have counted a great many things in my life. Ledgers of the Inquisition. Names of the condemned. The exact number of steps between my tent and the command pavilion, because knowing such things made the nights shorter. But I had never counted how long it takes to watch an army crest a ridge you intend to hold alone, until that morning at the Door of Ash.

The answer, for the curious, is roughly four hundred heartbeats. I counted them all.

Below me the pass narrowed to a throat of grey stone, and through that throat came the Crown's justice: two hundred riders in white-and-iron, banners snapping, breath steaming from horses and men alike in the killing cold. At their center rode Prelate Ossen, wrapped in enough fur to upholster a chapel, his face pink and furious above his collar. He had been promised a mountain to burn and was discovering that mountains, in winter, burn poorly.

He saw one woman standing in his path, sword drawn, no banner of her own.

"Sera Vane." His voice carried up the rock like something dropped down a well. "Step aside. Your orders were explicit."

"I read them," I called back. "Then I burned them. It's a whole discipline. You wouldn't understand."

Two hundred riders shifted. Somewhere below, a horse screamed at the cold. And behind me—above me, on the shelf of black glass where the mountain wore its scar like a knife wound—I could hear Elodie beginning to speak.

I will not pretend I didn't know what she was doing. I have known Elodie Oyelaran since we were nine years old and she stole my bread ration out of pure spite and then shared it with me anyway, which is the most honest portrait of her character anyone will ever draw. I knew what the words rising behind me meant. I knew what they cost. The oath we had both sworn—the oath I kept and she broke—was not one voice but two, braided, and only together did they make anything true.

So I turned my back on the Door, faced the army, and prepared to be the second half of a sentence I could not hear.

"Last chance, Vane," Ossen said.

"You know," I said, "everyone keeps offering me last chances. Do you know what I've learned? That people who offer them never mean to honor yours."

I raised my blade.

* * *

### Elodie

There is a thing that happens when you speak Vessarine after ten years of silence. It is not like remembering a language. It is like remembering you have lungs.

The first word came out of me broken, cracked down the middle like river ice, and I nearly stopped. Wren stood beside me with her small hand pressed flat against the black glass of the Door, watching me with those old, old eyes that had crossed a burning country to find me. Behind and below us Sera stood alone against an army, and every instinct I owned screamed at me to go down there and die beside her rather than stand here and speak.

But dying beside someone is just another way of leaving them alone.

So I spoke.

*"Ashen gate, ash-born key—"*

The mountain heard me. I felt it the way you feel a floorboard shift under weight. The black glass warmed beneath Wren's palm, faint as a sleeping animal's flank.

*"—sealed by the mouths that swore thee shut, opened by the mouths that swear thee open."*

The cold changed. Not warmer—Vessarine has no warmth in it, only truth, and truth burns differently—but *cleaner*, as if the air itself had been carrying grit for a decade and finally set it down.

Wren tugged my sleeve. When she spoke now, her voice arrived inside me rather than in my ears, the way it always did, translated by whatever the Veil had left in her:

*You're saying it wrong.*

"I'm saying it exactly as I was taught," I muttered.

*You were taught by someone who was afraid.* Her hand stayed flat on the glass. *Say it like you're not afraid. Like you're glad.*

Glad. Ten years of burying a language like a corpse under flagstones, and this eight-year-old wanted me to say the resurrection words *gladly*. I looked down the pass instead, at the small grey figure holding a throat of stone against two hundred riders, and thought: well. If she can stand there, I can be glad here.

*"By salt and by snow, by the names we carried—"*

And that was when the words changed under me.

* * *

### Sera

Ossen sent the first thirty riders up the throat of the pass the way a man throws stones into a well he doesn't want to look down. They came in good order, lances couched, because the Inquisition trains its soldiers beautifully and thinks about them hardly at all.

I had chosen my ground the night before, while Elodie slept and Wren dreamed whatever frozen dreams the Veil let her keep. There is a shelf of rock above the narrowest point, and ice on the switchback below it, and exactly one line of approach wide enough for three horses abreast. A swordspeaker could hold that ground. A former swordspeaker holding a borrowed blade could hold it once.

Once would have to do.

They came up the switchback in their beautiful order and I went down through it like weather. I will not romanticize it. Fighting is ugly work done close, all breath and elbow and the terrible intimacy of strangers' faces, and the only romance in it is that you are still alive at the end to be ashamed of how much of it felt easy. My blade—a smuggler's steel, nothing oath-sworn about it—took a lance off-line and I took its rider's wrist and he went down the ice and his comrades went around him and came on, because that is what training does; it makes grief into traffic management.

Three fell. Five. Seven. I stopped counting at seven because I heard Elodie's voice rise behind me, thin with altitude and distance but unmistakable, and something in my chest answered it before my mind could object.

*"—by the names we carried—"*

My mouth moved. Nothing came out. Ten years of saying *the traitor Oyelaran* in report rooms, ten years of pronouncing her name like an accusation so I wouldn't have to pronounce it like a prayer—and now her voice was climbing the mountain and mine was supposed to meet it halfway and I couldn't. I literally could not. The words sat in my throat like stones in a streambed.

A rider nearly took my head off during this crisis of faith, which I mention as evidence that the universe has a sense of humor.

I killed him. I want that recorded plainly. Whatever else this account becomes, I killed him, and I did not spare him, and the difference between me and the woman on the mountain is that she has always known exactly which deaths she refuses.

* * *

### Elodie

Halfway. I was halfway, and the Door had begun to listen, and that was the problem—because a listening Door does not accept half an oath any more than a lock accepts half a key.

The words faltered in my mouth. I felt the recitation stall like a cart in a rut, felt the black glass cool again, felt ten years of broken promise standing between me and the last word like a wall built of one brick, laid carefully, by my own hand.

*I broke it,* I told the Door, in the old tongue, because apparently I'd become the sort of person who argued with architecture. *I broke it and I ran and I lived. That's what I am. There's no whole voice in me.*

Wren looked up at me, and for the first time since she'd arrived barefoot at my door, she looked exactly her age—eight years old, exhausted, frightened, standing in the wind at the top of the world because everyone else had failed her.

*Then borrow one,* she said.

And below us, hoarse and ragged and furious, Sera Vane began to speak.

* * *

### Sera

I don't remember deciding. I want to be honest about that, because everything else in my life I decided deliberately, painfully, with ledgers. This I did not decide. The words came up out of me like water finding a crack, the counter-voice of the oath, the answering half that every swordspeaker learns beside her sister and swears beside her sister and breaks—or keeps—beside her sister.

*"—by the names we carried, by the names we buried—"*

Elodie's voice caught above me. Then caught fire. I felt our words braid across the distance, hers falling, mine rising, the old shared cadence locking together like hands, and I understood with a clarity that felt like being struck: the oath was never broken. It was *split*. Half of it had lived in her exile and half in mine, each of us carrying our shard of it through ten years of winter, and neither of us had ever been able to put it down.

We had thought we were keeping a grudge. We had been keeping a key.

The riders stopped coming up the switchback. I'll grant them this—they were not fools. When the mountain starts humming under your horse's hooves and the air tastes like lightning and the woman in your path is glowing faintly along the old scars of her sword-hand, retreat begins to look like a career move.

Ossen did not retreat. Ossen screamed something about heresy and pointed his mace at the sky, which is what prelates do instead of thinking. But even his voice sounded small now, a man shouting in a cathedral that had begun, politely, to disagree with him.

I backed up the switchback toward the Door, blade still up, eyes on the column, because a promise is not finished just because the hard part is over.

Above me, Elodie's voice and mine rose together, and I swear on whatever is left of my name—the mountain *sang*.

* * *

### Elodie

She came up the last stretch of rock with blood on her cheekbone and frost in her lashes and her sword-arm shaking, and she did not stop speaking. Neither did I. We reached the shelf of black glass together, two women and a child and a door older than every crown that had ever tried to erase it, and our voices closed the final interval like a chord resolving.

*"—and what was sealed in sorrow, let be opened in love. What was burned, let be remembered. What slept, let wake."*

The Door of Ash opened.

Not with thunder. With a sound like an enormous exhalation, like a country turning over in its sleep after ten years, and the black glass ran with light the color of dawn on snow. Beyond it—not beyond it, *through* it—lay terraces and orchards under a held breath of smoke, a city paused mid-burning, fires frozen like paintings of themselves, and people, thousands of them, asleep in streets and fields and doorways, waiting.

Waiting for a speaker. Waiting, it turned out, for two.

Sera stood beside me with her sword point-down in the snow, breathing hard, staring through the opening at everything we had lost and found, and she said, in plain border-tongue, wrecked and wondering:

"We sound good together."

It was the most honest thing either of us had said in ten years.

Then the light in the Door flickered—stuttered—like a candle feeling a draft it did not like, and far down the pass, Ossen's voice rose in the cadence of unbinding, the words the Inquisition used on doors of a different kind, and the mountain shuddered under our feet.

The Door was open. But we were not alone, and the army below had brought fire of its own.

Sera picked up her sword.
