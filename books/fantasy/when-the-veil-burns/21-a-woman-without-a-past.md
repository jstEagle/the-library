## 21. A Woman Without a Past

There is a woman who lives above the Salt Kettle tavern in Marrow's Ford, and she does not know her own name.

I know this because I am her, or I live where she lives, which may be the same thing now, or may not. The landlady, a broad-armed widow named Tamsin who smells of tallow and thyme, calls me *Lark*. "Because you sing," she said, on the first morning, when she found me standing in her kitchen with no memory in my pockets and snowmelt in my boots. "You were humming. Some song I've never heard. So: Lark."

It is as good a name as any. Better than most, probably. Names are just hooks to hang a self on, and mine came off the wall with everything else.

Here is what I have instead of a past:

I have hands that are scarred in interesting ways. A rope-burn across the left palm. An old puncture through the right that aches before rain. Calluses on my fingers arranged in a pattern Tamsin's blacksmith cousin looked at once and said, "That's a sword hand," and then looked at me differently for a week.

I have reflexes that frighten people. When a cart backfired outside the tavern in early spring—some fault of the axle, nothing worse—I was across the room and between Tamsin and the door before the sound finished. She asked me later how I'd moved so fast. I told her I didn't know. That was true. It remains the truest thing I own.

And I have songs. That's the strange part, the part I turn over at night like a coin worn smooth. Songs in no language anyone here speaks. Lullabies with words like water going over stones, and one marching tune that makes my chest hurt in a place I can't point to on any map of myself. I teach them to the children who come up the stairs on market days, because they ask, because children always ask, and because when I sing them something in my chest unclenches that I did not know was clenched.

The children don't know the words mean anything. Neither do I, not really. But little Pelli Osgood sang one walking home through the square last week, and an old drover stopped dead in the street and stared at her like he'd seen a ghost wearing her face. Then he shook himself and bought his sausages and said nothing to anyone.

Words older than crowns, someone might say. If someone had said it to me. No one has.

* * *

Sera comes on Thursdays.

I know this the way I know the rope-burn: as fact, without origin. There is a woman who rides up from the low country every seventh day, grey-cloaked, grey-eyed, with a scar through one brow, and when she arrives my chest does a complicated thing that I have decided not to examine, the way you decide not to poke a bruise.

"Hello, Lark," she says, every time, and her voice catches slightly on the name, as if it's a word in a foreign language she's still learning to pronounce.

"Hello," I say. "You're the Thursday lady."

"I'm the Thursday lady." She smiles. It doesn't reach all the way up. I've started to suspect it never has, with her.

We sit by the window. Sometimes I play cards with her; she cheats, badly, and pretends not to notice that I notice. Sometimes she asks questions—not many, and never the obvious ones, and I have begun to understand that this restraint costs her something. Once she brought a book of mountain wildflowers and we argued about whether the blue one on page forty was gentian or something pretending to be gentian. She knew more about flowers than an inquisitor's warrant would explain. I didn't say *inquisitor*. I don't know why that word sits in me like a swallowed stone, but it does, and around Sera it rattles.

Last Thursday she brought paper.

"I thought," she said, too casually, smoothing it on the table between us, "you might want to start writing things down. What you remember. However small. A taste you liked. A dream. It helps, sometimes, with—" she gestured vaguely at my head, at all that smooth white space behind my eyes— "putting yourself back together."

"And what if what comes back isn't worth having?"

She went very still. I watched her decide several things in the space of two breaths, none of which she said aloud.

"Then you'd finally know what you're rebuilding from," she said at last. "Foundations aren't always pretty. Doesn't make them less necessary."

I took the paper. I have written three things on it so far, in handwriting that slants hard to the left, like someone bracing against a wind:

*I hate the smell of tallow candles.*
*I was afraid of something for a very long time, and I am not anymore, and the not-being-afraid feels like falling.*
*There is a child I gave away. I do not know if this is a memory or a grief wearing a memory's clothes.*

When Sera read that third line—and she read it, though I watched her try not to—she put the paper down with great care, the way you set down something that might go off, and said, "Thursday again next week?"

"Thursday again," I agreed.

She stood at the door a moment longer than leaving takes. "For what it's worth," she said, "whoever you were, she'd be—" and then she stopped, jaw working, and finished with: "The soup's good today. Tamsin outdid herself."

She rode south. I stood at the window until the grey cloak disappeared into the grey road, and I thought: *she knows me.* Not suspects, not guesses. *Knows.* And I thought: *whatever it cost her to stop mid-sentence, she paid it gladly,* and I could not tell you how I know that either, only that knowing her is like knowing weather in my bones—a pressure, a coming change.

* * *

The dreams are getting longer.

That's how I think of it, anyway—as though the dreams are a country expanding its borders into my sleep. They used to be single images: a mountain seam glowing dull red, a child's bare feet on stone, a blade catching light. Now they have duration. Plot. Last night I dreamed in full sentences in a language I cannot speak awake, and woke with tears frozen on my cheeks in a room warm enough that Tamsin had left the window open.

In the dream there is a door. Black glass set in living rock, tall as three men. In the dream I am speaking to it, and it is answering, and the answer is my own voice coming back changed—older, or younger, or simply *other*—and every word I give it, it takes something from me in trade. A name. A winter. A face. I keep speaking anyway. In the dream this feels not like loss but like arithmetic: the correct sum, arrived at honestly.

I wake and lie there doing sums of my own. What can a person lose before the remainder stops being a person? Tamsin says I'm melancholy in the mornings and to drink my tea. Tamsin is wise about tea and wrong about almost everything else.

This morning I added a fourth line to the paper:

*I think I paid for something enormous, and I think I would pay again, and I think that's the only thing about myself I'm sure of.*

* * *

The letter comes on a Tuesday, which unsettles me before I even understand why. Tuesdays are wrong. Thursdays are right. The world has a grain to it and I've been planed against it.

It arrives with the post-rider, ordinary as bread, except that the seal is ash-white wax pressed with a mark like a sword crossed over a flame, and when I break it—when my thumb finds the seam exactly where the seal wants breaking—the smell hits me first: cold stone, high altitude, woodsmoke. A smell I know the way I know the rope-burn. A smell from before.

The handwriting inside slants hard to the left, like someone bracing against a wind.

My handwriting.

Except it isn't, quite. It's rounder. Younger. And the language—

I should not be able to read it. I know I should not be able to read it. Tamsin taught me my letters again in February like a patient woman teaching a large child, laboriously, in Trade-Tongue, and this is not Trade-Tongue. This is a script like river-stones and frost-ferns, and I read it the way I breathe, without deciding to, and each word lands in me like a pebble dropped down a well I'd forgotten I had.

*To the keeper of the door, whoever she is now—*

*The passes are open. The snows broke early in the east. Wren asks after you every season; she has your stubbornness, which I am given to understand you also had, and which she will deny inheriting. Her mother sends dried mountain berries, which taste like nothing grown below the treeline, and which I am eating as I write this, so fewer will arrive than intended.*

*Come and see us, if the woman you are now ever wants to meet the woman you were. We kept a room for you. We kept a great many things for you. Names are lighter than people think—they grow back, like calluses, like gardens after fire.*

*There is a second matter, less pleasant. Something is moving in the deep ranges where the Door stood. The rock remembers being a door, and doors that remember attract traffic. I would not trouble you with it except that the signs match old survey notes—notes in my own hand, from a life I am still doing penance for. Come east. Or send word. Or burn this letter. All three responses are, frankly, in character.*

*—A friend who owes you everything and a debt besides*

I read it four times. Then I sat with it in my lap while the light moved across the floor and the tavern noise rose and fell below me like tide, and I felt something happen in my chest that I had decided, months ago, never to examine: the clenched thing unclenching. Not pain. Recognition. Like a key finding—not turning, not yet, just *finding*—a lock it was cut for.

Wren. The name means nothing and everything. I hold it and it holds me back.

Tamsin knocked, called up that supper was ready, that the Thursday lady had sent word she'd come a day early this week, tomorrow, something about business in the hills, and wasn't that odd, and did I want lamb or fish?

"Lamb," I said automatically. Then: "Tamsin. Do I own a sword?"

The silence downstairs lasted long enough that I imagined her blinking at the pots. "There's a bundle under your bed you've never let me touch," she called back finally. "Wrapped in oilcloth. Been there since the day you came. Why?"

"No reason," I said, which was a lie, and both of us knew it, and neither of us pursued it, because that is the arrangement Tamsin and I have: I keep her secrets and she keeps mine, and mostly we keep them from ourselves together.

After supper, while the dishes were being done and the first market-carts were rumbling in from the west road, I knelt and pulled the bundle out. Oilcloth stiff with age, tied with cord in a knot I undid without looking, my fingers remembering a shape my mind never held.

Inside: a sword. Long, plain-hilted, well-oiled—someone had maintained it, and I suspected with a lurch that someone was me, some part of me, on nights the rest of me slept. The balance was perfect. The grip fit my hand like a sentence fits its ending.

Beneath it, folded small, a strip of leather stamped with a seal: a sword crossed over a flame. Ash-white even now. Even after everything.

I sat back on my heels on the floorboards with a sword in my lap and a letter in my teeth and the whole smooth blank of my life ahead of me like fresh snow, waiting for tracks.

Somewhere east, a door remembered me. Somewhere east, a child with my stubbornness asked after me every season. Tomorrow the Thursday lady would arrive a day early with business in the hills, and the stone in my chest said she would look at the letter's seal and go pale, and say a name—*my* name, the old one, the spent one—and I would watch her say it and feel, for the first time in this borrowed life, homesick for a person I couldn't remember being.

I folded the letter along its creases. I picked up the blade. I turned my face toward the window, toward the east, where the mountains stood up against the evening like a wall someone had built to keep something in—or out.

"Well," I said aloud, to the room, to the woman I might yet become. "Some things outlast names."
