## 22. When the Veil Burns

The letter arrives on a Tuesday, which is the only thing about it that is ordinary.

I know it's Tuesday because the baker told me. I know the baker's name is Marta because there's a sign on her shop and because she greets me every morning with the particular cheer of a woman who has decided I am her project. "Good morning, Ana," she says—Ana being the name Sera invented for me two winters ago, when the priest asked what to call the woman who couldn't say her own.

Ana. It fits like a borrowed coat. Warm enough. Someone else's buttons.

I live above the tavern in Hollowmere now, in a room with one window that faces east over rooftops toward the mountains. The mountains are the only thing I recognize on sight—not their names, which I've had to learn again like a child learning her letters, but the shape of them against the sky. That shape lives somewhere deeper than memory. Somewhere the price didn't reach.

That's the strange arithmetic of what I paid at the Door of Ash. I lost my name. I lost the *why*. Ask me why I climbed that mountain and I will tell you honestly: I don't know. There's a hole where the reason used to be, smooth-walled, like a stone worn round by water. But everything else—the roads, the songs, the way a blade sits in the hand, the taste of a language I supposedly never learned—all of it stayed. The oath took my name and left me my hands.

Some nights I think it got the worse end of the bargain.

* * *

Marta's boy brings the letter up with the breakfast tray, holding it at arm's length like it might bite. He's seven and convinced I'm a witch, which I have done nothing to discourage, because a woman without a past needs some kind of story and *witch* is at least honest about the mystery.

"It came with the dawn rider," he says. "He wouldn't wait for an answer. He looked scared."

"Riders are often scared of witches."

"Not this kind of scared." He sets it down and retreats to the stairs, watching me over his shoulder the whole way.

The letter is heavy paper, cream-colored, sealed with wax the color of old bone. Ash-white. I know that color the way I know the mountains. My thumb finds the seal before my mind does, and something in my chest goes tight and bright, a struck bell.

I break it.

Inside, three pages, written in a hand that loops and slashes like wind through a pass. And here is the thing—I should not be able to read it. Sera has kept the record of who I was; she reads to me from her little black book on Thursdays, patiently, like a woman reading scripture to someone who died. *Your name was Elodie Oyelaran. You were a swordspeaker of Vessar. You gave your name to open the Door.* None of it lands. It's a story about a stranger, well-told. I nod. I thank her. I go back to teaching tavern children songs I don't remember learning, and they sing them wrong, and I correct them without knowing how I know they're wrong.

But the letter—

The letter opens itself in me like a door.

*"To the one who stands watch,"* it begins, *"though you do not remember standing, though you do not remember the door—"*

I sit down on the edge of the bed because my knees have filed a complaint.

The script is Vessarine. I can hear its name in my head even if I couldn't say it aloud a year ago—and could say it now, I realize, testing the silence, tasting syllables like fruit from a country I've never visited. The words don't come to me as translation. They come as meaning, direct, the way you understand fire is hot or snow is cold. Whatever was taken from me, the language grew back around the wound like bone around a break.

*"You asked nothing for what you gave,"* the letter continues, *"so we will give you what you did not ask for: news. Wren is well. She sings. Her mother's arms remembered her, though her mother slept two hundred years waiting to hold her. The orchards woke confused and then glad. The king's line is ended and no one minds, which tells you something about kings. We are rebuilding in the long light behind the mountains, and we have built you a house. It faces west. It has your window in it."*

My eyes have gone hot. I don't understand why until I do, and then I let it happen, sitting alone in a rented room in a body that belongs to a stranger named Ana, crying for a woman called Elodie whom I know only the way you know a character in a book—except the book is written in my handwriting, in my scars, in the calluses on my sword-hand that no tavern-keeper's life explains.

There's more. The second page shifts tone, and whatever part of me wrote letters once leans forward.

*"One matter requires your attention, and we would not ask it if the matter were small. The Door holds, but it holds loosely now, the way a scab holds. There are those on your side of the mountain who have begun to dream of us. Not our people—strangers. Merchants who wake with words in their mouths. A magistrate in Corvane who drew our sigil in his sleep and burned his own records rather than show it to anyone. The erasure is unraveling faster since you opened the way, and unraveling things draw crows."*

Page three is short.

*"We do not ask you to come home. Home is a debt we cannot repay and would not insult you by pretending otherwise. We ask only that you keep watch a while longer—that you be the eyes on this side of the veil until we learn what is dreaming through it. You taught us once that a swordspeaker's word outlasts her name. Prove it to yourself. Come east in spring, if you can. If you cannot come, look east anyway. We will know you're looking."*

It's signed with a single mark: a child's drawing of a bird. A wren.

* * *

I put the letter down and sit with my hands flat on my knees for a long time, listening to the tavern below—Marta's boy thundering down the stairs, the smell of bread rising through the floorboards, the ordinary machinery of a life I assembled out of spare parts.

Then I take out the blade.

It lives under a loose board beneath the bed, wrapped in oilcloth, and I unwrap it the way you'd unwrap something holy or dangerous, which in my experience amounts to the same thing. I've kept it without knowing why. Sera offered once to take it away—"for your own good, Ana"—and I watched myself look at her with an expression that made her laugh and raise both hands and never mention it again. Some things you don't argue with a stranger about.

The steel is dark, older than any kingdom currently on the maps. When I draw it into the light it makes a sound I feel in my teeth, and my grip settles into the wraps like a foot into a boot worn soft by years. My body knows this. My body kept everything.

And there—along the fuller, where I've looked a dozen times and seen only old engraving too worn to read—the letters stand out sharp and silver, as if the metal just now decided to remember. Vessarine. Two words. A name and a title, or a title and a name; I can't tell which is which, and maybe it doesn't matter.

*Elodie Oyelaran. Swordspeaker.*

I read them twice. Three times. Nothing happens. No flood, no returning, no sudden arrival of the woman who owned this blade. The name sits in the steel and looks at me and I look back, and between us there is a window, not a door. She's real. She's gone. She left her sword arm behind as an inheritance, and apparently also her mail, because someone in Vessar knows exactly where I live.

A knock at the door nearly takes a year off my life I can't spare.

It's Sera. Of course it's Sera—it's Thursday, and I've lost track of Thursdays the way I lose track of most things. She comes in carrying her black book and a paper of candied ginger she pretends is for herself, and she stops halfway across the room the way she always does when something's wrong, because ten years of hunting a woman teaches you her weather, and apparently the skill survives the woman.

"You've been crying," she says.

"I received a letter."

"From whom?"

"That's the interesting part." I hold it out. "I can't show you. It's in a language that officially doesn't exist, and if you read it you'll spend the afternoon forgetting you did. But I'll summarize."

She sits. She's learned to sit quickly when I use that tone. She still wears grey, though she left the Crown's service within a season of dragging me down the mountain—resigned so abruptly her superiors assumed blackmail, which in a sense they weren't wrong about. Love is a kind of blackmail. So is guilt. She carries both with the posture of a woman who has stopped apologizing for either.

"There's a country behind the mountains," I tell her. "It's awake. A child I apparently saved sends her regards. And something on their side of the veil—or ours—is dreaming through the seal, and they want a watch kept."

Sera is quiet for a moment. Then, carefully: "Do you want to keep it?"

And there it is, the question underneath the question. Because the honest answer—the answer of the woman in the room, Ana, who likes her bread warm and her mornings slow and her life mercifully unhaunted—is *no*. The other answer belongs to the woman in the steel, and I can't consult her directly. I can only notice that my hand hasn't left the hilt this whole conversation. I can only notice that when I think of looking east, something in me leans like a plant toward light.

"I want," I say slowly, "to know why I'm holding a sword while we talk."

Sera follows my gaze down. She sees the letters along the blade, and I watch her face do something complicated—grief and relief fighting for the same ground. "You can read it," she says. Not a question.

"Some things outlast names." I shrug, and am surprised to find the shrug almost steady. "Apparently that includes me."

She opens her black book on her knee, out of habit, and then closes it again. For the first time since I've known her—as far as I know her—she doesn't write anything down. "So what now?" she asks. "Spring's a long way off. The pass won't clear before thaw."

"Then I'll be ready when it does." I wrap the blade back in its oilcloth—but I don't put it under the floorboard. I lean it against the wall by the window, in plain sight, where a sword has no business being in a room above a tavern. Let the whole town gossip. Let Marta's boy finally get his witch. "And meanwhile I'm going to teach the children better songs. Ones that might matter, later, if the dreaming gets worse. Songs are how the last erasure failed, if I'm reading my own handwriting right. Words outlast names. Might as well sow a few."

Sera laughs—short, wet, surprised. "You sound like her. Like Elodie."

"I'll take that as a compliment to us both."

She stays for tea. We don't talk about the letter again, but twice she catches me looking east through the window, and the second time she doesn't pretend not to see. Instead she gets up, refills my cup, and stands beside me at the glass, shoulder to shoulder, two women watching a horizon that owes us nothing and keeps asking anyway.

Below, the tavern noise swells and dims. Above the rooftops, past the smoke and the winter-bare trees, the Thornfells stand white against a sky going the color of a candle flame—ash-white, bone-white, the exact color of a seal I broke this morning. Somewhere beyond them is a house with my window in it. Somewhere closer, in the space between sleeping and waking, something is dreaming in a language only a handful of living mouths can speak.

One of those mouths is mine. Nameless, and mine.

I fold the letter along its creases and tuck it inside my shirt, over the heart, which seems fitting whether or not the heart remembers why. Then I pick up the blade—my hand finding the wraps without looking, the way it always does—and I turn to the window, and I look east.

The mountains look back. Spring is coming. Something is stirring on both sides of the veil, and a child's drawn wren is drying against my skin, and I am nobody at all, and I am entirely, irreducibly, the woman for this work.

"Watch with me," I say to Sera, quietly, in a language neither of us officially knows.

She answers in the same tongue. Of course she does. Half an oath, all these years, waiting for its other voice.

Together, we look east.
