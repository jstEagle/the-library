## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The nightshade came in the honey.

Ivo knew it before the spoon touched his tongue — knew it the way a fisherman knows weather, in the small hairs of his arms and the sour turn of his stomach. The king's breakfast sat before him on the tasting table: brown bread still warm from the ovens, butter folded like a swan, smoked river trout arranged in silver crescents, and a small clay pot of honey from the south meadows, its wax seal unbroken.

Unbroken. That was the first wrong thing. The seal was stamped with the kitchen-master's mark, but the wax had been pressed twice — once firmly, once lightly, as if someone had lifted it and set it back down. Ivo had spent nineteen years learning to read such small dishonesties. The valley taught patience to everyone who lived inside it; to him it had taught suspicion dressed up as devotion.

He dipped the spoon. The honey was amber-dark, fragrant with wildflower, sweet as a lie told gently. He held it on his tongue for a count of ten, then twenty, letting the poison find whatever purchase it wanted.

It found none. It never did.

There was a bitterness underneath the sweetness, faint as a whisper behind a door — hemlock's cousin, nightshade proper, the kind that stopped a strong man's heart between one breath and the next. Someone had stirred it into the honey with a needle-thin hand, careful not to disturb the surface, careful to leave no cloudiness, no crystallization. A professional. Which meant it wasn't the cook, who was careless and kind, but someone above the cook. Someone with access to the seals.

Ivo swallowed, set down the spoon, and waited for his body to file its report.

His body filed nothing. Nineteen years of small doses had built walls in his blood that most men couldn't imagine. Belladonna, arsenic, foxglove, the white death that came in powdered pearls — he'd tasted them all, a spoonful at a time, until his tongue could name them like old neighbors and his gut could shrug them off like rude ones. The palace physicians called it a gift. Old Marta, who had raised him more than anyone, called it something else entirely.

"You weren't made for this work," she liked to say, thumping his back when a bad dose left him retching into the courtyard drain. "You were *put* here. There's a difference, boy, and someday you'll know what it is."

He'd never asked what she meant. Some questions are doors you can feel the draft through, and you learn to keep them shut.

* * *

The morning proceeded around him like water around a stone.

At half past seven he gave his verdict to Captain Aldery, who commanded the king's table: "The honey is poisoned. Nightshade, subtle dose, introduced after sealing." His voice came out flat and professional, because that was what the work required. Inside, where no one checked, something always twisted when he said the word *poisoned* aloud — a small grief, as if each detection were a tiny funeral for the person who'd tried.

Captain Aldery wrote it down without surprise. Surprise would have meant the captain didn't already suspect, and the captain always suspected. That was the trouble with the Veiled Valley: nothing ever truly happened here, so everyone had become an expert at reading the shadows of things that might.

"Third attempt this season," Aldery said.

"Fourth. You're forgetting the almonds in spring."

"The almonds were rancid, not poisoned."

"Rancid almonds don't carry a bitter finish like that. Someone dosed them and they went off besides. Sloppy work." Ivo wiped the spoon clean and laid it on the cloth. "Whoever it is has gotten better since."

Aldery looked at him for a long moment — the look of a man deciding how much to tell a tool about the hand that holds it. "The king will eat elsewhere this morning. You'll take the midday meal as usual."

"I always do."

"Yes." The captain gathered his papers. Then, pausing at the door, without turning: "You've never asked to leave the valley, Brightwater. Nineteen years. Doesn't that strike you as strange?"

Ivo kept his face pleasant and empty, the face he wore for captains and priests and anyone else whose questions had drafts behind them. "Where would I go?"

"That's what I wonder." And Aldery left.

The tasting room settled into silence. Through its narrow window Ivo could see the valley floor spread out below the palace terrace: green fields stitched with stone walls, the river running bright through the middle of everything, the village with its smoking chimneys, and beyond it all — ringing the valley like the rim of a cup — the standing stones. Grey, ancient, taller than three men, spaced at intervals along every ridge. No gate in them. No road through them. Just stones, and mist that clung to their bases even on clear days, and beyond the mist a world Ivo had seen only in the flat colors of maps and the secondhand light of travelers' tales — except no travelers came. Nothing came in. Nothing went out.

He had asked about the stones exactly once, at age nine, sitting on Marta's knee in the wash-house while she wrung sheets dry.

"Why don't we leave?"

Marta's hands had gone still in the wet linen. Not frozen — *still*, the way a deer goes still, listening. "Leave where, love?"

"Over the stones. To wherever the king's spices come from. Wherever the maps end."

She'd resumed wringing then, slowly, and said, "The valley is the whole world, for people like us. The king keeps us safe inside it. Safe is worth more than far."

Even at nine he'd heard the shape of a non-answer. But Marta's lap was warm and her arms were strong and the sheets smelled of lye and lavender, and he had loved her too much to press. He'd been pressing ever since, quietly, in the privacy of his own skull — pressing against the valley the way a seed presses against its casing, without knowing yet whether it was a shell or a wall.

* * *

Midday passed without incident. So did the evening meal — roast fowl, root stew, wine from the terraced vineyards, all of it clean, all of it merely food. It was almost disappointing. A taster's day with no poison in it was a day the kingdom had decided, for a few hours, not to need him.

By dusk he walked down through the palace gardens to the servants' quarter, where the people who had raised him were lighting lamps and arguing about supper.

They lived in a crooked row of cottages beneath the east wall: Marta the laundress, wide as a doorway and soft as one too; Tam the under-cook, who had burned his hands so often they were smooth as wax; Odell the groom, grey-bearded, missing two fingers to a stallion's temper; and little Wren, the scullery girl, twelve years old and sharp-tongued as nettle, who had appointed herself Ivo's sister by decree at age six and permitted no appeals.

They were not his blood. He had no blood, or none that anyone would speak of. He'd been left at the palace gates as an infant — so the story went — wrapped in a blanket with no mark or token, on a morning so ordinary that no one could later remember anything remarkable about it at all. Marta had found him. Marta had named him. The others had simply absorbed him, the way a household absorbs a cat: completely, permanently, and with occasional complaints.

"You look pale," Marta announced, the moment he stepped through her door. She said it the way other people said *good evening*.

"He found poison in the honey," Wren reported, not looking up from the onions she was butchering with more enthusiasm than skill. "Nightshade. In the *honey*. Who poisons honey? That's just spiteful."

"Someone with a grudge against sweetness," Tam said from the hearth, where he was defending a pan of fried potatoes from his own tendency to neglect them.

"Or against the king," Odell offered from his chair by the window, where he sat every evening mending tack that did not need mending.

"Everyone's got a grudge against the king. That's what being a king means." Marta steered Ivo into a chair and put a bowl of broth in front of him with the authority of a woman administering medicine. "Eat. You taste death all day; you'll eat life at my table."

Supper at Marta's was the only meal Ivo ate without thinking. Here there was no counting of seconds, no cataloguing of finishes and aftertastes, no quiet professional grief. Here there was only Tam burning things, Wren stealing potatoes from the pan and denying it with onion-breath honesty, Odell telling the story of the two-fingered stallion for the four-hundredth time, and Marta presiding over it all like a general who had won her war long ago and now fought only for the last word.

"So the fourth time this year," Odell said, around a mouthful. "Somebody wants the king dead proper bad."

"Somebody always does. That's kings." Wren pointed her knife at Ivo. "But nobody ever catches them, do they? Four tries and no arrests. Doesn't that seem funny to you?"

"Wren—"

"It does seem funny," she insisted. "It seems funny like a puzzle with pieces missing. If I poisoned the king's honey, I'd be caught by supper. Somebody knows who it is and isn't saying."

Ivo opened his mouth to give the sensible adult answer — investigations took time, evidence was scarce, the captain handled such matters — and found, to his surprise, that he agreed with her. Something in the pattern itched. Poison attempts that grew more skilled instead of less. A captain who suspected everything and explained nothing. Seals pressed twice. And beneath it all, the sense — vague, persistent, like a word on the tip of the tongue — that the poisonings were not really attempts at all. That they were something else wearing an attempt's clothing.

He said none of this. Instead he ate his potatoes, laughed at Odell's stallion story despite himself, and let Wren fall asleep against his shoulder halfway through her third helping, her knife still in her fist, her fury at the world finally out-argued by exhaustion.

Marta covered her with a quilt and stood looking down at the two of them for a moment, her big hands folded. "You know what today is?" she said quietly.

"Tuesday?"

"Nineteen years ago tonight, I found you." She smiled, but it was a careful smile, set in place like a dish set carefully on a high shelf. "Nineteen years. And in all that time you've never once asked me the question you should have asked."

"Marta, I've asked you hundreds of questions."

"Not that one." She touched his cheek, briefly, with fingers rough from forty years of lye. "And I'm too much a coward to answer it if you did. So we'll call it even, you and I, and I'll get the bread rising early tomorrow."

She turned to the basin then, and began washing dishes, humming an old tune with words worn off it, and Ivo sat with Wren's weight warm against his shoulder and felt the unease arrive on schedule, the way it did every year on this night — the feeling of standing in a warm lit room with a locked door somewhere in the walls, and everyone in the room pretending not to hear the knocking.

* * *

He left late, when the lamps had burned low and Odell snored in his chair and Marta had shooed him out with the particular tenderness she reserved for endings. The path back up to the palace wound through the kitchen gardens, past beds gone dormant for the season, past the cold frames and the fig house, and emerged onto the terrace where the whole valley lay open under the moon.

He stopped there. He always stopped there. It was the best view in the valley and the worst, depending on the hour and the heart.

Below, the river caught moonlight and threw it back in broken pieces. The village slept. Smoke rose straight from a dozen chimneys into windless air. And all around, at the rim of the world, the standing stones stood their ancient watch, silvered by moon, holding the mist at their feet like dogs asleep on a leash.

Nineteen years, and he had never been past them. Never seen the other side. Never walked a road that didn't end at stone. Other people had horizons; Ivo had a ceiling, and everyone he loved lived beneath it with him, calling it safety, calling it home, calling it the whole world.

*Safe is worth more than far.*

He believed her. That was the terrible thing. He believed her the way you believe a mother — completely, and knowing all the while that belief is not the same as truth.

He was still standing there, working the thought like a sore tooth, when he saw the light down by the ford.

A lantern, moving along the river path where no one had any business at this hour. It bobbed and stumbled, went dark, came back. And then — faint across the water, thin as thread — a sound that raised every hair on his arms.

Someone was calling his name.

*Ivo.* Carried over the river on the still night air. *Ivo Brightwater.*

Nobody called him by his full name. Nobody used *Brightwater* except the captain, in reports. Whoever stood stumbling along the ford in the dark knew him — or knew of him — and was coming toward the valley with the certainty of someone who had run out of everything except purpose.

He should have fetched the guard. Every instinct trained into him by nineteen years of palaces said *fetch the guard*. But his feet were already moving, down the terrace steps, along the garden wall, toward the river and the lantern and the voice, and the strange thing — the thing he would think about for the rest of his life — was that his feet did not feel like they were disobeying him.

They felt like they were remembering the way.
