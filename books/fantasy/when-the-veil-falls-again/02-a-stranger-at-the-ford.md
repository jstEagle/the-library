## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river at dawn was the color of hammered tin, and Ivo Brightwater had been sent down to it with two copper pails and a scolding still ringing in his ears.

"You let Master Ollen's nightshade pass your tongue and didn't say a word until he'd drunk half his tea," Cook Marta had said, jabbing a floury finger at his chest while the kitchen roared around them both. "Nineteen years old, and you sit there smiling like a boy at his first tasting."

"It was a very subtle dose," Ivo had said.

"Subtle." She'd thrown her hands up. "Subtle nearly killed the man who taught you to walk. Take the big buckets. The morning kettles want the sweet water from the ford, not the well water that tastes of stone. And think about what you've done."

He thought about it as he walked. That was the trouble with being the taster — or one of the troubles; there were many, and most of them lived in his stomach like swallowed coins. A poison too small to notice was still a poison. A kindness too small to mention was still a lie by omission. He should have told Ollen at once, should have spat the taste into the spittoon and named it: nightshade, faint, masked with honey and something bitter underneath, foxglove maybe, clever, *very* clever. Instead he had sat through four more dishes because the king's breakfast waited for no man, and only when Ollen's hand had begun to tremble against his teacup did Ivo say anything at all.

Ollen would live. The physic said so. But Ivo carried the moment anyway, turning it over as he walked the river path, the way his tongue turned over a suspect flavor: what else had he tasted and not named? What else was sitting in him even now, waiting?

The Veiled Valley in early autumn smelled of woodsmoke and ripening apples. Mist lay along the riverbanks in long white ropes, so thick some mornings that the far shore simply ceased to exist, as though the world ended politely at the waterline. The valley folk said the mist was a blessing, that it kept out wolves and worse things, though nobody could quite say what the worse things were. Nobody, as far as Ivo knew, had ever tried to find out. The standing stones marked the valley's edges, gray and lichen-crusted, older than any record, and beyond them — nothing anyone spoke of. Not forbidden, exactly. Just unnecessary. Everything necessary was inside: the orchards, the mills, the palace with its crooked towers, the king who was less a king than a beloved fixture, like the bridge or the bell tower, and the long tables where Ivo had eaten every meal of his life among people who were not his blood but had become so through sheer accumulation of suppers.

He was thinking about supper — goose, if the geese cooperated, and Marta's onion pie that Pip stole crusts from — when the lantern caught his eye.

It stood on the far side of the ford, wedged in the reeds where the water ran shallow over pale stones. A river lantern, brass and green-glassed, the kind ferrymen used, though no ferry crossed here; the ford was for carts and cattle and kitchen boys with buckets. It shouldn't have been lit. Lanterns weren't lit at dawn unless someone wanted to be found.

And then he heard the voice again — the voice from the garden last night, thin and frayed, calling a word he couldn't make out. His feet had already decided about it once before. Now they took him off the path, down the slick grass to the water's edge, pails swinging and knocking against his knees.

"Hello?" His voice came out smaller than he meant. He tried again. "Is someone there?"

The reeds parted.

She was kneeling in the shallows as if she'd knelt there for years, as if she were part of the ford itself, one more pale stone among the others. A woman, middle-aged or aged hard, wearing traveling clothes gone dark with water — and not with water alone. Ivo had seen blood before. He'd seen a carter gored by an ox, seen a knife fight outside the Ram's Head that left two men leaking into the gutter, seen the butcher's block run red every morning of his childhood. Blood was red. Blood was supposed to be red.

What pooled around this woman's knees and threaded downstream in slow ribbons was silver.

Not bright. Not pretty. It was the silver of tarnished mirrors, of old coins found in walls, and it moved wrong — heavier than blood, clinging to the stones, catching the first real light of the sun so that the whole ford glittered as if someone had shattered a chandelier in the river.

Ivo dropped the pails. They rang off the rocks and rolled, and the sound seemed to wake her. Her head came up, and her eyes found his with terrible precision, the way a drowning man finds a rope.

"You," she said.

"I'll get help—" He was already turning.

"No." The word cracked across the water like a whip, and something in it stopped him — not fear exactly, though fear was there, cold in his gut, but the trained reflex of a boy who had spent his life learning to obey the urgent voice. "No help. No guards. Come here. *Here.*"

Every rule he had ever been taught said otherwise. Strangers did not come up the river road. Strangers did not bleed silver. Strangers did not give orders in voices like hers, hoarse and absolute, a voice that had given orders for a living. And yet his boots were already in the shallows, the icy water soaking through to his ankles, and he could not for the life of him have explained why except that looking at her felt like looking at a door left open in winter — you couldn't just leave it.

He knelt beside her. Up close she smelled of iron and rain and something else beneath, something burnt-sweet like a snuffed candle, and her skin was gray as ash except where the silver traced her veins — and it *did* trace her veins, he saw now, rising under her skin like frost ferns growing up a windowpane. Whatever hurt her wasn't content to stay at the wound. It was climbing.

"What happened to you?" he whispered.

Her mouth bent into something that might once have been a smile. "Long story," she said. "No time. You're the taster."

It wasn't a question, and it landed in him like a stone in a well. "How do you—"

"The way you kneel. Checking my breath without touching me. Looking at my lips for color." Her laugh was a wet, awful sound. "Palace-trained. Brightwater. Ivo Brightwater." She said his name the way Marta said it when he was in trouble — full weight, all syllables — and the cold in his gut spread outward. "Good. Good boy. Then listen, because I don't have the time to convince you, and the map doesn't have patience."

"My name— how do you know my—"

"*Listen.*" She moved faster than a dying woman should, one hand seizing his wrist with a grip like a manacle, and pressed her other fist against his chest. Something was folded in it, small and stiff. "Take it. Take it and don't show it to anyone. Don't fold it wrong, don't burn it, don't sell it, don't let it out of your skin. Do you understand me? Skin. It wants skin."

"I don't want anything, I don't know what—"

"You will." Her eyes were doing something frightening — flickering, the pupils widening and narrowing like a cat's in shifting light. "Ivo. Listen to me now, because the rest is going to go quickly, and I would rather it went to you than back to *them*. The crown remembers."

"...What?"

"The crown. Remembers." Each word cost her visibly, dragged up from somewhere deep, and the silver in her veins pulsed brighter with each one. "Everything they fed it. Every memory it drank. It's not full. It's never full. And it has learned—" she shuddered, a whole-body convulsion, silver weeping fresh from her sleeve where the wound was — "it has learned to reach. Past the stones. Past the mist. That's how it found me. That's how it will find—"

She coughed, and it was silver she coughed, droplets scattering on the river surface like flung coins, each one sinking unnaturally fast.

"Who's *them*?" Ivo asked, and hated how young his voice sounded. "Who found you? What's in the crown? What are you talking about?"

But she wasn't listening anymore, or wasn't able to. Her free hand fumbled at the folds of her coat, drew out the thing she'd pressed to his chest, and forced it into his palm, closing his fingers over it himself, one by one, with the deliberate care of a woman folding a shroud. It was paper, stiff as vellum and warm — warmer than her hands, warmer than his own skin, warm as a held bird — and it seemed to press back against his fingers, almost like breathing.

"The map," she breathed. "It knows the way. It always knows the way. Follow it or burn it, taster, but do not — do *not* — let them read it. If they read it through you—"

"Through me? Nobody reads anything through me, I'm a kitchen servant, I taste soup—"

"You're the valley's own," she said, and her grip tightened, nails biting, and her eyes fixed on his with such ferocity that everything else in the world went quiet — the river, the birds, his own hammering heart. "Born behind the veil. Do you know how rare — do you know what you *are*—"

"I'm a taster."

"You're what the veil made to keep its door shut," she whispered. "Ask yourself why nothing magic can touch you. Ask yourself why they keep you inside. Ask—" and then the strength went out of her all at once, like a snuffed flame, and she sagged forward into his arms.

Ivo caught her. Of course he caught her; nineteen years of kitchens had built shoulders, and grief and panic weigh less than a dying woman. He lowered her back against the reeds, shouting now, actually shouting, "Help! Someone! *Help!*" — his voice flying flat and dead across the water, swallowed by the mist as though the valley itself had put a hand over his mouth.

"Stop," she murmured. Her eyes had gone distant, fixed on something above him, or through him. "Waste of breath. It won't hear you. Nothing crosses for me. I made sure of that."

"What did you do?"

"Came here to die where it can't follow." Her hand rose, trembling, and touched his cheek — gently, motherly, unbearably sad. "Silly of me. Dying is a door too. Should have known it would try the same one." Her focus slid, returned. "The crown remembers. Say it back."

"The crown remembers," Ivo said, because she needed him to, because some deep-buried part of him understood that this was the only inheritance she had left to give and refusing it would be its own kind of murder.

"Good." The tarnished smile again. "You'll do. You'll have to do." Her gaze drifted past his shoulder, toward the valley, toward the smoke of the kitchens rising above the trees, and something in her face broke open — longing, pure and naked, the look of a woman watching a lit window from a frozen street. "Warm in there," she whispered. "Smells like bread. I'd forgotten bread could smell like that."

"Then come inside," Ivo said desperately. "Come inside, we'll get the physic, we'll—"

"I'm past physicking, child." She laughed softly, terribly. "Silver's the least of it. What they poured into me is already drinking. I can feel it finding my names." Her eyes came back to his, and held, and in them he watched a light gutter the way a candle gutters when the wax runs low. "My name was Wren. Had it forty-one years. Remember that someone was called Wren, will you? Even if it's only you."

"Wren," he said. "I'll remember. Wren."

"Such a good boy," she murmured. "Marta's pies. Tell her the crust needs more salt. She'll argue. Let her argue. Arguing is—" and her voice failed, not dramatically, not with a last gasp, but quietly, mid-thought, the sentence simply ending the way a path ends at a cliff edge.

Ivo knelt in the freezing shallows holding a stranger who was not breathing, and shouted for help until his throat tore, and no one came.

* * *

The sun climbed. The mist began, slowly, to lift off the river in rags, and Ivo became aware of things one by one, the way a dreamer surfaces: that he was soaked to the waist; that the folded thing in his fist was still warm; that the silver in the water had stopped flowing and was, instead, *rising* — beading up out of the current like mercury, gathering in the reeds, sliding along the stones, drawing itself inward toward the woman's body with soft, greedy little sounds.

He scrambled backward up the bank and watched, mouth open, as the last of Wren's spilled blood returned home.

That was bad. That was impossible. But it was nothing — nothing at all — compared to what happened next, which was that the valley mist, which had been lifting everywhere else along the river, began to *gather* around her.

It came from both banks and upstream and downstream, ropes of white coiling in off the water like snakes charmed by a flute, thickening over the shallows until Wren lay in a column of fog. Ivo had grown up with the mist. He knew its moods the way he knew the marts of the kitchen: the way it clung to laundry, the way it burned off sullenly before rain, the way it curled affectionately around the standing stones as if the valley were a sleeping animal and the fog its breath. The mist took things — that was one of the oldest sayings, one of the first things children learned. *The mist takes what the valley is done with.* Fallen branches. Dead leaves. Once, horribly, old Tam the miller's dog, and Tam had stood at the water's edge hat in hand and let it, saying that was the price of living behind the veil, that nothing buried here stayed buried, that the mist kept the valley clean.

The mist took everything. That was the order of the world, as fixed as water running downhill.

The mist would not take Wren.

It touched her and recoiled. Ivo watched it happen three times, four times — the white ropes reaching, curling over her body, and then flinching back as if scalded, recoiling, circling, trying again from another angle with something that looked horribly like reluctance. Like hunger meeting a locked door. Around and around her the fog churned, unable to close, unable to withdraw, and beneath it her body lay untouched, unclaimed, silver-veined and stubborn, refusing the valley's oldest law.

"No," Ivo heard himself say. "No, take her. Take her, please, take her—"

The mist ignored him. It had never ignored anyone in the history of the valley.

He backed away up the bank until the grass met his heels, clutching the pails he'd forgotten he was carrying, and the folded warmth in his other fist pulsed once — twice — like a second heartbeat answering the scene below. Without meaning to, without deciding to, he opened his fingers and looked at what Wren had died to give him.

It was a map. Folded small, drawn in ink the color of dried blood on paper the color of bone, and it showed — he tilted it toward the strengthening light — a city. Streets radiating from a great circular plaza like cracks in glass, a river bisecting it, a palace crowning the highest hill with a star marked at its heart. Names curved along the streets in a fine, slanted hand: *Corvane. The Gullet. Bellwether Row. The Undercroft Stairs.* Places he had never heard of, in a script that made his eyes ache to follow, as if the letters were slightly deeper than the page allowed.

And beneath the palace star, in the same slanted hand, five words:

*Beneath the crown, the well.*

The map was warm in his hands. Warmer than the morning justified. And as he stared at it, he had the sudden crawling certainty — irrational, total — that it was staring back. That somewhere in that fine slanted handwriting, something had noticed him noticing, and was pleased.

Down at the ford, the mist gave up at last. It withdrew in sullen coils, pouring back off the bank, leaving Wren lying exposed to the open sky in her pool of gathered silver — the first thing in the history of the Veiled Valley the mist had refused. Above the eastern ridge, the sun cleared the stones and struck her face, and Ivo thought, absurdly, that she looked like a statue in a temple, like something people would someday argue about having seen.

Behind him, up the river path, a bell began to ring.

Not the breakfast bell. The alarm bell — the one that hadn't sounded in Ivo's lifetime, whose rope Marta claimed was rotten with disuse. Its voice rolled down over the orchards and the rooftops, harsh and frightened, and doors began opening all along the far bank, and small figures came running, and someone was shouting words the wind shredded before they reached him.

Ivo looked at the map. The map lay in his hands, warm as a living thing.

He looked at Wren, whom the valley would not take, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling: they would ask questions now. Who was she. Where did she come from. Who had she spoken to. And the guards would come down the path with their questions and their careful hands, and they would find a kitchen boy soaked to the waist kneeling beside a corpse the mist itself had rejected, holding —

His fist closed over the map.

— holding nothing. Holding nothing at all.

He tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it settled with a warmth that was nearly a purr, and Wren's voice came back to him one final time, clear across the years he suddenly feared were numbered: *the map doesn't have patience.*

The footsteps on the path behind him were many, and getting closer.
