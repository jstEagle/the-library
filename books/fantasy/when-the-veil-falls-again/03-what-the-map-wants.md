## 3. What the Map Wants

The map would not stay folded.

Ivo had tried everything a sensible man could try. He had wrapped it in linen and buried it beneath the floorstones of his room, where he kept the few things that were truly his: a wooden spoon worn smooth by Marta's hands, a river-glass pebble from Tam, the stub of a candle saved for no occasion in particular. The map had burned through the linen by morning — not with flame, but with heat, as if it had been held too long over a cookfire, and when he dug it up the fold-lines glowed faintly, like embers breathing under ash.

He had tried the river. Cold water should have killed whatever lived in the paper. Instead the water had steamed, gently, politely, like a kettle deciding to be quiet about it, and the ink had darkened until the lines looked freshly drawn.

He had tried simply leaving it alone on his table, unfolded, face down, weighted at the corners with the spoon, the pebble, and the candle. That worked for most of one day. But maps are made to be read, and this one was made of something worse than paper, and sometime in the grey hour before dawn Ivo woke to find it standing upright against the wall, open, facing him, its creases glowing like veins of light beneath skin.

"Stop that," he whispered, because there was no one else to whisper to, and because whispering felt safer than screaming.

The map did not stop. But it did something else instead: it whispered.

Not words, exactly. Not at first. It was more the way a kitchen sounds from two rooms away — the murmur of many small activities, none of them distinct, all of them busy. Then, as the night wore on and Ivo lay rigid beneath his blanket pretending to sleep, the murmur began to sort itself into shapes, and the shapes began to have edges, and the edges became names.

*Coriander Row.* *The Stair of Nine Sighs.* *Bellows Street, where they mend what fire has broken.*

Streets. He was certain they were streets, though he had never heard of any of them, and the valley had only four roads worth naming and none so fanciful. The names came in a woman's voice — low, dry, patient, the voice of someone who has given directions to strangers a thousand times and expects nothing but gratitude or rudeness. Sometimes she said a name twice, as if checking whether he'd written it down.

Ivo had no paper. He didn't dare write anything down. Writing would make it real, and real things could be taken from you, and he had already lost enough in two days to last a careful lifetime.

Because the woman was still at the ford.

That was the other thing. The mist had refused her.

In the Veiled Valley, the mist took everything, eventually. Fallen branches, drowned lambs, and yes — bodies, when the valley's long peace was broken by one, which happened perhaps once in a generation. The fog came down off the hills the way an old housekeeper comes into a sickroom, brisk and unsentimental, and folded the dead away where no one had to look at them. Ivo had seen it do this to a shepherd's mule when he was nine. He had assumed, in the unexamined way of children, that it did the same for people.

It had not done it for her.

Three days now, and she lay where he had dragged her — behind the fallen alder at the ford's edge, out of sight of the path — and the mist rolled past her every dawn and dusk like a servant pointedly ignoring a beggar. Her blood had dried silver on the stones. Silver, like solder. Like the moon's own marrow. And her body did not stiffen, did not fade, did not smell. She looked less dead each day, which was somehow far worse than the opposite, and Ivo had begun to avoid going near the ford at all, fetching his water from the upstream shallows like a superstitious child, hating himself a little more each time.

*The crown remembers,* she had said, pressing the folded thing into his hands with fingers that left silver smears on his knuckles. *Keep it moving. Keep yourself—*

And then she had stopped, mid-word, the way a candle stops when pinched, and the rest of the sentence had gone wherever sentences go, and Ivo had been holding a stranger's dying ever since.

He thought about telling someone. He thought about it constantly, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Tell Marta, who had raised him from a foundling basket in the palace scullery and whose authority extended, as far as Ivo knew, to the edge of the world. Tell Old Tam, who had taught him which mushrooms lied and which stones remembered rain. Tell the captain of the valley guard, a decent man named Ferro who once let Ivo taste-test his wife's plum wine and pronounced it *survivable*, which was high praise.

Tell them what? That a bleeding woman had handed him a burning map that whispered street names? That the mist wouldn't take her? That the paper grew warm when he lied and warmer when he hid it?

They would ask how long he had known. They would ask why he hadn't come straight away. And then the guards would take the map, and take the body, and take the story, and somewhere between the taking and the telling, Ivo had a deep, wordless certainty that something would go wrong in a way he couldn't yet see — that he would be handing the wolf not just the door key but a chair by the fire.

So he waited. Tasters are good at waiting. His whole life was waiting — the pause after the swallow, the count to three hundred while the king's breakfast sat in him and decided whether it was food or murder. He had spent twenty-two years being the space between question and answer.

But the map was finished with waiting.

* * *

On the fourth morning, the guards came.

Ivo knew it before he saw them, because the palace kitchens went quiet in a particular way — a silence with a spine in it, different from the ordinary lulls of the workday. Sound in the kitchens was like weather; you learned to read it. This silence meant someone important had entered, and everyone was suddenly very interested in their own hands.

He was scrubbing the tasting bowls when they came through the archway: two men in the king's livery, which was strange, because the valley kept its own watch and the king's men rarely troubled themselves past the boundary stones. One was young, with the bored face of a man doing arithmetic he found insulting. The older one had a sergeant's build and a sergeant's eyes, which moved over the kitchen the way a hand moves over goods at market — weighing, pricing, dismissing.

"Mistress Marta." The older one inclined his head with exactly the right degree of courtesy. "Captain's compliments. A traveler's effects were reported missing near the ford some days past. We're told your people draw water there."

Marta didn't turn from her dough. Marta never turned from her dough for anyone below a duke, and possibly not for dukes either. "People draw water everywhere," she said. "Water doesn't keep."

"We're looking for a map," the younger one said, too quickly. "Folded. Leather-cased. Possibly bloodstained."

The word *bloodstained* landed in the kitchen like a dropped knife, and Ivo felt every back in the room go carefully, elaborately neutral. In the valley, blood was an event. Blood was for butcher-day and bad accidents and the fever year when Ivo was twelve. Blood was not a word you said in front of the bread.

"A map," Marta repeated. Her hands kept working. Ivo watched her forearms, which he had studied his whole life the way other sons study faces, and saw the tiny hitch in her rhythm — one beat of stillness, quickly recovered. She had seen something. Or heard something. Or guessed.

"The traveler," the sergeant pressed, "may have come to harm. If anyone found... remains, or articles..."

"Remains." Marta finally turned, flour to the elbow, and gave him the full weight of a stare that had survived forty years of palace politics conducted entirely through soup. "Young man, the last remains we had in this valley were old Hemmet's cow, and the mist dealt with it before I'd finished my mourning stew. If a traveler's gone missing, I'd say the valley's swallowed him whole, same as it swallows everything else, and good luck to whoever wants it back."

Something flickered across the sergeant's face — not doubt. Recognition. As if Marta had confirmed a suspicion rather than deflected a question. He thanked her with another precise inclination of the head, and the two men withdrew, but not before the younger one's gaze swept the room one more time and snagged — just for a heartbeat, just lightly, the way a hook snags a sleeve — on Ivo.

Ivo kept scrubbing his bowl. His hands were steady. Twenty-two years of steadiness, trained into him dish by dish, poison by survived poison. Inside, something was running, fast, in circles.

They hadn't asked about a woman. They'd asked about a traveler, singular, and a map. Which meant either they didn't know she was dead — or they knew exactly, and wanted to know who'd been standing next to her when it happened.

At midday, the map burned him through his shirt.

He was carrying a tray to the steward's office, minding his own business with the diligence of the truly terrified, when the folded square against his ribs went from warm to hot in the space of ten steps. He bit down on a yelp, set the tray down with exaggerated care, and pressed his palm flat over his heart. Through the linen of his shirt he could feel the creases glowing. If he didn't get it away from his skin, he was going to walk through the palace smelling of scorched cloth, and tasters who smelled of smoke attracted questions.

*Ivo,* whispered the dry voice inside the paper, and this time it wasn't street names. This time it was almost gentle. *They will find the ford today.*

He believed it. That was the worst part. He believed it the way he believed nightshade when he tasted it — instantly, bodily, below argument. The guards had come asking. The sergeant's eyes had priced the room and found him. By evening they would walk the riverbank, and they would find the alder, and behind the alder they would find a woman who should have been mist-taken and wasn't, and beside her tracks in the mud — his tracks, his boots, size nine, made by the cobbler in the lower village like every other pair of boots in the valley.

He could stay. He could tell the truth, all of it, throw himself on Marta's flour-white apron and the captain's decency and the valley's famous safety. Perhaps it would be fine. Perhaps the guards were honest men and the map would be confiscated and studied and returned, and the whispers would stop, and life would go on with its tastings and its suppers and its four roads.

Or perhaps the thing the woman had died carrying was exactly the kind of thing that made honest men dangerous, and everyone who stood near Ivo when it was found would stand in the splash zone of whatever came next.

He looked around the kitchen — really looked, for the first time in days — at the great scarred tables, the hanging bunches of thyme going papery at the tips, the boy Pip-from-the-village (no relation to any other Pip, though Ivo didn't know that yet) chasing a runaway onion across the flags while the under-cook shouted encouragement rather than correction. The afternoon sun came through the south windows in slanted gold bars and lit the flour dust drifting like slow snow.

This was his whole world. Four roads. One river. Walls of standing stone older than any record, beyond which even the king's tax collectors did not pass — the treaty was clear, the elders were fond of saying, the valley keeps to itself and is kept. He had never once been outside it. He had never particularly wanted to be. Why would you leave a place where you were loved, useful, fed, and safe?

Because safety, he was beginning to understand, was a flavor too. And he had just tasted something underneath it. Something metallic. Something that did not belong in the dish.

* * *

He packed nothing, because packing takes time and time was a liquid running out of a cracked jug. He took the map because it would not permit otherwise. He took his grandmother's — Marta's — wooden spoon, because sentiment is quick when it has had years to prepare. He wrote no note because he could not trust his handwriting not to shake, and a shaking note says *run* louder than footsteps.

Then he stood in the kitchen doorway like an idiot for one full minute, breathing the smell of bread and bay and thirty years of other people's lives, memorizing it against whatever came.

"I'm getting onions from the garden," he told the under-cook, loud enough for the room.

"Take the boy with—" she started, and Ivo was already through the door.

The kitchen gardens ran along the south wall in long green rows: beans, brassicas, the herb beds, the cold frames, and beyond them the orchard, and beyond the orchard the low wall, and beyond the wall the pasture, and beyond the pasture — though he had never seen it, never once — the standing stones, and beyond the stones the world. He walked the rows at a gardener's pace, unhurried, nodding to old Bess weeding the leeks, and it cost him everything he had to nod like a man with nothing in his shirt but a heartbeat.

The loaf was an accident. Truly. The day's baking was cooling on the windowsill of the garden shed — six round loaves, crusts split and golden, and one of them was sitting at the end of the sill nearest the door as if it had been set aside for him, as loaves often were for whoever ran the errands. Ivo picked it up out of pure habit, twenty-two years of *carry this, fetch that*, and then stood holding it like a thief caught mid-prayer.

He could put it back. He should put it back.

He put it inside his jacket, next to the map, where it formed an absurd warm bulge over his heart — bread and sorcery, side by side — and walked on.

At the orchard wall he stopped once and looked back. From here he could see the kitchen roof, and the chimney of the servants' hall already smoking for supper, and above it the grey shoulder of the palace hill, and beyond everything the standing stones, pale as pulled teeth against the afternoon sky. Somewhere in that smoke-smell, Marta was shaping tomorrow's dough. Tam was probably asleep in the woodshed with his hat over his face. Captain Ferro was counting his watchmen and finding, in a few hours' time, one taster missing and one mystery solved in the worst possible way.

They would grieve him. That was the cruelty of it — not anger, grief. Marta would grieve the way she did everything, thoroughly and with butter. And they would never know he hadn't chosen to leave them. They would only know he was gone, and that a stranger had died at the ford, and the two facts would sit together in their minds forever, married, inseparable.

*I am sorry,* he told the kitchen roof, silently. *I am sorry. I'll come back. Whatever this is, I'll put it down and come back.*

The map, against his chest, pulsed once — warm, almost comforting — and whispered: *Candlemakers' Walk. You'll like it. There's honey.*

"Don't make conversation with me," Ivo muttered, and climbed the wall.

* * *

The pastures were empty in the way that only places know they're being left can be empty. The sheep watched him with flat yellow indifference. The wind came down off the hills carrying the smell of the river and, faintly, impossibly, something else — something green and strange and vast that didn't grow anywhere in the valley, some plant from beyond the stones reaching across the distance like a hand testing a door.

He reached the standing stones at the hour when the light goes long and everything looks like a memory of itself. Up close they were bigger than he'd imagined — taller than three men, furred with moss on the north faces, carved with spirals so worn they might have been fingernail marks or might have been writing. The gap between the fourth and fifth stone framed the outside world: hills he had no names for, a road he had never walked, forests going blue with distance.

Twenty-two years. Never once past the stones.

His feet stopped there without consulting him, planted in the trampled grass like the feet of a man at the edge of a cold sea. Because here was the truth he'd been walking from all afternoon: it wasn't the guards alone. Even if they never found the ford, even if Marta's stare had bought him a month of grace — he could not stay. Not with the map whispering under his pillow. Not with a dead woman the mist refused, lying in his valley like an accusation. The veil that kept magic out apparently also kept Ivo in, now, bound to whatever the woman had died delivering. The valley's safety had a price, and the price had just come due, and it was due in the coin of everything he loved.

A sound behind him. Boots on grass, more than one pair, moving at the pace men use when hurrying would admit fear.

Ivo didn't look back. Looking back costs time, and time was the cracked jug again, running.

He stepped through the stones.

Nothing shattered. No bell rang. But the world *changed* the way a room changes when a window is opened — pressure shifting, air moving in from somewhere larger — and colors that had been merely colors sharpened to something almost audible, greens ringing like struck glass, the sky so blue it hurt a tooth. And pouring in with the light came memories, whole and vivid and utterly foreign: a woman laughing in a garden he'd never seen; the smell of a city after rain; a tower room and a hand writing by candlelight—

He staggered. The loaf fell. He caught it on the second bounce, clutched it like a shield, and ran — downhill, blind, tears coming from nowhere and everywhere, grief and wonder tangled past separating — away from the stones, away from the smoke of home, toward the crossroads shrine he could just see in the valley of the world beyond: a little roofed post, candles guttering, a god he didn't know.

Behind him, faintly, the valley mist rose off the fields and followed the boundary stones as far as it could go.

It did not cross.

Ahead, at the shrine, someone was watching him come — a figure in travel-stained robes, sitting perfectly still beside the candle-post, as if she had been waiting there for quite some time, and had known, the whole while, exactly who would come stumbling out of the stones with a stolen loaf and a burning map and nothing else in the world.

The map whispered, satisfied, one final street name — and Ivo, sobbing, running, hungry, free, ran straight toward her.
