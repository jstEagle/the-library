## 4. Past the Standing Stones

The map led me north.

I hadn't decided to follow it. That was the thing I would turn over in my mind for years after — that I never made the choice at all. I woke on the third morning in the hollow of an alder's roots, the loaf long eaten, my hands filthy with garden soil, and the map was already open in my lap, its creases warm as a sleeping bird. The whispering had changed overnight. Before, it had murmured street names — *Copperlace Row, the Weepers' Stair, Corvane* — like a lover reciting places it wanted to take me. Now it said only one word, over and over, patient as dripping water:

*North. North. North.*

And north was out.

I told myself I was going to look. Just to look. Just to stand at the boundary stones and see what lay beyond the veil, the way a fish might press its nose against the glass of its bowl. I even left a marker for Tam and Marta — a knot in the laundry line, three turns, the signal we'd used since I was small enough to hide under the wash basins. *Gone ahead, don't fret.* It wasn't true, but it was the closest shape the truth could wear without breaking something.

The valley gave me one last ordinary hour, and I think it knew. Mist hung low over the kitchen gardens, beading the bean rows silver. The ovens were already breathing warmth through the scullery vents, and somewhere Cook was singing the off-key hymn she sang every morning, the one about the river keeping its promises. My feet knew every stone of the path by heart. Twenty-two years of footsteps had worn a groove in this valley shaped exactly like me, and walking it toward the edge felt like walking up my own throat.

The standing stones came into view through the mist the way they always did — gray shoulders rising from the hillside, older than the palace, older than the king's grandfather's grandfather. Nine of them in a broken ring where the valley narrowed to a throat of green. As a child I'd been taught they were just rocks. As a taster I'd learned better: nothing crossed them that carried magic. Merchants were searched twice. Pilgrims turned back weeping. Once, when I was nine, a traveling physician had been dragged from his cart screaming that he'd left his art on the wrong side of the hills, that his hands had gone stupid, that he was nothing now. They'd sent him away. I'd thought him mad.

I stopped ten paces from the nearest stone.

Up close it was wrong in a way I had no words for yet. The granite was worn smooth as river rock on the inward face and rough, almost furry with lichen, on the outward face — as if time itself moved differently on either side. And there was a sound, very faint, like the hum a wet finger draws from a wineglass rim. I had lived twenty-two years within earshot of that sound and had never once heard it.

*North,* said the map, in my pocket, warm as a fevered brow.

"Just looking," I said aloud. My voice sounded thin. "Just to see."

I stepped between the stones.

* * *

I have tried, since, to describe what crossing the boundary felt like, and every description is a lie because language was built inside the veil too. But the closest I can come is this: imagine you have been underwater your entire life and have never known it, because water was all there was — and then you surface.

Pressure first. It came down on me like a hand pressed flat against both ears, deep and vast, and then it *popped*, the way ears pop when a road climbs out of a valley, and the world came rushing in.

Color hit me before anything else. The mist didn't just part; it *blazed*. The grass beyond the stones was so violently green I flinched. A jay screamed somewhere above me and the blue of it was a wound in the air. Every leaf had veins I could count. I could smell rain that hadn't fallen yet, smell the iron in the standing stones themselves, smell — gods help me — *yesterday*, a whole day's worth of woodsmoke and horses clinging to the ground like frost.

And then the memories came.

They weren't mine. I want to be clear about that, because for a few terrible minutes I believed they were. A woman's hands braiding a child's hair — but not my mother's hands, I'd know those anywhere, these were broader, ink-stained. A man dying slowly in a bed of straw, counting his own breaths like coins. A wedding in a field of barley, everyone barefoot, someone laughing so hard she fell into the grain. A boy drowning. A boy drowning. A boy drowning — that one came three times, from three different minds, three different rivers, and each time I felt the water close over my own throat.

I was on my knees. I don't remember falling. The map was out of my pocket and pinned under my palm as if it had crawled there, and it was *singing* now, no longer whispering, a high thin note like a kettle about to scream, and the world kept pouring into me — memories of strangers layered on memories of the dead layered on the raw animal roar of magic itself, which turned out not to be a force at all but a *presence*, everywhere, attentive, like being looked at by the sky.

"Stop," I said. "Please. Stop."

It didn't stop. Of course it didn't. You can't ask the sea to stop being wet. The veil had been the only kindness anyone had ever shown me, and I had walked out of it carrying a map drawn by whatever had needed me gone, and now the whole breathing weight of the unhidden world was crushing down on a man who had spent his life tasting other people's food because he couldn't trust even bread.

I thought of the valley. I thought of Marta's flour-white forearms, Tam's terrible whistling, Cook's hymn, the king's breakfast tray, the nightshade I had caught in the honeyed pears and spat into my napkin while the court laughed at some joke I'd never learn. Home. The word cracked down the middle.

The last thing I saw before the dark took me was the crossroads shrine fifty yards past the stones — a little roofed post with a candle guttering behind glass, offerings of bread and ribbon, a god's face worn blank by weather. Someone still tended it. That seemed important. Someone, in all this roaring wilderness, still lit candles.

Then the ground tilted up and struck me, and the memories drowned me all the way down.

* * *

I dreamed of the valley, but wrong. In the dream the mist was a lid, and beneath it the valley sat in a dish like a specimen, and around the dish stood figures in gray robes, watching, taking notes. One of them leaned close to the glass and I saw that its face was the face of the map — the same lines, the same folds — and it mouthed a word at me through the barrier. I couldn't hear it. In the dream, sound didn't cross either.

When I woke, a fire was burning.

That fact arrived before any other. Fire meant people. People meant danger or rescue, and in my experience the two wore the same boots. I kept my eyes slitted and took inventory the way I'd been trained — because a taster learns to assess rooms the way soldiers learn to assess battlefields. Low ceiling, timber, smoke-darkened rafters. Herbs hanging in bunches from every beam, some of them moving gently though there was no draft. Jars on shelves, labeled in a cramped hand. A cauldron muttering to itself over the coals. And a smell underneath the rosemary and smoke that I recognized from the poisoner's manuals I studied at night: something bitter, something mineral, something that made the back of my teeth ache in warning.

"You snore," said a voice, "like a landslide arguing with itself."

She sat on a stool near the fire, mending what looked like a fishing net, though the knots were wrong for netting — too deliberate, too patterned. Old, or old the way a gnarled apple tree is old: bent but unmistakably alive in a way young things aren't. Gray hair cut blunt at the jaw. Eyes the color of pond ice, and they were already on me when I opened my eyes fully, as if she'd been waiting for the exact moment.

"Where—" My voice was gravel.

"My floor," she said. "Two days. You're welcome." She nodded at a cup on the floor beside my head. "Drink that. It tastes like a donkey's regret. It will keep the shaking in your blood from shaking you apart."

I didn't touch it. Habit: never drink what you haven't tested. I dipped a fingertip, touched it to my tongue. Bitter, yes — valerian, willow bark, something I couldn't name — but no alkaloid burn, no almond ghost, no metallic lie at the finish. Clean. I drank.

"What's your name?" she asked, still working the knots.

"Ivo."

"Ivo *what*?"

"Just Ivo."

Her fingers paused on the cord. Only for a breath, but I'd spent my life reading faces across banquet tables, watching for the flicker that said *this dish is not what it claims to be*, and her pause was that flicker, writ small. She resumed tying.

"Ivo," she repeated, as if tasting it herself. Then, softer, half to the fire: "Well. So it's true, then. They did manage it."

"Managed what?" I pushed myself up onto my elbows. My body ached like a single bruise. "Where am I? What happened to me at the—"

"The stones?" She snorted. "You walked out of the Veiled Valley wearing a warden's map in your pocket and no ward-mark, no charm, no blessing, nothing between your skin and the whole howling weight of the world — and you ask what happened. Boy, most folk raised behind that veil die of their first sunrise. You slept two days and complained of nothing but your own snoring." She set down the net and looked at me properly, and I felt the look go through me like a skewer through meat. "What happened is that you surfaced. Everything has magic in it out here. The grass. The rain. Grief. Bread. Behind your veil it's all been wrung out like washing, and you were steeped in that absence for twenty-some years, and then you stepped out and the world poured back in and there was nowhere in you to put it."

"The memories." My mouth went dry. "The ones that weren't mine."

"Mm." Something moved behind her eyes — recognition again, and this time she didn't bother hiding it. "A hedge-witch's trick, that. Ground yourself in your own name until the flood finds its banks. Brannoc. Say it."

"Brannoc."

"There. First spell you ever cast, and you didn't even notice." She stood, joints crackling like the fire, and crossed to the shelf of jars. "Now. Let's talk about the map."

My hand went to my coat before I could stop it. She laughed — a dry, rusted sound.

"Relax. If I wanted it, I'd have had it while you drooled on my floorboards. I only want to know one thing, and I want you to answer honestly, because I'll know if you don't." She turned, holding a jar of honey-colored salve, and the firelight made her pond-ice eyes glitter. "Who gave it to you? And what color did they bleed?"

The question landed in my stomach like swallowed ice. I had told no one. There had been no one to tell. The woman at the ford, the silver welling from the wound in her side, the map folded into my hands with fingers already going cold, the words — *the crown remembers* — and then the mist closing over her body without taking it, leaving it lying on the bank like something the valley refused to digest. I had run. I had hidden. I had not told a soul.

"How do you know about her?" I whispered.

"Because I've seen that map before," Brannoc said. "Forty years ago, in hands just as bloody. And because the whole hedge-craft from here to the capital has felt the veil shudder twice in one week, like a door rattling in its frame, and doors rattle when someone means to open them." She crouched by my pallet, and up close I could see that the pattern in her net-mending wasn't decoration at all — it was writing, knotted into cords, names maybe, dozens of names, hundreds. "So. Answer the question, taster. Because I know what you are — or I know what you must be, and it should be impossible, and impossible things are exactly what that map was drawn to find."

"What am I?" I asked. It came out smaller than I intended.

Brannoc held my gaze for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the herbs, and every bunch stirred except one, which hung perfectly still as though listening.

"You're a man who walked out of a prison," she said finally, "that doesn't let anyone walk out. You're a man who bled red at the ford while something else bled silver. And you're the only creature in forty years to cross those stones without the veil trying to kill him — which means the veil knows you." She pressed the jar into my hands. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Eat. Sleep. At dawn you'll tell me everything, starting with why the architect of the Veiled Valley drew a map that leads to the Heartwell under the palace — and why he addressed it to you."

The word *Heartwell* went through me like cold water through cloth, and I understood, with a taster's certainty, that I had just been poisoned with knowledge instead of nightshade — and that unlike the king's food, this dose had been measured exactly right.

There would be no antidote. There would only be going forward.

*North,* whispered the map in my pocket, contentedly. *North.*
