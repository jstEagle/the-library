## 5. The Witch Who Kept Names

Brannoc smelled the boy before she saw him — that was the wrongness of it, the first wrongness, and her bones knew it before her head did.

She had been crouched at the crossroads shrine for the better part of an hour, renewing the chalk sigils on its weathered stone with ash and tallow, when the wind changed. It came down off the Veiled Valley's rim carrying river-water and woodsmoke and something underneath both: a silence. A hole in the world's hum. Every living thing sang a little, to those who knew how to listen — hedge-witches listened for their bread. Grass sang thin and green. Stone sang slow. Even the dead left echoes in the air like fingerprints on a cup.

This boy sang nothing at all.

She straightened, one hand going to the charm-bag at her hip out of old habit, and watched him come up the pilgrim road from the valley stones. He walked like someone who had never once been lost, which meant he had also never been far from home. Twenty years old, perhaps a little less. Brown hair stuck up where he'd slept on it wrong or been dragged through a hedge — she could see burrs in it from thirty paces. He carried a loaf of bread under his arm like a stolen thing, because it was one; she'd watched him pocket it through the kitchen garden's gap in the wall not two hours past, though he didn't know he'd been watched. In his other hand he held a folded square of paper the way you hold a snake: carefully, and hating that you have to.

The map. She knew it before he crested the rise. She would have known it blindfolded, by the way the air bent around it, by the faint smell of hot iron and old blood. There were three things in this world Brannoc feared, and she was looking at one of them, held by a barefoot boy with burrs in his hair.

*Don't get involved,* said the sensible voice in her head, the one that had kept her alive through two purges and one very bad marriage. *Walk into the trees. He'll pass by. Whatever comes for that map will come for whoever stands near it, and you have already stood too near too many doomed things.*

Brannoc stayed exactly where she was.

It wasn't kindness. She wanted that understood, if anyone ever asked, and no one ever would. Kindness was expensive and she'd stopped buying it years ago. But the map was walking toward her wearing a boy, and there are debts in this world that follow a woman across borders and burnings, and hers had followed her all the way from the capital to this forgotten crossroads where four roads met and none of them went anywhere good.

The boy saw the shrine and stopped. Then he saw her, and flinched — a small, honest flinch, the flinch of someone raised among people who liked him. That, too, told her everything.

"You're hurt," he said, because she was. Her leg had been weeping through the bandage since dawn, the price of a shortcut through bramble-thorn that grew thorns only where hedge-witches stepped. The valley's edge did not love her kind. "You're bleeding. Through your — is that a wound or a curse? I've read about cursed wounds."

"Have you." Brannoc leaned on her staff and studied him while he studied her. His eyes went to the blood, then to the charm-bag, then to her face, cataloguing without cruelty. A taster's habits, she thought, though she didn't know yet why she knew that word fit him. She would learn. "And what does your reading say about them?"

"That most healers refuse to touch them," he said. "In case the curse jumps."

"Sensible healers." She let him look at the wound a moment longer than was comfortable. "It's neither. Brambles here grow spiteful toward my sort. It'll close by moonset."

"Your sort being—"

"Witch." She said it flat, watching his face, and gave him credit: he didn't step back. His knuckles whitened around the loaf, but his feet stayed planted. "You've heard the word. Say what you've heard."

He considered her with that same careful attention, and then, unexpectedly, he sat down on the shrine's low step as though his legs had simply finished arguing with him. He was shaking. She'd seen it before in soldiers after battle and mothers after births — the tremor that comes when the body finally admits what the mind has been outrunning.

"I've heard witches eat children," he said. "I've heard they keep the hearts of kings in jars. I've heard—" he stopped, and laughed, a short unhappy sound. "I've heard a great many things about the world outside the valley, and until this morning I believed almost none of them. Now I believe the ones I don't like."

"And the map?" Brannoc nodded at his hand. "What do you believe about that?"

His fingers curled over it. For a heartbeat she thought he'd lie to her, and she found herself — absurdly, unpleasantly — hoping he wouldn't. Then he unfolded it halfway, wincing as if it burned, which it likely did, and turned it so she could see.

The map showed itself to her the way it showed itself to no one else. To the boy it was probably ink and guesswork, streets and rivers in a hand gone brown with age. To Brannoc it was a lit fuse burning backward through time. She saw the pilgrim road they stood on. She saw Corvane with its memory-markets marked in the cartographer's bitter little shorthand — *here they sell yesterday*. She saw the capital, and beneath the capital, drawn in lines so fine they might have been scratched with a needle rather than a pen, the undercroft, and beneath the undercroft—

Her mouth went dry.

"Heartwell Gate," she said aloud, before she could stop herself, and the boy's head snapped up.

"You can *read* it? The names keep changing when I look at them. One minute it says Ford Street, the next it says something I can't pronounce, and last night it whispered at me for an hour. It knows street names in cities I've never seen. How does it know them better than I do?"

Because the man who drew it walked every one of them, Brannoc did not say. Because Aldous Veyne drew this map with his own hand and sealed it with his own blood and hid it inside the one place in the world where magic could never find it, and now magic has found it anyway, which means either Veyne's wards have failed after ninety years or something on the other side has gotten hungry enough to reach through.

"It's an old map," she said instead, "and old maps remember their roads. Sit still."

She crouched beside him, keeping her weight off the bad leg, and held out her hand. He hesitated — a long, honest hesitation — and then gave her the map, and the moment it touched her palm she felt it *recognize* her, the way a dog recognizes the hand that beat it, and she nearly threw it into the shrine's offering fire just to be done with the whole business.

She didn't. Ninety years of cowardice ended and began somewhere in that half-second, though she couldn't have said so at the time. She looked at the fine-needle lines beneath the palace, and at the chamber drawn at the center of them, and at the single word written across it in Veyne's cramped hand: *the rite requires.*

Requires what, the map did not say. Maps never wrote the important part down. That was the whole art of maps — to promise a destination while leaving the price blank.

But Brannoc had read the rest of Veyne's work, the parts that survived the burning. She had spent eleven years and one ruined name digging those fragments out of archive-cinders and private collections, and she knew what the unbinding rite required. Everyone who had ever studied the Vessel knew, in the vague way people know the shapes of things they pray they'll never see clearly.

A life lived wholly outside magic. A vessel of the opposite kind. Someone the hunger could not taste, could not tempt, could not touch — a clean blade for a filthy wound.

She looked up at the boy sitting on the shrine step with burrs in his hair and a stolen loaf under his arm, born behind the veil, untouched by anything but nightshade and kindness, and she felt the debt settle onto her shoulders like a yoke settling onto an ox. Warm. Well-fitted. Permanent.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Ivo. Ivo Brightwater." He said it simply, the way people say names that have never cost them anything.

*Ivo Brightwater.* She rolled it around inside herself and found it fit the fragments perfectly — the wardens' accounts, the ledger of the valley's founding, the line in Veyne's marginalia that scholars had dismissed as madman's scrawl: *the last ward shall be born, not made, and shall walk out of the silence wearing nothing but a name.* Eleven years she'd hunted that sentence's meaning. It had been walking up a road toward her with bread in its fist.

"Well, Ivo Brightwater," she said, and heard her own voice take on the smoothness she used at market stalls and border crossings, the voice that left things out, "you're in trouble, and I know these roads, and I'm going in the direction you need to go regardless. I'll guide you as far as the capital. After that we'll see."

"Why would you do that?" No suspicion in it — genuine bafflement, the bafflement of a man whose whole life had run on the assumption that help arrived freely. It made her chest ache in a place she'd thought scarred over.

"Because I owe a debt," she said, which was true, "and debts to the crown collect interest," which was truer than he could know, "and because whatever killed that woman at the ford will be along this road soon enough, and I'd rather be walking away from it beside you than standing still ahead of it."

He went pale at the mention of the ford. Good. Fear kept travelers honest.

"The mist wouldn't take her," he said quietly. "The valley takes everything eventually. Fallen branches, drowned lambs, my father — the mist folds over them and by morning there's nothing. But her body just lay there. Silver blood. And the water around her was warm, Brannoc, like something enormous had just passed through it and left heat behind." He wrapped his arms around himself. "What bleeds silver?"

"A dead mage," Brannoc said, "if you're lucky, and something worse than a mage if you're not. Get up. We've light enough to make the tree-line before dark, and I don't camp in the open anymore. Bad habit of mine, surviving."

* * *

They walked, and she listened, and everything he said confirmed what the map had already told her.

He talked the way starved people eat — quickly, messily, unable to stop. About the valley where nothing magical survived longer than a candle-spark. About the king's kitchens, where he'd been raised by cooks and stable-hands and a laundress named Maren who taught him letters on flour-dusted tables. About the tasting — how he'd begun at seven years old with weak broths dosed with a drop of bitterness, building immunity dish by dish, year by year, until he could stand before the king's plate each morning and swear it clean. About the stranger at the ford, the silver blood, the words pressed into his ear with her dying breath: *the crown remembers.*

"She said that to you specifically," Brannoc said. Not a question.

"She said it like she knew me. Like she'd been looking for me." He kicked a stone off the path with more force than it deserved. "I've never left the valley. Nobody leaves. The guards won't even open the boundary gate past dusk. So how does a dying stranger in the middle of nowhere press her map into *my* hands? Out of everyone in the world?"

Because you weren't in the middle of nowhere, child, Brannoc thought. You were the center of it, and she was spending her last breath delivering a package to its rightful address.

"She chose you," is what she said. "Dying women choose well. They haven't the energy to choose badly."

He glanced at her sideways. "You're not telling me everything."

"No," Brannoc agreed pleasantly. "Neither are you. You haven't mentioned what happened to the body, for instance, and you've been circling it since the shrine."

That stopped him mid-stride. The road climbed gently here through gorse and grey rock, and the valley's rim rose behind them like the lip of a bowl, mist pooling white along its edge. Ahead, the world opened out into country he had never seen — hills the color of old bronze, a sky enormous and indifferent, birds wheeling in patterns that meant nothing and therefore everything.

"The mist finally took her," he said at last. "At noon. Just — folded over, like it does. But it took three days' worth of trying, and the whole time..." He swallowed. "The whole time, the map burned against my chest. Like it was impatient. Like it wanted me to leave her and start walking. Is that — am I mad, Brannoc? Tell me honestly. My grandmother always said honesty is the one dish you can't fake."

"Your grandmother sounds wise." Brannoc limped a few steps in silence, choosing. This was the hinge, she knew. Everything after it depended on what she said now and what she withheld forever. "No, you're not mad. The map wants what it wants. It was drawn by a man who bound his intention into it with blood, and intentions outlive their makers. It wants to reach the Heartwell. It has waited ninety years inside a prison of silence for someone to carry it out, and now you've carried it out, and it is — eager."

"And the Heartwell is?"

She looked at him. Young, frightened, brave in the specific stubborn way of people who have never had to be brave before and don't know any better. Exactly what the rite required. A life lived wholly outside magic — and the rite did not say *lived*, she remembered suddenly, coldly, with the clarity of a knife coming out of a drawer. Veyne's fragments said *given*. A life wholly outside magic, given to the well.

There might be another way. There had to be another way. She had eleven years sunk into finding another way, and if there wasn't —

Then the debt collected everything.

"A place under the palace," she said. "Where something old is kept, and something beloved is kept prisoner beside it. If the woman at the ford sent you, then she believed you could free the prisoner. Whether you survive the freeing—" she watched him, weighing, "—is a question I intend to answer before you ask it."

"So you do know more than you're telling me."

"Boy, I have kept names for forty years — mine and others'. Names are how the crown finds people. Names are how it erased me." She tapped her own chest. "There is no record anywhere in this kingdom that Brannoc of the Ash Row ever existed. I made sure of it. What's left of knowing me lives in this body and nowhere else, and I spend it sparingly. So yes. I'll tell you what keeps you alive and hide what gets us both burned. That's the shape of any alliance worth having. Take it or sit back down at your shrine and wait for whatever bled that woman silver to come asking after its property."

He took it. Of course he took it. He had a found-family's faith in strangers and a taster's discipline about swallowing what was set before him, and the combination would break her heart eventually, she suspected, in some way she couldn't yet predict.

But as dusk came down and they made cold camp in a hollow beneath a lightning-split oak, Brannoc lay awake listening to the boy breathe, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the shrine.

The rite required him. The crown wanted the map. Something under the palace was growing hungry enough to bleed its agents silver at a river-ford ninety miles away. And she had just tied herself to all three of them with a promise she'd made while omitting the single fact that mattered: that in Veyne's arithmetic, the boy wasn't the hero of this story.

He was the payment.

She could still turn back tomorrow. Send him home with a blessing and a false map. Watch from a distance as the Vessel ate the kingdom bite by bite, memory by memory, starting with the court and ending — she had seen the ledgers, she knew the trajectory — ending with the valley itself, because the veil was not eternal and hunger is patient and the silence behind those standing stones was the only meal the thing in the crown had never tasted.

Or she could walk him to the Heartwell and hope — gamble eleven years of searching and one ruined name and whatever scraps of soul she had left — that between here and that black water she would find the other way she'd promised Sera she'd find, back when Sera was still a name and not a ghost in a bottle.

Beside her, Ivo murmured in his sleep. Street names, she realized, leaning closer. The map was whispering street names to him in his dreams, teaching him a city he'd never seen, and he was learning it, and somewhere far to the south a thing wearing a king's face turned its cold eyes northward and felt, for the first time in ninety years, its prison door begin to rattle.

Brannoc closed her own eyes and did not sleep again.

In the morning they would reach Corvane, the city that sold yesterday, and the boy would see what his kingdom paid to forget — and she would watch his face as he learned it, because his face was the only honest ledger she had left.
