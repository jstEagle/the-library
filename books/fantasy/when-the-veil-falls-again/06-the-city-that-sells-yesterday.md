## 6. The City That Sells Yesterday

Corvane announced itself by smell before it showed itself at all — woodsmoke and wet stone, frying onions, horse, and beneath all of it something sweeter and stranger, like honey left too long in the sun. Ivo stood at the crest of the ridge with the wind pulling at his coat and looked down at a city built in rings, tier after tier descending into a bowl of hills, each ring lit by lanterns of a different color. At the very center, where a market square should have been, glowed a soft violet light that did not flicker. It pulsed. Slowly. Like breathing.

"What's the light?" he asked.

Brannoc didn't turn from tightening the straps on their one mule. "The Exchange," she said. "The heart of the trade. You'll see soon enough." She had said little in three days of walking, answering most of his questions with questions of her own, or with silences that felt like doors quietly closed. But when he'd slept, he'd caught her watching him — not with suspicion exactly. With arithmetic.

"Is it true?" he pressed. "That they sell memories here? Actual remembering? You can put it in a bottle?"

"You've seen the map do what it does." Brannoc swung up onto the mule's back with a grunt. "You've felt it burn through your shirt like a coal. And you still ask whether remembering can be handled like flour or fish." She clicked her tongue and the mule started down the switchback road. "Come along, taster. Keep your hands in your pockets and your mouth shut unless someone's buying."

"I'm not selling anything."

"No," she said, and there was that arithmetic again, plain in her voice. "You're not."

* * *

They entered through the Salt Gate on the city's western rim, and the first thing Ivo learned about Corvane was that everyone in it was carrying something invisible.

He saw it in the way people moved. A woman sweeping her doorstep paused mid-sweep to stare at nothing, her face going slack as an empty room, then resumed as though no time had passed. Two men argued cheerfully over a card game in a doorway; one laughed, and the laugh stopped halfway out of him, cut clean, his expression folding into confusion before smoothing over again. Nobody else seemed to notice. Nobody but Ivo, who had spent his life watching faces for the small tells of poison — the tremor at the eye's corner, the swallow a half-beat late. He knew what people looked like when something foreign moved through them.

These people had gaps in them. Holes worn smooth, like stones in a riverbed.

"Brannoc," he murmured, catching up beside the mule. "The sweeper woman. What happened to her?"

"Sold something. Grief, maybe. Or the memory of a husband." Brannoc's eyes stayed forward. "Or bought forgetting. Same coin, different direction."

"That's not possible. You can't just take a thing out of a person. Memory isn't — it's not soup. You can't ladle it into jars."

Brannoc finally looked at him, and for once there was no calculation in it, only something tired and old. "You grew up behind stones that keep magic out, boy. Everything is possible out here. That's rather the problem."

The streets narrowed as they descended toward the center. The violet glow strengthened, washing every face a faint bruised color. Shopfronts here were not shopfronts at all but long counters open to the street, and behind the counters, ranked in rows like apothecary drawers, stood the bottles.

Ivo had expected grandeur. What he found was inventory.

Thousands upon thousands of small glass vessels, some tall and thin as reeds, some round-bellied as plums, each stoppered with wax of a particular color — white, grey, amber, black. They caught the violet light and held it, and inside some of them things *moved*: a swirl of gold that might have been sunlight on water; a slow red bloom like a rose opening underwater; in one squat black bottle, nothing at all, just a darkness so complete it seemed to drink the lamplight around it.

He stopped without meaning to. His hand came off the mule's harness and hung at his side, forgotten.

A memory of summer, in a bottle. He could feel it from three feet away — not see it, not know whose it was, but *feel* it, the way you feel warmth off a hearthstone. Grass. A lake. Someone calling a name he couldn't hear. The bottle hummed against his awareness like a struck tuning fork, and the longing that rose up in him was immediate and shameful: he wanted to uncork it. He wanted to climb *inside* it.

"Don't touch," said the shopkeeper, an old man with spectacles thick as bottle-bottoms and ink stains to both elbows. He said it mildly, the way a baker says *don't touch the bread*. "Some of them bite. Especially the good ones." He squinted at Ivo, then at Brannoc, then back at Ivo, and his mildness sharpened by a degree. "Fresh-faced. Valley-born?"

Ivo said nothing. Brannoc said nothing. The old man shrugged and turned back to labeling a drawer with a brush finer than an eyelash.

They walked on. Ivo's skin prickled. Every counter they passed, every window, every stall, the bottles called to him in tiny voices — not words, nothing so kind as words. Just *want*. A hundred small hungers humming at the edge of hearing, and underneath them, threading through the whole descending city, the great slow pulse of the violet light at the center, keeping time like a heart.

*What are you?* he thought at it. *What are all of you?*

And the light, absurdly, seemed to consider the question.

* * *

Brannoc found them lodging above a chandler's shop two rings out from the center — a narrow room with one bed, which she took, and a pallet, which Ivo got, and a window overlooking a lane where laundry lines crossed like bunting. She went out at dusk with instructions he was fairly sure amounted to *don't*, and Ivo, being twenty years old and valley-raised and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone, waited exactly as long as it took her footsteps to fade before slipping out after her.

The evening market was worse than the day market. Or better. He couldn't decide.

By night the counters burned with lamps, and the sellers came out — not the brokers with their neat drawers, but ordinary people standing in queues outside certain marked doorways, clutching tickets, holding each other's hands. Ivo drifted along the edge of a queue outside a house with a blue door, close enough to hear, far enough to be furniture.

The woman at the front of the queue was young. Younger than the grief she carried, which sat on her like a coat made of lead. She held a child's shoe — one shoe, worn at the toe — and she turned it over and over in her hands while the broker's clerk read her terms aloud in a bored, gentle voice.

"—full extraction, grief-mass only, associated imagery remains but loses its charge. You'll remember the fever. You'll remember the burying. You won't *feel* it. Most clients describe it as reading a letter about someone else's loss." The clerk glanced up. "Payment as agreed: forty crowns and one draught of calm, standard grade. Do you consent?"

The woman nodded. Then she stopped nodding and said, in a voice scraped nearly raw, "Will I still love her?"

Ivo's breath stopped.

The clerk had answered this question before. You could hear it in the pause — not hesitation, but *procedure*. "Love is not grief, madam. Love stays. What leaves is the weight. Many clients tell us—" the clerk softened, actually softened, "—they tell us it's the kindest money they ever earned."

The woman looked down at the shoe. Turned it over. Turned it over.

"Yes," she whispered. "Yes. Take it."

They led her through the blue door. The queue shuffled forward one place, and Ivo stood frozen at the lane's edge, his stomach a cold stone, because he had watched a mother sell her daughter's death and call it kindness, and the worst part — the part that would follow him into sleep for years — was that she was right. It *was* kindness. That was the horror of it. Not cruelty. Kindness, priced and ticketed and paid out in crowns, until a city full of people walked around with holes worn smooth where their worst days used to be.

He thought of Marta in the kitchens, who had lost two sons to the border wars and wept every year on the same autumn day, and how Cook would set aside her work and sit with her and let her weep, and how Marta always said afterward, red-eyed, "There. It's out. I'll keep it another year." *I'll keep it.* In the Veiled Valley, grief was kept like treasure, because it was all you had of the ones who were gone. Here, grief was stock. Here, you could spend your dead.

He was still standing there when a commotion started further down the lane — cheering, of all things. Actual celebration, raucous and drunk, spilling out of a tavern front. He edged closer, and saw why.

They were toasting a soldier. A big man, scarred to the jaw, being passed from shoulder to shoulder by comrades, and on the tavern's counter behind him, in pride of place among the drinks, sat a small amber bottle, already empty, its wax seal cracked open like an eggshell.

"Courage!" someone bellowed. "He bought it fresh! Full measure!"

"To Tam!" the crowd roared. "To Tam who couldn't—"

"Who *couldn't sleep* for the shaking!" the big man finished, laughing, tears standing bright on his scarred face. "Three years, boys! Three years my hands wouldn't hold steady on a sword hilt, and tomorrow — tomorrow we ride, and I'll *sleep*." He raised his cup. "To the Exchange!"

"To the Exchange!"

They drank. Ivo backed away into the shadow of an awning, and his thoughts churned like silt in a flooded river. A mother sells her grief so she can stand upright. A soldier buys his courage so he can ride to war. Both of them smiling. Both of them *healed*. And somewhere between the selling and the buying, between the holes and what filled them, a whole economy of human souls flowing through this city like blood through a body — and Ivo understood, standing in the bruised violet dark, that he was looking at the kingdom's true wealth. Not gold. Not grain. *Remembering.* The kingdom paid for everything with pieces of itself, and called the spending freedom.

*What happens,* he wondered, *when it runs out?*

Somewhere in his chest, unbidden, the map stirred against his ribs where it lay hidden beneath his shirt. It warmed — barely, barely — like a dog lifting its head at a familiar voice. And though it made no sound, Ivo felt the whisper arrive anyway, the way he'd felt it every night since the ford, threading through his dreams in street-names he'd never walked:

*Alder Street. The Chime Steps. Under the fountain of the seven weepers.*

It was naming the way. It was always naming the way.

* * *

Brannoc found him at the chandler's window past midnight, or perhaps she had never left at all. He heard the bed ropes creak and turned, guiltily, and she was sitting up in the dark with her grey hair loose around her shoulders, looking at him with those eyes that never stopped counting.

"You followed me."

"You left me in a strange city with instructions that were mostly *don't*."

"Mm." She scratched under her jaw. "And what did you learn, disobeying?"

Ivo sat down on the pallet. His legs ached from walking; his head ached worse from thinking. "That they're right about it," he said finally. "The healing. That's the terrible part. The mother — she sold her daughter's dying, Brannoc. Her actual grief, bottled and gone, and she'll wake tomorrow lighter, and she'll be able to eat breakfast, and everyone will call it mercy." His voice roughened despite himself. "But she asked one question before she went in. She asked if she'd still love her. And the clerk said yes, love stays, only the weight goes." He stared at his hands. "How do they know where the weight ends and the love begins? How does *anyone* know? When you pour a person out into a jar, how do you know what you've kept?"

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. The violet glow from the city's heart seeped faintly through the shutters and lay across the floorboards like spilled wine.

"The Vessel knows," she said at last, so softly he almost missed it.

"The what?"

Her head came up sharply, and he saw — genuinely saw, for the first time — fear move across her face. Real fear, quick and involuntary, the flinch of a woman who has said a word she swore not to say. "Nothing," she said. "An old story. Sleep, boy. Tomorrow we go to the Exchange itself, and you'll want your wits about you."

"Why?" Ivo asked. "What are we doing there?"

Brannoc lay back down. For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then, into the dark:

"We're looking for someone who remembers too much. There's a rumor of a boy in the underlevels who's never sold a single thing — a vault, they call him. Living storage for whatever the brokers don't trust to glass." Her voice flattened. "And if the rumors are half true, he may hold the one memory in this entire city that matters."

"What memory?"

Silence. Then, quieter, almost to herself, almost like a prayer or an apology:

"Yours."

Outside, far below, the violet heart of Corvane pulsed on in the dark — slow, patient, breathing — and Ivo lay awake beneath it, listening to the city dream in other people's stolen voices, feeling the map warm against his chest like a second heartbeat, keeping time.
