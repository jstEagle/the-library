## 7. Sera of the Broken Company

The knife work was the easy part. It always had been, even after.

Sera crouched on the loading dock behind Vellum & Ash, the memory-broker's shop on Corrow Lane, and watched the three of them come down the alley the way she'd watched a hundred supply columns come down a hundred mountain passes. Old instincts didn't need memories to run. That was the ugly joke of it — she'd sold the war to survive the war, and what remained was the war's shape: the counting of exits, the reading of shoulders, the arithmetic of who dies first if this goes wrong.

Three targets. A witch in road-leathers with a staff that wasn't quite a walking stick — Brannoc, the hedge-witch whose name had been whispered through three markets like a bad debt. A boy, twelve maybe, walking too close to the witch and looking at everything like it might bite him. Pip. The vault-child everyone in the underlevels was suddenly pretending they'd never seen.

And between them, a young man with flour still ghosting his forearms and a loaf of bread tucked under one arm like a shield. He moved wrong for the city — no slouch, no sidestep, no practiced indifference. He walked like someone who had never once needed to check over his shoulder.

That was what made him interesting. That was what made him worth the contract price, which had doubled twice since sundown.

*The taster,* her employer had said, sliding the coin across the table with fingers stained bottle-blue from handling memories. *The valley-born. Bring him breathing, or don't bother bringing him.*

She checked her exits out of habit. Two: the alley mouth ahead, the drainage grate behind her that she'd already loosened. She rolled her shoulder where the old scar pulled — a scar with no story attached anymore, just a seam of white flesh she couldn't account for. Most days she didn't try. Trying led to standing in doorways at odd hours feeling like a house with rooms bricked shut.

The trio reached the midpoint of the alley. The boy was chattering about something — Sera caught the word *"bottles"* and *"the green ones taste like rain"* — and the taster laughed at whatever he said, an unguarded sound that bounced off the brickwork. The witch kept scanning rooftops, which meant the witch was smart, which meant Sera should have taken them on the open street where crowds made cover.

Too late now. The alley narrowed here, funneled by stacked crates and a collapsed awning, and Sera had chosen it weeks ago for exactly this geometry.

She stepped off the dock.

The drop was eight feet; she landed soft, blade drawn before her boots finished settling, and put herself between them and the far mouth of the alley. "Evening," she said. "Walk with me, valley-boy. The rest of you can keep your legs if you're quick about being slow."

Everything happened the way she'd known it would, right up until the part where it didn't.

The witch turned first, staff coming around, words already gathering in her throat like spit before a curse. The boy — Pip — did the worst possible thing, which was to grab the taster's sleeve and pull him backward, putting himself in reach. And the taster—

The taster looked at her.

Not at the blade. At her. With those flat, unremarkable eyes that nonetheless went through her like a fish-gutter through soft flesh, cataloguing, weighing, and finding — gods help her — *concern*. As if she were the one standing in an alley at night with strangers' blood-money in her boot.

"Your left hand," he said quietly. "You're favoring it. Old break, badly set?"

"It healed," Sera said, and hated how much the answer cost her.

"No," he said. "It stopped hurting. Those aren't the same thing."

The witch snarled something — *Ivo, don't you dare talk to the knife-girl* — and Sera made her decision the way she always did, fast and clean: take the witch's legs, use the boy as leverage, keep the taster moving toward the docks. Three moves. She'd done harder with worse odds against better fighters.

Move one: low lunge at Brannoc's knee.

Move two never came.

Because Ivo — fool, baker's-handed, bread-carrying fool — stepped *into* her lunge, into the space between her blade and the witch, and caught her wrist.

Just caught it. Bare hand on bare wrist, the grip of a man who kneaded dough for a living, and Sera felt it go through her like a struck bell.

*

It came as sound first.

Laughter, high and bright and slightly off-key, the kind of laugh that snorted in the middle because the laugher had never learned to be embarrassed about it. Then color: a kitchen with a crooked window, steam rising off a pot, and a girl — a *young woman*, dark-haired, gap-toothed, stirring with a wooden spoon held like a scepter — turning to say something Sera couldn't hear over her own laughing, because she was laughing too, she could feel her own ribs shaking with it, feel the ache in her cheeks, and the girl said something and pointed the spoon at her and—

*"Sera. Sera, you great useless hero, sit down before you fall down."*

A name. Her name, in a voice that loved it. In a voice that—

The kitchen tilted. The spoon-girl's face began to blur at the edges like wet ink, and Sera clawed at it, at the memory, with everything she had, and got one more second: the girl's hand, warm, gripping hers, and the words *come home safe, you always come home—*

Gone.

Sera was on her knees in the alley. She didn't remember falling. Her knife was somewhere to her left and her whole body was shaking and there were tears on her face that she had absolutely no memory of producing, and the taster — Ivo — was kneeling in front of her with his hand hovering an inch from her arm, not touching, asking permission with his eyes.

"What," she managed, "was that."

"I don't know," he said. Honest as sunrise. "It happens sometimes. Since I crossed the stones. Things come back to people when I touch them." His brow furrowed. "Was it yours? Did it belong to you?"

"It was my sister." The words fell out of her like coins from a cut purse. She heard herself say it and wanted to swallow them back and couldn't. "Her name is—" *Was.* The tense snagged in her throat. "Her name is Wren. She has a gap in her teeth and she laughs like a dying goose and I sold—" Her voice cracked clean through. "I sold her. Not her. The *remembering* of her. To pay for passage out of the war. And I told myself it was fine, it was just grief, grief was the expensive thing anyway, and then I couldn't remember why I never eat goose."

Pip made a small wounded noise. Brannoc's staff lowered slowly, and the witch's face did something complicated — recognition, Sera thought later. Recognition of a debt of her own.

"They bottle it," Sera said, staring at the filthy cobblestones. "At the market. They pour it out of you and cork it and sell it to strangers, and the stranger gets to feel my sister's laugh for an evening and I get to wake up every morning knowing there's a hole in me shaped exactly like her and not even knowing her *name* until ten seconds ago." She looked up at Ivo, and her voice went flat and cold and steady, the mercenary's voice, the one that negotiated. "How much of that do you have? When you touch people. Is there more? Could there be more?"

"I don't know," Ivo said again. "Maybe. If the memories still exist. If they haven't been—" He hesitated on the word.

"*Drunk*," Sera supplied. "Say it. Someone drank my sister's laughter out of a little glass bottle and probably paid four silver for the privilege."

Nobody said anything. Down the alley, the city went on being Corvane: a drunk singing two streets over, the clink of bottles being unloaded somewhere, lamplighters working their rounds. A city built on yesterday, selling it by the dram.

Sera picked up her knife. All three of them tensed — fair enough — but she only sheathed it, slowly, and stood, wiping her face with the heel of her hand in one rough businesslike pass.

"Here's how it is," she said. "I took a contract on you, valley-boy. Double rate, bring you breathing. My employer has a blue-fingered broker named Hask who pays on time, and Hask answers to people above him, and those people want you very specifically and very badly." She watched that land. Watched Brannoc's knuckles whiten on the staff. Good — let the witch know the bill was coming due. "So either I finish the job, or I'm out of work, marked, and hunted by the same people hunting you. Those are my options."

"And?" Brannoc said sharply. "Which do you choose, sword-arm?"

Sera looked at Ivo instead. At the man who'd caught her wrist instead of running from her blade, and handed her back her sister's laugh like it was nothing, like it was a borrowed coat.

"You touched me," she said, "and gave me ten seconds of Wren. Ten years I've been walking around a hole, and you handed me ten seconds of the shape of it." She jerked her chin toward the alley mouth, toward the memory-market glowing amber in the middle distance, all its lanterns lit for the evening trade. "Somewhere in this city is a bottle with the rest of her in it. Her laugh. Her voice. Whatever they poured out of me the day I signed. I want it back. Every drop."

"And you think *he's* the way to get it," Brannoc said.

"I think wherever he goes, the brokers go crazy. I think the price on his head doubled in six hours, which means he's worth more than any bottle in the market, which means he's leverage, or bait, or both." She shrugged. "Mostly I think I've got no better lead, and I've survived twenty years on worse plans than following a miracle and a witch."

"That's not trust," Brannoc said.

"No," Sera agreed. "Trust comes later. Right now it's shared damage and mutual usefulness. Best foundation there is. Marriages have been built on less."

Something almost like a smile twitched at the corner of the witch's mouth and died there, suspicious to the last.

Pip tugged Ivo's sleeve and whispered — though in the narrow alley whispering was theater; she heard every word. "Is she going to stay? She smells like iron and river water and somebody else's smoke. But also she cried, so."

"I cried *once*," Sera said, "and it was tactical."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. That cataloguing gaze again, but softer now, and she understood suddenly why the crown wanted this man so badly, why a price doubled itself around him like weather. There was something in him that returned things. Something that made holes into shapes again.

"Come with us," he said simply. "If anyone hurt you by taking her, then we'll find them. And if you're hungry, I have bread."

"He has bread," Pip confirmed solemnly, "and he makes more, apparently, it's a whole thing."

Sera laughed — short, rusty, surprised out of her — and it sounded strange in her own ears, like a language she'd studied but never spoken.

"All right," she said. "But ground rules. One: nobody touches me without asking. Including you, especially you, miracle-boy — what you gave me tonight I'll be chewing on for a month. Two: I walk rear guard, always, and if I say *run*, you run, and we argue about it alive. Three—" she hesitated, and the hesitation cost her more than the crying had. "Three. If we find the bottle. If we find her. Nobody opens it but me. Even if it would be easier. Even if I beg. Some things you drink alone or not at all."

"Agreed," Ivo said, without a heartbeat's pause, and offered his hand.

She looked at it. Flour-dusted, honest, dangerous in ways she couldn't yet map. Everything in her training said don't, everything in her hollowed-out heart said *don't you dare* —

She shook it.

His grip was warm. And beneath it, faint as a bell rung in another room, she thought she heard — or hoped she heard — a gap-toothed girl laughing, waiting, keeping her place.

*

They moved out within the hour, because staying anywhere was dying slowly in Corvane, and Brannoc knew a smuggler's stair down to the river quarter. Sera took point despite her own rule — rear guard could wait until they were clear of Hask's territory — and it was she who saw the lantern-signal blink from the rooftop opposite Vellum & Ash: three flashes, pause, two. The broker's men marking their position.

"They know you're with us now," she said quietly to Ivo as they threaded the drainage alleys. "By morning every knife in this city will know. You understand that. There's no un-choosing this."

"I know," Ivo said. He walked with the loaf still under his arm, absurdly, like a talisman. "There's something I need to find in the underlevels first. Before we leave the city. A boy who remembers too much — Pip mentioned him once, and Pip doesn't mention things lightly." He glanced sideways at her. "The brokers keep him. Like a vault."

Sera felt the plan assemble itself in her head the way battle-plans used to, before she'd sold the battles: approaches, numbers, timing. She hated how good it felt.

"A living vault under a broker-house," she said. "Of course. This city eats children twice." She rolled her favored shoulder — the badly-healed one, the one some past version of her had broken doing something brave, probably — and found she was smiling, thin and sharp.

"Then we'd best move fast," she said. "Because tomorrow, valley-boy, we're robbing the people who own yesterday — and I intend to start with mine."

Behind them, unseen, a second lantern answered from a third rooftop. And in a bottle-shop on Corrow Lane, a blue-fingered broker opened a ledger, ran one finger down a column of names, and paused at one written in older ink than all the rest:

*Sera of the Broken Company.*

He smiled, and sent for her file — all eleven years of it — including the page that listed what she'd sold, and to whom, and where it was kept.
