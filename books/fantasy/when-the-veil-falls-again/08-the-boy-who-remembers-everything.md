## 8. The Boy Who Remembers Everything

The underlevels of Corvane smelled of cold copper and old rain.

We had come down through the back of a bottle-shop whose proprietor charged Brannoc two coins and asked no questions, down a stair that narrowed until Sera had to walk sideways with her sword drawn flat against her hip, down past doors marked with chalk sigils I did not understand but which made my teeth ache faintly, the way the standing stones used to. The deeper we went, the more the walls changed — from dressed stone to rough-cut rock to something older still, a black glassy substance that reflected our lantern light in colors it shouldn't have held.

"Memory sinks," Brannoc said quietly, her voice swallowed by the dark ahead. "Bottled things are heavy. The brokers keep their vaults below the water table because grief, especially, wants to run downhill." She glanced back at me, her face half shadow. "Stay close. And whatever you hear in there — whatever you *feel* — remember none of it is yours."

That was the trouble. Down here, everything felt like mine.

It had started at the top of the stair, a whisper against the inside of my skull, and it thickened as we descended: stray images, unbidden, flickering behind my eyes like moths against glass. A wedding in a courtyard I'd never stood in. A fevered child. A woman laughing over a card game, then crying over the same cards a year later. Thousands of small human moments, stacked around me like grain in a silo, pressing through the walls into my skin.

I had spent my whole life untouched by magic. Inside the valley, nothing strange could reach me; beyond it, on the road, the pressure of the world's enchantment had been like water in the ears — uncomfortable but external. This was different. This was not magic trying to touch me. This was memory itself, raw and bottled and leaking, finding in me something porous.

"You're flushing," Sera said. She fell into step beside me, close enough that her shoulder nearly brushed mine. In the lantern light her face was all hard planes and watchful eyes, but she had taken to walking near me since the alley, since the fragment of her sister's laugh that had poured out of my hand into hers. "I know that look. First time in the deep vaults, everyone wears it. Like drowning in other people's lives."

"Is it always this loud?"

"It gets quiet if you pay for silence." Her mouth twisted. "Or if you sell enough of yourself that there's less of you left to be bothered."

Brannoc raised a fist. We stopped.

Ahead, the passage opened into a chamber that stole the breath from my chest. It was vast — an old cistern, maybe, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a lattice of stone channels where shallow water trickled and glimmered. And rising out of that water on iron racks were bottles. Hundreds. Thousands. Row upon row upon row, receding into the dark like a forest turned to glass, each vessel glowing with its own faint interior light — amber, blue, a sickly green, one far corner pulsing a deep bruised violet.

Some of them whispered.

Not all. But some, softly, in voices too worn to carry more than fragments: *—the day she was born—* *—don't go back to the house—* *—I never told him, I never—* A chorus of the discarded, murmuring in the wet dark.

"Marek's teeth," Sera breathed. "There must be ten thousand memories here."

"Fifteen, by the brokers' own tallies, if you believe their boasts." Brannoc moved along the rack-line with the careful tread of someone entering a temple she despised. "Stolen, sold, seized as debt-payment, harvested from the condemned before execution so the state can study what guilt looks like from the inside. Corvane calls this the Undervault. The people who end up here call it nothing, because they no longer remember enough to name it."

I touched the nearest bottle without thinking — just brushed it with one fingertip.

A man's whole morning bloomed through me: bread and honey, a wife's cold shoulder, a letter he read three times by the window, the specific weight of dread. Then it was gone, and I was staggering back, gasping, my hand burning where I'd touched the glass.

"Ivo!" Sera caught my arm.

"It's all right. It's—" I shook my head, clearing it. "It's like tasting. Everything at once. I could taste every dish in a kitchen if they set them all in front of me. That's what this is." I looked up at the forest of glowing bottles, and something in me went very cold and very steady. "Someone is keeping all of these. Someone is deciding who gets their mornings back."

"That someone is why we're here," Brannoc said. "Come. The vault-master keeps his ledgers below, and the ledgers will tell us how to reach the Heartwell road without walking through the Crown Gate. And—" she hesitated, and I saw her weigh the words before letting them out, "—there is supposed to be a boy."

"A boy?"

"The brokers' finest instrument." Her voice had gone flat, which for Brannoc meant furious. "They found him young, a child with a gift for holding — not just remembering, but *containing*, the way a jar contains water. They use him as a living vault. Memories too fragile or too valuable for glass get pressed into his head. He walks around carrying thousands of stolen lives, and he has never once been allowed to keep a single one of his own."

Sera's jaw tightened. "How old?"

"No one says. Ten, perhaps. Perhaps younger when they took him."

We went on.

* * *

The vault-master's office sat at the chamber's far edge, a cage of iron and lamplight built into the rock, and it was empty. Ledger books lay open on the desk, ink still wet. A cup of tea sat cooling, untouched. Whatever had called the man away had called him urgently.

"He'll return," Brannoc murmured, already riffling the ledgers with practiced fingers. "Buy me a hundred count. Sera, watch the passage."

Sera posted herself at the mouth of the office, blade loose in her hand. I drifted toward the open door at the cage's back — and stopped.

Beyond it was a smaller room. A cell, really. No window. A straw pallet. A bucket. And bolted to the wall, a harness of leather straps and iron rings, sized for a child, worn soft at the buckles from years of use.

And sitting cross-legged on the pallet, watching me with enormous, unblinking grey eyes, was the boy.

He was thin in the way of things deliberately kept small — not starved exactly, but rationed, maintained rather than nourished. His hair had been cut short with something blunt. His wrists bore pale grooves where straps had ridden for years. He didn't flinch when he saw me. He simply looked, the way you'd look at weather.

"You're new," he said.

His voice was strange. Layered. A child's voice, yes, but underneath it, faintly, like sediment under clear water, ran other tones — a man's gruffness, an old woman's crackle, a dozen accents braided together. When he spoke, it sounded like many people agreeing to speak at once.

"I'm Ivo," I said.

"I know. You walked in and now I've got your name." He tapped his temple. "You leaked it. Everyone leaks. Names, mostly. Sometimes smells. Once a whole song." He considered me. "You leak louder than most. Your whole head is like a closed door with wind behind it. What are you?"

"I don't—" I knelt slowly, bringing myself to his height. Up close I could see the fine tremor in his hands, the way his eyes tracked not quite at me but slightly beside, as if he watched a crowd standing behind my shoulders. "What's your name?"

Something crossed his face — quick, hungry, gone.

"They took it," he said simply. "First thing. Said names take up room. I had a name once. It's in here somewhere, probably. Packed away with the tax collector's sins and a baker's last summer." He shrugged, and the shrug broke my heart more cleanly than any tears would have. "The brokers call me Pip. Because I'm small and full of things."

"Pip." I said it gently, giving it back to him like something dropped. "Pip, we're going to get you out of here."

"You can't." No drama in it. Arithmetic. "The straps aren't the lock. I'm the lock. If they move me wrong, or if I'm frightened, or if I die badly, everything in here—" he patted his skull, and beneath his palm, faintly, I swear his temple glowed, "—comes out all at once. Fifteen hundred lives, spilling across the floor. Some of them are worth more than this city. Some of them belong to people who paid good money to make sure no one ever feels them again." His grey eyes fixed on me. "Including yours."

"My what?"

He tilted his head. "You don't know? Oh." And then, horribly, kindly: "Oh, you really don't."

Behind me, Sera's voice cracked sharp down the corridor: "Lanterns! Two, coming fast — Brannoc, we're out of hundred counts!"

* * *

It happened fast after that.

The vault-master returned with two hired blades, and Sera met them in the narrow passage like a door slamming. I heard steel, heard a man cry out, heard Brannoc snarl a word that made the lamplight gutter green — and I stayed kneeling in front of Pip, because he had grabbed my wrist with both small hands, and his grip was astonishingly strong.

"If they come for me, I break," he said, rapid and urgent. "That's the rule. That's what they trained into me. Frightened boys spill. So listen — listen — you want to know what you are? I've got a memory in here that doesn't fit any of the ledgers. It came in a consignment eleven years ago, sealed, private, paid for by a woman with a hedge-witch's accent, and it's been sitting in my head like a stone in a shoe ever since because it *wants* to go home and I never knew where home was." His eyes were huge, wet, blazing. "Then you walked in leaking, and the stone started singing. Here. Take it. Take it *out* of me. Please. It's the only thing anyone ever gave me to hold that wasn't stolen first."

"I don't know how—"

"Yes, you do. You taste things. So taste."

He pressed my palm flat against his temple.

The world folded.

* * *

I was walking. No — I was seeing walking, from outside, from above, the way a memory sees: two figures on a road at dusk, a road I knew with a violence that made my ribs hurt, because it was the valley road, the road past the ford, the road I had walked ten thousand times and never once seen anyone arrive on.

A man and a woman. My height in the memory was nothing, a bird's view, but I knew them the way you know your own hands. The man wore a warden's grey travel-cloak gone dark with rain, and he carried himself like a soldier even exhausted, even bleeding — there was blood, silver-dark in the twilight, seeping through the cloth at his side. The woman stumbled beside him, and wrapped in her arms, bundled against the autumn cold, was a baby.

They reached the standing stones. The great ring of them, veiled in mist, the boundary I had lived inside and never questioned.

The woman was crying. Not loudly. The crying of someone who has agreed to be brave and regrets the contract. She lifted the bundle and kissed its forehead, and the baby — I knew that face, I knew that exact frown, I had seen it in every reflective surface of my childhood without knowing what I was looking for —

"His name is Ivo," she said, to someone I couldn't see, to the stones themselves perhaps. "Whatever you have to make him forget, whatever you have to make him *be* — let him be safe. Let him be kind. Promise me the veil will hold around him even if it holds around nothing else."

And the mist answered. Not in words. In the way the valley mist answers everything: by closing, softly, like a hand folding shut.

The man leaned his forehead against the tallest stone. "The ward holds because he holds it," he said, and his voice was breaking. "Our son. Our boy is the lock." A ragged laugh, half sob. "We hid the treasure by making it a child."

Then the memory folded too, and I was back.

* * *

I came to on the floor of the cell with Pip's arms around my neck and my own voice hoarse, though I didn't remember screaming. The lamplight swam. Somewhere beyond the wall, Sera was shouting my name.

"There you are," Pip said, matter-of-fact, patting my shoulder with the gravity of a physician. "Big ones do that. The important ones. Like swallowing a river."

"Pip—" My mouth barely worked. "The memory. Your memory. Where did it—"

"Gone. Out of me, into you. Feels better." He smiled, and it was the saddest, brightest thing I had ever seen. "See? Told you I keep things. Now you keep one for me."

The cell door burst open. Sera filled it, blood-spattered, breathing hard, and took one look at my face and went still in the particular way she went still before violence. "What happened. What did they do to him—"

"Not them." Pip released me and stood, wobbling, straightening with dignity. "Me. I gave him a present." He gathered a battered satchel from under the pallet — packed, I realized, long ago, waiting. Waiting for what? For a door with wind behind it, maybe. For me. "And now," the boy said, in his layered, crowded, ancient-young voice, "you should probably decide fast whether you're stealing me, because the vault-master's friends stopped being friends and started being messengers, and messengers ride to the palace."

Brannoc appeared behind Sera, and when she saw the boy, when she saw *me*, her face did something complicated and terrible — recognition and calculation and guilt, all at war. Later I would understand it. Later I would learn what she had already guessed about what I was, and what she had been planning to do about it, and how close I had walked to being spent like a coin.

But in that moment all I knew was this: I had spent twenty-odd years believing I was an orphan of nowhere, a valley-foundling, a taster with a lucky tongue. And now I carried, warm and terrible behind my breastbone, the sound of my mother's voice saying *our boy is the lock*.

The valley had never been my home.

I was its shape. Its hinge. Its ward — and someone had built a prison around me and called it kindness.

"Ivo." Brannoc's voice, gentle. "We need to move."

I picked Pip up. He weighed almost nothing, this boy stuffed with fifteen hundred stolen lives, and he hooked his chin over my shoulder and sighed like someone setting down a load he'd carried alone for years.

"All right," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt, steadier than anything in me deserved to be. "We steal him. And then, Brannoc, you and I are going to talk about what else you know."

Her eyes met mine — and did not deny it.

Above us, faint through the rock, bells began to ring, and not the market's cheerful hour-chimes. These were the deep palace bells, the alarm-tones, rolling down through the Undervault like thunder looking for somewhere to break.

Pip tensed against my shoulder. "They know," he whispered. "The crown remembers. It always remembers."
