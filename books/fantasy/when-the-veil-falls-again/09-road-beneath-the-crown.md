## 9. Road Beneath the Crown

Brannoc had walked this road before, though not in this direction and never with so much at stake.

The old pilgrim way unspooled ahead of them through the hill country like a grey ribbon someone had dropped and forgotten. It was older than the kingdom, older than the crown that sat upon it — you could tell by the stones. Kings built their roads straight because kings liked to see where they were going. Pilgrims built theirs winding, because a road that curved kept secrets, and secrets were the only currency the founders of this land had ever truly trusted.

She walked at the rear, staff in hand, watching Ivo's back the way a shepherd watches a lamb that doesn't yet know what wolves are.

He was different out here. She'd known he would be — she'd seen it at the crossroads shrine, when he'd collapsed like a man surfacing too fast from deep water — but knowing and seeing were different debts. In the valley he must have moved through the world like a fish in still water, unaware there was such a thing as current. Out here, every hedgerow hummed against his skin. Every wayside charm made him flinch. The boy tasted poison for a living, and now he'd been set down in a world where everything was poison of one kind or another.

*And he's the antidote*, she thought, and hated herself a little for the shape the thought took. Not *he's a person*. *He's the antidote.* That was the arithmetic she'd been doing since the ford, and she was going to have to stop doing it before it calcified into something she couldn't take back.

Ahead, Sera moved like the soldier she no longer remembered being — all economy, weight on the balls of her feet, eyes sweeping the treeline with a fluency her missing memories hadn't managed to erase. Pip rode on Ivo's shoulders, which the boy tolerated with an air of enormous condescension, and chattered about the hills.

"That one's got a name," Pip said, pointing. "I've got its name. Somebody sold it. An old woman who used to sing to it." He frowned, digging through whatever vast and terrible attic lived behind his eyes. "Marrowfell. The hill's called Marrowfell. She sang, *sleep, bone-sleeper, sleep*—"

"Enough," Brannoc said, sharper than she meant.

Pip shut his mouth. Ivo glanced back at her, one eyebrow up, and she realized how it had sounded — not caution but fear.

"The names draw things," she said, softer. "Out here, a named hill isn't just a hill. Say it three times under the wrong sky and something might answer."

"That's not true," Pip said, with the flat certainty of a child stating weather.

"It's true enough," Brannoc said. "Keep it."

They made camp that night in the shell of a pilgrim hospice, its roof long fallen, its hearthstones still holding heat the way old holy places did. Brannoc laid the fire with a word and two fingers' worth of effort, and tried not to watch Ivo watch her do it. He looked at magic the way starving men looked through bakery windows — hunger and grief in the same expression, because he'd grown up behind glass.

Sera took first watch without being asked. Pip curled against a wall like a cat finding the warmest brick. And Ivo sat across the fire from her, turning the map over in his hands.

It lay open now. It always lay open, these days; folding it had become a ritual of futility they'd abandoned somewhere past the second ridge. By firelight the ink seemed less drawn than grown, root-threads of brown-black spreading from the valley at its western edge all the way east to a small, precise circle beneath the capital. The Heartwell. The route to it. The route *into* it, if you knew how to read the margins, and Brannoc knew how to read the margins.

"You've been looking at me all day," Ivo said. "Like you're working up to something."

"I'm an old woman. I'm always working up to something."

"I'm serious."

Brannoc fed a stick to the fire. She had rehearsed this speech for three days, on ridgelines and riverbanks, and every version of it had been a lie of one size or another. There wasn't time left for comfortable lies. The road bent toward the capital now, and roads like this one collected debts.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll be serious. What did they tell you the valley was?"

Ivo blinked. "A refuge. A place magic can't reach. The safe country. Everyone says it — we're the blessed ones, the spared ones. When the wars came and the blight years, the valley stayed clean. That's why the king keeps his household there. Why—" He stopped. "Why I was raised in the kitchens instead of anywhere else."

"And you never wondered why the safest place in the world needed a food-taster?"

The question landed the way she'd wanted it to and hated wanting that. Ivo went very still. She watched him run his whole life backward through the question — the taste tests, the immunity built dish by dish, the standing stones he'd never once been permitted to cross, the guards who weren't there to keep danger out but—

"No," he said slowly. "No, I wondered. Everyone wonders. They say it's tradition. The king's household is old-fashioned."

"Tradition." Brannoc laughed, and it came out as ash. "Sit properly, boy. This will take telling, and I'll only say it once."

* * *

"Two hundred years ago," she began, "there was no veil. Magic ran through this land like blood through a body — wild in places, sick in places, but alive. The mages of that age weren't hedge-folk like me. They were architects. They built with enchantment the way your builders build with stone, and some of what they built was beautiful, and some of it was the worst thing this world has ever held."

The fire cracked. Somewhere on the dark hillside, a fox screamed and fell silent.

"They found something," Brannoc said. "Down deep, under what would become the capital, under the water-table and the bedrock, in a place where the world's oldest waters collect. A wellspring. But wellsprings don't just hold water, boy. They hold what the water remembers. Everything ever poured into the earth upstream of it. Every grief, every death, every memory the ground has drunk in ten thousand years. It pooled there. It *woke* there. And it woke hungry, because memory is the only food it can eat, and it ate the mages who found it before they could even scream."

Ivo's hands had gone white around the map's edges.

"They couldn't kill it," she went on. "You can't kill a hunger. Hunger isn't a creature; it's a *shape*. So they did what architects do. They built around it. They bound it beneath the wellspring with rites so heavy they cost the lives of everyone who spoke them, and then — because bindings rot, boy, bindings always rot — they built a second wall above the first. A ward so wide it covered a whole valley. A place where magic simply could not exist. No spell could reach across it, no hunger could smell through it. They called it the Veiled Valley, and they told the world it was a sanctuary."

"But it wasn't," Ivo said. His voice was very quiet.

"It was a lock," Brannoc said. "The valley sits directly over the binding's anchor-stone. The veil doesn't shelter the people inside it — the people inside it feed the ward with their ordinary, magicless living. Their plainness. Their *safety*. Every generation born inside those stones thickens the wall. Your grandparents didn't live in a refuge, Ivo Brightwater. They lived in mortar."

The silence stretched. Pip, she noticed, was awake and had been for some time, curled against the wall with his eyes open and fixed on her, drinking it in the way he drank in everything, storing it in that terrible vault of his.

"My parents," Ivo said finally. "They came from outside. Pip showed me — I saw it, a fragment. They carried me in through the stones. Why would they walk into a prison?"

"Because they were the wardens," Brannoc said, "and the prison needed new keys."

She hadn't meant to say that part. It slipped out the way truth does when you've been carrying too much of it uphill. Ivo stared at her, and she saw him assemble the sentence, examine it, fail to make it fit anything.

"Wardens," he repeated.

"Ask me again when we're closer to the capital, and I may have the courage to answer." She said it gently, and it was still a cruelty. Some debts you pay in installments because paying all at once kills the debtor. "What matters tonight is the other half. Look at your map again. Who drew it?"

"I don't know. The woman at the ford — she was bleeding silver, she said the crown remembers—"

"The map was drawn by the architect," Brannoc said. "Not one of the architects. *The* architect. Warden Aldous Veyle — yes, that's where the valley's name comes from; your teachers scrubbed the rest. The last mage to survive the binding. He spent his final thirty years carving this route: every shrine, every underpass, every door into the deep places beneath the palace. Because he knew what I know. Bindings rot. One day the Vessel would start chewing through, and when it did, someone would need to get to the Heartwell who could finish what the dying mages started. Unbind the king. Face the thing below. End it or be ended."

"And the route leads through the veil," Ivo said. "That's why it burns when I fold it. That's why it wouldn't let me hide it. It's not a map to the Heartwell. It's a map *from* the valley. It's been waiting for someone to walk out."

"It's been waiting for *you*," Brannoc said, and the fire chose that moment to collapse in a shower of sparks, and none of them moved, and the dark pressed in close as a held breath. "The veil can't be crossed by magic. Nothing touched by power gets through those stones in either direction. But you — a child born inside it, grown wholly outside magic's reach — you're the one thing in this world the Vessel cannot see coming and cannot digest if it catches you. You're not traveling to the Heartwell, Ivo. As far as the old rites are concerned, you *are* the journey."

Across the fire, Sera had stopped pretending to scan the treeline. She was looking at Brannoc with an expression Brannoc recognized from soldiers' faces on the eve of battles they'd already lost — the look of a woman doing arithmetic and disliking the total.

"And what happens to him at the end of the journey?" Sera asked.

"That," Brannoc said, "is tomorrow's lesson."

* * *

But Sera did not forget it, and neither, it turned out, did the night.

Brannoc took second watch after all, waving off Sera's protest, because sleep had gone somewhere she couldn't follow. She sat with her back to the hospice's fallen altar-stone and watched the stars wheel over Marrowfell, and let herself remember — briefly, the way you let a sore tooth remind you it's there — the debt she owed the crown. The name she'd sold. The reason she, of all the hedge-witches between here and the sea, had been the one waiting at a crossroads shrine for a boy with a bleeding map.

Fate didn't arrange things. Debts arranged things. And hers had come due.

"You're afraid of him."

Pip's voice, from the darkness near the wall. She hadn't heard him move. Children like Pip moved like cats or ghosts, depending on which story you preferred.

"I'm an old woman. I'm always afraid of something."

"Not like this." He crept nearer, knees dirty, eyes catching the coals' red light. "You talk about the Vessel like it's a storm coming, and storms don't scare you. But when you look at Ivo, you look like my mother did when the brokers came. Like you've already counted the cost and decided someone else pays it."

The boy remembered everything. That was the horror of him and the use of him, both. You could lie to armies. You could lie to kings. You could not lie to a child whose mind held ten thousand stolen truths and weighed each newcomer against them.

"What do you want me to say, Pip?"

"I want you to say it out loud," he said. "So I know if you mean to change it."

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment. Then at Ivo, asleep by the fire's edge, the map spread over his chest rising and falling like something breathing. Like something alive, she corrected herself, because it was, and the sooner she started thinking of it that way again, the better her odds of dying as something other than a monster.

"There may be another way," she said at last. "There are always other ways. Most of them cost more than the simple one. That's why the simple ones get chosen."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Go back to sleep."

He went, but she felt his eyes on her back long after, two small weights like coins placed on a corpse.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and smelling of rain. They broke camp in the cold and took the road east, and by midmorning the hills had opened onto the first true view of their destination: far off, hazy as a watermark, the capital crouched on its river plain with the palace spires rising from its heart like needles of bone.

Ivo stood beside her on the ridge and looked at it a long time.

"It's smaller than I imagined," he said.

"Everything is, until it has you."

Below them, where the pilgrim road bent down toward the lowlands, stood a waystone — one of Veyle's, she realized, one of his markers, worn nearly smooth by two centuries of weather. Someone had recently tied a strip of cloth to it. White cloth, marked with a single black circle.

Brannoc's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She knew that sign. Every hedge-witch knew it. It was the crown agents' mark, the quiet one, the one that meant *watched road, report passage* — and it was fresh, the cloth still bright, tied within the last day.

Someone knew they were coming. Not vaguely, not by rumor. Someone had walked this exact stretch of road ahead of them and left a message for whoever came next.

"Sera," she said quietly. "How good are you in a fight you can't remember starting?"

Sera looked at the waystone, then at her own blade, then at Brannoc, and smiled the thin smile of a woman who had nothing left to lose and had finally found something worth spending it on.

"I suppose," she said, "we're about to find out."
