## 10. The Hollow King

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

We smelled it first — woodsmoke and river-mud and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun — and then we heard it: a low, ceaseless hum of ten thousand lives braided together, carrying on the wind the way a hive carries sound. Pip stood up in the cart bed so suddenly that Sera had to grab his collar to keep him from tumbling out.

"I can hear the bells," he said. "There are forty-one of them. No—forty-two. One's cracked." He said it the way other children reported seeing birds, and Brannoc's hands tightened on the reins until her knuckles went pale as candle wax.

"Sit down," she said quietly. "And stop counting."

"Why? I'm good at counting."

"Because things in Corvane count *you*, love," Sera said, and pulled him down beside her, and though she smiled when she said it, I saw her other hand rest near the knife at her hip, the way it did whenever she spoke of the city that had eaten pieces of her.

I sat at the back of the cart with my legs dangling over the tailboard and watched the road ahead, where pilgrims and merchants and farmers' carts were all funneled toward the same distant glitter. In the Veiled Valley, I had never seen more than two hundred people gathered in one place, and that was for the harvest festival, and half of them were related to me by supper rather than blood. Now there was a whole city ahead of us, a city built around a palace built around a well, and somewhere beneath all that stone was a king who needed unbinding, and a thing in a crown that needed starving.

My tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth. It did that sometimes now — an old habit from the tasting bench, checking itself, readying itself. Twenty years of the king's food had made my mouth into an instrument. Nightshade sang bitter-sweet at the back of the throat. Arsenic whispered of garlic. Hemlock tasted of mouse. I could find poison in a spoonful of honey, in a cup of small beer, in the steam rising off a stew. It was the only magic I had ever owned, and I had owned it inside a valley where magic couldn't exist.

I wondered, not for the first time since the standing stones, what else my tongue might taste now that the world outside had come flooding back around me.

* * *

We entered Corvane through the Tanners' Gate because Brannoc said the Crown's eyes were thickest at the Processional Way, and because Sera knew which gate-warden owed her a debt he'd rather pay quickly than remember slowly. He took one look at her, went the color of old tallow, and waved us through without asking for papers.

Inside, the city swallowed us.

I had thought the memory-markets of the lower quarter were the worst of it — the bottled griefs gleaming in their racks, the mother in the gray shawl trading her daughter's laugh away bottle by bottle until she could afford bread. But the capital was the market entire. Every third shop sold remembrance: vials, philtres, wax-sealed jars of somebody's childhood. Children ran through the streets playing a game where one of them pretended to forget the others' names, and the others "bought" him back with shouted memories, and I watched them and felt something cold settle behind my ribs that had nothing to do with autumn coming on.

Pip walked close to me the whole way. He didn't say why, and I didn't ask, but twice I caught him flinching at nothing — or at something I couldn't see — and once he whispered, very fast, "*They're loud here,*" and pressed both hands flat against his ears even though there was no sound but cart wheels and hawkers' cries.

Brannoc led us through streets that grew cleaner and quieter as we climbed, until we reached a widow's lodging house above a chandler's shop, four rooms with a view of nothing but chimney smoke. She paid the widow in silver she claimed came from selling herbs and would meet no one's eye while she said it.

"We go to see the procession tomorrow," she told us that night, over bread and hard cheese and a candle burning low. "All of you. Especially you, Ivo."

"Especially me," I repeated. "Why especially me?"

"Because you need to see him before you decide anything else." She turned her cup in her fingers, around and around, a nervous habit I'd never seen in her before. "You've heard me tell what the Vessel is. You've heard what the rite asks. But hearing isn't knowing. Tomorrow you'll look at the king's face, and then whatever you choose, you'll choose it with your eyes open."

"And if the crown sees me first?" Pip asked, from the corner where he'd curled himself up like a cat.

The room went still. The candle flame leaned sideways though no draft moved.

"Then we run," Sera said simply. "That's always been the plan B. Plan C is knives."

"That's not a plan, that's a mood," said Pip, and Sera grinned, and just like that the room breathed again. I lay awake long after the others slept, listening to the cracked bell toll the small hours, and tried to imagine a face under a crown that hunger wore like a hat.

* * *

The procession began at midmorning, and the city lost its mind for it.

Every window along the Processional Way was hung with blue-and-gold bunting. People crowded six deep behind the barriers, throwing petals, screaming names — *Aldric! Aldric! Long live King Aldric!* — with a fervor that sounded less like love than like fear wearing love's clothes. We found our place near the fountain at the corner of the Way, Sera and Brannoc flanking me, Pip perched on the fountain's rim where he could see over everyone's heads.

"He comes at the third bell," Pip recited, eyes unfocused, voice going flat and far away in the way that meant he was reading someone else's remembered morning. "He rides a white horse named Lamentation because his father named it as a joke and the court was too frightened to laugh. There are twelve guards in front and twelve behind and one who walks beside who is not really a guard at all."

"One who walks beside," Brannoc murmured. "A warden-captain. Watch for grey gloves, boy."

"I know. I already know. Everyone knows. Everyone remembers this every year." Pip rubbed his temples. "It's too loud. All these people remembering the same day at the same time, it's like — like standing inside a bell."

The third bell rang.

The crowd's roar swelled and crested, and around the bend of the Processional Way came the king of Corvane.

He was beautiful. That was the first thing, and it struck me like a slap. Stories of hollow kings had filled my head since the ford — I had pictured something gaunt and grey-eyed, a corpse propped on a throne — but the man riding toward us was broad-shouldered and warm-faced, with silver threading his dark beard and laugh lines carved deep around eyes that the crowd clearly adored. His white horse stepped high and proud. He raised one gloved hand and the roar doubled, tripled, women weeping, men shouting themselves hoarse, children held up on shoulders to see.

And then the sunlight crossed his face at a certain angle, and I saw his eyes.

They flickered.

Not blinked — flickered, the way a lamp gutters when something moves through the oil, a stutter of light that wasn't light. Cold. Pale. For half a heartbeat the beloved face was a mask stretched over emptiness, and behind the emptiness something looked *out*, sweeping across the crowd the way a fisherman sweeps a net across still water.

The flicker passed. The king smiled and waved. The crowd screamed its love.

But I had spent twenty years watching faces for the small betrayals the mouth makes before the body betrays the man. I knew what a smile looked like when it was worn rather than felt — I'd seen it on the kitchen boy Tam who smiled through his mother's funeral because grief was improper below stairs, I'd seen it on the chamberlain who toasted the king each morning with eyes like locked doors. This was that smile. Perfect. Practiced. *Put on.*

Beside me, Sera's hand had gone to her knife. On the fountain rim, Pip had stopped breathing.

"There," he whispered. "Did you see? Did you see the—"

"I saw," Brannoc said. Her face was stone, but her voice had a hairline crack running through it. "Twelve years I've heard reports. Reading about it is different from—" She stopped. Started again, colder. "Look at his hands. Look at how he holds the reins."

I looked. The king's hands were wrong. Not injured, not deformed — simply *unattended*, resting on the reins the way a mannequin's hands rest on whatever they're given, arranged rather than placed. When the horse stumbled slightly on a loose cobble, the king's body corrected with fluid grace, but a half-beat late, like an echo arriving after its shout.

And walking beside the horse, matching its pace exactly, was a guard in grey gloves.

* * *

It happened because of a child.

Later I would understand that it probably wasn't chance at all — that the whole day may have been arranged around it, bait laid out on the Processional Way for a taster's tongue — but in the moment it seemed like nothing, a small accident in a sea of pageantry.

A flower-girl, maybe eight years old, wriggled free of her mother's grip at the barrier and darted forward to press a posy up toward the passing king. A guard lunged; the crowd gasped; and the king — moving with that same strange, echoing grace — leaned down from the saddle and accepted the flowers from her small hands.

For one instant, the child's face was directly beneath his.

And the king's eyes flickered again — and fixed on her — and the smile stayed perfectly in place while everything behind it *leaned*, the way a wolf leans over a sleeping lamb, and the little girl's own smile faltered and died and she made a small confused sound, like someone waking from a dream they can't quite hold—

Sera's hand closed on my arm. "Ivo. Don't."

I hadn't known I was moving. I hadn't decided anything. But the crowd was between me and the barrier and my feet had already carried me two steps into it, and some part of me that lived below thought, below language, below choice, had recognized what was happening to that child the way my tongue recognizes hemlock: instantly, bodily, without being asked.

Because I knew that feeling. I knew what it was to be looked at by something hungry. The stranger at the ford had looked at me that way, bleeding silver, pressing her map into my hands. And the map in my breast pocket — folded, wrapped, tied down with cord because it would not stay folded on its own — had begun, very softly, to burn.

"*Ivo.*"

Brannoc's voice cut through and I stopped. The moment passed. A guard swept the child back behind the barrier; the king straightened in the saddle; the procession rolled on past us, twelve guards, the king, twelve guards, the grey-gloved walker whose head turned — slowly, smoothly, like a weathervane finding wind — and whose eyes passed over our little group at the fountain and did not stop, and did not hurry, and saw everything.

Then the pageant was past, and the crowd's roar washed over us again, and the burn in my pocket faded to warmth, and I was breathing hard as if I'd run a mile.

"It saw us," Pip said. Not a question.

"No," Brannoc said. "It *looked*. Seeing requires wanting, and it doesn't want yet. It's fishing." She turned to me, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "Which means, boy, that it's time we talked about why you're here. Truly here. Because I don't think you've understood it yet, and I need you to understand it before we take another step."

"Not here," Sera said, low. "Walls first. And Ivo—" She hesitated, then nodded at my pocket. "What was that? The burning?"

I drew out the bundle. The map lay inside its wrapping, and through the linen I could feel it pulsing faintly, patiently, like a second heartbeat that was not mine.

"It's been quiet since the stones," I said. "Since we left Corvane's lower city, it's barely stirred. But when the king leaned down toward that girl—"

"It answered," Brannoc finished. Something in her face shifted, and for the first time since I'd met her at the crossroads shrine, she looked genuinely afraid. "Of course it did. The map is the architect's hand. The Vessel wears the architect's prison. They know each other." She looked down the Processional Way, where the last banners were disappearing around the bend toward the palace. "And now, I suspect, they know *you*."

* * *

We should have gone straight back to the lodging house. Brannoc wanted to. Sera wanted to. Even Pip, pale and silent, tugging at Brannoc's sleeve, wanted to.

But the palace had set out wine.

It was custom, the criers announced, that on Procession Day the royal cellars poured a ceremonial vintage for the commons — a blessing of the harvest, a gift of the king's grace, free cups at every public house on the Way. Barrels stood broached on corners, pages in livery filling clay cups, and the whole city drank the king's health until the bells rang evening.

"You want me to drink it," I said.

They had found a quiet alcove behind a shrine to some forgotten saint, and Brannoc had bought a single cup from the nearest barrel and set it in my hands, and now the three of them watched me across its dark red surface.

"I want you to *taste* it," Brannoc said. "Not swallow. Swirl, touch it to your tongue, spit. Like you used to do for the valley's king." Her mouth twisted around the words. "Like you were raised to do. All those years, Ivo — all those dishes, all those doses — I don't believe the valley wasted you on practice alone. Whatever made you, made your tongue for this. So use it. Tell me what's in the king's wine."

I lifted the cup. Around us the city drank and cheered and grew pleasantly stupid on charity, and the bells began their evening counting, and Pip covered his ears against the noise of ten thousand memories being made at once.

The wine smelled of black cherry and oak smoke and something floral underneath, elderflower perhaps. It was beautiful wine. I let a mouthful spread across my tongue and held it there and listened, the way I had listened ten thousand mornings on the tasting bench while Cook hovered and the king's breakfast cooled and my life hung in the space between one swallow and the next.

Cherry. Oak. Elderflower. Tannin. A ghost of clove from the cask.

And then, beneath it all, thin as a wire drawn through silk:

Something else.

Not bitter. Not sweet. Poison has flavors — that was the first lesson, the deepest lesson: poison wants to hide, so it hides in strong tastes, and the taster's art is tasting *around* the strength to what lies under. This had no flavor at all. It was a texture, a pressure, a sensation like the memory of cold water poured down the back of the neck — and as it touched my tongue, a thought arrived in my mind that was not mine, gentle as a hand on the shoulder:

*Be calm. Be content. Go home. Forget.*

I spat the wine into the gutter so fast I nearly dropped the cup.

"What?" Sera was on her feet, knife half-drawn, scanning the street. "What is it? Poison?"

"No." My heart was slamming. I wiped my mouth with the back of my hand and the taste — the non-taste — clung anyway, a residue of suggestion like grease on a pan. "No poison. Worse. Compulsion."

Brannoc had gone absolutely still. "Say that again."

"The wine is dosed. Not with anything herbal, not with anything I know — with something that isn't a substance at all. It doesn't hurt anyone. It doesn't sicken anyone. It just—" I struggled for it. "When I tasted it, I *wanted* to go home. To the valley. It wasn't fear, it wasn't doubt, it was a certainty, warm as supper, that none of this was my business and the best thing in the world would be to turn around and walk east until I saw the standing stones. For about three seconds it was the truest thing I have ever felt in my life." I looked down at the spilled wine darkening the cobbles. "If I'd swallowed a full cup of that, I'd be on the east road by midnight, whistling."

"Sweetheart," Sera said carefully, "it's wine for the *commons*. The king drinks from his own cellar. Why would anyone dose the whole city?"

"They wouldn't," Brannoc said. Her voice had gone quiet in the way it did when she was assembling something terrible out of small parts. "This barrel isn't for the whole city. This barrel is on the route *we* would walk. The shrine alcove. The fountain corner." She looked at me. "Someone bought this vintage, doctored it, and put it precisely where a hedge-witch, a broken soldier, a stolen child, and a valley-born boy would pass on Procession Day. It's not a weapon. It's a message."

The bells tolled seven. Somewhere down the street, a drunk was singing the king's praises, off-key and joyful, and the sound curdled as I understood.

"They know I'm coming," I said. "They've known since before we entered the walls. Maybe since the ford. Since the stones." I turned the empty cup over in my hands. "The grey-gloved guard. The way he looked at us. They weren't hunting us today. They were *welcoming* us — and offering me a door home, hoping I'd be too tired, too scared, too human to refuse it."

"And if the compulsion works on you," Brannoc said, "imagine what it does to a court. Imagine a thousand cups a day, every day, for years. A kingdom drinking obedience with its supper." She rose, pulling her hood up, and for a moment the candlelight — no, the lamplight, we were in the street, but her shadow moved like candlelight — for a moment her shadow bent oddly around her. "No more public roads. No more processions. Tomorrow we go underground, into the archives, and I show you what the Vessel actually is, all of it, every ugly syllable. Because the enemy has just told us it's frightened enough to reach for you gently." Her eyes glinted under the hood. "Gently is the last courtesy it will extend."

We slipped out through the shrine's side arch into the gathering dark. Behind us the city sang its loyalty to a puppet, and in my breast pocket the map pulsed once — slow, deliberate, unmistakable — like a heartbeat answering another heartbeat across the dark.

Pip fell into step beside me and took my hand without asking, the way children do, and whispered:

"It's not trying to make you leave anymore, Ivo. It changed the wine." His grip tightened. "Tonight it started trying to make you *come to it.*"
