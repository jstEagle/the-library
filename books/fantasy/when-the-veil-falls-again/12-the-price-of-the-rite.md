## 12. The Price of the Rite

Sera had learned to read people the way other soldiers read terrain — for ambush points, for the places where the ground gave way. Brannoc was a map she'd been studying since the shrine at the crossroads: every kindness catalogued, every silence noted, every half-truth filed away in the part of her mind that still worked like a blade.

So when the witch sat down across the campfire on their third night out from Corvane and said, "There's something you should know about the rite," Sera was already braced.

Pip was asleep against Ivo's shoulder, the boy's breath slow and even, his fingers curled around a crust of bread he hadn't finished. Ivo himself was half-dozing, firelight moving on his face, and Sera watched him even as she listened to Brannoc, because watching Ivo had become a habit she didn't remember forming — the way she'd once, presumably, watched her sister.

"Tell me," Sera said.

Brannoc turned her hands over the flames. They were old hands, knotted at the knuckles, ink-stained in the creases from years of drawing sigils in ash and blood. "The unbinding isn't a spell you cast. It's a key you turn. The Heartwell was sealed by mages who understood hunger better than they understood mercy. They didn't lock it with iron or wards." She looked up, and her eyes were steady in that terrible way old people's eyes could be steady. "They locked it with a life. A life lived wholly outside magic. Untouched by it. Unshaped by it. The veil itself is made of such a life."

The words went into Sera's chest like cold water into a wound.

She looked at Ivo.

Ivo, who had been born inside the valley stones. Ivo, whose skin magic slid off like rain off oiled leather. Ivo, who had crossed the boundary expecting to die and instead had become the most important thing any of them had ever seen.

"You're saying," Sera heard herself say, very quietly, "that the rite requires him. All of him."

"I'm saying the rite requires what he *is*," Brannoc said. "Whether that means his death, I don't know. The texts are not generous with detail. But yes. The ward must be spent."

"Spent." Sera tasted the word. It was a butcher's word. A word for grain and livestock and soldiers. "You've known this since the crossroads shrine. Since before Corvane. Since you looked at him and recognized—" She stopped, because her memory had a hole where recognition should have been explained, and holes were the one thing she could not abide. "How long?"

"Sera—"

"*How long?*"

The fire cracked. Pip stirred but didn't wake. Ivo opened his eyes.

He didn't say anything. That was the worst part. He just lay there looking between them with those valley-clear eyes, waiting, trusting — trusting Brannoc, trusting Sera, trusting the shape of a family none of them had planned and all of them had stumbled into — and Sera felt something in her go hard and flat, the way it used to go before a breach.

"Since the moment I saw him," Brannoc said at last. "Since I saw the map in his hand and knew what walked out of that valley carrying it."

"And your plan," Sera said, "was to walk him to the Heartwell and feed him to it."

"My plan," Brannoc said, and now her voice had an edge too, old and honed, "was to save a kingdom. You want to know what the Vessel eats, girl? It eats memories. Whole ones. It has eaten a king until there's a smiling puppet left, and it will eat this court, this city, this country, until there is nothing anywhere but its appetite. I have seen what's coming. I owed the crown a debt I have hated every day of my life, and I intended to pay it by giving it the only coin that works." Her jaw tightened. "Yes. I meant to spend him. And I would have carried it, the way we carry everything, alone."

Sera stood up. She didn't decide to; her body simply rose, the old reflexes taking over, hand nowhere near a weapon because the enemy was seventy years old and sitting by a fire, and yet every muscle in her sang with the readiness of violence anyway.

"You don't get to carry it alone," she said. "That's not how this works anymore."

"Sera." Ivo's voice, quiet. "Sit down."

"No." She kept her eyes on Brannoc. "No, because this is exactly the thing, isn't it? This is exactly what they did to us. To me. The Company sold our memories to buy our lives and nobody asked us first. Somebody decided what we were worth and spent us, and I woke up in a ditch with my sister's name gone and everyone telling me I should be grateful to be alive." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it crack; she was done being ashamed of cracks. "You're doing it again. You're doing it to him, and worse — you're doing it while calling yourself his guide. His friend."

Brannoc didn't flinch. Sera wished she would. A flinch would have been human. Instead the witch looked at her with something that might have been respect, and that was almost unbearable.

"You think I haven't told myself all of this?" Brannoc said softly. "Every night since the crossroads. Every night I've lain awake listening to that boy breathe and told myself: this is the arithmetic of salvation. One life weighed against millions. And every night some small dead woman in my memory asks me if I'd do the arithmetic if he were mine." She closed her eyes. "I don't know what I'd do. That's why I told you tonight. Because I am not certain I can trust myself when we reach the gate."

The honesty landed harder than defiance would have. Sera's fists stayed clenched, but the killing edge of her anger dulled into something more dangerous — something that wanted to fix things rather than break them.

"There's another way," she said. "There has to be."

"The texts—"

"The texts were written by mages," Sera said. "Mages who built a prison and hid behind a veil and called it refuge. Mages who bound a hunger under a well instead of destroying it because destroying it was hard. Those same careful, cowardly men wrote down that the seal needed a pure life, and everyone since has read it like scripture." She crouched, bringing her face level with Brannoc's, firelight jumping between them. "But seals fail. Prisoners slip chains. The Vessel got *out* far enough to wear a king. So the seal isn't perfect, which means the rulebook isn't perfect, which means somewhere in that dusty head of yours there's a version of this rite that doesn't end with Ivo as a burnt offering."

For a long moment Brannoc said nothing. Then, slowly, she began to laugh — low, dry, humorless laughter that shook her shoulders like a cough.

"Merciful gods," she said. "A soldier with no past lecturing a witch about the limits of texts." She rubbed her eyes with those ink-stained fingers. "There may be something. An older recension. The rite as it was first attempted, before they simplified it for safety. It doesn't require a whole life." Her gaze flicked toward the sleeping boy and back. "It requires a shared one. Not one soul given — many souls, each giving a portion. Enough of themselves, willingly, that the well can't tell where one person ends and another begins. It was abandoned because it demands absolute trust among the participants, and mages trust no one, least of all each other."

"Absolute trust," Sera repeated.

"It would mean each of you pouring part of yourselves into the working. Memories. Years. Pieces of who you are. And there's no guarantee it holds. There's no guarantee of anything except that Ivo wouldn't stand alone at the edge of it."

Sera sat back on her heels. Around them the night pressed close, pine-dark and star-scattered, the old pilgrim road winding off toward a capital full of hollow-eyed courtiers and a king wearing something ancient like a hat.

She thought about the fragment Ivo had given her back in that alley in Corvane — her sister's laugh, sudden and bright as struck flint, gone again almost immediately but leaving a warmth behind it that she'd been hoarding like a coal ever since. She thought about what she would give to hear that laugh whole. What she would give so that no one else ever woke in a ditch with their name sold out from under them.

She thought: *I already know my answer.*

* * *

Ivo hadn't gone back to sleep. Sera found him sitting up when she returned from checking the pickets, knees drawn to his chest, watching the coals.

"She told you everything," he said. It wasn't a question.

"She told me enough. Then I made her find enough more."

He looked up at her, and there was something in his face she recognized from the ford, from the morning a dying stranger bled silver onto his hands — the look of a man realizing the world was larger and crueler than he'd been raised to believe. "You're angry at her."

"I was."

"And now?"

Sera sat beside him. Up close she could see the fine tremor in his hands that he probably thought he was hiding. Taster's hands. Steady around poison cups, shaking around truth. "Now I'm angry at the world, which is where I keep my spare anger. It stores better there." She nudged his shoulder with hers. "Listen to me, Ivo Brightwater. Nobody spends you. Not the witch, not the crown, not some three-hundred-year-old text written by cowards. If we do this rite, we do it together, or we find another road entirely. That's the whole of the law now. Sworn on whatever I've got left to swear on."

"Why?" The question came out rough. "You met me three weeks ago. You owe me nothing. You came along for your memories, not for me."

Sera considered lying, then remembered she'd promised herself, somewhere between the alley and the road, that she was done with the small dishonesties that kept people safe and alone.

"Because in that alley," she said, "when you touched my arm and gave me back her laugh — even for ten seconds — it was the first time anyone had given me something instead of taking. Not bought. Not traded. Given. I've been following that feeling ever since like a compass needle." She shrugged, uncomfortable with her own honesty, pushing through it anyway. "And because Pip calls you brother in his sleep. And because Brannoc looks at you like a debt she's terrified of paying. And because somewhere between Corvane and here I stopped being a mercenary escorting a mark and started being—" She gestured vaguely at the sleeping boy, the snoring witch, the fire, the dark, all of it. "*This.* Whatever this is. Family, I suppose, if family is the people you'd bleed for without being paid."

Ivo's throat worked. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the fire's whisper. "In the valley, belonging was something you were born into. Walls around you. You never chose it. Out here I thought I'd lost it forever, and instead—" He shook his head. "You'd really give pieces of yourself? For me? Your memories are all you have left of—"

"Exactly," Sera said. "Which is why it counts. Cheap sacrifices are just theater." She managed a grin. "Besides, half my memories are missing anyway. I can afford to spread the rest thin."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

A sound came from the other side of the fire — Pip, awake now, sitting up with his hair wild and his eyes enormous in the ember-light. Children always woke at the worst moments; Sera was starting to suspect they did it on purpose.

"If everybody gives some," Pip said, in the precise, too-old voice he used when he was reciting from the vault of stolen things in his head, "then the well gets confused. It can't find the edges of anybody. That's what the brokers feared about me — that if I mixed the memories together instead of keeping them sorted, they'd stop being sellable. Mixed things lose their price." He hugged his knees, suddenly young again. "Maybe that's the point. Maybe the well can't eat somebody who's all tangled up with other people."

Silence held the camp. Even the coals seemed to lean in.

Out of the mouths of babes and living vaults, Sera thought. She reached over and tugged one of Pip's curls. "Remind me to pay you properly someday."

"I don't take coin," Pip said solemnly. "I take dessert."

* * *

Brannoc joined them at dawn, when the sky was going grey-gold above the tree line and mist lay along the road like spilled milk. She moved slowly, the way she did in the mornings, and she stopped at the edge of the fire ring and stood there a moment like a woman approaching a judgment seat.

"I've been thinking on the old recension all night," she said. "It exists. I can reconstruct it. But understand what you'd be agreeing to, all of you. The well doesn't distinguish between gift and theft once it starts drinking. Willing or not, you will feel pieces of yourselves go. And if the working fails—" she met each of their eyes in turn, "—we will have weakened four people instead of spending one, and gained nothing."

"Then we make sure it doesn't fail," Sera said.

"With what? Hope?"

"With practice," Sera said. "With trust. Which is a thing you build, not a thing you find written down." She stood, brushing pine needles from her knees, and stuck out her hand — a soldier's gesture, blunt and plain. "Swear it, witch. On whatever name you lost. We find another way, or we all go into that working together, equal shares, and you never again make a decision about any of our lives without telling us first. All of us. Even the child. *Especially* the child."

Brannoc looked at the offered hand for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes — relief, maybe, or grief, or the strange cousin of both that lived in people who had carried things alone too long.

"I swear it," she said, and took the hand. Her grip was dry and strong. "On the name I cannot remember, I swear: no more omissions. No more arithmetic done in the dark."

"Good," Sera said. She released the hand and turned to the east, where the road ran on toward the capital, and the mist was beginning to burn away.

Which was when Pip, standing by the horses with the map open in his hands — the map that never stayed folded, the map that whispered street names — went suddenly rigid.

"Sera," he said. His voice had gone thin and high. "Sera, the map changed."

She was at his side in three strides. Brannoc and Ivo a heartbeat behind. The parchment lay flat in the boy's trembling grip, and the ink was moving — streets rearranging themselves like a living thing, the route to the capital redrawing itself, and at the city's heart, where the palace should have been, new letters were surfacing as though rising through water.

Not directions anymore.

Words.

*I see you, taster,* the map wrote, in a hand like frost creeping across glass. *Bring the others. Bring them all. The table is set, and every seat is filled but yours.*
