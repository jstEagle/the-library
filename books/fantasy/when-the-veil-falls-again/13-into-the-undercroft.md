## 13. Into the Undercroft

The palace of Corvane smelled wrong.

Ivo had spent his whole life with his nose in food — the king's breakfasts, the kitchen's stews, the valley's bread and river water — and he had learned that every place had a smell it could not hide. The Veiled Valley smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and, underneath it, nothing at all. Corvane's memory-markets had smelled of copper and lavender and something sweeter beneath, like fruit going soft. But the palace, as they slipped through a servant's gate in the blue hour before dawn, smelled of absence. A clean, swept, scoured absence, like a room where someone had died and the family had burned everything they owned.

"Stay close," Sera murmured. She moved ahead of him the way water moves downhill, without decision, without wasted motion. Her hand rested on the knife at her hip but did not draw it. "The night guard changes at the fourth bell. After that we're walking into men who've forgotten what they're guarding."

"That's supposed to help us?" Ivo whispered.

"It's supposed to make them hesitate." She glanced back at him, and in the dark her eyes were just two pale coins. "Hesitation is a door, if you're quick enough to walk through it."

Pip pressed against Ivo's side, small and warm and trembling slightly. The boy had not slept. None of them had, not truly, since Brannoc had been taken at the Heartwell Gate — since the seals had ground shut behind her and the crown's agents had dragged her away while she shouted at them to *run*, to *not come for her*, words she had never once meant in all the months Ivo had known her. They had run. They had regrouped in the cellar of a shuttered chandlery that Pip remembered from a broker's memory, a room no one alive had cause to recall. And now they had come back anyway, because there was no other way down, and because leaving Brannoc in the Vessel's reach was not a thing any of them could do and still call themselves people.

"You remember the way?" Ivo asked Pip quietly.

Pip nodded. Then, because he was Pip, he said it out loud too, in his thin careful voice: "Down the servants' stair past the laundry ovens. Left at the alcove with the broken saint. Through the wine cellar that isn't a wine cellar. There's a door with no handle." He swallowed. "In the memories I carry, a mason built that door two hundred years ago and then they took the memory of building it out of him so he couldn't be made to talk. He cried for three days. I can show you his hands if you want."

"I believe you," Ivo said, and meant it, and hated how easily he said such terrible things now. *I believe you* — to a nine-year-old holding ten thousand stolen lives inside his skull like water in cupped palms.

They went in through the servants' gate, and the palace swallowed them.

* * *

The first thing Ivo noticed was the dust.

Palaces were not dusty places. Palaces were scoured hourly by armies of servants, and even a palace dying from the top down should have had its floors swept by habit, by ghost-ritual, by the simple momentum of ten thousand unremembered mornings. But here the dust lay along the gallery railings in grey velvet ropes. It furred the arms of the statues. It sat on the banquet tables of the long hall they crossed, on platters laid for a feast that no one had cleared away, and when Ivo passed close he saw that the food was untouched and perfect and utterly without smell.

Food with no smell. His stomach turned over. Twenty years of tasting had taught him that smell was the soul of a dish; a meal without scent was a meal without memory, and something in this palace was eating exactly that.

"The court's been feasting," Sera breathed beside him. "Look at the quantities. This is a coronation spread."

"For a king who hasn't appeared in public in weeks," Ivo said. "Except in procession. Like a puppet on a wire."

They heard the guards before they saw them — boots, unhurried, two men walking a round they had perhaps walked a thousand times or perhaps had only begun walking this morning and would forget before it ended. Sera pulled them into an alcove behind a tapestry gone soft with moth-eaten shadow, and through the gap Ivo watched the patrol pass.

He had expected armored men, hard-faced, alert. What he saw stopped his breath.

Two soldiers in palace livery, spears shouldered, helmets on. Walking correctly. Turning correctly at the correct places. And behind their visors, faces that were simply *empty* — not slack, not sleeping, but vacant in the specific way of a house whose owner has gone out and left the windows open. One of them was humming. Three notes, over and over, a fragment of some song whose rest of it had been eaten. The other walked with one hand resting flat against the wall, fingertips trailing the stone, as if he needed the wall to tell him where he was going.

"They don't know why they fight," Ivo whispered. "Sera, look at them. They don't—"

"I see it." Her voice had gone very quiet, and very flat, which he had learned was the sound of Sera being afraid. "I sold pieces of myself to survive, Ivo. I know what an emptied mind looks like from the inside. Those men aren't guarding anything. They're *placeholders*. Someone wound them up and pointed them at the corridors."

Pip tugged Ivo's sleeve. When Ivo looked down, the boy was staring at the guards with tears running silently down his face.

"I have their memories," Pip said. "Some of them. They came loose — they drift now, like things off a shelf. That one—" he pointed at the humming soldier, already vanishing around a corner, "—his name is Aldous Verrey. He has a daughter who draws horses on the back of his letters. He joined the guard because his father was a guard and his grandfather, and he used to be proud. He was proud right up until about two months ago. Then it starts getting... patchy." Pip wiped his face with his wrist, angry at himself for crying. "There's holes in him shaped like his daughter."

Ivo crouched and put his hands on the boy's shoulders. "Can you hold onto them? The pieces that drift?"

"Some. If I catch them fast." Pip's chin came up, stubborn, the same stubbornness that had let a child live as a vault in the market underlevels without breaking. "That's what I'm for, isn't it? That's what you got me out for. To be the keys."

"To be a boy who helps us," Ivo corrected gently. "The keys are just what you brought with you. There's a difference."

Pip looked at him for a moment, deciding whether to believe it. Then he nodded once, and led them onward through the dust.

* * *

The servants' stair past the laundry ovens was exactly as Pip had described, down to a cracked flagstone on the seventh step that sang a low note under Ivo's boot. The laundry ovens stood cold. A basket of linens sat folded and abandoned, and Ivo noticed — with the particular horror available only to someone who had spent his life among working kitchens — that the folding was flawless. Perfect corners. Perfect stacks. Work done by hands operating on pure habit long after the mind driving them had been hollowed out.

*It's getting worse,* he thought. *Brannoc said it would, once we came close. Once the map came close.* The map rode against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth inside his shirt, warm as a sleeping animal. It had been warm ever since the Heartwell Gate sealed. Warm, and faintly pulsing, like a second heart that was not his own.

Left at the alcove with the broken saint. The saint stood in a niche no bigger than a coffin, its head snapped off centuries ago, its marble hands held out as if in perpetual offering or perpetual surrender. Someone had recently set a candle stub at its feet — fresh wax, a flame still alive. Ivo stared at it.

"Someone's praying," he said. "Here. Now."

"Or someone remembers lighting candles and doesn't remember why," Sera said. She did not slow down.

But Ivo lingered half a heartbeat, looking at the little flame standing straight in the dead air, and thought: *the palace is full of people doing the shapes of their lives without the insides. Lighting candles. Folding linens. Humming three notes of a song.* The Vessel wasn't devouring the court all at once. It was drinking them the way a tick drinks — slowly enough that the body kept moving, kept performing, kept being *shaped* like itself. A kingdom of outlines. A court of traced hands.

And somewhere beneath all of it, wearing a beloved face, the thing in the crown was growing fat.

The wine cellar that wasn't a wine cellar opened off a corridor lined with empty racks. Racks built for bottles, filled instead with — Ivo slowed — jars. Rows upon rows of glass jars, each sealed with black wax, each holding a curl of pale vapor that moved sluggishly when he passed, pressing toward the glass like a moth toward lamplight. Memory, bottled. He'd seen the markets in Corvane sell these by the vial. Here they were stored by the hundredweight. Royal vintage.

"Don't touch them," Pip said sharply, and his voice cracked on it. "Please don't touch them. Some of them are *loud*."

Ivo drew his hand back. As he did, one jar nearest him seemed to shudder, the vapor within coiling once against the glass, and though he heard nothing, felt nothing, he was suddenly certain — the way you are certain in dreams — that whatever was in that jar had just recognized him. Had just *wanted* him.

"Sera."

"I saw it. Move."

Through the false wine cellar, to the door with no handle.

* * *

It stood at the end of a short passage, plain grey stone in a wall of identical grey stone, and it was only visible at all because Pip knew to look for it. No ring. No keyhole. No hinge visible on this side. Just a rectangle of fitted stone with a hairline seam, and carved into the center — so shallow Ivo would have called it a stain if Pip hadn't guided his fingers to it — a mark. A circle crossed by seven lines.

"That's the architect's sign," Pip whispered. "From the memories. The man who built the veil drew it on everything he warded. Doors. Wells. People." The boy swallowed. "On people most of all."

"On me," Ivo said. Not a question. He had seen the mark before — inked on the map, scarred faintly into the skin over his own heart, a birthmark his found-family in the valley had always teased him about. *Our lucky stamp,* Marta the cook used to call it, tapping his chest. He had not yet told them what it meant. He might never get the chance to tell them anything again.

"How does it open?" Sera asked, checking the passage behind them. Her knife was out now, though there had been nothing to fight. "Tell me it doesn't need blood, because I'm out of dramatic gestures for one lifetime."

"It needs remembering," Pip said. "The door is warded against being known. You have to give it a memory it doesn't have." He looked up at Ivo, and in the dark his eyes were enormous. "A true one. Freely given. The mason's notes say — say that the architect believed a thing freely given couldn't be stolen back. So the door only opens for gifts."

Ivo considered the door. Considered the warm weight of the map against his sternum, pulsing like something impatient. Behind them, far above, the palace hummed with its hollow population. Somewhere below his feet — he could feel it now, a pressure in his back teeth, a taste at the root of his tongue like water from a deep well — the Heartwell waited.

"What kind of memory?" he asked.

"A good one," Pip said. "It has to be real and it has to be yours, and—" his voice dropped, apologetic, "—it won't come back. The door eats it. That's the price of the lock."

Silence in the passage. Sera shifted her weight. "Ivo," she started, "we could find another—"

"No," Ivo said. He was surprised how steady his voice came out. "No, it's fine. I know which one."

Because he had been turning the problem over for days, ever since the gate, ever since Brannoc's shout cut off mid-word, and he had already chosen. There was one memory he guarded more carefully than all others, one he returned to on bad nights like a man warming his hands: supper at the long table in the servants' hall, the winter he turned fourteen. Snow against the kitchen shutters. Marta's roast with the crackling done right, and old Tam telling the story of the fish that got away until everyone threw bread at him, and Ivo sitting at the middle of the table, in the middle of the noise, in the middle of belonging — thinking, with the simple dumb astonishment of an orphan, *these are my people, this is my seat, I am home.*

He had thought, in the dark watches, that if the rite demanded everything, that was the memory he would grieve losing. Better to spend it here, on a door, than feed it to a hunger.

He put his palm flat on the stone, over the seven-lined circle, and closed his eyes.

"Marta," he said softly. "Tam. The winter I turned fourteen. The snow on the shutters. The roast. The bread thrown at Tam's head. My seat at the table. I give it freely. Open."

The door drank.

There was no flash, no grinding of ancient mechanisms. There was simply a moment when Ivo reached for the memory and found the shape of it — a table, winter, warmth — with the center gone out of it, the way a word goes strange when repeated too many times. He knew supper had happened. He knew he had loved it. He could no longer feel it. A hole, clean-edged, sat where the best evening of his life had been.

The seam in the stone brightened faintly, then swung inward on hinges he still couldn't see, exhaling air that tasted of deep water and old iron and, beneath both, of *hunger*.

Sera caught his arm. "What did it cost you?"

"An evening I can describe but not feel." He managed something like a smile. "Cheaper than I feared."

She searched his face, unconvinced, but the passage above them stayed silent and there was no time. Pip went first — he always insisted on going first now, into the dark places, because he was the only one who knew what waited in them — and Ivo followed, and the door closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The stairs down were older than everything above. Older than the palace, older than the city, maybe — stone worn in the centers of the steps into smooth green-grey channels, as if a river of pilgrims had flowed this way for a thousand years. The walls bore the seven-lined circle again and again, carved by different hands across different centuries, some crude, some beautiful, all of them the same sign. Warding. Warning. Or perhaps, Ivo thought, watching the marks repeat in the witchlight Sera had bought from a hedge-charmer in Corvane — perhaps all those carvers had been saying the same prayer: *remember this place, whoever comes after. Remember it, even if we don't.*

The pressure grew with every descent. Ivo had crossed the valley boundary once and felt magic return to the world like depth pressing on his ears. This was that feeling multiplied, concentrated, *aimed*. By the twentieth flight his teeth ached. By the thirtieth, the taste in his mouth had sharpened into something almost recognizable — a flavor he catalogued automatically, taster's habit, the way another man would count stairs.

Copper. Sweetness underneath. And something like the smell of a feast with no scent at all.

"It tastes like the wine," he said aloud, and Sera looked back at him sharply.

"The ceremonial wine. From the procession."

"The same. Exactly the same." His blood ran cold. "They weren't poisoning anyone. The whole vintage — the whole *court* — it's all steeped in the same draught. Compulsion. Appetite. Whatever the Vessel is, they've been feeding it through the palace's own cups."

"Not feeding," Pip said quietly, from below. "Seasoning." They stopped. The boy stood on the landing where the stairs ended, before a final archway hung with curtains of chain, and his small face in the witchlight was grey. "That's what the brokers used to say about me. About keeping me hungry and scared. Scared memories taste stronger. The Vessel isn't fattening the court up. It's making them *taste better*."

Beyond the chains, the archway opened into a vast dark, and from that dark came a sound Ivo felt in his bones before he heard it with his ears: a slow, rhythmic drawing-in, like breath, like tide, like the pull at the bottom of a whirlpool. The Heartwell, drinking.

And above it, faint but clear, carried up the last stretch of the stair — a voice. Human. Exhausted. Familiar.

Brannoc, singing. An old hedge-song, the kind sung over cradles and cauldrons, and she was singing it badly, raggedly, the way you sing to keep yourself together — and threaded through her voice, cold and patient and terribly amused, something else was answering.

Sera drew her blade. Pip caught Ivo's hand. And the map against Ivo's heart pulsed once, hard, like a knock on a door — like something saying, at last:

*You're here. Come down. She's been waiting for you.*

Ivo stepped through the chains.
