## 14. The King in the Mirror Room

The corridor beyond the third gate was not a corridor at all.

It had been one once — Pip's borrowed memories said as much, a servants' passage running from the undercroft kitchens to the old queen's apartments, lined with whitewash and hooks for lanterns. But the Vessel's hunger had been chewing at this place the way water chews at stone, and what remained of the passage now curved in ways corridors should not curve. The walls breathed. Not moved — *breathed*, a slow swell and fall beneath the plaster, like the flank of something sleeping too deep to wake.

"Stay close," Sera murmured. She had taken point without discussion; she always took point, as though her body remembered how to be a soldier even if her mind had sold the memory of learning it. Her knife caught the light of Brannoc's witchlight, which burned low and green and made all our shadows look drowned.

Pip walked between me and the wall, clutching my sleeve with both hands. He had not let go since the guards — those hollow-eyed men who had forgotten why they were fighting and so simply stopped — parted to let us pass. "It's loud here," he whispered. "Not sounds-loud. Memory-loud. Everything down here is screaming where nobody can hear."

"I can hear it," I admitted.

I could. It came through my feet more than my ears, a vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. All those stolen memories the brokers had poured into Pip's head — they were only drops. Down here, in the dark beneath the palace, there was a river of them. The Heartwell drank, and everything it drank passed through these walls on its way down, and some of it stuck.

Brannoc stopped at a junction where three passages met, none of them straight. She held up a hand, and in the green light her face looked carved from old wood, patient and weathered and afraid. "The mirror room," she said. "It should be past this. If the architect's map still holds true."

"And if it doesn't hold true?" Sera asked.

"Then we are lost in the belly of the thing we came to kill, and I would suggest we begin apologizing early." She touched the wall with two fingers and closed her eyes. Whatever she felt there made her jaw tighten. "It holds. Barely. The room has been... fed recently."

"Fed?" I asked, though I suspected I did not want the answer.

"A king is not a small meal, taster. Even a hollowed one. Even a reflection of one."

We went left, because the map wanted left, and the walls kept breathing around us, and after a time — I could not say how long, because time down there pooled and stalled like water in a dead channel — the breathing stopped. That was worse. A held breath is the breath before a scream.

Then the passage opened, and there was light.

* * *

It spilled through a doorway ahead of us, silver-white and utterly steady, no flicker, no warmth. Ordinary torchlight dances. This light simply *was*, the way moonlight is, and it fell across the threshold in a hard-edged rectangle like a door laid open onto winter.

Sera raised a fist. We waited. Nothing moved in the light. No footsteps, no voices, no guard challenging us.

"The agents took Pip at the gate," she said quietly. "They'll have rung every bell in the palace by now. There should be soldiers here. There should be *someone*."

"There is someone here," Pip said. His voice had gone very small. "There's someone here, but he isn't coming out."

Brannoc glanced back at him. "What do you see, boy?"

Pip looked up at me, and his eyes were wet. "Him," he said, and pointed at me. "I see him. In the room. Lots of him."

I went first. I don't know that I decided to. My legs decided, and the rest of me followed, the way you follow a smell of bread from your childhood down a street you've never walked.

The mirror room had been a bedchamber once — the proportions were wrong for anything else, and there was a cold hearth, and a bed stripped to its frame, and tall windows bricked over so long ago the mortar had gone black. Every other surface was glass. Floor-to-ceiling mirrors ringed the chamber, dozens of them, set edge to edge so that the room became an unbroken band of silver, and each mirror held a reflection, and not one of them reflected the room.

They reflected a man.

He sat on the floor in the center of the circle, back against nothing, hands open on his knees, and he was perhaps fifty years old, broad through the shoulders, with a beard going grey at the jaw and a face that might once have been handsome in the heavy, honest way of farmers and kings. He wore a plain shirt unlaced at the throat. No crown. Chains ran from iron collars at his wrists and ankles into the walls themselves, sinking into the stone as if the stone had swallowed them.

Every mirror showed him. And every mirror showed him *differently* — or rather, showed him truly, each pane catching a different year of him. In one he was young, laughing, wine-dark hair falling across his forehead. In another he stood on a battlement in armor, older, graver. In a third he knelt at an altar. In a fourth he held a newborn wrapped in white, and the look on his face was the look I had seen on Tam's face in the kitchen when his daughter gripped his thumb for the first time — terror and wonder in equal measure, love so large it looked like grief.

In every reflection, the man was speaking. Their mouths moved soundlessly, dozens of years of words, all silent, all at once.

And the real man — the one chained in the center — watched them. Watched himself. Had been watching himself, I understood with a sickness that settled into my bones, for a very long time.

"He can't see us," Sera breathed behind me. She'd come through the door with her knife low, scanning corners trained into muscles that remembered war better than she did. "Look at his eyes. He's looking straight through."

"No," Pip said. "He sees us. He just doesn't believe we're real anymore. That's different."

The man's head turned.

Slowly, with the care of someone who has learned that fast movements bring pain, he looked toward the doorway. Toward me. And his eyes — brown, ordinary, human, so painfully human after the cold flicker of the thing that wore his face above — fixed on mine and widened.

"You're new," he said. His voice was rust and dust, a voice that had not been used for anything but silence in years. "You're new. You're — " He stopped. He laughed, one dry note, terrible. "No. No, you're not new. You're one of them. You're a trick. You come in new shapes sometimes. Once you came as my mother. You got the laugh wrong." His face crumpled and reassembled itself, and I saw the discipline underneath, the king's discipline holding a ruined man together seam by seam. "You get the laugh wrong," he repeated, quieter. "Everyone gets the laugh wrong."

"I'm not a trick," I said. I stepped into the room, and the light lay on me like cold water. "My name is Ivo Brightwater. I'm a taster. From the Veiled Valley."

The name landed in him like a stone in a well. I watched it fall, watched the ripples spread. His mouth opened. Closed. When he spoke again, his voice shook.

"The valley." He said it the way a drowning man says *shore*. "You're from the valley. Then the map found someone. After all these years, the map finally — " He surged forward and the chains caught him, snapped him back, and he didn't even notice, he was straining toward me against the iron with tears cutting tracks through the dust on his face. "Did you read it? Do you know what it says? Do you know what's *wearing me?*"

"We know," Brannoc said from the doorway. She had entered at last, witchlight cupped in her palm, and the mirrors drank its green glow and gave back only the man. "Your Majesty. Aldric the Third, called Aldric the Beloved. We know about the Vessel. We know about the Heartwell. We've come to unbind you."

For a moment he simply stared at her. Then he began, horribly, to smile — a smile of such naked hope that I had to look away, because hope like that on a face like his was worse than despair.

"Unbind," he echoed. "Yes. Yes, that's the word. That's the word I used to say, in the first year, before I learned to stop saying words it could hear." He sagged back against his chains. "Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I don't know how much time you have, and I know less. The thing in my crown — it doesn't wear my face above. It wears my *memories*. Everything it takes from me, it uses. It knows how I loved my wife, so it loves as I loved. It knows how I forgave, so it forgives. The court looks up at their beloved king and every warmth they see is stolen goods." His hands closed into fists on his knees. "It keeps me here to keep me *fresh*. A larder. It feeds on me slowly so the vintage stays good, and when I am nearly empty it will take the last of me, and then it will need a new king, and there will be no one who remembers that the old one ever existed, because it will have eaten that too."

"How long?" Sera asked. Her voice was flat, controlled, but I saw her knuckles white around the knife. "How long has it had you?"

The king considered. "I don't know," he said. "That's the cruelty of it. It eats backwards. My daughter — I had a daughter — I remember loving her and I cannot remember her face, and I know that once I knew her face, and I know that knowing it was taken, and I cannot even grieve properly because grief requires the shape of what's lost." He looked up at us, and his eyes went around our circle, cataloguing: the soldier, the witch, the boy, the taster. "You're four. The rite needs five, or it needed five when the mages wrote it. They were cowards with numbers."

"Four is enough," Brannoc said, "if one of the four is what this one is." Her chin tipped toward me, and I felt the king's gaze swing to me like a lamp.

"What is he?" the king asked softly.

"That's a long story," I said. "And I only know the half of it myself."

* * *

While Brannoc examined the seals along the walls — the chains did not end in locks; they ended in wards, sigils cut into the stone and packed with something that glimmered like powdered memory — the king talked. I think he would have talked until his throat bled. Years of silence had backed up behind his teeth like floodwater behind a dam.

His name was Aldric. He had been crowned at nineteen. He had loved a woman named Maerin who died birthing their daughter, and he had named the daughter Maerin too, and the daughter had died at seven of a fever that the court physicians swore had been natural, and he had begun to suspect otherwise, and that suspicion — that little stubborn coal of *wrongness* — had led him to the archives, and the archives had led him to the Heartwell, and the Heartwell had led him here.

"They let me find it," he said. His voice had gone quiet, the flood receding. "I understand that now. It wanted to be found by a king. What does a prison want more than a jailer with a crown? It let me descend. It let me stand at the well. And then it stepped out of the well wearing my reflection, and it put its hand on my face, and it said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It said, *thank you for carrying me this far. I'll carry myself now.*"

Pip made a small sound. I put my hand on his shoulder and felt him trembling.

"It took the throne in a day," the king went on. "A day! Twenty-two years of ruling and it needed a day, because it already had everything — my memories, my mannerisms, the exact weight of my signature and the precise warmth of my greeting. No one doubted. How could they doubt? It was more me than I was. It had my whole life to draw on and I had..." He gestured at the mirrors. "Reflections."

"Why keep you alive at all?" Sera asked. "Why not finish you?"

"Because finished things spoil." He smiled, and there was no humor in it, only a bitter precision. "It drinks best from living memory. A dead man's past goes flat. So it keeps me, and it feeds me scraps — a taste of my daughter's laugh, a glimpse of my wedding day — the way a farmer waters stock. And then it drinks again. Round and round. I have drunk my own life so many times it's gone thin as broth."

He looked at me then, directly, and something in his expression changed. Sharpened. The king surfaced through the ruined man.

"You taste," he said. "You said. A taster."

"All my life."

"Then taste the air in this room and tell me what you find."

I blinked. "Majesty?"

"Humor a hollow king, taster. Breathe deep and tell me what you find in it. Because I have wondered for years, and I have no tongue for it anymore, and you do."

So I did. I drew a slow breath through my nose, the way I'd drawn ten thousand breaths over ten thousand dishes, sorting poison from spice, nightshade from nutmeg, the subtle from the obvious. The room smelled of cold stone and old glass and, underneath, a faint sweetish rot — the smell of the walls, of the hunger soaked into them.

And under *that*. Something else. Something familiar in a way that made my stomach drop.

"Compulsion," I said slowly. "Faint. Old. Like — like a dose that's been sitting in a dish too long, gone stale. It's in the air. It's in the light." I looked at the mirrors, at the dozens of silent kings mouthing their soundless years. "It's in the reflections. They're not just showing him his past. They're dosing him. Every day, every hour, a little — "

"Obedience," the king finished. "Yes. So that when it comes to collect the last of me, I will walk to the well myself, smiling, grateful. It wants my ending to be willing. Willing surrender tastes sweeter, I imagine. I wouldn't know. I have never once been willing." His fists tightened. "But it has time. It has all the time in the world, and I have less every day, and every day I am less able to tell which of my thoughts are mine and which are its leavings. Some days I catch myself hoping it comes soon. Hoping for the peace of being finished." His voice cracked and he mastered it, barely. "Do you understand what I am telling you?"

I did. Gods help me, I did.

"I need you to promise me something," the king said. "All of you. But especially you, taster, because you're the one the map chose, and the map was drawn by the man who built this prison, and architects know where the doors are." He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, and the light of Brannoc's witchlight caught the wet on his cheeks. "When you go down to the Heartwell — and you must go down, there is no other road, I know the shape of the rite even if they've eaten the details — you may find that saving me and killing me are the same act wearing two faces. The binding runs through me. It may be that the knot cannot be cut without cutting the rope. It may be that the only way to free the kingdom from the thing in my crown is to free me *from my body*."

"Majesty—" Brannoc started.

"*Promise me.*" The word cracked out of him like a whip, and every mirror-king in the room fell silent at once, dozens of mouths stopping mid-word, dozens of faces turning — turning, though reflections cannot turn — to look at us with his eyes. For one horrible heartbeat the whole room was him, multiplied, begging. "I have been eaten alive for years in a room full of my own ghost. I have begged every night for either rescue or an ending, and I no longer know which I want, and that is the deepest wound it has given me — it has made me unable to know my own heart. So I ask you. If it comes to it. If the choice is the kingdom or me. Choose the kingdom. And do not carry guilt for it, because I am choosing it now, while I still own enough of myself to choose." He sagged. The mirrors released, returning to their silent years. "Promise me, taster. You, whose whole life has been tasting death in small doses so others need not die. Let me be the last dish. Let me be the one meal that was worth the tasting."

The silence rang. Pip was crying silently against my side. Sera had turned her face away, jaw working. Even Brannoc, who hid debts the way other people hid coins, had gone still and pale.

And I — I stood in the cold silver light with a dying king's request in my hands like a live coal, and I thought of the stranger at the ford pressing a blood-marked map into my palms, and of my family in the valley eating supper without me, and of Sera trading memories, and Pip holding thousands of lives in his small head, and Brannoc walking toward a debt she never explained.

Everyone I had met since crossing the stones had been paying for someone else. Everyone had been a dish set before a hunger larger than themselves.

"I won't promise to kill you," I said at last. My voice came out rough. "I'm sorry. I know what you're asking. I know why. But I've spent my whole life standing between people and poison, Your Majesty, and I won't stand here and call you poison. If there's a road that saves both the kingdom and the king, I'll find it. If there isn't — then I'll carry the choice, and I'll carry the weight of it, but I won't make it easy for myself by promising it to you in advance."

The king stared at me. And then, slowly, he laughed — really laughed, a cracked and rusty and unmistakably *human* sound, and this time I did not look away.

"The valley sends me a taster with a stubborn tongue," he said, wiping his eyes. "Good. Good. Stubbornness is the only seasoning the Vessel can't digest. Keep yours sharp, Ivo Brightwater. Keep it sharp until the very—"

He stopped.

Every mirror went dark at once.

Not dimmed — *emptied*, silver flooding to black, and in the sudden dark the faint sweetish rot in the air thickened until I could taste it on my tongue, and far below us, down through the breathing walls and the drowned corridors, something vast turned over in its sleep and noticed, for the first time in years, that guests were standing in its pantry.

Above us, muffled by stone, a bell began to ring. Then a second bell. Then all of them.

"They know," Sera said, knife already drawn, moving to the door. "They know exactly where we are."

And from the black mirrors, from every empty pane at once, a voice like honey poured over broken glass:

*"Bring me the taster."*

The door at the far end of the mirror room — the door that had been sealed brick an hour ago — swung open onto lamplight, and ranks of the palace guard filled it, hollow-eyed and silent, and at their head stood a woman in crown livery holding a coil of chain, and she was smiling with the king's own smile.

She looked straight at me.

"You're expected, taster," she said.
