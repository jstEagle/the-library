## 17. The Bargain of Small Mercies

Sera had sold things before. She knew the shape of it — the way a seller's hands went still on the counter, the way the broker's smile never quite reached the eyes, the small hollow click when a vial sealed and something that had lived in your head for years became merchandise. She had watched soldiers do it in the war camps, trading whole years of themselves for bread and passage out of the fighting. She had done worse than watch.

But she had never sold *Maren*.

She stood in the back room of Vessel-and-Vial with her palms flat on the counter, because if she didn't anchor them to something they would shake, and she would not shake in front of a broker. Not this one. Not any of them.

The broker's name was Odile Fen, and until three weeks ago she had been a memory-dealer of the Corvane underlevels, one of the jackals Sera had spent six years learning to avoid. Now she wore crown livery under her merchant's coat — grey silk at the collar, the king's tower stitched in thread so fine it looked like frost — because the court was eating itself alive from the inside and even jackals could smell which side needed feeding. The palace undercroft had swallowed Pip and Brannoc behind the Heartwell gate two nights ago. The gate answered to locks older than the kingdom, and the locks answered to keys that were not metal at all.

"They were memories once," Odile said, turning a vial of pale amber light between her fingers. "The gate-keys. The architect poured them into the market centuries ago, scattered them like seed corn, so no one hand could ever gather enough to open his door. The crown has spent generations buying them back." She set the vial down. "We have most of them. We lack one."

"And you think I'm carrying it," Sera said.

"I think the girl who walked into my shop four years ago and asked me to take her war out of her head is standing in front of me asking about Heartwell gates, and I think nothing walks into that gate without paying the architect's toll." Odile's eyes were the color of old pewter, patient as water wearing down stone. "I think you know what's missing, Sergeant Vael. You've known since you saw the gate."

Behind Sera, Ivo shifted. She heard him breathe in — that careful, tasting breath he did, the taster's habit of taking the air apart before trusting it — and she knew he'd caught it too. The shape of what Odile wasn't saying.

*Don't,* she thought at him. *Don't do the arithmetic yet.*

"I want to see the key first," Sera said. "All of them. Laid out."

"Of course."

Odile drew a long tray from beneath the counter and unlatched its lid. Inside, nested in black velvet, lay perhaps forty vials, each glowing faintly, each a different color of remembering: rose-gold and storm-grey, green as summer shade, blue as deep river. Memory-light. Sera had seen a thousand vials in her life and had never once gotten used to the way they seemed to look back.

And there, third row, near the end, one vial burned different from all the rest. Not amber. Not gold. It glowed the particular warm red-brown of late afternoon through a kitchen window, of woodsmoke and honey, of a voice singing slightly flat because the singer never cared about the note, only the song.

Sera's mouth went dry.

"That one," Odile said softly, "came to us eleven days ago. From a crown agent who took it off a captured courier near the pilgrim road. The provenance record says it was surrendered voluntarily by its owner some years past, in partial payment of a debt." Her pewter eyes lifted. "It has been very difficult to sell. Buyers find it too — whole. Too much of a piece. Grief that clean frightens people."

"It's not grief," Sera heard herself say. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Somewhere far away, the part of her that was still a soldier noted with detached interest that her hands had stopped shaking entirely, gone cold and steady as drawn steel. "Grief is what you have after. That's just — her. That's Maren."

* *

They had given her a room above the shop, because Odile understood negotiation the way Ivo understood poison: slowly, in measured doses, with courtesy. Sera sat on the edge of a narrow bed and held the vial without opening it, though Odile had told her she could. One unstoppered breath and she could taste her sister again — literally taste it; memory worked that way for some people, and Sera had always been one of them. Maren had smelled of rosemary oil and horse sweat and the cheap sugar sweets she bought off camp followers and hid in her boot heels.

Six years ago, in the mud outside Cael Ford, with the Broken Company broken and the ambush closing like a trap of teeth, Sera had walked into a broker's tent and said: *take the war.* And the broker, an honest jackal by trade, had said: *the war isn't one thing, sergeant. It's ten thousand things braided together. I can't cut the war out without cutting the braid.*

So she had let him cut. And Maren had come out with the rest — every memory that touched the war, and Maren touched everything, Maren had been in the company since Sera was fifteen, Maren had taught her how to hold a shield, how to swear properly, how to laugh the night before a battle so fear didn't get a foothold — Maren had come out in a dozen cuts, and Sera had paid only for the smallest one to be returned: a fragment, a single shard, her sister laughing in the rain at some joke Sera no longer had the context for. She had carried that shard for six years like a coal banked in ash. It was why she'd joined them. It was why, when Ivo's hand closed over hers in that alley and the fragment flooded back whole and burning, she had stayed.

Now the rest of Maren sat in a velvet tray downstairs, and the gate to the Heartwell wanted a key, and the key was memory freely given — the architect's little joke, or maybe his mercy: the door would only open for someone who gave up something they loved more than the thing behind it.

A knock. Two soft raps, then a pause, then one more. Ivo's knock. He'd invented it their second week on the road, a code so Pip could tell his footsteps from a stranger's.

"Come in."

He slipped inside and stood there a moment, uncertain, a loaf of shop-bread in his hands and that look on his face — the taster's look, the one that weighed everything before swallowing anything. He was getting better at the world outside the valley. He was still hopeless at waiting.

"You've been upstairs four hours," he said.

"Yes."

"Odile showed Pip the tray." He set the bread down on the sill, unnecessarily, the way people do when their hands need a job. "Pip says one vial is different from the others. He says—" Pip remembered everything, always, thousands of stolen memories stacked in his skull like cordwood; Pip read rooms the way other people read weather. "He says it's yours. He says it's been yours a long time."

Sera turned the vial in her fingers. The red-gold light slid along her knuckles.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "Maren told me that love wasn't a feeling. She said feelings were weather. Love was a decision you made every morning, like standing watch. You chose the person. Rain or shine, you chose them." She looked up. "I chose her every morning for twenty-two years, Ivo. Then the war got bad, and choosing her started costing me things I needed to survive, and I stopped. I put her in a bottle and I walked away, and I told myself I'd buy her back someday when the fighting was done." Her thumb found the seam of the stopper. "That was the day I stopped being someone worth following."

Ivo crossed the room and sat beside her on the bed. He didn't touch her. He'd learned that about her early — that comfort landed better beside than against.

"The gate needs a key," he said quietly.

"The gate needs a *sacrifice* dressed up as a key. Freely given. That's what Odile said. That's what the architect built into his own door, the sentimental bastard." Sera laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what's funny? If Brannoc were here she'd already be lecturing me about prices. About how nothing given freely is ever free, it's just a debt you owe yourself. She'd hate this. She'd be furious at me for even considering—"

Her voice went out like a snuffed candle.

Ivo waited. Then, carefully: "Considering what?"

"Trading." She made herself say it. Made herself hold the vial up between them where the light couldn't hide. "Not just this one. All of it. Whatever's left of her in my head. The gate wants freely given memory — real memory, living memory, not these bottled dead things. Odile says a vial can prime the lock but a person has to turn it. Someone has to stand at the gate and give." She met his eyes. "I'm the only one of us with something left worth giving that I can spare. You need your ward-nature. Pip needs his memories — they're not even his, we promised the brokers he keeps only what's given. And you—" her voice roughened, "you're going to need every hour of your own life where you're going. So it's me. It was always going to be me, from the moment they took Brannoc and I did the sums in that corridor and hated every number."

"No," Ivo said.

"Sergeant's orders, recruit."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again, and his voice had that quality it got sometimes, low and absolutely certain, the voice of a man who had tasted nightshade and knew exactly what death smelled like. "You told me once that you joined us to hunt down what you lost. That Maren's laugh was the last true thing you owned. I watched it come back to you in that alley. I felt it — your hand under mine, and suddenly there was rain and a joke and someone loving you so hard it leaked out of your skin into mine." His jaw tightened. "I will not stand at a gate and watch you give that away. There has to be another way. There's always—"

"There isn't always another way, Ivo." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "You of all people should know. Your whole life was a bargain somebody else struck — a valley traded for a child, a child traded for a kingdom's hiding place. Nobody found another way for you. People just decided what you were worth, and you lived with the bill." She stood, because sitting next to him while she said this felt like a cruelty. "The difference is that this time, the person doing the deciding is me. That's the whole point. That's the only mercy in it."

* *

Odile's back room, an hour before dawn. Candlelight. The tray of vials open on the counter like a field of small strange stars.

There were procedures. Of course there were; brokers loved procedure the way priests loved liturgy, both professions built on making the unbearable orderly. Odile produced a contract written on memory-paper — paper that recorded not words but the giving itself, so that the transaction could never be disputed or undone. Sera read every line. Old habit. Read the contract, check the exits, count your blades.

*The undersigned does freely surrender all remaining recollections pertaining to Maren Vael of the Broken Company...*

There it was. Her sister's name in a stranger's contract, reduced to a clause.

"One condition," Sera said.

Odile inclined her head, gracious as a spider.

"When it's done — when the gate's open and whatever happens down there happens — nobody tells me what I gave up. No summaries. No 'she was your sister and she loved you.' Nothing." Sera laid her hand flat on the counter, steady as a plank. "If you hand me back a shape instead of a person, I'll spend the rest of my life trying to pour myself into it and hating everyone who remembers what fit there. I've seen men do it. I've buried men who did it. Promise me silence."

For the first time, something moved behind Odile's pewter eyes — not pity, Sera was sure it wasn't pity, brokers didn't stock pity — but recognition, maybe. The look of one professional honoring another's terms.

"You have my word," Odile said. "Though I'll note, for the record, that silence is the cruelest term I've ever been asked to keep."

"Cruelty's just mercy with the details removed," Sera said. "Ask anyone in the Broken Company."

Ivo and Pip stood by the wall. Pip had cried earlier, silently, enormous tears rolling down a face that held ten thousand griefs not his own; now he simply watched, and in his watching was a strange solemnity, the gravity of a boy who had seen every kind of memory pass through human hands and knew precisely how rare it was to see one given instead of taken.

Ivo hadn't argued further. He'd tried — one last time, in the corridor, quietly, desperately reasonable — and Sera had put her hand on the back of his neck, the way Maren used to put hers on hers, and said: *This is what family means. It means someone pays so you don't have to. Take the gift, recruit. Taking it is half of giving it.* And he had gone still under her palm, and then he had nodded, slow, like a man accepting a wound.

Now he stepped forward as Odile unstoppered the first vial — the priming vial, the captured courier's prize, Maren's bottled afternoons — and said, "Wait."

Odile paused.

"He carries what she loses." Ivo's voice was steady. "That's the arrangement. That's the condition. Whatever leaves her tonight — I carry it. Not in a bottle. In me." He looked at Sera, and his eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "You told me belonging isn't being kept. Well, memory isn't being kept either, if someone else holds it for you. I'm the valley's ward. I was built to hold things. Let me hold this."

Sera opened her mouth to refuse — pride rising like a blade coming up on guard — and found she couldn't. Because underneath the pride was the truth she'd admitted on the bed: that she was terrified, not of losing Maren, but of the world losing her, of twenty-two years of a laughing woman evaporating into broker inventory, filed and forgotten. And here was this boy — this impossible, poisoned, brave boy — offering to be the ground she could fall into.

"If you ever tell me," she managed, "even a hint, even her favorite color—"

"I won't."

"—then I'll never forgive you."

"I know." Almost a smile. "Sergeant."

Odile, watching them with her pewter patience, murmured, "Unorthodox," and began.

* *

It did not hurt. That was the obscenity of it. Sera had expected pain, had braced for pain the way you brace before a surgeon's knife, and instead it was warmth — a slow tide going out, gentle as sleep, memories loosening like petals from a stem. She stood with her hands on the counter and felt Maren leave her in pieces:

Rain on tent canvas, and laughter, slightly flat, off-key, perfect.

Rosemary oil. Horse sweat. Sugar hidden in a boot heel.

*Stand straighter, little bird, the shield doesn't respect slouching.*

The morning Maren cut Sera's hair short before her first muster and called her handsome and meant it.

The last morning, actually the last one — the one Sera hadn't known was last, the one where Maren had complained about the porridge and stolen Sera's cloak pin and said *see you at dusk* —

—and then dusk, and the ambush, and the braid cut clean—

No. That was war-memory, long sold, long gone; there was only the absence where it had been, a socket in her mind, and now the socket itself was filling with soft warm nothing, and Maren was going out with the tide, all of her, every morning-choice, twenty-two years of *I choose you*, draining gently into a boy standing at her shoulder with his eyes closed and his fists clenched, receiving.

She heard herself make a sound. Small. Animal. She was distantly ashamed of it and refused to stop.

When it was done, the counter swam. Someone steadied her — Ivo, his arm around her ribs, holding her up the way you hold a comrade off a wall after battle. His face was streaming. He was crying her tears for her, carrying them already, first installment on a debt she'd ordered him never to mention.

"Well?" she rasped. "Is the contract satisfied?"

Odile was sealing the last vial — red-gold, kitchen-warm, singing faintly, impossibly, with a melody slightly flat. The broker's hands, Sera noticed, were not quite steady. Brokers' hands were always steady.

"The contract is satisfied," Odile said quietly. "The key is primed. And sergeant — the gate at the undercroft opens at moonrise. The crown's agents will meet you at the Salt Door." She hesitated, then added, against all professional instinct: "Whoever she was, she raised a woman worth selling a kingdom's door for. Forgive me. You asked for silence, and I've already broken a corner of it. Consider the rest a debt."

Sera looked at her own reflection in the dark window glass. A tall woman, scarred, steady-eyed. A soldier. Around her heart, where twenty-two years of Maren had lived, there was now a clean bright emptiness, like snowfield, like a room swept bare, like the space after a bell.

She felt nothing where her sister had been.

That was the worst of it. Not pain. Room.

"Right," she said, and heard her voice come out level, cold, serviceable — the voice she'd had before the alley, before the rain-laugh, before Ivo's hand. She picked up her sword belt and buckled it with hands that did not shake, because there was nothing left in her that knew how to shake about this.

"Pip. Get the map. Recruit—" she turned to Ivo, and for a moment something flickered across her face, some ghost reaching for a name it no longer owned and finding only the shape of trust, "—recruit, we move at moonrise. We're getting our witch back."

Ivo wiped his face with his sleeve and nodded, and pressed his hand briefly over his own chest — over the place, she supposed, where her sister now lived, banked like a coal in ash, kept safe for a woman who would never ask about her because she no longer knew to ask.

The moon was rising over Corvane as they went down toward the Salt Door, and Sera walked in front, because that was what sergeants did, and behind her she could feel her family falling into step — and if some hollow chamber in her ached at a rhythm she couldn't name, a cadence like laughter slightly off-key, she marched through it, and did not look back.

Ahead, the palace undercroft waited with its sealed gate, its hungry dark, and whatever Brannoc had become on the far side of it.
