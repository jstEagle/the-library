## 18. The Heartwell

The stairs went down longer than the palace had height.

Ivo counted at first — a taster's habit, counting everything, because counting kept the hands busy and the mind from wandering — but he lost the number somewhere past four hundred, and after that he simply walked. The steps were black stone worn smooth as river glass, and they descended in a spiral so wide that twenty men could have marched abreast, though no army had ever come this way. The walls bore no torches. They didn't need to. Something below was *glowing*, a pale gold light that seeped up through the dark like dawn leaking under a door, and it grew with every turn of the stair.

Pip walked ahead of him, one small hand trailing the wall. He hadn't spoken since the gate sealed behind them with Brannoc on the wrong side of it. His eyes, when Ivo caught them in the glow, held too many things at once — thousands of borrowed memories stacked behind a child's face, and among them, Ivo knew now, the ones that belonged to Ivo himself.

"You could give them back," Ivo said quietly. "Whenever you want. I'm not asking."

"I know." Pip's voice came out small and steady. "That's why I can carry them right."

Sera brought up the rear, sword drawn even though there had been nothing to fight for an hour. Her boots made almost no sound on the stone. Every hundred steps or so she glanced back up the spiral, toward the sealed gate, toward Brannoc, and her jaw tightened each time as if she were swallowing something with edges.

"She'll find another way down," Ivo said.

"Maybe." Sera turned forward again. "Or maybe she finished what she came to finish. She got you past the gate, didn't she? That was always the shape of her debt." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "I just keep hoping we're all wrong about her. I've been wrong about people before. It cost me pieces of myself learning it."

The stair ended without warning.

They stepped off the last step into a cavern so vast that Ivo's mind refused its dimensions. No mason had shaped it; no water had carved it. The walls curved inward like the inside of a throat, ribbed with veins of that same golden light, and the air tasted — Ivo's tongue knew before his mind did — like standing in a kitchen where a feast has gone cold. Richness everywhere, and underneath it, rot.

And at the center: the well.

It was not deep, as wells went. Perhaps thirty feet across, ringed by a lip of white stone carved with characters Ivo couldn't read and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face from a dream. The liquid inside did not move. It did not reflect. It was silver-grey and utterly still, like mercury, like the blood of the woman at the ford, like a mirror that had forgotten how to hold a face. And rising from its surface, drifting upward in slow spirals, came motes of light — thousands of them, tens of thousands — each one flickering faintly with images if you looked too long. A woman laughing in a garden. A soldier burying a friend. A child's first snow. Memories, dissolved. The kingdom's currency, spent into this bottomless account, feeding whatever drank here.

Above the well, floating where no man could stand, hung the crown.

It turned slowly, catching the golden light, and it was not ornate — that was the horror of it. It was plain. Iron-banded, workmanlike, the kind of crown a practical king might wear to a harvest festival. And seated within its circlet, visible only when the light shifted, was a suggestion of a face. Not a face itself. A *hunger* wearing the memory of a face, the way a glove holds the shape of a hand long after the hand is gone.

"It's beautiful," Pip whispered.

"Don't look directly at it," Sera said, and raised her sword anyway, which Ivo understood: some part of her remembered enough to be afraid, even if she couldn't name what of.

Then the crown spoke.

It did not open a mouth. The words arrived inside their skulls the way taste arrives on the tongue — instantly, intimately, uninvited.

***Three.*** A pause, savoring. ***The witch's debt is paid. The soldier's purse is nearly empty. And the little vault walks in carrying ten thousand keys. How thoughtful of you all to deliver yourselves.***

"Where is the king?" Ivo called out. His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated himself for it, and kept talking anyway. "The real king. What did you do with him?"

***Kept him.*** The motes above the well swirled faster. ***He is the best meal I have ever been given. Sixty years of royal memory, poured out drop by drop, and still he produces more. Grief makes such rich broth. He remembers his daughter's face every morning, you know. I take it every night. By morning he has grown a new one. We have an arrangement, he and I.***

"That's not an arrangement," Ivo said. "That's farming."

***Yes,*** said the thing in the crown, with something horribly like warmth. ***You would understand. You were farmed too, little ward. Raised in a pen behind a veil, fed on the king's own table, seasoned against poison your whole life so that you would arrive here... intact. Do you think it was accident? The valley, the taster's post, the map placed in a dying stranger's hands? You were grown for this table as surely as any pig for any feast.***

The words hit harder than Ivo expected, because they landed on ground already broken — Pip's memory, the parents walking into the mist carrying him, the valley that was never home but *shape*. He felt Sera shift beside him, angling herself between him and the well without being asked.

"You're lying," Sera said flatly. "It talks because lies are cheaper than fighting us. I've met officers like that."

***Have you?*** Amusement rippled through the light. ***And yet you sold your sister to survive, and regret nothing loudly enough to be believed—***

Sera moved. One stride, blade up — and Ivo caught her arm. "Don't," he said. "It wants that. It eats anger too, I'd bet my life on it." Because that was what he knew, in the end, better than anyone alive: hunger, and the shape of it, and the way it dressed itself up in other names.

He stepped toward the well.

The motes parted around him like a crowd making way.

"Ivo," Pip said sharply — and then, softer, in the voice of someone much older than nine: "The circle. You need the circle. Brannoc drew it for you before the gate. You remember?"

He did. Of course he did — Brannoc, half-consumed already by the time the crown agents took her, her left arm grey and fading like old ink, pressing chalk into his palm in the last seconds before the gate closed. *"Chalk's just a promise made visible, boy. The rite doesn't need magic. It needs a life that never touched it. Draw the circle. Stand in it. Speak the unbinding. And whatever it offers you —"* she had smiled then, a terrible smile with most of her already gone — "*whatever it offers, remember that you were loved by people who chose to love you. That's the one coin it cannot counterfeit."*

Now, at the well's edge, Ivo knelt and drew.

The chalk line came out of him more than out of the chalk. Each stroke burned faintly gold and held, and the characters from the well's rim — the ones he couldn't read — rose up in his memory one by one, and his hand copied them without consulting him. Sera stood at his shoulder, blade between him and the crown. Pip stood opposite, and from the boy's mouth, quiet as a lullaby, came fragments of Brannoc's voice — memories she had given him freely, once, in some camp on some road — feeding him the next phrase whenever Ivo faltered. The circle closed. The light in it settled like flour in a sieve.

***Stop,*** said the Vessel.

For the first time, it sounded afraid.

Ivo stood in the center of his own name, and spoke the first word of the unbinding.

The world *tilted*.

Not physically. Something deeper than physical — as if the whole cavern were a bowl of water and someone had lifted one edge. The well's surface shivered for the first time in centuries. The motes stopped their slow spiral and hung suspended, each tiny scene frozen mid-flicker, and Ivo felt the attention of the thing in the crown swing around and fix upon him with the full weight of a starving ocean.

***You do not know what you are unbinding,*** it said, and the intimacy of it, the way it slid beneath his skin, made him gag. ***Let me show you. Let me give instead of take, just once. Just once, taster. Taste THIS.***

The hunger turned toward him.

There was no other way to describe it. All that vast, patient appetite — sixty years of a kingdom's memories, an age of the world's dissolved hours, everything it had ever swallowed — rotated like a lens focusing sunlight and came to rest on Ivo Brightwater, standing in a chalk circle at the bottom of the world.

And it began to pour itself into him.

Not pain. Worse than pain. *Fullness.* Images crashed through him in floods — a coronation, a wedding, a plague year seen from a throne, a mother's voice singing in a language dead a thousand years, a battle fought before the valley stones were ever raised, the mages themselves young and frightened and building the veil around something exactly like *this* — and underneath it all, threading through every memory like a spine, the hunger itself, patient and enormous and *so tired*, so endlessly, catastrophically tired—

*Ivo.* Pip's voice, far away. *IVO.*

He was on his knees. The chalk circle was glowing so bright it cast shadows upward. Blood — red, ordinary, his own — dripped from his nose onto the white stone. And still the flood came, because the Vessel had learned something in this instant, something that changed everything:

It could not drink his memories. He had so few, and those guarded by a lifetime of ordinariness. But it could feel what he was. The ward-nature. The valley's shape living in his bones. Magic's perfect absence — and absence, to a creature made entirely of taking, was the one flavor it had never tasted.

***Oh,*** it breathed, and the sound was obscene in its tenderness. ***Oh, you are HUNGER'S OPPOSITE.***

It began to pull.

Ivo felt his edges softening. Not dying — dissolving. The boundary of himself going the way the veil was going, the way everything bound was going, his hands growing faintly translucent in the golden light, and distantly, absurdly, he thought: *the king's soup needs less salt, Marta always adds too much thyme, someone will have to tell them*—

The second word of the unbinding rose in his throat.

He spoke it through the flood.

The circle detonated with light. The motes above the well screamed — actually screamed, ten thousand small voices remembering themselves at once — and the well's still surface finally, finally *broke*, rings of silver spreading outward as if something below had surfaced after drowning for an age.

And through that breaking light, up from the depths of the well, something began to rise.

* * *

It wore the king's body.

That was the worst of it — not a monster, not a shape out of nightmare, but a man. Tall, grey-bearded, dressed in the plain clothes of the puppet-king from the procession, climbing out of his own dissolved memories as if ascending from a bath. But the movements were wrong the way the procession had been wrong, joints bending a half-beat late, and where the man's eyes should have been there was only the cold flickering light, and the crown settled onto his head as he stood, fitting itself with a sigh of relief audible across the whole cavern.

***Enough,*** it said, in the king's ruined voice. ***ENOUGH. You will stop, little ward, or I will spend this body's last hour showing you what I showed his daughter, the night she—***

"His daughter is alive," Ivo said.

The words fell into the silence like a stone into the well.

Ivo didn't know where they came from — except that he did. They rose out of the flood of stolen memory still churning in him, one small bright fragment among the thousands: a girl of six, hiding under a banquet table, giggling while guards searched the palace, and a king who found her and laughed until he cried and swore never to tell her mother. Alive. *Alive* — carried out of the palace years ago by loyalists, hidden beyond the Vessel's reach, because even a hollowed court had contained people who remembered how to love.

"She's alive," Ivo repeated, louder, standing now inside the burning circle with blood on his face and his hands still faintly transparent, "and she thinks her father is dead, and when this is over — when *you're* over — someone is going to tell her he can be found. So whatever you show me, whatever you threaten—" He planted his feet, feeling the third and final word of the unbinding waiting behind his teeth like a held breath. "You're not eating anyone ever again."

The Vessel looked at him with the king's dead-light eyes.

And then it smiled with the king's mouth, slow and wide and full of sixty years of practiced charm, and said:

***Then let us see, taster, whether you can finish a sentence.***

The crown flared. The well roared upward in a column of silver light, and every dissolved memory in it — every grief, every laugh, every stolen hour of a kingdom — came screaming down upon the chalk circle and the boy standing in it, and Ivo Brightwater opened his mouth and spoke the final word of the unbinding straight into the heart of the flood.
