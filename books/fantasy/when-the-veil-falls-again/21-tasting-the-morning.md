## 21. Tasting the Morning

The kitchens of the royal palace smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary, and for the first time in Ivo Brightwater's life, he cooked without tasting anything first.

It was a strange freedom. For nineteen years his tongue had been a tool, an instrument of the crown's paranoia, calibrated to detect hemlock in honeyed wine and nightshade folded into custard. He had tasted death so many times it had lost its flavor — until he'd left the valley and learned that everything else had one.

Now he stood over a copper pot of morning porridge, stirring with a wooden spoon worn smooth by other hands, and simply *cooked*. The oats turned golden with butter. He added a curl of honey from hives on the southern slopes, a pinch of salt, and — because Pip had asked twice already and would ask again — a handful of dried cherries that stained the whole thing the color of dusk.

"You're hovering," Ivo said without turning around.

"I'm supervising," Pip corrected from the doorway. The boy had grown into the palace like a plant finding sun after years under a stone. He still flinched at loud voices, still counted exits when they entered a room, but the haunted flicker behind his eyes — the constant churn of ten thousand stolen memories pressing against the inside of his skull — had gone quiet. What remained was only what people had given him freely: Sera teaching him to whittle, Ivo teaching him to knead bread, Brannoc's voice reciting herb-lore while she stirred her cauldron in memory if not in fact. "The king likes it with more honey."

"The king will get what he's given."

"The king is standing right here," said a dry voice, "and would like to negotiate."

Aldric Vane — no titles, not yet, maybe never again — shuffled into the kitchen in a plain wool robe, leaning on a cane he refused to admit he needed. He was thinner than any king should be. There were hollows under his eyes that no amount of sleep seemed to fill, and some mornings he woke screaming from dreams of a well that drank light. But he was *here*, and every day he was a little more here than the last.

He lowered himself onto the bench near the hearth with the care of an old man rather than the man of forty-five winters he actually was. His hands, once sculpted to wave from balconies and seal decrees, now trembled slightly as he wrapped them around a cup of cider Ivo pressed into them.

"More cherries," Aldric said.

"You sound like Pip."

"Pip has good taste." The king smiled — small, crooked, real. It was a smile Ivo had watched relearn itself over weeks, surfacing first in fragments, then in flashes, until now it arrived almost naturally, like a bird remembering its migration route home. "My mother used to put cherries in everything. Or—" He paused, brow furrowing. "I think she did. That might be something the Vessel left me. A kindness it stole from someone else and forgot to return."

Ivo set down the spoon. "Does it matter?"

The question hung between them. It was the question they circled most mornings, the one neither could answer and both kept asking.

Aldric stared into his cider. When the unbinding had finished, when the thing wearing his face had unraveled into starving threads of hunger and dissolved, it had taken nearly everything with it. Forty years of memories, poured down the Heartwell to feed a prison the old mages built and a crown perpetuated. What remained was a man assembled from scraps: his name, which Brannoc had carved somewhere safe before her end; his love of gardens; the muscle-memory of riding; fragments of his mother, perhaps true or perhaps borrowed. The archivists were working to piece him back together from records, letters, portraits — a biography rebuilt from the outside in.

"Some days," Aldric said quietly, "I think it matters very much. Some days I can't tell where I end and everyone else begins." He looked up, and there was nothing kingly in his eyes — just a tired man asking an honest question. "What do you taste, when you cook? Do you taste my mother's cherries, or your own?"

Ivo considered this with the seriousness he gave all questions of flavor.

"I taste cherries," he said finally. "And I taste Pip asking for them. And I taste you pretending you don't want them." He ladled porridge into three bowls. "Maybe that's what memory is. Not what you keep. What you share."

The king laughed — startled out of him, rusty, genuine. "You should have been a philosopher."

"I'm a taster." Ivo carried the bowls to the table. "I just taste things very carefully."

* * *

Sera came at midday, as she always did, smelling of road dust and horses.

She had taken rooms above the archive now, close to the letters. Every week a courier arrived from the eastern provinces carrying a packet sealed with wax stamped by a woman named Odessa Ferran — schoolteacher, widow, keeper of bees — who had once been the younger sister of a mercenary called Sera of the Broken Company, before Sera sold her memories of that sister to survive an ambush and then sold more to buy passage through a gate.

The letters had started as a courtesy arranged by the new archivists: *Your sister lives. She remembers you. She does not know why you don't remember her.* Then Sera had done the bravest thing Ivo had ever witnessed, braver than alleys or battles or selling pieces of herself: she had written back.

*Tell me about her,* the first letter said. *Tell me everything. I'll build her from the ground up.*

And Odessa — after a silence of two weeks that Sera had spent sharpening things that didn't need sharpening — had begun to write.

Now Sera sat across the kitchen table, a letter open before her, and read aloud the way she did everything: gruffly, like reporting terrain.

"'—and she bit the midwife. Mother said it was a sign of strong character. Father said it was a sign we should invest in leather gloves.'" Sera paused. Her jaw worked. "I apparently bite people."

"You bit me," Pip offered helpfully, "when I tried to take the last honeycake in Corvane."

"That was tactical."

"You drew blood."

"Tactical," Sera repeated, and went back to the letter, but Ivo saw the corner of her mouth fighting her. This was how it went, these days. She sat with Odessa's words like a scholar with a dead language, translating a sister into existence. *She keeps bees. She cries at weddings. She named her daughter Mara, after our grandmother.* Each fact a brick. Each letter a wall being raised where something had been torn down.

"It's not the same," Sera said suddenly, not looking up. "Knowing about her. It's like — secondhand armor. It fits wrong. I can swing a sword in it but it won't stop a blade."

"No," Ivo agreed. "But you're wearing it anyway."

Sera looked at him then, and something passed between them that needed no words — the language of people who had each paid the market its terrible price and walked away carrying what the other had lost. Ivo had promised to carry her sister's memory when Sera sold it. He still did. Some evenings, when the fire burned low, he told Sera about the girl in his returned fragment: the laugh like breaking glass and bells, the way she'd braided her sister's hair too tight and dared her to complain. Sera listened the way soldiers listen to reports from a country they can't reach.

"Odessa wants me to visit," Sera said. "Spring. She says—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "She says the bees will be waking up. She says her daughter asks about her aunt who fights monsters." Her voice roughened. "Ivo, what do I say to a child who knows me better than I know myself?"

Ivo reached across the table and took her scarred hand.

"You say hello," he said. "The rest builds itself. That's what families do."

Pip, perched on the counter stealing cherries, nodded sagely, as though this were wisdom of the ages and not something Ivo had made up on the spot because it was true.

* * *

In the afternoon, Ivo walked to the archive.

It was a cathedral of paper, the great hall where the kingdom's memory lived in ink since its living memory had been devoured. Under the new archivists — a fierce order of scholars who answered to the crownless king — the work of restoration continued shelf by shelf. And on the western wall, newly cut into pale stone above the shelves of hedge-craft and herb-lore, ran a single line of letters deep enough to catch the lamplight:

**BRANNOC OF THE HEDGE, WHO KEPT NAMES WHEN THE CROWN FORGOT THEM**

Ivo stood beneath it a long time.

She had completed her circle at the Heartwell. Half-consumed by the Vessel's hunger, she had spoken the final binding-word of the rite the architect-mages had written and the architect-mages had betrayed, and the word had cost her what little the years and the crown's debt hadn't already taken. They had found no body. Hedge-witches, Pip said solemnly, having read it in a memory someone had once given him, don't leave bodies. They leave seeds.

Ivo liked that. He chose to believe it the way he chose honey in his porridge — not because it could be proven, but because it made the morning better.

"She'd hate this," said a voice beside him. Sera had followed him in, quiet as she always was in stone halls. She tilted her chin at the inscription. "All that fuss. Carved in rock. She'd have called it vanity and charged us for the chisel."

"She'd have charged us double."

"And cursed us in a language that made the chisel rust."

Ivo smiled, but the smile faded as he looked up at the name. "Do you ever think about what she owed?" he asked. "Her debt to the crown. The name they erased. She spent her whole life paying for a bargain she couldn't even remember making."

"All the time." Sera's voice was low. "I think about the gate, too. When they took her and locked us out. I think about what it cost to buy our way back in." Her hand drifted to her temple, where the absence lived — the hole where her sister had been, filled now with borrowed bricks. "Debts don't die, Ivo. They just change hands. Someone has to carry them forward."

"Then we carry hers," Ivo said simply. "That's what the carving means. Not that she's finished. That we've picked her up."

They stood together in the lamplight a moment longer, two orphans of different disasters, keeping watch over a witch's name.

Then Pip's voice echoed down the stairwell, breathless and bright: "Ivo! IVO! The cart from the valley is here!"

* * *

The valley sent goods twice a season now — that was part of the new arrangement, the treaty of the open stones. Wool and river-clay and smoked trout, and letters, always letters, bundled in twine.

Ivo found the cart in the outer courtyard, and found Marta the cook's-wife standing beside it, older than his memories of her but unmistakable, flour still on her forearms as though she'd brought the bakery with her. Behind her, half-hidden in the cart's shadow, stood others: Tam the farrier's boy, taller now; old Fenwick with his bad hip; a cluster of faces Ivo had eaten supper with every night of his first life.

His chest did something complicated.

"Marta," he managed.

"Oh, look at you." She seized his face in floury hands and studied it like dough she was deciding whether to scold. "Thin. Are they feeding you? You look like a boy who needs feeding." She said it the way she'd said a thousand things across a thousand suppers, and just like that, nineteen years collapsed, and he was eight years old again, burning his tongue on stew, surrounded by people who had chosen him without knowing what he was.

Especially without knowing what he was. Because none of them had known. Not the cooks, not the guards, not the kindly cellar-keeper who'd taught him letters. They had raised the veil's final ward, the living boundary-stone of their prison-refuge, and fed him supper, and taught him to swim in the cold river, and never once suspected that the boy passing dishes down their table was the reason magic couldn't touch their valley at all.

"They know now," Marta said, reading his face as easily as ever. "Everything. We had quite the winter of it." She squeezed his cheeks. "You want to know the terrible thing, Ivo Brightwater? Nobody cared."

He blinked. "Nobody—"

"Not the way you feared. There was some crying. Fenwick took it hard — thought he'd imagined the swimming lessons, poor fool. But mostly—" She shrugged, flour drifting. "Mostly they said, *that explains the mist behaving oddly around him*, and *we always knew he was special*, and *when is he coming home?*" Her eyes softened. "They miss you, love. The valley misses you. The door's open, you know. It stays open now."

And there it was. The wound underneath the healing, the one that barked on quiet nights.

Because the door *was* open. The veil had fallen. Magic poured gentle as spring rain into the hidden valley, and the standing stones were just stones now, and the mist was just mist. Ivo could walk home along the river road in six days. But he couldn't return *as he was* — that was the truth he'd swallowed with the Vessel's temptation. The ward-nature that had been poured into the Heartwell had been *him*, the shape of him, the thing that made the valley hold its breath around him. He was still Ivo. He cooked, and tasted, and remembered. But some essential grain of him had gone into the world's remaking, the way flour goes into bread: present everywhere in the result, nowhere retrievable in itself.

Home was open. Home had changed. And so had he.

"Come see it," Marta said gently. "When you're ready. Come see what it's becoming. There are wildflowers on the north ridge that were never there before. Tam swears the river sings." She smiled, wet-eyed and fierce. "And your table's still in the cook-house. Nobody would let them burn it. Though—" she glanced around the courtyard, at the recovering king picking his careful way across the cobbles, at Sera lounging against the cart reading a letter with her sister's handwriting, at Pip hanging off the wagon wheel drinking in the visitors' faces like sunlight, "—it occurs to me you may have already solved that problem."

"What problem?"

"The table problem." Marta patted his cheek. "A table's just wood, love. Family's what you put around it. Seems to me you've got plenty."

* * *

They built it themselves, the following week, in the palace's east garden where the morning sun hit longest.

Aldric directed from a bench with the authority of a man who had never sawed anything. Sera did the actual sawing. Pip measured everything three times and argued about the mathematics. Ivo planed the wood smooth and thought about tables: the long scarred board of the servants' hall in the Veiled Valley where he'd learned that food meant belonging; the market-tables of Corvane where yesterday was sold by the vial; the witch's cluttered workbench covered in drying herbs and unlabeled jars of names.

Wood held everything, if you let it. Scratches, rings from cups, burns from careless pots. A table was just a memory you could eat off.

By the time the sun slanted gold through the garden wall, it stood finished under the old apple tree: broad, uneven, sanded smooth, big enough for ten and crowded happily at six.

They ate the first supper from it as the light went violet. Aldric told a story about his mother that might have been true, and everyone pretended to believe it, which was its own kind of gift. Sera read a passage from Odessa's newest letter — the daughter, Mara, had asked whether Aunt Sera had ever fought a dragon, and Sera declared she was writing back to say *yes, several, all smaller than advertised*. Pip distributed the cherries with the gravity of a treasurer. And Ivo moved among them with the dishes, tasting nothing for safety and everything for joy, and understood at last what the stranger at the ford had bought with her silver blood.

*The crown remembers,* she'd said. Let it. Let the crown remember whatever it pleased. What mattered was the table. What mattered was that belonging was never a place you were kept — not a valley, not a ward, not a wall of mist. It was a chair pulled out for you. It was a place set, and hands that stayed, and names carved in stone by people who picked you up and carried you forward.

He looked down the length of the rough-hewn wood at his strange, broken, mended family and felt the ache and the sweetness sit together in his chest like two flavors in one dish, each making the other truer.

Marta's cart rolled out at dawn the next morning. At the gate, she pressed something into his hands wrapped in cloth: a jar of valley honey, dark and slow, from hives that had drunk the new magic all winter.

"For the table," she said. "So it tastes of both places."

Ivo stood in the gateway long after the cart vanished down the river road, the jar warm in his hands, watching the road unwind toward the valley that had made him and could no longer contain him.

Behind him, Pip shouted that the porridge was burning.

Ahead, the morning waited, and it smelled — Ivo closed his eyes and breathed deep, taster to the last — like wildflowers on a north ridge, like a letter from the east, like cherries and woodsmoke and home being built instead of found.

He turned and walked back toward the smell of burning breakfast, smiling, and began setting another place at the table.
