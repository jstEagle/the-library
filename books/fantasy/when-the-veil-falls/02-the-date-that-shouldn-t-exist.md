## 2. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The sealed archive smelled of dust and candle tallow and a hundred years of nobody's business.

Elodie Ashgrove had been inside it for six days now, sent down by Brother Aldous with a wax tablet, a stack of fresh folios, and the temple's grudging blessing: *Catalogue everything. Number every spine. Report anything irregular.*

Irregular. In Vell, that word covered a great deal of ground. A crooked shelf was irregular. A moth-eaten psalter was irregular. Once, a novice had found a child's drawing tucked inside a hymnal—a stick figure with a scribbled sun above its head—and the drawing had been burned in the courtyard while the whole temple watched, and the novice had spent a month scrubbing the refectory floor for having touched it. Magic could not exist in Vell. Neither, apparently, could a sun drawn by a child who didn't know better.

So Elodie worked slowly, and he wrote down only what was safe to write down, and he kept his curiosity folded up small and flat, like a letter he would never send.

The sealed archive occupied the undercroft beneath the scriptorium—three long vaulted rooms where the light came through slits near the ceiling and moved across the floor like slow yellow animals. Most of it was exactly what he'd expected: ledgers of tithes going back decades, copies of canon, sermons transcribed in seventeen different hands, all of them saying the same things in the same order. *The Veil protects. The Veil provides. Beyond the Veil lies ruin.* The valley's whole history fit in four shelves, and three of those shelves were the same book copied over and over.

He'd been told the sealed rooms went back further. He'd been told there were things down here older than the temple itself. So far, the oldest thing he'd found was a tax record from eighty-one years ago, its ink brown as dried blood.

That bothered him, in the way a missing stair bothers you at night—not enough to stop walking, just enough that your foot keeps reaching for it.

On the sixth day, he reached the third room.

* * *

The third room was smaller than the others, and colder. Its door had no lock, which somehow made it worse; locked doors said *someone decided this*. Unlocked doors said *no one has cared in so long they forgot to care*, and Elodie wasn't sure which kind of secret was heavier.

The shelves here were nearly empty. A few crates of vestments gone grey with age. A box of broken seals—the wax kind, stamps for letters, their handles carved into shapes he didn't recognize. And on the bottom shelf, wedged behind a crate, lying flat as if someone had slid it there quickly and walked away: a single book bound in black leather gone green at the edges.

No title on the spine. No temple mark. Every other volume in the archive bore the seal of the Ninefold Flame stamped somewhere—a cover, an endpaper, a fore-edge. This one had nothing. It was as if the book had been born blank of authority.

Elodie looked at the door. Looked back at the shelf. Six days of discipline said *catalogue it, number it, move along*. His hand said otherwise.

He carried it to the reading table beneath the ceiling slit, where the day's ration of light had pooled, and set it down, and opened it.

The paper was fine. That was the first strange thing. Fine paper, in a valley where even the temple wrote its scripture on stock rough enough to sand a door. The pages had a weight to them, a faint sheen, like they'd been made somewhere rain fell differently.

The first page held a single line in a cramped, urgent hand:

*What is written here is written because it must be.*

Below that, a date. Elodie knew the dating convention instantly—it was the old reckoning, the one the temple used for the founding of the Veil, the one that appeared only in the liturgy. By that count, the entry fell one hundred years and some months past.

He turned the page.

More entries. Dozens. All in the same hand at first, then in others—different scripts layered over the years like sediment. Some entries were a line. Some filled pages. He skimmed, half-reading, the way you skim scripture you've heard recited since childhood, expecting nothing, receiving nothing.

Then the handwriting changed again—a sharp, slanted script, impatient, pressing hard enough to groove the page—and Elodie stopped skimming, because the language stopped sounding like scripture and started sounding like a person.

*The Veil holds, but the Veil bleeds. We sealed it with twelve names and eleven truths. The twelfth name we did not speak aloud, and the silence is the flaw in the stone. One day it will be spoken. Not by us. We will be ash. But the valley fills, the blood thins and spreads, and the name walks again in a body that does not know what it carries.*

There was no date on that entry. Just a small symbol pressed into the margin—a circle with a vertical stroke through it—and something about the symbol snagged on Elodie's eye like a burr. He'd seen it before. He couldn't place where. Somewhere ordinary. Somewhere he passed every day.

He kept turning pages. The entries grew sparser, then stopped altogether for long stretches—years, judging by the fading of the ink between them—and then resumed in new hands, each one a little shakier than the last, as if the book had been passed down a chain of people who each hoped to be the last to hold it.

Near the end of the volume, the pages changed character entirely.

They became dated.

One date per entry, precise as a ledger, marching forward through years Elodie had lived through without incident—dates that meant harvest festivals and debt payments and the quiet unremarkable passage of a valley that did not know it was being written about. He checked several against his memory of the temple calendar. They matched. They matched perfectly, including days that had fallen on fasts and days that had fallen on feasts, and a cold finger traced itself slowly up Elodie's spine, because these weren't memories being recorded. The dates ran ahead. Past today. Into next year, and the year after, in a hand that hadn't been written yet—or had been written long ago, pretending.

His mouth had gone dry. He noticed it the way you notice a change in weather.

He turned the page.

And the floor dropped out of his world.

* * *

Later—he would think about this later, in the dark, many times—he would try to reconstruct the exact order of what happened. Had he seen the date first, or the name? Had his heart stuttered before or after his breath? Memory refused to cooperate. What remained was not a sequence but a single frozen instant, the way a dropped cup exists most vividly in the moment before it hits stone.

The entry was short. Three lines. The ink was dark and fresh-looking, though the page's neighbors had yellowed around it like old teeth.

*On the ninth day of the Dying Moon, in the hundredth year of the Veil, the scribe Ashgrove opens the book. The Veilbearer speaks.*

Today was the ninth day of the Dying Moon. Elodie knew that the way he knew his own hands, because the ninth was when the indenture tithe was counted, when he signed the ledger in the steward's office, when his debt was read aloud and reduced by the price of six days' copying.

In the hundredth year of the Veil.

He did the arithmetic twice. It was the kind of arithmetic you do twice, hoping the second time will be wrong.

His name. Not *a scribe*. Not *the son of Ashgrove*. *Elodie Ashgrove*—spelled out entire, in ink older than his grandfather, on a page that had been waiting in the dark under the scriptorium since before anyone in the valley had been born.

*The Veilbearer speaks.*

He sat very still. Above him, through the ceiling slit, he could hear the ordinary sounds of the temple going about its morning: the bell for terce, distant singing, Brother Aldous's cough, someone arguing cheerfully about bread. The sounds came down the shaft thin and flattened, like voices heard through water, and for the first time in his life they sounded far away. As if the temple were the dream and this cold room with its impossible book were the thing awake.

*Coincidence,* he told himself.

The word arrived promptly, obediently, the way trained words do. Coincidence. The valley was full of Ashgroves—well, two households of them, but still. Scribes opened books every day. Dates aligned; calendars repeat; any given day is somebody's anniversary of something. If you wrote enough names and enough dates over a century, of course one of them would land square on a real person on a real day. It was probability. It was nothing. Brother Aldous himself said the mind was a pattern-making animal, that heresy began in the itch to find meaning where none was placed.

It was a very good argument. Elodie made it carefully, thoroughly, the way he copied scripture—every line justified, every margin clean.

Then he looked at the entry again and read the words he'd skipped the first time, the small line beneath, written in a different ink, a paler brown, squeezed close under the main text as if the writer had leaned in to whisper it:

*He will tell himself it is coincidence.*

* * *

He closed the book.

He didn't slam it. Slamming would have made a sound, and sound in the sealed archive traveled strangely—he'd learned that the first day, how a cough down here could arrive upstairs a heartbeat later, how the undercroft listened. He closed it gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping, and he sat with both hands flat on the black cover and felt the grain of the leather under his palms and tried to feel nothing at all.

The trouble was his hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to the boy in the prophecy rather than to him.

Think, he ordered himself. You're a scribe. Thinking is the trade.

Facts. The book predated everyone alive. The entry named him. The entry named today. The pale line beneath predicted his own disbelief, which meant either the writer had guessed—which anyone might guess, because of course a man would tell himself it was coincidence—or—

Or the book was not a record. Or the book was a schedule.

That thought entered him quietly, the way cold water enters a boot, and he stood up too fast and the chair scraped and the sound went up the shaft and he froze, listening, heart slamming. Terce had ended. The temple hummed on. Nobody came.

*Catalogue it,* said the part of him that had survived six years of indenture by being careful. *Number it. Write it in the folio. "One volume, untitled, contents devotional." Put it back on the shelf. Walk away. Never come down here again.*

And that would have been the end of it—that was the version of this morning he would carry for the rest of his life, the road not taken, the book returned to the dark—if his hand, reaching for the folio, had not brushed the book open again.

It fell to the last written page. He hadn't put a ribbon in it. He hadn't marked his place. But it lay open precisely where he had stopped reading, as if the spine remembered.

The entry there was undated. Four words, in the sharp slanted hand from the middle of the book—the impatient one, the one that pressed grooves into the paper:

*Turn back, scribe. Please.*

Please. A hundred-year-old *please*, aimed down the barrel of time at him, at this exact minute, and Elodie felt something crack open in his chest that was not fear. Fear he could have managed. This was worse. This was pity—pity flowing backward through a century, from some dead stranger who had sat in this same cold room, holding this same impossible book, and had wanted, more than anything, to warn whoever came next.

He turned the page instead.

Blank.

The next page. Blank. The rest of the book, blank—all those fine heavy pages with their faint sheen, waiting. He flipped faster, paper whispering, and the whisper sounded almost like speech, and he stopped, and stood in the pool of yellow light with his chest heaving, looking at all that emptiness.

A book half-written. A book that knew his name and today's date and his own thoughts before he'd thought them, and then stopped—as if it had run out of author. Or as if the rest of it hadn't happened yet.

Or as if the rest of it was waiting for him.

"No," Elodie said aloud, and his voice cracked in the cold air, embarrassingly young. "No. I copy scripture. I owe a debt. I'm nobody's—" He gestured at the book, at the word he wouldn't say. *Veilbearer.* The word sat in the air anyway, patient as ink.

Above him, faint through the shaft, came footsteps crossing the scriptorium floor. Slow ones. Measured. He knew every walker in this temple by their tread—Brother Aldous's shuffle, the novices' scurry, the cook's uneven gait. These steps belonged to none of them. They paused directly overhead, above the shaft, above the slit of light, as if someone upstairs had stopped to consider the floor.

Then they moved on toward the stairs that led down.

Elodie did the only thing his body knew how to do, the reflex six years of servitude had carved deeper than any scripture: he hid the book. Behind the crate, flat, spine flush to the wall, exactly where it had been, dust dragged carelessly across its face with two fingers. Then he picked up his folio and his stylus and stood beside the reading table wearing the expression he wore during inspections—blank, deferential, empty as an unsigned page.

The stair door opened.

Footsteps descended. And with them came a voice Elodie knew better than any voice in the world, warm and unhurried, calling down through the dark:

"Elodie? You missed the midday meal, lad." A pause, and then, lightly, in the tone of a joke that isn't quite a joke: "Brother Cassian worries. Come up, and tell me what the dead have left for you to find."

The book lay hidden three feet away, its last written line burning behind Elodie's eyes.

*He will tell himself it is coincidence.*

He already had. And it hadn't helped at all.
