## 3. Brother Cassian's Smile

The morning bells rang wrong.

That was the first thing I noticed, before the candle smoke and the ache in my shoulders from hunching over the copying desk until the second bell of night. The dawn bells came a half-beat early, or my ears had gone strange overnight, tuned to some frequency they hadn't known yesterday. Everything in the scriptorium felt slightly off-key—the scratch of quills, the shuffle of sand across wet ink, Brother Tam's habitual cough in the corner stall—all of it sounded like it was happening one room away from where I actually sat.

Because there was a book under my tunic.

It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. A slim volume bound in cracked green leather, tucked flat against my ribs beneath the rough weave of my robe, and every time I shifted in my chair it pressed against me like a hand. *Prophecies of the Founding Century.* Catalogue number 4-119. Sealed shelf, third row of the deep archive, dust on its spine thick as felt—which meant no one had touched it since before I was born, since before Cassian was young, maybe. And inside it, in faded iron-gall ink written by someone dead a hundred years:

*On this day, the Veilbearer speaks.*

And beneath that, in a different hand, fresher, darker, impossible:

*Elodie Ashgrove.*

My name. My hand didn't write it. No living hand wrote it. It had been waiting on that page for a century like a trap sprung slow.

I copied three lines of the Canticle of Provision and got two of them wrong. I caught myself writing *provision* with an extra *i* and scraped it back with the knife, heart hammering, certain everyone could hear it. Certain the scrape of blade on vellum was a confession shouted across the room. Brother Tam glanced up. I smiled at him. He went back to his psalter.

Under my ribs, the book waited.

I had told myself all through the sleepless dark that I would return it first thing. Slip back into the archive, reshelve it, dust the spine, done—no harm, no theft worth naming. It wasn't even theft, really. I was the archive's cataloguer. Handling the collection was my whole purpose here, my debt made flesh. Who would know?

But my hands had refused at the archive door. My feet had walked me to the scriptorium instead, carrying the one object in Vell that could kill me, because apparently some part of me wanted to be caught—or wanted, worse, to keep reading.

*Elodie Ashgrove.*

There is a particular quality to fear in a place like Vell. It doesn't shout. It seeps. In the valley we are taught from the cradle that curiosity is the sin beneath all sins—not pride, not wrath, but the simple itch of wanting to know what lies past the hills. The temple says the Veil protects us. The Veil keeps out the chaos beyond, the wild magic that burned the world down to its roots a century ago. We are the safe country. We are the quiet people. And quiet people who ask questions become loud examples.

I had seen it once, when I was nine. A quarryman's wife, Marta Ossen, who kept saying her son had been born with something in him, a warmth, a light—and she said it not once but for years, at market, at well-side, until the wardens came for her with their gray cloaks and their gentle voices. She wasn't executed. That's the detail everyone remembers, the mercy of it. They just took her up the mountain road to the retreat house, and she never came back, and her husband stopped speaking entirely, and her son grew up ashamed of his own name. That was the lesson. Not the stake. The silence after.

I was twenty-three years old and indentured to the Temple of the Quiet Word until the ledger of my father's debt ran dry, which the arithmetic said would take another nineteen years. I had a cot in the novices' wing, two meals a day, a talent for letters that even the prior acknowledged, and now I had a forbidden book against my chest and a date in it that was today.

*The Veilbearer speaks.*

I dipped my quill. I wrote: *Blessed are the hands that do not reach.*

My hand shook so badly the ascender on the *d* looked like a gallows.

* * *

He found me at midmorning, of course. He always knew.

Cassian came down the scriptorium aisle the way he did everything—without hurry, without noise, a big man gone soft at the edges, gray threading his beard, moving through the world like a man wading a stream he has crossed ten thousand times. Fifty-eight years old, forty of them spent in this temple, and he still carried himself with the loose-shouldered ease of the quarry worker he'd been before the cloth. He had been my father's friend before he was anything to me. Before my father died under a collapsed lintel in the east quarry with the temple's mark on the contract and the debt transferred, generous as rain, onto his orphaned son.

"Your margins are weeping," Cassian said, setting a cup of barley tea beside my elbow without asking whether I wanted it. He never asked. He'd been bringing me tea in this room since I was eleven years old and small enough to hide under the copying desks when the wardens did their rounds.

I looked down. Ink had pooled in the gutter of my page, a black bloom spreading into the margin like a bruise. "Slipped."

"Mm." He pulled the neighboring stool around and sat, which he wasn't supposed to do—scribes worked in silence, and conversation during hours was a minor infraction he committed daily and cheerfully, the way other men smoked pipe weed. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and beeswax and the particular cold-stone smell of the lower archive, and I understood with a lurch that he had come *from* the lower archive. From near the sealed shelves. This morning. While I'd been sitting here sweating.

"Inventory day?" I asked, too lightly.

"Spot check." He blew across his own tea. "The prior likes them unpredictable. Says predictable audits breed sloppy clerks." A pause, measured out like tea leaves. "Row four looked fine. Row three—" he sipped, "—had a gap."

The bells rang in my skull. Half a beat off. Everything half a beat off.

"A gap," I said.

"Dust shows where books sit, Elodie. Dust is honest." He said it mildly, watching the steam curl off his cup rather than watching me, and somehow that was worse than being watched. "One slim volume, green binding, if my memory serves. Which it does, because I shelved it myself, thirty years ago, when I was the one doing the cataloguing." Another sip. "Funny thing about memory. It holds on to the strangest details."

I should have lied immediately. Cleanly. *Must have been misfiled, I'll check the overflow crates.* The words were right there, ready, rehearsed in the dark hours between bells. Instead what came out of me was nothing—a silence about two seconds too long, filled only by the scratch of Brother Tam's quill and my own blood moving in my ears.

Cassian set down his cup.

"Walk with me," he said.

* * *

We walked the cloister walk, the long covered passage along the temple's east side, where the light came through arched windows in pale bars and fell across the flagstones like the rungs of a ladder nobody climbed anymore. It was the closest thing the temple had to a private place, though I knew better than to believe in private places here. The wardens' rounds passed the cloister twice hourly. The prior's study overlooked it. Even the stone itself seemed to listen; the valley listened; Vell was a town built like an ear.

For a long way neither of us spoke. That was its own language, ours. Cassian had taught me my letters on this very walk when I was small, chalk on slate, his huge hand closed over mine. He'd taught me the trick of stretching lamb's parchment, how to cut a reed pen at an angle so the ink sang instead of stuttered, how to read a marginal gloss left by a scribe three hundred years dead and hear the man's voice. When my father's funeral was paid for out of the debt account—another entry, another year added to my sentence—it was Cassian who stood with me at the graveside, and it was Cassian whose hand on my shoulder was the only thing that kept me standing.

He was the nearest thing to a father the ledger had left me. Which is why what happened next carved itself into me the way a chisel carves stone: permanently, and by intention.

"You've been somewhere else since yesterday's bell," he said finally. "Since you came up from the deep stacks with your little cart. You've been copying like a boy trying to outrun something." He stopped walking. The barred light lay across his face, cutting it into gold and shadow. "I'm not going to ask you what it was. I want you to hear that clearly, Elodie. I am not asking."

"Then what—"

"I'm telling you a story instead." He turned to face the window, the valley beyond it: the terraced fields going gold toward harvest, the slate roofs of Vell town, the ring of hills folded close around us like cupped hands. Somewhere up there, past the tree line, past the high pasture, stood the boundary stones, though you couldn't see them from anywhere in the valley. They were always described to children as being just out of sight. Like everything else here. "When I was a young brother, younger than you are now, I did the same work you do. Cataloguing. And I found things in the sealed rows that didn't match the histories we're given to copy."

My breath went shallow. Under my tunic, against my ribs, I felt the book's presence like a second heartbeat.

"There were records missing," Cassian went on, quietly, as if reciting scripture. "Not lost—missing. Cut out, with care, by someone who loved the bindings enough to leave no scars. Whole decades of the founding era, gone. And graves in the temple yard that stop at a certain year. Go look sometime. The oldest marker in our yard is ninety-seven years old, and the temple claims four hundred." He shook his head slowly. "A young man notices those things and starts to ask questions. And I had a mentor then too, and he caught me the way I have probably just caught you, and he told me the wisest thing anyone ever told me in this valley."

"What was that?"

"He said: *In Vell, boy, the truth isn't dangerous because it's forbidden. It's dangerous because it's true.* Forbidden things can be hidden. True things leak." He looked at me then, fully, and his eyes were kind and old and terribly sad. "So I put the questions away. I shelved them next to the sealed books, in the dark, and I locked my own curiosity behind the same door, and I had a long life of it. Quiet. Safe. Nineteen years of watching you grow up safe. And I have never once regretted it." His voice caught, just barely, on the word *once*, a splinter in smooth wood. "Never once."

The lie rose in me then. I felt it form the way ink forms on a nib—a gathering, a blackness, a readiness to fall. Because he was giving me the exit. He was handing me the door marked *safe* with both hands and tears in his eyes, and all I had to do was walk through it and say: *You're imagining things, old man. I misfiled nothing. There's no gap. Your dust deceived you.*

I thought of the prophecy's line again. *The Veilbearer speaks.* Spoke of what? To whom? What did the book want from me? What had the person who wrote my name a century ago wanted?

And underneath all of it, quieter, more shameful: *What else is in the sealed rows? What did they cut out?*

"Inquisitor Maren arrives within the week," Cassian said. The name landed in the cloister like a stone in a well. "You've heard."

Everyone had heard. The whole temple had been scrubbing and rearranging for days, the way a house scrubs when a magistrate visits. An Inquisitor of the Quiet Word, come out from the mountain retreat, hunting heresy in a valley that hadn't produced a heretic in a generation. "They say she's thorough," I managed.

"They say she's courteous," Cassian corrected softly. "That's worse. Thorough men miss things out of exhaustion. Courteous ones make you want to confess." He put his hand on my shoulder—the same hand from the graveside, heavy and warm and shaking slightly, and I don't think he knew it shook. "Whatever you're carrying, Elodie—and I'm not saying you're carrying anything—but whatever a boy in this valley might carry, there is a weight at which it stops being a secret and becomes a sentence. Find the weight. Stay under it. Promise me."

And there it was. The moment. The clean door, held open.

I looked up at the man who had taught me to write, who had buried my father with me, who had spent forty years swallowing the truth like a stone so that he could stay alive to bring me tea in the mornings. And I lied to him.

"I promise," I said. "There's nothing, Cassian. Truly. I've been tired, that's all. The inventory backlog. I've been sleeping badly."

Every word of it was smooth. Every word of it was practiced. And every word of it tasted like the ink I'd spilled in my margin—black, spreading, permanent. Because I saw him believe it. I watched it happen in his face: the small easing around his eyes, the exhale through the nose, the smile arriving like sunrise over the hills of Vell, warm and creased and trusting. Brother Cassian's smile. I had known it my whole life. It was the first thing I remembered seeing clearly in this world, bent over my chalk slate, saying *there, boy, that's an A.*

He believed me, and his believing was the worst thing that had ever been done to me, because I did it.

"Good lad." He squeezed my shoulder. "Sleep tonight. All night. The dead can wait a day to be catalogued—they've gotten very good at waiting."

He collected his cup and walked on down the cloister, unhurried, soft-footed, a big man wading his familiar stream. At the far arch he paused, half-turned, silhouetted in the pale light, and for one heartbeat I thought—he knows, he sees through me, he's going to turn and—

He just raised a hand, the old blessing of the Quiet Word: fingers open, palm outward. *Nothing hidden. Nothing taken.*

Then he was gone around the arch, and I stood alone among the rungs of light with a century-old book burning against my ribs like a coal, having learned the day's true lesson: not that lying was easy. That it was easy *with him*. That the first lie costs everything, and the second will cost less, and I had just spent the most precious coin I owned on the cheapest purchase imaginable—another quiet day in Vell.

* * *

I went back to the scriptorium. I copied the Canticle of Provision without error, line after perfect line, my hand steady now in the way of hands that have already broken. Around me the temple breathed its paper-and-wax breath. Down the hall, a warden's boots clicked past on schedule. Above, in the prior's window, a shadow moved and stilled.

At noon I asked leave to fetch water for the ink troughs, and I carried the jug out to the courtyard well the way I had a thousand times. The rope creaked. The bucket knocked the shaft walls, echoing up from the dark.

As I leaned over the mouth of the well, waiting for the splash, I let my mind do the thing it had been clawing to do all morning. *The scribe runs,* I thought, testing the shape of it, though the words weren't in the book yet—they couldn't be, I'd read ahead, the pages after my name were blank, blank, blank, waiting. *Runs where? Runs from whom?*

The bucket came up full and silver and trembling.

And across the courtyard, at the postern gate where deliveries came in, a pilgrim stood talking to the gate brother—gray-cloaked, dusty from the mountain road, hood thrown back from a face I couldn't see at that distance. Ordinary. Unremarkable. Except that she was looking directly at me, across sixty feet of cobbles, unblinking, as if she had crossed the mountains and the boundary stones and the dead air of the valley for no other purpose than to stand in that exact spot at that exact moment and find me exactly where I stood.

The rope slipped in my hands.

The jug shattered on the stones, water everywhere, and by the time I'd knelt among the pieces—heart slamming, ears ringing half a beat off—the gate was empty.

She was gone. But the direction she'd gone was inward. Toward the cloisters. Toward the archive stairs.

Toward everything I had promised Cassian I wouldn't touch.
