## 5. What the Veil Is

The Archive of Vell kept its oldest books in the coldest room.

Elodie had learned this in his third year of indenture, when the senior scribes still bothered to instruct him. Cold slowed rot. Dry air slowed mold. Darkness, they said, slowed curiosity, though he suspected that last part was doctrine rather than conservation science. The Founding Vault sat at the bottom of a stair that spiraled down past three locked doors, each one blessed by a different Prior, and it was cold enough that his breath showed even in high summer.

He was supposed to be recataloguing.

Prior Aldous had given him the task two days after the prophecy book had shown him his own name in ink a century old. *Reorganize the sealed collections, Brother Elodie. The dust is older than your grandmother.* It was either an honor or a test, and Elodie had spent those two days deciding which, and had concluded, somewhere around the second sleepless night, that it didn't matter. Either way, he would be alone down here with the founding records for weeks.

Either way, he could read everything.

He started with the obvious: *A History of the Sealing*, by Scribe Ondrel, commissioned by the temple forty years after the event and considered the definitive account. He'd copied excerpts of it during his training. Every child in Vell knew its opening by heart:

*In the hundredth year of the Long Dark, when magic's corruption had spread to every corner of creation and the world groaned under the weight of sorcerous tyranny, twelve righteous families came together in the valley that would bear their faith, and wove the Great Veil, and shielded their children from the blight forever.*

It was a beautiful sentence. Elodie had always thought so. It had rhythm, and certainty, and the comfortable weight of something everyone agreed on.

Now he read it like a scribe instead of a believer, and the beauty curdled.

*The hundredth year of the Long Dark.* Fine. That meant the Long Dark had lasted a century before the sealing—magic corrupting, tyrants rising, the world dying. The histories all agreed on that. What none of them agreed on was anything else.

Ondrel wrote that the twelve families came from "the eastern reaches." A later text, *The Veil and the Mercy* by Sister Ivetta, written eighty years after the sealing, said the families were "of the western lands, fleeing the Sorcerer-Kings." A third source—the *Litany of Founders*, recited every Sealing Day from memory—named only eleven families before trailing off into a prayer, as if the twelfth had been forgotten mid-sentence.

Twelve families. Eleven names. And no one, in a hundred years of faithful record-keeping, had ever noticed or cared.

Elodie set the book down and pressed his palms flat against the cold stone table until his hands stopped shaking. Coincidence, he told himself. Liturgies drift. Names fall out. That's what time does to stories.

But the next contradiction didn't let him look away.

Every account agreed on the date of the sealing. The ninth day of the Frostwane moon, in the year the valley chronicles called Year One. Every account disagreed on the year before it. Ondrel said the families wandered for seven years before finding the valley. Ivetta said they arrived in a single season, guided by a vision. The *Litany* skipped the journey entirely, as if the founders had simply been discovered standing there, ready to begin.

And here was the thing that made the floor tilt beneath his chair: the valley chronicles themselves. Elodie had catalogued them last month, before the prophecy book. They began at Year One. Not because earlier volumes had been lost—he'd checked the accession ledgers, checked them twice—but because there had never been any others. The first volume opened with a title page in a careful liturgical hand: *Here begins the reckoning of Vell.*

No chronicle of the journey. No chronicle of the Long Dark. Nothing written by anyone who remembered a world with magic in it. The people who had supposedly fled across a corrupted continent, who had woven a working of power so vast it silenced an entire valley—they had apparently never written down a single word about it.

Scribes don't do that. Elodie was a scribe. People who live through enormous things write about them obsessively; he'd seen it in the smallest records, the grain ledgers and burial rolls. Grief keeps accounts. Fear keeps accounts. Nobody survives the end of the world and then declines to mention it.

Unless, said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like the blank page refusing his ink, unless they wanted to be sure no one ever asked what came before.

He worked through the morning and into the afternoon, and the contradictions bred like mice in the walls. The founders' numbers changed—sometimes three hundred souls, sometimes nine hundred, once, in Ivetta, "a multitude beyond counting," which was a strange thing for a careful woman to write about a group small enough to fit in one valley. The ritual itself was described four different ways: woven from prayer, woven from sacrifice, woven from "the blood freely offered," and once—not in the temple texts but in a merchant's private journal, misfiled among the civic records—"bought with something dearer than blood, gods forgive us, bought and paid."

That one he read six times. *Bought with something dearer than blood.* Merchants don't use liturgical language by accident. That was a man writing down what he'd heard, or what he'd done, and being afraid of it.

By late afternoon his candle had burned down to a stub and his eyes ached, and he had a page of notes he couldn't show anyone, and he made himself do the thing he'd been avoiding since dawn.

He asked for the burial rolls.

Graveyards were temple business in Vell. Births, deaths, marriages—all recorded in the parish ledgers, all archived here, going back to—well. Going back, he had assumed, to the beginning.

The earliest burial ledger was also dated Year One. Of course it was. Everything was dated Year One, because everything in Vell began at Year One, because the whole world had supposedly been born on a single autumn day a century ago.

But cemeteries are honest in ways books aren't, because stones outlast stories. Elodie couldn't check the graveyards themselves—not tonight, not without permission—but the ledgers recorded plot assignments, and plot assignments meant sections, and sections meant age. The Old Field behind the temple had eight rows. Eight rows, a hundred years, generations of the dead laid out in orderly succession.

Except the ledger showed something odd. The first fifty years of burials were sparse. Terribly sparse. Fifty years, and the Old Field's first section held barely ninety graves—for a settlement of three hundred to nine hundred souls, in a hard valley, with quarry work and harsh winters and all the ordinary ways a body fails.

Ninety graves in fifty years.

Then, abruptly, the numbers doubled, tripled, settled into the steady rhythm of a real town where real people died at the real rate. As if, Elodie thought, his mouth gone dry, as if the population hadn't actually existed for that first half-century. As if the early entries had been written to fill space rather than to record fact.

Or—as another thought arrived, colder—as if the first generation had been smaller than the histories claimed. Much smaller. Small enough that you might ask: where did the rest go? Three hundred founders, and only ninety dead in the first fifty years? Where did the other hundreds go? Did they leave? Did they—

Did the valley exist at all, before Year One?

He sat very still in the cold and listened to the building settle above him, beams ticking, stone breathing. Somewhere overhead, a door closed. Footsteps crossed the archive floor, unhurried, and paused directly over the Founding Vault, and moved on.

He looked down at his notes—at Ondrel's beautiful sentence, at Ivetta's wandering families, at the merchant's terrified margin—and understood, with a vertigo so complete it felt physical, that he wasn't looking at sloppy record-keeping.

He was looking at a story that had been assembled. Deliberately. By someone who needed the people of Vell to believe their world was old and innocent and safe. The contradictions weren't errors. Errors scatter randomly. These pointed, all of them, away from a single missing thing—a hole in the shape of whatever had really happened on the ninth day of the Frostwane moon, a hundred years ago.

The histories didn't disagree because the truth was complicated. They disagreed because they were copies of copies of a story someone had invented, and each copyist had patched the holes differently.

You couldn't forge a document perfectly. But you could forge a tradition—give people songs and holidays and a Litany they memorized as children, and by the second generation they'd defend the invention with their lives, because attacking it felt like attacking themselves.

His whole world was younger than its story.

The valley of Vell, the temple, the debt his family had served for three generations, the sinfulness of curiosity, the sacredness of the Veil—all of it rested on a foundation exactly one century deep, and everything below that line had been dug away and filled with liturgy.

And the prophecy book had been written a century ago. Written, presumably, by someone present at the founding. Someone who knew what the Veil really was. Someone who had left a message addressed to a scribe who wouldn't be born for decades, naming him *Veilbearer*, naming this week of all weeks—

The candle guttered. Elodie became aware, gradually, of how long he'd been down here. Of how conspicuous that might look. Of Brother Cassian asking, gently, at supper, why he seemed tired lately. Of Prior Aldous assigning him, alone, to the one room containing every dangerous fact in the valley.

Of Inquisitor Maren, whom he'd never met, whose arrival the whole temple was whispering about.

He gathered his notes, hesitated, and then did the most scribe-like thing he could think of: he hid them inside honest work. He rewrote his findings as mundane catalogue corrections—*discrepancies noted in Founding-era holdings, flagged for doctrinal review*—the kind of pedantic memo a diligent indentured clerk produces by the dozen. If anyone found them, they'd read as boredom, not heresy.

As he climbed the spiral stair, past the three locked doors, past the reading room where lamplighters were making their rounds, one more detail surfaced in his memory and stopped him on the landing with his hand against the cold wall.

The merchant's journal. He'd catalogued it himself, weeks ago, before any of this. Misfiled among civic records, yes—but not carelessly. The spine had been broken inward, deliberately, so the book would sit on the shelf looking like a tax ledger. Someone, sometime in the last hundred years, had hidden that account and hidden it well.

Which meant someone else had known. Someone else had asked these questions before him. And that someone had either died safely forgotten, or—

Or was still alive. Still watching. Still waiting for the next person to find the holes.

The lamplighter passed him on the landing and wished him good evening, Brother Elodie, cold work down there, and Elodie smiled and said yes, very cold, and walked on with his heart hammering against his ribs like something trying to get out.

At the top of the stairs, in the warm yellow light of the archive corridor, Brother Cassian was waiting for him, leaning against the shelf-end with his arms folded, wearing the mild expression he wore when he'd been waiting a while.

"There you are," Cassian said. "I've been looking everywhere. You missed evening prayer."

"I'm sorry," Elodie said. "The cataloguing ran long."

"Mm." Cassian's eyes went to the folio under his arm, then back to his face, and stayed there a moment too long. "Founding Vault, is it? Heavy reading."

"The dust is older than my grandmother," Elodie said, and heard, distantly, how rehearsed the Prior's own joke sounded coming out of his mouth.

Cassian smiled. It reached his eyes. That was the terrible thing—it always reached his eyes. "Come on, then. Supper's cold, and Prior Aldous wants the discrepancy reports on his desk by week's end."

*Discrepancy reports.* Elodie followed his mentor down the corridor, toward the light and the warmth and the smell of bread, and tried to feel like the same person who had walked down those stairs that morning.

Behind them, at the bottom of the vault, in the dark and the cold, a merchant's broken-spined journal waited on its shelf—and beside it, filed by some long-dead hand in the wrong drawer on purpose, a thin folder marked in faded ink: *Sealing Day — eyewitness fragments — DO NOT BIND.*
