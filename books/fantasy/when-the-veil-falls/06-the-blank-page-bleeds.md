## 6. The Blank Page Bleeds

I did not sleep.

That was the first thing I noticed about the morning—that I had watched it arrive from the wrong side, sitting upright in my cot with the blanket pooled around my waist like something I'd forgotten how to use. The bell over the dormitory had rung for Vigil at some point and I hadn't moved for it. The bell had rung again for Lauds, and still I sat there, listening to the other scribes breathe around me in the dark, each of them anchored to the night by sleep while I drifted above it like a body in floodwater.

The date was real. My name was real. *Elodie Ashgrove* written in ink older than my grandfather's grandfather, on a page bound before anyone alive had been born, describing a day that had not existed until it did.

Coincidence. I said it out loud, into the grey light seeping through the shutters. Coincidence. A scribe's name is a common thing. Half the names in Vell repeat every three generations—Ashgroves have been indentured to this temple since there were ledgers to keep them in. Some long-dead prophet wrote down a name the way you'd write down *bread* or *rain*. It meant nothing. It would mean nothing by noon.

I got dressed. My hands were steady enough to knot the scribe's cord at my collar, which surprised me, and then didn't, because hands learn their work deeper than heads do. Nine years of copying scripture will teach your fingers calm even when everything behind them is screaming.

Brother Cassian was already in the scriptorium when I came down. Of course he was. Cassian occupied the scriptorium the way water occupies a well—totally, quietly, as if the two had always been one thing. He sat at the long desk nearest the window with his spectacles pushed up into his white hair, squinting at a folio of tithing records, and when he heard my steps he smiled without looking up.

"You look like a man who argued with his pillow all night," he said.

"I slept fine."

"Mm." He made a small correction in the margin of the ledger—a tithe of barley mis-entered by young Brother Tomas, probably—and said nothing else about it. That was Cassian's way. He could hold a silence open like a door and let you decide whether to walk through it. I had walked through that door a hundred times in nine years. Every confession I owned had gone through it: the time I broke the reliquary glass, the time I stole honey from the kitchen stores, the time I cried after reading the Lamentations aloud and couldn't stop.

This morning I stood in the doorway and did not walk through it. I felt the lie settle onto me like a second robe.

"Copying duty after Prime?" I asked instead.

"The archive," Cassian said. "Cataloguing. You and I both—the eastern cases, the sealed folios. Sister Verity wants an inventory before the solstice audit." He looked up then, and took off his spectacles, and studied me with those pale, patient eyes that had watched over me since I was eleven years old and orphaned and furious at the world. "Elodie. If something is weighing on you—"

"It's nothing. Tired."

He held my gaze a moment longer than was comfortable. Then he put his spectacles back on and returned to his ledger, and let me go.

That was the first lie. I want it recorded somewhere, in some ledger of its own: that I lied to Brother Cassian before I had done anything else worth lying about, and that the lie cost me more than anything I did afterward. Everything else—the hiding, the running, all of it—I might have excused as fear. But the lie was a choice, made in cold morning light, against a man who had never once lied to me.

Or so I believed, that morning. Which tells you how much any of us knows about the people we love.

* * *

The archive smelled the way it always smelled—dust and vellum and lamp oil and, underneath it, the faint mineral cold of stone that has never once been warm. The eastern cases were the oldest holdings in Vell: folios too fragile or too strange for the open shelves, chained to their cradles, catalogued but rarely read. Nobody came down here. Even Sister Verity, the archivist, treated the east cases like a rich relation she was obligated to feed but preferred not to visit.

Cassian worked the far end. I worked the near end. Between us stretched forty feet of shelving and a silence that felt, for the first time in nine years, like surveillance rather than companionship.

I found the prophecy book where I had left it—in the third case, wedged behind a register of quarry deaths, because I had told myself last night that returning it would be admitting it existed. Now I told myself that retrieving it was only due diligence. A scribe doesn't leave a misfiled volume. A scribe corrects the record. That was all this was. Correction of the record.

The book came out heavy, heavier than I remembered, its cover cracked leather gone soft as old skin, its spine stitched with thread that might have been gut or might have been hair and I chose not to examine which. The clasp was iron, green with age, and it opened under my thumb with a click that sounded obscenely loud in the archive quiet.

I turned past the flyleaf. Past the first page, with its single line in brown-black ink:

*On the eve of the thaw, the Veilbearer speaks.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, dated in the same century-dead ink:

*Tenthday, Thirdmonth, the year of the Long Peace.* Today.

And below *that*—

Below that, where yesterday there had been nothing but blank vellum, there were words.

I don't remember deciding to sit down. I was simply sitting, the case's edge biting into my thighs, the book open across my knees, my breath coming in a way I could hear myself doing, which is never a good sign. Because the new entry was not in the old hand. Or—it was, and it wasn't. The letters were the same letters, the same cramped, slanted script I had matched against a hundred archival samples yesterday to convince myself the book was genuine. But the ink was wrong. Yesterday's ink had been brown-black, oxidized, a hundred years dead. This ink was fresh. Wet, almost, at the tail of the final stroke, glistening faintly in the lamplight as though it had finished drying only moments before I touched the clasp.

As though it had been waiting for me.

*The scribe runs,* it read. *The Inquisitor follows.*

I read it four times. Five. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything more merciful. They sat there on the page, wet and black and small, and they knew things they should not have known. Not *a scribe*. *The scribe.* Not *an inquisitor*, in the abstract way the temple taught that Inquisitors had always existed and always would, like weather. *The Inquisitor.* Definite article. Proper dread.

Because here is what I had not told anyone, what I had barely admitted to myself: word had come down from the capital two days ago. Before the book. Before the date. A rider in temple grey, delivering notice to Father Abbot that the Office of Inquiry was dispatching an Inquisitor to Vell on routine inspection of provincial archives. Routine. Everyone said routine. But I had seen the way Father Abbot's face went when he read the letter, and I had spent nine years learning to read faces the way I read script, and his face had said *not routine* in a language anyone could understand.

An Inquisitor was coming. And a century-old book, hidden in a case no one opened, had written down that I would run from her.

Not *might*. The prophecy book did not deal in might. Yesterday it had named today's date and my name, and both had arrived on schedule. Today it named a chase that had not yet happened, written in ink that was not yet dry, and the only honest reading of that was the terrible one:

The book was not remembering the future. It was scheduling it.

"No," I said. Out loud, into the archive dark. A foolish, human word. As if the book were a horse I could talk down, or a child I could correct.

Down the aisle, forty feet away, Cassian's pen stopped scratching.

I closed the book so fast I pinched my finger in it and didn't feel the pain until later. I shoved it back into the case, behind the quarry register, and stood, and called out in a voice I was proud of later for how level it sounded: "Just dropped a folio. All well."

The pen resumed. Scratch, scratch, scratch. Steady as ever.

Steady as ever. That was the thought that followed me around for the rest of the day, through Prime and through the copying shift and through the midday meal where I ate nothing and rearranged bread crumbs into patterns I didn't look at. Cassian was steady as ever. The temple was steady as ever. The whole valley of Vell hummed along in its magicless, lawful, airless way, and somewhere inside it, inside *me*, was a book writing down the ending before the story had even begun to happen.

*The scribe runs.*

I didn't want to run. That was the thing nobody who found that book would ever understand, the thing I could not explain to Sera or to Maren or to anyone who came afterward asking why I did what I did: I loved Vell. I loved it the way you love a small room you've lived in all your life—its narrowness, its familiar drafts, the way you can cross it blind. I loved the temple bells and the terraced fields going gold in autumn and the smell of the tanneries by the river that everyone complained about and that I would have missed within a day of leaving. Running was not in my nature. Staying, enduring, keeping the ledger balanced—those were my natures. Those were the natures the debt had bred into me, generation after generation of Ashgroves who paid what was owed and asked no questions, because questions were how families like mine ended up owing more.

But the book said I ran. And the book, so far, had a perfect record.

There is a particular kind of panic that comes from being told your own next move by something older than your family name. It isn't fear exactly. Fear has direction—you flee from a thing toward safety. This was worse. This was the sensation of discovering that your legs belonged to someone else, that the script of your life had been drafted a century ago by a hand you'd never meet, and that every step you took to escape the page only pressed you deeper into the ink. I caught myself, twice, that afternoon, planning the run. Testing it. The gate warden changes at dusk; the western postern sticks unless you lift the latch just so; the road past the mill is watched but the drainage culvert under the wall is not, because no one in Vell imagines anyone would want to leave. I caught myself mapping my own flight like a rat memorizing walls, and hated the book for making me into that, and hated myself for obeying it even in imagination.

Which is when I understood the third thing about the book, the thing that finally broke whatever calm I had left.

It didn't need to force me anywhere. It only needed to tell me, and let my own terror do the walking. Prophecy, I realized, standing in the scriptorium with a stylus gone motionless in my hand while the words I was supposed to be copying blurred on the page in front of me—prophecy is a leash made of fear. You don't drag a man with it. You show him the far end of the yard, and he runs there himself, and calls it choice.

*The scribe runs.* Fine. Then the scribe would not run—not where it expected, not when it expected, not in any way it could write down. If the book wanted to author me, I would be the most uncooperative sentence it had ever set down.

That resolution lasted approximately six hours, which I record here as a matter of honesty, because everything afterward depended on understanding how little resolve is actually worth against a well-written page.

* * *

Nightfall. The bell rang for Compline, and the temple emptied toward the chapel, and I went the other way, down the servants' stair with its worn hollows in the stone, out into the cloister garth where the moon lay flat and silver on the flagstones.

The well stands at the center of the garth, as it has stood since before the founding, since before everything, a ring of old granite around a shaft so deep that the brothers joke—quietly, because jokes about depth are near blasphemy in Vell—that it goes down further than the temple's foundations, further than the mountain's roots, further than the Veil itself. There is no bucket anymore. The water was declared tainted a generation ago and piped in from the springhouse instead, and the well was capped with an iron grate and forgotten, which makes it the only place in the temple grounds where something can disappear completely and no one thinks to ask why.

I knelt at the grate. My hands were shaking now—finally, honestly shaking, all the steadiness spent—and the iron was cold enough to bite.

Here is what I had decided, in the long ugly hours between Compline and now, pacing the dormitory while the other scribes slept: I could not destroy the book. I had tried. After supper, alone in the copying room, I had held a candle flame to a corner of its cover until the wax ran and my fingers burned, and the leather would not catch. Would not scorch. It drank the fire the way the blank page drank my ink, and I sat there looking at my blistered thumb and understood that the book intended to survive me, whatever I decided. So: not destroyed. Hidden. Buried where no Inquisitor's inventory would find it, where no curious archivist would reach, where even I could not get to it quickly enough to obey it.

If it wanted me to run, it could wait at the bottom of a well.

I pried up the grate. The sound of iron on granite rang across the cloister and I froze, heart slamming, counting ten, twenty, thirty—nothing. Only the wind in the cypress, only the distant murmur of the chapel hymn. I lifted the grate aside and looked down into the shaft, and the darkness down there was total, absolute, a blackness with texture and weight, and I had the sudden irrational certainty that the darkness looked back.

"Don't," I whispered to it—to the book, to the dark, to whoever had written me a hundred years before I was born. "Whatever you think happens next. Don't."

Then I wrapped the book in my spare sash, tied it fast, and lowered it into Vell's oldest throat, and let it drop. I listened. I waited for the splash.

There was none.

The rope of sash paid out, and paid out, longer than I'd wrapped it, longer than the sash could possibly have been, and then it simply stopped—taut, quivering slightly, as though the bundle had come to rest on something. A ledge. A lip. A floor that should not be there at that depth. I hauled it back up, because I am a scribe and curiosity is supposed to be my sin and yet I have never once managed it, and the bundle came up lighter than it went down, and inside the wrapping the book was gone.

Only the sash remained, tied around nothing, smelling faintly of cold stone.

I knelt there at the mouth of the well with an empty cloth in my hands and the hymn drifting thin across the garth, and I felt the last of my ordinary life drain away through that iron-ringed hole. The book was not in the well. The book had never been in the well. The well was a hundred feet of granite and darkness and it had swallowed my one piece of evidence and given back a knot, and somewhere in the temple above me an Inquisitor was already on the road, or perhaps already through the gate, and a book I could no longer hide was writing down what I did next.

*The scribe runs,* it had said. *The Inquisitor follows.*

I replaced the grate. I smoothed the moss back over the iron scars. I walked back across the moonlit cloister with my empty hands and my full head, and I was halfway to the servants' stair when I saw the lantern light moving along the gallery above the garth—two figures, temple grey, moving with the unhurried purpose of people who know exactly which door they are walking toward.

One wore the robes of the temple watch.

The other wore a traveler's cloak the color of dried blood, and carried no lantern of her own, and walked as though the darkness had been instructed to make way for her.

They stopped at the guest wing door. The watchman bowed. The figure in the blood-dark cloak paused on the threshold—paused, and turned her head, slowly, and looked out across the garth, directly at the well, directly at the shadow beside the well where I stood not-breathing.

She could not have seen me. The moon was behind her. It was physically impossible, and I knew it was impossible, and I stood there anyway while she held the look for five heartbeats, six, seven, as if reading something written on the air where I stood.

Then she went inside, and the door closed, and I stood alone in the dark knowing—with the same terrible certainty the book wrote in wet ink—that the Inquisitor had come to Vell.

And that she had come for me.
