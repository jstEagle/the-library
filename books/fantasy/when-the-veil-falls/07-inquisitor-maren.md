## 7. Inquisitor Maren

The rain found Vell before I did.

I had been riding since the boundary pass opened at dawn, and by the time the valley's walls rose around me—those soft green shoulders of hills that the maps called the Veil's Edge, as if naming a thing made it understood—the weather had turned from mist to a steady grey drizzle that soaked through wool and settled in the bones. The temple wardens who met me at the gate did not expect me. That was the point. An Inquisitor who announces herself arrives to find a cleaned house. An Inquisitor who arrives unannounced finds the dust still on the sins.

Brother Aldous, the gatekeeper, squinted at my credentials twice before his face arranged itself into the particular expression I had learned to read across three dioceses: not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of a man deciding how much of himself to hide. He bowed. He offered an umbrella. He asked, with the studied casualness of the guilty, whether I would be wanting to see the Prior.

"Eventually," I said. "First I'd like to see the archive."

"The archive is closed for cataloguing, Inquisitor. Brother Cassian's orders. There's been a great deal of... reorganization."

"Reorganization." I let the word sit between us like a coin on a table. "How long?"

"A fortnight, perhaps. Perhaps longer. These things take—" 

"I'll find my own way."

He hesitated, and in that hesitation told me everything: that the archive was not merely closed but guarded, that someone had given orders about who might enter, and that those orders came from someone who feared what cataloguing might uncover—or what it might already have uncovered.

I did not go to the archive. Not yet. A hunter who walks straight at the quarry teaches the quarry the shape of her stride. Instead I asked Aldous to show me to the guest quarters, thanked him warmly, complimented the temple's famous honey wine, and spent the afternoon being visible: walking the cloister, blessing a sick novice, accepting tea from the Prior himself, a soft man named Ostell whose hands trembled slightly when he poured and whose eyes kept flicking toward the archive wing as if it were a wound he could feel through the temple's stone skin.

Everyone performed normalcy for me. That was how I knew something was wrong.

* * *

Heresy has a smell.

People imagine the Inquisition works from evidence—documents, testimony, the careful weighing of contradictions—and of course there is some of that, tedious and necessary. But after eleven years, I have learned that heresy announces itself first in smaller ways. In a scribe who copies scripture with a hand too careful, as if each letter were being watched. In a kitchen boy who stops humming. In a community that smiles a half-second too quickly when you ask an innocent question, the way a debtor smiles at the creditor's knock.

Vell smelled of all of these things at once.

The valley itself was part of it. I had served in the lowlands, in cities where magic was a rumor and the Veil a story told to children, and I had thought I understood what a magicless place felt like. I had not. Vell was not merely without magic; it was *sealed against* it, and the sealing had a texture. The air lay flat. Sound carried strangely, as if the valley resented being heard. And beneath it all—if you had been trained as I had been trained, if you had spent years learning to feel for the absence where power should pool—there was a pulse. Faint. Slow. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall, muffled and patient and very much alive.

The official doctrine said the Veil was a protection. The Founding Liturgy said the twelve families gave everything so their descendants could live in peace, wrapped safe from the war-mad world beyond. I had recited it myself as a girl. I had believed it the way one believes in the floor beneath one's feet.

But I had also read the fragments. Three pages, recovered from a burned scriptorium in the eastern reaches two years ago, smuggled to me by a dying archivist who would not say where the rest had gone. Fragments of a prophecy older than the Liturgy, written in a hand that predated every calendar the temple used. It spoke of a Veilbearer. It spoke of a choice. And it named a date—a date that meant nothing to me when I read it, because I had no way of knowing that a century later, in a valley I had never visited, a debt-bound scribe would open a sealed book on precisely that day.

I knew now. The archivist's fragments had included more than prophecy. They had included a list. Twelve family names, and beside them, in a different ink, a single word repeated: *watched*. Eleven of the names had been struck through over the decades by hands unknown. One remained.

Ashgrove.

* * *

I requested the temple's debt ledgers on my second morning, citing a routine audit of indenture practices—a fiction so bland no one questions it, because no one wants to think about the ledgers at all. The ledger-keeper, a nervous woman named Sister Prue, brought me the current volume and stood waiting while I read, which told me she had instructions not to leave me alone with it.

I sent her away anyway. She went. Fear obeys faster than loyalty.

The Ashgrove entry was easy to find. Debts are recorded with a clerk's indifference, but this one had been handled with unusual care: a quarry death, a widow pensioned off to distant kin, and a son—Elodie, aged fourteen—whose family obligation had been converted into a lifetime indenture to the temple scriptorium. The conversion had been approved personally by Brother Cassian, the Master of Archives, with a notation in his own hand: *"The boy shows aptitude. Let his service honor his father."*

Aptitude. I underlined nothing. I memorized instead.

For three days I moved through the temple like weather—present everywhere, noticed nowhere. I copied out duty rosters. I learned which novices avoided the archive wing and which lingered near it. I learned that Brother Cassian had taken to eating his meals alone, which he had not done before, according to the refectory sisters, and that he had requested leave to visit the boundary shrines, which was denied, and that he had then requested it again, which was unprecedented.

And I learned about Elodie Ashgrove the way one learns about anyone in a small community: sideways, in pieces, from people who thought they were saying nothing. The scribes called him precise. The kitchen staff called him kind. One elderly sister, whom I caught feeding scraps to the temple cats behind the laundry, called him *haunted*, and when I pressed her gently on the word she said only: "He looks at doors like they might open on their own."

Doors. I wrote that down that night, in cipher, in the journal I kept sewn into the lining of my coat.

On the fourth day, I stopped performing.

* * *

I found him in the copying hall at dusk, alone, which surprised me until I understood it didn't surprise me at all. The hall was a long room of slanted desks lit by oil lamps, smelling of oak gall ink and vellum dust, and he sat at the far end with his head bent over a page of scripture, his pen moving with the mechanical grace of long habit. He did not hear me come in. Or—he did hear me, and chose not to react, which was its own kind of answer.

"Inquisitor Maren," I said, introducing myself to his back. "You'll forgive the intrusion. I understand the hall is usually fuller."

"It empties at evening prayer." His voice was level. He set down his pen and turned, and I saw a young man of perhaps twenty, lean, dark-haired, with the pale complexion of someone who lived indoors and the watchful eyes of someone who had learned early that watching was survival. "Brother Cassian will return shortly, if you're looking for him."

"I'm looking for you, actually. Elodie Ashgrove."

Something crossed his face—not fear, not yet, but the space just before fear, the held breath. "Is there a problem with my work?"

"No problem at all. Your hand is remarkable. I've seen your copies of the Litanies—clean, faithful, not a flourish wasted. There's discipline in it." I smiled, and made sure the smile reached my eyes, because courtesy disarms better than menace and costs less. "May I sit?"

He gestured to the neighboring desk. I sat. We regarded each other across the gap of a single lamp, and I took my time arranging my coat, my papers, my presence, letting the silence do its slow work. Interrogation, done properly, is mostly patience. The questions are almost incidental. What matters is making a person feel that the truth is already known, and that lying is merely choosing the harder path.

"You've served here six years," I said. "Since your father died."

"Yes."

"That must have been difficult. The quarry collapse. I read the record."

His jaw tightened fractionally. "The temple was generous."

"The temple was generous," I agreed pleasantly. "It usually is, at the moment of grief, when generosity costs nothing and binds forever. Tell me—do you resent it? The debt?"

"I don't think about it."

"Everyone thinks about it. The question is whether thinking about it has made you angry or made you careful. My experience says it makes people one or the other, and rarely both." I leaned back. "Which are you, Elodie Ashgrove?"

"I'm a scribe, Inquisitor. I copy what I'm given."

"And what have you been given lately?"

The lamp flame shivered in a draft from the corridor. Neither of us looked at it. This was the moment the interrogation truly began—not with a question but with a silence, and I let it stretch until the silence itself became an accusation, until he understood that I could wait longer than he could breathe.

"There was a book," I said at last, softly, conversationally, as though remarking on the weather. "In your archive. Sealed. A century old, give or take. Catalogued under founding-era materials, though the catalogue entry is falsified—it lists a binding date forty years after the book was made. Someone wanted it forgotten, or wanted it found only by the right person. Do you know it?"

"I catalogued hundreds of books this month."

"Mm. But this one refused your ink."

That landed. I watched it land—the tiny contraction around his eyes, the way his hand drifted toward the desk edge as if the wood might anchor him. He was good, better than most, but no one is trained for the sentence that names the secret they believe is buried.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course not." I produced, from my coat, a folded paper—not the fragments themselves, never the originals, but a fair copy I had made in my own hand, disguised as scripture commentary. I unfolded it and laid it on the desk between us, facing him. "Then this will mean nothing to you. A curiosity from my travels. Read it aloud, if you would. I find old texts settle better in the ear."

He looked down. I watched him read the words I had copied from the burned scriptorium's surviving page:

*"In the hundredth year, the scribe of the low line shall open the sealed word, and the Veilbearer speaks, and the valley's sleep shall trouble."*

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the temple's foundations settled with a sound like a sigh.

"Old nonsense," he said. His voice was steady. His knuckles were white.

"Isn't it?" I folded the paper away. "And yet the temple keeps nonsense under seal, and falsifies its catalogue, and closes its archive for 'reorganization' the very month a stranger arrives asking questions. Nonsense, in my experience, is the most carefully protected material in any holy house. Truth defends itself. Only lies need guards." I stood, unhurried, and gathered my coat. "Thank you for your time, scribe. You've been very helpful."

"I haven't told you anything."

"No," I said, smiling down at him with genuine warmth, because in a strange way he had told me everything. "You haven't. That's how I know."

At the door I paused, and delivered the sentence I had carried up from the lowlands for this exact moment, the sentence I had been saving like a blade kept sheathed:

"One more thing. The fragments I mentioned—they're incomplete. Pages missing. Whoever holds the whole book knows things I don't. Dates. Names. Perhaps even..." I let my gaze rest on him, mild and terrible. "*Instructions.* Books like that rarely predict, Elodie Ashgrove. They teach. And somewhere in this temple, someone is being taught right now."

I left him with the lamp and the silence and the words settling into him like rain into old plaster.

* * *

In the corridor outside, Brother Cassian was waiting for me.

I had expected this too. He stood beneath the hall's arched entrance with his hands folded into his sleeves, an older man gone grey at the temples, with a scholar's stoop and a face built for kindness—which is the most dangerous kind of face, because it trains everyone around it to lower their guard. Forty years ago, I suspected, that face had belonged to someone else entirely. Faces do. So do men.

"Inquisitor." He bowed. "I hope our scribe hasn't tired you."

"Not at all. A charming young man. Precise. Careful." I returned the bow precisely enough to signal respect and shallowly enough to signal nothing else. "You've raised him well, Brother Cassian. Or the temple has. It's sometimes hard to tell which."

"He needed raising. His family owed much."

"So I've read. Curious, isn't it—how often the children of the indebted find themselves placed exactly where the temple needs watching eyes." I tilted my head. "Or perhaps I have it backward. Perhaps the temple places its watchers where the debts are. Either way, convenient."

Cassian's expression did not change, which was itself a change. A man with nothing to hide grows puzzled at such remarks; a man with something to hide grows still. He had grown still as stone.

"If you're investigating heresy, Inquisitor, I can assure you the archive is in order."

"I'm sure it is. Order is what worries me. Your archive has been closed for a fortnight. Your requests to visit the boundary shrines were denied—twice, which I'm told is unprecedented. You dine alone. And the last person to borrow a certain sealed volume, according to a borrowing record that someone neglected to burn quite thoroughly—" I watched his breath catch, a small animal sound he swallowed immediately, "—was you. Forty years ago."

The corridor stretched between us, lamplit and empty. When he finally spoke, his voice was gentle, almost sorrowful, the voice of a teacher correcting a favored student.

"You've been reading ashes, Inquisitor. Whatever record you found, whatever fragments—ashes tell any story you ask of them. I'd caution you against building certainties on cinders."

"And I'd caution you," I said, "against assuming I build anything on cinders. I build on patterns. And the pattern, Brother Cassian, is this: a century ago, twelve families did something to this valley, and the temple wrote a beautiful story over the wound, and once every generation or so, someone starts pulling at the thread. Last time, forty years ago, it was a candidate—a young person, I believe, with a name the records no longer contain. That attempt ended badly. You would know. You were there."

Something moved behind his eyes then—grief, or guilt, or the memory of both wearing each other's clothes—and for one unguarded instant Brother Cassian looked his true age, which was older than his face, older than his office, older than the lie he had been keeping.

"You don't know what you're digging toward," he said quietly.

"I know what I dig *for*," I answered. "Truth. It's the only thing I've ever wanted. It's why I'm dangerous, and why you should tell me now, tonight, while telling me is still cheaper than hiding from me."

He smiled then—a real smile, sad and brief, the smile of a man watching a younger version of himself walk into a fire he remembers. "Inquisitor Maren. When you have been in this valley as long as I have, you learn that some truths are not doors. They're weights. Lifting one does not free you. It only changes what crushes you."

"Poetry," I said. "The temple's favorite substitute for answers."

I left him in the corridor and walked out into the drizzle, past the cloister, past the chapel where evening prayer murmured like bees in a jar, and stood a while in the wet dark of the courtyard, feeling the valley's muffled pulse beneath my feet—that patient heartbeat behind the wall—and considering.

Three threads, now, and I could see how they braided. A scribe who had touched a living prophecy. A mentor who had touched it first and paid for it in forty years of silence. And beyond the Veil—an order, an exile, a hunger I could feel pressing against the valley's skin like a face against glass. The fragments had spoken of watchers. I had assumed, reading them in my cold rooms in the east, that the watchers were the temple.

I no longer assumed. The temple was a cage. Cages have keepers, but they also have thieves, and somewhere out in that dead flat air, someone was already moving toward the same prize I was.

The question that mattered—the question I turned over as the rain soaked my shoulders—was not whether Elodie Ashgrove was the Veilbearer. That was nearly certain now, and certainty is cheap. The question was what a Veilbearer was *for*. The Liturgy said protector. The fragments said chooser. And the pulse beneath the valley said, in its slow patient language, *prisoner*.

Prisons, I have learned, have three kinds of visitors: those who guard them, those who feed them, and those who come to open the door.

I intended to discover, before anyone else did, which kind had already entered Vell.

Behind me, in the copying hall, a single lamp still burned above an unfinished page of scripture. I did not see the young scribe return to it. I did not see him sit motionless for a long time, nor the way his eyes went, again and again, to the dark window—as if the night itself had begun to speak in a handwriting he recognized.

But I would have expected nothing less.

The hunter, after all, was already inside the house.
