## 9. Debt of the Ashgroves

The rain came sideways that morning, the way it always did in Vell in the last month of harvest—flat and gray and without any opinion about where it fell. Elodie was nine years old, and he remembered thinking, with the strange clarity children keep for the worst days of their lives, that the rain looked like the ledger ink his father complained about: endless, gray, covering everything.

He had been sitting under the eaves of the quarry office, waiting for his father to finish his shift, when the bell began to ring.

Not the temple bell. The quarry bell—the one they rang for cave-ins, for floods, for the things that happened twice a decade and were spoken of afterward in the flat voices of men who had learned not to feel anything before supper.

His mother's hand closed around his shoulder before he could stand. She didn't say anything. That was the first thing he understood about grief in Vell: nobody said anything, because saying something cost money you didn't have, and words, like everything else in the valley, had to be paid for.

* * *

Three days earlier, his father had come home with chalk dust in the creases of his knuckles and a joke on his lips. This was unusual. Tam Ashgrove was not a joking man; he was a careful man, a man who counted his steps and his coins and his words with the same methodical patience he brought to splitting stone. But that night he'd set down his supper bowl and said, "Elodie. Do you know why they call it a *vein* of stone?"

Elodie shook his head.

"Because the mountain bleeds when you cut it." His father had smiled then, tired and crooked. "And every man who works her learns to bleed a little back. It's only fair."

His mother had told him to stop filling the boy's head with quarry talk. His father had said the boy should know where his bread came from. And Elodie, nine years old and full of the valley's quiet hunger for stories—for *any* stories, because Vell permitted so few—had memorized the line the way he would later memorize scripture: word for word, breath for breath, as if it might be tested.

It was tested. Just not by anyone he expected.

* * *

The cave-in happened at the third seam, the deep cut, the one the overseers called the Widow's Gallery because everyone knew what the name meant even though no one said it aloud. Eleven men went in that morning. Four came out.

Tam Ashgrove was not among the four.

They kept Elodie and his mother at the edge of the pit for two days while the crews worked. He remembered the smell—wet stone and lamp oil and something underneath both that he refused, even years later, to name. He remembered the temple's almoner arriving in his gray robes, dry under an oiled canopy while everyone else stood in the rain, and he remembered the almoner's voice: gentle, unhurried, practiced. The voice of a man who had delivered this exact speech many times and had refined it the way a mason refines a chisel stroke.

"The temple grieves with you," the almoner said. "Brother Tam was a faithful servant of the valley."

Elodie's mother made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

"Of course," the almoner continued, "there is the matter of the account."

* * *

Here is what Elodie learned in the weeks that followed, learning it the way stone learns water—slowly, by being worn:

His father had owed the temple eleven marks. It was not a large debt, as debts went. It had begun as a loan against a bad winter, seven years back, when the crops failed and Tam had done the thing every family in Vell eventually did: he had walked to the temple counting-house and put his name in a book. The interest was small. The interest was always described as small. But the interest compounded in the dark, the way damp spreads through a wall, and by the year of the cave-in the eleven marks had become nineteen, and Tam had been paying four coppers a week against it for seven years and the number had never once gone down.

Elodie did the arithmetic himself, later, in the margins of a practice sheet, when he was old enough to understand compound interest and brave enough to hate it. Four coppers a week, fifty-two weeks a year, seven years. One hundred forty-five marks paid against a debt of nineteen.

He showed the figures to no one. There was no one to show them to who wouldn't already know, and knowing changed nothing. The ledger was a temple document. The temple wrote the ledgers. The temple read the ledgers. In Vell, the man who holds the pen holds everything, and this, Elodie understood much later, was the closest thing the valley had to magic—a power that worked invisibly, at a distance, on paper, changing the world without ever appearing to touch it.

* * *

The widow's settlement was read aloud in the temple counting-house on a gray morning, and Elodie stood beside his mother while a clerk with beautiful handwriting explained their options.

There were, the clerk said, three.

The first was payment in full. Nineteen marks, due within the season. The clerk said this gently, the way you mention weather to someone whose house has just washed away.

The second was forfeiture. The house on Tanner's Row, held against the debt since the original loan, would pass to the temple. Mother and son would be released into the community—"with the temple's blessing," the clerk added, as if the blessing were a coin pressed into the palm rather than a receipt for everything they owned.

The third option was the one the clerk's voice softened around, the way a road softens around a bend it doesn't want you to see past.

"The temple," he said, "is prepared to offer a conversion."

Elodie's mother asked what that meant.

"It means," the clerk said, "that the temple recognizes its loss too. Brother Tam was valuable to us. The temple does not wish the Ashgrove name to end in a ledger's red column. So we offer this: the debt is forgiven—entirely, today—and in exchange, the temple receives a term of service. Scribal labor. Room and board provided. A trade taught. At the end of the term, the young master walks out with a skill no quarry will ever give him, and the Ashgroves owe nothing to anyone."

He turned the page toward them. Beautiful handwriting. Beautiful terms.

"The term," Elodie's mother said, reading, and her voice went flat in a way he had never heard before and would not hear again until the day Maren smiled at him across an archive table, "is twenty years."

"He would begin at ten," the clerk agreed. "Which is young, yes. But the temple prefers to train scribes young. The hand is more supple. And consider"—and here the clerk leaned forward, and his kindness became almost unbearable in its sincerity—"consider what we are forgiving. Nineteen marks. A house. Consider what we are giving. Literacy. Position. Security. There are mothers in this valley who would kneel in the mud for such an offer."

Elodie believed, for many years, that this was true. That was the genius of it. The offer was real. The literacy was real. The room and board and the trade were real, and they were worth more than most children in Vell would ever hold. If you measured the offer against the life waiting outside the temple doors, the temple was generous.

If you measured it against what was taken—against a father, against a house, against twenty years of a boy's life priced out in coppers per week—it was something else entirely. But there was no way to make that second measurement inside the valley, because the valley had no unit for it. Grief was not legal tender. The ledgers had no column for it.

His mother signed. Her hand shook, and the clerk waited patiently for it to finish shaking, and then he blotted the page with a small sand shaker, gently, like a man closing a coffin lid.

* * *

The night before Elodie entered the temple, his mother sat him down at the kitchen table—the table that would belong to someone else within the month—and gave him the only inheritance the Ashgroves had left.

She gave him his name.

"You know why we're called Ashgrove?" she said. "Your father used to say it was because his grandfather planted trees in burned ground. After some fire or other. Nobody remembers which fire." She turned her cup in her hands. "Your father thought that was the whole duty of a man, do you see? To plant in burned ground. Not to pretend the ground isn't burned. Not to curse the fire. Just—to plant."

"I'll write to you," Elodie said. "Every week. Scribes can write."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone, and he was too young to read it. He would read it decades later, lying awake in a novice cell with a prophecy book hidden under his floorboard: it was the look of a woman who knows exactly how letters work, who knows that outgoing correspondence from temple novices passes through three sets of hands before it leaves the building, and who has decided, in this one moment, not to teach her son that lesson because teaching it would take away the last good thing he believed in.

"Yes," she said. "Write every week."

He wrote every week for six years. He received four letters. Each one was short, warm, and slightly wrong in its details—mentioning neighbors who had moved away, festivals on dates that didn't match the calendar—as though written from memory, or from a template, or by someone who had never met her at all.

By the time he stopped noticing the wrongness, he had already stopped writing. The temple's schedule had a way of eating the weeks. That, too, was part of the design. He understood this only now, at twenty-nine, standing in an archive that smelled of dust and secrets, holding a book that had rewritten itself in his own name: the temple did not need to forbid anything. It needed only to fill your hours until the forbidden thing became the forgotten thing.

Institutions, Elodie thought, do not devour you the way wolves do. They devour you the way the valley devours rain—patiently, everywhere at once, until the ground is dry and no one can remember what wet felt like.

* * *

He visited his mother once a year, on the feast of the Sealing, when novices were given half a day. She aged quickly and quietly, the way the poor do, and she never once asked him about the temple's inner workings, and he never once volunteered them, and between those two silences they built a love that fit inside a single afternoon a year the way water fits inside a crack—thin, persistent, finding its way through anyway.

On her last visitable year, she took his hands across the table and said, "You've got his hands. Your father's hands. Ink instead of chalk, but the same hands."

"Do you regret it?" he asked. He had never dared ask before. "Signing. Me."

She considered the question honestly, which was the cruelest kindness available to her.

"I regret the price," she said. "I don't regret the purchase. You can read, Elodie. You can write. You can count, which in this valley is nearly the same as being able to see through walls." She squeezed his fingers. "But listen to me. Listen, because I won't say it well twice. They bought your hands, not your head. Whatever they tell you to copy—read it anyway. Read everything. Reading is free, once they've taught you. It's the only thing in that building that's free."

He was fifteen, and he nodded, and he did not understand her until tonight.

Tonight, with a century-old prophecy open in front of him and his own name staring up from the page like a wound, he finally understood what she had been telling him all along: *they will give you the pen, my love, because a pen in a cage writes only what the cage allows—but the eyes, the eyes they cannot bind.*

* * *

Now, in the present, Elodie sat alone in the archive after midnight with the prophecy book closed beneath his palms, and he thought about debts.

He thought about how the temple had converted his father's death into his servitude with a signature and a sand shaker. He thought about how the valley converted its fear into faith, its ignorance into virtue, its poverty into proof of piety. Everything in Vell was a conversion. Nothing was created; nothing was destroyed; grief went in at one end and obedience came out the other, and the temple took its tithe at every stage of the exchange.

And he thought—lying awake in his cell later, watching moonlight crawl across the wall like a slow gray worm—that the blank page in the prophecy book had refused his ink for exactly the reason his father's blood had refused to stay in the mountain.

Some things are not meant to be covered over.

The mountain bled. The ledger filled. And somewhere beneath the neat columns of the world, a number was still running that no one had ever finished counting—a debt owed not by the Ashgroves but *to* them, or perhaps by them, he couldn't yet tell which, a debt a century old with interest compounding in the dark.

Tomorrow, Brother Cassian would notice the shadows under his eyes and ask him, kindly, what was wrong.

And Elodie—who had learned from the age of nine exactly how institutions feed, and had never once imagined he would feed one himself—would open his mouth and lie to the only father he had left.
