## 10. The Well Shaft

The well behind the temple of Vell had not given water in eleven years, and Inquisitor Maren thought that was the most honest thing about it.

She stood at its lip in the grey hour before dawn, when the wardens changed watch and the brothers were still abed, and she looked down into a darkness that smelled of wet stone and old rope. Below her, forty feet down, the shaft ended in a floor of silt and dropped coins—wishes, the valley folk called them, though Maren had never met a wish that came true. She had spent three nights confirming what a child could have told her: the well was dry, the rope was rotten, and the bucket at the bottom held nothing but silt.

And yet the book had been here. She was certain of it.

Certain, because certainty was her trade. She had spent nineteen years as an inquisitor learning to read absence the way other people read words—the drawer that stuck because something had been removed from it too often, the shelf whose dust lay undisturbed except for one clean rectangle, the novice who suddenly stopped asking questions. Absence had a shape. It had weight. And the well shaft below her wore its shape like a confession.

Someone had lowered something heavy on a line recently enough that the rope fibers still bore the stress. Someone had hauled it back up again, badly, in haste, leaving fresh scoring on the stone lip where the rope had dragged. And someone had done all of it within the last two days, because the rain two nights past had washed the courtyard clean, and the drag marks in the mud beside the well were newer than the rain.

Maren crouched and touched one of the marks with a gloved finger. Mud pressed flat by a knee. A boot heel, narrow, a scribe's boot or an acolyte's.

*The scribe runs,* the fragment had said. *The Inquisitor follows.*

She had read those words four days ago, in a stolen glimpse of a book that should not exist, in a room that should have been empty, belonging to a young man who should have been asleep. She had read them and felt the peculiar cold that only true prophecy produced—not the temple's manufactured portents, not the harvest blessings and debt-forgiveness verses the brothers copied out by the thousand, but the real thing, the old thing, text that moved like a living creature under glass.

*The scribe runs. The Inquisitor follows.*

It had named her before she knew she would come. That was the trouble with prophecy, and the reason the Order feared it above all weapons: it did not describe the future. It *assigned* it. Every person who read their own line became an actor reading stage directions, and no actor, however unwilling, ever quite escaped the part.

Well. Maren had spent her whole life being cast against her will. She could play this role too—and she intended to play it better than the author expected.

She rose, brushed the mud from her knee, and walked back across the courtyard with the unhurried stride of a woman who belonged exactly where she stood. The night warden, Brother Tomas, nodded to her from the gate. She nodded back. Everyone in Vell nodded to the Inquisitor; it was cheaper than conversation.

The book was gone from the well. Which meant the scribe had panicked, hidden it, then retrieved it—which meant he had decided he needed it more than safety required him to be rid of it. That was interesting. A frightened man throws evidence away. A determined man keeps it close.

Elodie Ashgrove was not running yet. But he was deciding to.

Maren climbed the outer stair to the guest cell the temple had assigned her—a courtesy she had neither requested nor needed, since she slept perhaps three hours a night and did it sitting up—and took out her papers. Not the official ones, the dossiers on heresy and unlicensed curiosity that justified her presence to the temple's council. The other papers. The ones that lived folded inside the lining of her case, written in a cipher of her own devising, addressed to no one.

On the top sheet, beneath yesterday's entry, she wrote:

*Book recovered from hiding. Subject retains possession. Subject is choosing.*

She looked at the words for a long moment. Then, because the morning was grey and honest, she added:

*I am following a script I have read. Find the flaw in that.*

There wasn't one. There never was. That was the whole horror of her position, the joke the universe had played on her specifically: she had come to Vell hunting a prophecy, and the prophecy had already hunted her first. Somewhere in that moving text there might be a line about her—about what she did next, about how it ended. She refused to look for it. Looking was how they got you. You found your name, and after that every choice you made felt like obedience, until obedience was all you had left.

Better to be the hunter who never checked the page. Better to move blind and call it freedom.

She sealed the papers away and went down to breakfast.

* * *

The refectory of the temple of Vell served the same meal every day of the year: barley porridge, black bread, tea of dried nettle. Maren had eaten in a hundred refectories across a dozen provinces, and she could date any one of them by its bread. Vell's was dense and joyless and cut thick, food designed to sustain labor rather than please the tongue, and the brothers ate it in silence while a reader recited scripture from the pulpit—the same twelve verses on humility that had been recited every morning for as long as the temple's records showed.

Maren sat at the visitors' bench with her back to the wall, her porridge untouched, and watched Elodie Ashgrove eat.

He sat with the other indentured scribes at the far table, and he was performing normalcy with the desperate thoroughness of a man who had rehearsed it. He passed the bread. He nodded at jokes. He kept his eyes down and his hands busy. All correct. All exactly what an innocent man would do.

That was the problem. Innocent men didn't perform innocence. They simply were, lumpishly, irritatingly themselves—they complained about the porridge, they forgot to pass the bread, they stared at the Inquisitor with the open, stupid hostility of the young. Elodie Ashgrove did none of these things. He was smooth. He was careful. He had the particular stillness of someone tracking a predator across a room without turning his head.

And there was one more thing, small enough that anyone else would have missed it, and Maren prided herself on being no one else.

His hands. He ate left-handed.

She had watched him copy scripture for two days now, ostensibly reviewing the scriptorium's output as part of her inspection. The boy wrote right-handed—a fine, quick, disciplined right hand, the hand of a man trained since childhood. But this morning, passing bread and lifting his cup, fumbling his spoon, he used the left, clumsy as a child, and the clumsiness was wrong. People favored their dominant hand without thinking. They switched hands only when the dominant one was occupied—or when they were holding something they could not put down.

Or when they'd burned it. She filed that possibility too. Prophecy books, in her experience, tended to run hot.

Maren lifted her nettle tea and let her gaze drift, unhurried, to the head table.

Brother Cassian presided over the morning meal the way he presided over everything: gently, competently, without appetite. He was sixty-odd, grey at the temples, with the soft hands of a lifelong copyist and the patient eyes of a man who had raised other people's children instead of having his own. The temple loved him. The debt-scribes adored him—he was the one brother who remembered their names, who argued their cases before the bursar, who slipped them extra bread on fast days. In nineteen years of hunting heresy, Maren had learned to distrust beloved men more thoroughly than arrogant ones. Arrogant men wanted things, and wanting could be traced. Beloved men wanted nothing, which meant whatever they actually wanted was buried deep enough to survive excavation.

Cassian was watching Elodie.

Not staring. Nothing so crude. He simply glanced—at the boy's untouched second helping, at the way he sat slightly forward in his seat like a man ready to rise, at the left-handed cup—and each glance lasted half a heartbeat longer than a mentor's glance should. And once, when Elodie shifted and the shape of a heavy book showed briefly beneath his tunic at the hip, Cassian's hand stopped on the teapot.

Just stopped. Half a second. Then it resumed pouring, steady as ever.

But Maren had spent nineteen years training herself to see the half-second. The body always confessed before the mouth did. And Brother Cassian's body had just said, in the only language that couldn't be coached: *he has it, I know he has it, and I am afraid for him.*

Maren set down her cup and smiled at the refectory at large, a thin administrative smile, and began planning her morning.

* * *

Her inspection of the scriptorium was, on its face, routine. She had informed the prior upon arrival that she would review copying standards, inventory the archive seals, and interview staff regarding irregularities—an investigation prompted, officially, by discrepancies in a provincial tithe ledger. Nobody in Vell believed this. Everybody in Vell believed it anyway, because believing it was easier than wondering what else an Inquisitor might want.

The scriptorium occupied the temple's east wing, a long bright room of south-facing windows where twenty scribes worked at slanted desks, scratching away at scripture, ledgers, and letters for the valley folk who paid by the word. The air smelled of ink and lamp oil and sheepskin. Maren walked the rows slowly, trailing her fingers along the desks, and the scratching faltered and resumed around her like water closing over a stone.

Elodie's desk was fourth from the window. She stopped at it.

"Brother Ashgrove."

"Inquisitor." He rose. His bow was precise. His face was a wall with a door painted on it.

"I'm told your hand is the finest in the valley," Maren said. "Show me your current work."

He turned the pages toward her: a fair copy of the Verses on Humility, flawless, the margins ruled straight as fence lines. Maren read three lines, then four, then let her eyes lift to his face with the mild expression of a woman making conversation.

"You've been with us six years," she said. "Since your father's accident."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"The temple is generous."

"The temple is generous," he agreed, and something flickered behind the painted door—something old and cold and carefully banked. Grief, maybe. Or hatred. With debt-scribes it was usually both, braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was which.

"And your family? Your mother lives in Millbrook, I understand."

"She does."

"You write to her?"

"Once a month. The temple posts our letters."

"Do you write her the truth?"

The question landed exactly as she'd known it would—a hair too hard, a beat too strange. Around them the scratching continued, but the scribes nearest had gone very quiet, pens hovering, ears working.

Elodie blinked. "The truth, Inquisitor?"

"About your work. Your health. Whether you're happy." She smiled. "Most sons lie to their mothers about happiness. I've always wondered whether they lie about anything else."

"I write her about my work," he said evenly. "She finds it dull. That's the truth."

"Good," Maren said. "Dullness is a blessing in Vell. Guard it." She tapped the fair copy once, approvingly, and moved on—and felt his eyes between her shoulder blades the entire length of the row, hot as a brand.

At the end of the room, at the master desk beneath the archive seal, Brother Cassian looked up from his ledger.

Their eyes met. And Maren gave him the smallest nod, the nod of one professional acknowledging another, and watched him return it with perfect courtesy—and watched, underneath the courtesy, the calculation begin. He was already revising his evening. Already rearranging whatever he had planned. Already asking himself what she knew.

Good. Let him calculate. Calculating men made mistakes, and mistakes left marks, and marks were how you followed a man home.

* * *

She cornered him at vespers, or rather, she arranged to be cornered, which amounted to the same thing with a man of Cassian's manners. She positioned herself at the chapel door as the brothers filed out, and he stopped, because stopping was what kindness did, and asked if the Inquisitor found Vell's services adequate.

"Adequate," Maren said. "Though I confess the liturgy puzzles me. Twelve verses on humility, every morning, for a century. No variation. Doesn't the congregation tire of it?"

"We find comfort in repetition, Inquisitor."

"Do we?" She fell into step beside him, walking the cloister at the pace of a woman with nowhere to be. "I've served in provinces where the liturgy changes with the seasons. Where the founding is celebrated with fire and masks and songs the children make up themselves. Here it's the same twelve verses, the same founding story, the same festival of the Veil every autumn—and the same story every time. The twelve families. The great sacrifice. The sealing of the world's wounds to save the valley." She glanced sideways at him. "You teach it beautifully, I'm told. To the novices."

"It's not a difficult story."

"No," Maren agreed. "That's what troubles me. True stories are difficult. They have loose threads, contradictions, witnesses who disagree. Only invented stories are seamless." She stopped walking. So, eventually, did he. "Tell me, Brother Cassian. When you teach the founding—do you teach it as history, or as comfort?"

The cloister was empty. Rain ticked softly on the tiles. Somewhere beyond the wall, the valley went about its evening, lamps being lit, doors being barred, a hundred small surrenderings to the dark.

Cassian looked at her, and for a moment—for one unguarded half-second, the kind Maren lived inside—his face was not a mentor's face at all. It was tired. It was old. It was the face of a man standing at a graveside he had dug himself.

"As comfort," he said quietly. "As I suspect you know perfectly well, Inquisitor."

Then the mask returned, gentle and bland, and he wished her a good evening and walked on, and Maren stood alone in the cloister with the rain coming down and the noose in her hands.

Not around the scribe's neck. Not yet. The scribe was careful, and careful boys were slow work. But the old man— the old man had just handed her a thread, and old men pulled threads the way children pulled wings off flies, without meaning to, without even noticing.

*The Inquisitor follows.* Fine. Let her follow. But prophecy had said nothing about which neck the noose would find first, and Maren had learned long ago that the surest way to a fugitive was through whoever loved him.

She turned up her collar against the rain and went to write her report—both reports, the true one and the false one—and to plan tomorrow's interviews.

Starting, of course, with Brother Cassian.
