## 11. Cassian's Confession, Part One

Brother Cassian had spent forty years learning the weight of a lie, and he could tell you this: it does not get lighter. It gets denser. Like a stone carried so long it stops feeling like a separate thing and starts feeling like part of your spine.

He was carrying his stone up the archive stairs the morning after the Inquisitor arrived, a lamp in one hand and a tray of ink pots in the other, when he saw the gap on the shelf.

The prophecy book was gone.

Not misplaced. Not reshelved wrong. Gone, and the dust line where it had sat for four decades was broken by a hand-shaped smear, fresh, small. Elodie's hands were small.

Cassian set down the tray very carefully, the way you do when your body wants to shake and your dignity won't permit it.

*Well,* said the old coward in him, the one who had kept him alive and hollow for forty years. *Now you know.*

He stood there a long moment, listening to the rain against the archive shutters and the distant sound of wardens' boots in the courtyard below—Maren had doubled the patrols overnight, as if courtesy were something you could spend only once—and then he climbed the rest of the stairs to find the boy.

Because that was the truth beneath all the other truths, the one he'd been avoiding since the day a frightened scribe with ink-stained fingers had first lied to him about where he'd been: whatever happened next, Cassian would not survive watching from the stairs again.

Last time he had watched from the stairs. He knew exactly how that ended.

* * *

He found Elodie at the copying desk, hunched over scripture with the particular rigidity of someone performing normalcy. Too straight. Too still. A guilty man relaxes into his work; a terrified one holds himself like a drawn bow.

"Your margins are drifting," Cassian said.

Elodie flinched hard enough to blot the page. "Brother. I didn't hear you."

"No," Cassian agreed. "You've stopped hearing things lately. Doors. Voices. Your name." He pulled the second chair around and sat, unhurried, and watched the boy's eyes flick toward the shelf where the book wasn't, then away, then back—the tell of a man checking a wound. "You've stopped hearing your own thoughts, I think. They're too loud."

"I don't know what you mean."

"You do." Cassian folded his hands. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the wardens changed shift. "Elodie. I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to remember that I have loved you since you were nine years old and too proud to cry at your father's funeral. The question is not *did you take the book.* I know you took the book."

The color went out of the boy's face like water through open fingers.

"The question is," Cassian said quietly, "what did it say to you?"

* * *

They talked in the copying room because it was the only room in the temple without ears—or rather, the only room whose ears Cassian had personally muffled, years ago, wedging wax into the ventilation channel behind the third shelf, a small treason he had committed at twenty-six and never explained to anyone, including himself, until this moment.

Elodie told him everything. The blank page that refused ink. The date. His own name. *The Veilbearer speaks.* Then the second entry, and the well shaft, and the way the words had felt less like prediction than instruction. He talked fast and low, the way the drowning talk, and Cassian listened without interrupting, because interrupting would have meant admitting how long he'd waited for this conversation and why.

When it was done, Elodie sat with his hands flat on the table, waiting. "You're not surprised."

"No."

"You knew about the book."

"Yes."

"You—" The boy's voice cracked and reassembled itself harder. "You knew, and you let me find it alone? You let me think I was losing my mind?"

"Yes," Cassian said again, because there was no honest answer but yes, and he had promised himself, coming up those stairs, that he would spend no more lies on this boy. He had spent enough. He had spent a lifetime. "I let you carry it alone because I am a coward, and because I hoped—I prayed, if the temple's god listens to men like me—that you wouldn't be what it says you are. That the date was a coincidence. That the name was a coincidence." He looked at his hands, which had copied ten thousand pages of scripture and never once trembled until now. "I have been hoping against a book for forty years, Elodie. It has never once lost."

"What is it?" Elodie whispered. "What did it say to *you?*"

And here was the door, and here was the stone, and Cassian set it down between them at last.

* * *

"Forty-two years ago," he began, "I was your age. Younger, maybe. A scribe in this same room, with these same ink stains, working off my mother's debt—she died in the fever year, and the temple forgave nothing, they only converted her debt into mine. You know how that arithmetic works. Grief in, service out."

Elodie nodded. His jaw was tight. He knew.

"There was a girl," Cassian said. "Her name was Ilse Varn. She was a novice—not a scribe, an acolyte, training for the healing wards—and she used to come sit where you're sitting and complain that my handwriting looked like a spider drowned in a puddle. She was clever. Too clever for Vell. Curious in a valley where curiosity is a sin." He smiled, and it hurt, and he let it hurt, because the whole point of tonight was to stop numbing it. "She found the book the same way you did. Cataloguing the sealed shelves. And she came to me the way you should have come to me, and she showed me her name, and the date, and two words: *the Veilbearer speaks.*"

"What did you do?"

"What any faithful son of the temple does with heresy." Cassian's voice went flat, reciting. "I reported it. To my superior. To Brother Aldous, may the ground hold him lightly, who thanked me for my diligence and my loyalty, and praised me before the chapter, and told me to say nothing more about it to anyone." He paused. "Ilse disappeared three weeks later. Officially: reassigned to a mission beyond the boundary. There is no mission beyond the boundary. There has never been a mission beyond the boundary. But we were young, and the temple said a thing, and the temple's saying made it true."

"And you believed it?"

"For eleven days," Cassian said. "Then I went looking for the book, and the book was back on its shelf, and its page was blank again. Clean. As if it had finished with her. As if it had eaten." He met the boy's eyes. "I went to Brother Aldous and asked where Ilse Varn was, and he smiled at me the way the temple smiles—with warmth and no light in it—and he said, *Brother Cassian, some questions are their own heresy.* And I understood. Not everything. But enough. Enough to know that the girl I had loved had been given to the Veil or the quarry or the river, and that I had handed her to them with both hands and called it diligence."

The rain filled the silence. Elodie had gone very pale. "It wasn't your fault. You didn't know—"

"I didn't know," Cassian agreed. "That's not innocence, boy, that's just ignorance wearing faith's clothing. I was raised in a house that taught me obedience was holiness, and I obeyed, and a girl died, and the book waited forty years for another name to grow in its pages." He leaned forward. "Here is what I have pieced together since, in forty years of pretending to copy scripture while actually reading everything the archive will give me. Listen, because Maren is under this roof now, and we may not get a second night."

* * *

"The Veil is not weather," Cassian said. "It is not a natural absence, the way the temple teaches—magic simply doesn't grow here, like nothing grows in salt. It is a wall. Built. Deliberately. A hundred and six years ago, give or take, every record in this valley begins, and every record before it ends, and there is not a grave in Vell older than the founding, and there is not a foundation stone in the temple that predates it either. Do you understand what that means? It means our history was written *after*. All of it. We live inside a story someone composed, and the composition is less than a lifetime and a half old."

"But the founders—"

"Were twelve families," Cassian said. "Or that's the shape of the shadow I can see. The official histories mention the First Covenant, the pious founders who sealed the valley from the world's corruption. Corrupting what, Elodie? Sealing out what? A valley needs no wall unless something is either getting in—" he tapped the table— "or getting out."

Elodie's breath had gone shallow. "Out."

"Out," Cassian confirmed. "And here is the part I have never said aloud, not to anyone, in forty years, because saying it is death and I am, as established, a coward: the prophecy book is not the temple's book. It is older than the temple. It was hidden here by whoever built the Veil—hidden, mind, not enshrined—which means the builders wanted it found, eventually, by the right person, at the right time. And the right person is named in it. And the right time," he said softly, "is tomorrow, according to the calendar on the wall."

"The Veilbearer speaks," Elodie repeated. "But speaks *what?* What am I supposed to say?"

"I don't know." Cassian spread his hands. "I know only what happened to the last person the book chose. She spoke to me first. And then she spoke to no one ever again." He stood, joints cracking, and crossed to the shuttered window, and looked down through the slats into the courtyard where the Inquisitor's grey horse stood steaming in the rain. "Which brings us to the present difficulty."

"Maren."

"Maren. Who did not arrive unannounced. Who did not arrive by chance. Who interviewed you yesterday with the gentleness of a woman setting a snare, and who requested the sealed archive's inventory this morning—an inventory, note, that only exists because I compiled it, which means she already knows which books are missing, and knows that you are the one missing them." He turned. "She will come for you. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps within the hour. And when she comes, she will not drag you screaming; she will invite you somewhere private and ask you questions, and the answers will be extracted so gently you'll thank her afterward."

"So I run."

"Not yet." Cassian held up a hand. "Running confirms everything and saves nothing—a scribe with no money, no allies, no road sense, in a valley ringed by wardens, dies in a ditch and tells us nothing new. No. If the book chose you, boy, then the book intends for you to reach wherever it's pointing, and my job—my penance—is to make sure you live long enough to get there. Which means tonight, you go back to your cell, you sleep if you can, and tomorrow you are exactly the obedient, incurious scribe you have always pretended to be. Let her look at you and see nothing worth burning."

"And you?"

"I," Cassian said, "am going to do the one thing I should have done forty-two years ago." He smiled, and it was the smile Elodie had known all his life—warm, wry, faintly tired—and for the first time the boy seemed to see the iron underneath it. "I am going to stand in front of the Inquisitor and be interesting. Every hour she spends unraveling me is an hour she doesn't spend on you. I have forty years of dull sermons and impeccable service to hide behind, and one great sin to confess if it comes to that, and a coward learns, late, what courage costs. I can afford it. I've been saving up."

"Brother Cassian—"

"Elodie." He put his hand on the boy's shoulder, the ink-stained shoulder, the shoulder he had guided through ten thousand pages of borrowed words. "You asked me once why I never took a family. I told you the temple was my family. That was a lie of convenience. The truth is that after Ilse, I decided never again to love anyone the temple could use as leverage." His grip tightened. "Then a quarry widow brought her son to the orphan steps, and he was too proud to cry, and I broke my rule in a single afternoon. So understand me. Whatever happens in that interview room—whatever I say, whatever I admit—you do not come for me. You run the other direction. Promise me."

Elodie's eyes were wet and furious. "You said you'd stop lying to me."

"It isn't a lie. It's a request."

"It's a lie you'll tell yourself while she takes you apart," Elodie said, "and you know it, and I know it, and—" His voice broke clean through. "I just found out my whole life is a story someone wrote. Don't make me lose the one true thing in it."

For a moment the old man couldn't speak at all.

Then boots sounded on the archive stairs below—heavy, deliberate, two sets, pausing at the bottom—and a voice, courteous as frost, called up through the dark:

"Brother Cassian? Forgive the hour. Inquisitor Maren wonders if you might spare a few moments of your evening. She has some questions about a book."

Cassian closed his eyes. Opened them. Squeezed the boy's shoulder once, hard, the whole sermon in the gesture: *run, live, forgive me.*

"My, my," he said pleasantly, raising his voice toward the stairs. "Everyone wants to discuss literature tonight."

He went down to meet her.
