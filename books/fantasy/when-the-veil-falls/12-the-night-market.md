## 12. The Night Market

The night market of Vell smelled of tallow and wet wool and the particular desperation of people who had nothing to spend.

I moved through it with my hood up and my pilgrim's staff tapping the cobbles in a rhythm I'd practiced until it sounded like a limp. Pilgrims got pity here. Pity was useful. It bought you a place at the edge of a fire and eyes that slid away from your face instead of into it. In Vell, curiosity was a sin, but charity was a virtue, and I had learned in three weeks which sin I could afford to commit and which virtue I needed to perform.

Three weeks. Three weeks of eating gruel and listening to temple bells that tolled a dead god's schedule, of feeling the air sit on my tongue like a cloth wrung dry. You don't understand what magic is until you've been somewhere it isn't. It's not power. It's not light spilling from your fingers. It's *pressure*—the weight of everything alive pressing against everything else, the hum under the world's skin. Beyond the Veil, you learn to stop hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Here, in Vell, the silence was so total it rang.

And underneath the silence—there. That faint pulse I'd felt from the moment I crossed the boundary stone. Like a heart beating under a floorboard. Slow. Patient. Sealed.

I found him at the candle-seller's stall.

He didn't see me at first. He was doing something so ordinary it hurt to watch: counting coins into his palm, lips moving, frowning at them like they'd personally wronged him. A scribe's hands—ink-stained at the nails even now, on market night, even after scrubbing. Elodie Ashgrove. The name from a book written before his grandfather was born, walking around in a threadbare coat, haggling over candles because he couldn't afford more than two.

I had crossed half a war-torn continent for this man, and he was buying tallow.

*Careful,* I told myself. The Order had sent me to find the Veilbearer, and the Order did not send anyone to do anything gently. But there was a difference between what Vey wanted and what I wanted, and I had spent three weeks telling myself the distance between those things was wider than it was.

I bumped his elbow as I passed. Not hard—a pilgrim's stumble, apologetic—and pressed the folded scrap of paper into his sleeve in the same motion.

He stiffened. Good instincts, for a man who'd grown up where nothing ever happened. He didn't look down. Didn't look around. Just paid for his candles with steady hands and walked on, and only when he reached the shadow between the fishmonger's stall and the cooper's did he unfold the note.

I watched him read it by the light of someone else's fire:

*The east well. When the last stall closes. Come alone or don't come.*

His head came up. His eyes swept the market—not frantically, but thoroughly, the way a man reads a page looking for one word. They passed over me twice without catching. I let my shoulders round, let the staff wobble. Pilgrims are furniture.

Then he folded the paper, tucked it into his coat, and walked toward home with the unhurried gait of a man who intended to think about this very hard and decide very carefully.

Good. I hadn't wanted an answer tonight. Men who say yes too quickly to strangers are men who say yes to everyone, and I needed him to be worth what was coming.

I bought a candle I didn't need and went to wait.

* * *

The east well sat in a square the market never reached, ringed by houses whose windows had gone dark hours ago. The moon was a fingernail paring behind cloud. I heard him coming before I saw him—no, that's not right. I heard the *absence* of sound coming, the way the quiet bent around his footsteps. The Veilbearer's blood made its own small stillness, even here, even untrained. Another thing he didn't know about himself. Another thing I'd have to decide whether to tell him.

"You came alone," I said from the well's shadow.

He spun, hand flying up, and knocked his own candle out of its holder. It rolled, guttered, died. We stood in the dark together, breathing.

"Who are you?" His voice was steadier than his body. "The note said—or I assumed—I don't—" He stopped, restarted, the way scribes do when they've written themselves into a bad sentence. "You're the pilgrim. From the market. You've been following me."

"I've been following the truth," I said, "and lately it keeps stopping where you're standing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have tonight." I stepped out of the shadow enough for him to see my face, my hood back, the staff lowered. "My name is Sera. And before you ask—the name doesn't matter, because it isn't the one I was born with, and the one I was born with is a crime in six territories beyond this valley. So: Sera. Exile. Pilgrim, currently. Something else, soon, if you're brave."

"Brave." He laughed, short and disbelieving, hugging the dead candle to his chest like a talisman. "Brother Cassian says bravery is just fear that found a reason to be useful. What reason are you selling?"

"I'm not selling anything. I'm offering you an education." I held out my open hand, palm up, between us. "What do you know about the Veil? The real Veil, not the scripture."

He recited it like a boy at lesson: "When the world broke itself in the Sundering, the founders of Vell raised the Veil to shield us from corruption. Within the valley, magic cannot take root, and so we live clean and safe, and gratitude is our first prayer." Then, quieter, with something flickering under the rote words: "That's what the archive says. Mostly. There are... gaps."

"There are always gaps." I turned my hand over. "Watch."

Nothing happened.

That was the whole demonstration, and I let the nothing stretch until he shifted uncomfortably, until the silence got so heavy he finally asked, "What am I watching for?"

"A spark," I said. "The smallest working I know. A mote of light, no bigger than a firefly, no hotter than breath. Every child beyond the Veil learns it before they learn letters. Watch my hand."

I called it up. Even drained as I was—even after three weeks of the valley's dead air leaching me like a fever—it came. A single point of gold light bloomed above my palm, trembling, alive, and I saw his face change. Saw the hunger hit him like a physical blow, the naked, unguarded yearning of a man who had spent his whole life being told that wanting this exact thing was the deepest sin there was.

Then I walked forward, one step, two, three, holding the spark before me like a lantern, moving toward the eastern edge of the square—toward the boundary, four streets and a fallow field away, but the Veil's gradient started early, thinning gradually, and I knew these streets now, knew exactly where the light began to starve.

At the seventh step, the spark dimmed.

Elodie made a small sound. Not a word. Something older than words.

At the twelfth step it guttered. At the fifteenth it shrank to an ember. And at the twentieth step—the edge of the square, where the last house gave way to dark fields—it died. Not extinguished like a flame in wind. *Starved*, like a fish pulled from water, folding in on itself, winking out with what I swear looked like relief.

I stood in the cold with my empty hand and turned to face him.

"The scriptures tell you magic can't exist in Vell," I said. "Like it's a law of nature. Like the valley is a room where fire simply won't burn." My voice cracked on the next part; I let it. "It's not an absence, Elodie. It's a wall. Something is *pressing down* on this valley, every hour of every day, crushing every living spark before it can draw breath. Your whole life has been lived at the bottom of a held breath. And something down there—" I pointed at the ground, at the temple hill looming black against the stars, "—something sealed beneath your feet is keeping that pressure alive. I can feel it. Can't you?"

He had gone very pale. The candle fell from his fingers; he didn't notice.

"No," he said. Then, honestly, terribly: "Yes. Since I found—" He stopped. Closed his mouth.

"Since you found the prophecy book." 

The night got very quiet. Somewhere across town, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"How do you—" He took a step back. "Who told you about—"

"No one told me. The book told me. It told the Order, a century ago, and we've been waiting for it to fill ever since." I kept my voice low and level, though my pulse wasn't level at all. "Listen to me. I'm going to say things that will get us both burned if the wrong ears catch them, so I'll say them once. A hundred years ago, twelve families did something at this valley's center. Something enormous. The temple calls it salvation. The Order calls it theft. Both of them are lying, and the truth is written in a book that updates itself, and you—" I pointed at his chest, at the ink-stained hands clutching empty air, "—you are in it. By name. By date. *Veilbearer.*"

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? It's yours."

"It's *nothing*. It's ink. It's a coincidence in a rotten old book that some fanatic wrote a hundred years ago, and fanatics are always writing books, and fools are always finding themselves in them!" His voice had risen, cracked, dropped again fast—he was terrified of being heard, which meant he understood exactly how heretical this conversation was. "I copy scripture. I owe the temple eleven years of my life. I buy two candles because I can't afford four. Whatever your book says I am, I can't be it. I *can't afford* to be it."

There it was. The debt. I'd done my homework; I knew about the Ashgrove indenture, the quarry, the father crushed under stone the temple then claimed as collateral. The Order's briefing had laid it out like a lock diagram: grief converted into servitude, servitude into obedience. *Find the pressure points,* Vey had said. *Everyone has them.*

I decided, in that moment, that I hated Vey a little.

"Eleven years," I said softly. "And then what? Another ledger? Another master? Elodie, I've seen what's outside the Veil. Yes, it's scarred. Yes, the war burned it hollow and it hasn't healed. But the sun rises on cities that remember their own names, and children there learn to carry light in their cupped hands, and no one owns anybody's whole life for the price of a grave." I stepped closer. "Your valley isn't peace. It's a held breath. And held breaths end—one way or another, they end. The only question is whether you'll be standing there when it happens, holding the book that saw it coming, or whether you'll be copying psalms while other people decide."

"You want me to run," he said. "Leave. With you. Tonight."

"I want you to *choose*, with your eyes open, for the first time in your life. Running might be part of it. Staying might be. But choose knowing what this place actually is." I glanced at the black fields, the invisible line beyond them where my spark had died. "Because I promise you, Elodie Ashgrove—the temple already chose for you. The day your father died, they chose. The question is whether you'll let that choice stand."

He didn't answer. For a long time he just stared at the fields—at the boundary he'd lived beside all his life and never once questioned—and I watched something move through him like weather. Fear, mostly. Under it, worse than fear: hope, which is fear's reckless brother.

"The spark," he said finally. His voice had gone strange and quiet. "When it died. Did it hurt? The magic. Did it feel like anything?"

"Like drowning," I said. "Every time. Imagine living your whole life underwater and being told the drowning is holiness."

He laughed—one broken, involuntary note—and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. When he looked at me again, he was crying a little and pretending not to, and something in my chest did a thing I refused to examine.

"If I said yes," he said. "If I ran. My debt doesn't vanish. They'd hunt me. Maren would—"

"Inquisitor Maren." The name tasted like iron filings. "She's already hunting. She arrived the day after the book showed your date, and she didn't come for weather. Elodie, whatever window you think you have, it's closing. The book said *the scribe runs, the Inquisitor follows.* Prophecies aren't warnings. They're schedules."

"Stop saying that word like it means something."

"It means everything. It means *hurry.*"

He wrapped his arms around himself, and for a moment he looked ten years old, standing at the bottom of a valley at the bottom of a lie. "I need time," he whispered. "Cassian—there's someone I have to talk to. Someone who might..." He trailed off, and I saw him decide, consciously, not to finish the sentence. Guarding his mentor even now. Even from me.

"Time," I repeated. "You have a little. Not much."

"And if I say no? If I stay, and burn the book, and go back to my ledgers?" His chin lifted—defiance trying to grow in soil that had never fed it. "Will your Order let that stand?"

I should have lied. The mission said lie. Vey's instructions were precise: *retrieve the Veilbearer by any means; his cooperation is preferable but not required.* And Sera the exile, Sera who had clawed her way across the ashlands to earn a place in the Order, Sera who owed them everything—Sera should have smiled and said *of course, refuse and be free.*

"The Order sent me to bring you back," I said. "However it's done."

His face closed like a door.

"But I'm telling you," I went on, faster now, "because you deserve one person in this story who isn't managing you. Everyone else—your temple, your Inquisitor, my Order—we all have plans for you, and none of them end with you deciding anything. So decide *now*, tonight, whether you're a character in a prophecy or a man with a name. Because I've read that book, Elodie. I've read what's coming. And I am begging you not to make me watch it happen to you from the outside."

The clouds tore. Moonlight came down into the square, silvering the well, the cobbles, his tear-tracked face, and for one suspended heartbeat neither of us breathed.

Then, from the direction of the temple hill, a bell rang.

Not the hour bell. Not the vesper bell. The third bell—the alarm I'd heard only once in three weeks, the night a warden found a poacher past the boundary stones. Its voice went rolling down through the sleeping streets, and lights began to bloom in windows, and Elodie went rigid.

"They're ringing for something," he breathed. "They never ring at night unless—"

We both thought it at the same moment. I saw it land on him like a dropped stone.

*Cassian.* The person he had to talk to. The mentor whose name he wouldn't say in front of me.

He was already running.

"Elodie—!" I grabbed for his sleeve, caught air. He tore across the square toward the temple hill with his coat flying and the moonlight making him briefly, unbearably bright, and I stood alone at the dying edge of the Veil with my empty hand still curled around a spark that wasn't there anymore.

The bell kept ringing.

Underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the pulse beneath the valley beat once—twice—faster than I had ever felt it.

Something had started. And I had a terrible, rising certainty that whatever it was, we were both late.
