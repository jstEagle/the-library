## 13. The Second Entry Fills

I didn't sleep.

I told myself it was the cold, or the well water still damp on my sleeves, or the ordinary insomnia of a man who owed the temple eleven years of his life and had just spent one of them hiding a forbidden book down a hole like a thief. Any of those explanations would have served. I lay on my cot in the scribes' dormitory and offered each of them to myself in turn, the way you offer a coin to a beggar—not because it will fix anything, but because refusing to give it feels cruel.

None of them took.

The truth was simpler and much worse: I could still feel the book. Not in my hands—it was thirty feet below the cloister garden, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the dry well shaft—but somewhere behind my ribs, the way you can feel a tooth that shouldn't ache. *The scribe runs, the Inquisitor follows.* Those words had been there when I fished the book out of Brother Aldous's sealed chest three nights ago, and they had not been there the night before. The page had filled while the book sat untouched in a locked room that no one entered but me.

Books do not do that.

Except, apparently, this one did.

The dormitory bell rang for matins and I rose with the others, forty men shuffling into habits in the grey dark, and I performed sleepiness so convincingly that Brother Tomas clapped my shoulder and said I looked like death warmed over, ha ha, get some wine in you at supper. I smiled. My face did all the correct things. Somewhere beneath the performance, a thought was pacing back and forth like a caged animal:

*It's watching.*

No. Books don't watch. Ink doesn't watch. Whatever had written those words had written them before I ever touched the book—a hundred years ago, according to the binding, the vellum, the hand. A hundred years ago, someone had dipped a pen and set down today's date and my name, *Elodie Ashgrove*, as calmly as a merchant entering a figure in a ledger. And then, at some point between then and now—between my finding the book and my hiding it—the second line had appeared.

Unless.

Unless it hadn't appeared. Unless it had been there all along, waiting behind some trick of the light, and I was a fool building towers out of nothing. That was the other explanation, the sane one, and I clung to it through matins and through the two hours of copying that followed, my pen moving across scripture about the mercy of the Veil while my mind ran its circuits: *coincidence, coincidence, coincidence.* Names are common. Dates are common. A book of old prophecies full of vague doom-sayings is bound to brush against somebody's life if enough people read it.

But the second line hadn't brushed against my life. It had described the last three days of it with the precision of a witness.

*The scribe runs, the Inquisitor follows.*

And here was the thing I kept circling without wanting to land on: I hadn't run yet. Not really. I had hidden a book. I had lied to Cassian. But running—that was still only an idea, a shape I sometimes saw at the edge of the night when the dormitory was quiet. And Inquisitor Maren had arrived in Vell two days after I found the book, unannounced, with her soft grey eyes and her terrifying courtesy, asking questions about archive access with the gentleness of a woman checking whether a door is locked.

*Follows*, the line had said. Present tense. Ongoing.

As though it were being written now.

* * *

At midmorning I was assigned to the scriptorium's outer bench, which faced the cloister, which meant that for four hours I copied psalms with a direct sightline to the garden well. This was either providence or cruelty; with temples it is usually impossible to tell the difference. I worked. I watched the well. Nothing moved except a gardener's boy chasing pigeons, and once, distantly, the grey habit of Sister Ondine crossing toward the infirmary.

Cassian found me at the noon meal.

He sat down across from me without asking, which he never did—we had an unspoken arrangement, born of his position and my debt, that our friendship would always look like mentorship from the outside, because mentorship could be defended and friendship could be used. He set his bowl down. Barley again. It was always barley. He looked tired in a way I had never seen him look tired, and I felt the old reflexive guilt, the son's guilt, rise up my throat.

"You didn't come to evening study," he said.

"I was copying."

"Until midnight? Your candle allowance is logged, Elodie."

"Then check the log."

He winced as if I'd struck him. I hadn't meant it as a strike—or I had, a small one, to keep him off the trail—and immediately I hated myself for it. Forty years of a man's kindness, and I'd learned to spend it like coin.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I couldn't sleep. The Maren business has everyone rattled."

"Maren." He chewed slowly. "Yes. She asked about you, you know. Yesterday. After the archive inventory."

My spoon stopped halfway to my mouth. "What did she ask?"

"Whether you were diligent. Whether you had family beyond your father. Whether you seemed—" he paused, choosing, "—*restless*."

"And what did you tell her?"

"The truth. That you are the most diligent scribe I have trained in twenty years, and the least restless man in Vell." His eyes came up, mild and brown and utterly unreadable, and held mine for exactly one heartbeat longer than comfort allowed. "Was that inaccurate?"

"No," I said.

"Good." He picked up his bowl. "The well shaft cover was loose this morning, by the way. I tightened it. The wind does that—loosens things we think are secure."

He left. I sat very still among forty eating men, my heart going like a trapped bird, doing the arithmetic. He knew. Or he suspected. Or—no, he *knew*, because the wind does not loosen iron covers, and mentors who have taught you for twenty years do not mention such things at meals unless they want you to hear them without hearing them. He was telling me, in temple-code, in front of God and the barley: *whatever you've hidden down there is not secure anymore, and I am not going to ask you what it is, and I may already know, and I love you, and please be careful.*

Or he was warning me that Inquisitor Maren had found it.

I genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing was worse than either.

* * *

I went to the well at the changing of the afternoon shift, when the cloister empties for the hour of labor, and I stood over the cover with my hands flat on the iron and made myself wait until my breathing steadied. Then I lifted it and went down the rungs into the dark.

The shaft smelled of stone and old rain and, faintly, of oilcloth. I had brought a shuttered lamp. Its light reached the bottom and found the bundle where I'd left it, wedged behind a loose stone, undisturbed—except that the oilcloth was wet. Not with seepage. The shaft was bone-dry; I'd chosen it for that. The cloth was wet along one seam, darkly, the way paper is wet when ink soaks through it.

I unwrapped it with fingers that did not feel entirely like mine.

The prophecy book lay open. I had closed it. I remembered closing it—I remembered pressing the covers together and tying the cloth with a double knot, because knots are the one craft a scribe trusts. The knot was untied. The book lay open to the second leaf, and the lamp flame leaned toward it as if reading over my shoulder, and the words were no longer alone on the page.

Beneath *the scribe runs, the Inquisitor follows*, in the same antique hand, the same brown-black ink, new lines had filled the space. They read:

> *the scribe hides what cannot be hidden.*
> *the mentor knows, and says nothing, and is known for saying nothing.*
> *the Inquisitor finds the mentor.*

I read it three times. My lips moved. Down in that dry stone throat of a well, with the sky a coin of white far above me, I heard myself whisper the last line aloud, and the sound of it in my own voice made my stomach turn over.

*The Inquisitor finds the mentor.*

Not *seeks*. Not *pursues*. *Finds*. Past-tense certainty wearing future clothes. And underneath the horror of the words themselves, a colder thing was forming, the way frost forms on a window—slow, silent, patterned. Because the lines weren't guesses. Look at them. Every entry mapped something that had already happened, or was happening, in exact order: I found the book; I hid it; I lied to Cassian; Cassian noticed and covered for me. Each event appeared in the book *after* I lived it—or in the case of the first entry, long before—but always in sequence, always accurate, never ahead by more than the space between one breath and the next.

Prophecies are supposed to lead. You consult them to learn what comes, and the whole economy of prophecy—every temple's trade, every fortune-teller's stall in the market square—rests on the promise of foreknowledge. But this book never gave me anything I didn't already know. It followed. It recorded. It was less an oracle than a mirror with a delay.

Which raised the question I had been circling since midnight, and which I finally let land:

If the book only ever confirmed what had happened, why hide it? Why fear it? What power did a record have?

And the answer came with a sensation like the floor of the world tilting one degree, just enough that everything on it began, very slowly, to slide.

Because I *had* changed my behavior. That was the poison in it. From the moment I read *the scribe runs, the Inquisitor follows*, every choice I made was made inside the shape of that sentence. When I hid the book instead of burning it or turning it over to Cassian—was that my choice, or was it *the scribe hides what cannot be hidden*, fulfilling itself through my hands? When I lied to Cassian at supper—was that me protecting him, or was it *the mentor knows, and says nothing*, arranging its own next line? The book didn't need to compel me. It only needed to be read. Once a sentence describing you exists, you live in its gravity. You bend toward it. You call it fate so you don't have to call it obedience.

It doesn't predict, I thought, standing in the dark with the lamp shaking in my grip. It instructs.

Someone wrote this. A hundred years ago or yesterday, someone wrote these lines knowing—knowing the way an author knows—that a scribe named Elodie Ashgrove would stand in a dry well shaft holding a lamp and read them at precisely the moment they would do the most damage. Knowing that the reading itself was the mechanism. I was not the reader of this book.

I was the medium it was written on.

* * *

I climbed out of the well with the book against my chest and no better plan than I'd gone down with, and that was when I heard the voices.

Two of them, low, coming from the arcade that bordered the cloister garden. I pressed myself against the shaft wall and pulled the cover to within a finger's width of closed, leaving a slit of grey daylight, and through it I saw them: Sister Ondine, and beside her, moving with that unhurried glide that made her seem to be strolling even when she was hunting—

Inquisitor Maren.

"—the eastern storerooms are complete, then?" Maren was saying. Her voice carried oddly well in the cold air, smooth as poured milk.

"All inventoried. Though I confess I don't know what heresy could hide in forty years of altar-linen requisitions."

"Heresy hides beautifully in requisitions, Sister. It's in the margins that people tell the truth." A pause. Their footsteps crossed the gravel, unhurried. "One more section. The deep archive. Your Brother Aldous catalogued it before his eyes failed, yes?"

"He did. It's sealed now. Scribe Elodie Ashgrove has been doing the inventory under Brother Cassian's supervision."

Another pause. Longer. Long enough for me to stop breathing altogether.

"Cassian supervises the Ashgrove boy," Maren said. It wasn't a question, but it had the weight of one, the way a hand on a door has the weight of a knock. "How convenient. How... tidy. Tell me, Sister—how long has that arrangement stood?"

"Twenty years, Inquisitor. Since the boy was indentured."

"Twenty years." A soft sound, almost a laugh, with nothing in it. "A debt-bound orphan and the brother who raised him. Bound together by gratitude and ledgers. Yes. That is exactly how it would be arranged."

They passed out of earshot toward the archive stair, and I stayed crouched in the dark with the book against my heart and the cold of the stones soaking through my habit, and I understood, with terrible clarity, what I was looking at.

She wasn't investigating the archive. She was walking a path that had already been drawn for her—eastern storerooms, deep archive, Cassian, me—turning over each stone in an order someone else had set. Maybe she knew it. Maybe she thought the sequence was her own cleverness. It didn't matter. The book had said *the Inquisitor finds the mentor*, and now here she was, asking after Cassian by name, calling the shape of our lives *tidy*, using the word *arranged* as though she'd read it off a page.

I had maybe a day. Perhaps less. If I went to Cassian and warned him, I fulfilled *the mentor knows* more completely than ever, and put us both in her sightline together. If I ran—if I took the book and went over the wall tonight—I fulfilled the first line, *the scribe runs*, and gave her exactly the hunt she was already prepared for. If I turned myself in, surrendered the book, confessed everything—

I actually considered it. Standing there in the well-garden cold, I honestly weighed confession, and the reason I weighed it should frighten me more than it does: because it would have been *tidy*. Because some part of me wanted the story to arrive at its next scene cleanly, and I caught myself preferring surrender to the mess of defiance, and I understood in that moment how completely the book had gotten inside my decision-making. Even my rebellion was starting to feel scripted. Even my fear had chapters.

There was one option the book hadn't mentioned. One line it hadn't written.

Burn it.

I had not burned it because it was the only proof, the only map, the only thing in Vell older than its own lies—and because some vain, starving corner of me, the corner that had copied ten thousand pages of other people's words without ever writing one of my own, could not bring itself to destroy a book that had my name in it. That is the ugliest sentence I have ever set down about myself, and it is true. The book promised me significance the way the temple promised my father dignity: cheaply, and in advance of payment. And I had kept it for the same reason debtors keep their ledgers—because the debt is the only identity anyone has ever offered.

But a book that instructs rather than predicts is not a relic. It is a weapon, and I was standing on the blade of it, and so was Cassian, and Maren was walking toward us both along lines someone had inked a century ago.

Tonight. Tonight I would take it back down—not to the well. Wells can be searched; Maren was already asking about Aldous, and Aldous had known everything. There was a place in the bindery, under the nipping press, a hollow the old binders used for storing glues... No. She'd search there too, eventually. She searched everywhere eventually.

I would burn it. In the scriptorium stove, tonight, after compline, when the ash bins were emptied at dawn. I would feed it page by page and watch the ink curl, and whatever came next would come next because I chose it, not because a dead hand had written me into the scene.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It lasted until dusk, when I returned to the dormitory to wait out the hours until the temple slept—and found, tucked beneath my pillow where no one could possibly have placed it without passing three brothers and a locked door, a single sheet of paper, folded once, in handwriting I had seen only once before, on an archive slip signed by a woman whose eyes had unsettled me across a market crowd.

Sera. The pilgrim. *The stranger at the gate.*

The note was five words long.

*It knows you've read it. Run.*

I stood there holding it, the book heavy in my pack behind the loose panel, the stove waiting downstairs, the bell tolling compline across the valley—and for the first time since I'd opened that sealed chest, the question stopped being whether the prophecy would come true.

The question was who else, besides me, was reading the book.

And whether any of us—including its author—had ever had a choice at all.
