## 14. Maren's Dossier

The dossier arrived the way all of Maren's orders did: hidden inside something ordinary.

This time it was a consignment ledger from a wool merchant in Harrow's Bend, one of eleven she'd requisitioned as part of a routine audit of temple supply chains. Nobody questioned an Inquisitor's paperwork. That was the beauty of heresy-hunting—it gave you license to read anything, open anything, delay anything. She'd spent six years building a reputation for thoroughness so suffocating that even the High Provost winced when he saw her seals on his correspondence.

She sat alone in the guest quarters the temple had assigned her—spartan, cold, a single candle—and slit the ledger's false binding with a bone knife.

Inside: three sheets of thin grey paper, written in a hand she didn't recognize but knew by style. The Ashen Order didn't use names. They used designations and instructions, as if names were a courtesy they'd outgrown.

*To the Hand-in-Vell.*

*Progress is noted. The candidate has been identified and confirmed. The text has spoken twice; you are reminded that a third speaking may occur at any point after contact between the candidate and the mentor. Accelerate.*

*You are further reminded of your obligations. The candidate is to be located, assessed, and prepared for retrieval. He is not to be harmed. He is not to be turned. He is not to be allowed to form attachments that will complicate extraction. If the mentor becomes an obstacle, remove the mentor.*

*The Order thanks its servant.*

Maren read it twice, then held the first sheet over the candle flame and watched the grey paper curl into ash that smelled faintly of iron. The second sheet followed. The third she kept—the third always contained the operational details, and tonight those details made her jaw tighten.

*A reminder regarding timelines. The Veil's integrity continues to degrade along the northern meridian at a rate exceeding projections. Full collapse within four years, perhaps fewer. When it fails naturally, everything within fails with it—the seal, the prisoner, the valley, and any chance of controlled release. The Order does not intend to let the world's fate rest on the decay rate of a century-old working performed by frightened farmers. Retrieval must conclude before winter.*

Before winter. Four months.

Maren fed the last of the ash to the candle and sat for a long moment in the dark, listening to the wind move through the temple courtyard below her window. Somewhere down there, Brother Cassian was probably lighting the evening lamps in the scriptorium, moving from desk to desk the way he had for thirty years. And somewhere down there, Elodie Ashgrove was asleep, or pretending to sleep, or lying awake with a prophecy book hidden somewhere clever, wondering when the floor of his life had opened beneath him.

*He is not to be turned.* Which meant the Order knew what Maren had known since her second week in Vell: that the boy was not a tool you picked up. He was a person who would have to be persuaded, deceived, or broken—and the Order preferred deception, because broken instruments performed poorly.

She lit a second candle and opened her own files.

Her room was supposed to contain nothing but an Inquisitor's standard kit: vestments, writs, instruments of questioning, devotional texts. Instead, the false bottom of her document chest held a leather folio thick enough to stop a sword thrust. Inside it, the real record of Inquisitor Maren's career—not the one the temple believed, but the one that had actually happened, entry by entry, in her own cramped handwriting.

She turned to the pages on Elodie Ashgrove and read them again, though she could have recited them.

*Subject: Ashgrove, Elodie. Scribe, second rank, indentured to Temple of the Unseen Light. Age nineteen. Father killed in quarry collapse eight years past; debt converted to indenture per standard temple practice. Mother deceased (fever). No siblings. No close friends beyond household staff and mentor relationship with Brother Cassian (see separate file).*

*Temperament: careful, observant, prone to questions he has learned to keep behind his teeth. Debt has taught him caution, not obedience—there is a difference, and the Order should note it. Obedience can be commanded. Caution must be circumvented.*

*Assessment: suitable. Possibly ideal. Bloodline confirmed through parish records and the boundary stone inscriptions (see appendix). The Ashgrove line thinned after the sealing—one branch dead, one branch scattered—but this subject carries the name and, we believe, the resonance. Confirmation pending physical proximity testing.*

*Note on the mentor: Cassian is a complication. Forty years ago he accessed the prophecy text without sanction. The previous candidate died under circumstances the Order has never fully reconstructed. Either Cassian is a coward who failed once and will fail again, or he is protecting something. Watch him. If he moves against the candidate, intervene only if the candidate's value is threatened. Otherwise, let the old man's guilt do our work for us. Guilt makes people predictable.*

Maren closed the folio.

*Guilt makes people predictable.* She had written that sentence herself, two years ago, sitting in a waystation outside Harrow's Bend, and she remembered thinking at the time how clean it sounded. How simple. She had been an instrument of the Order for nine years then, and instruments were permitted to believe in simplicity.

She was twenty-nine years old, and she could no longer remember what she had been before.

That was the part the dossier never recorded. The part she kept nowhere, because keeping it anywhere would make it evidence, and evidence was the thing she hunted in others. But she knew the shape of it the way an amputee knows the shape of a missing limb. There had been a girl once—a village called Sorrel's Ford, on the far side of the Veil, in the grey country where magic came back in fits and starts like a stuttering heart. A mother who worked the tanner's vats. A fever, when the girl was eleven. A hedge-witch who might have saved her, except the witch's gifts had guttered out that season, because magic in the outer world was a rationed thing, a dying thing, and there hadn't been enough left in the ley-line near Sorrel's Ford to boil water, let alone break a fever.

The girl had watched her mother die of a sickness that a single spark of true magic could have cured. And then the Ashen Order had come through the village recruiting, and they had told her about a valley where all the world's magic was hoarded behind a Veil, locked away by traitors a century ago while the outer world slowly starved of wonder, and they had said: *you could be part of taking it back.*

She had been thirteen. She had said yes.

It had taken her nine years to earn a field designation, four more to earn this one, and somewhere in those years she had stopped being a girl from Sorrel's Ford and become the Hand-in-Vell, and the transformation had been so gradual she'd never felt it happen, the way she imagined the valley itself had never felt the moment the Veil closed over it. One day the sky was just suddenly different, and everyone inside had already forgotten there'd ever been another kind.

Except the valley hadn't forgotten. That was the thing that had begun to trouble her, these past months. The valley *remembered*. It remembered in the contradictions she'd found in the founding records, in the graves that stopped a hundred years ago, in the blank page that refused a scribe's ink. The whole place was a held breath. And now the breath was coming undone, and the Order wanted her to make sure it came undone in a way they controlled.

*Retrieval must conclude before winter.*

Maren rose and went to the window. Below, the temple courtyard lay silvered under a half-moon, and she could see the well at its center—a low stone ring, older than every building around it, which nobody remarked upon because in Vell nobody remarked upon anything old anymore. Old things were either holy or dangerous, and the temple had spent a century teaching people that holiness and danger were the same word.

The well shaft was empty. She'd checked it that morning, before dawn, lowering her lamp on a cord and finding nothing at the bottom but wet stone and a smell of old iron. The book had moved. Which meant the boy had moved it, which meant the boy had read the second entry—*the scribe runs, the Inquisitor follows*—and understood that it was about him. About her.

Good. Fear sharpened candidates. The Order liked them fearful, right up until fear curdled into desperation and they did something stupid, and then the Order liked them dead, though the dossiers never used that word either. *Expendable.* *Loss acceptable.* *Asset written off.*

She thought of Elodie Ashgrove's face when she'd interviewed him in the archive—that careful blankness, the way his hands had gone still on the table, the half-second pause before each answer that wasn't hesitation but *calculation*. Most suspects paused because they were deciding whether to lie. He paused because he was deciding how good a lie he could get away with. There was a quality in that that the Order would call promise and that Maren, in an hour she didn't examine too closely, had privately called something else.

Something like respect.

She shut the thought away. Sentiment was a leak in a hull; you didn't argue with it, you patched it.

* * *

In the morning she requested a private audience with the High Provost, and got one, because Inquisitors requesting audiences was weather, not event.

Provost Aldous was seventy, soft-spoken, and terrified of her, which she knew and used the way a carpenter uses a level. His office smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, and behind his desk hung the temple's only image of the Unseen Light: a plain circle of white wood, empty at its center, because the god of Vell was a god of absence. Of what could not enter. Of what the Veil kept out.

"Inquisitor." He folded his hands. "Your investigation proceeds?"

"Quietly," she said. "I've found traces of unauthorized textual traffic in the archive. Records removed, returned improperly, catalogued falsely. I'm narrowing the source."

His face did the small flinch she expected. "Brother Cassian has held the archive keys for—"

"Brother Cassian's access is accounted for." She let that hang. Let him hear what she hadn't said. "What interests me, Provost, is older. Tell me about the founding."

"The founding?" He blinked. "The Sealing? That's... catechism, Inquisitor. Twelve families, faithful beyond the Veil's reach, blessed with deliverance from the war-torn world—"

"Twelve families," Maren repeated. "Named in order of merit in the Litany of Gratitude. I've been reviewing the liturgical manuscripts. There are four distinct versions of the Litany in this temple's collection, Provost. Four. And in the oldest one, the Ashgroves are named first. In the newest, they're eleventh. Someone revised the list over the course of a century. Someone demoted them." She leaned forward slightly. "Why would a family that helped found paradise be quietly moved toward the bottom of its own hymn?"

Aldous's mouth opened and closed. She watched him decide not to know what she was talking about, watched the decision travel across his face like weather. "Scholarly... variations. Copying errors. I'll have the archivists—"

"No," she said. "You won't. You'll leave the archives exactly as they are, including whatever's in them that frightens you. This remains my investigation. Am I understood?"

"You're understood." His voice had gone thin. Good. A rattled Provost signed whatever writs she asked for, and tomorrow she intended to ask for several.

She rose, gathered her documents, and paused at the door. "One more matter. The indenture ledgers. I'll need the original deed of conversion for the Ashgrove debt. The father's death, the quarry settlement. All of it."

"The Ashgroves? Why—"

"Because debts," Maren said pleasantly, "are how temples write their secrets in numbers instead of words. And numbers are harder to burn."

She left him sweating and took the cloister walk back toward the guest wing, her boots loud on stones worn smooth by a century of feet that had never once asked where the path went.

* * *

At midday she watched Elodie Ashgrove cross the market square.

She did it the way she did everything—peripherally, through the reflection in a chandler's window, her body angled toward a display of tallow candles she had no intention of buying. The boy walked fast, shoulders drawn in, carrying a basket of ink and reams he almost certainly hadn't been sent to buy. He was meeting someone. She'd tracked his errands for three days now, and today's errand was a fiction.

And there—at the mouth of Tanner's Row, pretending to examine a rack of dried fish—a pilgrim woman in a road-grey cloak who did not pray, did not beg, did not do any of the things pilgrims in Vell were required by custom to do. Just stood, and watched the boy, and when he passed close enough, fell into step beside him as casually as a shadow.

Sera. The exile. The Order's other hand in Vell, except Sera didn't know Maren existed, and that was precisely how Maren had designed it. Two agents, blind to each other, reporting through channels that never touched. Standard doctrine. Redundancy. If one hand was cut off, the other still worked.

Maren bought a candle she didn't want and followed neither of them.

Instead she walked back to her rooms, unlocked the folio, and turned to a section she visited less and less often these days: her own standing orders, the first ones, given to her at nineteen by an Archon whose name she'd never learned, in a hall of black stone beyond the Veil.

*"You will go into Vell as a hunter of heresy. You will hunt genuinely—false hunters are detected, and detected agents are lost. Find real crimes, punish real sinners, build a reputation the temple cannot afford to question. And underneath it, without the temple ever seeing, find us the Veilbearer."*

She had done all of it. She was very good at all of it. That was the trap nobody warned you about with competence: it didn't free you, it *buried* you, layer by layer, until the person you'd originally been was an archaeological site at the bottom of your own life, and you needed a dossier and a bone knife to dig her out.

Because here was the thing she had pieced together over nine years of reading the Order's correspondence with the attention of a woman who had learned early that her survival depended on understanding her masters better than they understood themselves:

The Order didn't want the Veilbearer saved. They didn't even, strictly speaking, want the Veil broken for the reasons they told recruits like a grieving tanner's daughter from Sorrel's Ford. They wanted the *prisoner*. The sealed thing beneath the temple—the tyrant, the Sunderer, whatever name the histories had burned away. They wanted him released under conditions they controlled, bound to instruments they owned, and the world's returning magic flowing through channels they governed. Freedom was the advertisement. Ownership was the product.

And the Veilbearer—the careful boy with the calculating pauses—was not the hero of that design. He was the *key*, and keys were carried, not consulted.

*He is not to be turned,* the letter had said. Not turned. Not harmed. Not allowed attachments. Every verb in the instruction was a verb you used for cargo.

Maren sat with that, in the cold room, in the flat afternoon light.

There was a version of her—the nineteen-year-old in the black stone hall—who would have read those instructions and felt only purpose. There was a version—the twenty-four-year-old who had knelt in a freezing chapel and sworn false vows to a false god and been proud of it—who would have felt nothing at all.

The version sitting here now, twenty-nine years old, holding a bone knife and a folio full of other people's designs, felt something she had no designation for. It wasn't conscience; she'd checked, thoroughly, over many years, and conscience appeared to be something she'd traded away too young to miss. It was closer to the instinct of a woman who has spent her whole life inside someone else's plan and has finally, belatedly, looked up and asked the question the plan was built to prevent:

*Who planned it? And what happens to me when it finishes?*

Assets were written off. She had written off assets herself. She knew the arithmetic. When the Veil fell and the Order's work was done, the Hand-in-Vell would be a loose thread in a new world—a woman who knew every secret route into Vell, every temple weakness, every grave that shouldn't exist. Loose threads got trimmed.

Unless the thread made itself useful. Unless the thread found a better loom.

Maren closed the folio and, for the first time in nine years, added a page to it that she did not write in her own hand. She wrote it in cipher, in the Order's own code, addressed to no one, dated with a calendar the Order didn't use:

*Contingency. If retrieval threatens the asset's function—if the boy breaks rather than bends—the Hand may act independently. The Order wants a key. A key that hates the lock is worth more than a key that loves the door. Consider recruitment. Consider honesty. Consider, if necessary, the unspeakable: telling him the truth.*

She stared at the words a long moment. Then she blotted the page, hid it at the bottom of the folio, beneath the boundary stone rubbings and the Ashgrove genealogy, and locked the chest.

Below her window, evening was coming on. Lamps bloomed in the scriptorium. Somewhere in the temple, a bell rang the hour, and the sound rolled out over a valley full of people who believed, with all their hearts, that the silence they lived in was peace.

Maren dressed in her Inquisitor's grey, pinned on the sunburst-and-circle that made servants flinch and Provosts sweat, and went out to do her job.

Tomorrow she would request the debt ledgers. Tomorrow she would rattle Brother Cassian's cage and watch what flew out. Tomorrow she would stand in front of Elodie Ashgrove with her courteous voice and her unsettling eyes and ask him questions whose answers she already possessed, and watch him decide, answer by answer, how much of himself to spend on lies.

But tonight, walking the cloister under the rising moon, Inquisitor Maren permitted herself one thought she had never once allowed in nine years of service, and spoke it silently to the absent god of absence, as if the empty circle of the Unseen Light could hear her:

*I was sent to find him. I intend to reach him first. And when the Order asks me to hand over the key—I will already have decided what the key is for.*

The bell finished ringing. The wind moved through the courtyard, stirred the surface of the old well, and died.

Three days later, she arrested Brother Cassian before the assembled temple.
