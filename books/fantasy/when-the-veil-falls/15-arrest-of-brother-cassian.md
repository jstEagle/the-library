## 15. Arrest of Brother Cassian

The summons came at dawn, folded into a slip of vellum and left on my copying desk the way all temple orders were: unsigned, unexplained, and impossible to refuse.

*All brothers and indentures will assemble in the nave at first bell. Attendance is mandatory.*

I read it three times, looking for something that wasn't there — a name, a reason, a mercy. Nothing. Just the flat institutional voice of the temple, which never announced anything good. In eleven years of indenture I had seen four mandatory assemblies. Two had been funerals. One had been the announcement of a grain tithe. The last one, when I was nine, had been the flogging of a kitchen boy caught stealing honey, carried out on the altar steps so that everyone could learn from it.

I looked across the scriptorium at Cassian's empty desk. His inkwell was capped. His quill lay parallel to the blotter, squared away with the precision of a man who expected to return. He'd been called up early — I'd heard his boots on the stairs an hour before dawn, before the bells, before the light. That alone should have frightened me more than it did.

*The Inquisitor finds the mentor.*

The words from the prophecy book rose in me like water coming to a boil, slow and then all at once. Three days since I'd read them. Three days of watching Cassian move through the temple's routines — matins, copying, the midday meal where he always saved me the seat beside him — while carrying the knowledge that every hour brought him closer to whatever the book had already written. I had told myself the words weren't fixed. I had told myself a hundred things: that I could warn him without explaining, that I could hide him, that the book might simply be wrong, that books could be wrong, that ink was only ink.

But you cannot hide a man inside a temple. The temple is the smallest room in Vell.

First bell rang. I put down my pen, and I went down to watch.

* * *

The nave was full. Every brother, every novice, every indentured scribe and servant stood in the long rows between the pillars, and above us the morning light came through the clerestory windows in slanted columns of dust and gold, falling across the stone floor like bars laid out for prisoners who hadn't arrived yet.

I found my place near the back, among the indentures, because indentures stood apart even in their own house. Sera would have been somewhere in the crowd too — she'd taken work as a market porter this week, enough to keep her pilgrim's disguise intact — but the nave was closed to outsiders. Whatever happened here, it would happen to us alone.

Inquisitor Maren stood at the front, below the altar, and the sight of her stopped my breath for a moment. She hadn't dressed for intimidation. She wore the same plain grey traveling habit, the same silver chain of office, and she stood with her hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting politely for a shopkeeper to finish with another customer. That was the thing about Maren I still couldn't shake, even now, even knowing what I know now: she never performed menace. She simply *arrived*, and let the room do the rest.

Brother Prior Anselm stood beside her, and he looked terrible. He kept touching his collar. He kept glancing at her and then away, the way a man glances at weather he can't do anything about.

Cassian stood alone at the center of the crossing, beneath the dome, where the light fell strongest.

They had made him wear the white robe.

I felt it land in my stomach before my mind understood it — the white robe, the robe of the accused, worn only during formal examination of heresy. It was clean, freshly laundered, and that was somehow the worst part. Someone had washed and pressed it. Someone had done this carefully, in advance, with the temple's ordinary diligence, the same diligence they gave to altar linens and festival banners. Heresy, in Vell, was a laundry task.

Anselm raised his hands and the murmur died.

"Brothers. Sisters. Children of the Veil." His voice carried the practiced steadiness of forty years of sermons, but I knew him well enough to hear the crack under it. "We are gathered not in celebration but in sorrow. For a hundred years, this valley has known peace. Not the fragile peace of treaties, but the true peace of the Veil — the blessed silence our founders won for us, in which no magic stirs and no magic can. We owe that silence everything. Our lives. Our children's lives. And we protect it by being vigilant against the one enemy the Veil cannot itself defeat."

He paused. The pause was liturgical; the whole nave breathed it with him.

"Doubt," Anselm said.

Somewhere behind me a novice shifted, cloth whispering on stone.

"Doubt is the crack through which the old fire returns," Anselm continued. "Doubt is how it began, a century ago, among people no different from us — clever people, learned people, people who read too much and believed too little. The founders sealed the Veil so that we would not have to fight that war again. But a seal holds only if the hearts beneath it hold." His eyes went, deliberately, to the figure in white. "And so it falls to us, with grief, to examine whether one heart has held."

Maren stepped forward. She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to; the nave's acoustics gave her whisper the weight of a pronouncement.

"Brother Cassian of the Ashgrove line," she said — and something cold slid down my spine at the surname, spoken aloud, spoken as though it mattered, as though she knew — "you stand accused of heresy against the Veil. Specifically: possession and study of prohibited texts concerning the founding era. Concealment of said texts from lawful inquiry. And instruction of others in forbidden history."

My ears went hot, then cold, then numb.

Instruction of others. *Others.* Me. She wasn't accusing him of reading. She was accusing him of teaching *me*, and either she knew it or she wanted the whole temple to wonder who the others were, so that every eye in the nave would begin searching its neighbor for the student.

I kept my head bowed. I had learned that much in eleven years: bow your head and become furniture.

"How does the accused answer?" Maren asked.

Cassian's voice came out level. I marveled at it, even then. Forty years of sermons had given him the same gift Anselm had, except his was real.

"I answer honestly, Inquisitor. I have read texts I should not have. I have kept them longer than I should have. I have concealed them." A ripple ran through the rows; confession was almost unheard of, and everyone knew it. "But I have instructed no one. What I learned, I have carried alone."

*Liar,* I thought, and the word broke something in me, because he was lying for me, and because the lie was good, and because I had taught it to him without meaning to — three days ago, over supper, when I'd lied to him first about where I'd been, and he had looked at me with those tired kind eyes and decided, apparently, to lie better.

"That remains to be established," Maren said. She turned slightly toward the assembly. Not addressing them, exactly. Letting them listen. "The examination will proceed by questions. Brother Cassian, forty years ago you served in this archive under Brother Halvard, may the Veil keep him. During that service, a certain volume passed through the cataloguing — a volume recovered from a sealed cache beneath the west cloister. Do you recall it?"

I stopped breathing.

There it was. Not the prophecy book — or not only. Something older. Something she had been circling since the day she arrived, asking her soft courteous questions about archive inventories and founding-era holdings while I told myself her interest was general, professional, harmless.

Cassian was quiet long enough that the silence became a sound of its own.

"I recall many volumes," he said.

"Do you recall this one?" Maren lifted a hand, and a warden stepped forward from the shadow of a pillar carrying a wooden case. She opened it herself. Inside, on black cloth, lay a book — small, bound in cracked leather gone the color of old blood, its pages edged in tarnished gilt.

Even from thirty feet away, even upside down, even with my heart hammering like a fist on a door, I knew it.

Not the copy hidden in the well shaft. The original. Or what the temple had always believed was the original — the one that had been listed in the confiscation records forty years ago, entered into the restricted vault, and then, according to every inventory I had ever audited, quietly missing. I had assumed it lost. Misfiled. Destroyed.

Maren had found it. Or been given it. Or brought it with her across the Veil.

And if she had the book, then the book in the well shaft — the book that wrote itself, the book with my name and today's date and the words that kept coming true —

— was the second one.

The thought arrived whole, like a stone dropping into deep water, and the ripples spread out through everything I understood. There had been two. There might have been more. The prophecy wasn't an accident I stumbled into; it was a text with a history, a provenance, a chain of readers stretching back through Cassian to whoever came before him. To the candidate who died. To whatever Cassian had done, forty years ago, that he had never told me.

"The accused will answer," Maren said gently.

"Yes," Cassian said. "I recall it."

"Describe its contents."

"No."

The word dropped into the nave like a coin into a well. Anselm flinched. Somewhere a warden's hand moved toward a weapon and stopped. Nobody said *no* to an Inquisitor. Nobody said *no* standing in the white robe beneath the dome with the whole valley watching.

Maren tilted her head, and I swear to the founders she looked almost pleased.

"Brother Cassian. You are offered this moment. Describe the book's contents, name any who shared your study of them, and the examination concludes with penance. Fasting, vigil, re-copying of corrected doctrine. The temple forgives what the temple understands." She folded her hands again. "Or decline, and the examination proceeds to judgment without your testimony. You know what judgment means, in a matter touching the Veil."

"I know what it means," Cassian said. "I watched it done to someone else, forty years ago, for the same reason. I was too much of a coward to speak then." He straightened, and the morning light caught the white robe, and for a moment he didn't look like my mentor at all — he looked like a man standing at the end of a very long road he'd been walking his entire life. "So I'll say it now, in front of everyone. The book is real. The histories are false. The founding story we teach is a story, stitched together after the fact, because there are no records before the sealing — none — and no graves older than a century, and every honest archivist who ever worked this archive has noticed, and every one of them was taught to be quiet about it, just as I was taught, and just as I taught—" 

He stopped himself. One heartbeat. Too late.

"—just as I was taught to be quiet," he finished. But the whole nave had heard the shape of the sentence he'd swallowed. *Just as I taught.*

I stared at the floor and felt every eye in the temple begin to slide along the rows of indentures, hunting for the student. Hunting for me.

"Enough," Maren said softly, and the word silenced the rising murmur more completely than a shout. She looked at Cassian for a long moment, and there was something in her face I couldn't read then and understand too well now — recognition, maybe. The look of one prisoner seeing another refuse the cell they've both been assigned.

"Brother Cassian," she said, "by authority vested in me by the Conclave of the Veil, I find you guilty of heresy against the silence. Sentence is remand to the temple cells pending deeper examination." She paused. "The deeper examination will begin tomorrow."

Two wardens crossed the nave and took Cassian's arms. He didn't resist. He looked past them, past Anselm, past Maren, out across the rows of faces — and his eyes found the section where the indentures stood, and they swept it once, quickly, without stopping on anyone, and in that single sweep I understood he was checking that I was there, that I was safe, that I was keeping my head down.

Then they turned him, and walked him out through the north door, and the doors closed, and the sound of them closing was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

* * *

We were dismissed in silence. No benediction. Anselm fled to his rooms. The brothers dispersed in knots of two and three, speaking in the low urgent voices of people processing a wound, and everywhere I passed I felt the attention snag on me — brief, speculative, moving on. *Instruction of others.* The phrase hung in the air like smoke.

I made myself walk the long way, past the refectory, past the washyard, unhurried, a tired scribe going about his morning. Only when I reached the stairwell down to the copying annex did I let myself lean against the wall and press the heel of my hand against my mouth until the shaking stopped.

*The Inquisitor finds the mentor.*

It hadn't needed me. That was the thing I finally understood, standing in the dark of the stairwell with my guilt sitting on my chest like a kneeler. I had spent three days believing the prophecy moved through my choices — that if I hid the book, if I stayed silent, if I was careful, the next line wouldn't come true. But Maren had found the original on her own, or been handed it, and Cassian had convicted himself with his own mouth rather than give me up, and the prophecy had simply been a description of a machine already in motion. The book didn't steer events. It read them early. Which meant the remaining lines were already rolling toward me like a cart down a hill, and no amount of carefulness would stop them.

*The scribe runs. The Inquisitor follows.*

Tomorrow, she would begin the deeper examination. I had seen the instruments room once, as a boy — I'd never been told what it was; I'd found it by accident, a door off the undercroft landing with a padlock and a smell of vinegar and iron that I still couldn't forget. Cassian wouldn't last a day. He had lasted forty years by protecting one secret; he would not survive the protection of two.

Which meant I had until tomorrow night to get him out, or get the truth to someone who could, or run — and running was written in the book, and following was written right after it, and I was so tired, God, I was so tired of reading my own life in someone else's handwriting.

I went down to the annex. I sat at my desk. I took up my pen and copied scripture for two hours with perfect mechanical obedience, the letters marching across the page in their beautiful dead order — *blessed is the silence, blessed is the Veil, blessed are they who ask nothing* — and around noon a novice came down with a tray and told me, not unkindly, that the Inquisitor wished to see me.

"She says," the novice added, shifting from foot to foot, "that she wants to talk about your family."

I set down my pen very carefully, so that it wouldn't snap in my fingers.

"The Ashgrove name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "She mentioned it this morning."

"Yeah," the novice said. "Funny thing, isn't it? There's a carving at the boundary stone with that name on it. My cousin hauls quarry loads out that way. Says it's older than the temple."

The floor did not drop out this time. It had already been gone for days. I simply stood at the edge of the space where it used to be and nodded, and picked up the tray, because refusing would look like fear, and followed the novice up the stairs toward the room where the Inquisitor was waiting — knowing, with absolute certainty, that whatever she wanted with my family's name, the well shaft, the second book, and the flight the prophecy had already written for me, all of it started in that room.

Behind me, far below the annex floor, in the dark of the temple cells, Cassian was sitting in the white robe, keeping silent for my sake.

Ahead of me, Maren was smiling her courteous smile.

I climbed.
