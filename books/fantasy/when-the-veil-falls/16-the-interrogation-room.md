## 16. The Interrogation Room

The room they took him to had been a wine cellar once. Cassian knew it from the old days, before the temple converted the underlevels—knew the shape of the arches, the salt stain on the eastern wall where the mortar had always sweated. Now there was a table bolted to the floor and an iron ring in the ceiling and nothing else, and the absence of the barrels was its own kind of confession.

They had not chained him. That was the first thing he noticed, and the first thing that frightened him.

Brother Anselm—the young warden who had held his arm on the walk down, apologetic as a man steering a drunk uncle through a wedding—patted his shoulder at the door and said, "Sorry, Brother Cassian," as though the apology were the procedure. Then the door shut, and the sound of it was very small and very final, and Cassian was alone with a single candle and the memory of forty years of careful, cowardly silence.

He tested the chair. It did not move. He folded his hands on the table, the way he'd taught novices to fold their hands during examinations of conscience: open palms, nothing hidden. He almost laughed. Forty years of teaching boys to show their empty hands, and here were his own hands at last, and they were not empty at all. They were full to the wrists with everything he had never said.

*You have one job now,* he told himself. *Not honesty. Not penance. One job.*

Keep them looking anywhere but at the boy.

The candle guttered when she came in. He hadn't heard the door.

Inquisitor Maren closed it softly behind her and stood for a moment just inside, letting her eyes adjust, letting *him* adjust to her. She was smaller than her reputation. He had imagined the Inquisitor as something carved—all angles and judgment—but she was compact, gray-eyed, with the kind of face you forgot while you were still looking at it. She wore no insignia beyond the plain iron clasp at her collar. Her boots made no sound on the stone.

"Brother Cassian." She sat opposite him without ceremony, laying a leather folder on the table but not opening it. A prop. He knew props. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't ask," he said. "Anselm carried me."

"He's stronger than he looks." She studied him with a frankness that was almost gentle. "You've been here a long time. I read your intake record. Thirty-nine years ago. You were twenty-six."

"I was."

"It says you wept at your vows."

Cassian kept his face still, though the memory moved through him like cold water: the incense, the fear, the enormous relief of surrendering a life he'd already ruined. "It says correctly."

Maren nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something on a list only she could see. Then she opened the folder—not to read it, he understood, but so he would know there was a folder—and turned it toward herself.

"Do you know what I do, Brother Cassian? Truly? The valley has ideas about it. They think I burn people."

"The temple thinks you keep us safe."

"The temple thinks whatever is easiest to think." She smiled, briefly, and it transformed her into someone startlingly human, someone you might sit beside at market. "I listen. That's most of the work. People lie to each other constantly, but they almost never lie well, and they never lie entirely. Every falsehood is built out of true materials. My work is sorting the lumber." She tapped the folder. "Yours, I'm finding, is a very interesting house."

"I'm a scribe and a teacher of scribes. My house is ink stains and bad knees."

"Mm." She turned a page she did not look at. "Three days ago, a book went missing from the sealed archive. Catalogue number eleven-seventy-one. *Foundational period.* Do you know it?"

"No."

"You didn't hesitate."

"I hesitated," Cassian said mildly. "You blinked first."

Something flickered behind the gray eyes—annoyance, or respect, or the place where those two things lived together in professionals like her. She closed the folder.

"All right," she said. "Let's not waste each other's time, because neither of us can afford it, though only one of us knows exactly how little he has. Shall I tell you how I found you?"

"You haven't found me. I've been sitting here since matins."

"How I *identified* you, then. As a person of interest." She leaned forward, forearms on the table, and dropped her voice as if the walls might be embarrassed by what she said next. "Forty years ago, there was another inquiry. Quiet one. Before my time—I've read the fragments. A candidate. A young woman with a name that appears nowhere else in any record I can find, which is itself remarkable, because everyone has records. Births, debts, deaths. Vell is a valley of ledgers, Brother Cassian. And this girl is a gap in the ledger. Gaps don't happen by accident. Gaps are made by hands."

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Cassian made himself breathe.

"And you think my hands made it?"

"I think," Maren said, "that the archive's access ledger shows a Brother C. signed out catalogue eleven-seventy-one twice in the year before the sealing records go dark. Twice. Once in spring, once in autumn. And then never again, for forty years, until—" she let the sentence die elegantly.

"Until a copyist pulled it by mistake and returned it. I remember the entry. Sloppy shelving in the sealed stacks; it happens."

"In forty years it happened once."

"Then I'm due credit for consistency."

She laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of her—and for one absurd moment they were two tired people in a cellar sharing a joke, and Cassian understood with sickening clarity why she was good at this. Not the questions. The moments between the questions, where you forgot you were being hunted and started being *known*, and knowing was the oldest hunger there was.

"That's the thing about you," she said, recovering. "Everyone in this temple is afraid of me. The wardens stand straighter. The cooks give me extra bread. Even Abbot Ossory laughs three seconds too long after his own jokes. But you—" she tilted her head, "—you're not afraid. You're *relieved*. You've been waiting for this room for forty years. I can smell it on you. So let's skip to the part where I stop asking clever questions, because clever questions are for people who still want to hide."

She reached into her robe and set something on the table between them.

A key. Plain iron, temple-stamped.

"Walk out of here tonight," she said. "No arrest record. No mark against your orders. Anselm forgets your face. You go back to your dormitory cell, your morning bells, your bad knees and your ink stains, and I never trouble you again. That is the offer. It costs you one thing."

Cassian looked at the key and thought, with strange tenderness, of Elodie at seven years old, standing in the scriptorium doorway too proud to admit he couldn't reach the shelf, refusing help, stretching until his whole body trembled. He thought of the boy at twelve, copying scripture with tears of frustration because his hand wouldn't obey his ambition. He thought of last week—last *week*—the boy's face going white as bone over some page, and lying to Cassian with his father's terrible transparent eyes, and Cassian letting him lie because asking the true question would have meant answering it himself.

*One job,* he reminded himself. *And it isn't saving yourself.*

"Tell me where Elodie Ashgrove is," Maren said quietly, "and take the key."

* * *

The silence stretched long enough that the candle burned down a visible fraction. Cassian used every second of it, because he needed the time more than she needed the answer.

Here was the truth he could not say: he did not know where the boy was. That had been the one mercy of the last two days—his ignorance was genuine. After the arrest, after they'd marched him past the assembled brothers with their lowered eyes, he had been locked in a guest cell, and whatever Elodie had done—fled, hidden, run to Sera or away from her—had happened beyond his reach or his knowledge. He could betray nothing he did not possess.

But ignorance was a perishable good. She would keep him. She would starve the truth out of the gaps in his story, or worse, she would stop believing the gaps were gaps and start reading them as design. Sooner or later she'd find the well shaft—he'd seen the way her gaze snagged on the courtyard, cataloguing, always cataloguing—or she'd find the boy himself. Three days. If the prophecy's arithmetic held, three days was what Elodie had bought, somehow, somewhere, and Cassian's task was to make sure those three days stayed worthless to her.

So he would lie. He had spent forty years telling novices that lies corrode the soul like water in mortar, and he would lie now, cleanly and completely, and he would carry the corrosion gladly.

He looked up.

"I'll tell you where he is," Cassian said.

Maren did not move, but everything in her stilled—the particular stillness of a hunter who hears the brush move. "Go on."

"He'll be at the ferry landing below Millbrook. Tomorrow night, second bell." He said it smoothly, and hated how smoothly, and made himself add texture, because smooth lies are naked lies. "There's a woman. Came through as a pilgrim a few weeks ago—dark hair, foreign accent, pays with coins nobody mints anymore. He's been meeting her. I told him to stop. I told him what happens to boys who talk to strangers at the edge of the map." He shook his head, wearied, rueful. "He didn't stop. Boys don't."

"Ferry landing," Maren repeated. "Millbrook. Second bell."

"There's a boathouse with a green door. The lock is broken; everyone knows. If he's running, that's where he'd run, because it's the one door in the valley that opens outward." He met her eyes and let the grief come through honestly, because the grief was honest, and honest material makes the strongest lumber. "I raised that child, Inquisitor. His father died under temple contract and I signed the ledger that bound the son. Whatever else I am, I am the reason that boy owes his life to these walls. If he's broken the law, then some part of that is mine, and I'll pay my share. But I won't guess for you. Guesses waste your time and damn him faster. This is where he'll be."

Maren watched him for a long moment. He watched her watch him, and understood that she was doing precisely what she claimed—sorting his lumber—and that the best he could do was build her a structure so complete she'd live in it rather than inspect it.

"You're offering this freely," she said. "No conditions?"

"One condition." He laid his hand flat on the table, an old man's hand, ink-stained at the cuticle. "Whatever he's done—when you take him—you don't hurt him. He's a scribe, not a soldier. He startles easy and he freezes when frightened, and a warden with a drawn blade will get a dead boy for his trouble. Take him gently. Promise me that, and the key can stay on your table."

"And if he resists?"

"He won't. Not if it's you who comes for him." *Because you frighten him less than the future does,* he thought. *Because he's spent his whole life obeying the temple, and you wear its face.* The thought curdled even as he used it.

Maren picked up the key. Turned it over once. Set it down again, closer to his side of the table—an artist's touch, generosity staged as temptation.

"Millbrook," she said. "Second bell tomorrow night. The green door." She rose, smoothing her robe. "If you're wrong, Brother Cassian—if I go to that boathouse and find spiders—we will resume this conversation, and next time there won't be a key on the table. There will be a different kind of furniture."

"I understand."

At the door she paused, her back to him, and said, without turning: "The girl who vanished, forty years ago. Did you love her?"

The question went through him like a needle finding an old wound he'd forgotten the location of. He saw, unbidden, a face he had spent four decades not seeing—laughing, furious, alive, and then not alive, and then not anything, because he had failed her in the exact manner he was now failing everything except the boy.

"Yes," Cassian said.

Maren nodded once, as though a figure on a ledger had finally balanced, and left him alone with the candle and the key.

* * *

He sat for a long time. Long enough that his joints complained when he finally stood; long enough that the candle had burned to a thumb's width above the table.

The key lay where she'd left it. He picked it up. It was warm from her hand, which felt obscene.

He turned it in his fingers and considered the shape of the trap he'd just walked through. She would send wardens to Millbrook—of course she would, but not all of them, and perhaps not her best ones, because some part of her, the part that had asked about the girl, suspected the gift. Or she would send everyone, emptying the temple for a night, leaving the archive thin and the gates soft. Either way, the boy gained hours. Hours were not salvation. But hours were what prophets dealt in, apparently, and Cassian had learned, far too late and at unbearable cost, that you worked with the currency you had.

He crossed to the door and tried the handle. Unlocked. Of course unlocked—the key had been theater, a test of appetite, and he had passed by taking it. He slipped it into his sleeve anyway. Let them search him later and find it. Let them draw whatever conclusion comforted them.

In the corridor, Anselm fell into step beside him, escorting the prisoner back to his cell with the same apologetic gentleness.

"Brother Cassian," the young warden ventured, as they climbed. "The Inquisitor—is it true she lets cooperators go?"

"She keeps her word," Cassian said. "That's the frightening thing about her."

At his cell door he paused. Below, through the narrow window, the valley lay blue and silver in the early dusk, the fields, the millpond, the far smoke of Millbrook, and beyond it all the rim of mountains that were not mountains at all but the hem of a lie stitched across the sky. Somewhere under that sky a boy was running with a burning book in his pack, carrying a name that had been a prison for a century.

"Second bell tomorrow," Cassian murmured, mostly to himself.

"Brother?"

"Pray for rain, Anselm," Cassian said. "Tomorrow night. Pray for rain."

He closed the door and began, in the dark, to count the bells.
