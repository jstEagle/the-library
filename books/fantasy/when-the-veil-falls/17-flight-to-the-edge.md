## 17. Flight to the Edge

The bell began ringing before dawn.

Not the morning bell—that one came at first light, measured and patient, calling the brothers to prayer. This was the alarm bell, the one I had heard exactly twice in my life: once for a quarry collapse that killed four men, once for a fire in the tannery district that took a whole street of houses. It had a different voice than the other bells. Urgent. Cracked. A sound like something breaking.

I was already awake. I hadn't slept.

I sat in my cell with my pack between my knees—my scribe's pack, the one I'd carried every day for eleven years, now stuffed with things it had never held. The prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth. A change of clothes. The knife Cassian kept in his desk drawer, which I had taken without asking because asking would have meant telling, and telling would have meant he'd try to stop me, or worse, try to come with me. Bread stolen from the refectory. My mother's ring, which I had sworn I'd never pawn and was now carrying anyway, not to sell but because leaving it behind felt like leaving her.

And beneath all of it, against the small of my back where I could feel it through the canvas, the blank page.

It wasn't burning. Not literally. But I could feel it the way you feel a coal through a glove—a heat with no temperature, a pressure with no weight. Since the third line had filled itself in, since *the Inquisitor finds the mentor* had appeared in ink that hadn't existed a moment before, the page had felt *awake*. Like something in my pack was watching me decide what to do next.

Maren had found the well shaft empty. That meant she knew the book moved. That meant she knew someone had taken it, and there were perhaps six people in Vell who could have, and only one of them was an indentured scribe with no family and no future worth protecting.

The bell rang on.

I should have run then. I know that now. There was a gap—the time between the alarm and the wardens being organized, maybe a quarter hour, maybe less—and I spent it standing at my narrow window looking down at the cloister, watching torchlight move between the buildings, watching men I'd eaten beside for a decade become hunters.

Because I couldn't stop thinking about Cassian.

They'd taken him two days ago. Two days of silence from the interrogation wing, two days of Brother Anselm avoiding my eyes in the scriptorium, two days of Maren's courtesy hanging over everything like smoke. And last night, as I'd packed by candlelight, I'd finally admitted what the prophecy's second entry had been telling me all along: they hadn't arrested Cassian to get to me. They'd arrested him because they already knew where I was going to be. The book didn't predict. It instructed. Which meant somewhere in its pages—or in Maren's reading of them—was a version of tonight where I stayed, where I let myself be found, where the story ended neatly with the Veilbearer in a cell and the truth buried under another century of scripture.

I wasn't going to give it that ending.

A knock at my door—soft, three beats, wrong rhythm for a warden.

I opened it with the knife palmed, stupid and useless, and Sera slipped inside before I could speak.

"You're still here." She said it flatly, but I heard the fear underneath. She looked terrible—smudged with soot, hair escaping its pilgrim's hood, a fresh cut along her jaw that hadn't been there at the market. "Elodie. The bell. Do you understand what the bell means?"

"It means they know."

"It means they've known since midnight and they were waiting for light." She crossed the cell in two strides and started repacking my bag with brisk, angry efficiency, shifting the weight off my spine, cinching straps I'd left loose. "Wardens don't hunt at night in Vell. No torch discipline, everyone trips over each other. So they waited. They're moving *now*, and the east gate is already watched, and the north road has been watched for a day."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been watching them watch it." She pulled the drawstring tight and shoved the pack into my chest. "South. Through the tannery district, along the millrace, out past the old kilns. There's a stretch where the wall comes down to the water and nobody guards it because the current eats anyone who tries to swim. We're not swimming. We're walking. There's a way."

"There's always a way with you," I said. "What does it cost?"

She looked at me then, really looked, and whatever she saw in my face made her answer honestly. "Everything you're carrying. Move."

* * *

The tannery district at night smelled like death pretending to be industry—lime pits and rendered fat and the sweet rot of hides waiting their turn. We moved through it fast, Sera ahead, me following the pale flicker of her hood, our footsteps swallowed by the rush of the millrace. Behind us the temple bell had stopped, which was somehow worse. Silence meant the searching had gone quiet and organized. Silence meant dogs.

Twice we froze in doorways as patrol lanterns swung past—wardens in temple grey, moving in pairs, checking shutters with a politeness that was more frightening than shouting would have been. One of them I recognized. Brother Tomas, who'd taught me my letters. He paused outside the doorway where we crouched, close enough that I could hear him breathing, and then moved on, and I pressed my hand flat against the wall and made myself breathe again.

"They'll check the archive first," I whispered when we moved on. "When they find my cell empty. They'll think I went for the book."

"The book's in your pack."

"I know. But they don't know that. It buys us—" I did the arithmetic and hated how small the number was. "An hour. Maybe."

Sera glanced back at me. In the dark her eyes caught the lantern-light strangely, a faint amber that no ordinary pilgrim's eyes would hold. "You're calmer than I expected."

"I'm not calm. I'm past it. There's a place past fear where it stops being useful and just becomes weather." I shifted the pack higher on my shoulders. The blank page pulsed against my spine, patient as a heartbeat. "Cassian taught me that. He said fear is a tool like any other, and a tool you can't put down owns you instead."

"He sounds like a good man."

"He is. Was. Is." My throat tightened. "Whatever they're doing to him right now, it's because of me. Because of a book I couldn't leave alone. Every step I take away from this valley is a step I'm taking on his back."

Sera stopped. We stood in the black mouth of an alley between the tannery and the mill, the race roaring below us, and she turned and gripped my shoulder hard enough to hurt.

"Listen to me. If you go back, he suffered for nothing. If you get caught, he suffered for nothing. The only thing that makes what they did to him mean anything is if you get beyond that wall and learn what's actually true." Her grip eased. "I've watched people freeze at moments like this. The ones who survive are the ones who decide the cost is already paid and start spending forward instead of backward."

"That's a cold way to live."

"It's the only way anyone lives past twenty-five where I'm from." She turned back toward the water. "Come on. The kilns are close."

* * *

The old kilns squatted at the valley's southern edge like the vertebrae of some buried animal—abandoned for sixty years, according to the temple records, though the records also said Vell had always been magicless, so I was done trusting records. We moved between them single file, and here, for the first time, Sera did something I couldn't explain.

She stopped at the largest kiln, laid her palm against the cracked brick, and *pushed*—not with her hands. With something else. I felt it pass through the air like a change in pressure, the way the valley felt before a storm that never came. The brick shivered. Dust fell. And a section of the kiln's rear wall, which had looked like solid stone, exhaled a breath of cold air through a seam that hadn't been visible a moment before.

"What did you—" I started.

"Later." Her voice was strained. Sweat beaded at her hairline despite the cold. "Magic doesn't work here. You know that. What works here is *pressure*—the Veil pushes back against anything that tries, and if you push carefully, the push goes somewhere. Doors are easier than most things." She wiped her face. "It costs me. Don't ask how much. Come on."

The passage behind the wall was a crack in the earth, barely wide enough for my pack, sloping down into darkness that smelled of wet stone and deep time. Sera produced a small glass vial from her cloak, uncorked it, and light bloomed inside it—soft, greenish, sourceless. Another impossibility, casually performed. I was beginning to understand that everything about her was a door pushed open at cost.

"How long?" I asked as we descended.

"To the boundary? Half a night underground, then open country until dawn. The boundary stone is another day's walk past that." She glanced back at me, and the vial-light carved her face into planes and shadow. "Then you cross, and everything changes. Elodie—I need to say this before we're out. Once you cross, your power wakes. All of it. It will feel like drowning and burning at the same time, and it won't stop, and I can't make it gentler. Some people break. The Order lost two candidates to it before you."

"And the one who didn't break?"

"Became Archon Vey." She said the name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Think about whether that comforts you."

We walked on. The tunnel narrowed, widened, forked; Sera chose forks without hesitation, and I stopped asking how she knew. Somewhere above us the temple bells started again—a new pattern, faster, and I understood they'd found my empty cell. I put my hand against the pack strap and kept walking.

It was in the deepest part of the tunnel, where the ceiling pressed low and the air went thin, that the page spoke.

Not in words. I want to be clear about that, because for years afterward people asked me what the prophecy said to me in the dark, and the truth was stranger: it didn't say anything. It *showed* me. A flash behind my eyes, bright as a struck match—Cassian in a grey room, sitting very straight, hands folded, blood at the corner of his mouth, and he was smiling. Smiling the way he smiled when a student finally understood something difficult. And beneath the image, not words but *certainty*: *he bought you time. Spend it.*

I staggered. Sera caught my arm.

"What? What is it?"

"I saw him." My voice came out scraped. "He's alive. He gave them something—false, I think—he's buying us time." I laughed, or something near a laugh, and it broke in the middle. "He's smiling, Sera. Why is he smiling?"

"Because he loves you, probably." She said it simply, without softness, which was the only way I could have borne it. "Can you keep walking?"

"Yes."

"Then walk. Grieve later. Everything later."

* * *

We came up out of the earth an hour before dawn, through a fissure in a hillside bramble-choked and invisible unless you knew, and the sky over Vell was the color of iron filings. Behind us the valley lay in its bowl, the temple's spire a dark finger against the greying east, a smudge of smoke rising from somewhere near the archive quarter that I refused to look at directly. Ahead, the land climbed in long grey folds toward the southern ridge, and beyond the ridge—

Beyond the ridge was nothing. Not emptiness. *Nothing.* The kind of nothing that has weight. I'd seen the valley's edge from high pastures all my life and thought it was haze, thought it was distance, thought it was the ordinary failure of far things to resolve. Standing here at ground level, I understood my mistake. The world didn't fade at Vell's border. It *stopped*, cleanly, like a page torn out, and beyond the tear was a white that wasn't light.

"That's the Veil," Sera said quietly. "From inside, it looks like fog. From outside, it looks like fog. From the boundary itself—" she shrugged. "You'll see."

"And the wardens?"

"Behind us. Two parties. They found the tunnel entrance at the kilns about twenty minutes ago, which means they know exactly where we're headed, which means we have until midmorning before they reach the boundary stone." She checked the horizon, then me. "Can you run?"

"I'm a scribe."

"That's not an answer."

I shifted the pack. The page burned steady against my spine—not painful now, almost encouraging, which frightened me more than pain would have. "I can run."

We ran.

The land rose in swells of dead grass and frost-brittle scrub, and my lungs lit up with fire within the first mile, and I ran anyway, because behind us the pursuit horns had begun—three notes, repeated, the quarry-call I'd only ever heard in festival reenactments—and because ahead of us the white nothing waited with a patience that felt older than the valley. Sera ran like water finding its level, effortless, correcting my course with small touches whenever I drifted. Twice she pulled me down into gullies as outriders crested ridgelines to our west, temple grey against the grey grass, close enough that I could hear their calls to one another.

The sun came up red through the Veil's white edge, smeared and strange, and we crested the final ridge together, both of us bent double, breath tearing.

The boundary stone stood thirty paces down the slope.

I had expected a marker—a post, a cairn, something human-sized. Instead it was a monolith taller than two men, black as cooled slag, standing alone in the frost. Even from here I could see it was carved, covered head to foot in lettering worn nearly smooth, and even from here, before I could read a single character, some part of me already knew what name would be cut deepest into its face.

My chest hurt. Not from the running.

"Elodie." Sera's hand closed on my wrist. "Before we go down there. Whatever you see on that stone—whatever name—you need to keep moving. Do you hear me? The wardens are ten minutes behind us. You can fall apart on the other side. Not here."

I nodded. I don't know if I agreed.

We went down the slope at a stumbling half-run, and the carvings resolved as we approached, and the letters were Old Script, the formal hand I'd spent eleven years copying, and the central inscription was deeper than the rest, cut by hands that had wanted it remembered forever:

**ASHGROVE.**

Below it, smaller, twelve lines of text I couldn't parse yet, and below those, a date. A date one hundred years and some months old. A date that matched, day for day, the founding feast the temple celebrated every autumn.

My ancestors hadn't just lived in this valley. They had built its walls.

"Elodie." Sera, urgent now, pulling at my arm. Horns behind us, closer. "Elodie, we have to—"

"I'm coming."

I touched the stone once—just once, just long enough to feel it hum against my palm like a plucked string, like something recognizing me—and then I stepped past it, into the white.

The world tore open.

There was no gentle crossing, no threshold, no moment of transition. One stride I was in the dead air of Vell, and the next, magic hit me like a wave hitting a man who'd forgotten the sea existed. It poured into me through skin and breath and the open wound of my own name—it filled places I hadn't known were empty, and the filling *hurt*, it hurt like thawing frostbite, like a limb waking after years asleep, every nerve screaming with returned sensation. I went to my knees on the far side of the stone. Colors detonated behind my eyes. I could hear the grass growing. I could hear the wardens' hearts beating forty yards behind us. I could hear, faint and vast and patient, something turning over in its sleep a hundred miles beneath my feet.

And threaded through all of it, calm as ink spreading through water, the blank page in my pack finished its work. I felt the fourth line write itself without seeing it, felt the certainty settle into my bones like a signature:

*the Veilbearer crosses, and the world remembers.*

"Get up." Sera hauled at my shoulder, her voice frayed, her amber eyes wide with something that might have been awe or terror. "Please. Get up. They're here."

I got up. The first thing I saw on the far side of the Veil was the wardens spilling over the ridge behind us—Brother Tomas among them, lantern raised, stopping dead at the boundary stone as if the ground beyond it were made of knives.

The second thing I saw was the horizon.

The outer world stretched away under a bruised and burning sunset, and it was scarred—craters grown over with black trees, roads that ended in midair, the skeleton of something enormous and winged half-buried in a hillside five miles off. A century of quiet had healed nothing. The war had only been waiting.

"Oh," I said, to no one, to the stone, to the name cut into it. "Oh, what did we do?"

Sera pulled me into the tall grass, and behind us the wardens turned back, and ahead of us the whole wounded world waited to learn what an Ashgrove was for.
