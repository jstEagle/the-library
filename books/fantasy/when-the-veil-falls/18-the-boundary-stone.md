## 18. The Boundary Stone

The wardens' lanterns were still swinging in the dark behind us when Sera decided they weren't going to catch up.

"They'll lose the trail at the millpond," she said, not slowing. "Wardens don't like water. It's the one thing in this valley they can't read footprints out of."

Elodie stumbled beside her, pack straps cutting into his shoulders, the prophecy book a hard rectangle against his spine that he kept touching like a man checking for a wound. He'd been touching it all night. Every mile of the flight from Vell, through the orchards and the quarry road and the long gray fields where nothing grew higher than knee-height because nothing in Vell was permitted to reach for anything.

"You don't know that," Elodie said. "About the millpond."

"I know it the way you know scripture." Sera didn't look back either. Looking back was how people got caught; she had learned that lesson in a different country, under different lanterns, at an age she did not permit herself to think about. "You recite it until it's true."

"That's not knowing."

"No," she agreed. "It's better."

He gave her a look—she caught the shape of it in her peripheral vision, wounded and stubborn at once—and said nothing, which was becoming his habit around her. Good. A scribe who could hold silence was worth ten who couldn't. She'd told him once, on the second night of their acquaintance, back when she was still only a pilgrim to him, that the quietest person in any room was usually the one carrying the most dangerous cargo. He hadn't understood then. He was beginning to.

They came down off the last ridge as the sky went from black to bruise, and there it was.

Sera felt it before she saw it—that faint wrongness she had been tracking since the day she walked through Vell's northern pass disguised in pilgrim gray, smiling at temple wardens, counting her heartbeats while the dead air of the valley pressed against her skin like wet wool. All through Vell the feeling had been one thing: absence. Magic didn't fail in Vell. It simply wasn't, the way sound isn't underwater. But here, at the valley's rim, the absence changed texture. Here it had edges. Here, if she let her senses open even a crack—and she had been keeping them shut for three days, because opening them near wardens was suicide—it felt less like emptiness and more like pressure. Like standing inside a held breath.

A century old. And still holding.

"There," Elodie said.

She had already seen it. Of course she had seen it; she had crossed half the world following rumors of it, and now it stood in front of them in the first gray light like a tooth left behind by some enormous jaw.

The stone was taller than two men. It rose out of the hilltop grass alone—no ruin around it, no road leading to it, no path worn by pilgrims or shepherds or children playing at dares. Just the stone, and the grass, and the wind coming over the ridge from the outside world and dying an inch from its surface.

"It's warm," Elodie said.

Sera frowned. "It can't be. Feel it again."

He had his palm flat against the granite. She watched his face as he did it—the small furrow between his brows that meant he was comparing something against something else, the scribe's reflex, cross-referencing reality against expectation. She had watched him do it over market prices, over Cassian's sermons, over the contradictions in the founding histories he'd spent weeks pulling apart. It was, she thought, the same gesture every time. Elodie Ashgrove met the world the way he met a page: looking for what had been altered.

"It's warm," he repeated. "Like a wall that gets sun all day. But it faces north."

"Let me."

She put her hand beside his.

Warmth, yes—but underneath the warmth, faint as a pulse taken at the wrist, something else. A rhythm. Slow, so slow she might have imagined it, except that Sera had grown up in a world where things pulsed, and she knew the difference between a heartbeat and a working. This was a working. Old, enormous, patient. The kind of spell that wasn't cast so much as *installed*—woven into stone and earth and air, given one instruction, and left to keep it forever.

Her hand came off the stone faster than she meant it to.

"What?" Elodie asked. "What is it?"

"Your valley," Sera said slowly, "isn't magicless. It's *warded*. There's a difference, and it matters enormously, and I think—" She stopped. Made herself start again. "I think we've been calling it the wrong thing. Everyone has. For a hundred years."

"The Veil."

"The Veil. Yes. Except a veil covers something." She looked up at the monolith, at the way the dawn light seemed to bend slightly around its edges, the way the grass within arm's reach grew in a perfect ring, neither greener nor browner than the rest, just *obedient*. "This doesn't cover. This holds."

"Holds what?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question the whole rotten century had been built on top of, and Sera had her orders about the answer—orders written in Archon Vey's beautiful, terrible handwriting—and she looked at Elodie Ashgrove's exhausted, hopeful face in the gray light and felt the first splinter go into the place where her loyalty lived.

"We should read it," she said. "The stone. Before anything else."

* * *

The carvings were on the eastern face, which meant they'd been made to be read by someone arriving from outside. That detail lodged in Sera's mind and stayed there, cold and growing colder, as she traced the worn grooves with her fingertips.

Twelve lines of names. Twelve families, cut deep into the granite in a script older than Vell's temple, older than the liturgy Elodie had spent his life copying, older than the debt ledgers and the doctrine and the gentle, suffocating rule of Brother Cassian's church. Weather had eaten some of them. Eleven of the twelve were so eroded she could barely make out the letterforms—ghosts of ghosts, families ground down to almost-nothing by a century of wind.

But the twelfth line was sharp. Unweathered. As crisp as the day the chisel lifted.

**ASHGROVE**

Beside it, smaller, in the same hand: *First Among the Sealing.*

Sera heard Elodie stop breathing.

It was a small sound, or rather the absence of one, but in the silence of that hilltop it was enormous. She turned. He was standing a step back from the stone with both hands hanging at his sides, and his face had gone the color of the fog coming off the low fields, and his eyes were fixed on his own name—not his name exactly, but the root of it, the trunk from which his whole life had grown—and he was reading it the way a man reads a verdict.

"Say it isn't," he said quietly. "Tell me it means something else. Ashgrove is a common enough name. There were Ashgroves in the quarry rolls, Ashgroves at the mill—"

"Scribe." She used the title deliberately, the way you'd press a cloth to a wound. "Look at the carving. Look at the others."

He looked. She watched him work through it, watched the scribe take over from the frightened boy, because that was the miracle of Elodie Ashgrove and she had known it since the market, since the moment she'd shown him a spark of magic die at the valley's edge and he hadn't run screaming—he'd *taken notes*. Under terror, under everything, there was a mind that reached for evidence the way drowning men reached for rope.

"The other eleven are worn away," he said. His voice came out level. Barely. "A century of weather. But this one—" He stepped forward again, touched the letters, traced them. "This one's been maintained."

"Yes."

"Stones don't maintain themselves."

"No."

"Somebody comes here." His finger was still resting on the A, and Sera saw his knuckles whiten. "Somebody has been coming here, regularly, for a hundred years, to re-cut my family's name. To keep it legible. To keep it *first*." He turned to her, and his eyes were too bright. "Who does that? Who keeps a name alive like that?"

"Someone who wants it remembered," Sera said. "Or someone who wants it *known*. There's a difference, and I don't yet know which side of it we're standing on."

"But it says First Among the Sealing." He laughed—one short, disbelieving syllable with no humor in it anywhere. "Do you understand what that means? We were taught—Cassian taught me myself, when I was nine years old, kneeling right there in the chapel—that the Veil was raised by the gods, or by the founders, or by no one, depending on which sermon year you got. That it fell like mercy on a suffering world. That we were the *protected*. The kept. The ones sheltered behind the curtain while the war burned itself out." He pressed his palm flat against the stone again, over the name, hard, as if he could push the truth back inside the rock. "We weren't sheltered, Sera. We were *sealed in*. My family built the box and put us all inside it."

"And you," Sera said, before she could stop herself.

He went still.

"The book named you Veilbearer. Not Veilkeeper. Not Veilpriest." She heard her own voice going somewhere she hadn't authorized, saying things her orders forbade, and she kept talking anyway, because some part of her had apparently decided three days ago that this stubborn, ink-stained, debt-bound boy deserved the truth more than the Ashen Order deserved their prize delivered intact. "*Bearer.* You carry it. Whatever your ancestors sealed, whatever this stone is holding shut—the prophecy didn't choose a stranger to mind it. It chose their blood. It chose *you*."

The wind came over the ridge again, from the outside, from the scarred world, and broke against the stone and died.

Elodie sat down. Not gracefully—he simply folded, knees in the wet grass, back against the monolith's base, directly beneath his family's name, and put his head in his hands. Sera stood over him and let him. There was nothing useful to say. She knew what it was to find out your family had been lying to you in the largest possible font; she'd found hers out at fifteen, in a room that smelled of ash, and she still hadn't finished paying for it.

"My father died in the quarry," Elodie said finally, to his own hands. "The temple called it an act of providence. Called the debt an inheritance. Worked me eleven years to pay for the privilege of grieving." He looked up at her, and his eyes were dry, which was somehow worse. "And the whole time, the real inheritance was *this*. A name on a prison door. I've been an indentured servant to the jailers' church my entire life, and I never even knew the jail was ours."

"Or," Sera said carefully, "you're descended from twelve families who ended a war. From the one who took point. We don't know what the sealing cost them. We don't know why the name needed keeping. We don't know anything except what a stone and a prophecy and a century of liars have chosen to tell us."

"You always do that."

"Do what?"

"Make it bearable." He got to his feet, grass-stained, shaking, furious at something larger than either of them. "Every horrible thing since we met, you find the version of it that lets me keep walking. Why?"

Because I was sent to deliver you, and I have started wanting instead to save you, and those two things cannot both be true, and one of them is going to destroy us.

"Because you walk better than you stand still," she said.

He almost smiled. Then he looked past her shoulder, east, toward the sunrise, toward the world beyond the ridge—the craters and the winged skeleton and the roads that ended midair that they'd glimpsed from the high ground in the night—and his smile died before it finished being born.

"Sera. If the stone marks the boundary. And the boundary is the Veil." He swallowed. "What happens when we cross?"

She had been avoiding that question since the well shaft. She was out of places to hide it.

"Nothing will happen to you," she said. "You're Ashgrove. Whatever the Veil does, it will know you."

"And to you?"

"I'm not Ashgrove." She shrugged, and hoped the shrug landed lighter than it felt. "Exiles cross back all the time. It stings. Like walking into cold water. Then you're through, and the world remembers what it's supposed to feel like, and—" She stopped.

Elodie was staring at her. At her hands, specifically. At the fine trembling that had started in her fingers the moment they'd touched the stone together, and hadn't stopped since.

"You've been feeling it all morning," he said. "Since you put your hand on it. Haven't you? The pulse. The pressure. What is it doing to you?"

"It's not doing anything to me. It's just—" She flexed her fingers. The tremor wouldn't obey. "It's loud. That's all. A working this old, this size, holding this much—I've never stood this close to the seal before. No exile has. We feel the valley from outside the way you feel a headache through a wall. But here, at the source—"

Here, at the source, it felt less like a wall and more like a dam. And dams, Sera knew, from a childhood spent in a country the war had drowned, did not fail gradually. They failed all at once.

"Elodie," she said. "Get your pack. We're crossing now. While the light's bad and the wardens are still searching the millpond."

"Why now?"

"Because I think the Veil knows you're standing on its doorstep." She looked up at the name cut sharp and bright above them, at the maintained letters, at the patient warmth radiating off stone that faced away from the sun. "I think it's been waiting a hundred years for an Ashgrove to come home. And I would very much like to be on the far side of that ridge before it decides what to do about it."

Elodie swung the pack onto his shoulders. His hand went to the prophecy book through the canvas, that unconscious touch, that check for wounds.

"One question," he said. "Before we go. Answer it honestly, Sera. You owe me that much."

"If I can."

"When we cross—" He looked at the stone, and then at her, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "If the Veil recognizes me. If I really am what the book says I am. Will I come out the other side the same person who walked in?"

Sera thought of Archon Vey's handwriting. Thought of the word *instrument*, written in that beautiful, patient script. Thought of everything she could not say.

"No," she said. "Nobody crosses a thing like that and stays the same. But that's true of every border, scribe. The only choice you ever get is which side of it you want to be changed *on*."

She held out her hand. After a moment, he took it—his grip ink-calloused and steady—and they walked together around the boundary stone, past the carved names, up the last few feet of the ridge to where the grass stopped obeying and the dead air of Vell thinned into something older, wilder, waiting.

Behind them, faint as a memory, the stone began—very softly—to hum.
