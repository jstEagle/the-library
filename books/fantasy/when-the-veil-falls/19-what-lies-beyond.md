## 19. What Lies Beyond

The boundary stone stood where Sera said it would, half-swallowed by a hillside of gorse, and up close I could see what the valley's fog had hidden from every traveler who'd ever passed this way.

It was carved. Not weathered into suggestion the way the temple's oldest monuments were—*carved*, deliberately, with letters cut deep enough to hold shadow even at dusk. Sera knelt and swept her palm across the face of it, clearing away a century of lichen, and the name emerged letter by letter like something surfacing from deep water.

**ASHGROVE.**

My family's name. My father's name, cut into stone at the edge of the world, on a monument no Ashgrove had ever been told existed.

"That's not possible," I said. My voice sounded very small against all that open sky.

"You keep saying that," Sera said quietly. "You said it at the well shaft. You said it when I told you about the Order. You said it when your own prophecy book wrote itself in front of you." She looked up at me, and in the last grey light before true dark, her eyes had that quality they got sometimes—the quality that made me forget she was an exile and remember only that she was the one person between me and everything else. "At some point you have to stop saying it and start believing it."

"I believe the book. The book is *here*." I touched my pack, where the prophecy volume sat wrapped in oilcloth like a wound I was carrying. "The book is proof. This is just—" I gestured at the hillside, the gorse, the fading light. "Stone. Someone with the same surname. Vell has maybe forty families. Names repeat."

"Elodie." She said my name the way she always did when she was about to hurt me on purpose because gentleness would be crueler. "There are no other valleys."

I knew what she meant. I had known since the night market, since I watched her cup a spark of light in her hands and carry it toward the valley's edge and feel it gutter and die like a moth in a closed fist. There were no other valleys. There was the Veil, and there was everything it had cut off, and the map in the temple's archive—which I had copied four times as penance exercises—showed Vell ringed by mountains on every side, and beyond the mountains, in the cartographer's careful hand: *Here be the Sundered Lands. Let no faithful soul pass.*

I had spent nineteen years believing those words meant danger.

Standing in front of the stone, I finally understood they meant shame.

"Whoever built the Veil built it to hide something," Sera said. She rose, brushing moss from her knees. "Your ancestors didn't flee here, Elodie. They *built* here. They built a prison with a congregation."

"Or they built a refuge," I said, but even as I said it, I heard how thin it was, how much it sounded like Brother Cassian's voice saying it, like every homily I'd ever copied out in my neat debt-bound hand: *The Veil protects us from the chaos beyond. Magic is the wound; the Veil is the bandage.* Nineteen years of scripture, and I could hear now, finally, how every line of it circled the same question without ever answering it.

Protects us from what?

Sera didn't argue. She just watched me work through it, patient as someone who had crossed this ground in her own mind years ago, and then she said, "We should cross tonight. The wardens won't give up, and Maren doesn't strike me as a woman who loses people twice."

"Maren isn't hunting us for the temple," I reminded her. "You heard what she said at the ford."

"I heard what she *claimed*. Inquisitors lie the way other people breathe." But Sera's mouth tightened, because we both knew the truth of it: Maren had found us at the river gorge with three wardens at her back and every reason to take us, and instead she had turned her horse aside and said, *Go north. Don't stop until the air changes,* and ridden back the way she came, and none of us had slept properly since.

"The air changes," I repeated now. It had become our shorthand for the boundary, the phrase Sera used because she couldn't explain what it felt like to someone whose whole life had been spent inside dead air. "What does it actually feel like? Crossing. Tell me honestly."

She considered the question with more care than I expected. That was Sera—all edges until suddenly she wasn't, and then you remembered why you'd followed her out of the only world you'd ever known.

"It hurts," she said. "Not the way a blow hurts. More the way—" She paused, frustrated, searching. "You know how when you've been sitting too long and your leg falls asleep, and then the blood comes back? Like that. Except the leg is everything. And it doesn't stop after a minute. It keeps coming back, over and over, for days. Weeks, if the stories are right. Your body has to learn that feeling exists again."

"And if it never learns?"

"Then you burn out." She said it plainly, without cruelty. "Some do. The Order calls them hollowborn. People who cross and can't hold what comes in." A beat, and then, softer: "That won't be you. I've felt it around you already—the way air moves near you. Wrong for this place. Right for the other side."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll bury you somewhere with a view," she said, "and I'll feel terrible about it for years," and that was so exactly, so perfectly Sera that I laughed—a startled, ragged sound—and the laugh broke something loose in my chest, some last knot of resistance, and I put my hand on the stone.

The carving under my palm was cold. Colder than stone had any right to be in early autumn, cold like the far side of a door, and beneath the cold I felt—I don't know how to write this, even now, even with everything I know—something *waiting*. A pressure, like water behind glass. Like the whole sky leaning its weight against the inside of my skin.

"Say goodbye to it," Sera said quietly.

I looked back down the slope. Below us, past the gorse and the grey rock, the valley of Vell lay in its bowl of mountains, lamplit and small and utterly familiar. Somewhere down there was the temple, its east wing still scorched from Maren's arrest of Cassian, its archive gutted by my own carelessness. Somewhere down there was Cassian in a cell, buying me days with lies he was better at telling than I had ever given him credit for. Somewhere down there was my mother's grave, and my father's quarry, and the ledger with my family's name in it, and the desk where I had copied ten thousand lines of scripture about a protection I now knew was a cage.

Every version of my life I had ever imagined ended in that valley. Every hope I'd had fit inside it like a coin in a purse.

"Goodbye," I said. It came out steady, which surprised me.

Then I stepped past the stone, and the world ended, and began.

*

They call it the Veil because from the outside it must look gentle—a shimmer, a heat-haze, a curtain of nothing. From the inside, crossing it, it does not look like anything. It feels like being unmade and remade in the space between two footsteps, and I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it accurately, but here is what I can say:

First, silence—not the valley's silence, which is an absence, a held breath, but a silence with texture, a silence like standing inside a bell that has just been struck. My ears rang with a sound below sound.

Second, pain. Sera had described it and she had undersold it. It came up through my feet like lightning slowed to the speed of blood, and it did not stop at my skin—it went *through* skin, through muscle, through bone, through whatever part of me had spent nineteen years starving without knowing it, and every part of me woke up screaming with the sheer animal shock of being fed.

Third—and this is the part I am ashamed of, the part I hid from Sera for the first mile—it was *glorious*.

I have tried to find a humble word for it. I have tried, in the weeks since, to make it sound like anything other than what it was. But the truth is that magic, when it entered me, felt like the first breath after nearly drowning, multiplied by every good thing I had ever felt and then squared. Color came back into the world in layers I hadn't known were missing—the gorse wasn't yellow, it was a hundred yellows, it was a chorus of yellows, each bloom singing slightly different note of gold. The wind had *weight*. The stars overhead weren't pinpricks; they were furnaces, ancient and enormous, and I could feel their light land on my skin like warm rain.

And underneath all of it, threading through it, something vast and slow and alive turning over in its sleep beneath the ground, and me lying against its flank like a child against a sleeping giant's chest, listening to a heartbeat the size of the horizon.

"Oh," I said. It was all I could manage. I may have fallen to my knees. I genuinely don't remember whether I chose to or not.

"There it is," Sera said, and there was something in her voice—not quite tenderness, not quite grief. Homesickness, maybe. Watching me feel what she'd been exiled from. "Breathe. Slowly. Fight the urge to grab at it—that's how hollowborn happen. You don't pull the magic in. You let it notice you and decide to stay."

"It noticed me," I gasped. "It's—everything is—"

"I know." She crouched beside me, and I saw her flex her fingers, and a flame no larger than a candlewick bloomed above her palm—casual, effortless, the most beautiful thing I had ever seen. Here, outside the Veil, her power came to her like a loyal dog. "Stay with me, Elodie. Eyes open. Breathe."

I stayed with her. I breathed. Slowly, the roar in my skull settled into something survivable, and the world resolved into shapes again: the hillside falling away northward into a long shallow plain, a road—an actual road, rutted and grass-grown but unmistakably a road—running along the base of the hills toward a darkness in the distance that might have been forest.

And ruins.

I saw the ruins next, once the wonder let go of me enough to let me see straight. They lined the road like broken teeth. A farmhouse with its roof caved in, walls blackened in patterns no ordinary fire makes—patterns that *spiral*, that reach, that climb. A watchtower snapped off at half height, its stones fused together in places, glassy where they should have been rough. A bridge over a dry streambed with one arch intact and two collapsed, the intact arch carved with figures I could not look at directly, not because they were frightening but because looking at them made the new magic in my chest flinch like a struck animal.

"What happened here?" I asked, though I knew. Of course I knew. I'd read the sanctioned history a hundred times: *the Sundering, the great war of mages, the age of fire from which the Veil mercifully shields us.* I had always assumed the histories exaggerated. Catastrophes shrink with distance; the temple taught that too.

Nothing had been exaggerated. If anything, the histories had been kind.

"The war," Sera said. "This was farmland, once. Some of the richest in the world, the old texts say. Now—" She shrugged. "A century later, and it still hasn't decided to grow back properly. Look at the soil."

I looked. In the starlight I could see it: the earth had a wrongness to it, a grey undertone, like ash worked too deep ever to wash out. Nothing taller than knee-height grew anywhere on the plain. No trees. No hedgerows. Just grass, sparse and sullen, and the bones of human works scattered across it.

"A hundred years," I said. "One century, and it looks like this?"

"High magic leaves scars that don't heal on any schedule mortals understand." Sera started down the hill, and I followed, walking carefully because my legs kept wanting to buckle, not from weakness but from abundance—every step brought new sensation, new input, the ground itself murmuring against my boots. "The Order's territory is worse, if you can believe it. The heart of the fighting. There are places where nothing grows at all. Places where the air still tastes burnt." She glanced back at me. "This is why the Veil matters, Elodie. Understand that before we go any further. The people who built it weren't cowards and they weren't liars—not exactly. They looked at *this* and they did the arithmetic, and they decided that a valley without magic was better than a world on fire. Twelve families. Twelve names on the ritual. Yours among them."

"And the cost?"

"The cost was everyone outside it." Her voice stayed level. Too level. "The outer lands lost their magic slowly—the Veil drinks it, pulls it inward, hoards it. Fields that needed charms to stay fertile just... failed. Children born with the talent found it starved out of them by the time they were seven. Whole traditions, gone in two generations. And the ones who could still work power—" her jaw tightened "—the ones like me became valuable. Then hunted. Then organized. You don't get an order like mine in a healthy world. You get it in a dying one, where people are desperate enough to worship anyone who promises the flow will come back."

I thought about that as we walked. The road took us past the ruined farmhouse, close enough to touch, and I did touch it—laid my hand against the fused, glassy stone, and the moment I did the magic in me surged up *eagerly*, hungry to answer, and for one vertiginous instant I felt echoes in the wall. Impressions. Heat and terror and a voice crying out words in a language I almost understood, and then—

I snatched my hand back. My heart slammed. "The stones remember."

Sera stopped. Turned. Stared at me. "What did you just say?"

"The stones—they hold something. When I touched them I felt—" I wrapped my arms around myself, suddenly cold despite the warmth flooding me. "Is that normal? Is that a mage thing?"

"No." Sera's voice had changed. Careful. Measuring. "That is not a mage thing. That is a *Veilbearer* thing, maybe, or—" She shook her head. "Don't do that again. Not until we're behind the Order's walls. Things that remember can also *notice*, and things that notice can be noticed by."

"By what?"

"Later." But she moved closer to me after that, walked at my shoulder instead of ahead, and scanned the dark more often, and I understood that I had just learned something about myself that frightened even her.

*

We walked most of the night. The plain gave way gradually to broken hills, and the hills to a ridge, and from the ridge at grey dawn I saw the Order's country for the first time: a fortress carved into a mountain's shoulder, black stone veined with lines that glowed faintly, faintly, like embers under banked ash. Roads converged on it from every direction. Even ruined, even a century dead, the land around it had the look of a place people still feared and obeyed.

And I felt it before Sera pointed it out—a current in the air, drawing everything toward that mountain. Toward the mountain, and specifically toward me, or toward what I carried. The pack with the prophecy book in it had grown warm against my back somewhere in the night's walking, and now it pulsed gently, in time, I realized, with my own heartbeat.

"It knows where home is," Sera said, following my gaze. "The book. Or it knows where it's going. The Order has wanted it back for forty years."

"Forty years," I said. "Since the last candidate."

"Yes." She didn't elaborate. She had refused to elaborate the entire journey, and I was too exhausted, too full of newness, to force it now. But the word *candidate* had begun to acquire a taste in my mouth, and the taste was ash.

We descended toward the fortress as the sun rose, and the rising light did something I wasn't prepared for: it showed me my own hands.

They were glowing. Faintly—from within, under the skin, a soft gold luminance like lantern-light through paper. Along my forearms, faint traceries had appeared, delicate as frost ferns, following the lines of my veins. I stared at them, and as I stared, the traceries brightened slightly, responding to my attention, and the sensation that accompanied the brightening was—warm. Welcoming. The magic liked being seen. It pressed up against the surface of me like a cat demanding acknowledgment.

"Sera," I said. My voice came out strange. "Look at my hands."

She looked. And then she did something I had never seen her do in all our weeks together, through wardens and rivers and burned temples and the death of every certainty I owned.

She stepped back from me.

Only a half-step. Only for a heartbeat. Then she caught herself and closed the distance again and made her face smooth, but I had seen it, and we both knew I had seen it.

"That's fast," she said. "That's very fast. Most takes weeks to show outward signs."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Everything about you is dangerous, Elodie. That was true before you crossed the Veil." She reached out and took my glowing wrist, turned it over, examined the traceries with a scholar's frown rather than a friend's concern. "The Order will want to see this immediately. They'll have questions. So will Archon Vey." Something flickered across her face at his name—there and gone, too quick to read. "When we arrive, let me speak first. Answer what you're asked, but let me frame things. There are politics inside those walls that you can't see yet, and the shape of your first day will matter for a long time."

"I thought I was the prophesied guest of honor."

"You're the prophesied *instrument*," Sera said. "Guests get rooms. Instruments get used. The difference is entirely a matter of who's doing the framing." She released my wrist and nodded at the fortress, closer now, its ember-veins brighter in the dawn. "So we frame carefully."

We walked the last miles mostly in silence. My body sang the whole way—that was the terrible, wonderful truth of it. Even exhausted, even grieving, even walking through the grave of a murdered landscape with a liar's mission coiled in my pack, some traitorous part of me was *happy*. For nineteen years I had been a cup left out in the desert, and now the rain had come, and the rain did not care what I had lost to get here. It filled me because filling was what it did.

I understood, walking those last miles, why people killed for this. Why tyrants had risen. Why twelve families had concluded that the only safe amount of magic in the world was none, because they had seen what men became when they held infinity in their chests and were told it belonged to them.

I understood, and I loved it anyway, and that frightened me more than Maren had, more than the prophecy had, more than anything.

The gates of the Ashen fortress opened before we reached them—as if they'd known, as if they'd felt me coming for hours, the way I was beginning to feel everything. Figures waited in the gateway: robed, still, backlit by the ember-light of the walls. At their center stood a man in grey and silver whom I somehow identified as the leader before Sera confirmed it, because the current in the air bent around him the way water bends around a large stone in a stream.

Archon Vey. He was smiling before I could see his face clearly. I could feel the smile the way you feel sun on closed eyelids—warm, total, and utterly without information about what lay behind it.

"Welcome," he called across the intervening ground, in a voice pitched to carry and crafted to comfort, "to the Ashen Order, Veilbearer. We have waited a hundred years for you. Come inside. There is so much you need to learn."

Beside me, Sera murmured, barely moving her lips: "Let me speak first."

And the gates closed behind us with a sound like a book shutting.
