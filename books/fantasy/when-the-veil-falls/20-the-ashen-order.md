## 20. The Ashen Order

The last mile to the Ashen Hall, Sera walked half a step behind Elodie and watched the way you watch a thing you're afraid of dropping.

He was still reeling from the boundary crossing—anyone would be. He'd vomited twice on the road, wept once without seeming to know he was doing it, and now walked in a strange, floating quiet, turning his hands over and over like they belonged to someone else. Magic had gotten into him out there at the stone, and it hadn't let go. She could see it sitting under his skin, a faint wrongness in the air around him, the way heat shimmered above a fire even after the flames were banked.

She'd felt it herself, that first crossing, eleven years ago. She remembered thinking: *so this is what I am. This is what was taken from everyone inside.*

Elodie didn't know yet that what had been taken from him was worse than magic.

"Tell me about them," he said, not looking at her. "Your Order."

"They'll want to help you."

"That's not what I asked."

Sera kept her eyes on the road. The Ashfell plateau rose ahead of them, black rock streaked with old burn scars from the Sundering, and the Hall sat on its shoulder like a blade left in a wound—tall, narrow, built of fused glass-stone that the first mages had made by melting the battlefield itself. From a distance it looked almost beautiful. Up close you could see the scorch marks no one had ever bothered to smooth away.

"The Ashen Order keeps what's left of magic alive beyond the Veil," she said. "We train. We preserve. We wait for the prophecy to open the valley again." A pause. "You're the waiting's over."

"And Vey?"

"Archon Vey has led us for thirty years. He found me when I was nine, begging in a border town. Fed me. Taught me everything I can do." She heard how it sounded before she finished saying it. "He's not a soft man. But he believes in the Veilbearer. He's waited his whole life for you."

*So have I,* she almost said, and didn't.

Elodie finally looked at her, and his eyes were still red-rimmed from the crossing, still wide with everything he'd swallowed in the last three days—the archive, the well shaft, Cassian's arrest, Maren's voice reading his name off a page like a death sentence. But underneath all of it there was something steady. A scribe's patience. A man who had spent his life copying other people's words and had just learned that his own name was written somewhere he never agreed to sign.

"If your Archon has waited his whole life for me," Elodie said quietly, "then I'm not a guest. I'm an answer. And answers get used, Sera. You told me yourself the Order wanted me *found*. Not saved. Found."

Her chest did something painful and complicated.

"I also told you I'd get you out of Vell," she said. "That part was true."

"It was," he agreed. "And I'm grateful. And I'm going to walk through that gate anyway, because I don't have anywhere else in the world to go." He turned back toward the Hall. "But I'd like you to remember, later, that I knew. That I walked in with my eyes open."

*Sera, he's going to walk in with his eyes open.* She thought about telling him then. About the retrieval order. About the words Vey had used when he'd sent her south—*bring me the Veilbearer, intact and willing; if willingness fails, bring him anyway.* She thought about how Elodie had refused her offer at the market, how he'd chosen his debt and his valley over freedom, and how the only reason he was here now was that the temple had made staying impossible.

She thought about the way he'd caught her hand at the boundary stone without being asked, when the magic hit him, and held on like she was the only solid thing in a dissolving world.

"Remember it yourself," she said. "I'll be beside you. Whatever this is, whatever they want—I'll be beside you."

It wasn't the truth. It was less than the truth. She gave it to him anyway, because it was the largest piece of truth she could carry without both of them falling down.

The gates of the Ashen Hall were open before they reached them.

* * *

They stood forty feet tall, black iron forged around cores of something that glowed faintly amber, and Sera watched Elodie stop dead in front of them, staring.

"What is that light?"

"Emberstone. War-era work. There hasn't been a forge in the world that could make these since the Sundering." She hesitated, then gave him the piece she always gave new arrivals, the piece that broke most of them. "There used to be thousands of gates like this. Roads between cities made of light. Rivers that carried boats uphill. The war burned most of it, and the Veil cut off what survived from everything it fed on. What the Order guards isn't power, Elodie. It's leftovers. We are living in the ruins of a civilization that could do things nobody alive remembers how to do."

He touched the gate, very lightly, the way he touched the old books in the archive—with reverence and fear in equal measure. And where his fingers met the iron, the ember-glow brightened.

Sera felt it happen. Felt the gate *notice* him.

Around them, the two sentries on the wall went rigid. One of them whispered something to the other, and the whisper ran down the stairs and into the courtyard faster than a person could run, and by the time Sera led Elodie through the gate, the Ashen Order had come out to meet them.

Not soldiers. Worse. Believers.

They lined the courtyard in their ash-grey robes, maybe two hundred of them, silent, and every single face was turned toward Elodie with an intensity that Sera had seen before only at funerals and ordinations. An old woman near the front had tears running down her cheeks. A boy of perhaps twelve was gripping his mother's sleeve so hard she winced.

"Sera," Elodie murmured, not moving. "What is this."

"You're the prophecy," she said. "They've been reciting it since before I was born. *When the Veil thins, the Veilbearer comes, marked by the dead air, bearing the book.*" She swallowed. "You're the first thing some of these people have ever hoped for."

"I haven't done anything."

"You exist. Today, that's enough."

And then the crowd parted, and Archon Vey came through it.

Sera had forgotten, in the weeks away, exactly what Vey's presence did to a space. He was not tall. He was not broad. He was a spare, silver-haired man in plain charcoal robes, moving slowly, leaning lightly on a staff of grey wood—and every step he took seemed to rearrange the air around him, the way a large stone rearranges a stream. The crowd bowed as he passed. Not knelt. Bowed, all together, like grass under wind, and the sound of two hundred bodies bending was somehow louder than shouting.

He stopped ten feet from Elodie and looked at him for a long, long moment.

Then he smiled, and it was the smile Sera had trusted since she was nine years old, warm and crinkled and utterly genuine, and it terrified her more than any expression she had ever seen on him.

"There he is," Vey said softly. "A hundred years, and the world remembers how to hope."

He extended one hand—not commanding, *offering*, palm up, the gesture of a man asking rather than taking—and waited. Sera watched Elodie look at the hand, look at the crowd, look at her. She nodded, barely.

Elodie took it.

The moment skin met skin, every ember-lamp in the courtyard flared at once. Two hundred people drew a single breath. Somewhere in the ranks a woman began to weep openly, and the sound spread like fire through dry grass until half the Order was crying and none of them were making any attempt to hide it.

Vey didn't flinch at the flare. He held Elodie's hand and studied him with those pale, patient eyes, and said, quietly enough that only the three of them could hear:

"Do you know what they buried under your temple, boy? Do you know what your ancestors stole from the world?" His thumb pressed, briefly, painfully, against Elodie's wrist. "No. Of course you don't. They made sure of that. A century of lies, wrapped around the greatest crime ever committed." He released the hand. The lamps dimmed. "Well. It ends now. You have my word."

"Archon." Elodie's voice was rough. "I came because I had nowhere else. I'm grateful. But I'm not—whatever they think I am. I'm a scribe. I copy things. I'd like to understand them before anyone asks me to carry them."

Something flickered across Vey's face—there and gone, fast, but Sera had spent thirty years learning to read that face, and she caught it. Not disappointment. *Assessment.* A merchant weighing goods.

"A reader," Vey said warmly. "Good. The last one who came to us wanted only to be told what to do, and look what that got us." He gestured, gracious as a host, toward the Hall doors. "Rest tonight. Eat. Tomorrow, we begin. The world outside your valley is dying, Elodie Ashgrove—magic is bleeding out of it year by year, like blood from a wound that won't close, and the wound has your family's name on it. You deserve to see that for yourself. All of it. Every scar." The smile again, wider. "We hide nothing here."

*We hide nothing here.*

Sera stood very still while the crowd surged forward to touch Elodie's sleeves, his hands, the hem of his travel cloak, murmuring *Veilbearer* like a prayer, like a brand. She watched a dozen hands pull him gently toward the doors, toward warmth and food and a bed, and she saw the exact moment he stopped resisting—not because he trusted them, but because he was too tired and too polite and too alone.

She started to follow.

"Sera."

Vey's hand rested on her shoulder. Light as a bird. Heavy as a mountain.

"You did well," he said. "Better than well. Intact, and willing." His eyes stayed on the doors where Elodie had vanished, on the crowd swallowing him. "Willingness is such a fragile thing, though. It needs tending. From tonight, you guard him. You sleep outside his door. You are his shadow, his shield, and his conscience—everything he hears, everything he doubts, I want to flow through you." At last he looked at her, and the warmth in his face was unchanged, which was the worst part. It was always unchanged. "Do you understand your charge?"

"Yes, Archon."

"He must come to love it here," Vey said. "Or come to need us. Either will serve." A gentle squeeze of her shoulder. "The boy is the key, Sera. Keys don't get to choose their locks. Make sure he understands that soon. Gently. In your own time."

He patted her cheek, almost fondly, and walked away into his bowing crowd, leaning on his grey staff, a small silver-haired man who had fed a starving nine-year-old and never once, in thirty years, given her a reason to distrust him—

—who had just, in eleven sentences, ordered her to help him use a frightened scribe as a tool, and known, absolutely known, that she would say yes. Because she always said yes. Because he had built her out of obedience the way the first mages had built the gates out of iron and emberstone, and neither of them had ever been asked whether they wanted to be what they were made of.

Sera stood alone in the emptying courtyard, under the flaring gate-lamps, and listened to the Hall breathing around her, full of believers, full of warmth, full of nothing hidden except everything.

Then she went up the stairs to keep her charge—to sit outside the door of a man she had lied to, guarding him for the man who owned her, and to spend the long dark hours trying to remember at exactly which point rescue had become capture, and whose capture it actually was.

Through the door, faintly, she heard Elodie turn over in his sleep, and murmur a name she couldn't catch.

It sounded, she thought, like *Cassian.*

Behind her, at the end of the corridor, an ember-lamp guttered and steadied, and in the morning there would be lessons, and fire, and worship, and the beginning of the rest of the prophecy—but that night there was only the watching, and the door between them, and the slow cold settling in her stomach of a question she was no longer able to unask:

*If I open that door and tell him everything right now, will he forgive me—or will I just be the second person in three days to teach him that everyone he trusts was sent?*

Her hand rose to the latch. Stopped.

Outside, far to the south, past the scarred plateau and the dying plains and the standing stone with the Ashgrove name carved in it, something in the sealed valley shifted in its hundred-year sleep—and in the Hall, every ember-lamp on every wall turned, very slightly, toward the sleeping room where the Veilbearer dreamed.
