## 21. Lessons in Fire

The first thing the Ashen Order taught me was that everything I knew about fire was wrong.

I had spent my life in Vell, where fire was a thing of hearths and forges—wood, air, heat, a chemistry any child could understand. On my first morning in the Ashen Hall, Archon Vey's instructors led me to a courtyard of black stone at dawn and told me to make a flame from nothing.

"There is no nothing," said my instructor, a gaunt woman named Kestrel whose left arm ended at the elbow in a knot of old scarring. "There is only distance. The magic is out there, scribe. It has been waiting a hundred years for someone with the blood to call it home."

I raised my hand because they expected it, and because some traitor part of me wanted it more than I wanted breath.

Nothing happened.

"Again," Kestrel said.

Nothing.

"Again."

On the eleventh try—or the fiftieth; I had stopped counting—a warmth gathered behind my sternum like a swallowed coal, slid down my arm, and bloomed at my fingertips: a flame no bigger than a candle's, trembling, blue-white at its heart.

I remember thinking two things in the same instant. The first was *it's real*. The second was *it knows my name*.

Because that was the truth I couldn't say aloud, not to Kestrel, not to Sera, not to the acolytes who watched from the colonnade with their hands pressed together as if in prayer. The magic didn't come when I called it. It came when it recognized me. It moved through my body the way water finds an old channel—eagerly, gratefully, as though it had been waiting a century for the Ashgrove line to open the gate again.

Kestrel saw the flame and did not smile. She stepped closer and studied my face with the flat attention of someone reading a ledger.

"Good," she said. "Now put it out."

"How?"

"You don't put it out. You take it back. It is not a guest. It is yours. Command it or it will command you." She held up her scarred stump. "I commanded poorly once. The Order rebuilt what it could."

I closed my fist and the flame died—but the warmth stayed, coiled under my skin like something sleeping, and I felt it settle deeper into me than before.

That was the first lesson. There were ninety more before the month turned.

* * *

They trained me in the mornings on the black stone courtyard, in the afternoons in a vaulted chamber they called the Reservoir, where the walls were lined with shelves of gray crystals that hummed when I entered.

"The stored work of the dead," Sera explained, the first time she brought me there. She had become my shadow since we fled the Ashen Hall's outer gates—the Order's courtesy to their Veilbearer, or their leash. "Every mage who died in the Sundering left something behind. What they could carry, they carried here. What they couldn't—" she touched one crystal, and it dimmed briefly under her fingers, like a candle guttering, "—they left in the world. In ruins. In bones. In places you'd rather not sleep."

"And mine can read them?"

"Yours can *drink* them. That's why you're here." Her voice was carefully neutral. She had been careful with her voice ever since the night I'd caught her watching me train with an expression I couldn't name—pride, maybe, or grief, or the arithmetic of a woman counting how many uses a person could be put to. "A crystal holds a working. You can pour it into yourself and make it your own. Most mages need years to build a single working of their own. You could have forty by winter."

"Is that what the Archon wants?"

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

I learned to drink the dead. It felt like swallowing cold sunlight. Each crystal gave me something different—one taught my hands to knit torn flesh; another showed me how to still the air so completely that sound itself lay down and slept; a third opened a door in my mind onto darkness, and Kestrel slammed it shut before I could see what waited inside, saying only, *not yet, never without a second mage beside you.*

I was fast. I knew I was fast. Within three weeks I could do things that made hardened veterans of the Order go quiet and touch their talismans.

And every time they looked at me that way—with awe, with hunger, with the particular reverence people reserve for things they intend to use—I thought of the temple archive in Vell, and the way Brother Aldous used to handle the reliquaries. He had kissed the glass cases before opening them. He had bowed his head over bones.

Reverence, I was learning, is just hunger wearing its good clothes.

* * *

The worship began small, the way rot begins small, in a place you'll never think to check until the beam gives way.

It started with the acolytes. Young ones mostly, initiates who had crossed the Veil as children and known nothing but ash and scarcity their whole lives. They began appearing wherever I trained—on the colonnade benches, at the courtyard's edge, standing in the rain once, a dozen of them, watching me sweat through drills while the water ran off their upturned faces.

Then the gifts appeared outside my door. A carved bird. A ribbon of woven silver. A loaf of bread with a sun worked into the crust. Once, a child's shoe, worn through at the heel, placed deliberately at my threshold—and I understood that somewhere in this fortress of exiles, a mother had decided her family owed my family a debt a century old.

"They're calling you the Dawn-Bringer now," Sera told me, leaning against the wall of my cell while I tried to mend a strap on my pack. My hands shook. They had shaken for days. "It's spreading through the lower halls. The Veilbearer who will give back the sun."

"I haven't given anyone anything."

"You exist. For them, existing is enough." She watched me work. "In the outer settlements, they tell the story differently than the Order does. They say the twelve families didn't seal the Veil to imprison a tyrant—they say the twelve stole the world's magic out of greed, and the tyrant was the one who tried to stop them."

"That's not what the archives say."

"The archives were written by the eleven." She shrugged. "You've read the prophecy book. You know stories get written by whoever survives to hold the pen."

I set down the strap. "Sera. Why are you telling me this?"

For a moment something honest surfaced in her face—something tired and young and afraid. Then it sank again, and she pushed off the wall.

"Because you should know what you're becoming before everyone else decides it for you," she said, and left.

* * *

Archon Vey summoned me to the spire on the thirtieth day.

His chamber sat at the top of the Ashen Hall, a round room with windows on all sides, and I noticed—as I always noticed around Vey—that the light in it was better than the light anywhere else in the fortress, though there was no reason for it. Magic, I was learning, was largely a technology of attention. Vey simply paid attention to himself so relentlessly that the world complied.

"You've surpassed Kestrel's projections," he said. He stood at the window with his back half-turned, watching the ash plains roll away toward the horizon. "Three weeks to workings that took our best adepts three years. Do you know why?"

"My blood."

"Your blood, yes. But also—" he turned, and his smile was the warm one, the one that made entire rooms believe him, "—your *want*. I have watched you train, Elodie. I have watched you fail a hundred times and reach for the hundred-and-first. The last Veilbearer candidate had power but no appetite. Power without appetite is a library nobody reads. You—" he crossed the room and rested both hands on my shoulders, and his grip was gentle, paternal, exactly the weight of Brother Cassian's hands on the morning he'd walked me to my first day in the scriptorium, "—you are a reader."

Something in my chest flinched away from those hands. I let him keep them there anyway.

"The Order has prepared a place for you," Vey continued. "A role. When the Veil falls—and it will fall, Elodie, within the year, the ritual work is nearly complete—the valley will need a guardian. Not a conqueror. A shepherd. Someone the people of Vell already trust. Someone with their faces and their faith." His eyes held mine. "Someone with the right name."

"The right name being Ashgrove."

"The right name being *yours*."

I should have felt honored. Some part of me did—a part I despised, a part that had spent twenty-three years copying scripture for men who called me *boy* and *scribe* and *the debtor's son*, and that part drank Vey's words like I drank the gray crystals, hungrily, gratefully, feeling myself grow larger.

But another part of me—the part that had spent twenty-three years reading other people's records, other people's histories, other people's lies—heard the sentence underneath the sentence.

*A shepherd.* Shepherds do not ask sheep for consent.

"What happens to the people who disagree?" I asked.

Vey's smile didn't waver, which was somehow worse than if it had.

"People rarely disagree with a miracle," he said. "Rest well, Veilbearer. Tomorrow you begin the deep workings. We'll want you strong for what comes."

At the door, I paused. "Archon. The last Veilbearer candidate. The one without appetite. What happened to him?"

The light in the room seemed, very slightly, to dim—or perhaps that was only Vey's attention shifting elsewhere, taking its warmth with it.

"He asked too few questions," Vey said pleasantly. "Learn from him."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep. I climbed instead to the rampart above the eastern gate, where the Order kept no watch—it was considered bad luck, or bad sense, to stand facing the Veil—and I looked south toward the mountain wall that hid my valley from the world.

Somewhere beyond it, Cassian was still in a cell, or dead, or walking free on a lie he'd sold to buy me time. Somewhere beyond it, Maren was hunting, or helping, or bleeding. Somewhere beyond it, the temple stood over a secret I was only beginning to guess at, and beneath the secret, whatever else the secrets were stacked on.

And here, on this side of the stone, I flexed my hand, and a flame rose from my palm without effort now—blue-white, obedient, *mine*, except that the longer I held it the less sure I became about the direction of ownership. Did I command the fire, or did the fire, patient and hungry, command me? Was I learning magic, or was magic learning the shape of the vessel it intended to fill?

"Beautiful, isn't it," said a voice behind me.

One of the acolytes—young, fifteen perhaps, the boy who had left the bread with the sun baked into it. He knelt on the cold stone without asking, and looked up at my flame with naked devotion.

"When you bring the dawn," he said, "my mother says we'll finally be forgiven. For surviving."

I looked at him—at the hollows in his cheeks, at the ash dust ground permanently into the creases of his knuckles, at the absolute certainty in his eyes—and I felt the full weight of what Vey was building settle onto my shoulders like a yoke fitted to my exact measurements.

"I'm going to sleep," I said quietly, and closed my hand, and walked away, and hated how much the boy's whispered *bless you, Dawn-Bringer* followed me down the stairs like something that had gotten under my skin and meant to stay.

* * *

Two nights later, Sera woke me past midnight with a hand over my mouth and her eyes wide in the dark.

"Get dressed," she breathed. "The archive wing. Now. And Elodie—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, and what I heard in it frightened me more than shouting would have, "—bring the prophecy book. I found out what the Archon's really keeping in there. And why he needs *you* to open it."

Down the corridor, faint and rhythmic, I could hear the acolytes in the night chapel, chanting my new name.
