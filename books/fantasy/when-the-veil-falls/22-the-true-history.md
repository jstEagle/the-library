## 22. The True History

They gave me a room with a window.

I didn't understand the gift until my third morning in the Ashen Hall, when I realized every other door on the corridor had been bricked over. My window looked out onto a courtyard where acolytes practiced forms I had no names for—hands tracing shapes in the air and the air *obeying*—and I understood that the window wasn't a kindness. It was a leash made of light. As long as I could see what waited for me if I cooperated, I would cooperate.

Sera called it an education. She stood in my doorway each morning with her arms crossed and her face arranged into something between apology and duty, and she walked me down through the Hall's bones to the archive beneath it.

"The Archon says you should learn," she said.

"The Archon says."

"He says a lot of things." A flicker of something crossed her face—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite—and was gone. "Most of them are even true."

* * *

The archive under the Ashen Hall was nothing like the temple archive in Vell.

Ours had been a place of hush and dust, of ledgers and liturgy, everything arranged to make you feel that history was finished—a closed book you were permitted to copy but never to question. This place felt *alive*. The shelves breathed warmth. Lamps burned without oil in niches cut into the living rock, and when I passed them they brightened slightly, the way a dog lifts its head when someone familiar walks by.

"They're keyed to blood," Sera said, watching me flinch from one. "Old wards. They know you're not one of us yet." She said *yet* the way the Archon said it: like a promise, like a sentence already written.

"Is there anything in here about Vell?" I asked. "About the founding?"

"There's everything in here about the founding." She stopped at a shelf near the archive's heart, where the books grew older and stranger—covers of grey leather, spines stitched with wire instead of thread. "That's the problem. There are forty accounts, and no two agree, because everyone who wrote one was trying to make themselves the hero of it."

She pulled down three volumes and set them in my arms. They were heavy the way stones are heavy, with a weight that seemed to have opinions.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "Or as close to it as anyone got."

* * *

I read for two days.

I read the way I'd learned to read in the temple archive—fast, cross-referencing, building contradictions into a lattice and looking for the shape of truth behind it. It was the only skill my indenture had given me besides a scribe's hand, and it turned out to be the skill this room feared most, though nobody in the Ashen Hall seemed to realize it yet. The temple had taught me to find lies in holy text. The Order had given me forty holy texts.

Here is what the lattice showed me.

There had been a war. That much every account agreed on, the way all maps agree there is an ocean even when they disagree about everything in it. Before the Veil, magic had not lived in far-off places or rare bloodlines. It had been everywhere, the way air is everywhere—in the soil and the rain, in the bodies of ordinary people, waking in children who had done nothing to earn it and could do nothing to refuse it. One account described a shepherd girl who sang storms out of a clear sky. Another described a city where the streetlamps were fireflies the size of fists, bred by a guild that had gone extinct so thoroughly that no record of its name survived except in a tax ledger.

And threaded through every account, growing larger as the pages turned closer to the end, was a name that most of the writers refused to write.

They called him the Tyrant. They called him the Unmaker. One chronicler, writing in a hand that shook—I checked the ink flow, the tremor was in every stroke, this was not style, this was fear—called him *the brother*, and then struck it through, and then wrote around the space where the name had been for eleven more pages, like a man walking in a wide circle around a well.

He had not started as a tyrant. That was the first thing the Order's own books admitted, in the ones they'd shelved deepest, and I wondered if Sera knew they were there or whether I'd found them by the accident of a lamp brightening as I passed. He had been a unifier. In the war-torn centuries before his rise—the Sundering Wars, when mage-lords had carved the world into fiefdoms and burned whole valleys for grain—he had gathered the scattered gifts into one hand and used them, at first, to stop the burning.

Then the burning changed hands. That was all.

One account put it plainly, in the flat voice of a clerk who has stopped believing in adjectives: *He did not wish to rule the world. He wished to correct it. The distinction ceased to matter around the year he razed Coriath.* Coriath. I found it on none of the surviving maps. Whatever Coriath had been, correcting it had meant erasing it.

The Sundering came after. Every source agreed on the word and almost nothing else. The world had broken—magic itself had fractured, run wild, turned on its bearers. Plagues of light. Rivers that flowed uphill into the sky and never came down. Children born with power enough to level towns and judgment enough to use it. The Tyrant's own works, some claimed; others said the world simply broke under the weight of what men like him poured into it, the way a bridge breaks under a weight it was never built to hold, no matter whose carts are crossing.

Into that breaking, twelve families stepped forward.

* * *

The ritual was called the Sealing, and it was the closest thing the old world had produced to a miracle, which is another way of saying it was the closest thing to an unforgivable act.

Twelve families. Twelve bloodlines, each carrying a gift strong enough to matter. They met—at a place the books named only as *the valley*, and I felt the floor tilt under me when I read it, because there was only one valley left in the world that fit, and I had grown up in it, copying scripture about how blessed we were to live somewhere clean.

They built the Veil in nine days. Or thirty. Or a single night, according to the account written by someone claiming to have been there, which also claimed the moon stood still and the dead spoke approval, and which I therefore weighed accordingly. But the shape of it held constant across every telling, and the shape was this:

They took the world's magic—all of it, not the Tyrant's share, not the wild magic, *all of it*—and pulled it through a gate, and shut the gate, and locked the Tyrant inside with it. Not killed. Killing him had been tried, apparently, more than once, by coalitions that no longer existed because he had outlived them. So instead: imprisonment. The valley became the lock. The Veil became the door. And the twelve families became the keyhole—bound to it, sworn to it, their blood the signature on the warrant, generation after generation, standing guard over a prison the rest of the world was never told existed.

Because that was the second thing, the thing the Order's official histories circled and the deep-shelf books stated flatly:

The rest of the world hadn't agreed.

The Sealing hadn't been a council of nations. It had been twelve families deciding for everyone alive and everyone unborn that magic would die so that one man could be caged. Outside the Veil, the gifts guttered and went dark within a generation. Whole cultures ended—not conquered, just *extinguished*, their firefly lamps going cold, their storm-singing girls born silent. The outer world had spent a hundred years learning to live without the blood in its veins, and it hated the twelve families for it, and it worshipped them for it, and the Ashen Order had been founded—this Sera confirmed later, reluctantly—to hold both feelings at once.

"We call them the Twelve," she said, when I asked. "We keep their names. We teach their descendants' obligations." Her jaw worked. "And we've spent a century looking for a way to undo what they did, because half the world is dying slowly without magic, and the other half forgot there was ever anything to miss."

"And the valley?"

"The valley remembers nothing." She said it without accusation, which was worse. "Your temple teaches that magic is sin and the Veil is mercy. You're the only people in the world who got to keep the story where your ancestors were the heroes."

* * *

I went back to the shelf that night, alone, after the lamps had dimmed to embers of attention. I told myself it was scholarship. I told myself a lot of things that week.

The twelfth account was different from the others. Older binding, wire stitching gone brown with rust. No author's name. It was written as testimony—first person, present tense, the voice of someone setting down events while the blood was still wet—and it covered the nine days of the Sealing from the inside.

It described the twelve gathering in the valley's heart, above a place of stone they had prepared. It described the working itself in terms I couldn't follow and didn't need to: anchors and gates, names spoken backward, blood given willingly to the circle because unwilling blood makes brittle locks. Eleven names it listed openly, and I read them one by one, strangers all—Vessik, Corane, Ilvane, Maren's own line, I realized with a jolt, the name Maren wore was on this list, her family had helped build the cage and then, generations later, sent her in through the front door hunting heresy—

And then the twelfth name.

The ink was faded there. Not damaged—faded, deliberately, rubbed by a thumb or a cloth until only the ghost of the letters remained, though whoever had done the fading had lacked the heart or the authority to finish. Enough remained to read it.

Ashgrove.

I sat down on the archive floor. The lamp in the niche beside me brightened, warm and attentive, keyed to a bloodline it recognized, and I understood suddenly why the wardens at the boundary had let us pass so easily, why the Archon had received me with such certainty, why the word *yet* kept appearing in sentences about me.

My family had built the Veil.

Not served it. Not guarded it from within, humble and debt-bound, copying scripture to pay off a quarry debt. *Built* it. Signed it. Given blood to the lock so the lock would know its makers forever. And the temple—the temple my family had served for a hundred years, the institution that owned my grandfather's grief and my father's death and my whole life—the temple had been built on top of the prison door by the very families who forged it, staffed by their descendants, teaching their children that curiosity was sin so that no Ashgrove child would ever dig beneath the floor and ask what, exactly, we were guarding.

The ledger debt. The indenture. The quarry accident that killed my father and the temple's generous conversion of grief into servitude. I had spent my life believing I'd been unlucky. Sitting on the cold archive floor with a dead woman's testimony open in my lap, I understood for the first time that it might have been policy. That the temple might keep its founder's blood close the way a jailer keeps the keys close—poor, indebted, watched, and utterly convinced that leaving would be unthinkable.

The past reassembled itself around me, piece by piece, and every piece fit, and every piece indicted someone. The Tyrant, who had wanted to correct the world and broken it instead. The twelve, who had saved the world by stealing from it. The outer mages, who had suffered for a crime committed in their grandparents' grandparents' time and now wanted restitution from people who didn't know they owed it. The temple, which had fed on its own congregation's ignorance for a century. And my ancestors—my own blood—who had signed the lock and then hidden the signature, buried it under liturgy and ledgers, so that their descendants would serve the prison without ever being told there was a prisoner.

Everyone had lied. Everyone had lied *well*, for *generations*, with *institutional support*.

Except, I noticed, with a small cold shock, one party.

Every account agreed on one detail, and it was the detail no one had any reason to lie about, because it reflected badly on absolutely everyone: the Tyrant had not been killed because killing him had failed. And nowhere, in forty books and a dead witness's testimony, did anyone say what had become of his body.

Prisons, in my experience as a scribe who had catalogued a century of temple records, always had two features: a lock, and a thing kept behind it.

The Veil was the lock.

I sat in the lamplight, my family's name fading on the page in front of me, and let myself think the thought all the way to its edge for the first time. If the twelve had sealed him rather than killed him—if the valley was the lock and the Veil was the door—

Then somewhere beneath the temple in Vell, beneath the archive where I'd found my own name in a prophecy, beneath the floor I had walked across every day of my indentured life, something was still alive.

And tomorrow, the Archon had summoned me to his tower, saying only that it was time I learned *what my blood was actually for*.
