## 25. Sera's Divided Heart

The Ashen Hall had a way of making you feel like a candle in a cathedral—small, and lit for someone else's purposes.

Sera stood at her post outside Elodie's door in the east wing, third night running, and tried not to think about the word *guard*. It was a soft word. A kind word. The Archon had used it twice when he gave her the assignment, and both times his voice had wrapped around it like a hand around a bird. *Guard him well, Sera. He is precious to us all.*

Precious. That was the other thing about the Hall: it never said *valuable* when it could say *beloved*, never said *asset* when it could say *hope*. Vey spoke the language of faith because it was cheaper than the language of ownership, and everyone in the Hall had agreed to pretend they couldn't do the conversion in their heads.

She could. She'd been doing it since she was nine years old.

Through the door she could hear Elodie moving—not pacing anymore, he'd stopped pacing on the second night—but the small sounds of a man trying to make a borrowed room into a life. The scratch of a pen. She'd brought him paper from the stores without being asked, and hated herself a little for how pleased he'd been, how quickly he'd smiled, as if kindness were still a currency he trusted.

*You were sent to retrieve him,* she reminded herself. *Not to save him.*

The distinction mattered less every day, which was exactly why she kept repeating it.

* * *

She had grown up in the gray country beyond the Veil, where magic came back to you in sips—a trickle through whatever cracks the Sundering had left, enough to light a lamp with your palm if you were born with the knack, never enough to warm a room. Her mother had died of a fever that any hedge-healer inside the Veil would have laughed at. Out here, there were no healers. There was the Order, and the Order had plans, and the plans took generations, and children like Sera grew up learning that their small hungers were contributions to a great work.

She had believed it. She wanted that on the record, wherever records were kept in a world where the great archive was a burning temple in a valley full of liars: she had believed, sincerely, for twenty-two years.

Then she'd walked through a pilgrim's gate into Vell and felt the Veil press down on her gift like a thumb on a wick, and in the dead air of that valley she had found a scribe with ink-stained fingers and a look on his face like a man reading his own death warrant in beautiful handwriting—and something in her chest had come loose that she hadn't known was tied down.

It wasn't love. She was careful about that word too. The Order taught you to distrust it, which meant it was probably the only thing left worth trusting.

But she remembered the market. She remembered cornering him between a fruit stall and a wall, showing him the spark that lived above her palm, watching it gutter and die three feet from the boundary of what the Veil allowed, and she remembered his face—the hunger in it, the terror, the terrible honesty of a man who had never once been permitted to want anything. And she had thought: *they built a whole prison around people like him and called it peace.*

That was before she knew whose hands had built it. Before the boundary stone with ASHGROVE cut deep and old. Before the archives under the Hall had shown her the rest.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Vey summoned her to the observatory.

It wasn't an observatory anymore—there hadn't been working glass in the dome since the war—but the name persisted, the way names persist over ruins, and Vey liked it because it implied looking at things far away. He stood at the center of the cracked floor with a chart spread out at his feet, and when Sera entered he didn't look up right away. He never did. He let you stand in the silence long enough to remember your place in it.

"Sister Vaelin." Her Order name. She hadn't heard anyone say *Sera* in weeks, and she found she missed it the way you miss weather. "How is our guest?"

"He sleeps badly," she said. "He reads constantly. He asks questions I can't answer."

"Can't or won't?"

"Can't." She held his gaze, which was a skill like any other and required practice. "He's starting to understand what we are."

"Good." Vey turned a marker on his chart—a black stone across a line of mountains. "Understanding is the first stage of belonging. You understand that now, don't you? What he is to us?"

"The Veilbearer."

"What he is *for* us." Vey's smile was famous in the Hall; young acolytes described it the way sailors describe weather, as something you survived rather than enjoyed. "The blood of the sealers, walking freely in our halls. Do you know how many of my predecessors spent their whole lives searching for a single descendant of those twelve families? How many died in the attempt?" He looked up then, and his eyes were pale and patient. "And you simply knocked on a pilgrim's gate and found him copying scripture. Providence has a sense of humor. We must be worthy of it."

"You told me to find the prophecy's subject," Sera said carefully. "You didn't tell me what you intended once I did."

"I told you exactly what I intended." Vey stepped off the chart, folding his hands behind his back. "I intend to end the Sundering's consequences. I intend to give magic back to a world that has been starving for a century. And yes—I intend to manage what comes through the crack when it opens, because someone must, and the temple in the valley has demonstrated for a hundred years that sealing things away is not the same as solving them." He tilted his head. "Does that trouble you, Sister Vaelin?"

"No, Archon."

"Liar." He said it gently, almost fondly. "Of course it troubles you. You've been guarding him for four days. Guarding breeds attachment—it's why I rotate sentries on important assignments, and why I haven't rotated you." He let that land. "Do you know why I haven't rotated you?"

Sera kept her face still. Inside, cold water moved.

"Because attachment is useful," Vey said. "A guard who cares will take a blade for the asset. Will warn me if he turns. Will report his moods, his fears, the words he says in the dark when he thinks no one hears." His voice stayed pleasant. "So. Report."

And there it was—the trap she had been living inside for four days, named aloud, its walls suddenly visible. He had given her the post *because* it would compromise her. Every kindness she'd shown Elodie was data. Every hour she'd stood outside his door listening to the scratch of his pen was intelligence gathered for a man who intended to open a prison and put a tyrant on a leash made of the boy's own blood.

"The Veilbearer is frightened," she said. "He dreams about the valley burning. He asks whether Cassian is alive."

"And what do you tell him?"

"That I don't know."

"Do you tell him what he is for?" Vey watched her. "Have you explained the ritual?"

"Not yet."

"Mm." He considered her for a moment that lasted a season. "Tell him soon. A man consents better to a thing he understands. And Sister—if you find your loyalty pulling in two directions, don't struggle against it. Bring it to me. Divided hearts are heavy, and I am very good at carrying weight." He returned to his chart. "Dismissed."

* * *

She went to Elodie's room that evening instead of taking her meal in the refectory, and she told herself it was routine patrol, and she knew she was lying.

He was sitting on the floor by the window with the prophecy book open on his knees. He'd taken to keeping it close since the third line had filled itself in—*the Veilbearer chooses, and the valley burns or the world burns*—and he read it the way you touch a bruise, again and again, checking whether it hurt yet.

"It doesn't change when I'm watching," he said without looking up. "I've tried. Four hours yesterday. It only fills when I've stopped expecting it. Like it knows."

"Books don't know things."

"This one does." He closed it. "Sera. Can I ask you something and have you answer it honestly? Not the way people answer here. Everyone in this Hall answers questions like they're paying a tax."

The observation was sharper than he knew. She sat down across from him, cross-legged, the way they'd sat in the market in Vell a lifetime ago. "Ask."

"When this is over—when I do whatever it is the book says I do—what happens to me?"

And there it was. The question she'd been sent to prevent him from asking clearly, or failing that, to answer badly.

She could feel the Order's answer waiting in her mouth like a smooth stone: *you'll be honored, you'll be central, the world will owe you everything.* All true, in the way that a noose is a necklace.

"Sera?"

"You weren't lost," she said. "In Vell. When I found you. I want you to understand that first, because everything else I'm going to say only makes sense after it."

His brow creased. "What do you mean?"

"The Order has been looking for the Veilbearer for a hundred years. Generations of searchers, dying in the gray country, following rumors of descendants. And the prophecy book—" she nodded at his knees— "was written inside the valley, but the Order knew it existed. Knew fragments. Maren quoted one to you, didn't she? *The scribe runs.*"

"She did." His voice had gone quiet and careful. "How does an Inquisitor from the temple know Order prophecies?"

"Because Maren serves the Order. Or served it. I think that changed recently, but that's not the point." Sera made herself keep going, made herself walk into it. "The point is that I wasn't tracking a feeling. I was tracking a name. Yours. They gave me your description before I ever crossed the Veil. Dark hair, scribe's calluses, works the archive, father dead in the quarry, mother dead of lung-rot, indentured to the temple at fourteen." She recited it flatly, the dossier verbatim, and watched each fact land on him like a small stone. "I could have drawn you before I saw you, Elodie. That's how well they knew who you were. That's how long they'd known."

The window light was going orange and thin. Somewhere below, acolytes were singing the evening offices, their voices rising through the broken dome.

"So when you offered to smuggle me out," he said slowly, "at the market. When I refused. That wasn't—"

"It was real. My offer was real." She leaned forward. "Elodie, listen to me. The retrieval was ordered. The offer was mine. I broke protocol the moment I showed you the spark—I was supposed to observe, report, wait for extraction teams. Instead I tried to get you out alone, half-cocked, because—" she exhaled, "—because you looked like a man drowning in a room with no doors, and I couldn't watch it. That part was true. I need you to believe that part was true, because the rest isn't pretty."

"The rest being?"

"The rest being that 'retrieve' and 'rescue' are different words, and the Archon chose the first one, and I signed on to the first one, and by the time I understood the difference I was already standing inside these walls telling myself I was your guard." She met his eyes. "Vey didn't send me to save you from the Inquisitor. He sent me to bring you to the altar. Everything since—the training, the lessons in fire, the worship—that's not rescue. It's preparation. You're not a guest here, Elodie. You're a component."

He didn't move for a long time. The singing swelled and faded below them.

"A component," he repeated. "For the ritual. To break the seal."

"To break the seal and release what's underneath it. Magic for the world—" she raised a hand before he could speak, "—and that part might even be sincere. I think Vey does want to free the world's magic. I think he believes every word he says about ending the starvation. But he also intends to control what comes out of that seal, and what comes out is a tyrant your ancestors couldn't kill, and the leash he means to use is woven out of you." Her voice cracked on the last words and she hated it. "Your blood opened nothing. Your blood closes everything. You're the lock and the key and the hand on the door, and he wants all three."

Elodie looked down at the prophecy book. When he spoke, his voice was strangely calm, the calm of a man standing in wreckage counting what survived.

"The second betrayal," he said.

"What?"

"Cassian told me once—in the temple, before they arrested him—that prophecy always comes in threes. First the naming, then the hunting, then the choosing." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and steady. "I thought he meant events. He meant people. The mentor betrays me. Then the friend." He swallowed. "Is that what this is? Are you the second line?"

"No." She said it fast, too fast. "Yes. I don't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Twenty-two years of discipline, and it came apart in front of a scribe with ink-stained fingers. "I was sent to retrieve you. That was the mission. I executed the mission. And somewhere between the market and the boundary stone and the boundary crossing, the mission stopped being the truest thing in me, and I don't know what replaced it, and I'm terrified, because the Order doesn't have a procedure for what replaced it. There's no form I can file."

He reached across and took her hand. His grip was ink-stained and trembling and warm.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "If Vey finds out—"

"He already suspects. He keeps me posted here precisely because he thinks caring makes me useful." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "He thinks my divided heart is a feature. He wants to carry the weight."

"Will you let him?"

The question hung there. Outside, the last light failed off the mountains, and the evening offices ended, and the Hall settled into its vast stone quiet.

Sera turned her hand over and laced her fingers through his.

"I stood outside your door for four nights," she said, "listening to you try to build a life in a cage, and every night I told myself the same sentence: *you were sent to retrieve him, not to save him.* And every night the sentence got thinner. Tonight I finally heard what it actually says." She lifted her chin. "It says I get to choose which verb is mine. Retrieve or save. The Order gave me the first one." Her grip tightened. "The second one I'm giving myself."

Elodie's breath caught. "Sera—"

"Don't thank me yet." She glanced at the door, at the shadow-line beneath it where torchlight passed and faded. "Because if I mean this—and I mean this—then we're past the point where feelings matter and into the point where plans do. Vey expects you compliant, prepared, and ignorant. We can use all three. But the window is short, Elodie. Once the ritual preparations begin in earnest, once they move you to the seal chamber, there won't be another market, another gate, another chance to run." She leaned close, voice dropping to almost nothing. "So tonight you need to decide, and decide for good: when I say the word—when I tell you it's time—we go. Through whatever stands in the way. Because next time there won't be an offer to refuse."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he pulled the prophecy book onto his knees and opened it to the last filled page, and she saw the fresh ink gleaming there—newer than the third line, newer than tonight, letters still dark and wet as wounds:

*The guardian's heart divides, and the division saves.*

They stared at it together.

"It's been writing ahead of us," Elodie whispered. "This whole time. It's not predicting, Sera. It's—" 

"—instructing," she finished. "I know."

Below them, faint through the stone, a bell began to ring. Not the offices. Not any hour she knew. A pattern she'd only ever heard described in the oldest training texts, the alarm reserved for one thing:

Breach. Something crossing from the valley side.

Sera was on her feet before the second toll, hand already at her knife, every soft thing in her folding away into the hard old shape of duty—and she hated how easily it still fit.

"Stay here," she told Elodie.

"No," he said, rising, the book clutched to his chest. "Whatever's coming through that Veil—it came for me. I'm done being guarded, Sera. From now on, I'm coming *with*."
