## 27. Escape from the Ashen Hall

**Elodie**

I had rehearsed this moment for three nights. In my head it was clean: a door, a corridor, silence, gone.

What I got instead was Vey's voice rolling through the Ashen Hall like a landslide, and every lamp in the mountain flaring at once.

*"The Veilbearer is leaving."*

Not angry. That was the worst part. Amused.

Sera's hand closed around my wrist—hard enough to bruise—and pulled me off the archive steps and into the dark of the west gallery, where the Order hung the banners of its dead. We ran between them, between names stitched in ash-grey thread, and I understood with a sick lurch that some of those names had been Veilbearer candidates too. The Order collected its failures and displayed them like trophies.

"The servants' stair," Sera breathed. "It comes out above the cisterns. There's a postern gate the night watch doesn't guard because they don't know it exists."

"You don't know it exists either."

"I know everything that exists in this hall that shouldn't." Her eyes caught the lamp-light, fierce and terrified in equal measure. "I've been planning to leave since before I met you, Elodie. I just didn't know what I was leaving *for*."

Behind us, boots. Many boots. And beneath them, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the hum of the Hall itself waking—the wards Vey had woven into the stone over thirty years, waking one by one like eyes opening.

"They'll seal the gates," I said.

"Then we go out through something that isn't a gate."

The first ward found us before we reached the stair. It rose out of the flagstones like heat-shimmer, a lattice of pale light, and I felt it taste me—the way it slid along my skin, reading my blood, my name, my freshly-taught, dangerously raw power. Reading the thing Vey had spent three weeks cultivating in me like a gardener checking a row.

*Sera of the Ninth Circle,* it seemed to say. *Permitted.*

*Elodie Ashgrove,* it said next. *Property.*

Something in me snapped—not fear, not quite. Something hotter and older, something that had been waiting under my ribs since the day a blank page refused my ink.

"No," I said, out loud, to a wall.

And the magic answered.

It came up through me like water finding a crack—no form, no training, none of the careful shapes the Order's tutors had drilled into me. Just *pressure*, mine, shaped by nothing but refusal. The ward didn't shatter so much as *flinch*, the lattice buckling inward, and then it tore with a sound like wet paper and went dark from the inside out.

Sera stared at me. At the space where it had been.

"That wasn't in your lessons."

"No," I said. My hands were shaking. I was grinning anyway. "That was all mine."

* * *

**Sera**

I had fought beside mages for eleven years. I had seen battle-craft that could unmake hillsides, duellists who moved like weather. None of it had prepared me for Elodie.

Because trained mages cast *shapes*. Circles, lattices, bindings—forms handed down from the Sundering, refined across a century of people trying to make power safe. What came off Elodie in the gallery had no shape at all. It was just will, wearing light.

And it was aimed at keeping us both alive.

"Left," I snapped, as two acolytes rounded the banner-hall's far arch. "Don't fight them, *blind* them."

He didn't ask how. He raised his hand and the lamps nearest the arch surged white, and the acolytes staggered back, hands over their faces, shouting about sorcery—which, in the Ashen Order, was the most ironic battle cry imaginable.

We took the servants' stair three steps at a time. Below us the Hall was sounding alarms in the old way: bells, then horns, then the deep thrumming note of the mountain's own voice being bent to Vey's purpose. He was speaking through it. Through the stone itself.

*Elodie.* His voice, everywhere, gentle as ever. *You are a key. Keys do not choose their locks. Come back, and we will pretend this never happened.*

"Keys," Elodie muttered, taking the stairs two at a time, "do not get *spoken through walls at*."

Despite everything—despite the horns, despite the fact that I had just burned eleven years of loyalty and likely my own life with it—I laughed. Actually laughed, breathless, in the dark.

He glanced back at me. "What?"

"Nothing. You're insufferable when you're frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"Liar. Your magic spikes when you lie. Didn't the tutors teach you that?"

"They taught me forty ways to bow," he said grimly, "and six ways to set a man on fire, and not one useful thing about myself."

The stair ended at the cistern level, cold air rising around us, black water stretching away into the dark. And there, exactly where she'd promised, the postern door—a slab of grey stone no wider than a coffin, sealed with a lock that hadn't been turned in decades.

I put my hand on it and fed it a thread of my own craft, the unlocking pattern they teach in the Ninth Circle before anything else. The lock considered this for a long moment.

Then it refused.

"It's warded," I said stupidly. "Vey re-warded the exits. Of course he did. Of course he—" My voice cracked. Eleven years. Eleven years of service, of belief, of telling myself the Order's cruelty was discipline and its worship was devotion, and now I stood at the only door out of my life and it wouldn't open.

"Sera." Elodie's voice was quiet. "Step back."

"You can't just—"

"*Step back.*"

She stepped back. And Elodie Ashgrove, scribe, indentured copyist, boy who had spent his whole life learning that power was something other people had, put both palms flat against the stone door and *pushed*.

Not physically. I felt it in my teeth—a great slow gathering, like the moment before lightning. The ward on the door recognized him, that was the horror of it; it had been keyed to the Veilbearer's blood, because Vey had always intended to walk his prize out through it someday. It opened *for* him.

But Elodie didn't open it gently. He tore the ward apart as he passed through it, unravelling thirty years of the Archon's work in a single ragged pull, and the door swung wide onto starlight and cold wind and the smell of pine.

He stumbled out into the night, grey-faced, gasping. I caught his arm.

"That cost you," I said.

"Everything costs." He straightened, wiping his mouth. "How far to the tree line?"

"Half a mile. Then the ravine. Then—" I looked back once at the Hall, its windows blazing, its voice still rolling out of the mountain after us. *Elodie. Elodie. Elodie.* Not anger. Grief, performed beautifully. "Then we run until the world runs out."

* * *

**Elodie**

They caught sight of us at the tree line.

Three shapes dropping from the crags above the path—pursuit-riders, Sera called them later, the Order's hounds, mages bred for exactly this work. They didn't shout. They didn't warn. The first bolt of crafted fire came down through the pines and took a tree apart ten feet to my left, and the world went white and loud and full of burning sap.

Sera shoved me behind a boulder and threw up her own craft—one hand weaving a shield, the other already answering, precise and economical, a decade of war distilled into small brutal motions. She was good. She was better than good. But she was one against three, and they knew her patterns, because they'd trained alongside her.

A bolt slipped her shield and hit the ground at our feet. Stone shrapnel opened a line along my cheekbone, warm and startling.

And that—that small stupid pain—was what did it.

I stood up out of cover. Sera shouted at me. The riders regrouped below, and I felt their craft reaching for me, three threads of intent converging, and instead of crouching back down I reached out and *took hold of them*.

Not deflected. Took. The way you'd snatch sparks from a fire.

For one suspended second I held three mages' killing-intent in my bare hands, and it screamed against my skin like live coals, and somewhere very far away I heard Sera saying my name in a voice I'd never heard her use before. Then I turned it. All of it. Threw it back down the slope in a single broad, uncontrolled, magnificent detonation that flattened the pines, blew the riders off their feet, and left a crater smoking in the mountainside where the path had been.

The ringing silence afterward was enormous.

I sat down. My legs simply declined to continue.

Sera crouched in front of me, checking my eyes, my pulse, the burn-marks on my palms. Her hands were steady. Her voice was not.

"You held their craft. Raw. Unshaped. Do you have any idea—" She stopped. Started again. "Nobody does that. Nobody has done that since the Sundering. The histories say only *he* could do that."

The tyrant. She didn't say the name. Neither of us did anymore.

"Maybe it's in the blood," I managed.

"Elodie." She took my face in her scorched hands and made me look at her, and there was something new in her expression—not awe, not fear. Recognition. "Listen to me. Whatever Vey told you that power was for—it isn't. What you just did, you did to protect someone. Remember that. On the hard days, remember which hand it came from."

Below us, groaning, one of the riders sat up in the smoking ruin of the path. Alive. Sera saw it too, and I watched her decide—watched the soldier in her measure the distance, the risk, the wisdom of finishing it.

"Leave him," I said quietly.

She looked at me. Then she stood, and offered me her hand, and pulled me up.

"We go down through the ravine," she said. "There's an old smuggler's track that skirts the Wardline. Two days' hard walking to the valley border." A pause. "Home, scribe."

"Not home yet."

"No," she agreed, glancing once more at the mountain behind us—at Vey's voice still echoing faintly off the stone, patient, certain, *waiting*—and at the smoke still rising from the crater where three of her former brothers-in-arms lay breathing. "But it's the first direction worth walking."

I took one step, and the ravine swallowed us both, and somewhere behind us, soft as a promise, the mountain said my name one last time.

*Elodie.*

*I will see you at the seal.*
