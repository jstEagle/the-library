## 28. Maren's True Mission

The road out of the Ashen Hall ran through a dead forest, and Maren had been waiting in it for six hours.

She had chosen her ground the way she chose everything: carefully, without sentiment. A narrow defile where the pines grew close and the snow never fully melted, even in high summer. Two ways in, one way out. She sat on a fallen trunk with her hood back and her hands folded in her lap, and she let the cold settle into her bones the way it always did before an interrogation—except this time there would be no interrogation. This time she was the one who would have to talk.

Her orders from the Order had been simple: find the Veilbearer, mark his path, report his progress toward the seal. Her orders from the other letter—the one that had arrived three weeks ago, sewn into the lining of a courier's coat by someone who should not have known she existed—had been equally simple.

*Stop Vey.*

She had read that second letter forty times. She had burned it twice and reconstructed it from memory both times, because that was what you did when you were Inquisitor of the Temple of Vell: you memorized things worth burning.

The handwriting had been her predecessor's. Sister Ondine, who had held the office of Inquisitor for eleven years before dying quietly in her sleep—a death Maren now understood had probably not been quiet at all. The letter said what Ondine had apparently spent her last years learning: that the heresy hunt was theater. That Vey had planted Maren in the temple herself, eleven years ago, a girl of nineteen with a talent for cruelty and no family to ask questions. That every Veilbearer candidate the temple had ever produced had been steered, tested, and harvested by the Ashen Order—and that the prophecy book was not a relic but a leash.

*You are not the hunter,* Ondine had written. *You are the hound. And the hand on your collar is the same one that holds the Veilbearer's.*

Maren had not believed it. Then she had done what she always did with things she did not believe: she had checked.

Eleven days of checking. Eleven days of pulling threads she'd been forbidden to pull, back through the archive of her own career, and every thread ended at the same place. The anonymous tips that had guided her investigations—all of them traceable to Order couriers. The "escaped heretics" she'd hunted in her early years—every one a mage who had learned too much about the Veil's founding. The fragment of prophecy text that had been slipped into her dossier last month, the one that let her find Elodie Ashgrove faster than any honest inquiry should have allowed—delivered by a man whose face she now recognized from Vey's inner circle.

She had been built like a tool. Sharpened like a tool. Aimed like a tool.

And tools, she had decided somewhere around day nine, are permitted exactly one act of will.

They choose the moment they stop cutting what they're told to cut.

* * *

She heard them before she saw them—which meant Sera was slipping, or exhausted, or letting her hear on purpose. Footsteps in frozen bracken, uneven, favoring a left leg. The boy's breathing, fast and shallow. And underneath it all, faint as a struck bell heard through a wall, the hum of raw magic being carried by someone who didn't yet know how to hold it quiet.

Elodie Ashgrove came around the bend first, and Maren studied him with the dispassion of long habit. He looked worse than the reports suggested. Hollow-eyed, singed at the cuffs, moving like a man who had discovered the ground could betray him. Behind him, Sera limped, one hand pressed under her ribs, the other holding a knife low and ready despite the way her arm shook.

Sera saw her and stopped dead. The knife came up.

"Inquisitor," Sera said. No fear in it. Just the flat recognition of one professional meeting another across an impossible distance.

"Sera Kell." Maren did not rise. "You're favoring the left side. The wound from the hall?"

"How do you—"

"Because I trained the man who gave it to you." Maren stood slowly, hands still visible, palms open. "Doran. Second blade in Vey's honor guard. He overcommits on the lunge; everyone he cuts survives if they drop their shoulder." She tilted her head. "You dropped your shoulder. Good."

"You're a long way from your valley," Sera said. "Or is this still your valley? You crossed the boundary chasing a scribe. That's not doctrine, Inquisitor. That's obsession."

"It was cover." Maren let the words fall into the space between them and watched them land. "All of it. Every step."

Elodie spoke for the first time. His voice was rough, scraped thin by flight. "The interrogations. Brother Cassian. The arrest." His jaw tightened. "Was that cover too?"

"No," Maren said. "That part was real. I was really hunting you. I just wasn't hunting you for the reason I said." She took a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed, and rehearsal was useless, and she knew it. "I'm going to tell you something, Veilbearer, and you have no reason to believe me, and I'm going to say it anyway because the hour is bad and getting worse. Vey sent me to find you eleven years before either of us knew your name. I was his instrument in the temple. My heresy hunt was designed to flush you out, test you, and drive you into the arms of whoever Vey had waiting beyond the Veil."

Sera made a small sound. Not surprise—recognition. Maren filed it away: the exile had suspected something like this all along.

"And now?" Elodie asked.

"Now I've received a letter from a dead woman telling me my entire life was a leash, and I've verified it, and I've spent the last three days deciding which end of the leash to bite." Maren reached into her coat—slowly, deliberately—and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases from handling. She set it on the fallen trunk between them and stepped back. "Ondine's letter. Read it if you want. It's the only proof I have, and proof is all I've ever dealt in."

Sera moved first, snatching it up, scanning it while keeping her body between Maren and the boy. Maren respected that. It was correct procedure.

"This is Sister Ondine's hand," Sera said quietly. "I saw her correspondence once, before I was exiled."

"Then you know I'm not forging anything. I couldn't forge that hand if I tried; I only met her twice, at her installation and her funeral." Maren paused. "What did she die of, do you know? Officially?"

"They said her heart," Sera said.

"Mm. Mine beats fine, currently. But I've been drinking willowbark tea for a week out of caution, and I don't drink tea I haven't prepared myself, so we'll see how committed anyone is." She said it lightly, and watched neither of them laugh, and continued anyway. "Here is the situation, plainly, because I don't have the energy for the elaborate version anymore.

"Vey doesn't want to free the world's magic. Freeing magic is a consequence he'll accept, the way a merchant accepts rain. What he wants is the tyrant. The thing under the seal—the Ashgrove patriarch, whatever name the histories burned—he believes he can bind it, command it, wear it like armor. He has been preparing for a century. The Order's founders didn't survive the Sundering to worship freedom; they survived it by serving the losing side, and they've never stopped serving it.

"If he reaches the seal with the Veilbearer's blood, we don't get a restored world. We get a god wearing a charismatic man's face, and every army that ever remembered what magic was will come running to kneel or burn.

"I was sent to make sure the Veilbearer arrived on schedule. I am now asking—" and here her voice did something she hated, something unsteady, something human— "I am asking to help make sure he doesn't."

* * *

Silence, then. The dead forest held its breath. Somewhere above them a crow complained once and went silent, as if it too wanted to hear the answer.

Elodie looked at the letter in Sera's hands, then at Maren, and Maren watched him do the arithmetic she had watched a hundred suspects do across an interrogation table: weighing fear against need, pride against survival.

"Why should we believe you?" he said finally. "You arrested Cassian. You broke him—I saw what the questioning did to him, afterward. You hunted me across half the valley."

"Yes."

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you have me say?" Maren spread her hands. "That I regret it? I regret the methods, some of them. I regret that the old man suffered, though I note he lied to me beautifully for two days and bought you time with his suffering, which means he'd likely tell you it was worth it. But I won't perform remorse for you, scribe. Remorse performed is just another kind of lie, and lies are what got all three of us here. I'll give you facts instead.

"Fact: I know the Order's protocols better than anyone alive outside its walls. I can get us through their outer pickets without a fight. Fact: Vey thinks I'm loyal, and that belief is worth more than an army—it buys us hours, maybe a day, before he knows his instrument turned. Fact: Doran cut Sera Kell and left her alive, which means either he's grown sloppy or he was ordered to leave a trail, and I know Doran, and he is not sloppy. Which means Vey wants you running. Running men go to familiar ground. Familiar ground, for you, is home." She let that settle. "He isn't chasing you to the seal, Veilbearer. He's herding you."

Sera lowered the knife an inch. "She's right about Doran," she said reluctantly. "And she's right about the pickets. I've been dreading the return route for two days. If she can walk us through it—"

"Sera."

"She's right, Elodie. You know she's right. I hate it too."

Elodie stared at Maren for a long moment, and she let him look. She had found, over eleven years, that the truth held up well under looking. It was everything else that flinched.

"The prophecy book," he said suddenly. "It updates itself. It wrote my escape before I escaped it. It wrote Cassian's arrest. If it's writing ahead—if it's instructing rather than predicting—" His eyes narrowed. "Did it write you?"

The question went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because she had asked herself the same thing, alone, at night, three times now. Because Ondine's letter had arrived precisely when Maren needed a reason to doubt, and doubts arrive too conveniently when someone wants them delivered.

"I don't know," she said. Honest. Bare. "Maybe. Maybe I'm the third line of someone's script, same as you were. Maybe the letter was bait and the trap is behind me and I'm leading you straight into it." She shrugged, a small, bitter motion. "But here's the difference between us and the book, scribe. The book writes what will happen. We're standing in a forest deciding right now, in front of witnesses, whether to trust each other. Whatever the prophecy says about this moment, it hasn't happened yet. That's the only country I've ever lived in. The undecided one."

Another silence. Then Elodie laughed—short, cracked, nothing like joy.

"The undecided one. You sound like Cassian. He used to say the temple taught that the future was unwritten ink, and I thought it was a platitude." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Fine. Alliance. Uneasy. On conditions."

"Name them."

"Cassian. When we take the valley back, he walks free, debt ledger burned, full pardon, in front of the survivors. Publicly."

"He'll have it. I'll write it myself."

"And you answer to us, not the reverse. You're not taking point. You're not steering. If your information and Sera's instinct disagree, we go with hers."

Maren considered objecting, found she didn't have the strength, and found, beneath the absence of strength, something that felt suspiciously like relief. Eleven years of being the sharpest mind in every room. It might be pleasant to follow for once.

"Agreed," she said.

"And one more." Elodie stepped closer, and up close she could see the exhaustion sitting on him like frost, and beneath it something new, something banked and bright that hadn't been there in the archive a month ago. "When this is done—inquisitors, orders, prophecies, all of it—I want to know why you did it. Eleven years pretending. I want the whole account, Maren. Every entry."

"Then live long enough to audit me," she said.

He almost smiled. It was terrible and human and it was the first thing resembling trust any of them had exchanged in weeks.

* * *

They moved at dusk, Maren on point after all—because Sera's leg couldn't manage the pace and everyone knew it, and because some habits of competence outrank even fresh vows of obedience. The dead forest gave way to scree, the scree to a ridge overlooking the long gray scar of the old war-road, and far below, tiny as toys, the outer pickets of the Ashen Order: cookfires, sentry-walks, the orange glow of wards strung along the pass like a festival that had forgotten to be festive.

"There," Maren murmured. "The western ward-line has a gap. Maintenance rotation, fourth watch. Forty minutes, maybe less if they're lazy tonight."

"You're sure?" Sera whispered.

"I built the rotation schedule myself, four years ago, as a demonstration of loyalty." She smiled without warmth. "Vey rewards thoroughness. It's one of the few things we have in common."

They began to descend. And it was on the third switchback, in the blue half-dark, that Elodie suddenly stopped and put a hand to his pack.

"The book," he breathed.

He pulled it out. Even in the failing light Maren could see the change: pages that had been blank for a century now crowded with ink that gleamed wet and black, lines filling even as they watched, letters crawling into place like insects arranging themselves.

Elodie read aloud, and his voice went flat and strange:

*"The hound turns her head. The hunter's mask slips. And at the gate, the gatekeeper waits—who was never the enemy at all."*

Silence.

"That's me," Maren said. "The hound. Obviously." She kept her voice level through sheer professional discipline. "Turning my head. Right now."

"But the gatekeeper," Sera said slowly. "At the gate. Who waits at the gate?"

Maren opened her mouth to say *no one*, and then closed it, because a memory had surfaced, cold and unwelcome: the last report she'd received from the temple, three days ago, before she turned. A routine dispatch. Wardens doubled at the boundary stone. And one line, flagged as trivial, that she'd skimmed past—

*A pilgrim arrived at dusk, requesting sanctuary. Elderly. Says he knows the Veilbearer's family.*

She looked at Elodie, and saw him arrive at the same thought at the same moment, and watched the color leave his face.

"Brother Cassian," he whispered. "Cassian got free. And he's not running away from the valley." His grip tightened on the book until the leather creaked. "He's standing at the gate. Waiting for us."

The prophecy's final line was still drying.

*"And the mentor carries the truth that will cost him everything."*
