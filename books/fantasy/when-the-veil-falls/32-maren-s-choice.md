## 32. Maren's Choice

Maren had spent her whole life learning how people lied, and so she recognized the truth in Elodie Ashgrove the moment she saw it: he was going to do something stupid and noble, and no one was going to stop him.

She stood at the mouth of the undercroft stair with her back to the dark and her face to the fire. Above her, the temple burned. The great vaulted ceiling of the nave had begun to shed burning rafters like a tree dropping limbs in a storm, and through the smoke she could hear the wardens coming—boots on stone, the flat clack of warden staves against marble, voices calling to each other in the clipped cadence of trained men who believed they were the last sane people in a mad world.

Twelve of them. Maybe more behind.

She counted them the way she'd taught herself to count everything—by their sounds, their spacing, their fear. Wardens were brave in numbers and cowardly in doubt, and right now they were full of doubt. They had come down into their own temple expecting heretics and found their Inquisitor standing guard over one.

"Mother Maren." Warden-Captain Doss's voice, hoarse with smoke, careful the way men are careful around something that might bite. "Step aside."

"Inquisitor," she corrected. It was an old habit, and it steadied her. "And no."

"The temple burns because of that boy. The Archive is gone. Four centuries of scripture." Doss's voice cracked on scripture, and Maren understood—he wasn't angry about the books. He was grieving them. That made him dangerous; grief made men certain. "He did this."

"The Ashen Order did this," Maren said. "Vey breached your Veil with stolen rites while you were all asleep inside it. The boy went down those stairs to end it. I am going to make sure he has time enough to try."

"You were sent to find him," Doss said. "You told us yourself. Heresy in Vell, the prophecy book, the scribe. You stood before the Consistory and swore it."

"I did." She let the silence stretch. "I've since read the file I was sent to bury."

A murmur ran down the line of wardens. Doubt, feeding itself. Good. Doubt bought seconds, and seconds were what she was selling tonight, and the price was hers alone to pay.

* * *

She thought, absurdly, of ledgers.

That was how Maren understood the world—in entries, in columns, in debts carried forward. She had been keeping accounts since she was nine years old, when the famine took her father's farm and the temple took his debt and converted it into a daughter. Twenty years as an indentured novice, then a scribe, then a hunter of heresy, and every year of it entered neatly in the temple's books under *assets acquired*.

The Ashen Order had found her there, in the red ink. They had offered her a different ledger—one where the temple's cruelty was written plainly, where the Veil was named a lie, where her twenty years of servitude became evidence instead of fate. She had signed. Of course she had signed. A woman who has been owned will burn anything for the promise of being free.

But the Order's ledger had its own hidden columns. She'd seen them over the years—the missions that ended with witnesses dead, the questions that were not permitted, the way Archon Vey said *the Veilbearer* the way other men said *weapon*. And when she'd finally pulled the threads far enough, she'd found the truth underneath both ledgers alike: nobody wanted the truth. The temple wanted the lie preserved. The Order wanted the lie exploited. And somewhere between them, a scribe from Vell had done the one thing neither institution had planned for.

He'd *read*.

So she had come down here not for the Order, not for the temple, but for the only entry in forty years that balanced. The boy deserved time. Time was the whole of what she had left, and she meant to spend every second of it.

* * *

"Last chance, Mother Maren." Doss again, closer now. Through the smoke she could see him—a big man, gray at the temples, warden stave held low and ready. Behind him, eleven shadows with eleven staves. "Stand aside and the Consistory may show mercy."

"The Consistory," Maren said, "executed a fourteen-year-old girl in the square at Solmere two years ago for teaching letters to mill children without license. Do any of you remember her name?"

Silence. One of the younger wardens shifted.

"No one does," Maren said. "That is what mercy looks like in Vell. That is what a hundred years of the Veil has built. A valley where curiosity is a sin and memory is a crime and the only thing anyone is permitted to feel about it is gratitude." She drew her blade—not the ceremonial knife, the real one, the one she'd carried since Solmere—and set her feet. "I am done being grateful."

"Then you're a traitor," Doss said, and there was sorrow in it, which she appreciated. Sorrowful men hesitated. Hateful men didn't.

"Yes," she said. "Finally."

They came.

* * *

Fighting twelve men in a narrow stairwell should have been impossible. Fighting them in a burning building was merely arithmetic. The smoke worked for her—it came down the stair in rolling banks, and the wardens came up out of it blind, coughing, in single file, because the stair would only hold two abreast and fear narrows men further than stone does.

She killed the first one almost by accident, a young warden who came up swinging wide, and felt nothing she'd have expected to feel. Later, if there was a later, she supposed she'd pay for that. Grief keeps honest books too, and hers always came due.

The second and third came together and she gave ground, step by step, backward down the stairs toward the sealed door, buying distance the way she'd once bought confessions—one coin at a time. A stave caught her across the shoulder and something cracked, white-hot, and she heard herself make a sound she hadn't made since childhood, and then she stopped hearing anything much at all except the rhythm of it. Parry. Step. Cut. Step. The oldest ledger there is.

Doss fought last. He always was the best of them.

He came up through the smoke bleeding from a cut along his jaw, dropped his broken stave, and drew steel, and for a moment they simply looked at each other through the haze—two people who had spent six years trading courtesies across an interrogation table, each convinced the other was the temple's conscience.

"You knew," Doss said. "All those heretics we burned. You knew some of them were innocent."

"I knew all of them were innocent," Maren said. "That was the job."

He struck, and she caught it on her blade, and her ruined shoulder folded, and she went down to one knee on the stones with his sword pressing toward her throat and the fire raining down around them both like a decision already made.

"Maren." Quietly. Just for her. "Give me the boy."

"He's not mine to give." She smiled—she felt it, strange and light, the first easy thing she'd done in twenty years. "He's everyone's. That's rather the point."

She turned her wrist inside his guard and drove her knife up under his ribs, and they went down together, and the last thing she saw before the smoke took everything was the door below—sealed, unbroken, holding.

Holding. For now.

Behind it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, she felt the seal begin to answer the blood that had come home to it, and Maren Almeida, who had spent her whole life reading the last lines of other people's stories, closed her eyes content in the knowledge that this one, at least, was still being written.

* * *

Down in the dark beneath the burning temple, Elodie Ashgrove pressed his palm to the burning name and did not hear her die. But the seal shivered once—as if something old and patient had just paid a debt in its favor—and the voice beneath the Veil said:

*"Someone loved you enough to bleed for minutes. Spend them well."*
