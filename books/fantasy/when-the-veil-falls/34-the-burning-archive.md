## 34. The Burning Archive

The temple burned the way everything in Vell did—slowly at first, then all at once.

I was three streets away when the first true roar went up, and the sound reached me through the soles of my feet before it reached my ears. The ground itself seemed to shudder, as if something enormous had rolled over in its sleep beneath the valley. Which, I supposed, something had.

Magic was bleeding into the world again. I could feel it now—I'd been feeling it since the boundary, since Sera pulled me over the stone with the Ashgrove name carved into its face—but here inside the Veil's dying radius it came to me thin and strange, like breath through a cracked window. Flickers. Embers of a fire that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to be a fire.

Then the temple spire caught, and there was no more ambiguity about anything.

I ran.

* * *

The streets of Vell were full of people who had never once, in their entire lives, seen a building burn. We didn't have fires here. That sounds like a small thing until you watch an entire town stand in the middle of the road doing nothing while their holiest place goes up like a signal pyre. No buckets. No lines. Just faces turned upward, mouths open, lit orange by a color most of them had only ever read descriptions of.

An old woman grabbed my sleeve as I pushed past. "Boy—the temple—the temple is—"

"I know," I said, and kept running.

Behind me someone started screaming about the end of days. They weren't entirely wrong. The end of one set of days, anyway.

The heat hit me half a block out. It rolled off the sanctuary doors in waves, thick enough to taste—pitch and parchment and old incense cooking in its own censer. The great doors had been flung open from inside; wardens and acolytes spilled down the steps coughing, some of them carrying armloads of books they'd clearly grabbed at random. A young novice dropped a stack of hymnals on the flagstones and stood staring at them like she couldn't remember why she'd bothered.

Hymnals. They were saving hymnals.

"Elodie!" A warden I knew—Brother Tomas, gray-bearded, the man who'd taught me to bind folios—caught my arm as I shoved toward the doors. His eyebrows were singed. There was ash in the creases of his face like he'd aged thirty years in thirty minutes. "You can't go in. The east stair is gone. The whole undercroft level is filling—"

"The undercroft." My heart did something complicated and painful. Cassian was somewhere in this building. Maren was bleeding out on the stairs holding back wardens-turned-traitors—or maybe not traitors anymore, maybe just men following the last order that made sense to them—and above all of it, beneath all of it, was the seal, and the circle, and the name still burning on the floor: *Ashgrove*.

"Cassian," I said. "Where's Cassian?"

Tomas's face flickered. "They got him out. He was shouting for you—he's at the fountain, they're holding him—"

"Then hold him there." I pulled free. "Tomas. The prophecy archive. Is it still standing?"

"The west wing—" He looked past me at the west wing, where smoke was beginning to curl from the clerestory windows, black and greasy. "Elodie, it won't be standing for long."

I know, I almost said. I know exactly how long it has. I've read tonight's date in a book older than every grave in this valley.

I went in.

* * *

Inside was another world. Outside, the fire was spectacle. Inside, it was weather—heat and light and a sound like a river running through every wall, the Veil's stolen magic finally loose in the timber, feeding on a century of dry scripture. The nave was a canyon of falling embers. Tapestries I'd dusted every spring of my indentured life hung in flaming ribbons. Somewhere overhead, glass burst and rained down in bright shards, and I thought absurdly of the inkwell I'd shattered my first week in the scriptorium, and Brother Cassian kneeling beside me, showing me how to blot the spill without smearing the page.

*Blot, don't smear. You can't save what's already run. You save what's still wet.*

I saved what was still wet.

The west wing corridor was already smoking at the ceiling but passable if I stayed low. The archive door stood open—of course it did; the wardens had evacuated what they could carry—but the shelves beyond stretched back farther than any rescue party had time to reach. A hundred years of records. Birth ledgers and debt rolls and tithe schedules and, buried in the sealed section no scribe was permitted to enter without two countersignatures, the founding documents I'd spent weeks quietly proving false.

It should have hurt, watching it burn. For a moment it did—a clean grief, simple as a cut. This was the only library I'd ever known. Every word I'd ever copied, I'd copied *here*. The smell of this room was the smell of my whole life: vellum and binding glue and lamp oil.

But then I saw it clearly, the way you see a thing only when it's already being taken from you: this wasn't a library. It was a ledger. A hundred years of entries in a book that began with a lie, kept by men who either believed the lie or profited from it, recording the lives of people who were never told the story they were living inside. My father died in a quarry to feed this building. My mother's grief was converted, line by careful line, into my indenture. Somewhere on these shelves was the actual receipt.

Let it burn.

I took three things.

First, the parish register—the real one, the one with the births and deaths of actual people, the quarry accident reports and the debt conversions, the paper trail of how the temple fed. If we were going to rebuild anything on the far side of tonight, we would need to know what we were rebuilding from. I wrapped it in my cloak.

Second, the map drawer from the cartography case. Not for the maps—for the thing I'd found beneath them during my researches, folded flat and forgotten: a survey of the valley made *before* the sealing, its margins annotated in a hand I now recognized as my great-whatever-grandfather's. Twelve names in the corner. Eleven crossed out. One circled. Proof, in ink, of the founding families. Of what we'd done.

Third—

Third I had to go deeper for.

* * *

The sealed section was behind the iron grille at the back of the archive, and the grille was hot enough to raise blisters through my sleeves. I wrapped my hands in cloth and pulled. It shrieked on its hinges and gave.

The prophecy book was where I'd left it before everything went sideways—no. That wasn't true. The last time I'd held it, Maren had been alive on the undercroft stairs and Vey's acolytes were still outside the walls and the third line hadn't yet filled itself in. Since then the book had traveled in my pack through the escape, through the Order's halls, through our flight back across the boundary. When the fighting started in the sanctuary above, when Sera shouted at me to *go, get to the seal*, I'd done the coward's arithmetic and stashed it here, in the one place I could find blind, rather than carry it into whatever was happening below.

Coward's arithmetic. But also, some part of me had understood even then: this book was the thread. Lose everything else, lose the temple, lose the archive, lose the whole rotten beautiful lie of Vell—but don't lose the book. Because the book was the only witness. The book remembered the design.

I found the shelf. Found the niche. Found the gap where its weight should have pressed the dust.

Empty.

For one horrible second the fire noise swelled and my ears rang and I thought: *it's gone, it burned, the one thing—*

Then a shape moved at the edge of the smoke, and a voice said, hoarse and familiar: "Looking for this?"

Cassian stepped out of the haze with the prophecy book clutched to his chest like a child, his robes scorched, a gash above his eye still seeping. In his other hand he carried a single slim volume I didn't recognize—black leather, no title.

"You're supposed to be at the fountain," I said stupidly.

"They were supposed to be holding me at the fountain." He coughed, waved at the smoke. "I've escaped better-guarded places than a bucket line. When I saw the west wing catch—" He held up the prophecy book. "I knew what you'd come back for. And I couldn't let you find it gone."

"You took it out and brought it *back in*?"

"I took it out, realized you'd tear the valley apart looking, and came back." A ghost of his old smile. "Sentiment is inefficient, but it's honest."

He put the book in my hands. It was warm—not fire-warm. Warmer than that, warmer than it should have been, the way it had always been slightly too warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm. Through the leather I felt it pulse once against my palms.

The final line stood open on the page, ink still faintly wet:

*the Veilbearer chooses, and the valley burns or the world burns.*

Below it, new words had appeared. Small ones. Almost shy.

*and the scribe writes the ending himself.*

I stared at it. "Cassian. What's the black book?"

His face closed, then opened again—forty years of closing, giving up in one breath of smoke. "The candidate's journal. The last Veilbearer before you. Her name was Ilse." He swallowed. "I told you I refused to kill her. I never told you she wrote all of this down before she died. Everything she learned about the seal. Everything the Order wanted her for. I've carried it for forty years because I was too much of a coward to destroy it and too much of a coward to use it."

"Why are you giving it to me now?"

"Because the page just told you the truth." He nodded at the prophecy book. "Prophecy picks the character. Someone still has to write the plot. And I am done letting dead men and hidden orders do the writing." He pressed the black book after the first. "Go down, Elodie. Finish it. Whatever it costs."

The ceiling groaned. A shelf collapsed somewhere behind us with a sound like a spine breaking, and sparks came through the smoke like a swarm of furious stars.

"What is worth saving?" I asked him—not really asking him, asking the burning room, the burning century. "Out of all of it?"

Cassian looked around at his life's work going up in gold and black. He didn't hesitate, which surprised me, or maybe it didn't.

"Not the books," he said. "The books were the cage. Save the people who can read. Save whoever's left standing downstairs. And save yourself—" his voice cracked, just once, "—because I have lost one Veilbearer to this already, and I will walk into this fire after you before I lose a second."

I hugged him. Briefly, hard, ash and blood and forty years of borrowed love between us. Then I tucked both books against my body, wrapped them tight, and turned toward the stairwell that led down into the dark beneath everything.

The undercroft door stood at the bottom of the east stair, past the interrogation rooms, past the cells, behind a sealed door no scribe had ever questioned—including, especially, me.

The stair was filling with smoke. Below it, faintly, I could hear Sera shouting, and steel ringing on steel, and beneath all of it, rising through the stone like water through a crack, a voice I recognized from the ritual circle, patient and reasonable and full of lies:

*Ashgrove. Come home.*

I wiped my eyes with a filthy sleeve, checked that the books were secure against my heart, and started down.
