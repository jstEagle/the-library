## 39. Reckonings

The temple's ashes were still warm when the survivors started arguing about what to do with them.

I stood in the courtyard on the third morning after the Shattering and watched two dozen people—scribes, wardens' widows, quarry families who had walked in from the outlying farms—form a rough circle around a heap of scorched timber that had once been Brother Aldric's reading hall. Someone had planted a shovel in the ash like a flag. Someone else was already talking about rebuilding on the same footprint, same walls, same rules.

"We start again," said Widow Hessa, arms crossed, chin up. "Same as before. The temple gave us order."

"The temple gave us a lie," I said.

Every face turned to me. I hadn't meant to speak. But that was becoming a habit—the Veilbearer speaks, and people listen, even when he'd rather they didn't. My voice carried differently now. Or maybe it always had, and I'd just never been worth listening to before.

"Order," Hessa repeated, "that kept us alive for a hundred years."

"And cost us everything else." I looked down at my hands. There was ash in the creases of my knuckles, and beneath it, faintly, the shimmer of something that wasn't quite light—a residue of the tyrant's essence coiled behind my sternum, stirring whenever my pulse rose. "We were never in danger, Hessa. That was the whole point of the Veil. It wasn't protection. It was a lid. And someone paid for that lid every single day we lived under it, without knowing we were paying."

I didn't say: and now I pay it instead, forever, from inside my own body. Some truths you ration.

Hessa held my gaze for a long moment. Then she picked up her shovel and turned it over, considering it like a weapon she no longer knew how to use. "Then tell us what to build," she said.

That was the trouble. Nobody could tell me. Not the prophecy book, which sat wrapped in oilcloth under my cot and had not written another word since its last line dried—not one instruction more, as if it considered its work finished and mine only beginning. Not Cassian. Not Sera. The valley of Vell had spent a century being told what to believe, and now the telling had stopped, and the silence was louder than the fire had been.

* * *

I found Cassian at dusk, sitting alone where the archive steps used to be, turning a charred quill over in his fingers like a relic from a saint who'd turned out to be a fraud.

"You should eat," I said.

"You should stop sounding like an abbot." He almost smiled. Almost. His face had aged years in days; the fire and the arrest and Maren's death had carved lines into him that no confession seemed able to smooth. "The east wall of the dormitory is standing. That's something."

"Cassian." I sat beside him on the broken step. The stone was cold through my trousers. "We need to finish this. You and me. Properly."

He set the quill down. "I killed her."

"You refused to kill her. She died anyway, forty years ago, and it broke the prophecy's first attempt—and you have carried it like a stone in your chest ever since, which is why you flinched every time I looked at that book, and why Maren saw it before either of us did." I kept my voice level. It took work. "I know the whole shape of it now. You told me in the undercroft with the temple burning above our heads. So don't retell it. Just let me say my part."

He waited. He was good at waiting. Forty years of practice.

"I forgive you," I said. "For the candidate. For lying to me about the book. For letting me grow up inside a lie because you thought the truth would break me worse than the debt did." I paused. "But forgiveness isn't absolution. You chose obedience over a girl's life, and then you chose silence over honesty for four decades, and both of those choices are yours. They stay yours. What I'm offering isn't a clean slate. It's this: you carry it, openly, and you help me make sure nobody in this valley ever has to choose obedience over truth again."

Cassian looked at the ruins. A wren landed on a blackened beam, considered it, and left, as if the temple no longer met its standards.

"That's harsher than absolution," he said.

"It's harder. Absolution is free. This costs you the rest of your life." I stood, brushed ash from my knees. "First payment's tomorrow. We're burning the ledgers."

His head came up sharply. "Elodie—"

"Not the histories. Not the records of who married whom, who was born, who died. Those are ours and we keep them." I looked back at him. "Just the debts. Every page that says a family owes the temple for the crime of grieving."

* * *

We burned them in the courtyard on a day so clear the mountains beyond the valley's rim showed their teeth for the first time in living memory—because the Veil was gone, and with it whatever haze had blurred the world down to Vell and nothing else. People came from all the outlying farms. They brought ledger books wrapped in sacking, carried like sick children, and stacked them in a pyre taller than I am.

Widow Hessa struck the first light herself. When the first book caught, she made a sound I'll remember until I die—half sob, half laugh, the noise of a woman discovering that a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her spine could actually be put down.

My own family's ledger went in somewhere in the middle. I watched the Ashgrove name curl and blacken and come apart into sparks that rose straight up in the windless air, and felt—nothing. Then, a breath later, something enormous. Not relief exactly. More like the moment after setting down a load so heavy your body doesn't know yet whether it can stand upright.

Sera stood at the edge of the crowd, arm still bound in a sling, watching the fire with an expression I couldn't read. The healers said the wound Vey's acolyte gave her on the undercroft stairs would take months to close properly, maybe longer—magic had returned to the world, but flesh remained stubbornly ordinary. She'd spent three days in the cot they'd given her in the warden's barracks, sleeping eighteen hours at a stretch, waking only to drink broth and ask, each time, in the same flat voice: "Is he still holding?"

He was. I was. The whisper behind my ribs stayed quiet most hours, a cold pebble of presence I was learning to carry the way you learn to carry a limp—by refusing to let it change your walk more than it must. Sometimes at night it murmured things. My name, sometimes, in a voice like dry paper. Arguments, other times, beautifully reasoned ones, about why the Veil should never have been shattered, or why it should be reforged, whichever suited the hour. A prisoner with a century to practice persuasion is a dangerous thing, and I had become his cell.

At the fire, Sera caught me looking and crossed the courtyard to stand beside me. Heat washed over us both.

"Ashen Order scouts were sighted at the northern pass yesterday," she said quietly. "Two riders, grey cloaks. Watching, not approaching. Vey's regrouping. By summer there will be factions here—his, and whatever the temple remnant becomes, and everyone else choosing sides." She flexed the fingers of her good hand. "You should know the Order hasn't abandoned the idea of using you. Vey doesn't abandon instruments. He shelves them."

"Let him shelve me. I'm not a tool anymore. I'm the box."

Something moved across her face—fear for me, quickly mastered. "That's not comfort, Elodie. Boxes get opened."

* * *

The rebuilding went faster than anyone expected, mostly because it went smaller. Without the temple's grand architecture to imitate, people built low stone houses with wide doors, a meeting hall with no altar in it, a school with windows on every side—"so the children can see out," Hessa insisted, and nobody argued. The valley had a hundred years of quarried stone stacked in ruined walls. We built with the debris of the old lie, which felt appropriate.

What we argued about was the haven question. Riders came within the fortnight: refugees from beyond the old boundary, mages and mageless alike, drawn by rumor that magic had returned and by rumor that a valley existed where neither kind ruled the other. Some of them frightened the valley folk badly—a woman who lit her cooking fire with a snap of her fingers caused three separate households to sleep elsewhere that night. Old habits. A century of being taught that power was the enemy, and the teaching doesn't burn as fast as ledgers do.

So we wrote rules. I drafted them myself, at a table in the new meeting hall, with Sera dictating clauses from her sickbed and Cassian arguing every one of them on principle, because that was his penance and his gift both: he would never again let me write anything unchallenged.

No one shall be indentured for debt. No one shall be punished for curiosity. Magic may be practiced openly but never compelled upon the unwilling. No faction's law supersedes the valley's while its guests are here. And the last one, the one I added against everyone's advice:

Power shall be held only by those who agree to be watched holding it.

"That rule is aimed at yourself," Cassian observed, reading over my shoulder.

"Yes."

"And you think the others will hold you to it?"

"I think if they don't, we've already failed, and better to find out in year one than year fifty."

He was quiet a long time. Then he signed it below my name, in his careful abbot's hand, as witness. One by one, over the following weeks, so did the others. Hessa pressed her thumbprint in ink. A quarryman named Dorran signed with an X and grinned about it. Even the fire-fingered mage woman signed, after reading every clause twice.

The valley didn't have a name for itself yet, other than Vell, which suddenly seemed too small a word. But it had rules now, and a door, and—for the first time in a hundred years—no lock.

* * *

On a gray morning six weeks after the Shattering, I climbed to the boundary stone.

The road past it was open now—no wardens, no veil of dead air, just a cart track winding north toward the pass where Vey's watchers had been seen. Magic hummed everywhere along the ridge, thin and wild and newly returned, like sap rising in a tree cut down too long ago. I felt it press against my skin and slide off. The Veilbearer's burden included a strange immunity: the returning magic of the world flowed around me without entering, because the space inside me was occupied. A prison cannot also be a guesthouse.

Sera was already there when I reached the stone, standing with her sling off and her arm moving carefully at her side, testing it. She'd walked up alone. The healers would have scolded her. She'd clearly decided not to inform them.

"You're leaving," I said. Not a question. Her pack leaned against the stone, buckled shut, provisioned.

"My Order expects a report. If I don't give one, they send someone worse than me." She looked out at the pass. "And there's something I have to go back and take from Vey—the ritual work he stole to breach the valley. While it exists, he can do what he did again, anywhere there's a seal. And there are other seals, Elodie. Lesser ones. Older ones. The world is full of locked rooms, and some of them shouldn't be opened, and none of them should be opened by him." She turned to face me. Wind pulled at her hair. "Also, I owe the Ashen Order a betrayal, and I intend to deliver it personally."

"When?"

"As soon as my arm agrees to cooperate. Another week. Maybe two."

I nodded and looked at the stone. The Ashgrove name was still carved there, deep and weathered, my ancestors' signature on a century of stolen sky. I'd thought about chiseling it off. I'd decided against it. You don't sand down the record of a crime; you build the court next door to it.

"Sera." I made myself say it plainly, because she deserved plain words and I'd spent a lifetime learning to hide inside script. "Come back."

She was silent long enough that the wind filled the space between us twice over.

"This valley is the only place in the world," she finally said, "where I've been neither a weapon nor a fugitive. Do you understand what that's worth? I was nine years old when the Order found me. Everything I am, they built. Going back isn't courage—it's accounting." Her jaw tightened. "But coming back. Coming back would be a choice. And I've seen what happens in this story when people don't get to choose."

"Then choose slowly," I said. "The door stays unlocked. That's rather the point of everything we burned."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, the first I'd heard from her since the undercroft. Then she sobered, stepped close, and pressed her forehead briefly to mine, an exile's farewell, older than any country either of us belonged to.

"One thing, before I go," she murmured. "The whisper. Last night, when I passed your window—you were talking in your sleep. Arguing. In two voices." Her eyes searched mine. "Who won?"

I told her the truth, because she of all people had earned it. "Neither. That's how I know I'm still winning. The day it goes quiet in there—" I touched my sternum, where the cold pebble sat. "The day it stops trying to convince me is the day it's stopped needing to. As long as it argues, I'm still the jailer. When it starts agreeing with me—" I shrugged. "Then somebody had better come back and check on me."

She held my gaze. "Count on it."

* * *

She left at dawn two days later, walking north with her pack and her crooked arm and her unbuckled sword, because she said a healer's orders were just another kind of prophecy and she'd stopped believing in those. Half the valley turned out to watch her go. Hessa pressed a loaf of new bread into her hands at the gate. Dorran saluted with his X-marked hand. Nobody asked her to stay, which was, I suspected, the only reason she might.

Afterward I climbed the hill behind the rebuilt school, where I'd begun keeping the new book—the history, not the prophecy—and I wrote the day's entry by afternoon light. *Sera departed north. Arm healing. Promised to return.* Three sentences. True ones.

Below, smoke rose from honest chimneys. In the meeting hall, Hessa was chairing the first real argument about the winter grain stores, and from the sound drifting up the hill, it was a vicious, glorious, entirely ordinary dispute among equals. Cassian was teaching letters to quarry children in the ruins of the old scriptorium, using salvaged pages, and had reportedly wept twice this week, which he denied. The world beyond the pass was arming itself—Vey in the north, temple remnants in the south, a hundred small ambitions circling a valley that had chosen, improbably, to be a door with no lock.

And behind my ribs, faint as a draft under a door, the prisoner stirred and began, patiently, persuasively, to argue that the grain stores were mismanaged, that Hessa was a fool, that I should really go down and take charge—

"No," I said aloud, to him, to the hills, to whoever might someday need to hear it. "They get to choose."

I dipped my pen. The blank page accepted the ink.

Far to the north, past the boundary stone, past the pass where two grey-cloaked riders still watched, a signal fire answered another signal fire—and I marked it, in my history, as the place where the next chapter would begin.
