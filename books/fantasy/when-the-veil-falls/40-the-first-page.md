## 40. The First Page

The ink froze in the well of the pen twice before I managed a single word.

Not because it was cold—though the mornings had turned cold since the Shattering, as if the world were remembering what seasons were for—but because my hand shook every time I sat down to write. A scribe's hand does not shake. For eleven years I had copied scripture in a room that smelled of tallow and dust, my letters so uniform that Brother Cassian once joked I could have been replaced by a printing press and no one would know the difference. That hand was steady through debt and grief and the Inquisitor's questions. It shook only now, when the writing finally mattered.

The word I managed was *Vell*.

I set it down at the top of the first page of a new book—a real book, bound in undyed goatskin by a widow named Petra who had lost her husband to Vey's acolytes and taken up binding to keep her hands from remembering other work—and I looked at it for a long time. One word. The name of a valley that had spent a century believing itself the whole world.

Behind me, through the open door of the scriptorium—which was no longer a scriptorium but something the survivors had started calling, without ceremony or vote, the *record house*—the sounds of the morning came up the hill: hammers, saws, someone laughing. Laughing. Three months ago the only laughter in Vell had been the brittle kind, the kind you made when the temple warden walked past and you wanted to seem content. This was different. Rougher. Realer. The sound of people who had decided, collectively and without discussion, that they would go on.

I dipped the pen again. My hand steadied—not all at once, but the way a fever breaks, in degrees, until you notice it's gone.

*In the hundredth year of the Veil,* I wrote, *a scribe found his own name in a book that should not have existed.*

It wasn't a good sentence. It was probably, if I was honest, a terrible one—the kind of opening line that a century from now some student would underline in irritation and annotate, *vague; whose Veil? why should it not exist?* But it was true, and it was mine, and for the first time in my life the words on the page belonged to me instead of to the temple, the debt ledger, or a prophecy written by hands I would never identify. The old prophecy book lay closed on the corner of the desk, wrapped in oilcloth. Its last line had stopped changing the night the seal broke. *The Veilbearer chooses.* Past tense now. Done. Whatever force had moved through those pages—whether it was the tyrant's leaking will, or the ritual's residue, or something older and stranger than both—had spent itself against my choice and gone quiet. I kept it anyway. You don't burn the map just because the road is behind you.

Sera found me there an hour later, the way she always did: without knocking, without apology, with two chipped clay cups and the particular expression of a woman about to say something she'd rehearsed.

"You've written four lines," she said, reading over my shoulder with the shamelessness she had never once tried to correct. "In an hour."

"Five," I said, and wrote a sixth out of spite while she watched.

She laughed—that low, surprised laugh she still seemed to find embarrassing—and set the cups down. Outside, the light came slantwise through the repaired window, catching the dust motes, and I thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that dust could look beautiful once you'd seen a city burn. Perspective, Cassian called it. He'd been saying a lot of things like that lately, small aphorisms delivered with the careful lightness of a man testing whether he was still allowed to speak.

"I leave tomorrow," Sera said.

My pen stopped mid-stroke. I hadn't expected it—I had, in fact, spent three months quietly assuming the opposite—and hearing it aloud landed somewhere under my ribs like a dropped stone. "Back to the Order?"

"What's left of it." She turned her cup in her hands. She had a scar now along her forearm where Vey's acolyte had opened her on the undercroft stairs, and I watched her thumb find it the way she always did when she talked about that night. "Half of them scattered when Vey retreated. Half of the rest want to follow him into whatever hole he's crawled into. And maybe twenty—" she shrugged, "—maybe twenty are like me. People who signed on to restore magic to the world and woke up one morning serving a man who wanted to own it." Her eyes came up to mine. "Someone has to make sure the Order becomes a school instead of a weapon. Or dies trying."

"And you're the someone."

"I'm the someone." She said it without false modesty, which is one of the things I loved about her. Sera had never once pretended to be smaller than she was. "You could come," she added, too casually, which meant she'd rehearsed this part too. "Beyond the boundary. There are libraries out there, Elodie. Whole cities of them. Archives that survived the war. Everything your temple burned, everything the Order hoarded—it exists, and no one has ever tried to gather it honestly. No one with your—" she gestured at the page, the ink, the whole stubborn apparatus of me, "—your compulsion."

"It's not a compulsion. It's a discipline."

"It's a compulsion. You alphabetize your thoughts. I've heard you do it."

I almost said yes. I want to be honest about that, here, in the record, because the whole point of this book is honesty even when it costs—*especially* when it costs, Maren used to say before she died, though she said it about confessions extracted under lamplight, which is a different thing entirely. I almost said yes. The outer world was vast and wounded and waking, full of magic returning like blood to a numb limb, and every instinct I had developed since crossing the boundary stone wanted to run toward it, to see all of it, to stand in the ruins of the Sundering and understand everything.

But I looked out the window instead. Down the hill, where the valley lay in its morning light—the terraced fields going green again, the quarry where my father died now half-dismantled, its stones being carted off to rebuild the eastern houses; the temple itself a black skeleton on the hill above town, which we had voted, after long argument, to leave standing exactly as it burned, as a warning rather than a home. Children were running along the aqueduct path, chasing something, shrieking. One of them stumbled, fell, got up laughing. Behind them, faint as breath on glass, the air shimmered—the first wild magic of the valley, threading through the grass where the Veil had been, thin and untrained and free.

"They'll need someone here," I said. "When the factions come. And they're coming, Sera. Vey isn't finished. The temple loyalists in the southern provinces aren't finished. Sooner or later everyone is going to remember that this valley sits on top of the most important grave in the world, and they're going to come here with arguments, and armies, and very persuasive pamphlets." I tapped the page. "And when they tell these people their history, it will be a story designed to make them kneel. Unless someone has already told them the truth. Boringly. Thoroughly. With citations."

"With citations," Sera repeated, and something in her face softened. "God help the future."

"Something like that."

She drank her tea. We sat in the particular silence that had grown between us over the winter—not empty but full, the silence of two people who had held a stairway and a burning archive together and no longer needed to explain themselves. Then she stood, and kissed the top of my head like an annoying older sister, and said, "Five lines an hour. At that rate the book will be done when we're dead."

"That's roughly the plan," I said. "History should take longer than prophecy. Prophecy only has to scare you. History has to survive being checked."

She paused at the door. "I'll send copies of everything the Order archives. Every fragment. Even the ones that make us look bad."

"*Especially* the ones that make you look bad."

"Yes, fine, especially those." And then, quieter, at the threshold, not turning around: "For what it's worth—I'm glad you're staying. Someone has to guard the grave." She raised a hand without looking back and was gone down the hill, her stride long, her pack already heavy, toward the boundary and the world beyond it.

* * *

Cassian came at dusk.

He still came at dusk, always, as though daylight required a permission he hadn't yet granted himself. He stood in the doorway of the record house with his hands folded in his sleeves and waited until I looked up, and I felt the familiar ache of seeing him—older, thinner, grayer at the temples than he'd been a year ago, as if the confession had aged him faster than the year warranted.

"The ledgers are done," he said. "The last of them. Petra's son and the Aldermans' girl finished the northern holdings this afternoon. Everything owed to the temple, everything owed by it—all of it entered into the new register, reconciled, and then—" he smiled, and it was a strange smile, half grief, "—burned. On the temple steps. There was quite a crowd."

"Was there singing?"

"There was drinking. Singing came later, badly." He came in and lowered himself into the chair across from me, the one that had become his by habit, and looked at the page in front of me for a moment before speaking again. "May I ask what you're writing about today?"

"Today? Today I'm writing about a blank page." I turned the book toward him. "A page in a prophecy book, a hundred years old, that refused ink for a century. That refused *my* ink, specifically, on the night everything started. I've been trying to figure out why."

"And have you?"

"I have a theory." I dipped the pen. "I think the page wasn't refusing ink. I think it was waiting for the right question. The prophecy asked *what will happen*. What will happen is a tyrant's question—it wants the future pinned like a moth, so it can be owned. A page can't answer that honestly, because the future isn't fixed; it's chosen. So it stayed blank. Blankness was the honest answer." I touched the nib to the paper in front of me, and the words flowed, ordinary black ink, obedient, alive. "This book asks *what happened*, and *what did it cost*, and *who decided*. Those questions have answers. They're just hard ones, and they take a long time to write down."

Cassian was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way it always was now, each sentence set down like a man stepping onto ice he had personally cracked.

"I read the entry about myself," he said. "In your draft. Chapter eleven, you called it—*the mentor's guilt surfaces.* You were generous."

"I was accurate."

"You wrote that I refused to kill her." His jaw worked. "Write the rest of it, Elodie. Write that I didn't warn her either. That I told myself silence was mercy. That I let her walk into the Inquisitor's office the next morning believing she was safe, because I was a coward who wanted to keep my hands clean and my position warm. Ilse died because I refused to act and refused to speak, and both of those were refusals, and the prophecy was right about me in every particular." He met my eyes. "Forgive me if you choose—I think perhaps you already have, and I'm grateful beyond words—but do not absolve me in the record. Let it stand as it was. Some other young man in some other valley may need to see what silence costs."

I looked at him across the desk—at the man who had taught me letters, lied to me gently, confessed to me terribly, and carried a dead candidate's name for forty years—and I understood that this, too, was part of the inheritance I'd reclaimed. Not the power. Not the whisper coiled sleeping beneath my breastbone, the tyrant's essence bound in my blood, the prison I had volunteered to become. *This.* The insistence that truth be recorded whole, unflattered, unsentimental. Cassian had failed Ilse by editing himself. I would not edit him.

"I'll write it as you said it," I promised. "All of it."

"Thank you." He rose, paused at the door as Sera had, and for a moment I thought he wouldn't say what he clearly wanted to say. Then: "There's a girl in the village—Mara Ostrow, the cooper's daughter, twelve years old. She pulled a spark out of the hearth fire last week and made it sit on her palm like a tame bird. First natural-born talent in the valley's history." He smiled, and this time the smile reached his eyes. "Her mother doesn't know whether to be proud or terrified. I told her neither. I told her to bring the girl to the record house when she's ready."

"Why the record house?"

"Because whoever teaches her should start by teaching her what happened the last time people with power refused to write anything down." He nodded once, formally, the old Cassian gesture suddenly restored, and went out into the blue evening.

I sat for a long time after that, listening to the house settle, feeling the faint double pulse inside me—mine, and the other one, slow and cold and patient, the prisoner I carried. Some nights it stirred and murmured things in a voice like reason, like regret, like lies dressed in their best clothes. Some nights it was silent as a sealed tomb. Tonight it was silent, and I took a certain grim comfort in the fact that I had grown able to tell the difference.

* * *

I worked until the candle guttered, and near the end, without quite deciding to, I turned back to the first page and reread the opening lines. Five lines an hour, Sera had said. But the last line of the day had come easily, the way truth sometimes does when you stop performing it:

*A century ago, twelve families sealed the Veil and called it protection. Eleven of them believed it. The twelfth knew better, and paid for the knowing with a prison, and his descendants paid with a debt no ledger ever named. This book is the payment coming due. It is not a prophecy. Nothing in it will happen unless someone chooses to make it happen. That is the whole point. That is the only point.*

I set down the pen and blew out the candle and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the valley breathe.

Then I went back, struck a light, and wrote one more line at the bottom of the first page—the dedication, though I didn't know that's what it was until the words were already down:

*For Mara Ostrow, and for everyone born after the lie: the page is yours now. It accepts your ink.*

The ink dried black and clean and permanent, and the page took it, and kept it, and asked for more.
