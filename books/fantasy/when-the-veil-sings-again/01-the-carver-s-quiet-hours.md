## 1. The Carver's Quiet Hours

The bone told Wendell everything before she ever touched the knife.

It was a river bone—cattle femur, bought honest from Marta's slaughterhouse up the lane—and it had spent its second life soaking in water that wasn't clean. She could smell the silt in it. That was the first wrong thing, and she noticed it before she'd even lit her third lamp, because good carving began with noticing and ended with knowing when to stop.

Outside, Thornfell was doing what Thornfell did on a market-eve: drinking too loud, arguing about tolls, and pretending the mountains to the north were just mountains. Inside her workshop above the chandler's, the air smelled of beeswax and scraped marrow and the faint mineral ghost of all the bones she'd ever worked. It was, Wendell had long ago decided, the only honest smell in town.

She turned the femur under the lamp. Oiled it once more. Let her thumb find the place where the grain ran true.

Then she picked up her finest blade—the one with the whale-ivory handle she'd traded six months of quiet work for—and began.

The name went first, because names always went first: **OSKAR VELL**, in letters deep enough to survive a century of handling but shallow enough not to crack the shaft. Below it, the dates of his short, unlucky life. Below that, the line every drowned man's family wanted and no family could afford twice:

*He did not suffer.*

Wendell carved that lie with particular care. It was, in her experience, the most requested sentence in Thornfell, and the least often true.

"He suffered," said Oskar Vell, from the chair by the window he had no business occupying, "considerably."

Wendell didn't flinch. Flinching encouraged them.

He looked as he'd looked pulled from the Graywater three days ago—bloated at the jaw, hair full of weed—but ghosts settled quickly into their best versions of themselves if you let them talk, and Oskar was already improving by the minute. The swelling had gone down since yesterday. His coat had dried. He'd found a comb somewhere, or believed he had.

"The Graywater," he said again. "You've put the Graywater on it."

"I haven't put anything on it yet," Wendell murmured, and kept her knife moving through the flourish beneath the dates. A curl of bone spiraled off onto the bench. She swept it aside without looking.

"You have. I can see it from here. You're going to write *drowned in the Graywater*." He leaned forward, and his voice took on the aggrieved edge of a man who had been complaining about this since the moment his lungs filled. "I didn't drown in the Graywater. I drowned in the *Sere*. Upstream, past the old weir, where the Sere runs fast and cold and nobody fishes because of the—"

"Because of the tollhouse," Wendell said quietly.

"Because of the tollhouse." He nodded, satisfied, then remembered to be dead about it. "Nobody will know that. Nobody will ever know that. My own sister will stand over this—" he jabbed a finger at the femur, which could not see him, "—and read *Graywater*, and she'll think I died like an idiot, hauling nets off a sandbar, instead of dying like a professional."

"And how did you die like a professional?"

Oskar's mouth opened. Closed. The bloat came back a little around his jaw, the way it always did when a ghost got close to the truth of their own ending.

"That," he said, with dignity, "is between me and the river."

Which meant smuggling, which meant the tollhouse, which meant he'd been running something north across the Sere at night and the Sere had caught him at it. Wendell knew. She'd known within a breath of finishing the commission, because his ghost had walked in behind his grieving sister and stood dripping on her floorboards while the woman wept and asked for a memorial piece, something fine, something that would last, and Oskar had spent the entire visit saying *don't tell her about the cargo, don't tell her about the cargo* until Wendell had wanted to carve the words into her own skull to make room for quieter things.

That was the work. That was the whole shape of it.

Everyone in Thornfell thought Wendell Lockridge was the best bone-carver in three valleys and the worst conversationalist in four. They brought her their grief and she gave it back to them in polished ivory and bleached femur, and they paid her fairly, and they left her alone, and none of them—not the chandler below, not Marta at the slaughterhouse, not the priest who commissioned ossuary panels and never asked why hers were so uncannily tender—knew that every piece she finished came with a passenger.

The dead talked. They talked constantly. They talked to her the way weather talks to a roof, indifferent to whether she wanted to hear it, and she had learned very young that there was exactly one rule worth building a life on:

Never repeat what the dead say aloud.

Not to comfort. Not to warn. Not to prove, if proof were ever demanded of her, that she wasn't mad. Her mother had taught her the rule with a switch and then with silence, and her mother's own ghost had confirmed it years later, standing at the foot of her bed on the anniversary of the fever, saying, *Good girl, good girl, you kept your mouth shut, it's the only door that locks.*

Wendell had been eleven when she understood that her gift was also a target. Towns that burned women for reading cards would do far worse for a woman who heard the murdered naming their murderers. So she'd built herself into a narrow, careful life: a workshop with good locks, a trade that required silence, a reputation for dullness worn like armor. She spoke little. She listened enormously. And every night she sat alone with the bones and the lamplight and the accumulated company of everyone Thornfell had ever lost, and she called that discipline, because calling it loneliness was unbearable.

"Fine," Oskar was saying. "Fine. Put the Graywater. See if I care. I'm dead, it's not like the river matters now."

"It clearly matters."

"It matters *professionally*." He paced to the window and back, trailing a faint smell of river-bottom. "Twenty-two years I ran the Sere without a scratch. Twenty-two. And the one time I take the northern crossing in spring melt—because *Bramble* swears the toll's been bribed and we can save a whole day—"

He stopped. Wendell's knife stopped too, a half-hair's width from gouging the polish.

"Bramble," she repeated. Carefully. As though the name meant nothing. As though Captain Bramble of the *Kestrel*, smuggler, sometime client, occasional rescuer of Wendell's more delicate shipments, hadn't just been named by a dead man as the reason he was dead.

Oskar's ghost looked at her with sudden, terrible attention. Dead men noticed things living people couldn't be bothered to. "You know her."

"Everyone knows the captain of the Kestrel."

"Not like that." He drifted closer, and the lamplight passed through him and lit nothing, which never stopped being horrible. "You went still. Carvers go still when they hit a flaw. You went still when I said her name." His voice dropped, and for the first time since he'd walked into her workshop, it sounded frightened rather than annoyed. "Who are you, Wendell Lockridge?"

"A carver," she said. "Finish your story or hold your peace. The Graywater stays either way. Your sister paid for the Graywater."

"My sister is paying for a lie."

"Your sister," Wendell said, setting down the knife at last and meeting the dead man's eyes, "is paying for a kindness. There's a difference, and it's the only difference my trade deals in."

He stared at her. Outside, a drunk in the street below was singing something about a girl from the low country, badly. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the walls the house settled the way old houses do, with small cracking sounds like knuckles.

And Oskar Vell—smuggler, drowned man, ghost of three days' standing—did the thing almost none of them did.

He stopped talking about himself.

"You hear all of us," he said slowly. "Don't you. Every one. All these years. The whole graveyard's worth." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, thin and amazed. "All those funerals. All those memorials. Everyone in this town has a piece of your work on their mantel, and half of them are talking to you every night, and you just—you sit here and take dictation from the dead and carve lies for the living."

"Yes," Wendell said.

"Why?"

The question landed harder than any accusation. She looked down at the femur, at OSKAR VELL half-finished, the flourish waiting for the river that wasn't his river, and she heard herself answer honestly for the first time in twenty years, to a man who couldn't carry the answer anywhere.

"Because someone should remember you correctly," she said. "Even if I never say it out loud. Even if the bone says otherwise. Someone knows."

Oskar was quiet for a long moment. Then he sat down in his chair by the window—the one he'd claimed the first night, the one no one else could see—and he said, softly, "The Sere, then. Past the old weir. Put it somewhere small, under the flourish, where only a fool would look."

"I can't."

"I know you can't." He smiled, and for a moment he was just a young man with wet hair, tired, done with arguing. "But I'll know. And now you'll know better than anyone alive." He paused. "There's a cargo still in the reeds past the weir. Small chest, sealed, wax over the lock. If it's ever found—if some fisherman hooks it up—tell Bramble it goes to no one. Not the Crown. Not the guilds. No one. Burn it unread."

"I don't speak for the dead," Wendell said. It was her oldest sentence, her truest one, and it tasted like ash every time.

"No," Oskar agreed. "But you listen. That's rarer."

She picked the knife back up. She carved the Graywater, deep and beautiful, exactly as paid. And beneath the flourish, in strokes so fine they'd read as texture to any eye but a carver's, she added four letters that were not a river and not a name and would mean nothing to anyone who ever held the piece: *S-R-W*, for the Sere, past the weir, for the one listener in a century who would understand.

"There," Oskar said, satisfied at last. He stood, and he was already fading at the edges, the way they did when their unfinished business got finished enough. "You'll want to watch yourself, carver. Word is there's a scholar come up the valley road asking after passage north. Asking odd questions. Old-book kind of questions." He was nearly gone now, just a voice and a suggestion of wet wool. "Questions about Vael."

The word went through the workshop like cold water through cloth.

Vael. The kingdom that wasn't on any map. The massacre no history admitted to. The name mothers used to hush children and scholars used to end careers. Wendell had grown up hearing the dead whisper fragments of it—snatches of a court song, a woman screaming a title no one held anymore, old men reciting borders that existed nowhere—and she had spent thirty years filing every scrap of it under *never, never, never*.

"They're asking," Oskar's voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, thin as smoke, "who carves bones in Thornfell. Who listens."

The lamp burned low. Wendell set the finished piece on the sill to cure in the cold, OSKAR VELL gleaming faintly, the false river bright in the moonlight and the true one hidden beneath the flourish where only she would grieve it.

Below, the chandler's dog began to bark at the empty street.

Wendell blew out the lamp, and in the dark, listening to the town settle and the dead murmur their thousand overlapping dreams, she heard hooves on the valley road. More than one rider. Slow, deliberate, the pace of people who wanted to be heard coming.

One set of hooves belonged to a tired mule carrying books.

The others belonged to soldiers.

Wendell stood in her dark workshop, surrounded by the patient dead, and reached for the rule that had kept her alive for thirty years—*never say it aloud*—and understood, with the clarity of the drowning, that by morning, someone was going to ask her a question she could not carve her way out of.
