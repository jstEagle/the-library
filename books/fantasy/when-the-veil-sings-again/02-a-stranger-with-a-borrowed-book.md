## 2. A Stranger with a Borrowed Book

The dead of Thornfell kept banker's hours, which was one of the few mercies Wendell had ever found in her gift. They came at dusk and lingered past midnight, murmuring in the corners of her workshop like customers waiting for a fitting, and by morning they were gone—drifted back to whatever held them, leaving only their requests behind.

So it was a strange morning indeed when she woke to a voice that did not belong to any of them.

*She's here,* it said. Not a ghost's voice at all—a living voice, pitched low, coming through the shuttered window from the street below. *Thornfell. Finally.*

Wendell lay still beneath her blanket and listened the way she listened to everything: without moving, without breathing harder than necessary, without giving anything away. Even alone in her own bed, even with no one watching, she listened like a woman who had spent thirty years pretending to be furniture.

Below, cart wheels ground over cobbles. A mule complained. And then footsteps—deliberate ones, the kind that belonged to someone used to long roads and short tempers—crossing her street toward the inn.

She told herself it was none of her business. Strangers came through Thornfell all the time, bound for the mines or the passes or simply lost. She finished her tea. She dressed. She picked up yesterday's carving—the memorial for Tam Voss, drowned smuggler, whose ghost had spent three nights complaining about the wrong river—and set it on the workbench beside her tools.

And then, because she was weak, because she was curious, because after thirty years of discipline a person will crack over something small as a dropped stitch—she opened her shutters and looked down at the street.

A woman stood outside the Drowned Bell, talking to Old Sefa, who ran the inn and knew everyone's business within an hour of them knowing it themselves. The stranger was tall, travel-worn, with ink-stained fingers and the particular posture of someone carrying something heavy in a satchel—not coin-heavy, but *important*-heavy. Her coat was good quality, patched at the elbows. Her boots were wrong for mountains. Scholar's boots, Wendell thought. City boots.

Sefa was pointing up the street, toward the pass road, and shaking her head.

The stranger said something Wendell couldn't catch. Sefa shook her head again, more firmly.

Then the stranger turned, and looked directly up at Wendell's window.

Not at the window. At *her*. As if she'd known exactly which shutter would open, exactly which face would be looking down.

Wendell stepped back so fast she knocked her elbow against the wall. Her heart did something undignified. She stood in the middle of her dark workshop, listening to her own blood, and waited for the feeling of being seen to fade.

It didn't fade.

* * *

The trouble with being invisible your whole life is that you get very little practice at handling the opposite.

Wendell went about her morning as she always did: water drawn from the communal pump, bread from Marta's stall, a nod to the ferryman, no conversations longer than four words. The town let her pass the way weather passes. Thirty years of careful nothing had made her a fixture, like the leaning fence post by the mill—present, unremarkable, unremarked upon.

But today the town kept glancing at her.

"Carver," said Marta, sliding her the usual loaf. "You hear anything about the scholar come in last night?"

"No."

"They say she's Crown-touched. Got letters and seals." Marta lowered her voice with relish. "Says she's going up into the high passes. Alone. In *autumn*."

"Bold," Wendell said, which was two words, well within budget.

"She asked after guides. Asked after—" Marta hesitated, enjoying herself. "Asked after bone-carvers, would you believe. Said she needed one who worked *memorials*. I told her there's only you."

There it was. The crack in the day. Wendell felt it open under her feet like river ice.

"What did you tell her where I live?"

"Didn't have to. Everyone knows the carver's on Tanner's Row." Marta frowned at her. "You look peaked, Wendell Lockridge. Eat something."

She ate nothing. She walked home fast, telling herself that a scholar asking after memorials meant nothing—that travelers wanted gravestones cut all the time, that names were names, that the dead were hers alone and no city woman with patched elbows could possibly—

The knock came at midday.

Three raps, evenly spaced. Patient. Wendell stood in her workshop holding a chisel and considered, briefly, not answering. But silence from the bone-carver's house would be noted; silence was its own kind of answer in a small town, and she had spent decades making sure her silences read as shyness rather than secrecy.

She opened the door.

The stranger was taller up close, gray at her temples, with the sunburned, wind-scoured look of someone who had been traveling for months. Her eyes were the problem. They went over Wendell's face like a reader scanning a page, unhurried, thorough, and Wendell felt every year of her careful invisibility examined and found legible anyway.

"Mistress Lockridge." The woman's accent was southern capital, softened by distance. "Ilsa Verrow. I'm told you're the finest memorial carver between here and the coast."

"I carve what people bring me."

"Yes." Verrow smiled faintly. "That's rather what I'm counting on."

She didn't ask to come in. She simply waited, and Wendell—hating herself, hating curiosity most of all—stepped aside.

Inside, the scholar's eyes moved across the workshop with the same thorough attention. The hanging racks of finished pieces. The raw antler and donated bone in their barrels. The half-finished Voss memorial, the drowned man's name curling into waves along its border. Wendell watched her look and felt oddly naked, the way she imagined other people might feel if someone read their letters aloud.

"You work quickly," Verrow said. "Or recently, at least. This one's still fresh."

"Drowned man. His family wanted it done before winter."

"And did his family choose the river?"

Wendell went very still. "What?"

"The border inscription." Verrow touched the edge of the carving lightly, not quite touching the waves. "*Taken by the Graywater.* Is that correct?"

"It's what they paid for."

"Mm," said Verrow, in a tone that suggested she had already interviewed the ghost, which was impossible, and filed the impossibility neatly away for later.

She set her satchel on the workbench. It landed heavier than a book should land—or rather, with a particular kind of weight, the weight of something old, the way a century-old beam settles differently than new timber. She unwrapped it from oilcloth, layer after layer, with the care of someone uncovering a wound.

The book underneath was black leather gone gray with age, its corners worn round, its spine stitched twice with newer thread. Small. Hand-sized. The kind of book a person writes for themselves, not for an audience.

"My family has kept this for a hundred years," Verrow said. "I've brought it a very long way. I need passage into the high passes, and I'm told the caravans won't go this late, and the smugglers who might are lying low because of the Inquisitor on the road south."

Everyone in Thornfell knew about the Inquisitor. Nobody said his name. Saying it was thought to be like whistling in an avalanche field.

"So I find myself in need," Verrow continued, "of people who know roads that aren't roads. And of a carver, because the book says—" She stopped. Something crossed her face that Wendell recognized, because she wore it daily: the look of a person deciding how much truth a stranger can carry without dropping it.

"The book says what?" Wendell asked. Her voice came out flat. Careful. Four words or fewer, Wendell.

Verrow opened the book.

The pages were dense, cramped script, faded to brown, with marginalia in at least three different hands added over the decades—generations of readers arguing with the author in ink. Verrow turned past several pages with practiced ease, clearly knowing exactly where she was going, and stopped.

"There's a prophecy here," she said. "Written a century ago by a woman named Sera Verrow. My ancestor. Most of it is... rambling, frankly. Visions mixed with grocery lists, some of it beautiful, much of it mad. But one passage has always been treated as load-bearing. My family built its whole understanding of itself around it." She turned the book around so Wendell could see.

Wendell looked.

The page showed a hand-drawn sketch first: a doorway in a mountainside, rendered in quick confident strokes, with figures gathered before it—some upright, some kneeling, some that seemed halfway between. Below it, the prophecy itself, in tight brown letters:

*When the veil sings again, the last door shall stand open one night only, and the carver who hears shall stand before it, and the dead shall know their hour.*

And beneath that, smaller, squeezed against the bottom margin in the same hand but fresher ink, darker:

*Borne in Thornfell. Named Lockridge. Come when the frost takes the rowan. She will be sought. Let no crown find her first.*

Tonight was the night the frost took the rowan tree on Tanner's Row. Wendell knew this because she had watched it happen that morning, the tree's leaves silvered and curling, and had thought nothing of it except that winter was early this year.

Her name was on the page. Tonight's date was on the page. Written a century ago by a dead woman, in a book carried a thousand miles by a stranger standing in her workshop.

Wendell did what she had trained her whole life to do.

Nothing visible.

"Prophecies are cheap in border towns," she said. "Half the fortune-tellers on the coast road claim a famous grandmother."

"I know." Verrow closed the book gently. "And I'll confess I came expecting to be disappointed. A hundred years of hoping makes a person cautious." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and sorry and absolutely certain. "But I watched you just now, Mistress Lockridge. You read that page faster than I can. You read it *sideways*, the way people read things they already know. And when you saw your own name, you didn't go pale, and you didn't laugh. You went *still*. Like prey. Or like someone who has been waiting to be found and dreads both outcomes equally."

The workshop was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls—in the old bones of the house itself—Wendell heard a faint shifting, the way the dead shifted when something important was happening, gathering like spectators at a trial.

"I think you should leave," Wendell said.

"I think you heard my great-great-grandmother's voice before I ever opened that door," Verrow said quietly. "I think you've been hearing the dead of this valley your whole life, and telling no one, because telling no one is the only thing that's ever kept you safe. And I think tonight, for one night, that stops working."

Every instinct Wendell owned screamed at her to deny it. Deny it, close the door, return to her chisel and her careful nothing, let the prophecy rot in its margins, let the scholar walk into the passes alone and freeze like scholars did.

Instead she heard herself say: "Even if any of that were true, why would I help you? What's in it for me?"

Relief broke across Verrow's face like dawn—careless, unguarded, the relief of a woman who had been braced for failure for a thousand miles. "Because the dead of Vael have been waiting a hundred years," she said, "and you're the only living soul who can hear what they're waiting *for*."

Vael.

The name went through Wendell like cold water through cloth. Because she *had* heard it—whispers in the high passes when she was a child, fragments from dead travelers on roads that shouldn't exist, a word that surfaced again and again among the restless dead of the mountains like a stone in a streambed: *Vael, Vael, home, the door, home.*

She had never told anyone. She had never even told herself, really. She had folded it away with everything else.

"I don't know anything about Vael," Wendell lied.

It was, she would reflect later, the second lie she'd told in Verrow's presence, and far more costly than the first.

Verrow studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, rewrapped the book with those same careful hands, and rose.

"Then I've wasted your morning, and I apologize. I'll seek passage elsewhere." She paused at the door, satchel on her shoulder, and looked back. "For what it's worth, Mistress Lockridge—my ancestor's margin note says *let no crown find her first*. There's an Inquisitor on the south road who burned a smuggler for less than what you just hid behind your eyes. Whatever you're carrying, whoever you've been—tonight the frost takes the rowan either way. I'd rather it found you as a friend."

She left. Her footsteps faded down Tanner's Row, deliberate as ever, toward the Drowned Bell and whatever negotiations awaited there.

Wendell stood in her workshop a long time.

Then, slowly, she picked up the Voss memorial and set it down again, and went to her window, and looked out at the rowan tree, silver with frost, dropping its leaves one by one onto the stones.

In the walls, in the floor, in the bones of the old house, the dead had gone quiet—all of them, every lingering whisper, every patient murmurer, silent as a held breath. Listening with her. Waiting with her.

And beneath the silence, faint as a memory of a memory, rising from somewhere north and high and impossibly far away, she heard it for the first time in years:

singing.

* * *

She lasted until dusk. It was, she decided, a respectable interval.

The lie sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Wendell had told lies before—the small social lies of an invisible woman, *no thank you, nothing's wrong, I didn't see*. But she had never lied about the dead. The dead were the one true thing she owned, and she had handed them over to a stranger's doubt like a coward handing over a purse.

By full dark she had her cloak on and her best chisels wrapped in oilcloth, though she could not have said, if pressed, whether she was packing to flee or to follow. Both, probably. The rowan had dropped half its leaves since noon.

She was checking her stores of tallow candles when the singing changed.

It didn't get louder. It got *closer*—the way a sound moves when its source stops being distant geography and becomes present company. Wendell froze, candle in hand, and listened as the voices resolved out of the general hum of the mountains into something with words.

*She knows,* said a voice near the window, dry as autumn. An old voice. A voice she knew.

Tam Voss stood—or stood the way ghosts stood, more suggestion than substance—in the corner of her workshop, dripping river water that wasn't there. Behind him, dimly, others: shapes in the rafters, faces at the edges of lamplight, more dead than Wendell had ever sensed gathered in one room. All of them looking at her. All of them *waiting*.

"You're supposed to complain about rivers," she said to Voss. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. "Not—whatever this is."

*Graywater's wrong,* Voss agreed comfortably. *Always was. But that's done now, girl. Done now.* He tilted his head, water streaming from nowhere. *She's still at the Bell. The book-woman. Asking her questions, getting her lies. But she'll come back to you before midnight. And there's men on the south road wearing crowns on their collars, asking questions too, and they don't stop at lies.*

The cold that went through Wendell had nothing to do with the season. "How many?"

*Enough,* said Voss, and shrugged the shrug of the dead, who measure such things differently. *Enough to matter. Enough that the book-woman needs more than a scholar's legs to get where she's going. Enough that you do too.*

"I'm not going anywhere."

*No,* Voss said, and smiled the smile of a drowned man who has finally gotten to the point. *But you're deciding to. I can hear it from here. We all can. Been listening to you decide for thirty years, Wendell Lockridge. First time we've ever liked the sound of it.*

Around him, the gathered dead made a sound—not laughter exactly, but the shape of it, the warmth of it, rustling through the rafters like wind through wheat.

Wendell looked at them—at the crowd of the patient dead filling her workshop like a congregation—and felt the first crack in her invisibility widen into something that might, eventually, become a door.

"Midnight," she repeated. "She comes back at midnight."

*If you're still here,* said Voss. *And girl—* the drowned man's grin faded into something older and graver, *—when she shows you that book again, don't lie twice to the same woman. Liars who lie twice end up belonging to somebody. And you've belonged to yourself too long to give that up cheap.*

He began to fade then, the way the dead did, thinning into the walls, into the mountain's long memory. But the last thing he left with her was not a warning but a fragment of song—one line of it, in a language she didn't speak, in a melody that made her teeth ache and her eyes burn, the same melody that was drifting down from the high passes even now, patient as snowfall.

At midnight, precisely, there came three raps at Wendell's door. Evenly spaced. Patient.

Wendell looked at her packed bag. At her wrapped chisels. At the rowan tree, bare now except for one stubborn branch, silvered and burning white in the moonlight.

She opened the door.

Ilsa Verrow stood on her step with the satchel heavy on her shoulder and snow beginning to fall behind her, and the first thing she said was: "The Inquisitor reached the tollhouse at dusk. He's asking after a book and a carver. By name."

And Wendell, who had spent thirty years perfecting the art of saying nothing, heard herself answer:

"Then we'd better talk about roads that aren't on maps."
