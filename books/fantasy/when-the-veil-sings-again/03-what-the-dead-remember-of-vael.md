## 3. What the Dead Remember of Vael

Ilsa Verrow had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years, and in all her rehearsals the bone-carver was never this quiet.

They sat in the back room of Lockridge's workshop, which smelled of tallow and marrow-dust and something older underneath—stone, maybe, or the memory of stone. The carver had lit a single lamp and turned its wick low, as if even flame were a guest to be rationed. Her tools hung on the wall behind her in careful ranks: chisels fine as needles, rasps worn smooth by decades of hands, a mallet whose handle had gone dark with oil and grip.

She was not looking at Ilsa. She was looking at the book.

Ilsa had set it on the workbench between them, wrapped in oilcloth, and watched Wendell Lockridge unwrap it the way other people approached sleeping animals. Slowly. With respect for what might wake.

"You've seen it before," Ilsa said. It wasn't quite a question.

"No." The carver's voice was low, even, the voice of someone who had practiced saying very little. "But I know the binding. Sheepskin over oak. Corner-guards of horn." A pause. "The horn is wrong, though. That's not goat."

"It's stag," Ilsa said. "Vaeli stag. Extinct now—or so we're told."

Something moved behind Wendell's eyes. Not fear exactly. Recognition, perhaps, arriving where recognition had no business being. She pulled her hands back from the book as though it had grown warm.

"Tell me why you came through my door at dusk asking about mountain passes," she said, "when any guide in Thornfell could have sold you one."

Because you are the reason I came, Ilsa thought. Because your name is written in ink that dried before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. What she said was: "Because I needed to see how you'd react to it first."

"React to what?"

"The book."

"I haven't touched the inside."

"You don't need to." Ilsa leaned forward. "You looked at the cover like you were listening to it."

The lamp hissed. Outside, Thornfell's evening sounds carried faintly through the shuttered window—a cart, a dog, the river doing its patient endless work under the bridge. Wendell Lockridge sat with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral she'd been asked not to cry at.

"I'm a carver," she said finally. "Memorial work, mostly. Names on bone. Families like something that lasts longer than wood."

"And do you ever hear anything? When you carve?"

The silence that followed was so complete Ilsa could hear her own pulse. She counted four beats of it. Five. Six.

"No," Wendell said.

* * *

Liar, Ilsa thought—not with contempt but with a strange, aching tenderness—and opened the book.

The pages were ragged at the edges now, softened by a century of handling, but the ink had been mixed with something that refused to fade: gall and iron and, according to family legend, ash from the prophet's own hearthfire. Her great-great-grandmother Maren Verrow had written it in a hand that started out furious and ended, by the final page, almost gentle. Ilsa knew every line. She had copied them out by lamplight as a girl, forbidden to, punished for it, unable to stop.

"This is the *Canticle of the Veiled Year*," she said. "Written ninety-eight years ago by Maren Verrow, who lived in Vael before Vael stopped existing. You won't find it in any library. You won't find Vael in any library either. That's rather the point."

"There's no such place as Vael," Wendell said. Flat. Automatic. The words came out with the cadence of a lesson recited in school.

"Say it again."

"There's no such—"

"Notice how easily it comes?" Ilsa turned a page. "That's what a hundred years of careful burning does. Not just the records—the word itself. Children learn the sentence before they learn why it's there. There is no such place as Vael. Seven words that cost a fortune in ink and blood to make universal." She smiled without humor. "There used to be eight sentences like it. This is the only survivor. The others were about the king, and the queen, and the mountain road, and they all died harder than this one did."

Wendell's gaze had dropped to the open page despite herself. Ilsa watched her read—watched the way her lips didn't move but her breathing changed, quickened slightly, the way a person's breathing changes when they recognize a melody they've heard in a dream.

"Read it aloud," Ilsa said. "Please. I've read it alone so many times the words have gone dead in my mouth. I want to hear them in someone else's voice."

"I don't read other people's books aloud."

"It isn't other people's. Not entirely. But very well—I'll read." And she did, because dread dressed as scholarship was still the only garment she owned, and she wore it well after eleven years of practice:

"'In the two-hundredth year of the Kingdom of Vael, in the month of Falling Leaves, the court of King Aldous the Patient was put to death in a single night. Not defeated—put to death. There was no battle. There were no banners. The soldiers who came wore no colors, and afterward wore none again, and their names were struck from their own muster-rolls, and their muster-rolls were burned, and the men who burned them were burned in turn, until no thread led back from the deed to any hand that did it.'"

Ilsa glanced up. Wendell had gone very still. Stillness sat strangely on her; it was too practiced, too load-bearing, the stillness of someone holding up a ceiling.

"Continue," Wendell said quietly. Which was not permission, exactly. But it was not refusal.

"'The kingdom did not fall,' Maren wrote. 'Kingdoms fall in war, and there was no war. Vael was *closed*. Its roads were renamed. Its rivers were given the names of rivers elsewhere. Its survivors were scattered along the borders and told to forget, and those who would not forget were buried under stones with no marks, and the stones were scattered too.' " Ilsa turned another page. Her fingers trembled slightly, and she hated them for it. " 'And the Crown decreed that the mountains themselves should forget, and sent men to seal every gate and hollow and old door that the kingdom's dead might use to come home. All but one. One door they could not find. And of that door the prophet wrote—' "

She stopped.

Wendell noticed. Of course she noticed; she was a woman built of noticing, Ilsa was beginning to understand, the way a lock is built of tumblers. "Wrote what?"

Ilsa took a breath. Eleven years of rehearsal, and here was the moment her throat always closed, because the next lines were the ones that had ended her engagement, estranged her uncle, and made her aunt burn a portrait of Maren in the yard like a witch's effigy.

Here, in a stranger's workshop in a border town that smelled of marrow-dust, she read them anyway:

"'One night in every century the veil grows thin as mourning-cloth, and the last door of Vael will stand open till dawn. It opens once. It opens only when the carver who hears stands before it, her hands unbound, her tongue unsilenced. Woe to the crown that finds her first. Woe thrice over to the crown that silences her. For the door knows its own, and it will not be argued with, and it will not wait.'"

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the cart had passed and the dog had gone quiet, and the river kept on.

"That's superstition," Wendell said at last. Her voice was steady. Her hands, Ilsa saw, were white-knuckled in her lap. "Old women's rhyme-making. Prophets wrote a thousand books like that. None of them came true."

"Some did," Ilsa said. "We just call them history instead." She reached into her coat and drew out a slip of paper—her own transcription, made years ago, carried against her heart through three countries and one disastrous betrothal. She set it beside the book. "Maren dated the prophecy. She was precise about it; precision was her religion. She wrote that the door would open on the anniversary of the massacre, in the town where the carver dwelt, on the night of the thin veil." She let the paper sit there between them, small and damning. "The massacre was on the twenty-ninth of Falling Leaves. Tonight is the twenty-seventh. We are two days from the anniversary, Wendell Lockridge, standing forty miles from where the door is supposed to stand."

"And the carver who hears," Wendell said. Each word placed like a stone across a stream. "That's vague. Every village has a carver. Half of them probably fancy they hear things. Grief talks. People imagine—"

"Her name is in the margin."

The sentence fell between them like an axe.

Ilsa hadn't wanted to play it this way. In her rehearsals, she revealed the name gently, apologetically, wrapped in context and caveat. But rehearsals were for cowards with time, and she had watched this woman lie twice in ten minutes with a face like still water, and something in Ilsa—something tired, something frightened, something that had spent eleven years carrying a book that got heavier every mile—simply ran out of patience.

"Maren wrote in margins," Ilsa said softly. "Notes to herself. Guesses. On this page, beside the phrase *the carver who hears*, there's a note in her hand: '*A daughter of the valley-line, born late, who keeps the voices and tells no one.*' " She met Wendell's eyes. "And beneath it, smaller, added later, in different ink: '*Lockridge.*'"

The lamp guttered, steadied. Wendell did not move. She had gone beyond stillness into something geological.

"That proves nothing," she whispered.

"No. It doesn't. Names can repeat. Coincidence is the mortar of bad scholarship, I know it better than anyone." Ilsa closed the book gently, as if closing a coffin lid. "But here's what proves everything, and why I knocked on your door tonight instead of hiring a guide and moving on. Yesterday, in the market square, a man was selling memorial pieces. Little carved tokens, bones with names on them. I bought one—a drowned smuggler, the seller said, carved by the best hand in Thornfell." She reached into her pocket and set the token on the bench beside the book. Small, pale, beautiful. A name in letters finer than anything Maren had ever managed. "And when I held it, Wendell—me, ordinary, deaf-as-a-post Ilsa—I heard the river. Just for a heartbeat. Just a whisper. Like something left behind in the grain of it."

Wendell stared at the token. Something broke open in her face then—briefly, terribly, like ice shifting on a pond—and Ilsa understood that she was looking at a woman who had spent her whole life holding a door shut from the inside, and who had just heard someone knock.

"I don't know what you think you heard," Wendell said.

"I think," Ilsa said, "that in two days the veil goes thin, and a door that has been shut for a hundred years tries to open, and the prophecy says it needs a carver who hears to stand before it. And I think that the Crown has men looking for this book—you may have noticed I'm traveling light and sleeping badly—and if they find it, they'll burn it, and then they'll start looking for the carver. And I think, Wendell Lockridge, that whether or not you believe a word of Maren's Canticle, you have been hearing the dead your entire life and telling no one, and the hour when that stops being safe is approximately forty-eight hours away."

* * *

For a long time the bone-carver said nothing. She rose, crossed to the window, and eased the shutter open a finger's width—not enough to see out, Ilsa realized, just enough to listen.

"What happens," Wendell said, still facing the wall, "if the door opens?"

"I don't know. Maren's second half—" Ilsa stopped. Swallowed the sentence whole. *Careful,* she told herself. *Not yet. Not that card yet.*

"Her what?"

"Her notes are incomplete," Ilsa said smoothly, and hated herself for how good she'd gotten at it. "The book survived; some of her papers didn't. As far as the text goes—the dead of Vael have waited a hundred years to go home. If the door opens, I believe they go home. That's all. That's the whole of it. No army comes through, no curse spills out. Just the dead, walking the road they were stolen from."

"And the Crown?"

"The Crown sealed those doors once. They'll want them sealed forever. And they'll want the carver—" she gestured gently at Wendell's back, at the straight shoulders, the careful hands— "*quiet*. Permanently quiet. Corvane's office doesn't issue warnings, Wendell. It issues ledger entries."

At the name Corvane, Wendell turned around. Her face had reassembled itself, mostly, but her eyes were wet and furious about it. "Why are you telling me this? Really. If I'm in danger, knowing makes it worse. If your prophecy is real, telling me makes it realer. You could have hired your pass and vanished. Why come sit in my workshop and take my whole life apart?"

It was the fairest question anyone had asked her in years, and Ilsa found she had no polished answer for it—only the true one.

"Because eleven years ago I found this book in a locked chest my family swore was empty," she said. "And reading it was the first honest thing that ever happened to me. Everyone I love built their lives on top of a lie, and they knew it, and they called the knowing *duty*. I promised myself I would spend the book before the Crown burned it. Spend it right." She laid her hand flat on the sheepskin cover. "And because the prophecy says *her tongue unsilenced*, Wendell. Yours. Not mine. I can carry the book across every mountain range on the map, but I cannot open a single door. Whatever happens in two days, it begins with whether you tell me the truth. So." She spread her hands. "Do you hear them?"

Outside, the last light failed off the mountains, and the room went blue with dusk, and somewhere down the lane a neighbor called a child home to supper in a language that had once, a century ago, been spoken in a palace.

Wendell Lockridge stood in the middle of her own workshop with her jaw tight and her eyes bright, and Ilsa watched her weigh a lifetime of discipline against a single question, and saw the scale begin, at long last, to tip.

"I need you to leave," Wendell said hoarsely. "Now. Tonight. Before anyone sees you here."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." Wendell was already moving, snuffing the lamp, gathering Ilsa's coat from its hook with brisk, trembling efficiency. "There's a stable behind the chandler's on Low Street. Yellow door. Ask for Bramble. Tell her—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had changed in them, some door inside the door, and her voice came out quieter. "Tell her the carver sent you. She'll feed you and ask nothing. Stay off the main road tomorrow. And Ilsa—if a tall man with gray gloves comes asking after a scholar, you were never in Thornfell."

"Why? Who is he?"

"He's already here," Wendell said. "He arrived at dusk. I carved his dinner companion's memorial token last week—he'll be wanting information, and he gets it." She pressed the oilcloth-wrapped book back into Ilsa's arms and, at the threshold, in the dark, added six words in a voice so low Ilsa nearly missed them:

"I've heard them since I was six."
