## 4. The Inquisitor Comes at Dusk

Corvane counted the town's sins before he dismounted.

Thornfell sat in the crook of the mountains like a hand cupped around something it didn't want seen — a smuggling town wearing the costume of a market village, all shuttered windows and doors that opened too quietly. He had been in forty towns like it in his nineteen years of service to the Crown, and he had learned that the first thing to count was what people did with their eyes. In honest towns, strangers were stared at. In guilty ones, they were watched sidelong, and only when they weren't looking back.

Thornfell was watching him sideways already. Good. Guilty towns talked faster.

He handed his reins to a boy who wouldn't meet his eyes and let the seal on his breastplate do its work. Silver and black, the Crown's eye stamped in wax-red enamel at the center. He didn't need to announce himself. The badge announced him; his face confirmed it. He had inherited both from his father, as his father had from his grandfather, and there were men in the border provinces old enough to flinch at the name Corvane alone.

"Inquisitor," said the innkeeper, when Corvane pushed through the door of the Drowned Bell. The man's voice came out flat, rehearsed, the tone of someone who had practiced this greeting for decades without ever wanting to use it.

"Water for my horse. Bread for me." He set a crown on the bar, then left his hand on it. "And your attention."

The common room emptied itself with impressive speed. That suited him. He preferred rooms that emptied. It meant the people leaving had something to carry away and discuss, and discussion was how information moved in places like Thornfell — not through records, which could be burned, but through fear, which could not.

He ate his bread standing up, studying the room. Low beams, blackened by a century of peat smoke. A hearth swept clean. Behind the bar, bottles arranged by height rather than value, which told him the innkeeper was a man of small vanities and smaller ambitions. On the wall near the stair, a memorial carving set into a plank of oak: a name, dates, a river.

*Fenn Arlowe, drowned in the Sere.*

Corvane looked at it longer than he needed to. Not because the name meant anything — yet — but because the carving was wrong. Not badly wrong. Subtly. The lettering was too fine for a frontier town, the flourishes too controlled, and there was something in the way the lines curved inward at their ends, like the strokes were listening to each other. He had seen work like it once before, thirty years ago, in a burned archive his father had shown him before he died: fragments of Vaeli craftwork, saved from the fires out of some ancestor's superstition.

He filed the observation away. His mind worked like a ledger. Everything went in; nothing came out until the sums demanded it.

The smuggler's name was Doss Merrow, and Corvane found him an hour later in the tannery yard behind the wool-factor's warehouse, counting hides by lantern light and lying about it with every relaxed line of his body.

"Master Merrow," Corvane said. "You came down from the high passes eleven days ago."

Doss didn't startle, which was its own confession. Men who startled were innocent; men who went still were calculating. "Did I? Days run together in autumn."

"They do." Corvane walked the yard slowly, letting his boots sound against the packed earth. "Eleven days ago, a scholar passed through the high country. A woman. Carrying a book older than this town. She would have needed a guide, because the northern trails are closed with snowmelt, and she would have paid well, because scholars always pay well for other people's legs." He stopped. "You guide for money. You were in the high country. The arithmetic is simple."

"The arithmetic is wrong." Doss set down his ledger with deliberate care. "I don't take scholars. Scholars talk."

"Everyone talks eventually." Corvane said it without heat, the way a man might remark on weather. "That is the entire principle of my profession. I simply decide when."

"I don't know anything about any book."

"Then you won't mind answering questions about the roads instead." Corvane moved closer, close enough to smell the tallow and the hides and the sourness of fear beginning under the other man's composure. "The Kestrel Pass. Is it passable this season?"

"Ask the garrison."

"The garrison reports what it's paid to report. You know these mountains the way a tongue knows a mouth. So: the Kestrel Pass, and the old road beneath it — the one that isn't on maps anymore. Is anyone using them?"

Something flickered across Doss's face. There it was. Not guilt — recognition. The book wasn't rumor here; it was fact, moving through the passes like water through limestone, invisible above ground.

"I've got hides to count," Doss said.

"You have one question left to answer honestly," Corvane said, "and then you'll have no more questions at all. Where is the scholar?"

Doss looked past him, toward the darkening street, toward the houses where his family slept, and Corvane read the whole calculus in his face — the weighing of one stranger's life against the safety of everyone who shared his roof. It was the same calculation every man in every guilty town performed, and it almost always ended the same way.

"Go home, Master Merrow," Corvane said quietly. "Tell whoever hired you that I'm here, and let them make their own arithmetic. I'll be in the square until dusk."

He turned his back and walked away. Behind him, silence. Then, soft as a dropped stitch: "The carver's lane. The bone-carver keeps a shop on the carver's lane. She doesn't know anything — she never knows anything — but the woman went there first. Yesterday."

Corvane kept walking. He didn't thank informants. Gratitude implied equality.

He gave Doss Merrow until dusk anyway, because he was a fair man, and fairness in Corvane's understanding meant giving people exactly the time they'd been promised and not one minute more.

* * *

At dusk, Doss ran.

Not far, and not fast enough. Corvane had posted two of his dragoons at the mouth of the tannery lane with instructions to let the man reach the third house and no further, and Doss made it precisely that far before the horses' shadows crossed his path and he understood. To his credit, he didn't beg. He drew a knife — a hide-scraper's knife, more tool than weapon — and made his choice with the courage of a man who had always known the bill would come due someday.

Corvane ended it cleanly. One motion, learned from his father, perfected over two decades: efficient, untheatrical, final. The knife went into the mud. Doss followed it a moment later.

He knelt beside the body in the blue evening light and closed the man's eyes, because his office required it, and because his grandfather had taught him that an Inquisitor who enjoyed the killing would eventually enjoy the wrong killing. Corvane took no pleasure in it. That was important to understand about him, though few ever got the chance. He felt about this the way a mason felt about mortar: it held things together; it was necessary; no one thanked you for it.

"His family gets his wages for a year," he told the dragoons. "Paid from my purse, not the Crown's. And none of them learn why."

They nodded. They thought it was sentiment. It was not. It was accounting: a dead informant bought nothing if his death poisoned the well, and Corvane intended to drink from this well for years.

But standing there, wiping his blade on the clean grass while Thornfell's shutters closed one by one along the street — he heard the message the town was sending him, loud as church bells. *We will die before we tell you.* Not the words. The shape of the silence after them. This town had chosen its side, and it hadn't chosen the Crown's.

Which meant the book mattered to them. Which meant the book was real.

Corvane stood in the emptying square and felt, beneath his discipline, the old family cold settle into his chest — the cold his father had called *inheritance* and meant as a compliment. Somewhere in these mountains was a door that his great-great-grandfather had helped nail shut with fire and royal decree, and somewhere in these mountains was a book that claimed the nails were rusting. If that door opened, it would not merely let out ghosts. It would let out the truth, and truths, unlike ghosts, could not be banished. They could only be survived, and his family had spent four generations ensuring the Crown would not survive one.

He would burn the book. He would burn the scholar. He would burn, if it came to it, the whole crooked little town that sheltered them.

Fairly. Efficiently. Without pleasure.

* * *

The bone-carver's shop smelled of tallow and stone dust, and it was lit by a single lamp that made everything look like it was remembering being alive.

Wendell Lockridge was younger than her reputation suggested — and her reputation had reached even the capital, dressed up as anecdote: the carver whose memorials brought weeping strangers across three provinces. She was small, plain-dressed, hands stained white to the wrist, and she did not flinch when the door opened and the Crown's seal filled it. She simply set down her graver and waited, the way a stone waits.

Corvane catalogued her in three seconds. Steady pulse visible at the throat. Eyes that went to his face rather than his badge — either very brave or very practiced. Hands still. Breath even. And beneath all of it, so faint he nearly missed it, a quality he had encountered perhaps five times in his career and had learned to trust absolutely:

She was listening to something else while looking at him.

"Madam Lockridge. Inquisitor Corvane, service of the Crown." He let the title sit in the room like a dropped stone. "I'm investigating the movement of restricted texts. A book entered the high country recently. I believe it passed through Thornfell."

"I don't deal in books." Her voice was quiet and level. "As you can see, I barely deal in words."

"No. You deal in bones." He stepped inside uninvited, turning slowly, reading the shop the way he'd read the inn. Workbenches. Half-finished memorials. A wall of completed pieces, dozens of names, each carved in that same fine inward-curving hand he'd seen on the tavern wall — the hand that reminded him of Vael. "A trade most families here use you for, judging by the volume. Tell me about your clients."

"Families with dead people. Most of Thornfell qualifies."

"And strangers?"

"Strangers have dead people too."

"A woman came to you yesterday." He watched her hands. "Scholar. Traveling alone. Carrying a case too large for clothing and too heavy for paper."

For the first time, something moved behind her stillness — not fear, he noted, but a kind of weary recognition, as though she had expected this visit the way one expects weather. "A traveler asked me about passage south. I told her what I tell everyone: ask the caravan master. I carve bones, Inquisitor. The dead don't carry books, and neither do I."

"The dead," Corvane repeated. "An interesting choice of phrase."

"It's a trade phrase. Everyone in my line says it."

It was true, and it was a lie, and Corvane recognized the construction because he built it himself, daily, professionally. He crossed to the wall of finished carvings and studied them in the lamplight. Names. Dates. Rivers. Causes. A hundred small griefs rendered in bone, each one lovingly exact — and yet.

"This one," he said, touching a plaque near the center. "*Marta Vey, lost in the Sere floods.* Yet the Sere flooded east of here, at Marrow Ford. Why does the carving place her loss upstream, at the confluence?"

Wendell was silent for a moment. When she answered, her voice hadn't changed at all. "Because that's where her family believes she died. I carve what gives them peace. Accuracy is for clerks."

"Is it."

"In my experience, accuracy is mostly for people who've never lost anything."

The answer landed harder than he expected, and he noted that too — that he had expected nothing from her and received a blade. He turned from the wall. "Madam Lockridge. Let me describe the consequences of obstruction plainly, since this province has a habit of mistaking my patience for ignorance. Possession of restricted prophetic text carries a sentence of life imprisonment. Concealment of such a text from a Crown officer carries death. Harboring its carrier carries death. These are not threats. They are the law, written before either of us was born, and I enforce them whether I admire them or not." He paused. "Do you understand?"

"Perfectly."

"Do you have anything to declare?"

"No."

She met his eyes while she said it, and her heartbeat, as far as he could read it in the small tendons of her throat, never changed. Either she was telling the truth, or she was the finest liar he had ever interviewed, or — and this possibility arrived unbidden and unwelcome — there was something about her that his instruments of judgment simply could not weigh. He distrusted that possibility more than any lie. Lies fit in ledgers. Mysteries did not.

"One more thing," he said, moving toward the door. "The carving on the wall of the Drowned Bell. Fenn Arlowe. Your work?"

"Yes."

"The drowned smuggler buried last week."

"He'd want the company," she said, and there was the first true thing she'd given him, wrapped in a joke, and he couldn't parse which part was which.

Corvane paused on the threshold, lamplight spilling past him into the street. He looked back at Wendell Lockridge, small and still among her dead, and made the entry in his mental ledger: *Knows more than she says. Says nothing actionable. Watch her.*

"There will be soldiers in Thornfell tonight," he told her. "They will knock on doors. Some of your neighbors will find that frightening. I'd advise you to sleep deeply, madam. Guilty consciences make poor beds."

"Mine sleeps fine," Wendell Lockridge said. "It's used to company."

He left. Outside, the mountain wind had come down with full dark, carrying the smell of snow off the high passes, and Corvane stood in the street a moment, listening to the town hold its breath around him. Every window dark. Every door silent. An entire settlement agreeing, wordlessly, to protect whatever moved through it — a book, a woman, a secret, a century old.

His grandfather had burned an archive to keep this silence from ever being broken. His father had hunted the survivors of that burning into exile. And now the silence itself had turned against the family that made it, thickening into complicity, hardening into conspiracy, gathering in these mountains like stormwater behind a dam.

Tomorrow he would close the southern road. Tomorrow he would put a price on a scholar's head. But tonight, standing in the dark in a town that hated him rightly, Corvane allowed himself one moment of the family cold, and under it — deeper, where no audit would ever find it — one flicker of the question his grandfather had forbidden on pain of disinheritance:

*What are they so afraid we'll hear?*

In the shop behind him, faint through the walls, he could have sworn he heard the bone-carver begin to sing.
