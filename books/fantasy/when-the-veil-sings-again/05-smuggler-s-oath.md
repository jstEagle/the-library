## 5. Smuggler's Oath

The Drowned Rat smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who had bet their wages on a losing dog. Wendell had been coming here for eleven years and had never once been asked to sit down.

Bramble held court at the corner table the way she held everything—with her boots up and her hand already resting on whatever you'd brought to trade. Tonight it was a chipped clay pipe she wasn't smoking, turning it over and over like a rosary.

"You look like bad news wearing a good coat," Bramble said. "Sit anyway."

Wendell sat. Across the room, Hesk stood at the bar with his back to the wall—old soldier habit, even two years after he'd stopped being a soldier—and Tally was perched on a barrel by the door, doing something fiddly with a lock that wasn't hers and a pick that definitely wasn't hers either. Her whole found family in one room, which almost never happened, which meant someone had died or money had changed hands, and Wendell was here to make sure it wasn't the second one.

"I need a crossing," Wendell said. "Over the Spine. Tomorrow, if it can be tomorrow. Quiet road, no tolls, no questions."

Bramble's boots came down. That alone told the room something was happening; three card players folded without looking at their hands.

"Quiet roads cost quiet money," Bramble said. "Who's crossing?"

"A scholar. And a book."

"And why does a bone-carver care about a scholar's book?"

Wendell had rehearsed this on the walk over. She had a lie prepared, a good one, smooth as sanded bone. She opened her mouth to use it and heard instead—faint, from somewhere behind her left shoulder, where they always were—the dead man's voice saying *tell her the truth, carver, lies travel worse than cargo*.

He was right. Dead men usually were, once you got past the complaining.

"Because the book has my name in it," Wendell said. "Written down a hundred years ago. And because there's an Inquisitor in Thornfell tonight who will burn this town down to get his hands on it."

The room went still the way rooms do when the word *Inquisitor* lands. Tally's pick stopped moving. Hesk turned around slowly, and Wendell watched him do the arithmetic soldiers did—exits, sightlines, odds.

"Corvane," Hesk said quietly. "Grey coat, silver chain, arrived at dusk with six riders?"

"You've seen him."

"He hanged Ferro Lune off the tollhouse beam." Hesk's voice didn't change at all, which was how Wendell knew it mattered. "Asked him about a book. Ferro didn't know anything. He died not knowing anything." He set his cup down with great care, the way men do when putting it down hard would end something. "So. Yes. I've seen him."

*Tell them about the dead too,* whispered the drowned smuggler, whose name was supposed to be Jory Marsh but who insisted—had been insisting all week—that his name was Jory Vael, actually, Jory Vael before anyone in his family knew what that word meant, and wouldn't it be fine if somebody wrote it down right for once.

Wendell pressed her hands flat against the table. "There's more," she said. "And you're going to think I'm mad, so let me finish before anyone laughs."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about the Drowned Rat: everyone in it had seen something on the mountain roads they couldn't explain, and none of them could afford to call another person mad.

"I hear the dead," Wendell said. "I've heard them since I was seven years old. Every soul that dies on those passes, every smuggler the mountains took, every soldier left in a ditch—they talk. All the time. And one of the things they've been talking about, louder every night this week, is a kingdom called Vael."

Tally made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"The dead don't haunt," Wendell went on, because she'd earned this speech across twenty years of silence and she was going to spend it all at once. "They wait. They've been waiting a hundred years for a door that opens once. And according to a book a scholar is carrying—a book the Crown wants burned—it opens tomorrow night, and it opens because of me. So I need to be on the other side of the Spine by then, and I need people I trust to get me there, and—" she looked around the table, at Bramble's narrowed eyes and Hesk's stillness and Tally's white knuckles, "—and you're the only people in this world I trust. Which is a terrible thing to say out loud, and I said it anyway, so."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a drunk rolled over.

Then Tally slid off her barrel and said, "My brother's name was in your ledger."

Wendell blinked. "What?"

"The memorial ledgers. You carve names for families who can't afford priests. My brother Col. Died on the Kettle Steps, three winters back. Nobody would take the job—smugglers don't get stones, everybody knows that." Tally's chin was up, defiant, daring Wendell to make it sentimental. "You carved him anyway. Charged me nothing. And when I came to collect the piece, you said—" her voice wobbled and steadied, "—you said, *he wanted you to know he wasn't cold at the end.* I never told anyone. I thought maybe I dreamed it. I didn't dream it, did I."

"No," Wendell said softly. "Col was warm. He was singing. Badly."

Tally laughed wetly and wiped her face with her sleeve like an angry cat. "Right. Well. I'm in. Obviously I'm in. Somebody owes the dead a favor and it might as well be me carrying the debt."

Bramble hadn't moved. She was looking at Wendell with the expression of a woman re-reading a map she thought she knew by heart and finding a river where a road should be.

"Say a name," Bramble said finally. "A dead man. One of mine."

"Bramble—"

"Say one. Or I'll believe this is grief-talk and fever, lovely as it is."

Wendell closed her eyes. There were so many. She reached for the ones attached to Bramble specifically, the crew she'd lost over nine years of runs—the *Kestrel*, lost with all four hands off the Widow's Tooth; old Nix, whose heart quit on the Salt Road; a boy called Pip who'd been nineteen and lied about sixteen.

"Pip," Wendell said. "Your youngest. He fell at Greywater Ford, and the report said the river took him fast. It didn't. He hit the rocks first, and it hurt, and he wasn't afraid, though. He says—he said—you held his hand until the end, and he wants you to stop paying for a headstone you can't afford, because he doesn't need one. He needs you to stop drinking yourself stupid every anniversary and start telling his stories instead. He says stories are headstones that walk."

Bramble stared at her. Then she turned her face away toward the fire, and her shoulders shook exactly once, and when she turned back her eyes were dry and ferocious.

"Nine years," she rasped. "Nine years I carried that boy's death like it was my fault for hiring him. You're telling me he forgave me before he finished dying."

"He didn't think there was anything to forgive."

"Ha." Bramble dragged a hand down her face. "Fine. Fine! I'll ferry your scholar and your haunted book over the roof of the world. But I want it understood"—she jabbed a finger at Wendell—"that when we come back, you're carving my crew. All of them. Proper pieces. Names spelled right. None of this priest-script nonsense."

"That was always the plan."

"It's a plan now. It counts double when I agree to it." She stood, and just like that she was Captain again, boots planted, voice filling the room. "Hesk. Tally. We move at grey light. Cold rations, no fires above the treeline, nobody sings."

"I always sing," said Tally.

"Not on this road."

Hesk still hadn't spoken. He stood with his arms crossed, looking at Wendell with an expression she couldn't read, and the silence stretched long enough that the dead started murmuring again—*ask him, ask him, ask him about the ford*—and Wendell realized she already knew, the way she sometimes knew things without wanting to.

"Hesk," she said carefully. "Two years ago. The retreat at Marrow Bridge. You carried a lieutenant out under fire, and he died anyway, in your arms, on the north bank. The army listed him as deserted alongside you, because it was easier than admitting where the order came from. His family got a letter full of lies. You've never been able to tell them the truth because the truth ends with *and then I ran*."

Hesk had gone very pale.

"He doesn't blame you," Wendell said. "For any of it. Not the bridge, not the running, not the letter. He says the running was the bravest part, because you ran carrying him, and he says—" her throat tightened, because some of the dead were heavy, "—he says a deserter who saves one life outranks ten colonels who spend a thousand. He's been trying to tell you for two years. You keep almost hearing him. On windy nights."

Hesk sat down. Slowly, like a man setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was wearing. When he spoke, his voice was rough gravel.

"What do you need me to do?"

"Walk point," Wendell said. "There are roads over the Spine that aren't on any map. Roads the dead kept using after the living forgot them. I can find them—but I need someone watching the living while I listen to everyone else."

"Roads the dead use," Hesk repeated. He almost smiled. It looked strange on him, like spring frost on a summer field. "Inquisitor Corvane won't have those on his maps either."

"No," Wendell agreed. "He won't."

* * *

They spent the rest of the evening preparing, and it felt—strangely, uncomfortably—like a festival. Tally vanished and returned with papers: passes, seals, a customs writ for a wool merchant named Herrick Foss who did not exist and whose signature Tley reproduced with alarming fluency. Bramble counted out rope, oilskin, dried meat, and argued cheerfully with the innkeep about the price of silence, which he eventually gave them for free because Bramble had pulled his brother's body out of the Kettle Steps herself, eight years ago, and some debts ran both directions.

Wendell went home once, to close the shutters and bank her forge and stand for a moment in the workshop among her half-finished pieces. Jory Marsh—who was Jory Vael—floated near the window and complained that she was packing too many chisels and not enough courage.

"You don't need the small gouge," he said. "You're going to carve names into a mountain, girl. Bring the big one."

"There's no such thing as carving names into a mountain."

"Not yet," he said comfortably. "Give it till tomorrow."

She left the small gouge behind. It felt like superstition. It also felt like obedience, and she'd learned long ago that the dead rarely wasted your time—only your pride.

Back at the Drowned Rat, past midnight, Ilsa Verrow sat wrapped in a borrowed blanket, watching these smugglers prepare her escort with the wide-eyed look of a woman who had studied adventure exclusively through footnotes. The prophecy book lay between them on the table, and Wendell kept her eyes away from it. She'd read enough of it. Her own name. Tonight's date. *The carver who hears.*

"Tally," Ilsa said suddenly, as the fence-girl stowed forged documents in her coat lining. "May I ask—are you frightened?"

Tally considered this with the seriousness she gave everything, which was to say, none outwardly and everything underneath.

"Yeah," Tally said. "Course. But my brother used to say scared is just your body counting the cost before you pay it. Means the thing matters." She patted the coat pocket full of lies. "This matters."

Ilsa looked at Wendell across the table. In the lamplight her scholarly composure had cracked, and beneath it was just a tired young woman carrying a century of other people's guilt in a satchel.

"They each joined for someone dead," Ilsa murmured. "Not for the prophecy. For the dead."

"That's the only currency that's ever worked on this mountain," Wendell said.

* * *

They mustered in the stable yard at grey light, exactly as promised. Frost on the cobbles. Breath hanging. Six souls, one satchel of forbidden history, and a mule named Duchess whom Bramble addressed with more courtesy than she addressed most humans.

Hesk checked the yard gate twice. Tally counted the coin pouch three times and hid it four different places. Bramble stood at the lead mule consulting no map at all, because maps were for people with destinations the roads already knew about.

Wendell stood in the middle of it, listening.

And the mountain was *loud* this morning. Louder than she'd ever known it—not the usual scattered murmur of individual dead, but a low collective hum rolling down from the high passes like wind through a canyon, hundreds of voices braided together, and beneath the hum, threading through it, a rhythm. Almost like footsteps. Almost like a procession, walking toward her out of the heights.

Bramble noticed her go still. "Carver. What is it? What do you hear?"

Wendell looked up at the Spine, where the peaks caught the first light and burned rose-gold, beautiful and ancient and full.

"They're coming down to meet us," she said. "All of them. And they want us to take the road that isn't on any map."
