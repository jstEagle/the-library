## 6. The Road That Isn't on Any Map

I'd deserted from the Ninth Foot eleven years ago, and in that time I'd learned one rule better than any drill sergeant ever taught me: never trust a road you can't see.

So naturally, at dawn on the second day out of Thornfell, I was standing at the mouth of one.

It didn't look like much. A fold in the hillside where two rock faces leaned together like conspirators, choked with juniper and scree, no wider than a cart track and half as welcoming. To my left, the proper road ran east toward the Kettering tolls — wide, graded, patrolled, exactly where Inquisitor Corvane's riders would be looking. To my right, nothing but mountain.

"Through there," Wendell said.

She said it the way she said everything, flat as a slate, arms crossed over her bone-carver's apron she still wore out of habit even on the road. Behind her, Ilsa Verrow clutched the prophecy book to her chest like a breastplate, and Tally stood on a boulder peering back down the valley with the spyglass she'd lifted off some merchant or other.

"Riders," Tally said. "Three. Maybe four. Coming up the valley road slow, the way men do when they're not chasing yet but want to be ready."

"Then we're through there," Wendell said again, and started walking into the crack in the mountain like it had personally invited her.

Bramble caught my elbow. The captain of the *Magpie's Mercy* — which was to say, the captain of whatever cargo happened to need moving without paperwork — had a face like a dropped pie and eyes that missed nothing. "Hesk," she said quietly. "You've got the most soldier left in you. If this goes wrong, it's your job to say so loud."

"It's already wrong," I told her. "We're following a bone-carver into a hole in a mountain because she hears dead people."

"Yes," Bramble said. "And I owe the dead. So did you, once." She squeezed my arm and let go. She meant the ambush at Redford Bridge, though she'd never once said so directly. She meant the nineteen men of my company who hadn't come home, and how I'd walked away from the army that spent them, and how for three years afterward I couldn't sleep unless I was drunk enough not to hear them.

Wendell had heard them too, that first night in Thornfell, before any of this started. I'd been drinking in her workshop because it was the only room in town where nobody asked questions, and she'd looked up from her carving and said, "Your sergeant. The big one. He wants you to know he doesn't blame you."

I hadn't told anyone about Sergeant Voss. Not his name, not how he died, not the promise I failed to keep. And this woman who barely spoke had known.

So here I was. Following her into a hole in a mountain.

* * *

The first hundred yards were the worst, because they were ordinary — loose stone, wet rock, roots grabbing at your boots. Then we rounded a shoulder of granite and the road appeared.

Not appeared like something built appears. Appeared the way a face appears when you turn around and realize someone's been standing behind you the whole time. One moment there was scree and juniper; the next there was a track, six feet wide, running level along the mountainside, paved with fitted stones gone soft-edged with age. No cart ruts. No hoofprints. Nothing living had walked it in a long time.

"Ghost-road," Tally breathed. She'd come up beside me without me hearing, which was Tally all over. Sixteen years old, quick fingers, quicker tongue, and a way of materializing wherever trouble was thinnest. "I thought those were stories. Like, literally. Stories. My gran used to say—"

"Your gran," Wendell said, not turning around, "was right about everything except the part where they're dangerous. They're just roads. Nobody's used them since Vael fell, so nobody maintains them, and the dead remember the maintenance schedule better than the masons' descendants do."

"That sentence made no sense," I said.

"Good," she said. "Means you're paying attention."

Ilsa had her book open now, walking and reading, which was going to get somebody killed. "The chronicles mention *veinways*. Roads of the old kingdom that were said to follow the 'listening lines' of the mountains. Scholars assumed it was poetry."

"Scholars assume everything's poetry until it kills them," Bramble said cheerfully. "Lead on, carver."

Wendell led. And here is what I will say about that day, and I have been a hard man to impress since the Ninth Foot taught me the price of trusting officers: she navigated by listening. Actually listening. Every quarter mile she'd stop, tilt her head like a bird dog, and stand there with her eyes half-closed while the rest of us sweated and watched the ridgeline for riders. Then she'd point — left here, down through that gap, mind the third step, it's rotten — and every single time she was right.

"How?" I asked her once, during a water stop.

She considered whether to answer. She did that a lot, weighing words like a fence weighs coin. "There's a man standing next to you," she said finally. "Old drover. Been dead sixty years. He says the bridge ahead washed out in his grandfather's day and never got rebuilt properly, so we cross at the ford below instead, and he's insulted we even considered the bridge."

I looked to my left. Nothing there. Looked to my right. Nothing there.

"He's also," Wendell added, "commenting on your boots."

"What about my boots?"

"He says deserters always buy cheap boots because they're saving money for the running."

Tally laughed so hard she had to sit down. Even Bramble snorted. And I found — this is the strange part, the part I've turned over many nights since — I found I wasn't angry. Because Sergeant Voss would have said the same thing, and said it laughing, and for one moment the road was full of people instead of ghosts.

* * *

Trouble found us an hour past noon, the way trouble does: suddenly, and from the direction you weren't watching.

Wendell stopped mid-stride and went rigid. "Off the road. Now. Down."

We scrambled into a fold of rock above the veinway, and I got my first look at why she'd frozen. Below us, maybe four hundred yards down the slope, the Kettering road curved around the mountain's knee — and on it rode eight men in Crown grey, moving at a walk, scanning the slopes. Not searching. *Sweeping.* Corvane wasn't chasing us; he was fencing us in, closing each road ahead of us like a man shutting gates on a sheep trail.

Except he didn't know about the veinway. He couldn't. So we lay flat among the junipers and let him ride past beneath us, close enough that I could hear the creak of tack and one soldier complaining about his boots.

"Deserters always buy cheap boots," Tally whispered into my ear, wicked as sin.

I put my hand over her face and pushed gently until she stopped talking.

But the soldiers didn't pass. They stopped, at the bend below us, and dismounted. Watered the horses. And their sergeant — I could see the stripes from up there — started walking the slope below our position, head down, reading the ground.

Reading *our* ground. Eight people leave prints. Eight people break juniper branches. It had been dry for nine days.

Bramble saw it same as I did. Her hand went to the hatchet she carried, and I shook my head — noise carries downhill, sound travels farther than you'd think in mountain air — and she grimaced, because she knew I was right and hated it.

The sergeant stopped. Straightened up. Turned his face uphill, straight toward us.

And Tally stood up.

I grabbed for her and missed. She stepped out of the juniper onto open rock, alone, hands raised, and called down in a voice pitched to carry: "Hello the road! Is that the Kettering sweep? Thank all the saints, I've been lost two days!"

Every instinct I owned screamed to drag her back down. Instead I watched, and learned later that Bramble was doing the same, both of us frozen by the same calculation: any rescue would kill her faster than leaving her be.

The sergeant shaded his eyes. "Who's up there?"

"Tally Fenwick! Fence-girl out of Thornfell — well, ex-fence-girl, if my employer has his way." She came scrambling down the slope toward them, loud the whole way, tripping theatrically, swearing colorfully, the picture of a girl too tired and too relieved to be afraid. I could see only fragments between the rocks, but I could hear everything, and Tally's voice never wavered once.

"—looking for a lost mule train, don't laugh, yes I know, but Mr. Quillman pays recovery fees and Quillman's fees feed me, so — anyway, I cut up through the high track after the mules spooked and now I can't find the low road again, this mountain has too many roads, that's my professional opinion—"

"The high track," the sergeant said. I couldn't see his face. My whole body had gone cold.

"There isn't one!" Tally said, laughing. "That's the joke of it! I've been walking a goat path that isn't on any map, which I suppose is fitting, because neither am I, most days. Listen — you're sweeping for someone, aren't you? The Inquisitor's men? Everyone in Thornfell's been talking about nothing else. Grey cloaks, hard faces, asking about a scholar?"

Silence. Long enough that my heart stopped and started twice.

"You've seen them?" the sergeant asked carefully. "The scholar's party?"

"I've seen *sign* of them, maybe, two days back, if it was them — campfire ash on the east fork, and someone had bought the last of Mrs. Halloway's travel bread, and Mrs. Halloway said a woman paid in southern coin, which everyone knows is odd—" Tally rattled it off like gossip, which was exactly what it was: true things arranged to mean something false. "They went east. Toward Kettering, I'd swear it. Why would anyone go west? West is nothing but the old passes and bad water."

Another silence. Then the sergeant laughed, short and relieved. "You might be right at that. We've wasted today on the western bend as it is."

"I could look at your map?" Tally offered brightly. "I'm good with maps. Occupational hazard."

More murmuring. Then — footsteps on scree, horses gathering, the creak and jingle of a troop mounting up. Tally's voice, floating up one more time: "If you find a grey mule with a torn left ear, that's Buttercup, and there's a reward, and I love her very much!"

Laughter from below. Hoofbeats fading east.

She climbed back up to us ten minutes later, cheeks pink, eyes shining, and collapsed dramatically against a boulder. "Buttercup," she said, "does not exist. I want it noted that I committed to the bit."

"You absolute lunatic," Bramble said, and hugged her hard enough to lift her off the ground.

"They'd have found our tracks within the hour," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "You knew that. You walked down there knowing that."

Tally shrugged, suddenly sixteen again and embarrassed by praise. "Wendell says the dead remember everything. Well, so do I. You spend your life watching what people believe and how they believe it, you learn to hand them the belief they want." She paused. "Also I really did lose a mule once. Named Buttercup and everything. Felt right to give her a job."

Ilsa had come forward during all this, book clutched tight, staring at Tally with an expression I couldn't read. "You lied to the Crown's soldiers," she said. "On an open road. With eight of them."

"Six of them were bored and one was hungry," Tally said. "Only the sergeant mattered, and sergeants want good news. Always have. My da was one." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Anyway. They went east. We should go west fast, before the sergeant remembers girls who talk too much."

Wendell had said nothing through all of it. Now she looked at Tally — really looked, the way she looked at a piece of bone before making the first cut — and nodded once.

"The drover agrees with you," she said. "He says you'd have made a fine herald, back in the old days. He says the court always listened best to the ones nobody expected."

Tally blinked. "Did you just compliment me via ghost?"

"Don't get used to it."

But she was smiling when she turned back up the road. We all were, a little. Even me, and I hadn't smiled at anything in years that wasn't bitter.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow off the veinway, cold food, no fire, watches split four ways. I took second watch with Wendell, mostly because I wanted to ask her something and couldn't ask it in daylight.

The moon came up fat and white over the eastern peaks. Below us, miles off, a light moved along the Kettering road — a lantern, patient, methodical. Corvane wasn't sleeping either.

"Do you ever wish you couldn't hear them?" I asked.

Wendell was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. "No," she said at last. "I wish I'd answered sooner. There's a difference." She turned her head. In the dark, her eyes caught the moonlight. "Your sergeant. Voss. He's been with you since Redford Bridge, hasn't he? Walking behind you. Left side."

My throat closed. I managed a nod.

"He says you kept the promise. The one you think you broke. He says getting the others out *was* keeping it." She paused. "He says you can stop carrying it now. Or keep carrying it, if you must, but stop pretending it's heavy."

I sat with that a long time. Below us the lantern crept eastward, and somewhere behind us in the dark, a dead drover hummed an old song about a country that wasn't on any map anymore.

"Hesk," Wendell said quietly, when I still hadn't spoken. "In three days we reach the old border. After that, they'll get louder. All of them. The ones from Vael." She looked west, where the mountains stacked up dark against the stars. "And I need to tell you all something about what they're saying. Before it's too late to turn around."

Above us, the veil between the living and the dead hung thin as morning frost, and I felt — soldier's superstition or not — that we were being counted.

West, the mountains waited. And something in them was starting to sing.
