## 7. Voices in the Pass

The dead got louder the way weather does.

Not all at once—no thunderclap, no single voice rising above the rest—but a pressure change, a thickening of the air behind her ears. Wendell had been walking for two days with Ilsa Verrow's borrowed book strapped to her back and Bramble's smuggler's oath ringing in her memory, and somewhere in the last hour the whispering that lived at the edge of her hearing had stopped being an edge.

It was a shore now. And the tide was coming in.

*They're close,* said a voice she didn't know, old and cracked as river clay. *Close, close, so close to home.*

She kept walking.

The company strung out along the trail in the order they'd settled into without ever discussing it: Hesk in front because he trusted nothing he couldn't see first, Tally behind him because she trusted Hesk, then Wendell, then Ilsa, and Bramble sweeping the rear with a crossbow slung casual across her shoulders like she was out gathering herbs instead of hauling contraband prophecy over a mountain range the Crown had closed at dusk three days ago.

"Your face is doing something," Tally said, falling back beside her. The girl had a way of moving that made no sound at all, even on scree. "Doing something bad."

"I'm fine."

"You've got your listening face on. The one you make when you're pretending you can't hear what you're hearing." Tally squinted up at her. "It's louder here, isn't it."

Wendell didn't answer, which was an answer, and Tally nodded like she'd confirmed a suspicion about a mark and dropped back into silence. That was the thing about smugglers. They knew when not to ask twice.

The pass narrowed ahead, walls of gray stone leaning together overhead like conspirators. Old road under their boots—not the King's Road, which wound wide and graded through the southern passes Corvane had sealed, but something older, rougher, cut before there was a Crown to grade anything. Hesk called it the Drover's Scar. Wendell knew better. She'd known since the second day, when the dead travelers started giving her directions.

*Left at the split stone,* a woman had murmured, a woman who smelled of woodsmoke and had died with her hands full of somebody else's hair. *The right path drowned in '61.*

And the right path had indeed ended at a rockfall, and they had gone left, and nobody had asked Wendell how she knew.

*Home,* said the clay-cracked voice again. *So close. We were so close.*

Ahead, Hesk raised a fist. Everyone stopped. He stood listening with his whole body, a deserter's habit, ten years of listening for officers' boots—and then he relaxed by a degree, which from Hesk was practically a speech.

"Clear," he said. "For now."

"For now," Bramble echoed from the rear, cheerful in the way she got when things were worst. "Lovely. Let's put some miles on before the mountain changes its mind."

They walked. The whispers walked with them.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower just past sundown—a tower built in no style Wendell recognized, its stones fitted without mortar, its doorway tall enough for men who had been taller than anyone in Thornfell. Ilsa ran her fingers along the doorframe with an expression Wendell had seen on her only once before: the scholar looking at scripture.

"Vaeli work," Ilsa said quietly. "First physical evidence we've touched."

"First evidence you've touched," Bramble said, feeding the small fire they allowed themselves. "Our carver's been twitching at ghosts since the border shrine."

"I don't twitch," Wendell said.

"You absolutely twitch," Tally said. "Like a dog hearing a whistle."

Hesk said nothing. Hesk had watched her navigate the ghost-road without asking questions, and Wendell suspected his silence wasn't ignorance but courtesy—the particular courtesy of a man who had deserted an army and did not enjoy being reminded that other people also carried secrets the Crown would kill them for.

Ilsa sat down across the fire from Wendell, knees drawn up, the book in her lap. She'd taken to sleeping with it strapped to her body. Wendell understood. Some things you didn't let out of arm's reach once you knew what they cost.

"The voices," Ilsa said. "Are they louder tonight?"

Wendell looked into the fire. The fire was easier. Fires didn't ask things.

"Yes."

"What are they saying?"

And there it was. The question she had spent her whole life building walls against, asked plainly, across a fire, by a woman whose family had written a book that named her.

Her rule rose up in her like a wall of its own. *Never repeat what the dead say aloud.* She'd made it when she was nine years old, after the miller's daughter, after telling the girl that her mother's ghost said the well water was sweet and the girl had run home sobbing with joy and then come back the next week hollow-eyed, demanding more, begging, haunting the bone-carver's shop the way her mother haunted the mill. Wendell had learned that grief could be fed until it became a hunger that ate the griever alive. So: never repeat. Never confirm. Never let them know what she heard.

But the wall had cracks in it now. Had had them since the night she'd opened Ilsa's book and seen her own name in ink a century old.

"Fragments," Wendell said.

Tally's head came up. Bramble went still. Even Hesk turned, slow, like a man watching a door he'd assumed was painted open onto a wall.

"Fragments of what?" Ilsa asked, very gently, the way you spoke near a frightened animal.

"Of the massacre." The word came out of her flat and strange, like someone else's coin spent from her purse. "Of that night. It's—I think it's the same night, over and over. They don't move forward. They're all still standing in it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the tower's empty windows, and the sound it made was almost—almost—a voice.

"Tell me," Ilsa said.

"No." Wendell heard her own voice harden. "That's the rule. I have a rule. I don't repeat what they say. Not to anyone. Not for anyone. You don't know what it does to people, hearing—" She stopped. Breathed. "You don't know."

"I know what it does to people not hearing," Ilsa said. "A century of silence. A kingdom erased so thoroughly that children in Thornfell grow up singing songs with holes in them where Vael used to be. My grandmother taught me a lullaby and couldn't tell me why it was in no language she knew." Her hands tightened on the book. "I've spent my life studying the shape of the hole, Wendell. You're the only person alive who can hear what fills it."

"That's exactly why I won't." Wendell stood. Her legs were stiff from the trail; she let the stiffness be anger. "Because the moment I start saying it aloud, I stop being a woman who hears things and become a thing you use. A well you drop buckets into. I've watched what wanting does to people. I'm not doing it."

"Wendell—"

"Goodnight, Scholar."

She walked to the far side of the camp, wrapped herself in her cloak, and lay down facing away from the fire. Behind her she heard Tally mutter something sharp, and Bramble's low laugh, and Ilsa's silence, which was the loudest sound of all.

The dead, meanwhile, kept talking.

*The bells were wrong,* said a man near her left ear, a man with a court accent, vowels rounded like river stones. *We always rang them wrong. Seventeen strokes for the changing of the guard and they rang twelve, twelve, and old Marta said twelve means mourning, twelve means—*

*—the doors were barred from inside,* said another, a young voice, a soldier's voice, terrified and formal at once. *Barred from inside, Captain, I swear it on my mother, we didn't bar them, someone barred them from—*

*—my sister,* said a child, simply, endlessly. *My sister. My sister. My sister.*

Wendell pressed her palms against her ears, which had never once worked, and stared at the dark, and did not sleep.

* * *

Sometime past midnight, footsteps came soft around the fallen tower. Wendell didn't turn. She'd known Ilsa Verrow for less than a week and already she knew the weight of her step.

"I'm not asleep," Wendell said.

"I know. You flinch every time the wind hits the tower." Ilsa sat down against the stones beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to be refused. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them the valley held its breath; above them the stars came through the thin cloud like coins at the bottom of a well.

"When I was seven," Wendell said, before she could stop herself, "there was a flood in the Lowmark. Forty-one people drowned. The bodies came through Thornfell on the way to the burial grounds, and I was in the market with my mother, and I heard them. All forty-one. Talking over each other. Arguing about whose fault it was, whether the levee had been sound, calling for children, calling for God." She kept her eyes on the stars. "I screamed. Loud enough that half the market thought I was the one drowning. My mother dragged me home and I told her what happened and she went white as bone-meal and said, *Never. Never say it again. Not to me, not to anyone.* And then she looked at me like—" Wendell's jaw tightened. "Like I was something that had gotten into the house. Something you don't kill because killing it is worse luck than keeping it."

Ilsa said nothing. Waiting. Scholars waited well; it was the first honest thing Wendell had learned about the trade.

"So I learned to pretend. Nodded along when people talked to empty rooms. Said *yes, the weather's turning* when what I meant was *your dead husband is standing behind you complaining about the roof.* I got good at it. Twenty years good." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You want to hear the funny part? The invisible woman. The quiet carver nobody notices. That's not shyness. That's camouflage. If nobody looks at you, nobody asks what you're listening to."

"And now?"

"Now a book written before my grandmother was born says I exist. Says this night exists. And I'm sitting on a mountain that is full—" her voice caught, steadied— "*full*, Ilsa, like a cup too full, like a dam too full, of people who died screaming about doors barred from the inside, and I can't pretend anymore, because pretending was possible when they whispered. They're not whispering anymore."

Ilsa was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, stripped of scholarship, just a woman in the dark.

"Why tell me? An hour ago you wouldn't give me a sentence."

"Because you waited." Wendell finally turned her head. In the starlight Ilsa's face was all planes and shadow, and her eyes were wet, and she hadn't wiped them. "Everyone else wants. My mother wanted me normal. The grieving want messages. Corvane wants the book and probably my head attached or detached depending on his mood. You—" She shook her own head. "You sat down next to me and waited. Nobody waits."

"My grandmother waited," Ilsa said softly. "Four generations of Verrows waiting for the door. I inherited the waiting. I'd rather inherit the truth."

"The truth has teeth."

"So does loyalty. I've been chewing on both for six days." Ilsa pulled her cloak tighter. "Here's mine, freely given, since you gave me yours: if it comes to choosing between the book and you, I choose you. The prophecy needs a carver who hears. It doesn't need a specific book. Paper burns. You don't."

"You don't know that," Wendell said. "You haven't read the whole thing. Neither have I. There are pages in that book that make the hair on my arms stand up, and I can't read the old script, but I can feel them. Like standing near a drop in the dark."

"Then tomorrow we read them together. In daylight. With Bramble arguing and Tally heckling and Hesk pretending not to listen." Isla almost smiled. "Nobody reads the teeth alone."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she unclenched something in her chest that had been clenched for twenty years, and found that what rushed into the space wasn't relief exactly. It was sharper than relief. It felt like standing at the edge of the drop in daylight.

"There's one more thing," she said. "One fragment. It keeps coming back, and I think—I think it's important, and I hate that I think that, because it means the rule is already broken."

"Tell me."

"*Barred from inside.*" Wendell repeated it aloud for the first time in her life, and the words tasted like cold iron. "A soldier's voice. Young. He's been saying it since the massacre, Ilsa. Whoever barred those doors did it from within. The court wasn't slaughtered by invaders breaking in." She met Ilsa's eyes. "Someone locked them in with the killers."

Ilsa had gone very still. The kind of stillness Wendell had only seen once before—in Corvane's eyes, in the interview room, when he'd sensed the shape of what she was hiding.

"That's not possible," Ilsa whispered. "The histories—every account agrees—it was the northern lords, a border war reprisal, they broke through the gates—"

"The histories were written by the winners. Your own book says the records burned." Wendell sat up fully now, and the dead around her seemed to lean in, dozens of voices dropping to a hush like a congregation. "Ask yourself, Scholar. Who benefits from a century of silence? Who closes passes and burns records and sends Inquisitors after paper?" She paused. "Who bars doors from the inside?"

Below them, far down the valley toward the south, a light flickered. Then another. Then a line of them, patient and orderly, moving up the old road they had left behind at dusk.

Torches. Marching in formation.

Hesk's whistle cut the night—one short note, the signal for *company, armed, coming fast*—and the camp exploded into motion, Bramble swearing, Tally kicking dirt over the fire, Ilsa clutching the book to her chest.

But Wendell stayed on her knees a moment longer, because the dead had gone silent—all of them, every voice at once—and in that enormous silence she heard, faint and clear as a bell struck once, a single new voice speaking from the direction of the torches:

*They know you're coming, carver. Run.*
