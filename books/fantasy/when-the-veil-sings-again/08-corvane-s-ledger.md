## 8. Corvane's Ledger

The smuggler died at dusk, and Inquisitor Corvane made certain it was clean.

Not merciful—mercy was a currency he did not carry—but clean. A single cut, administered by his own hand rather than a soldier's trembling one, in the yard behind the tollhouse where the man had been caught running messages out of Thornfell. The body was laid straight afterward, hands folded on its chest, because Corvane believed that even a liar deserved an orderly grave. Order was the whole of his creed. It was what separated him from butchers, and he told himself this every morning when he shaved, and most mornings he almost believed it.

"His name," said Sergeant Veyl beside him, not quite a question.

"Merric Dole." Corvane closed his ledger over the entry he had just written: *M. Dole, courier, executed for obstruction of Crown inquiry. No family claimed.* "He carried letters for the Bramble line for eleven years. He knew every pass between here and the coast."

"And he gave us nothing."

"He gave me nothing worth having." Corvane looked north, where the mountains stood black against the last red light. Somewhere up there was a scholar and a book, and somewhere beyond them, a door that had been shut for a hundred years and would stay shut if he had to fill the valleys with graves to keep it so. "That is not the same as knowing nothing."

Veyl said nothing. Veyl was a good soldier in the way of good soldiers: he understood that silence after his commander spoke was sometimes more valuable than agreement.

Corvane opened the ledger again. He did this often, though he never needed to. The ledger lived in him now, page upon page of his grandfather's handwriting, copied out by Corvane's own hand at nineteen years old as a condition of his commission—a condition no other cadet had ever been given, because no other cadet bore the name Corvane.

*Received, per instruction of the Privy Council: all maps, rolls, and registers pertaining to the province formerly designated Vael. Same to be destroyed under seal. Witnessed: Aldous Corvane, Clerk of the Erasure.*

*Clerk of the Erasure.* They had actually titled him that. His grandfather had signed it forty-one times across three years of work, in a small precise hand, and then come home to Wexbridge and raised his children and taught his grandchildren their letters from ledgers that contained, on their final pages, only ash-marks where names had been struck through.

*Struck through,* not burned outright. That was the detail that had kept young Edmun Corvane awake nights at the academy. You could burn a record in an afternoon. Striking through took patience. Someone had gone name by name, holding each one up to the light first, reading it, before drawing the line. Forty-one days of signatures meant thousands of names, read aloud or silently, and then erased by a careful, conscientious, honorable man.

Edmun had asked his father about it once, at fourteen, over supper. His father had set down his knife with great deliberation and said: *The kingdom asked our family to do a hard thing, and we did it well, and that is why you will never want for anything. Do not make it heavier than it needs to be.*

Then his mother had sent him to bed without the sweet course, which in the Corvane household was the true punishment, and the conversation had never resumed.

But the question had never stopped either. It had simply gone underground, like everything else about Vael, and grown roots.

*

By full dark Corvane had commandeered the tollhouse and turned its common room into a map room. Candles in ranks. The southern passes inked in fresh: Greywether, the Kettle, Hollow Stair, and the old drover road that wasn't on any survey but that every smuggler between here and the capital could walk blindfold.

"Seal them all," he told Veyl. "Greywether and the Kettle with garrisons. The Stair gets caltrops and a watch fire every quarter mile. And the drover road—" he tapped it twice, "—gets patrols. Not checkpoints. Patrols. I don't want travelers stopped and questioned. I want anyone moving at night found before they know they've been seen."

"That's a lot of men for a road nobody admits exists."

"Nobody admits it exists because everybody uses it." Corvane rolled the map closed. "Which means somebody in Thornfell has already sold them passage, or will by morning. Find out who. Quietly."

"And if they resist?"

"Then write their names in my ledger." He heard how it sounded as soon as he said it, and hated that he heard it. Veyl's face did nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer. "Detain first," Corvane amended. "Force is for those who force it."

But the words were already written, in the way words are. *Find out who.* As if the town were a lockbox and its people the tumblers, and it were merely a matter of applying pressure until something gave. He had done exactly that to Merric Dole this evening, and Merric Dole had died of it, and Corvane had written *no family claimed* in a steady hand while thinking: *that isn't true. Everyone is someone's family. I have simply decided their claiming doesn't count.*

His grandfather would have understood. That was the worst of it. Aldous Corvane would have read the entry, nodded once, and turned the page.

*

He slept badly, in the tollhouse's back room, on a straw pallet that smelled of the last traveler's pipe smoke. He dreamed of his grandfather's study—not a memory, precisely, but a reconstruction built from a child's impressions: the green lamp, the smell of sealing wax, the tall cabinet whose bottom drawer was always locked.

In the dream he was nine years old again and the drawer was open.

Inside there was a book. Not the prophecy—that he would not learn of until decades later, when the Crown's archivists flagged a reference to it in a confiscated letter—but a census roll, Vaeli script marching down columns in a beautiful old hand, and his grandfather standing over it with a pen dipped in ash-black ink, striking through a name. And looking up, and seeing him, and saying, in a voice of terrible gentleness:

*You see, Edmund. This is how a country forgets. One name at a time, carefully, so it can be called mercy.*

*It was mercy,* dream-Edmund said, because he had been trained to say it, because the whole edifice of his life stood on saying it. *They were rebels. The massacre saved bloodshed later. The erasure ended the rebellion. Grandfather's work made peace.*

*Aldous smiled, and struck through another name, and another, and Corvane woke with his heart slamming and the candle dead and the taste of ash in his mouth.*

He lit the candle. Sat up. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the dark went orange.

It had been rebels. It *had*. The histories were clear—the histories that remained. Vael had harbored sedition, hoarded grain during the famine years, refused the crown's levies, and when the king's justice finally came to the mountain court, the court had chosen resistance and paid the price. A tragedy of their own making. The erasure that followed had been policy, not malice: a wounded kingdom healing itself by forgetting the wound.

And yet.

And yet the archives had been sealed *first*, before any verdict was published. He had checked that himself, two years ago, late at night, telling himself it was diligence. The seal order preceded the tribunal's findings by six weeks. You did not erase a rebel province's records before proving it rebellious. You did it the other way around—or you did it because the records proved something else entirely.

Six weeks. He had carried that number for two years like a splinter too deep to draw.

His family's honor rested on the lie staying buried. More than honor. Lands, title, the commission itself—three generations of Corvanes had been paid, in one form or another, out of the erasure. If the door opened and the truth walked through wearing bones and speaking names, everything his name meant would burn with it. And he would deserve it, some cold honest corner of him admitted, because he had spent thirty years polishing the lie instead of testing it.

He was not a stupid man. He had simply decided, long ago, that being a loyal one was safer, and had let the decision stand unexamined the way you leave a load-bearing wall alone in an old house.

Now the house was shaking anyway.

*

Dawn brought the report he'd been dreading.

"A bone-carver," said Lieutenant Ashe, a sharp-faced woman he'd pulled from the garrison at Greywether. "Wendell Lockridge. She does the memorial work for half the valley—smugglers, miners, drowned kids off the river barges. Everyone loves her. Nobody knows anything about her."

"Why does she interest us?"

"The scholar stayed with her. Two nights. And—" Ashe hesitated, and Corvane marked it; hesitation in a report was usually a thing the reporter wished she hadn't learned. "There's talk. Old talk, the kind grandmothers do. That the carver hears things. That she knows things about the dead she shouldn't."

Corvane set down his pen. Outside, the patrol mounts were being saddled, and the sound of them came through the window like a slow drum.

"Hears things," he repeated.

"Folk-talk, sir. Probably nothing."

Probably nothing. Except that the prophecy fragment quoted in the confiscated letter had spoken of *the one who listens*, and except that Merric Dole had died with a strange smile and one word on his lips when the end came close, a word the sergeant had reported reluctantly: *carver*.

"He said *carver*," Corvane said aloud, and watched Ashe's face change. "Yes. I thought so too."

"You think she's the one the book names."

"I think a century-old book names a date, and the date is nearly here, and the only person in this valley the dead might trouble themselves to speak about is a woman who talks to them for a living." He rose, took up his coat. "I'll speak with her myself. Before noon. And Ashe—if she's warned them, if they're already on the mountain, I want to know which way she looks when she lies to me."

"And if she won't talk?"

"She'll talk." It came out flat and certain and he despised the certainty even as he used it. "Everyone talks. My grandfather proved it ten thousand times."

Ashe left. Corvane stood a moment at the window, watching the sun climb the eastern ridge, and thought about the locked drawer, and the six weeks, and the names struck through one careful line at a time.

Then he opened his ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in his own small precise hand—the same hand, he realized, the same inherited slant and pressure, as though the pen itself remembered:

*W. Lockridge, carver, Thornfell. To be interviewed.*

Below it, after a moment, he added a second line, smaller:

*If she is the listener, do not harm her. Whatever else is true, we owe the listening ones that much.*

He did not examine why he wrote it. Some walls you leave alone.

He buckled his sword belt, called for his horse, and rode for Thornfell with the morning fog still clinging to the fields—and behind him, far up in the high passes, unseen by any patrol, eight figures moved along a road that was not on any map, carrying a book toward a door, and one of them, the quiet one with the carving tools, paused at the crest of a rise and looked back south, directly, impossibly, toward the distant speck that was him.

The dead had told her he was coming.

He spurred the horse, and did not let himself wonder how.
