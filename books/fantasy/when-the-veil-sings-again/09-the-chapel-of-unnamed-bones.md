## 9. The Chapel of Unnamed Bones

I'd smuggled everything a body could put a price on—salt, silk, guns, saints' fingers, once an entire disassembled river barge over three passes in winter. So when I say the chapel was the strangest cargo I ever carried, understand I mean it as a professional assessment.

We found it two hours past sundown, or rather Hesk's horse found it, stumbling off the trail with a hoof gone soft and Hesk swearing in three dialects. The storm came down the mountain behind us like something owed money, and there wasn't shelter for miles except—and Wendell pointed, calm as a woman ordering tea—a fold in the rock that hadn't been there this morning.

"That's not a fold in the rock," Tally said.

"It is tonight," said Wendell.

The chapel sat inside the fold like a pearl in an oyster. Small, older than anything Crown-built, its walls fitted stone to stone without mortar that I could see. No bell tower. No consecration mark. Just a door of black wood gone silver at the edges, and above it, carved deep enough to survive weather: no words at all. A pattern of interlocking lines.

"Vaeli," Ilsa whispered. She'd had that look all day, scholar's hunger fighting traveler's exhaustion and losing badly. "That's Vaeli knotwork. That's—this shouldn't be here."

"Then we won't tell anyone," I said, and shouldered the door open.

* * *

Inside smelled of cold stone and old dust and nothing else, which was wrong. Chapels smell of incense, of tallow, of the people who've prayed in them. This one smelled like nobody had breathed in it for a hundred years.

Hesk got his boot off and his ankle wrapped by firelight while Tally rigged the door shut with rope and spite. Ilsa stood just inside the entrance with her arms wrapped around herself, staring at the far wall. I did the captain thing first—checked sightlines, counted our stores, made sure my mare was settled in what had probably once been a vestry—and only then let myself look at what she was looking at.

Bones. Racks of them, floor to ceiling along both walls. Skulls stacked in careful rows, long bones laid crosswise like cordwood, ribs fanned out in patterns that were almost decorative until you realized they weren't decoration at all. They were arrangement. Somebody had done this deliberately, lovingly, and then walked away.

An ossuary. I'd seen them before—the big one under the cathedral in Corvale where they stack the city's paupers ten thousand deep. But those bones are anonymous because anonymity is the point. Pauper bones go in nameless; that's the whole arrangement.

These bones had been *un*-named. That was different. You don't take names off bones unless someone worked hard to put them there first.

"Wendell," I said, because she'd gone still by the nearest rack, her lantern held up, her face doing something complicated.

"I know these voices," she said.

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "You've been here before?"

"No." She turned. Her eyes were wet but her voice was steady, the way it gets when she's working hardest at being calm—I'd learned that about her somewhere on the ghost-road, the way you learn the tells of any crew member. "I mean I know their voices. From the pass. From the road. They've been talking since we crossed the old border, getting louder, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a woman here who complains about her shoes. Has complained about them for a hundred years. They gave her court slippers for the ceremony because the formal pair didn't fit, and she knew she'd be buried in them."

Tally had stopped rigging the door. Hesk sat up straight despite his ankle. Even Ilsa had quit breathing, near enough.

"There's a man," Wendell went on, turning slowly, reading the racks like ledger pages, "who sang. Badly. He knows he sang badly and he sang anyway, at every feast, because his sister loved it. There's a girl—she's not on the racks, she's—" Wendell crossed to the corner where a small casket sat apart from the others, child-sized. Her voice came out sanded thin. "She's asking if the storm woke anyone else."

Nobody spoke. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside the wind found a crack in the mountain and played it like a reed.

"Bramble." Wendell looked at me, and underneath everything else in her face was a question, almost shy. "Can I?"

It took me a moment to work out what she was asking permission for. Then it landed, and I understood why she'd asked me instead of Ilsa, why me, the smuggler, the captain, the one whose word governed this crew in the places law doesn't reach.

She wanted to say the names aloud. The rule she'd lived by her whole life—never repeat what the dead say—was standing in front of her wearing a century of dust, asking to be broken.

"It's your cargo," I said. "Your call."

* * *

Here's what I know about grief, having carried plenty of other people's across plenty of borders: it wants witnesses. That's the whole trade. A grief with no witness curdles into something else—shame, mostly, or rage—and I've hauled enough of both to last several lifetimes.

Wendell stood in front of that wall of bones and gave them their witness.

She started with the woman and the shoes. "Maren Ostrevik," Wendell said, and the name fell into the chapel like a coin into a well. "She was the Keeper of the Queen's Wardrobe. She hated the ceremony because ceremonies meant formal shoes." A pause, her head tilted, listening. "Her daughter survived. Maren doesn't know that. She's been asking about her daughter for a hundred years and nobody's answered."

"We can answer," Ilsa said. Her voice cracked on it.

And so it went. Wendell moved down the racks and the dead came off them one by one, not as bones but as people, which is the only miracle I've ever witnessed firsthand and I'll stand by it. The bad singer was Lord Aldric Vane, Master of Revels, and his sister had been Lady Cessily, and Cessily's skull sat two rows up and Wendell named her too and told how Aldric used to slip extra honey-cakes onto her plate because she was thin and worried. The chamberlain had hidden the royal seal in a bread loaf during the massacre and been killed carrying it toward the mountains—he'd been trying to get it to safety, all this time the histories called him a thief, and Wendell relayed his correction with such flat anger that I felt it in my teeth.

The guard captain. The herbalist who'd treated soldiers on both sides and been shot for it by her own. The stable boys. Two of the stable boys were fourteen.

Tally cried first. She tried to hide it, the way she hides everything, shoulders up around her ears, but Hesk reached over without looking and pulled her down to sit against him and she went, and after that there wasn't much point in anyone pretending. I didn't cry. Captains don't, on duty. But I'll admit my eyes were running, because the chapel was cold, that's all, and I'm fifty-one years old and my mother taught me that dead strangers aren't your problem.

My mother also taught me never to carry cargo I haven't inspected, and I'd spent forty years hauling other people's silences without once opening the box. Sitting in that chapel, hearing a dead queen's seamstress complain that the embroidery on her burial shroud was crooked, I finally understood what I'd been complicit in. Not the massacre. The quieter crime after it. The erasure. Every unnamed bone in every unmarked pile in every border town like Thornfell, where I'd grown rich moving goods through passes built on somebody's grave and never once asked whose.

"You're doing that thing," Hesk murmured beside me.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're rewriting your own ledger." He nodded at Wendell, mid-name, lantern light catching the wet on her cheeks. "Stop it. You brought us here. That counts."

"Bringing people to a graveyard counts for nothing."

"It counts for showing up," he said. "Ask any soldier. Showing up is ninety percent of the job."

* * *

Somewhere past midnight, Ilsa opened the prophecy book.

I want to be clear that none of us told her to. She'd been sitting apart, holding the book like it might bite, and eventually she set it on her knees and began copying—names, dates, titles—in the margin, in that cramped scholar's hand, cross-referencing Wendell's spoken list against whatever the book contained. When Wendell faltered on a name—I could see her straining, listening harder—Ilsa would offer a guess and Wendell would nod or shake her head, and between the two of them, the living book and the living voice, the roster filled in.

"How many?" I finally asked.

"Forty-three," Wendell said, hoarse. "So far. There's a lower level. Under the floor." She pointed at flagstones near the altar that I'd have sworn were ordinary. "Servants. They were sealed in faster, no ceremony. Their voices are muddier. It'll take longer."

"Then we're staying longer," I said.

Ilsa looked up. "Captain Bramble—"

"Don't 'Captain Bramble' me, scholar. We've got a wounded man who can't ride till morning regardless, a storm that means to kill us, and an Inquisitor who's a day behind us at best. We're not going anywhere. And while we sit here waiting to die or not die, we're going to do the one thing nobody's done for these people in a hundred years." I jerked my chin at the racks. "We're going to learn their names."

Corvane would burn this chapel. That was certain as taxes. Whatever his reasons—and I'd heard enough secondhand about the Inquisitor to know he had reasons, real ones, the dangerous kind—those racks were evidence, and evidence of the founding lie doesn't get archived. It gets ash. Which meant every name Wendell pulled out of the dark tonight was a name rescued from a second death, and I have never in my life refused a rescue contract, no matter the pay.

So we stayed. Tally fetched water. Hesk propped his ankle and acted as clerk, scratching each name into his notebook in case the rest of us died and someone found his body first. And Wendell worked her way down to the flagstones, knelt, pressed her palm flat against the cold stone, and listened to the servants of a murdered court come up out of the ground one voice at a time.

* * *

Near dawn, the small casket.

Wendell had saved it. I understood why without her saying—the hardest cargo goes last, that's just good practice. She knelt beside it for a long time before she touched the lid, and when she lifted it, the lantern light showed a girl of six or seven, curled like she was sleeping, dressed in white that had gone the color of candle wax.

"She's not afraid anymore," Wendell said softly. "She was scared for a long time. But the others found her. The lady with the crooked shroud has been telling her stories." A breath. "Her name is Lira. Lira Ostrevik."

"Maren's daughter," Ilsa whispered. "The one who survived."

"No." Wendell shook her head slowly. "Maren thinks her daughter survived. Maren's daughter is Lira. The one who actually escaped was Maren's son." Wendell's hand hovered over the small bones, not quite touching. "Maren's been calling into the dark for a hundred years for a girl who's right here, and a boy who got away, and she doesn't know either thing."

The silence after that had weight. I felt it settle on all of us.

"So tell her," Tally said. Her voice was wrecked and furious and young. "Tell her now. Both things."

"Tally—" Hesk started.

"No. No, I mean it." She was on her feet, pointing at Wendell, at the casket, at the whole impossible chapel. "This whole trip everyone keeps saying the dead can't know, the dead can't hear, we can't change what happened. Fine. But she can know *this*. Right now. Tonight. Tell the mother her boy made it out. Tell her where her girl is. What's the point of—" her voice broke and she shoved through it— "what's the point of hearing them if you can't give them *that*?"

And Wendell looked at her for a long moment, and then at me, and I shrugged, because it wasn't my call and it wasn't really against any rule I recognized.

Wendell turned back to the racks. Found the row. Leaned close, the way you lean toward a sleeping person.

"Maren," she said. "Your son Tomas reached the coast. He lived. He had children. There are people alive today with your eyes." A pause—listening—and then, gentler than I'd heard her speak to anyone: "And your daughter is here. She was here the whole time. She's not alone. She's been listening to your stories."

What happened next, I'll tell it plain, because I've told it fancy in taverns since and fancy doesn't hold. The temperature in the chapel rose. Not dramatically—no wind, no lights—but like a door opening somewhere onto a warm room. The lantern flames leaned. And Wendell made a sound I've never heard a person make, half laugh and half sob, and said:

"She knows. She says—she says thank you. She says she can stop shouting now."

Tally slid down the wall and cried properly, no hiding. Hesk put the notebook aside. Ilsa pressed both hands over the prophecy book like she was keeping it warm. And I sat in an ossuary full of the murdered court of a country my country pretended never existed, and did the only honest thing left to do.

I took off my hat.

* * *

We buried Lira at first light, or as close to burying as the frozen ground allowed—we laid her casket beneath the window where the sunrise would reach it, and Wendell cut a small piece of pine and carved her name into it with the bone-knife she carries everywhere, quick and sure, letters like birds. Maren's too. Forty-five names by then, and Wendell carved them all into the pine board and set it over the door where the knotwork pattern ran, filling in the blank space a century had left.

She stepped back and looked at it, and I watched her realize, watching it, that she couldn't undo it. The names were public now. Carved. Witnessed by four living people and however many dead ones were still hanging around.

"Corvane will want that board," I said quietly, coming to stand beside her.

"I know."

"And the book. And Ilsa. And probably you."

"I know that too."

Down the trail below the chapel, small with distance but unmistakable, riders. Six of them, maybe eight, moving fast through the thinning storm, and at their head a figure in grey who didn't ride like a soldier rides but like a blade being carried.

Tally saw them first after me and swore with real artistry. Hesk started pulling his boot on over the bandage, jaw tight. Ilsa clutched the book.

But Wendell kept looking at the carved board over the door, and when she finally turned to me, her face was calm in a way that scared me more than the riders did.

"They knew he was coming," she said. "They've known all night. Maren says—" Wendell paused, listening to something I couldn't hear, and her mouth curved, not kindly. "Maren says the man in grey is angrier than he lets himself feel. She says anger like that is a locked room, and locked rooms always open."

Below us, the lead rider raised a hand, and the column halted.

"Well," I said, reaching for my hat. "Let's hope she's right."
