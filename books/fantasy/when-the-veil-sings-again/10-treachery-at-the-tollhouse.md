## 10. Treachery at the Tollhouse

The tollhouse at Marrow Bridge had been Tally's second home for six years, back when her only crimes were small ones. She'd learned to pick a lock in its cellar, learned which of the ferryman's daughters could be bribed with ribbon and which needed coin, learned that the woman who ran the toll—Marta Quill, wide as a doorway, sharp as a fish hook—kept a ledger in her head worth more than any magistrate's records.

So it was Tally who led them off the ghost-road and onto the honest one, saying, "We can cross the river here. Marta owes me."

Bramble squinted at the bridge through the drizzle. It was a squat stone thing, older than Thornfell, with a tollhouse crouched at the near end like a toad waiting for flies. Beyond it the road climbed toward the high passes—the fast way, the way Corvane's riders would expect them to take eventually.

"Marta owes you," Bramble repeated. "That's a debt or a grudge?"

"Both." Tally grinned. "Best kind. She'll charge us double and warn us true."

Wendell stood a little apart, hood up, listening to something none of them could hear. Lately she did that more and more as they climbed eastward, her head tilting like a bird's, her lips moving around words she never let out. Hesk had taken to walking beside her without asking questions, which Tally thought was its own kind of tenderness from a man who'd spent nine years in the Crown's army learning not to be tender.

"We shouldn't stop long," Wendell said quietly. Not to anyone. To whatever she was hearing.

"How long?" Ilsa asked. The scholar had the book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest these days, carried the way a mother carries a child, and she hadn't slept properly since the chapel. Tally had watched her try. Everyone had.

"An hour," Wendell said. Then, after a silence: "She says less."

"She?" Tally asked.

"The bridge." Wendell said it flatly, as if it were nothing, as if bridges spoke. But her knuckles were white on her strap. "It remembers being built. There were Vaeli stonemasons on the crew. It wants—I think it wants us to hurry."

Hesk checked the sky, then the road behind them, then the road ahead, in that order, the way he did everything. "Then we hurry."

* * *

Marta Quill had gone grey at the temples since Tally last saw her, but she hadn't gotten slower. She looked at Tally across the tollhouse counter—past Bramble's easy smile, past Hesk's soldier's stillness, past the scholar clutching her bundle—and her eyes did a quick cold arithmetic that Tally recognized because she'd inherited it from this very room.

"Five travelers," Marta said. "No cargo. No cart. Heading east in autumn, off-season, in the rain." She leaned on the counter. "Tally Fenn. You always were trouble with a face on it."

"Two coppers each, same as ever."

"Four each."

"Three."

"Three and you tell me what you're carrying that makes a deserter and a bone-witch travel together."

Tally kept the grin nailed on. Inside, something went very still. *Bone-witch.* That word wasn't hers. Nobody in Thornfell called Wendell that. That word had come down out of the capital with the Inquisitor's writs.

"Cheese," Tally said. "And a very boring book."

Marta held her gaze for a long moment. Rain drummed the tollhouse roof. Somewhere behind the building, water worried at the bridge pilings, and Tally thought—irrationally, ridiculously—that she could almost hear it too, a low patient hum under the rain.

Then Marta counted their coins, stamped their passage chit, and jerked her chin toward the bridge. "Cross before dark. And Tally—" her voice dropped, and for one heartbeat Tally saw the old woman who'd taught her which magistrates took bribes and which took fingers, "—there was a man here yesterday. Grey coat. Paid me gold to send word if anyone matching your description came through. I didn't send anything."

"But?"

"But my boy works the far side now. And my boy's debts are his own business." Marta turned away, suddenly busy with her ledger. "Go."

Tally didn't breathe right until they were all four onto the span, the river loud beneath them, grey and swollen with autumn. Halfway across, she told them.

Hesk stopped walking immediately. He planted himself mid-bridge, facing back the way they'd come, and became exactly the kind of obstacle he'd been trained to be. "How much does the son owe?"

"I don't know. Gambling, probably. He was always stupid with coin."

"Grey coat means Inquisitorial outriders, not Corvane himself," Hesk said. "Six men, maybe eight. They'll have ridden hard to beat us here. If they're coming, they're close."

"Then we don't wait to find out," Bramble said. She'd gone quiet-serious, which Tally knew was worse than loud-serious. "Off the bridge, into the trees on the far side, and we cut north before the road bends. There's an old drover's track—"

"THERE!"

The shout came from behind them, thin with distance but not with certainty. Tally spun.

Riders. Six of them, bursting from the tree line on the western bank, mud flying, grey coats dark with rain. One raised a horn to his lips.

"No," Hesk said, mostly to himself. "Not the horn."

He moved.

Later, Tally would try to remember it as a sequence of decisions, because that was how Hesk talked about fighting—as arithmetic. Nine years in the Crown's army had taught him that a fight was just numbers done quickly: numbers of bodies, angles, seconds. He calculated that the narrowest point of the bridge was the middle, where the parapet funneled everything to a lane two men wide, and that six riders could not arrive six abreast, and that every second the first horse spent refusing to trample him was a second the rest of them spent running.

So he stood in the middle of Marrow Bridge with his sword drawn—a deserter's blade, worn and honest—and made himself a number that didn't work.

"HESK!" Bramble screamed it.

"GO!" he roared back, and there was no argument in it, none, the voice of a man who had already finished the sum. "Get them over! GO!"

They went. Of course they went; that was the whole horror of it. Tally grabbed Ilsa's sleeve and hauled, and Wendell was already moving, and Bramble covered the rear walking backward with her hand-axe out, and behind them came the sounds Tally knew she would hear for the rest of her life: the scream of a horse forced toward a man who wouldn't move, steel ringing on steel, Hesk's voice bellowing the old army cadence—*"hold, hold, HOLD"*—as if the bridge itself were a formation he could command.

And under it all, rising through the stone under Tally's boots, a sound like singing. Low, furious, vast. The bridge remembered Vaeli masons. The bridge, apparently, remembered whose side it was on.

The first rider went down screaming when his horse shied at the parapet as if shoved. Tally didn't look. Looking cost time, and time was what Hesk was buying at a price she couldn't count yet.

They reached the far bank. Bramble caught Tally's arm. "Treeline. Now. We hold there and we take him back."

"He told us to—"

"I know what he told us." Bramble's face was rain and iron. "We're not leaving our dead on a bridge, girl, and we're not leaving our living either. Get to the trees. Count to three hundred. If we're not there—"

"If you're not there, I'm coming back," Tally said.

Something flickered across Bramble's face—not surprise, maybe pride, maybe grief arriving early. Then she turned and ran back onto the bridge, and Tally dragged Ilsa into the trees and counted.

At forty, the horn finally sounded—but wrong, strangled, cut short. At sixty, a horse shrieked somewhere in the river mist. At ninety, the singing under the ground swelled so loud that even Ilsa heard it; she pressed both hands to her ears and stared at Tally with huge wet eyes. "What is that? What is *that?*"

"The bridge," Tally said. "I think the bridge is helping."

At one hundred and ten, Bramble came out of the rain half-carrying Hesk, and Tally stopped counting forever.

* * *

They laid him under a rock overhang off the drover's track, and Tally wished desperately that they hadn't, because once he was lying still he looked like exactly what he was: a man opened up.

The blade had gone in under his ribs on the left side, a rider's downward stroke that Hesk had half-turned so it carved him instead of skewering him. His left arm hung wrong. Blood soaked his shirt in a way that kept spreading no matter how much linen Bramble pressed into it.

"Keep pressure," Wendell said. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "Keep pressure and don't let him sleep."

"I'm awake," Hesk said. It came out as a whisper. He tried to smile at Tally and managed perhaps a third of one. "You counted. Good girl. Knew you'd—come back."

"You idiot," Tally said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle. "You absolute—six of them, Hesk, six, and you just *stood there*—"

"Arithmetic." A bubble of blood at his lip; he coughed it clear. "Narrowest point. Couldn't—fit more than two abreast. I only had to be worth two men." His eyes found hers, fierce even now. "I was worth five."

Bramble laughed, one harsh note, and it broke into something else halfway out. She bent her head over his arm and stayed there.

Ilsa knelt and unwrapped the book—not to read it, Tally realized, but to tear a strip of clean linen from inside its wrapping, giving up the oilcloth protection without hesitation, pressing it to the wound alongside Bramble's hands. The scholar's mouth was moving silently, and Tally caught fragments of it, words in no language she knew, and decided she was better off not asking whether it was prayer or prophecy.

Wendell crouched at Hesk's head. She had gone very pale, paler than the wounded man, and she was looking at him the way she looked at things she heard.

"What," Tally demanded. "What do you hear? Tell me."

Wendell flinched. "Nothing."

"Liar."

"Not—he isn't dying," Wendell said slowly, and the words came out like stones pried loose. "But there are others here. Under the track. Old soldiers. They keep saying—" her jaw worked, "—they keep saying *the wound is honest.* I don't know what that means."

"It means," Hesk whispered, eyes half-closed, "the army taught me to take a blade like a man. Finally—found a use for it." His breath hitched. "Listen to me. All of you. Corvane's riders will report. He'll know your faces now, know you crossed Marrow Bridge. He'll close the high road and sweep the drover's tracks. You have maybe—half a day."

"So we carry you," Bramble said flatly. "We make a litter. We go slower and we go together."

"No." Hesk's hand found Bramble's wrist with surprising strength. "You split. Three move fast with the book and the carver. Two stay with me till I can ride or—" he coughed, "—or don't."

"Or don't," Bramble repeated, deadly quiet. "Say that again. I dare you."

"Bramble—"

"*Say it again.*"

Hesk looked at her, and Tally saw something pass between them that had clearly been passing between them for years, unspoken, unpaid, the kind of debt smugglers don't put in ledgers. Whatever it was, it settled him. "All right," he murmured. "We stay together. Stubborn woman."

"That's Captain Stubborn Woman."

Rain ticked on the rocks above them. Down in the valley, faintly, a second horn sounded—farther off, answering the first. Somewhere Corvane was gathering his web tighter, and Tally sat in the wet dark with her family leaking out onto the stones, and understood with terrible clarity that someone had sold them.

"Marta's boy," she said aloud. The words tasted like copper. "It was Marta's boy. She warned me and he sold us anyway, or she warned me late because he'd already sent the bird." Her fists closed. "I've known that family since I was nine years old. She taught me *everything.*"

"And he taught you something too," Ilsa said gently. She'd rewrapped the book, and her eyes over it were tired and kind. "That debts make doors. Corvane didn't break your friend's loyalty, child. He bought a stranger's. It will happen again. Men like him pay well, because most people's honor has a price and it's usually small."

"Then we stop having prices," Tally said.

Hesk laughed—wet, weak, real. "Oh, she's going to be *trouble,*" he breathed. "Captain. Keep her. She's going to be magnificent trouble."

Bramble pressed her forehead to his good shoulder and didn't say anything at all.

Wendell rose suddenly, head tilted east, listening. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, certain, the voice Tally was learning meant the dead had said something worth repeating, breaking her rule at last because the rule mattered less than the warning.

"The old soldiers under the track," she said. "They say the road ahead is watched. And they say—" she swallowed, "—they say there is one who walks with us who carries a name the book knows. And the door will ask for it by blood."

Everyone looked at Ilsa.

The scholar's hand had gone to the oilcloth bundle at her chest, and her face, in the grey light, was the face of a woman reading her own sentence in a language she'd hoped never to learn.

"The high road's watched, you said," Ilsa said at last, very quietly. "Then we'd best take the one nobody watches. My grandmother wrote about it. And I think—it's time I told you all what else she wrote."
