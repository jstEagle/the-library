## 11. The Prophet's Descendant

They made camp in the hollow of a fallen watchtower, its stones furred with moss, its broken arch framing a slice of sky gone the color of pewter. Hesk lay propped against his pack, feverish but stable, Tally fussing over him with a stubbornness that fooled no one. Bramble had taken first watch and pretended not to listen. Wendell sat across the small fire from Ilsa, sharpening a bone needle with slow, patient strokes, and waited.

She had been waiting for three days. Ilsa had felt it like a hand resting lightly on her shoulder.

"You've been chewing on something since the tollhouse," Wendell said at last, not looking up. "You get a line here." She touched the space between her own brows. "Same one every time you're about to lie."

"I'm not—"

"There it is."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through a stand of pines, and beneath it—Ilsa was almost sure, though she had no right to be sure of anything anymore—the dead were murmuring. They had been murmuring more each night as they climbed, and only Wendell seemed able to pick out individual voices, the way a mother picks her child's cry from a crowd.

Ilsa set down the prophecy book. It had traveled wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat for eleven days now, pressed against her ribs like a second heart, and every mile north it had grown heavier in a way that had nothing to do with weight.

"My name isn't Verrow," she said.

Bramble's head turned on the far wall. Tally stopped mid-fuss. Even Hesk opened his eyes.

"It is," Ilsa went on, before courage could desert her, "but it isn't all of it. My family name is Verrow-Ashe. The Ashe part was dropped four generations ago, when my great-great-grandmother decided it would be healthier to be nobody in particular." She laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "She was right, as it turns out."

"Healthier than being what?" Bramble asked, crossing the camp to sit, which she did rarely and only when she intended to stay.

Ilsa looked at Wendell. "Than being descended from Sera Ashe."

Wendell's hands went still over the bone needle. "The prophet."

"The author. Yes." Ilsa pulled the book toward her but didn't open it yet. Her fingers rested on the oiled cloth the way you'd rest them on a sleeping animal you weren't sure wouldn't bite. "Everything in this book that Corvane wants burned, everything we've been running with—my ancestor wrote it. Not transcribed it. Not collected it. Wrote it, in her own hand, in the last year before Vael fell."

"That's why you knew the passages by heart," Wendell said quietly. "Back in Thornfell. When I asked how a scholar could recite prophecy like scripture."

"I was raised on it. Literally. We said it aloud every year on the anniversary of the massacre, the whole family, standing in a row in my grandmother's parlor like children reciting a catechism." Isla heard her own voice flatten into the tone she used for lectures and despised it. "My grandmother called it keeping the flame. What it actually was, was keeping a debt."

Tally had come closer without seeming to move, the way she always did. "What kind of debt?"

Ilsa finally unwrapped the book.

* * *

It looked the same as it always had: calf-bound, water-stained, the spine repaired twice with different-colored thread because the family could never agree whether repair should be honest or invisible. She opened it to the page that had started everything—the page with the carver's name and the date—and let them see it again, though Wendell had seen it once already and had gone gray around the mouth both times.

"This half you know. The door reopens once, for one night, when the carver who hears stands before it. The dead of Vael go home or they don't; the veil thins; the mountains sing." She turned pages slowly, deliberately, letting them see her doing it. "There are forty-one leaves in what I've shown you. There were eighty-three when Sera Ashe finished writing."

The silence in the ruined tower had a texture to it. Ilsa thought: this is the moment. Say it plainly. You have hidden behind footnotes your whole life.

"The other half has been in my family since the night she wrote it. Separated. Hidden. My grandmother kept hers sewn into the lining of a winter coat she wore to church so that no one would ever think to search it. Mine—" She reached into her coat, into the inner pocket where she'd carried it against her skin since Thornfell, since the night she first understood what the date meant, and drew out a packet wrapped in waxed linen no bigger than a prayer book.

Wendell was watching her hands. Of course she was. Ilsa wondered, not for the first time, what the dead said about liars, and whether they'd been saying things about her for days.

"Why hide half a prophecy from your own book?" Bramble asked. Her voice was careful. Bramble's voice was always careful when it mattered most.

"Because of what it says."

Ilsa untied the linen. Inside was a sheaf of leaves, smaller format, different paper—Sera Ashe had written the second half years later, on whatever she could get, in exile, in a hand that deteriorated from the beautiful court script of the first book into something cramped and hurried, a woman writing fast because she believed she didn't have long.

"She survived the massacre," Ilsa said. "That's the part the histories got wrong, or the part Corvane's ancestors made sure got wrong. Sera Ashe was a lady-in-waiting and an amateur astronomer—that's why the prophecies are all keyed to dates and stars, she thought in ephemerides. She was in the observatory tower when the soldiers came for the royal family. She saw it. She wrote the first book in the months after, hiding in cellars, and then she ran south with two other survivors, and somewhere on the road she began having dreams that stopped being dreams." Ilsa swallowed. "And she wrote down what they told her. And then she spent the rest of her life trying to make sure no one ever read it."

"Why?" Tally asked, and her voice was very young suddenly.

Ilsa spread the leaves open on her knee and read aloud, because reading it silently would have been another kind of cowardice:

*"The door opens twice. Once for the dead to leave, if the carver who hears calls them by their names. But hear me, whoever finds this: there is a second threshold beneath the first. The way home for the dead and the way in for the hungry thing that ate their country lie side by side, and they are opened by the same hand. The door does not distinguish. The door devours whoever opens it—not kills, devours—takes the opener through and does not give her back, for a door must be held from within by a living soul, or it is not a door but a wound."*

She stopped. The fire crackled. Above them the wind moved in the pines and under the wind, faintly, unmistakably now, the murmur of the dead.

"So," Bramble said at last, into the quiet. "When your book says the carver who hears opens the door—"

"It means she dies," Ilsa said. "Or worse than dies. It means she goes through and stays there, holding it, forever, so that everyone else gets one night and then the door closes gently behind them and the story ends happy for everybody except the person the whole prophecy was written about." She looked up and found Wendell's eyes across the fire. They were steady. That was somehow unbearable. "I've known since I was nineteen years old what this book asks of someone. And I came looking anyway."

* * *

Afterward—they needed time afterward, all of them, and Ilsa used it to check Hesk's bandages because checking bandages was a thing hands could do while a person fell apart privately—Wendell followed her to the far wall of the tower, where the arch framed the darkening pass.

"You knew my name before you met me," Wendell said. Not accusing. Just establishing, the way she established things, like setting a chisel against stone before the first tap.

"I knew the prophecy named 'the carver who hears' and gave a date. The name was added later—in a marginal note, in my grandmother's hand. She spent thirty years searching border towns for a bone-carver with a particular reputation. Quiet woman. Keeps to herself. Locals say she's uncanny." Ilsa hugged her arms against the cold. "When I found the guild registry in Thornfell and it said Wendell Lockridge, carver of memorial bones, I nearly vomited in the street."

"And you still knocked on my door."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Because it was the question underneath every other question, and Ilsa had rehearsed answers for twenty years and none of them had ever satisfied even her. She gave the truest one she had.

"Because my family hid the second half for four generations, and four generations of hiding accomplished exactly one thing: it guaranteed that when the night came, whoever the carver was, she'd walk into it blind." Her voice cracked and she let it. "My great-great-grandmother thought she was protecting people. My grandmother thought she was protecting the family. I told myself I was protecting the text—scholar's virtue, keep the artifact intact, don't interpret, just carry it. But I carried it straight to you, Wendell. I put it in your hands. And I let you believe the worst thing waiting at the end of this road was the Inquisitor."

The wind gusted through the arch. And Ilsa—watching Wendell's face, watching her tilt her head a degree, listening the way she listened to things no one else could hear—saw something shift there.

"What are they saying?" Ilsa asked. "Right now. Can you tell?"

Wendell was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was odd, gentle in a way Ilsa hadn't heard from her before.

"They're saying your grandmother's name. Sera. Over and over. Like they're calling a child home for supper." A pause. "There's one voice older than the others. It says—" Wendell's brow furrowed, listening harder. "It says, *tell the granddaughter the hiding was the mercy, but the telling is the cure.*"

Ilsa sat down on a fallen block of stone because her legs declined to continue being consulted.

"She's here," Ilsa managed. "Sera Ashe. All this way, all these years, and she—"

"They've been following us since the chapel," Wendell said. "Since I read the names off those unmarked bones. I think hearing their names out loud after a hundred years—" She shook her head. "I think it woke something up. Or maybe it just reminded them they're allowed to want things."

From across the camp, Tally's voice floated over: "So do we turn back? Is that what the fancy paper says we should do? Because I'd like to register that turning back means walking into Corvane, and I've met Corvane."

"We don't turn back," Wendell said.

Ilsa looked up sharply. "Did you hear what I read? The door devours whoever—"

"I heard it." Wendell came and stood beside her at the arch, shoulder to shoulder against the cold, looking out at the pass where the peaks cut black shapes out of a bruise-colored sky. "I've heard the dead complain about the wrong river for ten years, Ilsa. I've carved memorials with lies set into them because grieving families wanted comfort more than accuracy. I've watched a country pretend a kingdom never existed and call it peace." Her jaw tightened. "A hundred years ago somebody decided the price of the founding lie was worth paying, and every year since, the interest has come due in bones. If the door wants a keeper—if that's the toll—I'll pay it. But I'll pay it with my eyes open, and I'll choose it myself, and no prophet, no Inquisitor, and no guilt-ridden scholar carrying her family's sins around in her coat pocket gets to make that choice for me."

It was the longest speech Ilsa had ever heard her give. It landed like a verdict and a pardon at once.

"I'm sorry," Ilsa said. "For all of it. For the lying. For the inheritance."

"Don't apologize for Sera Ashe," Wendell said. "Apologizing is for things you did. You didn't write the prophecy. You just finally read it aloud." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might someday take root. "Besides. If your family had to guard a terrible secret for a century, at least they had the decency to raise one descendant too stubborn to keep guarding it."

Behind them, Hesk coughed, and Tally shushed him, and Bramble said something low about watches and cold and people who thought they were whispering. The ordinary sounds of the found and reluctant. Ilsa wrapped the hidden leaves back in their linen and slid them home against her ribs, next to the book, next to her heart, lighter by exactly the weight of one confession.

Then Wendell went rigid beside her.

Not much—a stillness, a held breath—but Ilsa had spent eleven days learning to read her, and she knew alarm when she saw it.

"What?" Ilsa whispered. "What is it? Corvane?"

"No." Wendell was staring out through the arch at the pass, at the darkness gathering between the peaks, and her voice came out hushed and strange. "The dead just changed what they're saying. All of them. At once."

" Saying what?"

Wendell turned to look at her, and in the last light of the fire her eyes were wide with something Ilsa had never seen on her face before—wonder, raw and unguarded, the wonder of a woman who had spent her whole life pretending not to hear miracles.

"They're singing," she said. "We're close. And they're singing the road home."
