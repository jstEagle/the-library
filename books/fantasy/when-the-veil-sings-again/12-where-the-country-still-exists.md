## 12. Where the Country Still Exists

The border, when we finally crossed it, was a stream no wider than a grave.

Hesk pointed at it from his litter—Bramble had rigged a travois between two mules, and he lay on it with his bandaged side rising and falling like a bellows that had seen better centuries—and said, "That's it? I've pissed bigger rivers."

"That's not the border," Wendell said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out of her the way the dead's words sometimes did, unbidden. She stopped walking. The company stopped with her, because by now they'd learned to stop when she stopped.

"What do you mean, that's not the border?" Tally asked.

"The river's the border *now*," Wendell said. "But it moved. Sixty years ago, maybe seventy—the flood year, the one the old-timers in Thornfell still complain about. The stream cut a new channel south." She looked up the valley, where the water ran bright and indifferent over stones. "This land was never ceded. The Crown drew its maps along the old channel. Nobody ever signed anything for what's on this side."

"So we've been in Vael since lunch," Bramble said.

"Since the flood," Wendell said. "Nobody just noticed."

Tally laughed, a short sharp bark, then pressed her hand over her mouth as if laughing were contraband too. Hesk didn't laugh. He was looking at the ground beneath his litter as if expecting it to confess something.

Wendell felt it before any of them could have named it: a change in the weight of the air, like stepping from a crowded room into a chapel. For eleven days the dead had been growing louder as they climbed—fragments, whispers, the massed murmur of a massacre remembered—but here the murmur changed pitch. It didn't fade. It *settled*. Like birds coming home to roost after a long migration, folding their wings one by one.

"They're quieter here," she said. "Not gone. Quieter."

"Quieter is bad?" Tally asked.

"Quieter is *rested*," Wendell said, and found she didn't have another word for it, and didn't try.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined waystation, or what Hesk insisted was a waystation and Ilsa insisted was a tollhouse and Bramble called, with the serene authority of a woman who had bribed officials in four provinces, "a tax office, which is worse than either." Whatever it had been, its walls still stood to shoulder height, and someone had stacked the fallen stones with care rather than scattering them, and there was a hearth swept clean of ash though no one had lived here in living memory.

Ilsa sat apart with the book open on her knees, cross-legged in the firelight, reading by the flame because she said candlelight made the ink swim. Wendell watched her lips move through the old script. She'd gotten faster at it. Somewhere on the ghost-road, somewhere between the tollhouse ambush and Hesk's blood soaking into a stranger's blanket, the scholar had stopped translating and started simply *reading*.

"Ilsa." Wendell lowered herself onto the stone beside her. "What does it say about the land? About—after. What happened to the country itself."

Ilsa turned pages with a carefulness that Wendell had come to recognize as fear wearing gloves. "Officially? Nothing. Officially there is nothing to say. The Edict of Unification declared Vael dissolved, its lands incorporated, its titles extinguished. You can't dissolve a kingdom the way you dissolve salt in water, but they wrote it as if you could, and then they burned everyone who might have objected." She tapped a passage. "But this—the prophet wrote a supplement. Marginalia. Listen: '*The Crown may burn the records and rename the roads, but a country is not its records. A country is its kitchens. Its lullabies. Its graves tended by hands that remember why.*'"

"She wrote that a hundred years ago," Wendell said.

"A hundred years ago," Ilsa agreed, "and tomorrow morning, if we walk far enough, I suspect she'll be proven right."

She was proven right before noon.

* * *

The shrine appeared out of the hillside the way things appear in fog: all at once, as if it had been waiting for the weather to cooperate. It stood in a hollow where two game trails met—a low dome of fieldstone, moss-eaten, older than any ruin they'd passed, and yet not ruined at all. The stones fit. The little door at its base, sized for no grown person, hung true on leather hinges that had been replaced recently enough that the leather was still dark, not grey.

Someone oiled those hinges. Someone, within the last season.

Bramble circled it twice, professional interest warring with superstition. "Offering house," she said finally. "Mountain folk keep them. Leave bread for the dead so they don't come take it warm." She glanced at Wendell. "Except this is a long way from any village I know."

"It isn't," Wendell said. Her heart was doing something strange. "It's a long way from any village *on the map*."

Inside the little door—Tally was the only one small enough, and she went in without being asked, wriggling through with her satchel held ahead of her—there was a clay bowl with fresh barley in it, and a stub of beeswax candle burned down to a coin-sized puddle, and a wooden plaque carved with letters none of them could read except Ilsa, who read them aloud in a voice that shook on the second word:

"*For the nine-and-forty who kept the pass. Remembered. Fed. Awaited.*"

"Nine-and-forty defenders of some battle," Hesk said from his litter, gruff, dismissive, the tone he used when feeling too much.

"No," Wendell said slowly. "Awaited. Not mourned. *Awaited.*" She looked at the barley, the wax, the oiled hinges. "Whoever tends this knows they're coming back."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Even the wind seemed to hold its opinion.

Then, from up the slope, thin and high and unmistakably human, someone began to sing.

* * *

It was a work song—that much Bramble identified instantly, because smugglers know songs the way priests know prayers, and she went rigid with her hand halfway to her knife. "That's a counting song," she breathed. "Cargo-counting. We sing it in the caravanserai yards in Marrowgate—it's *ours*, it's an old caravan song—"

"It isn't yours," Ilsa whispered. "Listen to the words. The Marrowgate version has nonsense syllables where the words wore off. This is the original. She's singing *names into numbers*. Every bale counted is a name kept."

The singer was an old woman, bent under a bundle of kindling, climbing the trail toward them with the unhurried certainty of someone who has walked a path ten thousand times. She saw the camp, the mules, the litter with the wounded man, the strangers around her shrine—and did not stop, did not drop her wood, did not do any of the things a solitary woman ought to do upon finding six armed travelers at a holy place. She simply finished her verse, set down her burden, and looked at Wendell.

Looked at Wendell's hands, specifically.

"You carve," the old woman said. It wasn't a question. Her accent was one none of them shared, vowels rounded in places the lowland tongue flattened, but every word intelligible. "Bone?"

"Some bone. Stone. Horn." Wendell heard her own voice go careful. "Mostly memorial work. Names."

"Show me your knife."

It was such an odd request that Wendell, off-balance, obeyed before caution caught up. The old woman took the carving knife—Bramble tensing, Tally already drifting closer with the loose-limbed readiness that preceded trouble—and turned it in the sun, examining the blade's wear pattern, the angle of its edge, the way the handle had been worn smooth.

"Court grip," the old woman said. "Left-thumb anchor. Push-cut on the curve, never pull." She handed it back, and her eyes, when they lifted to Wendell's face, were wet and furious and triumphant all at once, which Wendell would not have believed possible in one expression until she saw it. "There are perhaps thirty people alive who cut that way, girl, and I have buried twenty-nine of them. Where is your teacher?"

"Thornfell," Wendell said. "A carver named Odile Vess. She took me in when I was nine."

"Vess." The old woman closed her eyes. Opened them. "Her grandmother was Ondraya Vess, second carver of the Court of Veils. Your line survived the burning by getting small, girl. Getting quiet. Getting *border-town*." She said the word without contempt, which was somehow worse. "And you never knew."

"I knew my cuts were—" Wendell stopped. Started again. "Odile said our style came from across the mountains. She said it like it was a debt you don't mention in front of creditors."

"It's not a debt," the old woman said. "It's an inheritance. And inheritances are how a country survives when everything else burns." She straightened as much as her spine allowed, which wasn't much, and addressed the whole camp as though delivering a verdict. "You crossed the old channel. You're standing in land that was never surrendered. There are forty-one households within a day's walk that still keep this language, still feed the shrines, still teach the songs with the names in them. We are not ghosts. We are not remnants. We are *Vaeli*, and we have been patient for a hundred years, and my grandmother told me on her deathbed: *when the carver comes whose hands remember, get ready, because the door will not stay shut past her.*"

Every eye in the camp turned to Wendell. She felt the weight of it like snow-load on a roof.

"I'm not—" she began.

"You are," the old woman said, with terrible gentleness. "You heard us before you came, didn't you. All down the road. All up the mountain. You've been hearing us your whole life and calling it nothing." She picked up her kindling bundle again, settling it against her bent back, and nodded toward the shrine. "Feed the nine-and-forty before you sleep. They died holding that pass so the rest of us could learn to hide in plain sight. They'd want to know you by name."

She climbed away up the trail, singing again, the same verse, and this time Wendell understood the numbers: she was counting the households. Forty-one. Counting them like a miser counts coins, or a general counts soldiers, or a prophet counts days.

* * *

That evening, alone for once—Bramble checking the mules, Tally asleep sitting upright the way she always denied doing—Isla reading, Hesk sleeping a real healing sleep for the first time since the tollhouse—Wendell knelt at the little door and filled the clay bowl with barley from their own dwindling stores.

The moment her fingers touched the grain, the dead of Vael spoke to her all together for the first time. Not whispers. Not fragments. A voice, clear as a struck bell:

"*Carver. You brought the book. You bring the hands. Tomorrow, ask the old woman what waits on the other side of the door—and ask her why the prophet's family never told their own daughter the second half.*"

Wendell went cold. Across the fire, Ilsa looked up from her reading, and their eyes met, and Wendell saw in the scholar's face the same dawning horror she felt in her own chest—

Because Ilsa had been reading the marginalia aloud for days. And tonight, for the first time, she had stopped. And hidden the page.
