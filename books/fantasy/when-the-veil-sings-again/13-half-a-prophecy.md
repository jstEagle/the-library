## 13. Half a Prophecy

I'd smuggled everything in my time. Silk past tollhouses. Salt past tax-men. A fugitive countess once, folded into a wine crate with air holes bored where nobody would look. But I'd never smuggled a book open before a fire while everyone I loved argued about whether it was going to kill us.

The chapel at Vael's edge had gone quiet after Wendell named the bones. Too quiet, the kind of quiet that comes after a funeral when the food's been eaten and the neighbors have gone home and you're left standing in the doorway wondering who you are now. The dead of the ossuary had settled back into their shelves, satisfied or grieving—I couldn't tell which—and we'd spread Ilsa's hidden pages across the altar like a hand of cards nobody wanted to play.

Tally held the candle. Hesk lay propped against the pew where we'd carried him, gray as tallow but breathing, and watching us with the flat patience of a man who'd already decided how he wanted to be buried and was just waiting to see if we'd argue. Wendell sat closest to the pages. Of course she did. She'd been the one to find them—sewn into the spine of the prophecy book itself, a second text pressed so thin against the first that a century of scholars had thumbed right past it without ever knowing they were touching two books instead of one.

It was Tally who spotted it. Eleven years old and the sharpest eyes on any road from here to the sea.

"The stitching's wrong," she'd said, an hour ago, turning the book over in her small brown hands. "Look. Somebody re-bound this and they didn't hide it good." And then she'd picked the seam apart with one of her picking-knives, the way she picks everything apart, locks and purses and other people's tempers, and there it was: a folded quire of vellum, thin as onion skin, dense with a cramped hand that was not the prophet's flowing script.

Now Ilsa read it aloud, slowly, translating as she went, and I watched my family come apart by candlelight.

"Here," Ilsa said. Her finger trembled along a line. "The second half begins with a warning. *What the first half promises, the second half qualifies.* That's—she wrote it twice, actually, underlined it. She underlined very few things."

"Read the line," Corvane said from the door.

Even now he wouldn't sit with us. He stood apart, armor dulled, sword still on his hip though he hadn't reached for it since the tollhouse. Since his own soldiers had turned their faces away from him rather than follow his order. He looked like a man standing in the ruins of his own house trying to remember why he'd built it.

"Read the line," he said again.

"*There are two doors,*" Ilsa read. "*Not one. The kingdom has two thresholds, as a house has a door for leaving and a door for entering, and no wise household confuses them.*"

Wendell made a sound. Small. I'd have missed it if I didn't know her—the sound she makes instead of saying oh no.

"Go on," I said.

"*The first door opens outward. It is the door of the dead, and it opens only once, and only to those who speak both tongues: the living voice and the dead voice, together, willing. Through it the slain go home, and the debt of Thornfell's founding is paid, and the lie is unmade.*"

"That's what we thought," Hesk said. His voice came out sanded down, weak, and he hated it—you could see him hating it. "That's the whole trick. Names spoken, door opens, dead go home, we all go get drunk."

"Keep reading," Wendell said quietly.

Ilsa kept reading.

"*But the second door opens inward.*"

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. I want that written down somewhere, because people will call me a liar later: the flame leaned toward the pages, and Tally cupped her hand around it, and nobody said anything about it because there was nothing sane to say.

"*The second door is the door of the hungry,*" Ilsa read. "*It opens inward, and what waits behind it is not the dead of Vael. The dead of Vael wait outside both doors, patient, because they know the difference. But there are older things beneath the mountain that were shut in when the kingdom rose, and the massacre weakened the seal, and if the threshold is opened wrongly—if opened in grief alone, or anger alone, or opened by a single voice however pure—then it is not a door that opens but a wall that falls.*"

Silence. Long enough for Hesk to shift against the pew and stifle a groan between his teeth.

"And she wrote this a hundred years ago," I said. "Before any of it happened."

"She saw the night of the massacre," Ilsa said. "And she saw what came after. Both endings. That's why her family split the prophecy. The first half was meant to survive—the hope half, the door half. The second half was meant to be destroyed, or hidden so deep that only—" Ilsa stopped. Swallowed.

"Only someone desperate enough to tear apart a priceless book," I finished for her.

"I'm sorry." Ilsa's voice cracked clean through. "I've carried it since I was nineteen. My mother made me memorize where the stitches ran before she let me learn anything else. I thought—gods help me, I thought the second half was a forgery. Something the Crown planted to frighten people off. I never read it until three nights ago, in your wagon, Bramble, and by then I couldn't stop reading it, and I couldn't stop reading it *aloud*, and Hesk was asleep two feet away listening to every word."

Hesk shrugged, wincing. "Sleep through worse sermons."

"It's not a sermon." Wendell spoke for the first time in minutes, and when she did, every head turned, because Wendell almost never spent words where silence would serve. "She's right that it changes things. Listen to the rest before anyone decides anything. There's more. Isn't there, Ilsa?"

Ilsa's jaw tightened. She turned the page.

"*Know the signs of the true door,*" she read. "*When it opens rightly, the dead will sing, and the song will move outward, up the pass, away from the mountain's root. When the wrong door opens, there will be no singing. There will be a silence that moves. Flee from a silence that moves.*"

"Flee from a—" Tally laughed, high and thin. "Flee where? We're going *to* it. We're walking our little wagons straight up to a moving silence and knocking politely."

"We're doing what we swore," I said.

"Are we?" Tally rounded on me, and there it was—the thing I'd been dreading since the tollhouse, since Hesk bled out half his blood on the boards buying us forty seconds. Not fear exactly. Fear I could work with; fear was cargo like anything else, you just packed it tight and hauled it. This was worse. This was her looking at all of us like we'd already died and she was the only one who'd noticed. "Bramble. Bramble, look at me. Hesk can't fight. Half of us can't walk fast. Corvane's soldiers let us go but the Crown'll send others, worse ones, and you're telling me we keep climbing toward a door that might not even be a door?"

"It's a door," Wendell said.

"You don't *know* that! You hear dead people! That's not the same as knowing!"

"Tally." I put as much weight in it as I could without making it a wall. "Sit down."

"No." She stayed standing, fists at her sides, chin up, and gods, she looked like me at eleven, before the roads taught me better manners. "No, I want somebody to say it plain. Say we might be carrying Hesk up a mountain to die. Say the book says so. Say it out loud and then watch everybody do that thing you do, that look-at-each-other-and-decide-for-me thing, because nobody wants to be the one who killed him."

Nobody moved. The candle burned steady again, mocking me.

So I said it. "We might be carrying Hesk up a mountain to die. The book says the wrong opening brings something else through. It says a single voice isn't enough. It says flee from a silence that moves." I looked around the chapel, at each of them in turn, because if we were going to fracture I wanted the crack to run through me first. "There. Plain enough. Now somebody say the other half."

"The other half is that they've waited a hundred years," Wendell said.

Her voice didn't rise. It never rises. But it filled the chapel the way water fills a cup, right to the brim, and the bones on their shelves seemed to lean in.

"A hundred years, Tally. They were slaughtered and erased. Their names were pulled off the stones. Their country was renamed, remapped, lied about until the lie wore smooth as a river rock and everyone downstream forgot it was ever a stone. And they waited. Patiently. Because they knew the difference between the doors." She gestured at the pages. "That's what the second half actually tells us, if you read it whole. Not *don't open it*. Read it again. It says *open it rightly*. Two voices. Living and dead, willing, together. The prophet wasn't warning us off the door. She was warning us off doing it cheap."

"Or," Corvane said, "she was a madwoman who wrote two books and buried the sensible one, and you're all marching to her grave because a carver hears voices."

"You don't get to talk," Tally snapped.

"He gets to talk," Hesk said mildly. "Man marched his soldiers off a cliff for us. Earned a sentence or two."

Corvane's jaw worked. Whatever lived behind that face, it wasn't gratitude—it was something rawer and less comfortable—but he let it go and addressed me directly, which I noticed he'd started doing. "Captain Bramble. You're the one who decides routes. So decide this one. Turn back now, take the southern passes while my seal still holds them closed behind you, and every one of you lives. Push on, and you're gambling the girl's life, the deserter's life, the carver's life, on the reading of a dead woman's stitchwork."

"And yours," I said.

"My what?"

"You'll be there too. Standing at the door you swore to seal. What happens to you if it opens?"

He didn't answer. Which was answer enough.

* * *

We argued until the candles guttered. I won't write down every word of it—some wounds shouldn't be mapped, even by me—but the shape of it was this:

Tally wanted to turn back. Not from cowardice—from arithmetic. She counted our able bodies, counted Corvane's scattered garrison, counted the days Hesk could ride in a wagon without reopening the wound, and the numbers came out bad, and Tally trusts numbers the way priests trust prayer. "We can come back," she kept saying. "Stronger. In spring. With proper soldiers, with the truth already loose in the lowlands, with—" and every time she got there somebody pointed out that the door opened *once*, tonight, on the date in the first half of the prophecy, and spring wasn't on offer, and she'd go quiet and start the counting over like the numbers might behave differently if she stared hard enough.

Hesk wanted to push on, which surprised nobody except insofar as he admitted the reason. "I deserted from a war that wasn't mine," he said, "and I've been paying for it ever since in small coin. Here's a war that is mine. Every man in that chapel shelf died because folk like me kept their heads down and followed orders. Let me spend what's left on something worth the spending." And when I told him he was being a damned romantic he grinned through the gray in his face and said, "Course I am. You collected me off a road, Captain. You knew what you were getting."

Corvane wanted the door sealed—still, even now, even after his ledger of lies had been read aloud to his own men—but when Wendell asked him, quietly, whether sealing it would require killing everyone in this room, he looked at his hands for a long time and finally said, "I don't know anymore." From Corvane, that was a conversion. It bought him nothing with Tally, though.

And Ilsa—

Ilsa sat apart from all of it, holding her ancestor's book like it weighed a hundred years, and when the arguing circled back around to her, she set it down on the altar between us all.

"There's one more thing," she said. "In the second half. At the end."

"Of course there is," Tally muttered.

Ilsa read it. Her voice went flat the way voices do when they're carrying more than they can hold upright:

"*The door will demand a keeper. One who stands on the far side of the threshold while it is open, who speaks the closing words when the last of the dead has passed, and who does not cross. The keeper stays. The keeper holds the door from this side until it shuts. Choose your keeper before you choose to open it, for a door opened without a chosen keeper will choose its own.*"

Nobody looked at Wendell.

Everybody looked at Wendell.

Wendell looked at the bones.

"Well," she said at last, in that same level voice, as though discussing freight rates. "At least it's decided."

"No," I said.

"Bramble—"

"*No.*" I heard my own voice crack this time, and I hated it, hated it, but there it was. "You don't get to volunteer. None of you gets to volunteer. That's not—that's not how this crew works. Nobody gets left. That's the whole rule. That's the *only* rule I've got."

"Then break it," Wendell said gently, "or break the country a second time. Those are the choices, Captain. The prophet was clear."

"The prophet was a hundred years dead and full of riddles, and I'm still deciding what goes in my own wagons."

"Are you?" Tally said softly.

And that was the rupture, right there—not the door, not the hungry things, not the prophecy. That. Eleven years old, standing in the candle-smoke with tears cutting clean lines down her dusty face, asking me the question I'd been dodging since Thornfell: whether I was captain of a smuggling crew or a warden of people, and whether I'd known, deep down, from the very first mile, that those were different jobs and only one of them ended with everyone alive.

Hesk tried to stand. Failed. Tried again, and Wendell helped him up, and he leaned on her shoulder and looked at each of us in turn the way he looks at a map before committing to a route.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I've served under captains who'd trade any of us for the mission. You're not one of those, Bramble. Never been." He tapped the bandage at his side. "And I've seen doors before. Some you go through, some you hold. Wendell's right. It's decided. The only question left is whether we do it together or tear each other apart arguing about it in front of the dead, who, no offense, ma'am"—a nod at the ossuary shelves—"have had a long century and deserve better entertainment."

A sound came from the shelves. I'll swear to this too, though Tally swears she imagined it: dry, papery, brief. Like laughter. Or like a page turning.

Tally wiped her face with her sleeve, hard, the way kids do when they refuse to be seen crying. "Fine," she said. "Fine. We go. But I'm keeping the second half of the book. I'm carrying it. And if we get to that door and the silence starts moving, I'm running, and none of you get to be ashamed of me."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Hesk said.

"Smartest plan anyone's proposed all night," I said, and meant it.

We banked the fire. We repacked the book, and Tally sewed the second half into the lining of her own coat with her picking-knife and a length of waxed thread, quick and neat, a smuggler's stitch, and I watched her do it and thought: there's the heir. There's the next captain, right there, learning the oldest lesson the roads teach—which is that everything you carry, you carry for somebody.

Outside, the mountains had gone strange. The stars sat too close. The wind, when it came up the valley, carried fragments of sound that none of us could name and all of us recognized—a hum, a gathering, a vast patience drawing breath.

And below us, on the switchback road we'd climbed all day, torchlight. Many torches. Moving fast, in formation, flying the Crown's colors, coming up the pass behind us.

Corvane squinted down at them a long moment. Then he said, very quietly, in a voice with nothing left in it but iron:

"Those aren't my men. I sealed the southern passes myself. Whoever that is—they came anyway."

He turned to face us, and for the first time since Thornfell, the Inquisitor of the Crown looked afraid.

"They aren't stopping at the border," he said. "They're burning every shrine as they come."
