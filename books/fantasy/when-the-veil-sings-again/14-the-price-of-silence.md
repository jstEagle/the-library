## 14. The Price of Silence

The trap closed at the ford below Harrowmere, where the river went shallow and wide and every traveler had to slow. Corvane had chosen it himself, standing on the bank in the gray before dawn with his ledger open against his forearm, watching the water carry the last of the night downstream.

He had not chosen the girl.

She came down to the ford alone, which was the first strange thing. A courier, perhaps—a child sent ahead while the others took a longer road. She was small, quick-eyed, with a peddler's pack riding too high on her shoulders and a fence's way of walking, always half-turned, always counting exits. Corvane knew the type. He had been raised among them in the years before his commission, before his family's name had been polished into something respectable enough for the Crown's service. He knew what a person looked like when they were carrying something worth more than their life.

"Name," he said, when she reached the near bank and found six soldiers rising out of the reeds around her.

She looked at him. Looked at the soldiers. Did the arithmetic fast—he could see her doing it, lips slightly parted—and arrived at the only answer there was.

"Tally," she said. "Just Tally."

"Just Tally," he repeated. "And what does Just Tally carry in a pack that heavy, walking alone through country that isn't safe?"

"Buttons."

He almost smiled. It would have been a mistake to let her see it, so he didn't. "Open the pack."

* * *

They did not find the book. He hadn't expected to. What they found was buttons, actual buttons, three pounds of them in mismatched jars, and beneath the false bottom a bundle of letters in a woman's hand—love letters, or something shaped like love letters, addressed to no one Corvane could trace. The girl watched him read them with an expression of profound boredom, and he understood that these too were decoys, layers of nothing wrapped around whatever mattered, and that the real cargo was already over the ridge behind him, moving while he stood here ankle-deep in misdirection.

Which meant the girl herself was the cargo. Or the key to it.

"Bind her hands," he said. "Gently. She's more useful unbruised."

Tally's chin came up at that. There was fear in her—he could smell it, sour and human—but under the fear was something harder, a small stubborn coal that refused to go out even when he leaned close to look at it. He had seen that look before. He had worn it once himself, a lifetime ago, standing in his grandfather's study while the old man explained what the family honor was built on and why it must never be questioned.

"You're making a mistake," the girl said.

"I make very few."

"This one's big." She lifted her bound wrists as if presenting evidence. "You want the scholar and the book. I can't help you with that. I'm just the button girl."

"The button girl," Corvane said, "who was traveling with four people through a pass that doesn't exist on any map, toward a border that hasn't been a border for a hundred years. You've been walking with ghosts, child. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise."

Something flickered across her face then—not guilt exactly. Calculation. He filed it away. This one lied with her whole body, fluently, the way some people sang. But when he mentioned the dead, her eyes had gone briefly, involuntarily, toward the mountains. Toward the truth.

He ordered camp made on the south bank. If the others doubled back for her—and they might; smugglers were sentimental in his experience—they'd have to come to him.

* * *

The tent was army canvas, ugly and efficient. Corvane sat across from the girl with the ledger open between them, because the ledger was the only honest thing he owned and he liked to conduct business in its presence. Outside, the soldiers made supper noise. Inside, there was a lamp, a stool, and the long silence he had learned to use like a blade.

Tally sat on the ground with her back against the tent pole, legs crossed, radiating the specific insolence of someone who has decided to be comfortable in captivity as a form of resistance.

"You're the Inquisitor," she said. "Corvane. My gran used to tell stories about your lot. Said you could smell a lie through stone."

"Your gran exaggerated."

"She also said you burned a village once."

"Your gran," Corvane said, "was a liar, and I never burned a village. Villages burn themselves when people panic. I have only ever asked questions." He turned a page in the ledger, though there was nothing on it yet. "Here is my first question, and I will ask it plainly because you strike me as someone who respects plainness. Where is the road?"

"What road?"

"The road your friends are on. The one Wendell Lockridge is walking them down. The one that leads to Vael."

The name landed the way he intended. He watched it land—the flinch, quickly smothered, the way her breathing changed for one count, two. So. They had told her things. Not everything, but enough that the name meant something, and meaning something was already too much.

"I don't know any road," Tally said.

"No. But you know a direction. And here is what you may not understand about your situation." He closed the ledger and set both hands flat upon it, the gesture he used when it was time to stop being patient. "I do not need you. I want to be clear about that. I have forty men, maps older than your grandmother, and a family archive that contains every road through those mountains, including the ones that were erased from the public copies. I will find them. The only variable is time. And time, tonight, matters more than it has ever mattered before."

"Because of the prophecy." She said it lightly, testing.

"Because of the prophecy," he agreed, and gave her nothing else.

Silence stretched. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a soldier laughed at something and was shushed. Tally picked at a knot in the tent canvas with her bound hands, and Corvane let her pick, because he had learned long ago that people told the truth with their hands long before their mouths agreed to it.

Finally she looked up. "What happens to me if I don't talk?"

"That depends entirely on what you do instead."

"I mean after. Tonight. Tomorrow. When this is all done and you've caught them or you haven't. What happens to a girl who knows things and didn't say them?"

Ah. There it was. Not loyalty—loyalty was a luxury word, and this girl had never been able to afford luxuries. Fear, yes, but underneath the fear a colder, better question: what was survival worth, and who set the price?

"I am not my reputation," Corvane said. "I don't torture children. I don't punish silence for its own sake. What I do—" and here he paused, because the next part required precision, and precision was the only form of honesty available to a man in his position, "—is weigh. Everything gets weighed. What you know against what you cost me. What you give against what you take. Right now you are costing me time, and time is the most expensive thing in this country right now. Do you understand?"

"Say it plain."

"Tell me the road, and walk free tonight. Tell me the road, and I will forget your name exists. That is not a trick. I keep my bargains. Ask anyone I've ever bought."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I weigh you again tomorrow, and the price goes up, and eventually I stop weighing and start deciding. I would prefer not to reach that point with you." He meant it. He found, examining himself, that he genuinely meant it. There was something in the girl he recognized—the same desperate cleverness he had once aimed at his grandfather's ledger, the same refusal to be a line item in someone else's accounting. He did not enjoy the thought of crushing that. He simply would, if it came to it. Both things were true. Most true things came in pairs.

Tally was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, sharp, disbelieving sound.

"You're offering me freedom."

"I'm offering you a fair trade."

"For a road I don't know, to a place that doesn't exist, full of people who trusted me." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and she covered it with a shrug that fooled neither of them. "You really think I'm that cheap?"

"I think," Corvane said quietly, "that everyone is that cheap, when the price is named correctly. I have bought bishops. I have bought generals. I once bought a man who swore on his mother's grave, and he wept while he signed, and he slept well that night. It is not a moral failing, child. It is arithmetic. Everyone has a number."

"Not everyone."

"Everyone." He opened the ledger again, turned to a fresh page, and wrote the date at the top in his small, exact hand. "But I'll tell you what. I'll let you name yours."

* * *

She took until midnight.

He knew because he worked through the evening—dispatches to the southern garrisons, orders sealing the passes he could still seal, a letter to the Ministry that said nothing and implied everything—and all the while the girl sat in the corner of the tent under guard, and he felt her thinking. It was like hearing a lock being worked from the inside. Click, click, click. Testing tumblers. He did not interrupt. Interrupting a mind like hers mid-work was how amateurs lost prisoners.

At midnight she said, "Water," and he brought it himself, and crouched to put it in her bound hands, and stayed crouched, close, because closeness was a tool too.

"If I tell you," she said, "you let me walk. Tonight. No escort, no tail. I walk out of this camp and I'm gone and nobody follows."

"You have my word."

"Your word." She snorted. "Fine. Your word." She drank. Set the cup down. And then she looked at him with those quick merchant's eyes, and Corvane saw, with a cold sliding feeling in his stomach, that she had already decided—that she had decided hours ago, maybe before he ever offered, maybe back at the ford—and that everything since had been theater performed for an audience of one.

"There's a road," she said. "Through the Witherstones. Old shepherd track. It comes out above the chapel—you know the one, the ossuary, the one with all the unnamed bones. From there it drops into the valley past the tollhouse at Miller's Bend, and follows the river north to the old border stones. That's the way they went. That's the way they're going."

Every word of it was true.

Corvane knew it was true the way he knew forged signatures—by the absence of strain. She wasn't performing. She believed what she was saying, or rather she knew it, the way a fence knows the weight of honest coin. His heart lifted, and he hated it for lifting, because in the same instant another part of him, the part his grandfather had trained, the part that kept the ledger, whispered: *and why would she give it to you?*

"Why?" he asked.

"Because you were right." She met his eyes without flinching, and there was the coal again, burning steady under all that quickness. "Everyone has a number. Mine's Hesk. He got hurt at the tollhouse getting us out—your men, your doing—and Bramble says he'll live but Bramble lies when he's scared, and I can't—I can't sit here not knowing. So. You want the road. Take the road. Get there fast, get there ahead of them, do whatever it is you do. Just—" her voice did the thing again, the crack, and this time she let it show, weaponized it, and he admired her for it even as it worked on him, "—when you catch them, nobody dies. Nobody dies, Inquisitor. That's my price. That's my number. Write it in your book."

He wrote it. He actually wrote it, in his small exact hand: *No deaths. The girl's condition.* Because he kept his bargains, and because a bargain witnessed by ink was harder to betray than one witnessed only by conscience, and his conscience had been eating itself for weeks now and could not be trusted with anything.

Then he rose, called the duty officer, and gave the order.

"Ride for the Witherstones. Shepherd track, down past the chapel, tollhouse at Miller's Bend, river road north. Twenty men, fast march. I follow at dawn with the rest."

The officer saluted and was gone, and the camp began to move around them, torches blooming, horses complaining, the whole machine of the Crown grinding into motion along a road a girl had sold for the life of a deserter.

Corvane stood at the tent flap and watched them go, and the whisper in the back of his skull grew louder.

*True,* it said. *Every word true. And useless.*

Because he had walked the Witherstones as a younger man. He had surveyed that shepherd track himself, five years ago, when the Ministry still paid for surveys. The track ended at the chapel—ended, washed out, a hundred feet of collapsed hillside with no way down. No one had passed that way in a generation. The road she had given him was real, and accurate, and led nowhere, and twenty of his best men were at this moment riding hard toward a dead end while the actual company moved along some other route he had just failed to imagine.

She had known. She must have known. She had given him truth the way a card sharp gives a marked deck—every piece genuine, the whole thing worthless—and she had done it with tears in her voice and a dying friend's name on her lips, and he had written *no deaths* in his own ledger like a schoolboy signing his own ransom note.

He turned. Tally was watching him from the corner, and for one naked moment their eyes met, and he saw it there: not triumph. Something quieter and worse. Respect, maybe. Pity, possibly. The look of one professional acknowledging another across a table where the game had ended before he sat down.

"You knew," he said.

"I know roads," Tally said. "It's the family trade. You should've asked me about the other one." She smiled then, small and tired and utterly without malice, and it was the smile that did it, that made the anger curdle into something closer to awe. "You would've liked it. It's not on any map either."

Corvane held out his hand. A soldier put the keys in it. He crossed the tent, unlocked her wrists himself, and stepped back.

"Walk," he said. "South. Don't make me regret the arithmetic."

She rubbed her wrists, shouldered her ridiculous pack of buttons, and paused at the tent flap.

"They're close now," she said, not looking at him. "Closer than you think. Whatever you're planning to do when you find them—whatever your book says you have to do—you should know that Wendell hears the dead. Really hears them. And the dead aren't angry, Inquisitor. They're just waiting. There's a difference, and it matters, and I hope—" she stopped, started again, "—I hope when you see it, you're man enough to know what you're looking at."

Then she was gone, a small shape swallowed by the dark, and Corvane stood alone in his tent with the lamp and the ledger and the sound of twenty riders fading north toward nothing.

He opened the book to the page where he had written her condition—*No deaths*—and looked at it for a long time.

Then, far off in the mountains, faint as memory, he heard singing.

It was not possible. The distance was wrong, the wind was wrong, the hour was wrong. It was not possible, and he heard it anyway: many voices, old and patient, rising out of the passes like heat off stone, singing in a language that had been officially dead for a hundred years.

Corvane closed the ledger. He blew out the lamp. And he stood in the dark, listening, while somewhere ahead of him the veil began to thin.
