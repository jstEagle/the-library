## 15. Night of the Thin Veil

They heard the singing before they saw the valley.

It came up through the soles of Wendell's boots first, through stone, the way a bell's hum lives in its frame after the striking is done. Then it reached her ears, and then—she knew because she watched their faces—it reached everyone else's.

Tally stopped dead on the switchback trail. Hesk, propped between Bramble's shoulder and a walking staff he refused to surrender, went very still in a way that had nothing to do with his wound. Ilsa pressed both hands flat against the rock face as if checking whether the mountain itself was trembling.

"You hear it too," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. She'd spent thirty years being the only one. The strangeness of saying *you* nearly buckled her.

"I hear it," Bramble said quietly. "Gods help me, I hear it."

The song had no words they could parse, but it didn't need words. It was the sound of many voices keeping time with something slower than breath—the sound, Wendell thought, of patience that had outlasted everything there was to be patient for. A hundred years of waiting, set to melody.

"Is that them?" Tally asked. Her voice was thin. "The Vaeli dead? They're—they're *singing*?"

"They've always been singing," Wendell said. "Tonight the mountain's letting us listen."

She started down the trail again, and after a moment they followed, because there was nowhere else to go. The door-site lay at the bottom: a bowl of land folded into the high pass, where three ridgelines met like hands cupped around a flame. Old maps called it nothing at all. The dead called it home.

Wendell had been listening to directions from dead travelers for six days—turn where the drowned woman pointed, rest where the murdered carter remembered resting—and now those voices were falling away one by one, dissolving into the greater song, streams running into a river. She felt the silence of each departure like a hand lifted from her shoulder.

At the lip of the bowl, Ilsa caught her arm.

"Wendell." The scholar's face was pale in the last violet light. "The book said one night. One door, opened once. If Corvane comes—"

"He's coming," Hesk said. He'd gone gray around the mouth. Not from pain; Wendell had learned to read the difference. "You don't close passes, burn tollhouses, and march a column up a ghost-road unless you mean to be somewhere by nightfall."

"Then we have hours," Bramble said. "Maybe less. We do what we came to do and we're ready when he arrives."

"And what we came to do is—" Tally began.

"Open a door that eats people," Hesk finished. "Cheerful work. Let's get on with it."

They descended into the valley.

* * *

Up close, the place was wrong in a way Wendell had no craft-vocabulary for. She was a bone-carver; she understood materials, how stone remembered pressure and wood remembered the knife. This stone remembered *architecture*. Beneath the grass and a century of scree, the ground was scored with straight lines, foundations of walls that had never fallen so much as been *unmade*—dissolved back into the earth by decree, every map burned, every record, every name.

Except the names hadn't burned. They were here, all of them, humming under her feet.

"The chapel bones are here," Ilsa said softly. She'd unwrapped the book again, though the light was failing fast. "And the court. And—Wendell, there are thousands. The whole kingdom walked here when they died."

"Not walked," Wendell said. "Came home. There's a difference."

Bramble was pacing the perimeter with a smuggler's eye, reading the terrain the way Ilsa read text. "One way in worth defending. High ground on the east ridge—if soldiers come, they'll come down that spine. We can't fight a column. So we don't. We hold the center, we make them look at what's actually standing here, and we buy Ilsa time to read."

"If Corvane lets anyone stand still long enough to listen," Hesk said.

"He listened once," Wendell said.

They all looked at her.

"In Thornfell. Before he left. I sang him a fragment of the massacre record—Ilsa's fragment, the one from the hidden half of the prophecy. For about four seconds he wasn't an Inquisitor. Then he put himself away again. But the four seconds happened. I heard them happen."

"That was days ago," Tally said. "He's had days to bury it deeper."

"People don't unhear things," Wendell said. "They just decide what the hearing costs them. He's spent his whole life paying interest on a lie. Maybe tonight the debt comes due."

Hesk snorted. "Or maybe tonight we die on a hill for a dead country."

"Either way," Bramble said, "we're not leaving."

No one argued. That was the thing about found family, Wendell thought—arguing was optional; staying wasn't.

The sun went down behind the western ridge and the veil began to thin in earnest.

* * *

Wendell felt it happen the way you feel a fever break.

The song swelled—not louder, exactly, but *closer*, as if the singers had stepped forward out of some deep room of the world. And then, in the blue dark between sunset and full night, they arrived.

They came up out of the ground like heat-shimmer given shape. First just outlines, suggestions—a turn of shoulder, the geometry of a crowd. Then color bled into them: dyed wool, silver thread, the deep blues and iron-reds of a fashion dead a hundred years. They stood among the foundation-lines of their own houses, in the streets of their own erased city, and they looked at the five living people in their midst without hostility, without hunger, without anything but a vast, grave attention.

"There," Tally whispered. Her voice cracked clean in half. "There's a *little girl*. Wendell, there's a little girl looking right at me."

"She can see you too," Wendell said. Her own eyes were burning. Thirty years of hearing voices in the dark, alone, and now the dark stood up and became people. "That's Maren. She died at nine. She's been asking about her dog for a hundred years."

"Does she—does she want me to—"

"Just look at her. That's enough. Being seen is the whole of it."

The dead kept arriving. Hundreds. More than hundreds. They filled the valley floor along their ghost-streets, and above them the stars came out hard and cold, and the song resolved at last into something almost like words, almost like a hymn, almost like a list.

"It's the names," Ilsa breathed. She had the book open across her forearms, tears freezing bright tracks down her face, and she wasn't even trying to hide them. "They're singing the names. All of them. The roll of the massacred court and everyone who wouldn't leave and everyone who was made to forget—they've been singing themselves back into existence this whole time."

A woman near the center of the gathering turned her head. Even across the distance, even through the veil, Wendell felt the weight of that regard like a hand on her chin, tilting her face up. The dead woman wore a carver's apron. Her hands were strong and scarred the way Wendell's own were scarred.

*Court-trained,* the old woman in the ceded lands had said. *Where did you learn your knife, girl?*

Now Wendell knew. It had come down through apprenticeships, through generations, through hands teaching hands, all the way back to this woman and whatever master taught her. The gift in her fingers was Vaeli. She was Vaeli. She always had been; the knowledge had simply survived the burning by hiding inside bone and memory, the way everything true had.

The dead carver smiled at her. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone handing over a bill.

*I know,* Wendell thought at her. *I know what it costs. I came anyway.*

The smile gentled.

And then Hesk said, very quietly, "Torches on the east ridge."

* * *

They came down the spine of the ridge in a disciplined double file, torchlight bobbing like a string of low orange stars descending to rival the real ones above. Wendell counted by habit: forty, fifty, more behind them. Corvane rode at the front, dismounting where the slope gentled, and came on foot the rest of the way with his ledger under one arm like a weapon or a shield.

The company drew together in the center of the valley, among the ghost-streets, with the dead of Vael standing silent on every side. The song had stopped. Ten thousand patient faces turned toward the soldiers, waiting to see what the living would do.

"Stay behind me," Bramble murmured, stepping half-forward. "All of you. I've talked my way past worse than one stiff-necked Inquisitor."

"You haven't," Hesk said.

"No. But it sounds brave, doesn't it?"

Corvane stopped twenty paces out. His torchbearers fanned wide at some silent command, and Wendell saw the moment the light reached past the living and touched the dead—saw a soldier flinch, saw another make a sign against evil, saw a third simply stop walking and stare with his mouth open at a Vaeli housewife standing beside the road he was marching down.

"Your men can see them," Wendell called out. Her voice carried strangely well in the bowl of the valley, as if the acoustics had been built for proclamation. Perhaps they had. "Look at their faces, Corvane. Your soldiers are looking at the founding lie of your Crown, and the lie is wearing an apron."

Corvane's gaze swept the valley—dead and living alike—and came back to her. Up close, by torchlight, he looked like a man who had not slept since he heard singing in a locked room.

"Step away from them," he said. His voice was level. Almost gentle. "All of you. What happens next is bigger than you, and I would prefer you survive it. That is not sentiment. It is arithmetic—you are witnesses, and witnesses have value."

"We're not moving," Bramble said.

"The door opens tonight," Ilsa said, lifting the book. "It was prophesied. It is owed. A century of stolen names—"

"A century of *peace*," Corvane said, and for the first time something raw showed through the polish. "You think the massacre was the crime? The crime was necessary, Verrow. The erasure—that was mercy. Two kingdoms cannot share one border, and one kingdom cannot share one history with a wound in it. We cut the wound out. We burned the maps so no one would bleed on them. And it *held*. A hundred years without a war of succession, without a claimant rising from the grave, without—" He stopped himself. Breathed. "I am the last man who will ever hold that seal shut. Give me the book."

"No," said Wendell.

Everyone looked at her—even, she noticed distantly, some of the dead.

"You heard the singing," she said. "In your office, before you marched. You heard ten thousand people singing their own names, and you blew out your lamp and listened until it hurt, because some part of you knows arithmetic isn't all there is." She took one step forward, and the dead carver in her apron seemed to lean with her. "You can order your men to cut us down. Some of them might even obey. But then you'll have to explain to the survivors why the ghosts of a murdered kingdom were singing when you killed the only people willing to carry their names."

"Stand down," Corvane said—to his men, Wendell realized a beat too late. Not to her. "Blades sheathed. No one moves without my word."

The soldier nearest him—a young officer with a boy's face—looked from his commander to the ghost of a child standing ten feet from him, and did not appear to trust either his orders or his eyes.

"You're outnumbered and outmatched," Corvane said to the company, quieter now. "But I find myself unwilling to test my men against the dead. So we arrive at the same ledger page we always reach, Lockridge. You believe the door must open. I believe it must never open. The night will decide which of us the mountains agree with."

"The night already decided," Wendell said. "Can't you feel it? The veil's thinner than it's been in a hundred years. It's going to keep thinning until midnight, and then the door opens *whether you seal it or not*. The only question—the only choice anyone gets tonight—is whether the dead walk through to their own country, or whether something else walks *out*."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes. The scholar's discipline warring with the Inquisitor's certainty.

"And if you fail," he said. "If your voices falter and the wrong door swings. Will you swear to let me seal it then? With whatever force remains?"

Behind Wendell, the dead carver raised her head, and the whole valley of the dead drew one long breath that no living lungs had asked for.

"Yes," Wendell said. "If I fail, seal it. I'll hold the line myself."

"Mother save us from honest women," Corvane muttered. And then, louder, in the flat voice of a man setting down something heavy: "Then we watch. And we pray to whichever god is listening that you are wrong about the hour."

Above the eastern ridge, past the rim of the world, the moon was climbing toward fullness—and under their feet, faint but unmistakable, the mountain began to hum like a struck bell, and a seam of pale gold light opened in the bedrock at the center of the valley, widening by the width of a knife-blade, then two.

The door was waking.

And Wendell, staring down at it, felt the first cold tendril of the hidden half of the prophecy curl around her heart: *the door devours whoever opens it.*

She had promised the truth to these people. She was going to give it to them. Just—not quite yet.
