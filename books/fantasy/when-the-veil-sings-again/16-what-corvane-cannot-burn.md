## 16. What Corvane Cannot Burn

The book weighed nothing in Ilsa's hands.

That was the thing she could never explain to anyone who hadn't carried it. A century of hiding, three generations pressed flat under floorboards and sewn into coat linings and buried in a clay jar beneath a kitchen that had since burned down—and it weighed less than a loaf of bread. Her grandmother used to say the weight was in the carrying, not the carrying-case, and Ilsa had nodded along like a good descendant, and only now, standing at the mouth of a door that should not exist while soldiers leveled crossbows at her friends, did she finally understand what her grandmother had meant.

Her grandmother had meant this exact moment.

"Verrow." Corvane's voice cut across the hollow like a knife laid flat on a table. He stood twenty paces off with his lantern raised and his face lit from below, so his shadows moved wrong—up when they should fall down, like a man seen through water. "Put the book down and step away from it."

Behind him, thirty soldiers. Behind them, the dark of the pass, and beyond that, a country built on a lie so old most of its citizens had never thought to check the foundations. In front of Ilsa: Wendell Lockridge, bone-dust on her fingers, standing between the door and everything else like a woman who had spent her whole life invisible and had just decided to stop.

"No," Ilsa said.

Corvane's jaw tightened. "You are a scholar. You understand evidence. Whatever you believe you've found in those pages—"

"I haven't found anything." She heard her own voice come out steady and was grateful to whatever ancestor was lending her their spine. "I found what you already know. The difference is that I read it."

She turned—not away from him, which would have been surrender, but sideways, so she faced the soldiers. Thirty faces. Young, mostly. Cold-chapped and tired from the forced march over the southern passes, which Corvane had closed behind them, which meant they'd climbed for two days knowing there was no retreat. They were here because a man with an inherited title told them a door was a threat.

They were here because no one had ever read to them.

"My name is Ilsa Verrow," she said, pitching her voice the way she did in lecture halls, the way her mother had taught her—*from the chest, girl, truth travels better than lies, it has farther to walk.* "Three days ago none of you knew my name either. That's how this works. That's how all of it works. Things get erased, and then nobody checks."

"Arrest her," Corvane said quietly.

Nobody moved.

Not out of defiance—Ilsa watched them closely enough to see it wasn't that. It was something smaller and stranger. A hesitation like the one before a sneeze, collective, involuntary. Because the dead were still gathered at the door, and even men trained not to see such things were seeing them: hundreds of figures, translucent as breath on glass, arranged not as a mob but as a *court*, ranks and rows, patient as stone saints.

And one of them—a woman in robes gone gray with age—was looking directly at Corporal Dain Marrec, third rank, left file, and smiling at him the way you smile at a nephew you haven't seen since he was small.

Ilsa opened the book.

"You can arrest me after," she said. "Listen first. It's short. It's the shortest thing anyone's ever asked of you, I think—the whole truth takes eleven minutes, and your orders took your whole life."

* * *

The record began, as records do, with weather.

*"On the last night of Vael, snow fell early, though it was barely autumn. The court dined late. Queen Serevanne had dismissed her guards—this is important; write it plainly; she had DISMISSED them—because the men who came wore her own colors."*

Ilsa's voice filled the hollow. She kept it level, almost bored, because boredom was armor and because she wanted every word to land clean, without performance, the way a hammer lands without flourish.

*"They came up the mountain road at moonrise wearing Vaeli livery, and the gatekeepers let them in because the livery was real—stolen, we learned too late, from the garrison at Thornfell, from men who were paid in Crown silver to give it up. The Crown did not send an army against Vael. The Crown sent forty men dressed as brothers, and Vael opened its own gates."*

A soldier in the front rank shifted his weight. His crossbow dipped two inches.

*"The queen died in her own hall. So did her consort, her sister, her council of nine, and the keeper of the doors—which matters tonight, though I did not know why when I wrote it. The children were marched out alive and scattered south, given new names by priests who were also paid. This is why there is no blood in the histories, gentlemen. Blood is hard to hide. Names are easy. You rename a child and a kingdom disappears in a single generation."*

Ilsa looked up once. Corvane hadn't moved, but his hand had gone to the seal-chain at his throat—the old iron sigil his family had carried for five generations, the one they told visitors was a ward against plague. She'd seen its twin in an archive sketch, labeled *instrument of closing, house of Corvane, commissioned year one of the erasure.*

*"Who signed the order? I will write the name because names are the only coin I have left: Aldous Corvane, Warden of the Marches. Not the king—the king never knew, or chose never to know, which is the same crime wearing better boots. Aldous Corvane wrote in his own ledger: 'Let it be famine. Let the maps go blank. Let no stone say the name.' And his heirs have held the seal ever since, and called it duty."*

"That is enough."

Corvane's voice cracked across the hollow—but Ilsa heard the flaw in it, the hairline fracture running through the cold, and she pressed on before he could pour lead into the crack.

*"If you are reading this, you are standing where I stood, holding what I held, and you are afraid. Good. Fear means you understand. My descendants will guard this book until the night the veil thins, and on that night they must read it aloud, in the open, where the ones who serve cannot pretend they were never told. Ignorance was their shield. Take it from them. It is the kindest cruel thing you will ever do."*

Silence.

Then, from the third rank, Corporal Dain Marrec said, in a voice like a man waking: "My grandmother's name was Serel."

Ilsa's heart stopped and restarted. "What?"

"She said—" The corporal swallowed. His crossbow hung forgotten at his side. "She said our family came from somewhere north. She sang us a song about a white stag. We thought it was just—she was old, she made things up—" He looked at the translucent woman in the gray robes, the one who had smiled at him, and Ilsa saw the resemblance land on him like a blow. Same brow. Same way of tilting the head.

"The renaming lists are in the back of the book," Ilsa said gently. "Every child, old name and new. Yours might be there."

"It's there." Another soldier. Then another. "Mine's there. Gods. Mine's there—"

"Enough!" 

But Corvane had waited too long, and everyone heard it. The word came out of him stripped of authority, just sound, and the hollow ate it.

* * *

Wendell found her afterward—or during, or between; time had gone strange, stretched thin as the veil itself. The carver appeared at her shoulder and murmured, "What are you doing?"

"Reading."

"They're going to kill you for it."

"Probably." Ilsa turned a page. Her hands were shaking now, now that the reading was done, the tremor arriving on schedule like a bill collector. "But look at them, Wendell. Look at the ranks. Half of them aren't pointing their weapons anywhere. Corvane can't order a massacre of his own men, and he knows it. Truth isn't a weapon. Everyone always says that like it's a comfort. It's not a comfort. It's worse. It's a wound that doesn't close on either side."

Wendell looked. The dead of Vael had begun moving among the soldiers—not touching, never touching, just walking the lines slowly, reading faces the way Ilsa read pages. Finding grandmothers' songs and stolen liveries and the shape of their own brows on strangers' skulls. One old man stood before Hesk's bier—Hesk still breathing, shallow and stubborn, Bramble kneeling over him with bandages—and set a ghostly hand above the deserter's wound, and whether it did anything or not, Hesk's breathing eased.

"He's going to try anyway," Wendell said. "Look at him."

Corvane had drawn himself up to full height. He had the seal-chain in his fist now, iron sigil raised, and Ilsa saw the arithmetic happening behind his eyes—the same arithmetic his ancestor had done, four generations of it: *the lie costs less than the truth, the lie has always cost less—*

"Soldiers of the Crown," Corvane called. "That door opens onto nothing. Onto rot. Onto things that wear dead faces to get past your guard. Your duty is to hold this line. Form ranks. Prepare to close the breach."

No one formed ranks.

One soldier lowered his weapon entirely and sat down on a rock, deliberate as a man laying down a burden, and then a second, and then the front rank simply—dissolved, not into mutiny but into witness, thirty men turning by degrees to face the door, faces upturned, listening to a sound Ilsa realized she could hear too: singing, far off and near at once, the mountains themselves picking up a melody that had been forbidden for a hundred years and remembering every note.

Corvane looked at his empty ranks. Ilsa watched him arrive at the last resort of men whose arguments have all failed: force, alone, personally borne.

He started toward the door.

"No—" Ilsa lunged, but Wendell was faster, stepping into his path, small and unafraid, and the last thing Ilsa saw before the scene broke apart into motion—soldiers shouting, Bramble rising off her knees, the dead of Vael turning as one toward the threshold—was Wendell Lockridge planting herself between the Inquisitor and the open dark, and saying clearly, in a voice that carried to every living soul in the hollow:

"You'll have to carve through me first. And I'm the only one here who knows how."

Then Corvane struck the seal-chain against the stone of the door, and the light went wrong.
