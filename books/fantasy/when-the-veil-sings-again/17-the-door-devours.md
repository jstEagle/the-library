## 17. The Door Devours

The door was not a door.

That was the first thing I understood when I finally stood before it, close enough to touch. I had spent my whole life imagining it wrong—some great slab of stone in the mountainside, carved with the sigils of a dead kingdom, waiting for a key or a word. But the door of Vael was an absence. A seam in the air itself, thin as a knife's edge, running vertical through the hollow where the chapel of Unnamed Bones had once stood before the Crown pulled it down and salted the ground.

It didn't look like anything. That was the horror of it. You could look straight at the place where the world ended and see only more mountain, more night sky, more frost on black stone. Your eyes slid off it like rain off waxed canvas. Only my ears told me the truth: that behind the silence of it, something vast was holding its breath.

"They're all here," I said. My voice came out cracked. "Every one of them."

Ilsa stood beside me with the two halves of the prophecy book open across her forearms, the old pages trembling in the wind that came off the pass. Bramble had her hand on her knife without seeming to know it. Hesk lay propped against a boulder twenty paces back, gray-faced, breathing shallow, alive because Tally had refused—loudly, profanely, magnificently—to let him be anything else. Tally herself crouched at his side with her fingers pressed to his wrist as if she could hold his pulse in place by will alone.

And beyond us, filling the hollow like fog fills a valley, the dead of Vael.

I had seen ghosts my whole life. Heard them since I was six years old, when a drowned ferryman's voice followed me home from the river and wouldn't stop talking until I carved his name into a piece of driftwood so someone, somewhere, would know he'd existed. I thought I knew what the dead looked like. Faded things. Edges worn soft by time, like coins rubbed smooth in a pocket.

These were not faded.

They were *bright*. A thousand figures standing shoulder to shoulder in the moonlight, dressed in the fashion of a century ago, their faces clear and patient and unbearably sad. Courtiers and farmers and soldiers and children. An old woman held a spindle that hadn't turned in a hundred years. A boy of maybe ten held the hand of a man who might have been his father, and both of them were looking at the empty air of the doorway the way you look at a house you were driven from.

"Say the words," Ilsa said quietly. "As written."

The hidden half. The half her family had guarded for four generations, wrapped in oilcloth inside a false cover of a tax ledger, because who searches a tax ledger? She had read it aloud twice on the road, once for me and once for all of us, and each time the words had felt less like prophecy and more like instructions. *When the veil sings again, the carver who hears shall stand at the seam and speak the kingdom back into its bones.*

"Ilsa," I said. "Read me the warning one more time. The part your family thought was metaphor."

Her jaw tightened. She turned the fragile page with a scholar's delicacy and a soldier's reluctance. "*The door is hungry,*" she recited. "*It does not open. It takes. What passes must be given, and what gives must be willing, and the willing are not returned.*"

"And they decided that meant—what? That opening it would cost something abstract?"

"My grandmother decided it meant the door demanded a life," she said. "She decided it was a warning against trying at all. Four generations of Verrows have kept this book locked in a box and called it wisdom." Her voice caught. "I think she was a coward, Wendell. I think we all were."

I looked at the dead.

They weren't pushing toward the seam. They weren't clawing at it, wailing, the way ghosts were supposed to want. They stood at a respectful distance, the way you stand outside a lit window on a cold night, watching a warmth that isn't yours. The old woman with the spindle met my eyes—or seemed to—and inclined her head slightly. Not begging. Asking.

*We have waited,* a hundred voices said, not aloud, not in my ears but somewhere behind my sternum where I had always heard them. *We can wait longer. We will not ask you to die for us.*

"Well," I said. My throat was tight. "That's the trouble with polite ghosts. Nobody warns you they'll be considerate."

Bramble snorted. It was the closest thing to a laugh any of us had managed since Corvane's torches appeared on the far ridge an hour ago—a line of orange lights descending through the dark, slow and inevitable, giving us whatever time remained. Hesk couldn't be moved. So we stayed. So here we were, all of us, arranging our deaths around the convenience of a wounded deserter, and none of us pretending otherwise.

"Do it," Bramble said. Not gentle. She never was when gentleness would have been a lie. "Whatever it costs, do it before those torches get here. If the choice is you dying for a country that never existed or all of us dying for one that forgot how, I know which grave I'd rather dig."

"That's very romantic," Tally said. Her voice shook. "Shut up, Bramble."

I stepped toward the seam.

* * *

Up close, the absence hummed. Not sound exactly—the absence of sound, the shape of silence, like pressing your ear to a door and hearing nothing on the other side and knowing that nothing is listening back.

I did what the book said. I put my palm flat against the cold mountain stone beside the invisible edge, and I spoke.

Not the prophecy. Not yet. First I did the thing I had done ten thousand times over a workbench in Thornfell, the thing that made me what I was. I listened.

The dead came in like water finding a crack. Names first—they always came as names. *Maren Vael. Maren Vael. Maren.* A woman who had carried the crown's keys. *Ostrik. Ostrik Hale.* A groom. *Sefa, Sefa, little Sefa*—the boy with his father's hand. A hundred names layered over a hundred names, and beneath them, older, the deep sediment of them: the names of everyone who had ever lived in Vael and died in it, going back further than the massacre, further than the kingdom, back to people who had built shrines on this mountain before there was a word for shrine.

"By the authority given me—" I began, and spoke the words from the hidden half, the formal words, the opening rite four generations of frightened women had refused to say.

The mountain answered.

It was not gentle.

It was as if I had put my mouth to a wound. The silence tore open and everything behind it poured *into* me—not sound, not even voices anymore, but weight. Grief with mass. A century of unburied mourning compressed into a single point and driven through the narrow channel of one small bone-carver from Thornfell. I felt my ribs flex under it. I tasted blood. Somewhere very far away Ilsa was shouting my name, and Tally was screaming something else, and none of it reached me because I was full—I was *full*—there was no room left in Wendell Lockridge for Wendell Lockridge—

The last thing I registered clearly was the dead.

They had surged forward. All thousand of them, hands outstretched, faces twisted—not reaching for the door. Reaching for *me*. Trying to pull me back from it. And failing, because you cannot grab a drowning woman when you are made of mist, and the door drank, and the door drank, and the door

—let go.

I woke on frozen grass with three people kneeling on me and one of them slapping my face, which I later learned was Tally, and which I have forgiven.

"She's back," Tally was saying. "Wendell. Wendell, blink. Blink, you absolute—"

I blinked. The night swam back into focus: stars, ridge line, the distant crawling torches, now much closer. My chest hurt as though a horse had stood on it. When I tried to speak, blood came out. Not a lot. Enough.

"The hidden half wasn't a metaphor," I rasped, when I could. "In case anyone was wondering."

"No one was wondering," Bramble said grimly. She had my shoulders pinned to the ground and did not seem inclined to release them. "What happened?"

"It took." I worked my jaw. Everything worked. That surprised me. "It started taking. Me. All of me. Like drinking through a straw." I pushed myself up on my elbows; nobody stopped me, which meant I looked bad enough that even Hesk's survival instincts had gone quiet. "But then it stopped. Why did it stop?"

Nobody answered. I turned my head and found Ilsa sitting back on her heels, the prophecy book closed against her chest, staring at me with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

"It stopped," she said slowly, "because it wasn't finished reading you."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"When you spoke the rite—you used the words, but you weren't just using the words. You were *listening*. Weren't you. The whole time. Even while it was pulling you apart, some part of you was still doing what you do." She opened the book again, hunting through the dense script. "Here. This passage. My grandmother annotated it 'obscure, possibly corrupted.' *The seam opens to no single tongue. One voice is a meal. Two voices are a key.*"

I sat up fully. The pain in my chest settled into something livable, something I could carry, the way I had learned to carry every other heavy thing in my life.

"One voice is a meal," I repeated. "Two voices are a key."

"The door devours the living who come to it alone," Ilsa said. "Because the living come alone. Because we always come alone—we bring ourselves, our one small self, and the door takes it. But if—" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it a half-second after I did, and I watched the color leave her face.

"The dead don't come alone either," I said. "There's a thousand of them. They've had a hundred years to learn to speak together. To sing." I looked past her, at the bright crowd at the edge of the hollow. The old woman with the spindle had stepped closer. The boy Sefa was holding his father's hand again, and both of them were looking at me now, not at the door.

"That's what the prophecy meant. *The carver who hears*—not the carver who dies. The carver who hears. Someone who can stand between the living voice and the dead one and carry both. The door demands a willing voice of both kingdoms." My hands had started shaking. I let them. "One living person saying yes. One nation of dead people saying yes. Spoken *together*, through someone who can hold both."

"Through you," Tally said flatly. "Again. Except properly this time, instead of almost dying."

"Except properly this time." I laughed, and it hurt, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was crying and there wasn't time. "Do you understand what it's asking? It's not asking me to sacrifice myself. It's asking me to *stop being separate*. For as long as the door stands open, I'm not one voice in Thornfell who hears things. I'm the hinge. Every name they speak goes through me. Every breath I give, they carry. There's no version of this where I stay safely Wendell and the door politely opens."

Hesk, from his boulder, in a voice like gravel settling: "Sounds lonely."

"It sounds," I said, "like the last hundred years of my life, except finally honest about it."

* * *

We had perhaps half an hour. Maybe less. Corvane's torches had come down off the ridge and were moving along the valley floor in disciplined pairs, closing like a hand folding shut. Somewhere among them was the Inquisitor himself, and whatever he intended—sealing the seam, burning us, burning the truth—it was coming up the same path we had.

So we prepared, quickly, badly, the way everything real is prepared.

Bramble rigged Hesk onto a travois of cut pine branches, over his furious objections, because if this went wrong he was not dying alone in a hollow while we ran. Tally counted our remaining knives, gave me two, and taught me in ninety seconds where to put them. Ilsa marked the exact lines of the rite with charcoal on her forearm so she could read them by starlight, and then, in the last minutes, she did something I didn't expect.

She apologized.

"To whom?" I asked.

"To my great-grandmother. And hers. And hers." She smoothed the page with her thumb. "They read *the willing are not returned* and they hid the book. They thought the door wanted death, so they chose never to knock. But it doesn't want death, Wendell. It wants *company*. It wants the one thing the living almost never offer the dead." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious. "It wants to not be alone while it happens. That's all it's ever wanted. That's why it nearly ate you—you knocked and offered yourself like a payment, and the door has spent a century being paid in corpses and didn't know what to do with a gift."

The dead had drawn near. Not crowding—never crowding—but near enough that I could feel them the way you feel a crowd's warmth through a wall. The old woman set her spindle down in the frost, deliberately, the way you put down a tool when the day's true work begins.

*I will speak first,* she said, into the place behind my sternum. *My name was Maren. I kept the keys. I will lend you my voice, carver, and you may keep it after, if keeping helps.*

"You'll drown in it," I whispered. "All of you, pouring through me—"

*Child,* said a hundred voices, gently, *we have been drowning for a hundred years. Tonight we learn to breathe.*

Torches rounded the valley's shoulder. I heard boots now, many boots, and the creak of armor, and above it all a voice I knew—Corvane's voice, cold and carrying, calling out something formal about surrender and treason that I did not wait to hear the end of.

I walked to the seam. Ilsa fell in at my left with the book open. Behind me, ragged and terrified and absolutely refusing to leave, my strange, stubborn, borrowed family arranged themselves around a wounded man on a pine-bough bier, and I loved them so much it felt like another kind of wound.

"Maren," I said aloud. My voice sounded small in the enormous night. "Give me your voice. I'll give you mine. Both kingdoms, one door. Together."

The old woman smiled like sunrise coming up over a country that no longer appeared on maps.

Behind us, steel rang on stone, and Corvane's soldiers broke into a run.
