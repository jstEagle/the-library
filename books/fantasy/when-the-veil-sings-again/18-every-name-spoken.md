## 18. Every Name, Spoken

*Tally*

The ropes came off her wrists mid-battle, which was the most Tally thing that had ever happened to her: freed by chaos, in the middle of a mountain meadow full of glowing dead people and soldiers who couldn't decide what they were afraid of.

Corvane's sergeant had cut her loose himself—one hard yank of his knife, no apology—because he needed his hands free to hold the line that was dissolving around him. She'd spent two days as Corvane's prisoner, and in those two days she had learned exactly one useful thing about the Inquisitor's army: half of them were boys from villages near the old border, and boys from villages near the border grew up on songs nobody would admit were Vaeli songs.

So when she got loose, Tally didn't run for the trees. She ran *toward* the sound.

Wendell Lockridge stood at the door with her hands raised and her voice cracked down the middle, calling names into the thinning dark, and the dead of Vael stood behind her like an audience waiting for the play to finally start. The name Tally caught first was one her grandmother used to sing at the washline when she thought no one listened. *Maerlin. Maerlin of the low fields.* Tally said it out loud before she knew she meant to, and the nearest ghost turned toward her voice like a sunflower finding morning.

"Oh," Tally said. "Oh, that's—that works."

She started shouting names. All of them. Every scrap of song and rhyme and lullaby the border villages had smuggled through a hundred years of pretending, dressed up as nonsense so no one would burn it. It wasn't much. It was maybe thirty names against however many thousands stood in that silvered field. But thirty voices joined Wendell's, and then forty, and the dead began to move toward the door in something that wasn't quite a line and wasn't quite a flood but was unmistakably *homecoming.*

A soldier grabbed her arm. She spun, ready to bite him, and found him crying.

"Say the one about the miller," he whispered. "My gran used to—the one about the miller's daughter."

Tally said it. He let go of her arm and said the next name with her, and after him another soldier, and another, until the Crown's own ranks were fraying into a choir.

*Bramble*

Bramble had carried men off battlefields before, but never like this, and never this one.

Hesk weighed too little now. That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing as she and two of the smugglers bore him on the bier of lashed oars and cloak they'd thrown together under the tollhouse ambush. Too little, for a man who'd once carried her out of a burning warehouse without breaking stride. His breathing was wet and slow, and every time he exhaled she counted the gap before the next inhale like a woman counting coins she already knew weren't enough.

"Put me down where I can see," Hesk rasped.

"You can see fine horizontal."

"Bramble." He caught her sleeve with fingers that had no grip left in them. "I deserted because I wouldn't burn a village. Nobody ever told me its name. Twenty years I've been carrying that. Put me down where I can hear the names."

So she put him down where he could see the door, and she knelt beside him, and she did the thing Bramble never did, which was to say out loud, in front of witnesses: "You're not dying tonight. I've decided. I'm very persuasive."

Hesk smiled with bloody lips. "Terrible negotiator," he said. "Always have been."

Then the singing started—not just Wendell's voice anymore, not just the dead's, but Tally's bright ragged shout and the wavering baritone of soldiers and, gods help her, Bramble heard her own crew joining in, the same crew who'd grumbled the whole road north—and Hesk closed his eyes and listened, and his breathing evened out just enough that she stopped counting.

*Ilsa*

Ilsa Verrow stood at the edge of the gathered dead with her family's book open in both hands, and for the first time in her life she understood why her great-great-grandmother had split the prophecy in two.

Not to hide the danger. Not only that. To hide the *hope.*

The hidden half, read alone, read like a warning: *the door devours whoever opens it; the door demands more than breath; the door will take a keeper.* Read alone, it was a reason to seal everything forever. But read alongside the public half—read the way Ilsa was reading it now, aloud, her voice shaking across the meadow while arrows flew and Corvane screamed orders nobody followed—it became something else entirely. A price tag. And prices could be paid.

"*And the door shall know its opener,*" she read, "*by the speaking of the names, living tongue and dead together, for a country is not its walls but its memory, and memory must be shared to live.*"

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Her whole life she'd been the girl holding someone else's book, guarding someone else's guilt, inheriting someone else's fear. Now the book was just paper, and the truth was standing in front of her in silver light wearing ten thousand faces, and Ilsa found she didn't need the prophecy to tell her what came next.

"Wendell!" she called. "It doesn't want your death! It wants your *voice!* Both halves—it was always both halves!"

Wendell turned. Across the distance between them, in the middle of a battlefield going soft with song, the bone-carver smiled—a small, tired, absolutely unhidden smile—and nodded.

*A Soldier Named Dren*

He hadn't given his name to anyone in years. Soldiers didn't, really; you were your rank and your company and the color of your sash. But standing in the meadow above the door-site, listening to a fence-girl sing his grandmother's lullaby in a language the Crown said was dead, Dren found he wanted his name back.

Dren. It was a border name. He'd never known that mattered until tonight.

Beside him, Sergeant Ovela—who had cut the prisoner's ropes, who had quietly ordered third squad to lower their bows twenty minutes ago, who had served Corvane eleven loyal years—was weeping openly and reciting names like a man reciting debts. Ahead of them, the Inquisitor himself stood alone in the trampled grass, sword drawn and useless, watching his authority dissolve one verse at a time.

"Orders?" Dren asked, because someone had to ask, and because asking gave Ovela permission to answer honestly.

Ovela looked at the dead of Vael—at their patience, their terrible gentle patience, how none of them had raised a hand against a single soldier all night despite having every right—and she looked at the door glowing like dawn underwater.

"Sheathe it," Ovela said. "All of you. Sheathe everything." Then, louder, over the singing: "Third squad! With me—we're going to carry that wounded man closer. He deserves to hear this properly."

Nobody objected. Somewhere behind Dren, a boy from Lowmarsh dropped his spear in the grass and started singing a harvest round in a language he claimed he'd forgotten, and everyone pretended to believe him, and nobody cared.

*Dren said his grandmother's name into the dark. It felt like putting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.*

*Wendell*

They came to her in waves.

That was the only word for it. Wendell stood before the last door of Vael with her palms pressed flat against living stone, and the names arrived in waves—the dead giving theirs, the living answering, each pair of voices braided together like rope. *Seren of the river courts. Yes. Aldous Vane, tanner. Yes. Maren, Maren, three Marens, all cousins, all drowned in the same winter. Yes, yes, yes.*

Every name spoken made the door warmer under her hands. Every name spoken made the veil thinner, brighter, closer to song.

She had spent her whole life believing her gift was a burden to be endured in silence. One rule, learned young, kept absolutely: *never repeat what the dead say aloud.* And here was the joke the century had played on her—that the rule was the lock, and she was the key, and the moment she broke her silence the whole country's silence broke with it.

"Ilsaline Verrow," she heard behind her, clear and ringing, and turned to see the scholar standing among soldiers and smugglers alike, hand on her heart. "Descendant of Maera the Prophet, who wrote the truth and was burned for it. My name, spoken. Let it be counted."

"Talia Quick, called Tally!" from her left. "Fence-girl, liar, and granddaughter of the border, and I never met a name I wouldn't sell except these ones!"

"Hesk Marrow," said the deserter, faint but fierce, propped on his bier. "Soldier of the Crown who refused. Count mine twice if it helps."

"Captain Bramble Oakwright and the free crew of the *Kestrel*, all present, some reluctant, none leaving."

And then the soldiers, one by one, rank by rank, names like stones dropped into still water, ripples going out and out.

Wendell pressed her forehead to the stone. It hummed against her skin like a struck bell that had waited a hundred years for the rest of the chord.

"Not yet," she told it softly, the way she'd speak to any carving halfway finished. "Almost. But not yet. There's one more voice."

*Corvane*

Inquisitor Edran Corvane stood apart from his shattered command with his sword point-down in the earth, both hands on the pommel, because if he let go of the sword he did not trust what his hands would do.

They had taken his army without touching it. That was the unbearable part. No charge, no spell, no blood—just names, sung back at the Empire by its own soldiers, and the Empire's founding lie coming apart like wet paper in front of him. He had crossed the mountains to prevent this. He had executed a smuggler in Thornfell square to prevent this. His family had held the seal for five generations, honor built on honor built on ash—

On ash. On the ashes of a massacre record his grandfather had burned, the record Ilsa Verrow had just recited from memory, word for word, to men whose grandfathers' grandfathers had signed it.

*Vael was real. Vael was murdered. We did it, and we wrote ourselves the heroes.*

His knees nearly went. He locked them.

The dead were turning toward the door now in their thousands, and at the center of it all stood the carver—the woman he'd interrogated in Thornfell, the one he'd known was hiding something, gods, she'd been hiding *everything*, hiding an entire kingdom inside her skull since childhood. She lifted her head and looked directly at him across the meadow, and her expression held no triumph. That was somehow worse than hatred would have been. It was pity, worn gently, offered freely.

"Inquisitor," she called, and her voice carried the way bells carry. "Your ancestor's name is in my head. He's been there since I was nine years old. He's been asking forgiveness since I was nine years old."

Corvane's breath left him like a wound.

"He says his name was Alaric Corvane," Wendell said, "and he helped light the fires, and he has regretted it every night for a hundred years, and he wants to go home with the others. He wants to stand in his own country one time before the end."

The sword handle was slick under Corvane's grip. Around him his soldiers waited—not for orders, he realized. They were past orders. They were waiting to see what kind of man he was when nothing was left to enforce.

Edran Corvane let go of the sword.

It fell in the grass. He walked forward through the parted crowd—past Ovela, past the fence-girl, past the scholar with the book—up the long slope to where the carver stood with her hands on the door of a country everyone had sworn did not exist.

"The record my family burned," he said, when he reached her. His voice came out wrecked. He did not try to repair it. "I know parts of it. From my grandfather's private papers. Names the others don't have."

"Then speak them," Wendell said.

And Edran Corvane, last warden of the seal, opened his mouth and began to give the dead of Vael the names his family had stolen—his voice cracking, his honor in ruins, choosing rightly at last and far too late and anyway, anyway—and behind him the whole meadow took the names up, living and dead in one rising chord, and the door beneath Wendell's hands began, at long last, to sing.

* * *

The stone warmed. Then it shivered. Then, beneath Wendell's palms, it began to move—slowly, grindingly, like a jaw remembering how to open—and every soul in the meadow, living and dead alike, fell silent to watch a kingdom's last door swing wide onto light that had no source and no shadow.

Beyond the threshold: not darkness. Not heaven. Home. Hills that existed on no surviving map, green under a sky the color of remembered summer, waiting.

The dead began to move toward it.

And Wendell felt, rising through the stone and into her bones, the door's final question—the one the hidden half of the prophecy had warned of, the price that remained unpaid:

*Who stays?*
