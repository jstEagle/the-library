## 20. The Dead Go Home

I've run cargo through the Thornfell passes for twenty-two years, and in all that time I learned one law older than any Crown statute: the mountains keep what they take. You don't get things back. Not cargo, not people, not kingdoms. The mountains are a ledger written in loss, and every smuggler worth her salt learns to read it and look away.

Tonight the mountains gave it all back.

I stood at the threshold with Tally's hand crushing mine—she'd come back to us at the last, Corvane's soldiers releasing her into the chaos like she was a coal too hot to hold—and I watched a door that had been stone for a hundred years open onto light that wasn't firelight or moonlight or anything I could name. And out of that light, singing, came the dead.

Not walking so much as *arriving*, the way weather arrives. They came through in their hundreds, then their thousands, and every one of them was singing. It wasn't a song I knew. It was in Vaeli, old and round and full of falling notes, and I understood maybe one word in twenty—but understanding wasn't the point. The point was that it was *loud*. The point was that the mountains took the sound up and threw it from peak to peak until the whole range was ringing with it, canyon after canyon catching the melody and passing it along like smugglers passing contraband, hand to hand to hand, until there was no place in those hills where the song wasn't.

A country remembering itself. That's what it sounded like. Not a haunting. A homecoming.

Wendell stood closest to the door—I'd learn later why, what it cost her, what she'd promised—and the dead passed around her like water around a stone in a river, never touching, always acknowledging. Some of them bowed as they went by. One old man in robes gone gray as fog pressed his ghostly hand briefly over his heart toward her before he stepped through, and Wendell's face did something I'd never seen it do in all the years I've known her: it broke open.

Hesk lay on a bier behind us, propped against Bramble-cargo crates we'd emptied for the purpose, breathing. Breathing. I kept checking, the way you check a coin purse in a crowd. He'd taken a blade meant for Wendell at the tollhouse ambush and then a musket ball meant for all of us at the standoff, and he should be dead twice over, but Hesk had always been a stubborn son of a deserter and apparently death had finally gotten tired of asking. His eyes were open. He was watching the dead go home with an expression I'd only ever seen on him once before—the night his regiment burned and he walked away from the Crown forever.

"Captain," he said, without looking at me. "You hearing this?"

"Everyone's hearing this," I said. "The deaf are hearing this."

"It's the muster call." His voice was rough as gravel road. "My grandmother used to hum it. Never told me what it was. Said it was just an old tune." He laughed, wet and cracked. "It wasn't just an old tune. She was Vaeli. They were all—" He stopped. Started again. "All these years I thought I deserted one country. Turns out I was born in the wrong one twice."

Tally was crying. I want that on the record, because Tally would deny it under torture. Fence-girl, lockpick, liar of the first order, standing in the cold with tears running down her dirty face while ghosts sang past her. She wasn't even trying to hide it. I think that's when I understood what tonight was doing to us. It wasn't giving the dead something. It was giving *us* something—we who'd spent our whole lives in the borderlands pretending the silence up there in the peaks was empty. It was never empty. We'd just been taught not to hear.

* * *

Corvane stood apart from his soldiers, and his soldiers stood apart from each other, and the whole formation of the Crown's finest had come apart like a knitted sleeve with a pulled thread. Some still held their weapons. More didn't. A sergeant—a broad, scarred woman I'd have crossed the street to avoid an hour earlier—had her helmet off and was singing. Badly. Off-key, missing half the words, singing anyway, and two Vaeli dead paused on their way through to correct her, patient as music teachers, and she adjusted and got it right and laughed like a child.

You can't fight a door that opens onto your own grandmother's lullaby. Corvane must have known that better than anyone. He'd built his whole life on the lie that the silence was righteous.

He looked hollowed out. Ilsa Verrow had read the massacre record aloud an hour ago—names, dates, orders signed by his own ancestor—and I'd watched the truth go through his ranks like fever through a barracks. But it was the singing that finished it, I think. Truth is a weapon; grief is a siege. He stood at the edge of the light with his ledger-family honor in ashes around him and did nothing. For a long moment I honestly believed he'd draw steel and die badly, sealing nothing, saving no one.

Instead he walked to the bier.

Hesk watched him come. So did I, with my hand nowhere near my knife because my knife would have been a joke against what happened next, which was nothing. Which was Corvane kneeling down in the mud beside a wounded deserter he'd have hanged three days ago and saying, quietly, in a voice stripped of everything official:

"The southern pass will be open for the crossing. All of them. No tolls, no patrols." A breath. "As long as I hold office."

Hesk studied him the way you study a forged coin, looking for the flaw. Then: "Your family wrote the orders."

"Yes."

"And now you'll write the reversal."

"I can't reverse them. There's no undoing a century. There's only—" Corvane's jaw worked. "There's only what gets carved after."

That phrase. *What gets carved after.* I looked at Wendell, still standing in the stream of the dead, and I saw her hear it too.

* * *

The crossing took most of the night. I won't pretend I witnessed all of it; grief and wonder are exhausting in equal measure, and at some point Tally fell asleep sitting up against my shoulder mid-sentence, mid-story about the tollhouse betrayal, mid-apology that nobody wanted or needed. The dead kept coming. Farmers and fiddlers, courtiers and cooks, a whole kingdom's worth of ordinary souls who'd been standing in a cold anteroom for a hundred years waiting for someone to remember they existed.

Near dawn, the song changed. Slowed. The way a tide turns—not stopping, just reversing its intention. The last of them came through in ones and twos, and some lingered at the threshold, and I realized with a start that they were lingering near Wendell.

An old woman stopped before her. Ghost-pale, but with eyes bright as new coins. She said something in Vaeli, soft, and Wendell answered—in Vaeli, halting, the accent of a border-town bone-carver who'd learned her language from whispers instead of cradles—and whatever passed between them made the old woman smile like sunrise.

Then she stepped through, and the light behind the door dimmed like a lamp being banked for the night rather than snuffed.

And the door closed.

Gently. That's the part I keep returning to, the part I've told in every tavern from here to the coast. Not slammed, not sealed with thunder and judgment, not swallowed by avalanche. It simply eased shut the way a mother eases a nursery door, careful of the sleeping child inside, and the stone knit itself smooth, and the mountain went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. There's a difference, and I know it now in my bones. The silence before had been a held breath. This quiet was an exhale.

* * *

We buried no one, in the end. I want to be clear about that, because I'd braced myself for graves. Hesk survived—fevered for two days in a shepherd's hut below the pass while Tally bullied a herbalist and I bullied everyone else, but he woke on the third morning hungry, complaining about the food, asking after his horse with more concern than he'd ever shown his own limbs. Deserter, deserter no longer; Corvane's first act of the new dispensation was a pardon signed in ink that probably shook.

We mourned anyway. You don't need a body to mourn. We sat outside the chapel of unnamed bones on the way home—the real one, the one where Wendell had spoken a hundred names into the dark—and we left offerings. Bramble cargo: salt, honey, a bottle of the good plum brandy I'd been saving for a celebration that never seemed to arrive. Tally left a stolen ring, then un-stole it, then left it again. Hesk, leaning on my arm, hummed the muster call. Off-key. Getting it right.

"They're home," Tally said, when the humming faded. "So why does it feel like a funeral?"

"Because it is one," I said. "Good funerals are. They're for the living, love. Always were."

Wendell said nothing. She'd been saying very little since the mountain. But she'd brought her carving kit up the path with her, and I caught her testing a chisel edge against her thumb, thoughtful, the way she does when a shape is already forming in her mind and the rest of us just haven't seen the stone yet.

I asked her, on the road down into Thornfell, what she was going to do now. The door closed. The prophecy spent. A girl could rest.

She was quiet long enough that I thought she hadn't heard. Then:

"There are names," she said, "that no one has ever said. In the chapel. In the records Corvane burned. In the bones I've handled my whole life without knowing whose hands they'd been." Her thumb moved over the chisel. "Someone should say them. Someone should carve them where the rain can't reach and the Crown can't burn them."

"In stone?" I asked.

"In living stone," she said, and there was something in her voice I'd never heard before—not the discipline, not the hiding, but a kind of settled fire, like a forge banked and ready. "Granite remembers. And I'm done pretending I don't."

We came down out of the mountains in the grey of morning, five of us where seven had set out and six where four had nearly ended, and Thornfell lay below us smoking its chimney-smoke, ordinary as bread. Somewhere in that town was a commission block waiting for a carver, and somewhere in the hills was a country that had finally gone home, and somewhere in Wendell Lockridge's pockets were chisels that were about to change what this border remembered of itself.

I put out feelers that same week for granite. Good granite. The kind that outlives empires.

Because I've carried contraband of every kind across those passes—salt, silk, secrets, souls' belongings—and I know contraband when I see it. What Wendell means to carve is the most dangerous cargo this country's seen in a hundred years.

Truth, cut deep enough to be read by anyone.

And I intend to help her smuggle it.
