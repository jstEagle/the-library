## 21. Carved in Living Stone

The mountain took three days to stop singing.

That was what the survivors called it afterward—the ringing that hung over the passes like the aftertone of a struck bell, so deep you felt it in your teeth rather than heard it. The dead of Vael had gone through their door singing, and stone remembered what flesh forgot. Shepherds on the high slopes swore their sheep would not graze facing north. Children pressed their palms to boulders and claimed they felt a pulse.

Wendell Lockridge felt all of it, every hour, because she had chosen to stay on this side of the veil when it closed, and staying had consequences no one had warned her about. The dead who remained were quieter now—most of them—but quiet for Wendell had never meant silence. It meant listening whether she wanted to or not.

She sat at her workbench in Thornfell on the first morning of the rest of her life, and for the first time since she was six years old, she did not pretend she was alone.

"You're holding the chisel wrong," said Master Ondrey's ghost, drifting through the wall of her workshop the way he'd drifted through it for eleven years, ever since his heart gave out mid-lesson and left his apprentice to finish his commissions without knowing she could still ask him how.

"I'm holding it exactly how you taught me."

"I taught you to carve bone. That's granite." He peered at the great slab taking up half her floor—a single block of living stone hauled down from the pass on twelve oxen and a great deal of stubbornness. "Bone forgives. Stone holds a grudge."

"Then I'll learn its grudge," Wendell said. "I have time."

She had more time than she knew what to do with, actually. That was the strange part. For thirty years her hours had been shaped around concealment: carving by day, flinching from voices by night, arranging her whole self around the secret like a splint around a broken limb. Now the secret was public knowledge, argued about in taverns and pulpits across three provinces, and her days lay open before her like an unmarked page—which, she supposed, was exactly what Ilsa Verrow had gone off to write on.

* * *

Ilsa had stayed two weeks after the door closed. Long enough to see Hesk upright again, moving stiffly but moving, his wound bound with Vaeli linen that the old woman in the ceded lands had pressed into Bramble's hands as if she'd been keeping it for a century. Which, Wendell suspected, in some sense she had.

Long enough for the arguments to start, too. Thornfell had never been a town that agreed about anything except weather and taxes, and now half of it wanted to celebrate the dead of Vael as martyrs while the other half wanted to arrest someone, anyone, for letting them go home. The Crown's writ had gone strangely soft since that night. Corvane's soldiers had returned south carrying accounts no commander wanted written down, and more than one had deserted on the road rather than report what they'd seen: their own Inquisitor laying down his seal-tools to hold a door open with his bare hands.

Corvane himself had ridden out of Thornfell the morning after, alone, without a word to anyone. Wendell had watched him go from her window and felt something she refused to name—not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but perhaps the recognition of a fellow keeper of terrible knowledge. He had spent his whole life guarding a lie. She understood the weight of that better than anyone alive.

On Ilsa's last evening, they sat in Wendell's workshop among the bone shavings and lamp oil smell, sharing a bottle of Bramble's terrible plum brandy.

"The histories will call it the Reconciliation," Ilsa said, turning her cup. "Or the Night of Names. Something tidy. They always want something tidy."

"What will you call it?"

"The truth." Ilsa smiled tiredly. "With footnotes. I've been granted access to the royal archives—actual access, signed orders, Corvane's seal countersigned by the Chancellor herself. Do you understand what that means? Everything his family burned, there are duplicates of. Everything they erased, someone kept a copy out of spite or sentiment or simple bureaucratic incompetence. A hundred years of clerks quietly refusing to forget." She laughed, a little wetly. "Empires fall to armies. Lies fall to filing errors."

"And you'll put it all back?"

"All of it. Every name, every village, every song. The maps get redrawn. The schoolbooks get rewritten. There will be riots in three capitals, probably, and my name will be cursed in at least one dynasty's worth of ancestral halls." She looked up. "Your name won't be, though. Yours goes in the front. *Recorded by Wendell Lockridge, carver of Thornfell, who heard the dead when none of us could.*"

Wendell's ears went hot. "Put Bramble's name instead. Or Tally's. They bled for it. I just listened."

"You just listened," Ilsa repeated softly. "Do you know how many people would have given anything to do only that? To hear and be believed?" She stood, pressed both hands briefly on Wendell's shoulders—an old scholar's gesture, formal and warm—and left before either of them could make the moment heavier than it needed to be.

At the door she paused. "There's one thing I didn't tell you. In the archive copies. The prophet's second half—the part my family guarded—it doesn't end where we thought. After the warning about the devouring door, there's a final line. My grandmother thought it was corruption in the manuscript." She recited it slowly, the way you handle something fragile: "*And when the last door is shut, one shall remain who hears, and the hearing shall become her country.*"

They looked at each other for a long moment.

"That's not a curse," Wendell said finally. "That's a job description."

"That's a prophecy fulfilled differently than feared," Ilsa agreed. Then she shouldered her satchel full of copied pages and walked south toward the capitals and the burning of old lies, and Wendell went back to her stone.

* * *

The monument took shape the way rivers take shape—not by plan but by pressure, finding its course through resistance.

She had decided early on that she would not design it. The dead who chose to stay would do that. And they came, in the weeks after the door closed, filtering back to Thornfell the way travelers return to an inn that fed them once: a drowned smuggler named Ferren Cade whose memorial she had carved wrong-rivered a lifetime ago; a Vaeli court seamstress named Maren who had wandered the borderlands for a century looking for her lost needle-case; two shepherd children, a tollkeeper, a soldier of the old kingdom who had died holding a bridge nobody remembered. Twenty-three of them, in the end. Not enough to fill a city. Enough to fill a workshop.

"They stay because of you," Maren told her once, watching Wendell work. "Not duty. Not the door. You. You're the only place in the world where being dead is ordinary."

"It shouldn't be ordinary," Wendell said. "It should be *listened to*."

"Same thing, for people like us." The seamstress drifted closer to the slab. "Put the border where it really was. Not where the maps say. The river bend. We used to picnic there."

So Wendell carved the true border first—a long line of riverbend running across the face of the stone, accurate to the inch because she had walked it with ghosts guiding her hands. Around it grew the map of Vael itself: villages rising under her chisel, the capital with its seven towers, roads and shrines and the little chapel of unnamed bones that was unnamed no longer. And into each village, each house, each field, she cut names.

That was the work that mattered. That was the work that took everything.

Every name had to be right. She had learned that in the chapel, reading the massacred court's names aloud from memory of their voices—one mistake, one syllable softened or hardened wrong, and the whole room had shuddered like a struck bell. Names were not labels. Names were doors in miniature. So she carved slowly, checking each against the whispers, and when a ghost corrected her—and they always corrected her, fussing over vowels like Master Ondrey fussing over chisels—she re-cut the letter without complaint.

Tally came by most evenings, ostensibly to fence Wendell spare tools and actually to sit on the workbench swinging her legs and narrating town gossip at a volume calculated to include the ghosts.

"—so then Marta Kess says the priest can't bless a monument to a *foreign* kingdom, and Bramble says, sweet as you please, 'Funny, the Crown taxed it as domestic for a hundred years,' and Marta went purple—oh, and Hesk ate solid food today. Real food. Swore at the cook for under-salting it. Doctor says the scar's clean." Tally's chatter faltered, just slightly, on the last word. She still had nights, Wendell knew. Nights where she woke reaching for the tollhouse wall, for the moment the ambush broke and Hesk went down between her and the crossbows, buying her escape with his body. Everyone had those nights. Wendell's were about a door and the feeling of it drinking her voice.

"He'll dance at the dedication," Wendell said. "He'll complain about the music the whole time."

"He'd better." Tally recovered her grin. "Bramble wants to know when you'll need the oxen again. She says the east face is going to need another slab, and she knows a man in Lowmarsh who owes her."

"The east face isn't designed yet."

"No," Tally said, hopping down, suddenly serious in the way she occasionally let herself be, brief as a flash of silver under river water. "But it will be. That's the thing nobody gets about you, Wendell. You were always going to build this. Even when you were pretending you couldn't hear anybody. This was always where the listening went." She kissed Wendell's cheek—grease and lamp-oil smelling, familiar—and let herself out.

Wendell stood a while in the lamplight, chisel idle, looking at the west face where the finished names caught shadow.

Belonging, she had always assumed, was something that happened to other people by accident. You stumbled into it, the way you stumbled into a river, and either you drowned or you learned to swim. But standing here, in a workshop full of the patient dead and the loyal living, she understood it had never been an accident at all. Belonging was carved. Stroke by stroke, correction by correction, year by year. You made the space and then you made yourself fit it, and if the fit was imperfect—if the carver's hand shook, if the stone held a grudge—that too became part of the record.

She picked up her chisel and went back to work.

* * *

The dedication came on the first anniversary, which no one had planned and everyone somehow knew.

By then the monument stood nine feet tall at the center of Thornfell's market square, on the spot where the town gallows had been until Bramble bought the timber and Hesk, walking with a cane and a permanent scowl, had personally supervised its demolition. The west face bore the true map and four hundred and eleven names. The east face waited blank—for the ones still being found, Wendell said, and for the ones yet to speak. Master Ondrey hovered over her shoulder through the final polishing, offering unsolicited critiques, and she let him, because a teacher's ghost deserved a masterpiece to haunt.

Half of Thornfell came. The other half watched from doorways, arms crossed, and that was fine too. Truth didn't require unanimous applause. It required only to be spoken where it couldn't be unspoken.

Bramble spoke first, short and rough, about debts owed to the dead. Tally read the roll of the twenty-three who had crossed and the twenty-three—coincidence, Maren insisted, meaningful coincidence—who had stayed. Hesk leaned on his cane and said nothing at all, which for Hesk was a eulogy, a confession, and a love letter combined. When the crowd began, hesitantly, to repeat the names after Tally—the way they had learned to do it in the mountains, living voices and dead ones together—Wendell stood beside her stone and listened to her town sing a country most of them had never heard of, and found she was crying without any shame about it whatsoever.

Afterward, in the emptied square, with lanterns guttering and the mountain's deep bell-tone fading under the stars, Maren drifted to the monument's edge and laid her insubstantial hand against the carved riverbend.

"Well," the seamstress said. "Now we're on a map again."

"Now you always were," Wendell said. "People just stopped looking."

Maren smiled, and around her the others gathered—Ondrey and Ferren Cade and the shepherd children grown tall as trees in death, the tollkeeper, the bridge-soldier, all of them luminous faintly in the dark like embers deciding not to go out. They had a country again, even if it was one workshop wide and one stubborn woman deep. They had a carver. They had, at last, someone who would say their names correctly.

Above the pass, far off, the stone sang one low note—the door acknowledging the anniversary, or acknowledging them, or maybe just acknowledging that some things, once opened honestly, never fully close.

Wendell touched the blank east face, feeling under her palm the grain of the living stone, already listening for the next name.

It wouldn't be long. She could hear it coming.
