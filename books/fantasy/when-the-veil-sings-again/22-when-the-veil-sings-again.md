## 22. When the Veil Sings Again

The apprentice broke her third knife that morning, which Wendell considered a fair average.

"You're gripping it like you're angry at the bone," Wendell said, not looking up from her own work. Her hands moved on their own now, after twenty years—no, that wasn't right. After one year and then nineteen more. She still counted from the door. "The bone remembers everything. You don't have to force it to speak."

Pell scowled at the snapped blade in her fist. She was fourteen, all elbows and freckles, with a father who'd drowned in the Greywash and a mother who worked the dye-works and a silence in her that Wendell had spotted across a crowded market square the way you spot smoke over rooftops. The girl had been staring at nothing—at someone—with her lips pressed white shut, and Wendell had known that look because she'd worn it for thirty years.

"She keeps asking if I've told you," Pell said. "The lady in grey. She's very insistent."

"What does she want told?"

"That her name was Maren. Not what the stone by the ferry says."

Wendell nodded and turned back to the memorial slab she was carving—the new one, the big one, the one half of Thornfell had argued about and the other half had wept over: the Wall of Names, rising at the town's heart where the well used to be, every name recovered from Vael cut into living granite in her own hand. It had taken four years. It would take four more. Some mornings her wrists ached so badly she could barely hold a spoon, and some mornings she woke before dawn with a name singing in her skull like a struck bell, and she went to the wall in her nightclothes and carved until the sun came up, and no one stopped her, because everyone in Thornfell knew whose hands those were.

"Tell her I'll fix the stone," Wendell said. "Tell her it'll be done before winter."

Pell's shoulders came down an inch. That was the whole art of it, really—not the carving. The telling.

* * *

On the anniversary, Thornfell closed its shops.

It had started small—a few candles, a moment of quiet—and grown into something the town guarded fiercely, though the Crown had tried twice to make them stop and once sent a minor official to lecture them about sedition. The official had stayed for the vigil and gone home the next day without giving his speech. There were things even the Crown had learned not to stand in front of.

They gathered at dusk in the square around the Wall of Names, as they did every year on the night the veil sang. Lanterns. Song, sometimes—the old Vaeli song, the one the old woman beyond the passes had taught them, the one Hesk hummed off-key because he claimed it made the ache in his leg behave. He walked without a stick now. Tally said the bier had embarrassed him into healing.

Wendell stood where she always stood, at the base of the wall, near the first names she'd ever carved there: the court of Vael, massacred in a single night and buried nameless in a chapel for a hundred years. Maren of the ferry-stone was further down. So was the drowned smuggler from her first carving, whose ghost had complained about the wrong river right up until the day he'd crossed through the door, waving at her on his way home.

Ilsa stood beside her, as she always did, with her grey streak coming in early and her prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle that never quite arrived on this night but always threatened. She'd published, three years ago. *The True History of Vael*, six hundred pages, banned in two provinces and taught in schools in five others. The Crown had given up banning it when burning it only made people curious.

"Page forty-one tonight?" Wendell asked.

"Page forty-one." Ilsa smiled. Every year she read the same passage aloud, the one that named 'the carver who hears,' and every year the crowd looked at Wendell, and every year Wendell pretended to find this embarrassing, and every year nobody believed her pretending.

Bramble had the lanterns lit before full dark. Corvane's soldiers used to send an honor guard; now they just sent flowers, laid at the wall's northern end where the soldiers' own dead were listed—the ones who had joined the recitation, the ones whose descendants wrote letters thanking a town they'd never visited.

Corvane himself had died two winters back. Quietly, in his sleep, in a modest house he'd bought within sight of the mountains he'd spent his life sealing. He'd left Wendell his ledger—the family ledger, the one that recorded the erasure—and a note with no signature that said only: *Hold the door open in whatever way you can.* She kept both in the drawer of her workbench. She thought about him most years on this night. Forgiveness hadn't come, exactly. But something adjacent to it had moved in and gotten comfortable.

* * *

It happened at moonrise, as it always happened.

The veil thinned. You couldn't see it thinning—you could only feel it, a pressure change like weather, like the moment before a summer storm. The lantern flames leaned all in the same direction, toward the mountains, toward the pass, toward the place where the door had stood and closed gently behind ten thousand singing dead.

And the door glowed.

Not opened. Never opened again—that was the whole mercy of it, the thing the hidden half of the prophecy had feared and the visible half had promised, reconciled at last: the door devoured only unwilling voices, and having heard every voice in both kingdoms speak together once, it wanted nothing more. It simply glowed, deep in the pass, a seam of gold light along the mountain's flank, visible for perhaps a quarter hour, acknowledged by everyone in the square who turned toward it as one body.

The ghosts came out for it. They always did.

Not many anymore—each year fewer, each year more of them choosing, finally, to cross—but enough. The lady in grey drifted down from the dye-works hill to stand near Pell. A dead shepherd Wendell knew took his usual post at the square's edge. And one or two strangers, always strangers, newly dead souls who'd heard somewhere that on this night, in this town, someone could tell them how to go home.

That was the other part of Wendell's work now. The part no one had planned.

"They're asking," Pell whispered, eyes wide, hand tight on Wendell's sleeve. "There's a man—he doesn't know how he died—he's asking if this is the night."

"This is the night," Wendell said. "Tell him to wait for the song. Everyone goes home during the song."

* * *

The song began the way it always began, with Hesk's cracked baritone finding the wrong key and Bramble hauling him bodily into the right one, and Tally laughing, and the crowd taking it up—hundreds of voices, living and dead, filling the square the way the pass had filled once, years ago, with a country remembering itself.

Wendell listened.

She had learned, over the years, that listening was not the same as hearing, and hearing was not the same as obeying. The dead spoke constantly; they had always spoken; the gift had never been the burden. The burden had been the silence around it—the rule she'd kept for thirty years, *never repeat what the dead say aloud*, built brick by brick out of loneliness until Ilsa Verrow had walked into her shop with a borrowed book and broken every window in it.

Now the rule ran the other direction: *never let a name go unspoken*. It was heavier work, in its way. It was better work.

The glow in the pass brightened. The song crested. And in the space between one verse and the next, Wendell felt the dead move—felt them turn toward the light the way seedlings turn toward a window—and the man who didn't know how he died lifted his face and understood, all at once, everything, and stepped into the gold and was gone, and the lady in grey squeezed Pell's shoulder once, lightly, like a mother deciding she could wait one more year, and stayed.

Then the glow faded, gentle as a banked fire, and the veil settled back into its ordinary thickness, and the square exhaled.

"Same time next year," Tally said, snuffing lanterns.

"Same time next year," said everyone, which was not a plan but a vow.

* * *

Later—much later, the square emptied, the town asleep—Wendell walked Pell home past the wall, and the girl stopped halfway down its length and put her palm flat against the granite.

"I can hear them in here," Pell said. "In the stone. Is that normal? Am I—"

"You're a carver who hears," Wendell said. "Or you will be, once your knives stop breaking."

Pell laughed, wet-eyed, thirteen going on apprentice going on something the old books had feared and the new books celebrated. "What happens to me? Do the books say?"

"The old book said the door opens when the carver who hears stands before it." Wendell touched the granite, feeling beneath her palm the faint warmth that never quite left the stone, the way a hearth stays warm long after the fire dies down. "It was afraid of the wrong things. It thought opening meant ending. But the door didn't devour anyone in the end—it fed us instead. Filled us up with names until we couldn't be lonely anymore."

She looked up at the pass, dark now, keeping its secret the way good doors do.

"And it'll open again someday, maybe. For her, maybe, when she's ready. Not as a wound. As a welcome." She steered Pell gently onward, toward lamplight and supper and the long apprenticeship ahead. "The prophecy got one thing right, though. The veil does sing again. Every year. You just have to be the kind of person who listens."

Behind them, in the warm stone of the wall, the dead murmured their names to anyone willing to hear—and the names, at last, sounded less like warnings than like a lullaby, sung by a country that had come home, to a world that had finally learned to answer.
