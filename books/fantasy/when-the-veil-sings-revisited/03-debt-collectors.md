## 3. Debt Collectors

The Inquisition arrived on a Tuesday, which I have always considered a tactless day for catastrophe. Mondays are for disasters — everyone expects them, the kitchens stock extra bread. Tuesdays are for ledgers and quiet copying, for the slow accumulation of small holy work. Instead I got boots.

I heard them before I saw them: forty pairs of iron-shod heels striking the flagstones of the Processional in perfect unison, which is itself a kind of miracle in a valley where nothing is permitted to resonate. The Temple of Quiet Hours had rules about sound. Bells were felt, not heard. Hymns were sung into wool. And yet here came the Inquisition, marching like a metronome with opinions, their arrival announced by a herald whose voice carried all the way to the scriptorium windows as though the valley's famous silence had decided, overnight, to stop doing its job.

"The silence of the Veil Valley is failing," the herald proclaimed, and there was something almost tender in his delivery, the way you'd announce a death in the family to someone who hadn't yet heard. "The Conclave demands the singer responsible. Surrender them, and the valley will be spared audit."

*Spared audit.* The Inquisition had a gift for making annihilation sound like bookkeeping.

I was at my desk when the words came through, mid-stroke on a copy of the Litany of Stillness, and my pen did a thing pens should not do — it hesitated, hovered, trembled like a compass needle finding north toward trouble. Because I knew. Of course I knew. I had known since the night old Maren the pilgrim whispered her plea at the shrine wall and something *answered*, something with a voice like a cave full of bees dreaming. I had known since I found my own handwriting crawling through manuscripts three centuries older than my grandfather, words in a language that predated the valley's founding, written in an ink that matched no recipe in the Temple stores.

The elders had burned those pages. They'd stood me in front of the fire and made me watch, and Mother Superior Kessia had told me — kindly, gently, in the tone one uses for children and the condemned — that some doors are better left painted shut. That the debt I owed the Temple was not merely financial. It was ontological. My silence was part of the architecture.

But architecture, it turns out, can crack. And cracks sing.

* * *

They gave the Inquisition the refectory, because the refectory had the thickest walls and the fewest windows, and because the elders believed — sincerely, pathetically — that courtesy might soften them. I watched from the cloister as the delegation filed in: six inquisitors in slate-grey robes cut so severely they looked like they'd been folded rather than sewn, and at their head a woman whose name I would learn soon enough and hate longer.

But the person I watched most closely was Mother Kessia.

She walked beside them. Not behind, not being escorted — *beside*, matching their pace, her hands folded in that way she had, fingers laced over the ledger of her own survival. She had taught me my letters. She had taught me that ink is a covenant, that every word copied true was a brick in the wall between the world and whatever waited underneath it. She had held my wrist steady through ten thousand hours of practice and told me I had the finest hand in the valley, and that this was both a blessing and a burden, because fine hands are noticed by the wrong people.

And now she walked arm in arm with the people who had come to collect me.

I want to tell you I was surprised. I want to tell you betrayal arrived like lightning, sudden and blinding. But the truth is uglier and more ordinary: I had seen the signs for weeks. The extra portions at her table. The way she'd stopped assigning me the sensitive copies. The conversation I'd overheard through the infirmary wall, her voice low and arithmetic: *"Fourteen years of interest. What they're offering forgives it all."*

Kessia had debts of her own. Debts to the Conclave, debts to a cousin in the capital who'd lent against the Temple's future, debts denominated in favors and silences and one very expensive habit of patronage she'd never discussed with anyone. Her ledger and mine had always been kept in the same book, and I had simply assumed — naively, filially, stupidly — that when the collectors came, she would protect the asset she'd spent twenty years training.

Instead she spent me. One signature. One line in the record: *"The scribe Ansel Ravensworth, indentured, is remanded to the Holy Office of Inquiry as settlement of outstanding obligations."*

I was worth exactly fourteen years of her interest. I did the math later, huddled in the bell-tower where no one could hear me not-crying, and it came out to roughly nine coppers per year of my life. Even by the standards of the Temple's accounting, I was a bargain.

* * *

They came for me at dusk, which was considerate of them — dusk meant fewer witnesses, and witnesses require paperwork. Two inquisitors and a lay brother whose job, as far as I could tell, was to carry the manacles and look disappointed in advance.

Here is what nobody tells you about being arrested by people who believe themselves righteous: they are gentle. The lay brother took my pen from my hand with genuine reverence, wrapped it in cloth, set it aside. "You'll get this back," he said, "if the inquiry goes well." As if my pen were a hostage. As if we were negotiating.

Mother Kessia stood in the doorway of the scriptorium and did not look at me. That was the worst part. Not cruelty — cruelty would at least have been honest, would at least have let me hate her cleanly. She arranged her face into administrative neutrality and studied a point slightly above my left shoulder, and when I passed her she said, in a voice pitched for the record rather than for me:

"He was always a diligent student."

A eulogy, delivered early. Nine coppers a year, and not even a goodbye.

They walked me down the Processional toward the gatehouse, and the valley did something I had never felt it do in fourteen years: it hummed. Under my feet, in the stones, in the air itself — a vibration too low to be called sound, too present to be ignored. The other prisoners — there were no other prisoners, but I imagined solidarity anyway — the stones had never done this before. The silence of the Veil Valley was a held breath, and I had spent my whole life inside it without knowing I was holding it too. Now the breath was going ragged, and everything could feel it.

At the gatehouse, the lead inquisitor — grey-eyed, grey-robed, grey in the way that suggested color had been surgically removed from her personality — reviewed my papers and asked me one question.

"Do you know why the silence is failing?"

I said, "No," which was true, and then I said, "Yes," which was also true, and she marked down both answers without comment, which told me everything about how much truth mattered to the Holy Office.

Then, from somewhere behind her — from the direction of the shrine wall, from the direction of the place where Maren had died whispering — came a sound.

Not loud. Barely a thread. A single sustained note, like wet glass rimmed by a fingertip, rising out of the ground itself, and every inquisitor in the courtyard went rigid, and the lay brother dropped the manacles, and the grey-eyed woman turned her head slowly toward the wall with the expression of someone watching a debt come due that she had personally declared unpayable.

The note bent. Curved. Resolved into something almost like a word, and the word, as far as I could tell, was my name.

Or half of it. Or the shape of it, sung in a language older than naming.

I ran.

I don't remember deciding to run. There was a gap in the sequence of events — inquisitor's face, note, wall, and then I was moving, over the low fence by the tannery where the smell covered everything and no one patrolled, through the goat paths above the terraces, up and up along the spine of the valley where the rock was too poor to farm and too steep to build on. Behind me, horns. Ahead of me, the border.

Everyone in the valley knows where the border is, even though there's no wall, no marker, no line drawn anywhere. You know it the way you know the edge of sleep. Fourteen years I had lived inside the silence, and I had never once crossed out of it, because crossing meant debt-breach, and debt-breach meant the Temple's claim followed you everywhere, enforced by treaty, collected by worse people than inquisitors.

But the Temple had just sold my debt to the Inquisition, and the Inquisition wanted me for the song, and the song wanted me for itself, and somewhere in that triangle of wanting there was a gap exactly the size of a running man.

* * *

I crossed the border at moonrise, and I want to describe it properly, because no one had ever described it to me properly, because no one who crossed ever came back to file a report.

Magic hit me like a tide.

That's the phrase, and it's imprecise, but language fails here the way a rowboat fails in open water. It wasn't a sensation added to the world. It was the world, suddenly, having *depth*. For fourteen years I had been a drawing of a man — flat, silent, pressed between pages. One step past the invisible line and I was poured into three dimensions, four, more. Color got louder. The moonlight had texture, weight, a grain like wood. The air tasted of copper and rain and something older than either, something that had been waiting outside the valley all this time like a dog outside a shop, patient and enormous.

And the song — the fragment I'd been carrying without knowing I carried it, the thing in my blood that the old manuscripts had recognized, that had answered Maren's dying prayer, that had sung my name through solid stone — the song woke up.

It didn't ask permission. It rose out of me like water finding its level, and for one vertiginous moment I was singing — actually singing, aloud, my first sound in a valley that forbade sound, except I wasn't in the valley anymore, was I? I was on the slope beyond it, on my knees in the wet grass, and my voice was producing harmonies I had never learned, in a language I couldn't read but my hand had once written, and the grass around me glowed faintly where the notes fell, and the moon came out from behind the clouds specifically, personally, to watch.

I laughed. I don't know if you've ever laughed while crying while singing while fleeing for your life, but I recommend it, conditionally, the way I'd recommend drinking the whole bottle. It was the freest I had ever been and the most terrified, simultaneously, and the two feelings did not cancel out. They compounded.

Behind me, the horns stopped. The border worked both ways; the Inquisition's authority ended at the same invisible line, and their silence-keeping treaties with the outer world were, apparently, complicated enough that forty armed men declined to test them at midnight. They would wait. They would write letters. They would be patient, the way institutions are patient, the way glaciers are patient.

Ahead of me, downslope, in the black fold of the hills: firelight. A camp. Tents the color of mud, horses hobbled, and figures moving among them with the unhurried economy of people who have done violence before and expect to again.

Mercenaries. Or bandits. At this distance, in this light, the distinction was academic.

I had maybe an hour before they found the glowing grass. Maybe less. I had no money, no weapon, no plan, and a voice that was currently broadcasting my location to anyone with ears and a grudge.

I thought about Mother Kessia, and her neat signature, and her nine coppers a year, and I understood something with total clarity: debt follows you across borders. It's the one thing that does. The valley's silence couldn't cross, but the *owing* could — the Inquisition's claim on me was valid out here, enforceable out here, worth killing for out here.

So I did the only thing a debtor can do when the collectors are coming and the collateral is himself.

I walked down toward the fires, and I prepared, in my head, the best sales pitch of my life.
