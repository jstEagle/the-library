## 4. The Company of Small Mercies

The first thing I learned about the Company of Small Mercies is that they did not, in fact, believe in mercy. They believed in *prices*. The name was a joke at their own expense — a ledger entry, Sera Voss would later tell me, from the day they were founded, when a dying man paid them three coppers and a promise to kill them all if they didn't finish the job.

The second thing I learned is that they tied knots very well.

I know this because I spent my introduction to them face-down in the dirt of their camp, wrists bound behind me, a boot between my shoulder blades, while someone went through my satchel with the tenderness of a customs officer and the greed of a magpie.

"Ledgers," said a voice above me, disappointed. "Ink. A spare shirt. Voss, he's got nothing. He's got *ledgers*."

"I heard you the first time." That was a woman's voice, dry as old paper, coming from somewhere near the fire. "Is he armed?"

"He's armed with a *pen*, Captain."

"Then we're all in terrible danger."

I twisted my head enough to see her. Sera Voss sat on an overturned cart with her boots on a crate, watching me the way one watches weather — not hostile, exactly, but already deciding whether to bring in the laundry. She was perhaps forty, grey-shot braid, a scar through one eyebrow that had clearly been earned by something stupider than a sword. Around the fire: seven or eight figures of varying shapes and levels of menace, including a very large man who was eating my apple, and a wiry girl who appeared to be reading one of my ledgers upside down with genuine fascination.

"My accounts are balanced," I said into the dirt. It came out muffled. The boot pressed down harder.

"He talks," said the magpie, delighted.

"They usually do," Voss said. "Right up until the Inquisition's price on their head gets mentioned." She leaned forward. "You crossed the border out of the Veil Valley yesterday, scribe. Alone, on foot, running like the hounds of heaven were after you. Which, given who was camped at the ford this morning, they may have been. Grey cloaks. Silver suns. Ringing any bells?"

I thought about lying. Then I thought about the fact that I was face-down in the mud of a mercenary camp with no weapon, no money, and a bounty, and I decided that honesty was the last luxury I owned and I might as well spend it.

"Yes," I said. "They want me."

"For what? You steal something? Burn something?"

"Worse. I *am* something."

That got a laugh out of the big man with my apple. Voss didn't laugh. She studied me for a long moment, and I watched her do the arithmetic every captain does with a stray: cost of feeding him against worth of selling him.

"The Inquisition pays well for runners," she said. "Especially ones out of the valley. There's been notices in three towns. 'Singer sought.' Odd phrasing for a fugitive. Usually it's 'heretic' or 'thief.' *Singer*." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "What do you sing, scribe?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's rather the point of where I come from."

* * *

Here is what I knew about selling myself, gleaned from nine years of watching Mother Kessia negotiate with creditors: never apologize, never explain more than the debt requires, and always, *always* make yourself more valuable intact than rendered into parts.

So when they dragged me to sit by the fire — bound, but seated, which felt like a promotion — I gave them the pitch.

"You can sell me to the Inquisition," I began.

"Good start," said the magpie girl.

"—and they'll pay you, certainly. And then they'll burn your names into a register of collaborators, because that's how the Inquisition works; I copied those registers for six years, I should know, there's a whole column for 'auxiliaries, disposable.' Every bounty they pay you comes with a hook in it. Whereas—"

"Whereas?" Voss prompted, when I paused too long.

"Whereas I'm a temple-trained scribe of the Order of Quiet Hours. Nine years copying prayers, contracts, inventories, and confessions. I can read four scripts, cipher two of them, forge a seal credibly enough to fool a toll clerk, and balance a company's books so cleanly that even a tax assessor would weep." I took a breath. "You have a warband. Warbands live and die by paperwork nobody among you wants to do. Contracts unfulfilled, wages disputed, that man—" I nodded at the big one, "—is owed back pay from the Harrowford job, I'd wager, because his captain keeps meaning to write it down and hates ink."

Silence around the fire. The big man stopped chewing.

"How much back pay?" he asked the camp at large, wounded.

"Fen owes me eleven marks," the magpie girl confirmed. "Since *spring*."

"I've been busy," Voss said flatly.

"You've been *avoiding sums*. There's a difference." The girl turned my ledger right side up, squinted at it, and whistled. "Captain. His hand is beautiful. Look at this. My grandmother kept books like this before the tax men took the farm."

"That was the Inquisition too," muttered Fen, the big one, darkly, as though the Inquisition were personally responsible for most of the world's ills, which — I was beginning to understand — in the Company of Small Mercies' cosmology, it was.

Voss hadn't moved. She watched me over the fire with those weather-watching eyes, and I understood that none of it — the ledger, the back pay, the pretty handwriting — was actually the question. The question was simpler and colder.

"Why are they calling you a singer?" she asked again.

And here I made my second decision of the evening, the dangerous one. Because a lie would buy a night's sleep and a knife in the dark later, and the truth might buy something stranger.

"Because the gods of my valley answered a prayer out loud," I said, "and everyone believes it came through me. They're wrong. Mostly. Probably." I held her gaze. "I don't fully know what I am yet. But I know what I'm not: I'm not going to be anyone's weapon. Not theirs. If you sell me, sell me knowing that the moment the Inquisition has me, whatever I am becomes theirs, pointed at people like you. People the law never fixed anything for."

Another silence. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird sang — actually sang, notes hanging loose in the air, and I flinched at the sound of it, still not used to a world where sound did things.

Voss looked at me for a long time. Then she sighed the sigh of a woman adding a line to a ledger she already resented.

"Cutter," she said to the magpie girl, "cut him loose. He cooks tomorrow. And he balances our books. Starting with Fen's eleven marks."

"Starting with—" I began.

"You're a debtor, scribe. Welcome to the company of debtors. We're all owing somebody something; it's practically our founding charter." She stood, stretched, and looked down at me without warmth and without cruelty, which I would learn was simply how she looked at everything. "One wrong note out of you — one whisper of silver-sun business, one prayer answered anywhere near this camp — and the deal changes. Understood?"

"Understood," I said.

My wrists, freed, burned with blood returning. Cutter handed me my satchel, grinned, and said, "Welcome aboard, Singer."

"I told you, I don't—"

"Yeah, yeah." She jerked her chin toward the pot over the fire. "You cook *tomorrow*. Tonight you watch how Bren does it so you don't poison us. Bren! New boy's shadowing you!"

From across the fire, the large man raised my half-eaten apple in salute.

I sat there in the heat and the noise of strangers, wrists stinging, and felt something I hadn't felt since the valley cracked open: not safe, nothing like safe, but *held* — the way a ledger holds numbers. Accounted for. Entered somewhere.

It wasn't trust. Trust takes years. But it was a transaction, and transactions, in my long experience, were the closest thing to love that money-adjacent creatures ever managed.

I fell asleep by their fire listening to the nightbird sing, and for the first time since the pilgrim died at the Temple steps, I didn't dream of silence.

**Sera**

He lied to me twice that night. Small lies, the kind a man tells who's spent his life being owned — the kind you learn to count the way other men count scars.

The first lie was "probably," when he said the song probably didn't come through him. Nobody crosses the Veil Valley border on foot with the Inquisition three days behind them over *probably*.

The second lie was "understood." He agreed too fast and too smooth, a scribe's agreement, the agreement of a man signing terms. I've hired enough soldiers to know the difference between a man who agrees and a man who accepts. This one accepted. Which meant the terms weren't final to him, just filed.

Fine. I wasn't honest either. I told him he cooked tomorrow; I'd already decided he was worth more than his bounty before he finished talking, and I let him think his little speech did it, because a man who thinks he sold himself behaves better than a man who knows he was bought.

The real arithmetic was this: grey cloaks at the ford meant the Inquisition was sweeping the whole river country, which meant they wanted this soft-spoken bookworm badly enough to spend real coin doing it. Things the Inquisition wants badly are either very dangerous or very useful, and in my experience the two categories overlap more than priests admit. Either way, the smart play was the same: keep him close, keep him fed, keep him where I could see the exact moment he decided to run — because he would decide to run. Men with secrets always do. The trick isn't stopping them. It's making sure that when they go, they go *toward* something instead of away from you.

Cutter liked him immediately, which worried me; Cutter likes everyone until she doesn't, and her affections have gotten us into two sieges and one wedding. Fen liked him because Fen likes anyone who mentions his wages. Old Marette took one look at his hands and said, "That boy's never held anything heavier than a pen," and then quietly re-hemmed his sleeve where the ropes had torn it, which was Marette's way of adopting someone, and which worried me more than Cutter.

By morning we had a scribe. By the second morning we had balanced books for the first time in three years, and Fen had his eleven marks, and the whole camp treated Ansel Ravensworth like a clever dog that had wandered in out of the rain — fed, teased, tolerated.

By the fourth morning, I caught him standing alone at the tree line at dawn, lips moving around words that made no sound at all, and the frost on the grass around his feet had melted into a perfect ring of green.

He didn't see me. I didn't say anything.

But I sent Cutter to buy another horse, and I started thinking hard about a job nobody wanted — the Heartspire road — and about what a man carrying a fragment of godsong might be worth to a company whose whole charter, written in jest on a bad night years ago, read: *small mercies only, nothing holy.*

The gods, I figured, could wait their turn in the ledger like everybody else.

I just hadn't yet worked out what happens when they refuse to wait.

And neither, I suspected, had he.
