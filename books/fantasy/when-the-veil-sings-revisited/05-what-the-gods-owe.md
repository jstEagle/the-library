## 5. What the Gods Owe

I want it on the record that I did not hire a scribe. I hired a liability with good penmanship.

The contract was simple enough: escort one nervous Temple refugee from the valley's edge to the ruins of Heartspire, three weeks' travel through hill country nobody with sense travels through. Payment came in the only currency Ansel Ravensworth actually possessed — information, and the promise of more of it, sealed in that careful clerk's hand of his. He wrote out the terms himself, twice over, one copy for each of us, because apparently debt is the one religion he still practices.

"Three weeks," he said, signing.

"Longer, if the Inquisition follows," I said. "Longer if you keep flinching every time a bird sings."

He didn't argue. That should have been my first warning. People who don't argue are either very wise or carrying something heavy, and this man had the posture of someone hauling an anvil under his coat.

* * *

The company took his measure around the first fire, the way we take everyone's measure — by making him sit through supper without being asked to leave.

There are eleven of us when we're whole. Ten, since the winter. I won't do that arithmetic out loud yet.

Brannic, who is large enough to be mistaken for furniture and has been leaned against by strangers in taverns, served the stew. He serves everyone before himself; it's a habit he picked up somewhere he doesn't talk about, some noble house that worked him like a mule and paid him in bruises. Ansel noticed. Scribes notice things. It's their entire trade.

Maren sat across the fire sharpening a knife she never uses on anything but whetstones, which is her way of saying *I'm listening*. She was a midwife once, before a magistrate decided her fees were witchcraft and burned her practice down with everything else she owned. She keeps a ledger too — names, debts owed to her, none of them collectible through any court in the land.

And there's Pip, sixteen going on ancient, who talks the way rain falls, constantly and all at once, until you realize half of what he says is armor. His village sold him to a work gang when he was twelve to cover a tax debt. Three years. He escaped. The gang's overseer did not survive the escape, though Pip tells the story differently every time and I've stopped correcting him.

Ansel listened to all of it. He had that look clerks get — the ink-stained stare, cataloguing — but underneath it, something softer. Like a man hearing music after years of silence.

Which, I'd learn later, was closer to literal than any of us liked.

"You're all running from ledgers," he said finally.

Pip snorted stew. "We're running from lots of things."

"No. Ledgers." Ansel turned his cup in his hands. "Debts the law wrote down wrong. Or wrote down and never collected. Or collected from the wrong person entirely." He looked up, and for a moment the nervous scribe was gone and someone older looked out through his face. "The Temple taught me that every account must balance. They just never said who gets to decide what counts as payment."

Brannic grunted, which from him is a standing ovation.

Maren stopped sharpening.

I told myself it was coincidence that our new hire fit so neatly into the empty space at our table. Coincidence has never once explained anything about my life.

* * *

The third night out, the singing started.

I'll describe it properly, because I've had time to get it right and I hate telling things badly. It wasn't loud. That was the worst part. A sound like someone humming behind a wall, except there was no wall and no someone — just Ansel, asleep by the fire, and a thread of melody rising off him like heat off a stone.

It moved through camp the way candlelight moves through a room. Brannic's bad knee — the one that aches before every storm — went quiet. Maren's knife, which had a nick in the blade she'd mourned for two years, gleamed suddenly whole. And the fire, which I had banked low because the nights were getting cold and wood was scarce, burned brighter. Not bigger. Brighter. Warmth without smoke, light without cost.

I put a knife to Ansel's throat and woke him up, because I am a professional.

"Don't—" he started, hands up, eyes wide and wet with sleep. "Don't stop me."

"You weren't doing anything. You were asleep."

"I know." He said it the way men confess murders. "It happens when I sleep now. It started at the valley border. It's getting stronger."

The melody faded as he spoke, and with it the fire dimmed, and Brannic made a small wounded sound in his sleep like a man losing a warm blanket.

"What did you bring out of that valley, scribe?" I asked.

"I don't know. A song. Part of one. It's — it was written into my family, Sera. Into our blood. I'm not carrying it. I *am* it." He laughed, high and unhappy. "The elders spent four generations teaching the valley to forget the old gods, and it turns out forgetting isn't the same as erasing. Somebody kept a copy."

"And the Inquisition wants the copy."

"The Inquisition wants to *burn* the copy. There's a difference between wanting something destroyed and wanting it safe, and I've read enough Inquisition correspondence to know they're very precise people."

I took my knife back. I want that noted too: I had every reason to cut my losses right there, leave him sleeping in the dirt, and ride east with a clear ledger.

Instead I said, "Does it hurt?"

He blinked. "What?"

"When it sings. Does it hurt?"

"...No. That's the problem. It feels like coming home, and I have never once trusted anything that felt like that."

Fair. I gave him first watch anyway, mostly so I could think, and partly because watching him sit up with the fire while the rest of us slept felt like the honest division of risk. He carries the thing. He stands guard over it.

* * *

By the second week, we'd stopped pretending the miracles were weather.

Small ones. Stupidly small ones. A horse's stone bruise healing overnight. Rain holding off until the tents were pitched. Once — and Pip swears to this on his mother's grave, which is complicated, because his mother's grave is also a thing Pip invented — a river crossing where the current parted just enough for Maren to keep her boots dry, and Maren, who has not smiled since I've known her, smiled like a girl for a full three seconds before catching herself.

Ansel hated it. That's the part nobody believes. Most men given gifts from heaven start building altars; ours walked twenty paces into the trees after the river incident and I followed him because I don't let my assets wander off alone, and found him gripping a sapling like it owed him money.

"It's choosing," he said, without turning around. "Do you understand? I'm not doing it. It's doing it *through* me, and it's choosing, and I don't know what it wants."

"What did it fix tonight?"

"Maren's boots." A bitter breath of a laugh. "A woman whose life was taken apart by a corrupt magistrate, and the song gives her dry feet. Either it's blind or it's cruel or it's — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Or it's paying attention to the wrong ledger entirely. Small mercies. It only knows how to give small mercies."

"Maybe that's all anyone can afford," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because he turned around and looked at me with those cataloguing eyes, and I could see him adding a page to whatever book he was keeping on the Company of Small Mercies. Yes, that's what Pip named us. Yes, it stuck. Naming things is the one magic everyone can do.

"Sera Voss," he said. "You were a soldier of the Duke's March, weren't you? Before."

"I was a lot of things before."

"You don't talk about your debt."

"Nobody here does. That's the rule. We carry them, we don't recite them."

"But the others recited theirs. For me. Around the fires." He said it gently, which was somehow worse. "They trust you. They trusted me because you didn't kill me on night three. Whatever you're carrying, captain — it balances against mine."

I left him among the trees and walked back to the fire, and Brannic handed me stew I didn't ask for, and Maren said nothing in the eloquent way she says nothing, and Pip was asleep with his mouth open and his hand curled around a wooden bird he whittles and re-whittles and will not finish, because finished things can be taken away.

My debt. Since he raised it.

There was a garrison town called Harrow Fen, and orders that came down in proper ink on proper paper, and I was young enough to believe paper made things true. We held a granary against rioters who weren't rioting, they were starving, and the order said hold it anyway, and I held it, and eleven people died in front of me reaching for bread, and afterward the magistrate who'd issued the order got a commendation and I got a conscience that wouldn't shut up.

So yes, scribe. I know exactly what kind of god answers prayers with dry boots while the big accounts sit uncollected.

I just haven't figured out yet whether I'm angry at it or grateful.

* * *

On the eighteenth night, Heartspire was visible from the ridge — a broken spire against the stars, blacker than the sky behind it — and Ansel stood looking at it for a long time without speaking.

Then he sang. Awake, this time. On purpose, or as close to purpose as he ever got with it. One line, low, barely more than breath.

Every torch in camp lit at once.

And on the road below us, in the dark, lanterns answered. Dozens of them. Moving in formation. Discipline like that only belongs to one kind of traveler.

"Inquisition," Maren said, beside me, flat as slate. "They're between us and the spire."

Ansel's song died in his throat. When he turned to me, his face had gone the color of the ash the valley elders used to burn — I know, because he'd described the burning of his manuscripts to me once, in detail, the way trauma describes itself whether you ask or not.

"They're not following me anymore," he said quietly. "They knew where I was going. They were always ahead of me." A pause, and then, smaller: "Sera — the contract. You're contracted to deliver me. Nobody said anything about delivering me *to them*."

I looked at the lanterns below, then at my ten — my strange, damaged, unbought family, already reaching for weapons without one word of instruction, because that's what we do, that's the whole company charter, unwritten, unpaid, and worth more than anything I ever signed in service of a duke.

"Amend the contract," I said, and drew my sword.
