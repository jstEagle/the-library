## 6. Ash and Ledger

The village was called Miren's Rest, which struck me as a joke the gods had been saving for years. It had one street, two wells, and a shrine to no particular deity — just a stone with a cup-shaped hollow where travelers left bread for whoever came after. Nobody asked who ate it. That was the whole theology, and it suited me fine.

We'd been there three days. Sera had traded escort work for grain and mending, because the Company of Small Mercies never took anything it couldn't pay for, even when the payment was only goodwill. The villagers knew what we were. They let us in anyway.

I should have known better than to feel safe. Safety, in my experience, is the sound the trap makes just before it closes.

It began with smoke on the eastern ridge at dawn — not hearth-smoke, which rises lazy and grey, but something blacker, moving in columns. Then riders. Then the sound I will hear until I die: not screaming first, but singing. The Inquisition always sings before it burns. A plainsong hymn about cleansing, beautiful in the way that a knife is beautiful, all clean lines and single purpose.

"They're early," Sera said, standing in the doorway of the cooper's shed with her spyglass to her eye. "Two days early. Someone talked."

"Someone always talks," said Brannoc, their tracker, already saddling horses that did not belong to us. "Question's whether they talked about us or about him."

Everyone looked at me. I felt the look like weather.

"I didn't—" I started.

"I know you didn't," Sera said. "But they're not here for the grain."

There were forty of them, maybe more, white cloaks and iron masks, and at their head rode someone small and precise, mounted on a grey mare that moved like arithmetic. Even at distance, through Brannoc's glass, I knew the way she sat a horse. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

I knew her.

* * *

Her name was Odile Marchette. We grew up in the same ledger, if you understand me — two children entered into the Temple of Quiet Hours' books the same winter, hers as an orphan tithe, mine as debt-collateral for my father's funeral costs. We learned our letters together on the same slate. She was faster. I was neater. She used to say my handwriting looked like prayer and hers looked like war, and she meant it as a compliment to both of us.

When she was twelve she held my head above water in the millrace when I slipped, and laughed at me the whole time, and told no one, because telling would have meant paperwork and paperwork meant the elders asking questions neither of us could afford.

I loved her. Not the way the ballads mean it. The other way — the way you love the person who knew you before you were anyone, who saw you copy out ten thousand prayers without once believing them, and stayed anyway.

She'd been recruited by the Inquisition at fifteen. Bright children get taken; indebted children get taken faster. I'd watched her go with something like envy, and she'd watched me stay with something like pity, and we had both been wrong about everything since.

Now she wore the iron mask, and under the mask, presumably, the same face that had pulled me from the millrace.

"You know her," Sera said. It wasn't a question. She'd watched my face do whatever it did.

"The lead inquisitor. Yes."

"Friend?"

"Worse. Family."

Sera closed the spyglass with a snap. "Then here's what happens. We get the villagers out the back way, we don't fight forty whitecloaks on open ground, and we ride. If she offers you a deal — and she will, they always offer the deal first — you listen, and then you tell me every word."

"And if I take the deal?"

Sera looked at me for a long moment. Under the exhaustion and the road dust there was something in her face I hadn't seen before, and it took me a heartbeat to name it. Not suspicion. Hurt. The specific hurt of a captain doing arithmetic on how much of her company she can afford to lose.

"Then you take it," she said finally, "and you walk down that road alone, and I hope to every god still breathing that you're a better liar than you are a scribe." She turned away. "Get the people moving. Now."

* * *

We got perhaps half of them out. Miren's Rest was old and its back paths were older, and Brannoc knew country the way I knew manuscripts — intimately, obsessively, line by line. But some of the villagers wouldn't leave. The old ones especially. They'd buried everyone they loved in that soil, and they said — each of them, separately, in words so similar it was eerie — that they'd rather burn on top of their dead than run from strangers again.

So we burned with them, or near enough.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the last thing I own outright. When the whitecloaks came up the street singing, I stood in the shrine doorway and I looked at Odile, and part of me — a part I am not proud of, a part that has been fed on twenty years of quiet resentment like a fire fed on damp straw — wanted her to see me. Wanted her to recognize me standing among the outlaws, unburned, unbroken, *chosen*, while she chose iron and ash.

That is an ugly thing to admit. I admit it anyway, because the song taught me something in the nights it leaked out of me around the campfires: everything true is also ugly somewhere, and the ugliness doesn't cancel the truth. It just keeps it honest.

She saw me. Of course she saw me. Her mare stopped twenty paces from the shrine, and the mask turned, and behind the iron I heard her voice change — drop out of the hymn's register into something older, something from the copying room, something that said *Ansel* the way you say a word you've kept in a locked drawer for years.

"Ravensworth."

"Marchette."

Even now I don't know which of us flinched first.

They had halted the burn for parley, which is Inquisition doctrine: mercy first, because mercy is cheaper than kindling. She dismounted. Walked toward me across ground that was already smoking at its edges. And I watched her do the thing I had watched her do a thousand times over slates and ledgers — assemble herself, piece by piece, into the officer, folding the girl away somewhere behind the mask where the fire couldn't reach her.

"Ansel Ravensworth," she said formally, for the record, for the scribe riding at her flank who was writing all of this down. "You are accused of heresy of the highest order. You carry a fragment of the Old Song. You are offered amnesty — full amnesty, restoration of your name, cancellation of every debt your family owes the Temple, and passage anywhere in the empire — upon surrender of the fragment and submission to examination."

Examination. I knew what that word meant. Everyone from the valley knew. It meant they would take the song out of me the way you take a splinter out of a foot, except the song wasn't in my foot, it was in my voice, my memory, my bloodline, and examination meant they would take all three and leave me a hollowed thing that used to be a scribe.

But the amnesty was real. I checked it against everything I knew of ledgers, and it balanced. Debt cancelled. Name restored. Freedom. Everything I had chafed under since I was nine years old, signed away in exchange for one secret.

Twenty years of longing, itemized on one page.

And standing there in the smoke, I found — this is the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a coin that won't sit flat — that I didn't want it anymore.

Because the debt they were offering to cancel wasn't the only debt I carried anymore. Somewhere between the millrace and the campfire, between the margins of old manuscripts and the company's rough supper table, I had incurred new obligations. To a woman who'd decided I was worth more alive than useful. To a tracker who'd shown me which plants meant water. To a girl barely older than my students who'd mended my coat sleeve without being asked and called me "scribe" like it was an endearment.

You cannot amortize a family. There's no column for it. Believe me, I've tried the arithmetic.

"No," I said.

Odile went very still. Stillness was always her weapon; she could hold it longer than anyone. "Ansel. The valley is dying. You've seen it. The silence is collapsing, and when it goes—"

"When it goes, the world gets its songs back. Yes. I've read the margins too."

"The margins lie." Her voice cracked, just slightly, just once, and I heard the twelve-year-old underneath. "Everything the old gods touch lies, Ansel. You of all people know what's written in blood stays written."

"That's why I'm not giving it to you," I said. "Whatever you'd write with it."

And then, because I am weak, because she was crying behind the mask or close to it, because twenty years is twenty years, I stepped forward and said, quietly, off the record, hoping the Inquisition's scribe had a bad memory: "Odile. Whatever they told you the song is for — ask them what *they* plan to sing with it. Ask who holds the cage door."

She looked at me for three heartbeats. Then she remounted, gave the order in the hymn-register again, and the singing started, and the burning started, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

Here is what I will not detail, because some pages deserve to stay ash: the fighting itself. Sera's company fought the way they did everything — badly matched, outnumbered, improvising, furious, magnificent. Brannoc dropped two riders before most men would have finished drawing. Sera fought like a woman settling accounts. The villagers who had stayed fought too, with tools, with stones, with the pure obstinate spite of people defending graves.

And Pip died.

Pip. Perrin Quill, youngest of the company, seventeen, who had joined at fourteen after the magistrate who ruined his family died comfortably in bed, and who had appointed himself my personal guard within a week of my capture because, he said, somebody had to keep the scribe from walking into rivers reading. He mended my coat. He stole honey cakes for the table and distributed them with the gravity of a man distributing sacraments. He asked me once, shyly, in the dark after watch, whether the old gods answered everyone or just the marked ones, and I said I didn't know, and he said, "Well, when you find out, put in a word for the rest of us," and laughed like the laugh was the whole answer.

He took a crossbow bolt meant for me. Offhand, at thirty paces, from a rider I never even saw. He was dead before he hit the ground, and I know that because Sera checked, because Sera checks everything, and she told me afterward in a voice with nothing left in it: "Instant. He didn't suffer. Write that down somewhere, will you? Write it down right."

I wrote it down right.

We broke free at dusk through the orchard rows, eleven of us riding out of a village that was a red smear on the horizon behind us, and nobody spoke for the first mile, and then Brannoc began to sing — low, tuneless, a marching song from some border war, and one by one the others joined, and I could not join because my throat had closed around a boy's name, and I rode with my hand pressed flat against my chest where the song lived, feeling it stir, feeling it *want* to rise, wanting to scream at it: not now. Not for this. Don't you dare make this beautiful.

The song does not make things beautiful. I understand that now. It only makes things *loud*, and grief needs no help being loud.

* * *

We made cold camp in a quarry two valleys over, and Sera sat apart with Pip's boots in her lap, cleaning them, though they'd never be worn again, and I understood that she was not cleaning them. She was doing what I do with ledgers. Balancing. Failing.

I came and sat near her, not too near. Company etiquette. Grief has distances the way fencing has lines.

"He believed in the song," Sera said at last. "Told me so. Said if the old gods were waking, maybe the world owed him one. Maybe somebody's magistrate got what was coming." She turned a boot over. "What do I tell his grave marker, scribe? 'Died for a debt'? Because that's what he'd say it was. He collected debts. That was the whole company charter, if you ever bothered to read it."

I hadn't. I did, that night, by firelight, the little book of company rules Pip kept — rules scrawled on a flyleaf, amended in different hands across years. Rule one: *No debt goes uncollected.* Rule two: *Collect it from whoever can pay.* Rule three, in Pip's childish letters, added at the bottom: *Even if the debtor is the world.*

I read rule three four times. Then I took out my own book, and I wrote beneath the account of the burning, beneath Odile's name, beneath the amnesty refused, one line of my own:

*Owed: one life. Debtor: the Inquisition, the Temple of Quiet Hours, and every institution that mistakes silence for peace. Collector: pending.*

It was not a prayer. I checked. It was not addressed to anyone. It was a ledger entry, and I am a scribe, and a scribe's justice is written where it cannot be erased.

But as I closed the book, deep in my chest, faint as a pulse under bandages, the fragment stirred — and this time it didn't leak out in sleep, didn't slip loose around a campfire. This time it rose toward the surface deliberately, like something that had been waiting for me to mean it.

Somewhere behind the iron mask, Odile had cried. Somewhere in the ashes, a boy's debt sat uncollected. And the song, patient as any creditor, had begun keeping its own books.

In the morning, Sera broke camp an hour before dawn and pointed us east, toward the Heartspire, and said only: "Faster now. Before they decide they don't need him talking."
