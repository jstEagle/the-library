## 7. The Song Beneath the Stone

I've been paid to guard caravans of silk, salt, and once — I swear this on my mother's unmarked grave — a cage of singing crickets bound for a minor lord with more money than sense. So when Ansel Ravensworth finally told us what we'd been escorting across half the known world, my first professional thought was: *at least it isn't crickets.*

My second thought was that I was going to kill him.

We'd made the Heartspire by dusk on the ninth day out of Ashfall. The mountain road had narrowed to a spine of black stone, and there it stood at the valley's throat — a tower broken off clean at the middle, like something enormous had snapped it in half and then thought better of finishing the job. The air around it hummed. Not sound, exactly. More like the memory of sound, the way your teeth remember biting into something cold.

The company made camp in the lee of a fallen lintel the size of a barn door. Wick started a fire. Marrow checked the perimeter twice, which meant she was nervous, which meant I should be too. And Ansel sat apart from all of it, staring up at that ruined tower like a man looking at his own gallows.

"Voss," he said. "Can I talk to you?"

"No," I said. "But you're going to anyway."

* * *

Here is what he told me, sitting by that fire with his hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking from:

The song in his head — the one that had been leaking out of him at night for weeks, the one that had mended Marrow's shoulder and ripened Wick's frostbitten fingers and made old Tam's bad knee quiet for three whole days — wasn't a blessing. It was a key. Two keys, actually, wearing each other's clothes.

Sung forward, at the Heartspire's heart, it would wake the old gods fully. All of them. The ones the Veil Valley's silence had been smothering for nine hundred years, the ones our grandmothers' grandmothers had bound there when the world got tired of being drowned in miracles.

Sung backward, it would seal them away forever. A lock instead of a door.

"And here's the part where you throw me off the mountain," Ansel said, "either choice starts the war again. Wake them, and every kingdom that ever worshipped them picks up its old grudges. Seal them, and everything that still loves them — and there's more of that than you'd think — comes to tear the binding open. Unless it's done *here.* At the exact place they were first bound. Which is why the Inquisition wants me alive, and why they'll burn anything between me and this tower."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, the broken tower hummed its toothless hum.

"You've known this how long?" I asked.

"Since before Ashfall."

"Before the village burned."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. That was answer enough.

I want to tell you I felt betrayed. I did, a little. But mostly what I felt was tired, and older than my years, and furious at myself — because I'd known. Some part of me had known since the night his singing stitched Marrow's shoulder closed and none of us thought to ask *how*. Mercenaries survive by not asking questions. It's also how mercenaries die.

"So," I said. "Which way do you sing it? Forward or back?"

"I don't know," he said, and his voice broke on it, just slightly, and I hated him a little less. "That's the truth, Voss. The song doesn't come with instructions. It comes with *weight.* Every time I sing a piece of it, I feel both doors. And I feel something behind them, listening."

* * *

I called the company together after full dark. All seven of us who were left of twelve, because Ashfall had taken five and I don't say their names casually anymore.

I laid it out flat, the way I lay out any contract. What we'd been carrying. What it could do. What it would cost the world either way. Then I did the thing captains aren't supposed to do: I told them what I thought, and I let them see me thinking it.

"The Inquisition will be here within two days. Maybe less. They've got forty lances, fire-priests, and a lead inquisitor who grew up with our scribe. We are seven people with mismatched armor and a payroll that exists mainly in my optimism." I looked around the fire. "So. Are we escorts or executioners? Because those are the only two jobs on offer now. We walk him into that tower and let him choose, or we put a blade in him tonight and sell the Inquisition his body for enough coin to retire on. Nobody would blame us. I wouldn't even blame us."

Silence. Wick poked the fire. Tam's knee clicked when he shifted, an old habit of an old wound.

It was Marrow who spoke first, which surprised no one who knew her and would surprise everyone else. Marrow, who'd been a slave in the salt mines until her mid-twenties, who trusted nothing with a pulse.

"When my shoulder healed," she said slowly, "I dreamed for the first time since I was a girl. Not nightmares. Just — dreams. Ordinary ones. Doors and rain and my mother's kitchen." She looked at Ansel. "Whatever's in you gave me back something the law never did. That's worth more than Inquisition coin."

"They'll hunt us forever," Tam said. "Every company, every bounty board from here to the coast."

"We're already hunted," said Wick, who was nineteen and had been hunted since she was fourteen. "Might as well be hunted for something."

Piotr said nothing. Piotr never says anything. But he took off his boot knife — the one he sleeps holding, the one we all know better than to touch — and set it down in front of Ansel. Handle toward him. In Piotr's language, that was a speech.

Seven votes for escort. Zero for executioner. I'd have been lying if I said my chest didn't do something embarrassing about it.

"All right," I said. "Then hear my terms, scribe. Tomorrow you walk into that tower, and you make your choice, and whatever you choose — you choose it *yourself.* Not the gods. Not the song. Not the weight. You. If I catch even a whiff that something else is driving your mouth, I'll knock you out and drag you back down this mountain, war or no war. Understood?"

"Understood," Ansel said. And then, because he's Ansel: "Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me if we live."

* * *

I couldn't sleep. Big surprise.

I found Ansel at the edge of camp, sitting where the fallen lintel met the mountainside, looking up at the Heartspire. The moon had come out from behind cloud, and the broken tower caught it and threw it back wrong — bent, shimmering, like light through water.

"Can't sleep either?" he asked.

"I sleep fine. I'm brooding. There's a difference. Brooding builds character."

He almost smiled. Then it fell off his face. "Ask me, Sera. Whatever you've been chewing on since the fire. Ask."

Fine. I asked. "When you're standing in there tomorrow, and the song is pulling at you — how do I know you'll pick *us?* Not the gods. Not the world, even. Us. Seven people you've known for two months."

He was quiet a long time. Long enough that I counted his breaths. Nine of them.

"Because the gods offered me purpose," he said finally. "And the Temple offered me absolution. And the Inquisition offered me amnesty. And every single one of them wanted to spend me like coin." He turned and looked at me, and in the dark his eyes were just eyes, tired and human and scared. "You people fed me first. Before you knew what I was. Wick gave me her spare blanket when I was shivering and didn't ask why. Marrow taught me to gut a fish. Piotr — I think Piotr likes me? It's hard to tell. My point is—" His voice wobbled. "Nobody ever asked what I could *do* for them before they decided I was worth keeping. Except you. And you asked, and then you kept me anyway."

"That's terrible negotiating," I said. "You should have led with that at the fire."

"Next apocalypse," he said, "I'll take notes."

I sat down next to him on the cold stone. Below us, the valley spread out black and silent, magic-dead, the prison the whole world had agreed to forget. Above us, the broken tower hummed its half-remembered hymn. And somewhere down that mountain road, I'd have bet my sword arm, forty lances and a woman who used to share a childhood with our scribe were climbing through the dark with fire in their pockets.

"Sera?" Ansel said quietly. "If I sing it backward — seal them forever — do you think the silence comes back? For everyone?"

"I think," I said, "that tomorrow you're going to find a third option, because you're annoyingly good at that. And I think if you don't, we'll handle what comes. That's the job. That's always been the job."

"And if handling it kills you?"

"Then haunt me efficiently. I hate chatty ghosts."

He laughed — a real laugh, short and startled out of him — and for a moment the humming of the tower seemed to bend toward it, listening. Listening, and maybe learning.

That should have frightened me more than it did.

What frightened me came an hour later, when Wick shook me awake with a face white as bone and pointed down the mountain, where the darkness below the switchbacks had begun to glow — dozens of small fires, moving in formation, patient as a noose.

They hadn't waited two days.

They'd marched through the night.

"Inquisitors," Wick whispered. "A lot of them. And Captain — there's someone walking at the front with no torch at all. Just... light following her."

I reached for my sword and looked at Ansel, already awake, already standing, already staring down at the fires with an expression I couldn't read.

"Well," I said. "Hope you took those notes."
