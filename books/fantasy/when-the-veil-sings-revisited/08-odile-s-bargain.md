## 8. Odile's Bargain

I have burned a hundred villages in the name of the Quiet, and I remember every one.

That is the part no one tells you about the Inquisition. They tell you about the discipline, the white cloaks, the certainty. They do not tell you that the discipline exists precisely because certainty is a lie we rehearse nightly, like actors who've forgotten their lines and must paint them on each morning before the audience wakes. You burn a village, you write its name in your ledger, and then you lie awake listening to the ledger read itself back to you in a child's voice until dawn.

Ansel Ravensworth was in my ledger. Had been for eleven days. *Fugitive scribe, Temple of Quiet Hours, carrier of the Song.* Under his name I had written the standard notation: *apprehend by any means.*

By any means. Three words that had once meant nothing more to me than ink. Now they meant the ash of Millbrook, where a boy named Tam had died holding a frying pan against armored men because someone had told him mercenaries were honorable and Inquisitors were not. He'd been half right. We were neither.

And now I stood at the mouth of a mountain pass at dawn, with four hundred soldiers behind me and a fragment of divine song somewhere above me, and the light — yes, the men said light followed me now, and they whispered about it in the ranks as though it made them holy rather than afraid — the light did nothing for me except make it impossible to hide from myself.

Because I knew him. That was the rot at the center of it, the splinter under the nail. Ansel Ravensworth had been the boy who sat beside me in the temple school and let me copy his letters when my hand cramped. Who gave me half his bread during the famine winter and told our instructor it had fallen into his satchel by accident, a miracle of clumsiness. Who cried — quietly, in the way the valley taught us, soundless as everything else — when his mother was taken for debt, and who never once blamed the gods or the temple or the world. He just wrote. He wrote prayers for people who couldn't afford them, beautiful ones, and he charged them nothing even though it cost him hours he owed to the Temple himself.

The valley taught us silence. Ansel had always heard something in it anyway. I should have known, all those years ago, that a boy who listened that hard would eventually be answered.

"Domina." Captain Rell, my second, at my shoulder. "Scouts report the mercenary company is dug in at the old shrine, maybe forty souls. They'll fight."

"They'll fight," I agreed.

"And the scribe?"

I looked up the switchbacks, at the thin thread of smoke from a dead fire. "He'll surrender."

Rell blinked. "You believe that?"

"I believe," I said carefully, "that Ansel Ravensworth has never fought anyone in his life. I believe he will come down if I ask him to, because he is decent, and decency is a lever that has never once failed me." I heard my own voice and hated how much it sounded like Mother Superior's. Like the voice that had recruited me at fourteen, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, saying *you have such clarity, Odile, such clarity* — and meaning, though I didn't know it yet, *you will be useful, and useful people don't get to flinch.*

"Prepare the parley flag," I said. "I go up alone."

Rell's face did the thing faces did around me lately: the flicker of doubt, quickly buried. My soldiers were starting to notice that the light following their Domina flickered too, sometimes, when she lied. The gods' silence was failing everywhere now. Little miracles leaked through the cracks like water through a dam — candles relighting themselves, dying men briefly lucid, and inquisitors trailed by unasked-for halos. The Church called it contamination. I was beginning to suspect it was testimony.

* * *

They let me come up with my hands empty, which was more courtesy than I deserved, and Sera Voss met me ten paces from the shrine with a sword that she did not draw but very much wanted to. Behind her, her company watched from the rocks: a giant with a farmer's face, a scarred woman with an axe, a red-haired boy too young to hold the line he stood on. Outcasts, the lot of them. Debts the law never collected. And there, at the edge of the firelight —

There he was.

Eleven years older and twenty pounds thinner, but it was Ansel. Same ink-stained fingers. Same way of standing as though he were apologizing to the ground for weighing on it.

"You look well," I said, which was a lie, and felt the light behind me gutter.

"Odile." Just my name. He said it the way you'd say the name of a house that had burned down — grief and fondness in the same breath. "You brought four hundred people."

"The Church prefers enthusiasm."

Voss snorted. "The Church prefers hostages. What's the offer, Inquisitor? Amnesty for the scribe, heads for the rest of us?"

"Amnesty for everyone," I said. "That's real. I have the authority." It was true. It was also worthless, and I watched Voss know it, watch her weigh the word *amnesty* against the smoking memory of Millbrook, and find it lighter than ash.

"But?" Ansel said softly. He had always heard the sentence underneath the sentence.

"But," I said, and the light behind me dimmed as I spoke, and every soldier of mine would have known what that meant, and none of these outlaws did, and somehow that made it easier, "the amnesty requires surrender of the Song. To be sealed. Permanently."

Silence. Mountain wind. Somewhere below, a hawk crying over the tree line, and I thought: *even the hawks are louder than the gods ever were, and no one ever built a church to the hawk.*

"That's the official version," Ansel said.

It wasn't a question. He'd found the words in margins again; I could see it in his face. Eleven years of reading everything twice.

I am going to record here, in whatever account survives this day, the exact moment I broke my oath. It was not dramatic. There was no thunderclap. I simply looked at Ansel Ravensworth, at the boy who had given away bread he didn't have, and I asked myself the question the Inquisition trains you never to ask: *what if we are the thing that needs to be stopped?*

And the answer came back in a child's voice, from a village ledger, and I could not live inside my own cloak another hour.

"Come with me," I said. "Away from them." A glance at Voss — *no offense intended* — and she returned it with the flat stare of a woman deciding whether to gut me later or sooner. "Both of you, if she likes. Ten paces. What I say next ends my life if it reaches the wrong ears."

We walked. The company watched. The mountain held its breath.

"The Church is not sealing the gods," I said. "Sealing requires the Heartspire's binding-stone intact, and the binding-stone was shattered two hundred years ago on orders from the first Mother Superior. The rite they've ordered me to perform — the one they've given me the relics for, the censers, the chains, the choir-iron—" My voice cracked and I let it. Let him hear it crack. "It doesn't close the door, Ansel. It builds a cage. It takes the gods' song and holds it, compresses it, makes it *usable*. A weapon. The Inquisition means to wake enough of the old divinity to arm it, and point it at whoever the Synod fears next. Kingdoms. Heretics. Anyone. They call the project the *Hymnal*."

Ansel had gone very pale. "Caged gods."

"Gods in a bottle. And the cork pulled whenever convenient." I reached into my coat and drew out the thing I had carried against my heart for three weeks, wrapped in choir-silk, warm as a living thing, which in a sense it was. "This is the Keystone Fragment. Half the key to the binding place. The other half is in your bloodline — your grandmother didn't flee the valley, Ansel, she was *sent out*, seeded, a failsafe. The Temple has always known. Your whole life has been a lock waiting for its other half."

He took it the way you take a wounded bird. His hands shook. Behind us, Voss muttered something profane and theological.

"Why?" Ansel whispered. "Why give this to me? Why turn?"

I thought about telling him it was conscience. It was. But he deserved the uglier truth too.

"Because I burned Millbrook," I said, "and I wrote Tam's name in my ledger, and last night I dreamed he was copying letters for me, the way you used to, and my hand cramped, and he laughed at me. Because the light follows me now and I think — I think the old gods know what the Church intends, and they're marking the inquisitors who might still choose otherwise." I looked at my own hands, clean, always so clean. "Or perhaps I'm just tired. Perhaps treason looks exactly like exhaustion. Either way: the Synod expects me to bring them the Song in chains. Instead I'm handing the last key to a debtor scribe and asking him to do what neither waking nor cating can — end it properly, at the Heartspire, before my masters arrive. Which gives us—"

I calculated. Dawn had come late; the column moved slow in the pass.

"—perhaps six hours."

"And when they learn what you've done?" Voss asked, arriving beside us with the unhurried menace of weather.

"Then I am excommunicated, stripped, and most likely hanged," I said. "If I'm lucky." I unbuckled my sword and laid it on the stone between us, then the white cloak over it, folding the light in with it like a bird in a box. The halo guttered and went out. Gods, it was quiet without it. Quiet in the old way. Quiet like home. "My career, my freedom, my name — all of it, spent. On one condition."

Ansel waited.

"When you reach the Heartspire — whatever you choose, wake them, seal them, unmake them — *write it down*. Plain language, copies scattered wide, so no one can ever cage the truth of it again. You're the scribe, Ansel. Be the scribe. Make the secret impossible to keep."

He stared at me. And then he did the thing he'd done across the schoolroom bench twenty years ago: he smiled, small and crooked, as if I'd handed him back half a loaf of bread.

"I can do that," he said.

Below us, a horn sounded — wrong rhythm, wrong pitch, not my signal. Rell's voice carrying up the switchbacks, tight with alarm: scouts returning at a run, and behind them, far down the pass, a second column. White cloaks, but not mine. Not anyone's. The Synod had sent watchers after the watchers, and they had seen the parley, and they were coming up the mountain with choir-iron already singing.

Voss drew her sword at last, and it sounded almost cheerful.

"Well," she said, echoing a certain captain's habit I was beginning to understand ran through her whole damned company, "hope you can fight, Inquisitor."
