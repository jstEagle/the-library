## 9. Where the Veil Sings

I had imagined the end of my life many times — usually while copying the same psalm for the four hundredth time, because a scribe's imagination is the one thing the Temple of Quiet Hours never taxed. In most versions I died old, in a chair, mid-word. A few versions involved fire. None of them involved standing in the beating heart of a dead god's prison with an army of inquisitors at my back and a song inside me that was trying to claw its way out through my ribs.

Yet here we were.

The heart of the Heartspire was not what the hymns promised. There was no throne, no altar, no towering idol with jeweled eyes. There was only a chamber like the inside of a bell — curved stone rising into darkness, walls scored with grooves so deep and regular they might have been cut by rain over ten thousand years, except that rain does not sing back when you hum at it.

And it did sing back. Faintly. The whole chamber answered the fragment in my chest like a tuning fork finding its twin, and every note it returned cost me something I couldn't name yet but would soon enough.

"Ansel." Sera Voss stood at my shoulder, sword drawn, which in Sera's language was roughly equivalent to holding my hand. "Tell me you have a plan that ends with all of us alive."

"I have a plan," I said. "It's just short on that particular ending."

"Wonderful. I knew I liked you."

Behind us, boots on stone. Many boots. The Inquisition had found the pilgrim stair faster than Odile's warning said they would, which meant either she'd been wrong or they hadn't trusted her to lead it. Given what her own letter had confessed about the Order's true intentions — *not sealing them, Ansel. Caging them. Making weapons of them* — I suspected the latter. They'd sent someone who wouldn't hesitate.

The song surged again, and I tasted copper. It had been pulling at me for three days now, growing louder as we climbed, threading itself into my heartbeat until I could no longer tell which rhythm was mine. Waking or caging, waking or caging — those were the two doors it offered, over and over, patient as interest accruing on a debt.

I had spent eleven years as property of the Temple of Quiet Hours. I knew a debt when I felt one. And I had learned something else in eleven years of copying other people's prayers: every contract has a third option nobody writes down, because the party holding the pen prefers you not to look for it.

"You're doing your thinking face," Sera said. "I hate your thinking face. Your thinking face costs me men."

"This one might cost me everything," I said. "But nobody else."

"Liar," said a voice behind us — Bram, the company's graying quartermaster, limping up with his arm in a sling from the fight at the stairhead. "You'll cost us grief either way. You always do. Question is whether it's worth grieving."

"It is," I said, and found, to my surprise, that I meant it without any hedging at all.

* * *

They came down into the bell-chamber in white and ash-gray, forty of them at least, lanterns raised, and at their center walked a woman I had known since we were children stealing plums from the Temple orchard. Odile Marchand, First Cantor of the Holy Inquisition, in full regalia, her face arranged into official sorrow.

She stopped twenty paces away. Behind her, crossbows leveled at my family.

"Ansel," she said gently. "It doesn't have to be this way. Give us the song. Wake them or bind them — the choice can still be yours. That's mercy. That's more mercy than you'll get otherwise."

"The choice was never going to be mine," I said. "Your Order wrote both endings before I was born. Wake them and rule beside them; cage them and rule above them. Either way, someone holds the leash. It was never going to be the valley. It was never going to be the people who prayed and got silence for their trouble."

"You don't know what's down here. You don't know what they were—"

"I know exactly what they were." And I did, suddenly, completely, the knowledge arriving the way the handwriting had once arrived in my margins — not learned but remembered, surfacing through blood older than the valley. "They were singers who got tired of sharing the song. So they bound it into themselves and made everyone else listen quietly. The Veil wasn't protection, Odile. It was a gag."

Her jaw tightened. "Blasphemy."

"Yes," I agreed. "Probably. Most true things are, in my experience."

"Last chance," she said, and there — there, under the cantor's steel — was the girl who'd once split a stolen plum with me and lied to the elders about it without blinking. Her eyes flicked to the far archway. *The side gallery,* the gesture said. *When it starts, run.* "Surrender the fragment. Live."

"Odile," I said softly. "Thank you. But no."

I turned to the wall of the great bell, laid my palm against the stone, and began to unmake myself.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about divine songs: they are contracts too. Every verse a covenant, every refrain a signature. The old gods had written theirs in the bedrock of the world, binding the music of creation into their own throats so that nothing else could ever hold a note. The Veil Valley was simply where they'd filed the original copy.

Waking them meant countersigning — giving the fragment back, letting them speak whole again. Caging them meant amending the contract, transferring custody to new hands. Both options kept the document alive. Both kept the gods *owed*, and I had spent my entire adult life learning what it means to be owed.

But every contract can be sung backward. My mother taught me that — not in words, she never got the words out, but in the way she'd hum a lullaby in reverse when I had nightmares, unwinding the fear note by note until it was only sound again, harmless, home.

So I took the fragment in my chest, the piece of divine song that had ridden my bloodline like a debt across generations, and I sang it backward.

The chamber screamed. Not the stone — the *song* did, the forward version of itself, furious at being unwound, and it fought me the way a drowning man fights his rescuer. It poured memories into me to make me stop: my mother's face, Sera laughing at a campfire, Bram's terrible stew, Odile at nine years old with plum juice on her chin. It offered me the world. It offered me my voice forever, a throat that could part seas.

And it took things in payment as I refused. Each verse I reversed, it collected. Warmth went first — I felt it drain out of my hands like water through fingers. Then color, draining from the lantern light until the whole gray chamber looked like one of my old manuscripts. Then —

"ANSEL!" Sera had reached me, had her hand on my shoulder, and I felt her grip as though from very far away. "Whatever you're doing — keep doing it! We'll hold the line!"

Crossbows sang. Steel rang against steel. Somewhere Bram was roaring orders, somewhere the company of small mercies was spending itself to buy me the seconds I needed, and I could not turn around, could not let myself see which of them fell, because if I saw I would stop, and if I stopped the song would win and everything they paid would buy nothing at all.

Verse by verse, I unmade the secret.

The last line was the oldest — the one that named the singers, that gave them claim over every throat in the world. To reverse it, I had to give it something equal to what it held. It wanted a voice. Mine, specifically, the bloodline's portion, the inheritance I'd never asked for.

I thought about my mother, who had died mute rather than surrender hers to the Temple's ledger. I thought about eleven years of copying prayers for gods who never answered. I thought about how, in the end, the only prayers that ever mattered were whispered between people — Sera's rough grace before battle, Bram counting names aloud each night so none of the dead would be forgotten.

Those needed no god's acoustics. They just needed mouths willing to speak.

"Take it," I said — the last words I would ever say, and I chose them carefully, the way a scribe chooses a final line. "Take the voice. Keep the silence. But let it belong to everyone."

I sang the name backward.

The world went quiet — not the dead silence of the Veil, not the gag-silence of the binding, but the clean hush after the last note of a lullaby, when the child sleeps and the singer sits in the dark, satisfied. The pressure in my chest dissolved. The debt, eleven years and eleven generations of it, simply *closed*, the ledger balanced, the ink dry.

Then I was on the floor, and the floor was warm, and someone was shaking me, and I opened my mouth to say I was fine.

Nothing came out.

* * *

I want to report that I felt peace. Mostly I felt cold, and deaf to anything but my own pulse, and profoundly aware that I would never again say my own name aloud. Grief came later, and acceptance later than that. What came first, as I lay in the settling dust of an unmade god-contract, was a hand hauling me upright — Sera's, iron and certain — and her mouth moving fast, saying words I couldn't hear yet, her eyes asking a question.

I touched my throat. Shook my head. Touched my heart, then pointed at her, at the chaos of the company re-forming around us, battered, bleeding, fewer than we'd been at dawn.

*I'm fine. Are you?*

Sera read it off my hands the way she read everything — terrain, lies, weather coming. She barked a laugh I felt more than heard, hauled my arm over her shoulders, and half-carried me toward the archway Odile had marked, where the First Cantor herself stood with her white cloak torn and her insignia already ripped away, waiting for us like a woman who had finally decided whose side her conscience lived on.

The chamber behind us was empty now. No gods waking, no gods caged. Just a bell-shaped room, silent at last in the way silence should be — not a prison, not an absence. Just room to hear yourself think.

Or, in my case, to write it down.

Because even voiceless, coughing dust, leaning on a mercenary captain who'd started the week wanting to ransom me, I was already composing the opening line. Old habits. The only inheritance I had left.

Somewhere above us, the Veil Valley was breathing differently — softer, freer — and somewhere below, the Inquisition was discovering their weapon had dissolved in the hands of a debtor with a stolen plum's worth of stubbornness.

There would be consequences. There were always consequences; I'd copied enough confessions to know that much about the world.

But that was tomorrow's chapter.

Tonight, for the first time in eleven years, nobody owned me — and I intended to spend my first hours of freedom exactly the way I pleased: taking dictation from the loudest hearts I knew.
