## 10. Quiet Hours, Loud Hearts

I've stood on a lot of battlefields in my life, and I can tell you the sound they make when it's over. It's never silence. There's always something — a man groaning, a horse screaming, boots in mud, somebody laughing wrong because laughing right isn't possible yet.

The morning after Ansel unmade the gods, the Veil Valley made actual silence. Not the dead kind we'd all grown up fearing. The listening kind. Like the world had put down its weapons too and was sitting on a wall with us, sharing water.

We camped on the ridge above the Heartspire because nobody wanted to sleep inside that place, and I don't blame them. Below us the valley spread out green and gray in the early light, and for the first time in recorded history there was nothing humming underneath it. No pressure. No held breath. Just land.

Ansel sat apart from the fire, wrapped in a blanket, hands around a cup of tea he wasn't drinking. He hadn't spoken since the spire. Of course he hadn't. He'd poured his voice into the binding like wine into sand, sung the whole secret backward until there was no song left in him to sing and no throat left to sing it with. The healers among us — which is to say Petra, who knows herbs and lies about her confidence — said the damage was permanent. Whatever had lived in his voice was spent. What remained was just a man-shaped quiet.

I sat down next to him because someone had to, and because if I'm honest, out of everyone in this ragged collection of criminals I call a company, he's the one I'd have missed most. Don't tell him that either. He can't hear it anyway.

"You did it," I said. "In case you were wondering. You actually did the stupid thing you set out to do."

He looked at me. His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile but was clearly related to one, the way a hill is related to a mountain.

"I mean it," I went on, because talking at people who can't interrupt is one of life's underrated pleasures. "Every plan I've ever had ended in blood or money, usually both. You ended one in *quiet*. Do you understand how offensive that is to my professional sensibilities?"

He tapped his cup against mine. A toast. Then he reached into his satchel and pulled out his ledger — the same one he'd been scribbling in since the day we fished him out of a ravine with an Inquisition bounty on his head — and wrote:

*Thank you. For stopping me the first three times I tried to go back in.*

"Four times," I said. "You're forgetting the bit with the rope."

*That was you going back in. I was holding the rope.*

"Semantics." I took the charcoal stub from him before he could write more. "Here's what happens now. We walk north, slow, because half the company is held together with bandages and spite. You come with us. You're free of the Temple debt — turns out your creditors are dissolved along with their gods, which is the only debt settlement I've ever seen work cleanly. So you owe nobody. Which means for the first time in your careful little scribe life, you get to choose where you sleep at night."

His eyes had gone bright in that way that makes big men want to look away and pretend to check their gear. I handed back the charcoal.

He wrote: *You're offering me a job.*

"I'm telling you the job already exists. Company chronicler. Someone has to write down what we do so that when the law finally catches up with us, there's an accurate record of our crimes. Also so that Wren—" My voice caught on the name, the way it still does, probably always will. "So that what happened to Wren gets remembered properly. Not as a casualty number. As a person."

He looked at the page a long moment. Then he wrote, in his neat temple-trained hand that I'd once threatened to break all ten fingers over:

*I accept. On one condition. You let me write the truth about you too. All of it. Even the parts you'll hate.*

I should have said no. A captain who lets her own chronicler see everything is a captain asking for a knife between the shoulder blades.

"Deal," I said.

Because here's the thing I learned somewhere between the burning village and the Heartspire's door: a family that only sees your good side isn't a family. It's an audience. And I've had enough audiences for one lifetime.

* * *

Spring came up the valley like a rumor spreading through a garrison. One week the hills were brown, the next they were that impossible green that makes even mercenaries go quiet, and we went quiet a lot those days, because the valley itself seemed to be practicing being gentle.

The Temple of Quiet Hours still stands. Half its elders fled when the gods' claim dissolved; the rest stayed and, according to travelers we passed on the road, have started doing something radical — feeding pilgrims without charging them. Debt-freedom turns out to be contagious.

Magic crept back into the lowlands too, thin and shy, like a cat returning after you've moved house. Petra swears she saw a hedge-witch charm a well in Millbrook. Tamir swears he won dice against a man whose luck ran visibly hot. I believe neither of them, but I've stopped minding. A world with small wonders in it is a world worth escorting caravans through.

We took jobs. Honest ones, mostly. Guarding grain shipments, walking pilgrims safe past the old war-roads, one memorable month chasing off bandits who turned out to be starving farmers, whom Ansel — voiceless, furious, writing so hard his charcoal snapped — shamed us into paying out of company funds instead of hanging. That entry in the chronicle is four pages long and ends with the sentence: *Sera grumbled, but she paid.* Accurate. I hate him.

At night he writes by firelight, and the company arranges itself around him without anyone deciding to. Ferra braids her hair and listens. Old Hask, who claims he can't read, sits closest of anyone and stares at the pages like they might teach him out of sheer proximity. When Ansel finishes a chapter, he passes it around, and people point at lines and argue and laugh, and sometimes cry, and nobody calls it anything as soft as story time because we're professionals.

But that's what it is.

* * *

The letter came in autumn, carried by a one-eyed courier who charged us double and earned it. Odile's seal — not the Inquisition's sunburst anymore, just her own initial, pressed plain into gray wax.

She'd been stripped of rank, formally censured, and exiled from the capital for "unauthorized disclosure of operational doctrine." Which is Inquisition language for: she told the truth about the weapon-cages, and it cost her everything, and she did it anyway. Her letter was short. Most of it was about the weather in her exile town, which apparently has excellent bread. At the bottom, one line:

*They're learning new prayers in the villages. Small ones. Thanks for rain, for healed livestock, for safe roads. No gods answering — just people, saying thank you to each other. I think that was your plan all along. Tell the scribe his handwriting is better than his singing ever was.*

I read it aloud at the fire while Ansel watched my mouth move, and when I got to the last line he laughed — a rough, cracked, broken-instrument sound, the first noise he'd made since the spire, and every single person around that fire pretended very hard not to react because we love him and we're not monsters.

Then he took his ledger and wrote for a long time. This is what he wrote. I know because I checked, later, when he was asleep, which is exactly the sort of thing a family does:

* * *

I dreamed of singing again last night. In the dream I had my whole voice back, and I stood at the Heartspire's heart, and the old gods came to me wearing the faces of everyone I'd ever owed or been owed by, and they offered me the choice one more time — wake us, cage us, carry us, rule beside us — and in the dream I sang the unbinding again, and again, and each time it cost the same terrible price, and each time I paid it gladly, and Sera stood behind me with her arms crossed saying *about time,* which is somehow the most comforting thing I have ever heard.

I woke with salt on my face and no voice, same as every morning now. Same as every morning forever, probably. And here is the strange thing I keep having to learn again: it doesn't ache the way I thought it would. Losing the song felt like losing myself, back when the Temple owned my voice and called it wages. But this — this quiet — I chose it. There is all the difference in the world between a debt and a gift, even when they cost the same.

This morning Ferra taught Hask his letters. She used the chronicle as a primer, which means the first full sentence that man ever read was about himself, and he wept into his porridge and swore us all to secrecy and we immediately told everyone. Tamir is teaching Wren's younger brother the drill yard, because Wren's mother sent the boy to us with a letter that said *he belongs where she belonged,* and I cried reading it, silently, the way I do everything now. Petra has started calling herself a miracle-worker ironically, which is the only honest kind.

Odile's letter sits under my inkpot. New prayers, she says. People thanking people. No gods needed, no gods listening, and somehow the prayers land anyway — in bread shared, in roads walked together, in a mercenary company that keeps a ledger not of debts but of names.

Tonight Sera asked me what I'd write next, once the chronicle of the Veil Valley is finished. I wrote my answer and showed it to her, and she snorted, and kissed my forehead like an infuriating older sister, and said fine, but she gets editorial approval.

So here is the last line of the old book, and the first promise of the new one:

*The silence in this valley was never empty. It was waiting to find out what we would fill it with. We filled it with each other — and it was enough, and more than enough, and tomorrow we march north, and I will write down every loud, unruly, miraculous heart that walks beside mine.*
