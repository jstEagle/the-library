## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The pass called the Widow's Teeth had killed better guides than Vesper Halloran, and it knew it. The mountain wore its arrogance in ice: ridgelines like bared incisors, scree slopes that shifted their minds between one step and the next, and a wind that came down from the high snows with the particular malice of something that had outlived everyone who ever cursed it.

Vesper walked point, as she always did, three lengths ahead of the mule train, and let the wind try its worst.

"Ves." Tam's voice carried up the line, pitched to cut through the gale without carrying to anything else that might be listening. That was a skill too—knowing how loud a voice could be before the mountains overheard. "Marta's mule is doing the thing again."

"The thing."

"You know. The philosophical thing. Standing very still and questioning whether existence is worth the effort."

Vesper glanced back. Sure enough, the lead mule of the second string had planted all four hooves on a shelf of black stone and was regarding the drop-off to its left with the flat, ancient patience of an animal that had decided death might be preferable to another switchback. Marta, a broad-shouldered woman with frost in her eyebrows, tugged the lead rope with the weary resignation of someone who had argued with mules before and expected to lose.

"Tell her," Vesper called back, "that if the mule jumps, Marta gets its load, and Marta's load includes the brandy, and I will drink the brandy over its grave."

"That's not a comfort, Ves."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be leverage."

Tam laughed—that easy, unguarded laugh he'd had since they were children stealing apples off the temple carts in Greyhollow—and the sound did what it always did, which was make the cold feel less like an enemy and more like weather. He scrambled up the line with the loose-limbed grace of a man who'd spent half his life on goat tracks, took the rope from Marta, leaned his forehead briefly against the mule's long gray face, and murmured something to it. Whatever it was, the mule sighed through its nostrils and moved.

That was Tam's whole gift in a single gesture. Vesper could read a slope, read a sky, read the lie in a toll-keeper's smile. Tam could read everything with a heartbeat. The company said he'd been kissed by some small warm god of beasts and lost causes. Vesper knew better. There were no small gods left in Thornfell. There was barely one large one, and it had stopped speaking to her three years ago.

She turned back to the trail before her face could show anything.

* * *

They made the high camp at dusk, in the lee of a shrine so old no one remembered which god it had been built for. It was just a hollowed stone now, wind-carved into something like a cupped hand, and travelers still left offerings in the palm: a copper bit, a twist of dried herbs, once—memorably—a whole smoked trout, which had caused a diplomatic incident with a marten.

The caravan settled around fires built low and shielded, because light on the Widow's Teeth at night was an invitation. Six souls besides Vesper and Tam: Marta; the Ferro twins, who handled the cargo and asked no questions about it, which was why they got the work; old Coss, who drove the mules and claimed to have guided this pass before the border wards went up, which would have made him a hundred and ten and nobody argued, because Coss told that story differently every time and it was better each telling; a quiet wool merchant named Pell who was almost certainly not a wool merchant; and young Ivet, seventeen, on her first run, who watched Vesper the way people watched weather coming over a ridge.

Vesper ate last, as she always did, walking the perimeter while the others ate. Old habit. Old oath.

When she finally sat, Tam slid her a bowl without being asked—barley and salt pork and a suspicious amount of Marta's pepper—and settled beside her close enough that their shoulders touched. Warmth by contact. Neither of them commented on it. They'd stopped commenting years ago.

"Long day," Tam said.

"Mm."

"You've got your thinking face on."

"This is my face."

"Your thinking face has more forehead in it." He nudged her bowl with his own. "You've checked the north ridge four times since we stopped. There's nothing on the north ridge."

"There's never anything on the north ridge until there is."

"And you've got your sleeve pulled down past your wrist. Inside a sheltered camp. By a fire." His voice stayed light, but only on the surface, the way a frozen lake stays solid right up until it doesn't. "Cold, are you?"

"Freezing."

"Ves."

"I'm fine, Tam."

He let it go, the way he always did, and she hated him a little for the kindness of it, and loved him for exactly the same reason. He talked instead about nothing—the Ferro twins' ongoing feud over a debt of four coppers, Coss's latest version of the ward-raising story (in this one, the Emperor himself had climbed the Teeth barefoot), Ivet's heroic attempt to eat an entire wheel of hard cheese as a demonstration of maturity. Vesper listened, and answered when answering was wanted, and felt the evening do what evenings were supposed to do: settle, soften, become survivable.

It should have been enough. Most nights it was.

But when the fires burned down to coals and the others rolled into their bedrolls, and Tam's breathing went slow and even across the fire from hers, Vesper did what she did every night, and had done every night for three years.

She pushed back her left sleeve.

The mark sat on the inside of her forearm, from wrist to elbow: a line of script older than any alphabet in use, ink-dark, faintly raised, like a scar that had been written rather than earned. To anyone who saw it—and few did—it was a smuggler's tattoo, or a punishment brand, or a foreign curse. Vesper had a different lie ready for every asker. *Tally of debts. Mark of a dead cult. My mother's handwriting.* People believed what they wanted about other people's skin.

It was the oath-mark of the Veil, god of thresholds and crossings, keeper of the silence between words. Her god. Sworn to her at fourteen, marked into her at fifteen, singing to her every night of her life after that—a sound that wasn't sound, a warmth under the skin, a presence at the edge of sleep like someone sitting watch beside her fire. Every crossing she made in safety was a prayer answered. Every prayer she made was answered with that low, wordless hum, the way a mother answers a child's nightmare without waking fully: *here, here, here.*

Three years ago, on a night she could date to the hour, it had stopped.

No warning. No fading. Just silence, absolute and total, like a door closing in a house she'd lived in all her life. She'd thought, at first, that she'd done something—broken some term of the oath, crossed some line. She'd fasted. She'd retraced her steps. She'd gone back to the shrine where she'd sworn and knelt on the freezing stone until her knees went numb and her pride went with them.

Nothing. Three years of nothing.

She pressed two fingers to the mark now, the way she did every night, and felt what she always felt: nothing. Dead skin. A scar pretending to be a scar.

*Veil,* she prayed anyway, silently, in the dark behind her eyes where no one—not even Tam, especially not Tam—could hear. *I don't know where you went. I don't know what I did. But the passes are getting stranger, and the tolls are getting harder, and I'm tired of talking to you like a stone. If you're there—if any part of you is anywhere—*

She waited, out of habit, for the hum.

The wind moved through the old shrine's cupped stone and made a thin, hollow note, like breath over a bottle's mouth. Just wind. It was always just wind.

"—and if you're not," she finished aloud, barely above a whisper, "then I'm talking to myself, and I'd appreciate it if my own soul had the decency to answer."

Across the fire, Tam stirred in his sleep and murmured something. Vesper pulled her sleeve down and lay back on the frozen ground and did not sleep for a long time.

* * *

They came down off the high Teeth on the third morning, into the grey shoulder of the mountain where the pines started and the air thickened enough to taste. The cargo was safe, the clients would be paid, and by tomorrow night they'd be drinking in Greyhollow with money in their purses and lies in their mouths about how easy it had all been.

It was, Vesper would think later, the last good morning for a long time. But that morning it was only a good morning, and she spent it the way she liked best: ahead of the line, reading the trail, with Tam falling into step beside her.

"Question," he said.

"Answer's no."

"You don't know the question yet."

"The question involves either money, mules, or me doing something stupid for the company's benefit. Answer's no."

"It involves none of those things." He kicked a pinecone off the trail with unnecessary violence. "It involves Greyhollow. Specifically, it involves the fact that you've been twitchy about the town for two months, and I've known you since you were nine, and 'twitchy' on you means something's wrong that you're not saying."

Vesper kept her eyes on the trail. A switchback dropped away below them, all scree and shadow. "I'm not twitchy."

"You reorganized the cargo manifest twice. You reorganized *cargo*, Ves. You alphabetized it. Nobody alphabetizes contraband."

"It's efficient."

"It's a cry for help." He caught her arm—her right arm, deliberately, she noticed, and hated that he was careful even about that—and made her stop and look at him. Up close, his face had lost the morning's humor. "Something's wrong with the wards. Isn't it. That's what you're not saying."

She should have lied. She was good at lying; it was half her trade. But Tam stood there with pine needles in his hair and three years of watching her pray to a silent god written all over his patient face, and the lie died somewhere in her chest.

"They flicker," she said quietly. "The border wards. When I cross them, they flicker. Like a candle in a draft. They didn't used to. A ward either holds or it doesn't—that's the whole point of a ward—but these—" She shook her head. "It's like watching someone breathe wrong. You can't say what's off. You just know."

"When did it start?"

"Months ago. Getting worse."

"And you didn't tell me."

"What was I going to tell you? 'The magic fence stutters sometimes'? The ward-keepers would laugh me out of Greyhollow, and the ones who wouldn't laugh would start asking questions about how a smuggler knows what a healthy ward feels like." She pulled her arm free, gently. "I was going to look into it. Quietly. After this run."

"After this run," Tam repeated. "Your answer to everything. After this run, I'll deal with the mark. After this run, I'll figure out why you flinch every time a priest mentions the Veil. After this run, we'll talk about the fact that you haven't slept a full night in—"

"TAM."

The word cracked out of her louder than she meant, loud enough that heads turned up the line, and the silence that followed had weight. Below them, on the switchback, a mule stamped. Somewhere in the pines, a bird that had been singing decided against it.

Vesper breathed. Counted to four, the way the Veil's rites taught—*in for the threshold, hold for the crossing, out for the far side.*

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was—I'm sorry. Not tonight. Not on the trail. When we're behind walls, with the cargo delivered and the company paid, I'll tell you everything. Everything I can."

"*Everything you can,*" Tam echoed. But some of the hurt went out of his shoulders, because Tam had always understood the shape of oaths better than most sworn men, and he knew a boundary when he heard one. "You know what my grandmother used to say? 'After this run' is where good intentions go to die of exposure."

"Your grandmother also said the Veil was a story priests told to keep smugglers honest."

"She said a lot of things. She drank." He smiled, and it reached his eyes mostly, and they started walking again, and the moment passed the way moments do on mountains: buried under the next step, the next bend, the next problem.

Neither of them knew it was one of the last ordinary conversations they would ever have. Vesper would have walked differently if she'd known. She would have said something truer. She would have memorized the exact pitch of his laugh when she told him the mule had out-argued him, the way the light came through the pines and striped his shoulders gold and green, the specific quality of the silence between them that was better than most people's talking.

Instead she just walked, and bantered, and led them down out of the Teeth toward the world below, where the first cracks were already showing—hairline fractures in the great machinery of the border, invisible to everyone except a smuggler with a dead god's scar and a bad feeling she couldn't shake.

* * *

They sighted Greyhollow at dusk of the fourth day, and Vesper stopped so abruptly that Tam nearly walked into her back.

"What?" he said. "What is it?"

Below them, the town sat in its bowl of valley like a fistful of lit candles—window-glow, lantern-glow, the orange bloom of the gate-fires. And rising above it all, visible even from this distance, even in failing light: the ward-line. A shimmer in the air, faint as heat-haze, running along the valley's rim where the old stones stood. The wards that had sealed Thornfell's borders for two hundred years. The wards her mother had helped raise, though Vesper hadn't known that yet, wouldn't know for weeks.

Even from a mile out, even to eyes that shouldn't have been able to see it at all, the shimmer was stuttering.

Flickering. Guttering. Like candles in a draft.

Like something dying.

"Ves," Tam said again, quieter now, because he'd followed her gaze and seen it too, seen what no one was supposed to see from this distance. "Tell me that's the light playing tricks."

Vesper's hand had gone, without her permission, to her left forearm. Under the sleeve, against dead skin, the mark did nothing at all.

And yet—for the first time in three years, deep beneath the silence, so faint she could believe she'd imagined it—she felt something move. Not a voice. Not a hum. Just a shift, the way a house shifts on its foundations when something enormous turns over in its sleep a mile underground.

"No," she heard herself say. "It's not the light."

She started down toward Greyhollow, and the whole caravan followed her, and behind them the sun went down on the Widow's Teeth, and above the Teeth, unseen by anyone, the first stars were slower to come out than they had ever been before.
