## 2. Salt for the Border Wards

Greyhollow announced itself the way it always did: by smell before sight. Woodsmoke and wet stone first, then the tanneries' sharp reek, then—when the road bent around the shoulder of the mountain—the town itself, crouched in its bowl of granite like a badger in a den. The border wards rose above the rooftops in a lattice of pale light, thin as spider silk, strung from tower to tower to the raw faces of the cliffs. From this distance they looked the way they always looked. Beautiful, if you liked that kind of thing. Impersonal.

Vesper did not like that kind of thing. She had grown up under wards like these, or rather under the stories of them, and she knew what they were for. Not keeping things out. Keeping accounts.

"Salt first," Tam said at her shoulder, reading her mind the way he'd been doing since they were nine years old and sharing a single blanket in a mercenary camp that smelled of horse and rust. "Then the ward-keeper, then the wine. In that order or we'll be poor by midnight."

"You're the one who drinks like a man with two livers."

"One liver, but it's ambitious." He grinned, and the grin caught the last of the daylight, and for a moment Vesper forgot the silence under her skin entirely. That was Tam's gift. He could make forgetting look easy.

The caravan rolled through the gate without stopping—Brannoc's papers were good, bought honestly from a customs clerk who'd owed the company money since the war—and they made their camp in the yard of the Drowned Bell, which was neither drowned nor a bell but had been called both by men too drunk to argue with. While the drovers saw to the mules, Vesper counted out salt from the lead-lined crates. Thornfell salt, grey as old teeth, worth more per pound than silver in the lowlands because it held a ward's edge like nothing else mined under the sun. It was the reason caravans like theirs existed at all. The border towns ate salt the way furnaces ate coal, and every grain of it passed through hands that preferred not to be written down.

She was carrying the second crate into the yard when she felt it.

Not saw it. Felt it—a prickle along the mark on her left forearm, the scar she'd spent six years passing off as a rope burn, a knife wound, a childhood accident, depending on who asked. The mark never prickled. The mark hadn't done anything in three years except sit there like a dead thing stitched to living skin.

She set down the crate and looked up.

Above Greyhollow, the wards flickered.

Not the steady breathing shimmer she knew, the slow pulse that meant the lattice was fed and content. This was stuttering. Guttering. A candle in a draft, a lamp running dry. One strand near the north tower went dark altogether for the length of a heartbeat—long enough that Vesper saw, plain as day, the gap where light should have been—then flared back twice as bright, as if embarrassed.

No one else in the yard noticed. Mule drivers never looked up; that was practically doctrine. But Vesper stood with her hand pressed flat against the crate of salt and watched the wards hiccup again, and again, and understood that she was looking at something dying by increments. The world's machinery, failing. And no one had told her. No one had told anyone.

*You'd know,* said the bitter little voice that lived in her chest these days, *if your god still spoke.*

She shoved the thought down where she kept all the others and went to find the ward-keeper.

* * *

Ward-keeper Ossen received bribes in the cellar beneath his own house, which was either very corrupt or very careful, and in Vesper's experience the two qualities traveled together. He was a soft man gone hard at the edges, the way cheese goes, with ink stains up both forearms and the permanent squint of someone who read ledgers by candlelight for thirty years. He took the purse she slid across the barrel-top, weighed it in his palm out of pure ritual—he already knew what was in it—and said, "Salt manifests tomorrow, dawn bell. You'll want your tally stamped before noon or the toll doubles."

"The toll doubled last time you said that."

"And you paid it, and here we are, friends again." He smiled with about a third of his mouth. Then, because Vesper didn't move to leave, and because corruption has its own etiquette, he sighed and added, "Something else?"

"The wards," she said. "They're flickering."

Ossen's smile died the way a snuffed wick dies—not gradually, just gone.

"They flicker every winter," he said. "Cold makes the lattice brittle."

"It's midsummer."

"Then it's the wind off the Teeth." He began stacking papers that didn't need stacking, moving her toward the door without touching her. "You've been coming through Greyhollow how long, Halloran? Four years? Five? You've seen the wards gutter before. Everyone has. Salt prices go up, everyone grumbles, spring comes, everything settles. There's a rhythm to these things."

"There's a rhythm to heartbeats too," Vesper said. "And then there's the moment they stop."

He stopped stacking papers. For an instant—just an instant—the squint dropped away and she saw underneath it, and what was underneath was fear, old fear, worn smooth as a river stone from years of handling. Then he picked the squint back up like a mask.

"You want my advice, smuggler? Sell your salt, take your coin, and be over the pass before the new moon. There are people asking questions this season I don't like answering, and I answer fewer questions when the askers aren't standing in my cellar." He pushed the purse back across the barrel toward her. Half the coins. Refunded. "That's not a bribe anymore. That's advice, and advice is free, and free things are the only kind I give this month."

Vesper left the coins where they lay. She had been thrown out of better cellars by worse men, but never one who'd given money back. Money given back was a message, and the message was: *I am afraid, and my fear is worth more than your silver.*

In three years of silence, her god had sent her nothing. But apparently the world had started sending warnings.

* * *

The common room of the Drowned Bell was loud with caravan folk and garrison soldiers drinking on half-pay, and Tam held court at the corner table with the easy gravity of a man who'd never once doubted he was interesting. He had a fiddle he couldn't play and a story he could, and by the time Vesper came in he'd got a round of drinks out of a wool merchant on the strength of a tale about a mule, a widow, and a tax assessor that Vesper had heard forty times and still couldn't hear the end of without smiling.

She sat down across from him with her back to the wall, the way you do when you've been shot at professionally, and let him pour her a cup of the Bell's terrible red wine.

"You've got a face," Tam observed.

"I've got several."

"No, a face-face. The one where you've learned something and you're deciding how much of it is mine to know." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, voice dropping under the room's noise. "The ward-keeper talk?"

"He gave me back half the bribe."

Tam whistled softly. "Ossen? Ossen once charged Brannoc a handling fee for handling a bribe Brannoc hadn't offered yet." He turned his cup slowly on the wood. "So it's true, then. What the drovers were saying at the last relay. Wards going strange all down the Thornfell line. Candles in a wind, someone said."

"You believe drover gossip now?"

"I believe patterns." His eyes came up, and for a moment the court jester wasn't home and someone older was looking out. "Three years ago you stopped praying out loud. Did I ever tell you I noticed?"

The cup stopped halfway to Vesper's mouth.

"You pray in your sleep," Tam said, gentle as setting down something breakable. "Or you used to. Little bits of it. The litany, the responses. You'd murmur the words in your bunk like a kid reciting lessons. Then one year you just... stopped. And you never talked about it, so I never asked. That's the deal we have, isn't it? I don't ask about the arm, you don't ask why I keep a dead man's fiddle."

Vesper set the cup down. Around them the room roared on, oblivious, and above the roof she knew the wards were guttering like a lamp with no oil, and somewhere under the mountain—she could almost feel it tonight, almost—the enormous sleeping thing shifted in its foundations.

"Tess," she said, using the old name, the name from before the company, from the orphan years. "If I told you I thought something was wrong with the world itself—not the roads, not the politics, the world—what would you say?"

Tam considered this with the seriousness he gave to nothing else. "I'd say the world's been wrong my whole life and I've managed." Then he touched his cup to hers. "But I'd also say: tell me when you figure out what kind of wrong, and I'll start worrying on schedule."

It was as close to comfort as either of them could manufacture, and she took it, and for an hour she let herself have the wine and the noise and the ordinary miracle of her brother laughing at his own jokes.

She was the one who saw the stranger.

* * *

The woman sat alone at a table near the hearth, which no one did in midsummer unless they wanted to sweat, and she was dressed in a way that made Vesper's professional eye itch. The clothes were good—too good for a traveler, cut in a style Vesper didn't recognize, dark fabrics that seemed to drink the firelight rather than reflect it—and yet the woman wore them carelessly, rumpled, like a noble playing at vagrancy or a vagrant who'd robbed the right corpse. Her hair was black shot through with premature silver. Her hands, wrapped around an untouched cup, were long-fingered and ringless and very still.

None of that was why Vesper's spine went cold.

It was that the woman was watching her. Had been watching her. Not the way soldiers sized up strangers, not the way thieves marked marks, not even the way admirers lingered. She was watching Vesper the way you watch a door you're waiting for someone to come through. With recognition. With patience. With something that looked, unbearably, like grief held at arm's length.

"She's been at it twenty minutes," Tam murmured, following her gaze. "Since before you sat down. Want me to go be charming at her?"

"No." Vesper stood. "Stay charming here."

Crossing the room, she catalogued exits out of habit: kitchen door, cellar stair, front shutters open to the yard. The woman tracked her approach without moving anything but her eyes, and up close Vesper saw details that rearranged the picture entirely—the woman's ears were slightly too elegant, her pupils caught the firelight with a faint silver fleck like metal dust, and around her throat, hidden until now by the angle of her collar, was a thin scar in the shape of a closed eye.

Vesper knew that shape. She had spent six years hiding its twin on her arm.

"You're staring," the woman said. Her voice was low and dry, an accent Vesper couldn't place—old-fashioned, formal, each word placed like a coin on a counter.

"So are you."

"Yes." The woman didn't apologize, didn't flinch, didn't do any of the things a caught watcher does. "I've been watching you since you came through the gate. Watching you look at the wards." She turned her untouched cup a quarter-turn. "Most people see the wards the way they see weather. You looked at them the way you'd look at a sick friend. Do you know how few people alive can see that clearly? Do you know what it means, that you can?"

"It means I'm nosy."

"It means," the woman said quietly, "that you're Maerin's daughter."

The room's noise seemed to happen somewhere far away. Vesper kept her face still through sheer mercenary discipline while everything behind it went white and ringing. Her mother's name had not been spoken aloud to her in fifteen years. Her mother's name was not a thing people knew. Vesper Halloran had built her whole life on that fact, brick by careful brick.

"I don't know what you've heard," she said, "or who you think I am, but—"

"Sable," the woman said, as if Vesper had asked. "Formerly of the Deep Court, though *formerly* is doing a great deal of work in that sentence." She finally lifted the cup and drank, and Vesper saw her hand was not quite steady. "I knew your mother when we were both younger and stupider and the wards were new. I knew her when the bargain was struck. And I have crossed half the world and waited three weeks in this sweltering room"—a glance at the hearth, distaste flickering across her fine features—"to tell you one thing before the new moon."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind Vesper, Tam had gone quiet, listening.

"Which is?" Vesper asked.

Sable set down the cup and looked up at her, and this close, in this light, her eyes were the exact color of the gap between stars.

"That the silence you've been living inside isn't abandonment, Vesper Halloran. It's consumption. Something under Thornfell is eating your god's voice—one soul at a time—and the wards are flickering because the song that holds them is running out." She rose, unhurried, gathering her strange dark coat. "And the debt for what's been eaten has come due to your family. Again."

She left a coin on the table for wine she hadn't drunk, walked past Vesper close enough that the cold came off her like air off a glacier, and paused at her shoulder just long enough to say:

"New moon, child. After that, I won't be able to help you. None of us will."

Then she was gone through the crowd, and the common room noise rushed back in like water into a footprint, and above the roof of the Drowned Bell, unseen by anyone drinking there, another strand of the wards went dark—and this time, it did not come back.
