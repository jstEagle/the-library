## 3. The Night Without Stars

They made Greyhollow's outer camp by dusk, and the sky was already wrong.

Vesper noticed it the way she noticed most things—sideways, in the space between one chore and another. She was checking the mule's harness knots when she realized the light had no source. No sun. No stars. Just a flat, bruise-colored dark pressing down on the pass like a lid.

"Tam," she said. "When did we last see a star?"

Tam looked up from the cookfire he was coaxing to life, spat a curl of tallow into the flames, and squinted at the sky with the expression of a man appraising a horse that might be lame. "Saw one this morning. The bright one, low over the eastern ridge. The one you're always swearing is your god's lantern." He grinned. "Figured if Veil was watching, he'd have said something about how much salt we're smuggling."

The banter came easily because it always did. That was the trouble with Tam—everything came easily. His laughter, his temper, his forgiveness. He was the only person alive who could call her *Ves* without her hand drifting toward a knife hilt, and she'd have gutted anyone who suggested that was a weakness.

She crouched beside the fire and did not look up again. Looking up felt like an invitation.

Above them, where Thornfell's peaks sawed black notches out of the horizon, there should have been the river of stars the mountain folk called the Shepherd's Road. There should have been constellations old smugglers navigated by—the Lantern, the Hound, the Weeping Sisters. There should have been *something*. Instead the dark was total and smooth as poured ink, and it had a texture to it, almost a weight, like the underside of something vast holding its breath.

Her mark prickled.

It sat under her left sleeve, coiled from wrist to elbow—a scar, everyone believed, from a bar-fight in Saltmarsh gone badly. Only Vesper knew it was the sigil of the Veil, the silent god of thresholds and crossings, burned into her skin the night she swore her oath at nineteen. For six years it had been warm when roads were safe and cold when they weren't, a compass stitched into her flesh. And for three years it had been nothing but scar tissue. She prayed to it every night anyway, kneeling in whatever ditch or stable or rented room the company occupied, whispering into silence the way a widow talks to an empty chair.

Tonight the mark wasn't cold. It was *aching*, deep and rhythmic, like a tooth about to go bad.

The caravan was small tonight: nine wagons, four drivers, Brannoc up front with the lead team, and a cargo manifest that officially read *culinary salt* and unofficially read *enough ward-salt to keep three border towns lit through winter*. Ward-salt was the lifeblood of their trade—crystals quarried from deep veins where the mountain's old magic pooled, ground and mixed into the standing stones that kept the border wards humming. Legal salt kept hearthfires burning. Their salt kept the wards fed on the sly, because the Crown's tariffs had priced honest ward-keepers out of the market years ago, and nobody wanted to ask what happened to a town whose wards went dark.

Vesper was about to find out.

* * *

It began with sound—or rather, with the absence of it.

One moment the camp was its ordinary evening orchestra: mules stamping, drivers arguing over dice, the hiss of Tam's fire, wind combing the high pines. The next, every sound dropped away at once, snuffed like fingers pinching wicks, and in that perfect silence Vesper heard the wards.

Not heard, exactly. Felt. A high, thin vibration running through the bedrock, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her teeth—the song of the Greyhollow ward-line, the great ringed stones that stood sentinel along the pass. She'd lived beside that hum her whole adult life. It was as constant as her own pulse.

It stopped.

And then the world screamed.

Light erupted along the ridge—not warm light, but a hard white glare, the color of lightning trapped in glass. The ward-stones flared all at once down the whole visible length of the border, a chain of burning beads strung across the mountainside, and then—

Then they broke.

There was no better word for it. The nearest stone, a monolith older than any kingdom, simply came apart, shearing along seams no eye could see, shards of glowing crystal raining down its own flank like a shattered mirror. And the stone beside it. And the next, and the next, a cascade of ruin rolling away into the dark until the whole ward-line was coming apart in slow, glittering catastrophe, and the sound reached them at last—a vast crystalline groan, then a ringing, singing crash, thousands of tons of ancient sorcery dying all at once.

"Mules!" Brannoc's roar cut through the panic before it could take root. He came down the wagon line like a landslide given a voice, big hands hauling panicking animals back from the traces. "Hobble them, blindfold them, MOVE—and everyone else get under cover, now! Stone falls off mountains when wards die!"

He was right. It always did. The wards weren't just borders; they were old bargains stitched into the bones of the land, and when the stitching tore loose, the land remembered it was wild. Vesper felt it even through her boots—a tremor, faint but rising, the mountain clearing its throat.

She worked the mule lines with hands that moved on their own while her mind raced somewhere else entirely. Because she'd seen something, in that first white flare. In the instant before the nearest stone burst apart, its light hadn't been white at all. It had flickered—green, then violet, then a hungry gold she'd never seen in any ward-work—and for half a heartbeat the flare had cast shadows that moved *wrong*, shadows that leaned toward the mountain instead of away from the firelight.

Toward the Undercourt. The name rose unbidden from the stories her mother used to tell, the ones Maerin Halloran would murmur over darning or dishwater until Vesper was old enough to demand sense from them. *The court beneath the mountain, love. The ones who signed first. Every bargain has a counterparty, and ours lives underground.*

She'd thought they were fairy tales. Mothers' tales. She'd thought a lot of things.

"Ves." Tam appeared at her shoulder, breathless, a hobbling strap still in his fist. "Your arm."

"What?"

"Your arm. Look at it."

She shoved up her sleeve. The scar-mark was glowing. Faintly—a dull ember-red pulsing in time with the tremors underfoot—but glowing, unmistakably, for the first time in three years. Not warm the way it used to be, not the steady hearth-warmth of a god paying attention. This was fever-light. Warning-light.

"It's never done that," Tam said quietly.

"No," Vesper said. "It hasn't."

Around them the camp had dissolved into organized chaos, which was the only kind Brannoc permitted. Wagons dragged into a defensive ring. Drivers pulling oilskins over the loads. Someone had gotten a second fire going and someone else was shouting that the fires wouldn't catch right, and they were correct—the flames burned strange and low, leaning hard toward the mountain as though the air itself were being drawn inward. Drawn down.

Below them, far below, something exhaled.

Vesper felt it more than heard it: a long, cold updraft from the depths of Thornfell, carrying a smell like wet iron and crushed flowers, and threaded through it—so faint she'd spend the rest of her life unsure whether she'd truly caught it—a sound. A voice? Many voices? Something between a choir and a crowd murmuring behind a locked door.

The mark on her arm burned hotter.

And every star stayed missing.

That was the thing she couldn't stop circling back to, even as Brannoc bellowed orders and the mountain grumbled and grown men wept by the fires. Wards died. She'd seen wards die before, little deaths, single stones cracked by weather or sabotage, patched with fresh salt and bribes. But the stars going out—all of them, everywhere, in a single night—that wasn't weather. That wasn't sabotage. That was the sky itself being switched off, and whatever power could reach up and put out the stars could reach anywhere.

Including, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the mountain wind, into a caravan of eleven people camped on its slopes.

* * *

They survived the night the way mercenaries survive everything: badly, but thoroughly.

Aftershocks rolled through the small hours, knocking rockslides loose in the high passes and keeping the mules in a lather of terror. Two drivers deserted outright, slipping away with what they could carry—one of them old Hesk, who'd run with the company eight years and left without so much as a goodbye, just his bedroll gone from the wagon and a hole shaped like him in the morning.

By grey false-dawn the sky was still empty. No sun. No stars. Just a sourceless, sickly luminescence, like the world lit from beneath by deep water. The company gathered around the dead cookfire, and Brannoc stood at the center of it looking ten years older than he had at dusk.

Brannoc Vale was a wall of a man gone soft at the edges, grey-shot beard, knuckles like river stones, a voice that had talked two hundred fights down to forty. He'd pulled Vesper out of a gutter in Cindermarsh when she was sixteen and never once mentioned it since. He counted Tam and Vesper as the closest thing he had to children, and he ruled the company the way good fathers rule: rarely, firmly, and with terrible clarity when it mattered.

"We ride for Greyhollow at first light," he said. "What passes for first light." Murmurs ran around the circle. He let them die. "Ward-keeper's got stores, walls, and thirty men. We make ourselves useful, we eat. After that—" his jaw worked, "—after that we talk about what's left of the trade. Nobody's buying ward-salt if there are no wards. Nobody's buying anything if there's no sun. So we hold together, we hold our coin, and we wait for the world to make sense again. Questions?"

"I've got one," Vesper said.

Every head turned. She hadn't planned to speak. Her mouth had simply opened on its own, driven by something that had been building in her chest since the wards came down.

"The debt." She kept her voice level. Kept her eyes on Tam, because it was his face she needed to watch. "We lost the Marrowford contract last month. Then the tolls on the King's Road doubled, and the Greymist crossing fee ate half our reserve, and I know—I *know*—we didn't have coin to cover the Greyhollow shipment. So somebody paid our creditors. Somebody kept this caravan rolling. And I've been doing sums all night, Tam, and I can't make them work."

The silence stretched. Around the fire, the company exchanged glances with the particular eloquence of people who know something the speaker doesn't.

"Ves," Tam said, and his voice was gentle in a way that set every nerve in her body alight. "Don't."

"Don't what? Ask my sworn brother where the money came from?" She stepped forward. The mark on her arm pulsed, ember-bright, in time with her heartbeat. "The wards just died, Tam. The *stars* died. And I'm standing here realizing my family's name has been floating around the ledgers of this company for years, and I never asked why, because asking felt like distrust. Well. The world just ended, so I'm done being polite. Where did the coin come from?"

Tam looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at the ground.

That told her everything, and nothing.

"There was a broker," Tam said finally. He spoke slowly, the words dragged out of him like nails from green wood. "Came to us in Cindermarsh, after Marrowford. Well-dressed. Wrong, though—you know how some people are dressed right but *wrong*? Skin too smooth. Smile a half-beat late. Said he represented lenders from beneath Thornfell." A ragged laugh escaped him. "I thought it was theater. Some cult-flavored moneylenders trying to spook provincial sellswords. They offered terms nobody sane offers. No interest. No collateral demanded upfront. Just a signature."

"And you signed."

"They were going to take the wagons, Ves. The horses. Everything. We'd have been begging on the Cindermarsh causeway by midwinter." His voice cracked, and he hated it, and she watched him hate it. "I signed. That's it. That's the whole story. A signature, and the coin showed up, and I figured—we'd pay it back in a year, two at most—"

"Signed *what*, Tam?" Her heart was slamming now. "A contract with the Undercourt. With the court under the mountain. What were the terms?"

"I don't—"

"*What were the terms?*"

"A debt of service," he whispered. "Payable on demand. That's all it said. One line. My name, my mark, and one line: *the debt shall be collected when the veil thins.*"

The words landed in Vesper's chest like a thrown knife.

*When the veil thins.*

Three years of praying into silence. Three years of a dead mark and a god that wouldn't answer. And tonight the wards shattered, the stars went out, and the veil—if such a thing existed, if her mother's fairy tales had teeth—had never been thinner.

"How long ago," she said. It wasn't a question anymore; her voice barely recognized itself. "How long ago did you sign?"

"Eleven months."

Eleven months. Eleven months of Tam joking at the fires, teaching her dice games, stealing her boots, being gloriously, infuriatingly alive—while carrying a paper in some vault beneath the mountain that said he belonged to something old and hungry. And she'd known. God help her, some part of her had *known*—she'd seen the broker herself once, at the edge of the Cindermarsh market, watching their camp with that same wrong patience Sable had worn in Greyhollow—and she'd said nothing, because the company was finally solvent and she hadn't wanted to look the gift-horse in its too-smooth mouth.

"This isn't your debt," she said aloud, and her voice shook, and she let it. "You have to understand that. Whatever paper you signed—it was never really yours. The debts started long before you. They started—"

She stopped. Because she'd been about to say *with me*, and she didn't know why she knew that, only that she did, with the certainty of a scar remembering rain. Debts tied to her. To her blood, her name, her mother's careful, secretive life. Maerin Halloran, who never spoke of her past, who paid strange tithes to strangers at solstice, who died whispering apologies to a daughter too young to ask for what.

Brannoc crossed the circle and put a hand on her shoulder, heavy as a roof beam. "Whatever you're about to say, Halloran—don't. Not here. Not yet." His eyes went around the fire, to the drivers pretending not to listen, to the smoking ruins of the world they'd all woken up in. "Greyhollow first. Walls first. Then we figure out what's hunting us."

But Veper wasn't listening anymore. She was looking at the eastern ridge, where the first broken ward-stone jutted against the pale non-dawn like a snapped tooth, and beyond it, down in the valley road they'd have to travel, a figure stood motionless in the grey light. Tall. Cloaked in a color that swallowed the eye. Watching the camp with the stillness of someone who had been waiting a very long time, and who had all night to watch the stars go out.

As Vesper stared, the figure raised one pale hand—not in threat, not in greeting.

In invitation.

Beside her, Tam followed her gaze, and she heard his breath stop. On her arm, the mark of the Veil blazed like a brand, and beneath the mountain, faint and far and getting closer, something began—very softly—to sing.
