## 4. What the Company Owes

The vote was called before dawn, in the longhouse of the Waystone camp, where the smoke hole had stopped drawing properly and the fire sat sullen in its pit like something sulking toward extinction.

Vesper stood at the back with her arms crossed and counted the faces. Thirty-one of the Thornfell Jackals had made it back from the pass roads—out of forty who'd walked out two weeks ago to run salt for Greyhollow's wards. Nine empty places around the trestle tables, nine names that would go into the ledger under *lost, cause unknown*, because nobody wanted to write down what they all knew: the stars had gone out, and things had come out of the dark that did not leave bodies behind.

Brannoc stood at the head table with his fists planted on the wood. He was not a tall man, but he had the kind of stillness that made rooms arrange themselves around him. Twenty years he'd held the Jackals together through famine contracts, border wars, the winter the passes ate half their caravan and spat out only boots. Twenty years of keeping thirty-odd violent, grieving people fed and pointed in the same direction.

He'd never looked as tired as he looked now.

"You've heard the tally," he said. His voice carried without effort; it always had. "Wards are down along the whole Thornfell line. Not breached. *Down*. Like someone reached up and snuffed them. The toll-houses are burning, Greyhollow's sealed its gates, and the last courier out of the low country says the same thing happened on the Vellmark border three nights back." He let that sit. "So we vote. Stay and hold the camp, or scatter and hide until whatever this is passes over."

"Nothing passes over," said Odile, the company's quartermaster, a grey-streaked woman who could count a coin purse by weight alone. "You don't put out every star in the sky and then *pass over*. This is either an ending or a beginning, and I don't much care which. I say scatter. Small groups, old routes, off the ledgers. We've done it before."

"We've run from tax men," said Hesk, one of the road-captains. "We've never run from the *sky*."

"And where would you hide," Vesper said, before she could stop herself.

Every head turned. She hadn't meant to speak. She'd meant to stand at the back and keep her mouth shut the way she'd kept her mouth shut for three years about the silence on her arm, and instead here she was, voice cracking across the room like a whip.

Hesk squinted at her. "Higher, girl. Higher than this. There's caves past the Salt Spine the old maps don't show. We winter deep, we come out when—"

"When the stars come back?" She heard her own voice rise and couldn't rein it. "And if they don't? If this isn't weather, Hesk, if it's a *door*—" She stopped herself there, because the next word out of her mouth was going to be Tam's name, and Tam's name was a wound none of them needed torn open tonight. Tam had been missing since the night the sky went black. Taken, taken, taken—the word wouldn't settle into any shape she could carry.

"The vote is stay or scatter," Brannoc said quietly. "Not argue. Hands, if you please. Stay and hold?"

Three hands. Four. Five. Vesper counted them and felt something cold move through her chest: mostly the old ones, the ones with nowhere else to be.

"Scatter and hide?"

The rest. Twenty-six hands, some of them raised slow and guilty, some fast and certain, a few not raised at all but nodded, the way men nod when raising a hand feels too much like abandoning a post.

Odile looked around the room. "Carried."

Nobody cheered. That was the thing about the Jackals—they knew the difference between a victory and a funeral that happened to have a motion attached.

* * *

They broke camp in four hours, which was a company record even for bad news. Wagons stripped, stores split, the strongbox divided into seven shares that Odile weighed out with her lips moving in silent arithmetic. Vesper worked alongside them in silence, hauling canvas, cutting tent-lines, and watching the place that had been her home for eleven years come apart like a knot picked loose.

It was Brannoc who found her at the wagon line, when most of the others were already gone.

She heard his boots on the frozen mud and didn't turn. There was no mistaking his tread—heavy on the left foot since the arrow at Cold Ford, twelve years back. He came around beside her and stood looking at the last wagon, hers, half-loaded with rope and salt and a locked chest she'd told everyone was tools.

"Burn it," he said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Your arm." He said it flat, no ceremony, which somehow made it worse. "The mark. Burn it off, pack the scar with ash, and walk south past Vellmark and change your name and never sing again. You know what that mark means now better than anyone. Whatever took the stars took them *looking for something*, and you've got the only lit window in the whole mountain range painted on your skin."

Vesper set down the coil of rope she'd been holding very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands want to throw it.

"You know what it means too," she said.

"I know what you've *told* me. A birthmark. A scar from a forge accident. Eleven years of stories, Ves, and every one of them different, and I let it go because you bled for this company and a mercenary's past is her own business." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "But my past is the company, and the company is twenty-six people scattered along the roads right now with their families' names in our ledgers, and I will not have some god-hunt following our scent because you're sentimental about a piece of skin."

"It's not a piece of skin."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "That's rather the problem."

The silence stretched between them, cold as the smoke-hole air. Overhead the sky sat starless and whole, a black lid pressed down over the world, and Vesper felt her mark itch beneath her sleeve the way it had itched every night for three years—like a door someone had bricked over while she slept behind it.

"Where's Tam?" Brannoc asked suddenly, and she flinched.

"That's not fair."

"He vanished the same night the sky did. You've been asking after him in every camp between here and Greyhollow—I've watched you do it. So either you know something or you suspect something, and either way you're about to do something stupid, because you always do something stupid about him. He's not blood, Ves. I know you think he is, but he signed on eight years ago same as the rest of us, and—"

"He's my brother," Vesper said. Her voice came out low and dangerous and she let it. "Sworn. Which in this company used to mean something. Or did the vote burn that too?"

"Sworn means sworn *while the company holds*." Brannoc's jaw tightened. "The company just voted to stop holding. That's what scattering *is*, girl. It's admitting we can't protect each other anymore. It's every hand for itself until the world makes sense again—and maybe it never does, and maybe we all die in holes in the ground, but at least we die *hidden*."

"And Tam dies wherever he is, alone."

"If he's alive."

She hit him. She hadn't decided to; her fist simply arrived at his jaw with her whole shoulder behind it, and Brannoc—fifty years old, left-footed, tired—took it and rocked with it and did not fall, and did not swing back. He just stood there breathing hard through his nose with his fists half-raised and his eyes full of something that wasn't anger, and that was almost worse.

"You don't get to say that," she said. "You don't get to write him off in a ledger. Not him."

"Then tell me what you know." His voice dropped. "Tell me what's on your arm, Vesper. Tell me why a broker of the Undercourt sent a debt-token to *this camp* two days before the sky went black, addressed to the Halloran line. Tell me why Odile burned it and I let her, and why neither of us has slept since." He stepped closer. "I've been your captain for eleven years. Give me one truth and I'll spend the rest of this rotten company buying you time to chase it."

And there it was. The door, open, one truth wide. She thought of Tam's face the night he'd confessed—*I signed, Ves, I signed so the brokers wouldn't take the camp*—and of the mark going hot and silent against her prayers, and of Maerin, dead six years, whose name was apparently worth sending tokens across the border.

One truth. But truths, in her experience, were like the first coin out of a purse; you never got to give just one.

"I can't," she said. "Not yet. Ask me again when I've got him back."

Brannoc closed his eyes. When he opened them, the captain was back behind them, and the friend had gone somewhere he clearly hoped she couldn't see.

"Then hear my order, as your captain, on the last day this company exists." He straightened. "Burn the mark. Scatter south. Don't look for Tam, don't send word, don't sign anything, don't pray where anyone can see you praying. And if you disobey me—"

"You'll what?" She laughed, and it came out ragged. "Cast me out? Brannoc, the company *is* cast out. You voted us into pieces an hour ago. All that's left of the Thornfell Jackals is standing in this mud arguing, and one of us is leaving with or without your blessing, so save your breath for the road."

For a moment she thought he'd strike her. She almost wanted him to—it would have been easier, a clean wound, something to match the ache in her knuckles. Instead he reached out and gripped her shoulder once, hard, the way he'd gripped it the day she'd sworn her oath to the company, and said:

"Don't die stupid, Halloran."

Then he turned, limped to his horse, and rode north without looking back, because Brannoc had never once in twenty years looked back at a thing he'd finished breaking.

* * *

She was alone with the last wagon and the ash-grey sky for a long while before Odile came back for her. The old woman stood at the edge of the empty camp with her ledger under her arm and her breath smoking, and studied Vesper with the flat, weighing look she usually saved for counterfeit coins.

"He offered you the truth-for-time trade," Odile said. "I know that look on him. Did you take it?"

"No."

"Mm." Odile came closer, unhurried. From inside her coat she drew a folded square of oilcloth and held it out. "Then you'll want these. Ledger pages. The Halloran entries."

Vesper went still. "You kept copies."

"I keep copies of everything. It's why I'm still alive at sixty-one." Odile pressed the packet into her hands and closed Vesper's fingers over it. "Your mother's name comes up more than a smuggler's mother should, girl. Debts dated before you were born. Payments marked *in kind*. And one entry, thirty years back, in a hand I don't recognize, that just says: *the Veil accepts.*"

The mark on Vesper's arm burned like a coal laid against bone.

"Odile," she said, and her voice came out strange to her own ears. "What do you know about the Undercourt?"

The old woman smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Only what every quartermaster knows, dear. Nobody lends that deep into the mountains without expecting collateral." She tapped the oilcloth packet once. "And nobody collects collateral personally unless the debtor is *special*."

She turned and walked away into the grey morning, small and straight-backed, until the fog took her, and Vesper Halloran stood alone in the ruins of her family—the sworn one scattered, the chosen one missing, the blood one dead and lying in a ledger—with a packet of pages in her fist and a single direction her feet already knew.

South. Toward the mountain. Toward the man Sable had named on the road out of Greyhollow, the exile with her mother's eyes in a stranger's face, who had said, in that voice like water over stone:

*When you're ready to learn what Maerin actually sold, come find me. I'll be where the pilgrims go hollow.*

Vesper loaded the wagon. She did not burn anything—not the mark, not the letters, not the bridge behind her, though Brannoc would have said all three were the same act. She climbed onto the bench, shook the reins, and turned the last wagon of the Thornfell Jackals onto the south road, alone, while overhead the starless dark leaned close enough to touch.

Behind her, faint on the wind off the peaks, she thought she heard singing.

It stopped the moment she listened for it.
