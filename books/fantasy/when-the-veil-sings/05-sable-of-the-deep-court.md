## 5. Sable of the Deep Court

The road out of Greyhollow was a scar of mud and old cart-ruts, winding north along the shoulder of the Thornfells until the mountain wall swallowed it whole. Vesper walked it alone, which was a choice she'd made at the crossroads outside town when Brannoc's voice was still ringing in her ears—*burn the mark, Vesper, vanish*—and her feet had simply kept going north instead of east.

She told herself it was for Tam. It mostly was.

Three days from Greyhollow, the world had not grown any brighter. The stars were still gone. Not clouded over—gone, the way a tooth is gone from a jaw, leaving a black socket where something used to live. Travelers on the road spoke in whispers about it. A tinker claimed the sky was mourning. A pilgrim woman said the gods had blinked, and no one knew what they'd see when they opened their eyes again.

Vesper's mark sat cold under her sleeve, and she found herself touching it more often than she liked to admit. Three years of silence, and now this. Coincidence had never been kind enough to visit her twice.

It was late afternoon, light failing early because there were no stars coming behind the dusk, when she smelled woodsmoke that shouldn't have been there.

Someone had a fire going in the hollow below the road—a small one, banked low, the way you built a fire if you wanted warmth without attention. Vesper stopped, hand drifting to the hatchet at her hip, and looked down into the dip where a lone figure sat wrapped in a grey traveling cloak, hood up, hands extended over the coals as if warming them were the only business in the world.

"I know you," Vesper called down. "You were in Greyhollow. Watching me at the ward-house."

The figure didn't startle. That was answer enough. "I was," said a woman's voice, low and even, like water finding its level. "You noticed. Most people don't."

"Most people aren't being watched." Vesper didn't come down the slope. She'd survived nine years of smuggling by never letting anyone pick the ground before she did. "Who are you?"

"A question I'll trade for one of yours." The hood tilted back.

The woman's face caught what little light remained: sharp-boned, dark-eyed, with hair the color of wet slate cut short at the jaw. Beautiful in the way a knife is beautiful—all edge and intent—and somewhere around forty, or pretending to be. But it wasn't the face that stopped Vesper's breath. It was the eyes. In their depths, faint as fish beneath ice, something *glowed*. Not reflected firelight. Something from underneath.

"You're not surface-born," Vesper said quietly.

"No." The woman smiled without warmth. "And neither are you, whatever your papers say. Sit down, Vesper Halloran. Or stand there and freeze while I talk anyway—it changes nothing. But you'll want to be sitting for some of it."

Vesper should have turned and walked away. Every instinct bred into her by the passes and the border patrols said so. Instead she came down the slope, chose a rock with her back to stone and a view of the trail, and drew her hatchet across her knees where the stranger could see it.

"Sable," the woman said. "Just Sable. The rest of my name would take too long to say and cost you more than you can pay."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's a price list." Sable fed a twig to the fire. "Everything I tell you tonight has a cost. Not coin—I don't want your coin. Knowledge, Vesper, is like wine. Once it's poured, you've drunk it whether you meant to or not, and it changes the taste of everything after."

"Then pour carefully."

"Oh, I intend to." Sable's dark eyes lifted, and for just a moment the glow beneath them flared, and Vesper felt the mark on her arm shiver—not warm, not cold, but *aware*, like a sleeping animal scenting another of its kind. "First answer, freely given, because you'll need it to make sense of anything else: your god isn't silent because it abandoned you."

The words went through Vesper like a blade between ribs. She kept her face still. "I don't know what you mean."

"Your arm does." Sable nodded at the sleeve. "I've been watching it since Greyhollow. It hasn't slept once since the stars went out. Has it? It keeps reaching for something that isn't answering."

Vesper said nothing, which was itself an admission. She hated how easily this woman read her.

"The Veil doesn't abandon its marked," Sable continued, turning the twig in her fingers. "It can't. It's bound into you the way a song is bound into a singer's throat. If the throat is silenced, the singer doesn't stop loving music. She just—" Sable paused, and something flickered across her face, an old grief surfacing and being pushed back down with visible effort. "She just stops being able to say so."

"*If* the throat is silenced," Vesper repeated. "By whom?"

"There's your second answer, and that one costs." Sable set down the twig and met her eyes squarely. "Ask it, and you can't unask it. You'll spend the rest of your life knowing, and knowing will make the road ahead much harder and much shorter. Ask anyway. We both know you will."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound almost like voices.

"By whom?" Vesper asked.

Sable exhaled slowly, as if setting down something heavy. "The court beneath the mountain. My court. And I need you to understand, before you decide what my face deserves, that I have not been part of it for eleven years. They cast me out. I have been living on your side of the stone ever since, eating your bread and hating every crumb of it."

"The Undercourt." Vesper had heard the name the way children hear names for things in the dark—half story, half warning. Old ward-keepers muttered about it when the wards flickered. "That's a story mothers use to keep children off the scree slopes."

"So was I, once. A story with a title." Sable's mouth twisted. "House Vael, third seat of the Deep Bench. Exiled for asking questions in the wrong tone of voice. Which is why I'm sitting by a roadside fire talking to a smuggler instead of dining in vaults lit with drowned starlight."

"Why me?" The question came out harder than Vesper intended. "Out of everyone on this road, why follow me? Why do you know my name?"

"Because of your mother."

The world went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath. Vesper became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and heavy, and of the mark lying utterly still against her forearm, listening.

"My mother," Vesper said carefully, "was Maerin Halloran, who ran salt and tin through these passes and died of a fever when I was fourteen. She prayed to no one, owed no one, and left me a hatchet, a ledgers-book, and a hatred of border officials."

"She left you considerably more than that, and lied to you about all of it, and I helped her lie, which is the only reason I'm telling you now." Sable leaned forward, and the under-glow in her eyes steadied. "Maerin Halloran brokered the binding of the border wards, Vesper. Forty-one years ago. Not a ward-keeper. Not a college of mages. One woman from a smuggler's family, walking down into the mountain with terms written in her own blood, and the Undercourt agreed to them because they had no choice. Your mother held a pen over the most powerful court in the world, and she made them sign."

"That's—" Vesper laughed, and it came out wrong. "My mother counted barrels. She cheated toll-keepers and taught me which bridges rot first. She never—"

"Never mentioned that she could read Old Deep, the language the wards are written in?" Sable's voice gentled, and somehow that was worse than cruelty. "Never wondered why the ward-keepers of three towns took her word over their own instruments? Never noticed that the Halloran family never paid a toll, never got searched, never lost a caravan—not because you were good, though you were good, but because the wards themselves bent around your bloodline like reeds in current?"

Vesper opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the memory was there, rising: her mother standing at the ward-stone outside Greyhollow, palm flat against it, murmuring something too low to hear, and the stone's flicker stilling like a horse calmed by a familiar hand. She'd been eight. She'd thought nothing of it. Children think nothing of miracles performed by their mothers; they assume the world works that way for everyone.

"She told me the wards liked our salt," Vesper said hoarsely.

"They did like your salt. It's excellent salt." For one unguarded moment, Sable's expression cracked into something rawer—the ghost of a smile with grief behind it. "She used to say that too. Standing exactly where you're standing, with exactly that look on her face, insisting the world was small and explainable because the alternative terrified her. You have her chin, you know. And her talent for refusing to be impressed."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than Vesper meant. "Don't talk about her like you—like you *knew* her."

"I knew her for six years." Sable said it plainly, and let the plainness carry what it carried. "I loved her longer than that. She was the bravest coward I ever met, and the kindest liar, and she went down into my court alone and came back up having bought peace for the whole border with a contract whose terms she never showed anyone. Including me." A pause. "Including, I begin to suspect, you."

The fire burned down a notch. Vesper stared into it and saw nothing but the shapes her thoughts made: her mother's ledger-book, packed away in a cache in the high pass that Vesper hadn't opened in nine years. The strange clause on the last page she'd never been able to read, the script that slid off the eye like water off oil. The way Maerin had gone quiet, near the end, whenever the subject of the wards came up—quiet the way people go quiet about debts.

"What was the price?" Vesper heard herself ask. "For the wards. What did my mother pay?"

"That answer costs more than all the others together." Sable drew her cloak tighter. "And here is the terrible arithmetic of it, Vesper: I can give it to you for free right now, and watch you break beside my fire with nowhere to run until morning. Or I can point you at your mother's cache and let you find it yourself, in her own handwriting, with time to hate both of us properly. I've been debating which is kinder for three days, and I still don't know."

"Then don't choose." Vesper stood. Her legs felt distant, borrowed. "Point me at the cache. I'll pay the cost myself."

Sable studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw seemed to settle something.

"The cache under the cairn at Widow's Rest. Third stone from the top is loose; the book is wrapped in oilcloth beneath the salt stores you already knew about." She rose too, unhurried, and pulled her hood back up, though her eyes still glowed faintly through the shadow. "One more thing, freely given, because you'll need it to survive the reading: when you understand what the contract says, you're going to want to burn it. Don't. The contract is the only leash left on the thing that silenced your god, and a leash burns from the holder's hand first."

* * *

From the ridge above the valley, Sable watched the small figure descend toward the river road and turn, precisely as predicted, not south toward Greyhollow and safety, but north toward the high pass and Widow's Rest.

*You have her stubbornness too,* she thought. *God help us both.*

She let the mortal darkness swallow the traveler before she allowed herself to feel anything. That was discipline learned in a hard school: the Deep Court could read emotion across miles of stone, and though the Regent's reach no longer touched the surface—could not, thank every buried star, touch the surface anymore—old habits were armor, and Sable wore hers everywhere.

Eleven years of exile, and she had finally done it. Found the daughter. Spoken the name aloud.

*I loved her,* she reminded herself. *That's why.*

But love was such a flexible word. Love had stood in the Regent's hall forty years ago and sworn the House of Vael would never interfere with the contract. Love had spent six years beside Maerin and never pressed her, never demanded to see the terms, never followed her down into the dark the second time—the time Maerin went alone, the time she came back grey-faced and silent and started drinking before noon. Love had told itself that whatever was in that contract, Maerin had handled it, had contained it, had paid whatever it cost out of some deep private account Sable had no right to open.

And then the stars had gone out, and Sable had understood, all at once and completely, that Maerin hadn't contained anything. She'd deferred it. Passed it down like an heirloom. Wrapped it in lies and salt and a daughter's trusting love, and left it ticking.

*Every answer costs,* Sable had told the girl, and it was true. What she hadn't said was that the answers cost the giver most of all. Each truth she surrendered brought the Regent's attention one degree closer to the crack in the mountain where an exile might be hiding. Each truth moved Vesper Halloran one step further along a road that ended, Sable was nearly certain, either in the soul-vats or in front of a throne.

Unless the girl found something in that ledger that Maerin had hidden even from the court. Unless the coward's final trick was cleverer than the court's hunger.

Sable turned north herself, keeping to the ridgeline, moving with the particular silence of someone who had spent her life among listeners. Below, the world lay starless and blind, and somewhere beneath ten thousand feet of stone, a stolen song was tearing a crown apart, thread by thread.

She had perhaps a week before the Regent's brokers came looking for the last Halloran.

She intended to arrive first—but arriving first, she admitted to the empty dark, had never once been the same as being in time.
