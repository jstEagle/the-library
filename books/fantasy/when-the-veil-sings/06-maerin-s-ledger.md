## 6. Maerin's Ledger

The cache was where Sable said it would be, which was the first thing that made Vesper want to put a knife in her.

Not because the exile had lied—but because she hadn't. Because some stranger from beneath the mountain knew her mother's hiding places better than Vesper did, better than anyone on the surface, and that meant Maerin Halloran had spent years whispering to someone below while tucking Vesper into bed above. Three days down the old drover road from Greyhollow, past a shrine to the Veil whose votive candles had all burned out at once, the trail forked at a lightning-split rowan, and there—exactly as Sable had described it, down to the lichen shaped like a kneeling woman—was the door in the hillside.

Vesper stood before it for a long time, letting the mountain wind push at her back like an impatient hand.

The door was stone, cut flush into the slope, so old that the seam had grown a beard of moss. There was no handle. There never needed to be one, because it had never been meant for anyone but a Halloran. She pressed her marked forearm against the cold rock—the scar she'd spent eleven years passing off as shrapnel from a crossbow bolt—and felt the old familiar prickle of recognition travel up through skin and bone.

*Come in, little bird,* the door seemed to say, in a voice that hadn't been used in fifteen years.

She went in.

* * *

The cache was smaller than she remembered. That was childhood for you—it turned cellars into cathedrals. A single chamber hewn rough from living granite, maybe ten paces by eight, lit the moment she crossed the threshold by veins of pale luminescence running through the stone itself, the same cold blue-white light as the mark on her arm. Shelves had been carved directly into the walls. A cot with a folded wool blanket. A basin that still held rainwater, impossibly sweet, impossibly clear. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell of her mother: juniper smoke and iron and something floral underneath that Vesper had never been able to name and had stopped trying to name years ago, because naming it hurt.

Maerin Halloran had built this place before Vesper was born. Had stocked it with dried meat wrapped in waxed cloth, with bandages, with coin sewn into oilskin bundles, with weapons cleaned and greased and waiting. A survival cache. A *flight* cache.

Vesper had found it once, at nine years old, following her mother down through a morning fog, and Maerin had caught her wrist at the threshold hard enough to bruise and said, in a voice Vesper had never heard from her before or since: *"Never come here unless I'm dead or the sky is wrong."*

The sky was wrong now. The stars were gone. Her mother had been dead fifteen years.

"Fine," Vesper said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead woman, to whoever was listening. "I'm here."

Her voice came back flat off the stone. Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered anymore—not gods, not mothers, not doors. She started searching.

It took her most of the day. She went through the shelves methodically, the way Tam had taught her to search a room for hidden compartments—corners first, weight second, sound third. Tam. She made herself think his name and keep thinking it, because the alternative was to let the image surface unbidden: Tam signing a contract he couldn't read the fine print of, Tam's grin going slack as the brokers of the Undercourt took hold of whatever it is they take hold of, Tam already flickering somewhere in the dark below like a candle in a draft. Two nights since the stars died. The brokers had told him—had told *her*, shouting across the burning wreckage of the border wards—that he had until the moon returned full. Twenty-six days. Maybe less. Debts accrued interest down there; Sable had said that too, in that voice like water over stones, and Vesper had believed her, because everything Sable said cost something and therefore had the ring of truth.

Under the bottom shelf, behind a false panel that opened at the touch of her scarred arm, she found the papers.

Ledgers. Dozens of them. Contracts, correspondence, maps of passes Vesper had smuggled through a hundred times without knowing her mother had drawn them first. Maerin Halloran—quiet Maerin, who'd mended nets and brewed juniper tea and hummed songs to the hearth—had been the Thornfell's most careful spider. Vesper sat down on the floor amid the paper and began, with hands that would not quite stay steady, to read.

* * *

The first hour gave her her mother's business. The second gave her her mother's war.

There were letters in a cipher Vesper recognized because she'd seen its cousin in Greyhollow—in the ward-keeper's office, on documents stamped with the sigil of the Border Compact. Maerin had corresponded with ward-masters for thirty years. Not as a client. As an *architect*. Page after page of technical argument about anchor points and resonance lines, about where the great wards that kept the Undercourt sealed beneath the mountain should be sunk into stone, how deep, fed by what. Vesper read until the blue light in the walls seemed to dim and brighten with her breathing, and slowly, horribly, a picture assembled itself:

Her mother had helped build the cage. And then her mother had moved to a fishing village two hundred miles away and raised a daughter and never once said a word.

"Why?" Vesper asked the walls. The walls, being walls, declined to answer.

It was near midnight by her reckoning when she found the ledger proper—a slim volume bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its spine cracked white, tucked inside a hollowed-out copy of a book of children's rhymes. Vesper's children's rhymes. She remembered this book. She remembered sitting on Maerin's knee with it, sounding out words while the fire popped, while her mother's arms trembled very slightly around her in a way Vesper had taken for cold and now understood was something else entirely.

The ledger's first page carried no title. Just a date, sixty-one years old, and a line of text in her mother's younger hand—sharper than Vesper remembered it, all edges:

*Bargain concluded beneath the Thornfell, witnessed by root and star. Terms follow.*

And beneath it, the terms.

Vesper read them three times. Then a fourth. Then she set the ledger face-down on the floor and pressed both palms flat against the cold granite and breathed the way Brannoc had taught her to breathe before a fight, slow and counted, because otherwise she was going to scream loud enough to bring the hillside down.

*The Warding of the Thornfell shall bind the Deep Court for the span of five generations.* So far, so much what she'd been raised on—every child in the borderlands grew up on stories of the great binding, the pact that bought the surface its peace. But the stories ended at *peace*. They did not include the price.

*In consideration thereof, the Halloran line, principal and signatory, shall tender payment as follows: one soul of the blood, each generation, freely given to the Court Beneath. First payment due upon the death of the last living signatory.*

The first entry was dated fifty-eight years ago. A name. *Corwin Halloran, aged nineteen. Tendered.*

Vesper had never heard the name. She searched her memory—grandfathers, uncles, cousins, the scattered Hallorans she'd been told had died of fevers, of accidents, of the sea. *Aged nineteen.* Corwin Halloran had been nineteen years old, and he had not died of anything. He had been *tendered*, like a bill, like a shipment, and his soul had gone down beneath the mountain to feed the wards—or to feed whatever actually ate the payments, because Sable's voice was in her head again now, saying: *the wards don't consume souls, Vesper. Wards are locks, not mouths. Ask yourself who holds the keys.*

She turned pages with fingers gone numb. Each generation had its own section. Names she didn't know, then names she did—distant kin, a great-aunt supposedly lost crossing a river, a cousin who'd "gone south to seek his fortune" and never written. Each entry in her mother's hand. Each one annotated with dates, locations, and small, terrible marginal notes in a script that got tighter and more cramped as the years went on:

*Tendered willingly under misapprehension. Regret noted.*
*Brokered through the Salt Road brokers. Payment confirmed by the court's light.*
*He fought. It made no difference. It never makes any difference.*

And then, forty years ago, a new heading, and Vesper's whole body went cold around her spine:

*Second covenant. The line's debt continues, but the court has agreed to defer collection in exchange for services: maintenance of the Warding, and the bearing of a voice-bearer. The Veil requires a mortal anchor in every generation. This duty falls to me. In exchange, my daughter will be passed over. Once.*

*Once.*

Vesper read the word until it stopped looking like a word. Her mother had been the Veil's voice-bearer. Not Vesper. Maerin had carried the god's song, had anchored the wards with it, had negotiated and bargained and paid and prayed—and in exchange, she'd been promised her daughter would be spared the family tax. Passed over. *Once.*

Except Maerin had died fifteen years ago, and a dead woman could neither maintain a warding nor bear a god's voice. And the covenant, Vesper saw now, flipping forward with dread arriving ahead of her eyes like thunder ahead of lightning—*the covenant contained a default clause.* Of course it did. Everything with the Undercourt contained a default clause. She'd read enough contracts in Greyhollow's back rooms to know that you always buried the teeth in the middle, where tired eyes slid past them.

*Should the voice-bearer fail—by death, silence, or renunciation—all deferrals are void, and collection reverts to the original terms: one soul per generation, next of blood, together with all collateral tendered against the line's account.*

Next of blood. Together with all collateral.

Tam.

Vesper closed her eyes and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the night the stars went out. Tam had signed with the Undercourt's brokers to cover the company's losses—losses from a caravan run that had gone wrong, losses that had piled up because for three years the company's routes had curdled, their luck had soured, their margins had bled. Losses that had begun, if she traced the ledger's grim accounting honestly, right around the time the Veil went silent. Right around the time Maerin's deferral collapsed and the debt reverted and the Halloran account came due. The brokers hadn't come for Vesper because they couldn't find her—or because they'd found something easier. A sworn brother with a warm heart and bad arithmetic who loved her enough to sign his name without reading the teeth in the middle.

They hadn't taken Tam instead of her. They'd taken him *as* her. Collateral against the Halloran account. And her name—the ledger said so, in her mother's hand, in an addendum dated two weeks before Maerin's death, the ink blotched as though water had fallen on it—her name was next.

*I have tried to buy her free,* the final entry read. *The court does not sell what it has already tasted. If you are reading this, Vesper, then the sky has gone wrong and I am beyond helping you, and I am sorry. I am sorry for the debt. I am sorry for the silence. I am sorry that I let you believe your mark was only a scar. Find Sable of the Deep Court. She is the only one down there who ever loved anything besides power. Trust her exactly as far as you must and no further. And Vesper—when they offer you a bargain, remember that you are a Halloran, and we do not pay what we are asked. We pay what we choose.*

Below that, a single line, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*The Veil is not silent. It is being eaten. Listen for the singing.*

* * *

Vesper didn't remember getting to her feet. She was standing, though, in the middle of her mother's tomb of secrets, and the ledger was shaking in her grip, and the blue veins in the walls had begun to pulse—slowly, faintly, like something sleeping turning over.

Betrayal, she thought, should feel hot. That's what the ballads said. Betrayal was a knife between the shoulders, a burning brand, a shout. Nobody wrote ballads about this—about betrayal as *architecture*. About discovering that the floor you'd stood on your whole life had been a trapdoor all along, laid by the person who loved you most, who had walked across it herself for forty years praying it would never spring. Maerin hadn't sold her. Worse: Maerin had *budgeted* for her. Had weighed one daughter's soul against the peace of the borderlands and the survival of a line and the hunger of a court, and had done the sums, and had signed.

And had spent her whole life afterward humming songs to a child by the fire with her arms trembling.

"I would have paid it," Vesper said to the empty room. Her voice cracked in the middle and she hated it. "If you'd asked me. I would have—"

But no. That was a lie, and lying to a dead woman in her own house was a poor way to honor her. Vesper wouldn't have paid it. At nineteen, at twenty-five, she'd have run like a hare, and Maerin had known it, and had built the false panel and hidden the ledger and chosen the lie because the lie let her daughter grow up laughing.

The pulsing light in the walls steadied. Almost, Vesper thought, like a breath drawn to speak.

She looked down at the ledger's last page, at the final line her mother had left. *Listen for the singing.* Then at her own arm, where the scar sat quiet and dead as it had been for three years, and she thought about caves, and mountains, and roads leading east toward the Thornfell's roots, and a man named Tam who had twenty-six days, minus two, minus however long she'd already wasted reading paper in a hole in the ground.

She took everything. The ledgers, the letters, the coin, the blades, the waxed meat—everything that fit in a smuggler's pack, because a Halloran did not leave resources behind, even inheritance-shaped ones, even traps. At the threshold she paused with one hand on the stone and looked back at the little room where her mother's whole double life lay stripped bare, and she said, quietly:

"You should have told me. I'd have hated you for a year and then understood." She shouldered the pack. "Instead I get to hate you forever and understand anyway."

Behind her, as the stone door ground shut, she thought—for just a heartbeat, just long enough to doubt it later—that the blue light flared once, like a struck match. Like a voice starting to say something it never got to finish.

Outside, the wind had turned. It came up from the east now, from the deep roots of the Thornfell, and it carried a smell of wet stone and distant torch-smoke, and threaded through it—so faint she might have imagined it, might have invented it out of grief and ledger-ink and wanting—something that was almost a note. Almost a melody. Almost a voice, singing low beneath the world.

Sable was waiting at the fork in the road, leaning against the split rowan as if she'd grown there, silver-eyed in the starless dark.

"You found it," she said. Not a question.

Vesper tightened the pack straps across her shoulders and looked at the woman who had known her mother's secrets, who had loved her mother—maybe—who had lied to her mother's daughter for reasons not yet priced. "We need to talk," she said, "about exactly how much you're not telling me."

Sable smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Walk, then," she said, gesturing east, toward the villages, toward the mountain. "We have twenty-four days, and the road goes through places where you'll wish I'd told you less."

They walked. And behind them, in the hillside, the blue light in Maerin's cache pulsed on and on in the dark, keeping time with something far below that was beginning, very slowly, to sing.
