## 7. The Hollow Pilgrims

They left Greyhollow before dawn, when the ward-keeper's bribed silence was still warm and the mountains were nothing but a blacker shade of dark.

Vesper walked point out of habit, reading the road the way other people read letters—by feel, by the angle of ruts in frozen mud, by which way the crows were going. The road east from Greyhollow climbed through pine shadow and then spilled down into the first of the valley farms, terraced fields stitched to the hillsides like someone had sewn the mountain a coat too small for it.

"You're quiet," Sable said behind her. Of course she was quiet; Vesper had heard her coming for half a mile, which was itself strange. Most people made more noise than they thought they did. Sable made exactly as much as she chose.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always performing quiet. It's different." Sable came up beside her, hands folded inside a grey traveling cloak that probably cost more than Brannoc's horse and looked like it cost nothing at all. "You've been chewing on something since the wards fell."

Vesper kept walking. "The wards fell because the debt came due," she said. "Your words. So tell me what comes next, since you seem so eager to sell me answers one piece at a time."

"The next thing you need to know is about three hours down this road." Sable nodded ahead, toward where the valley opened up around a river gone thin and glassy with winter. "And it isn't an answer I can sell you. It's one you have to look at."

* * *

The village was called Merrow's Bend, according to the leaning signpost, though no one Vesper asked seemed to know why. She'd stopped asking by midmorning anyway.

The people came out to the fields at first light—all of them, every soul in the village, young and old, moving in a rhythm so smooth it took her a quarter mile of watching to understand what was wrong with it. They didn't hurry. They didn't lag. They didn't talk. Forty-some people turning frozen earth and hauling stone and mending terrace walls, working like water finding its level, and not one word between them.

"Plague?" Vesper said quietly.

"No."

"A funeral. A fast. Some—"

"Sable." One of them had noticed them on the road—a woman, broad-shouldered, her sleeves rolled despite the cold—and crossed the field toward the fence with that same unhurried glide. Up close, Vesper saw her face was weathered and ordinary, a farmer's face, laugh lines cut deep around her mouth as if she had once laughed all the time. Her eyes were the problem. They were open, and aware, and looking directly at Vesper, and there was nothing behind them looking back. Like windows in a house where the fire had gone out long ago but someone still swept the floors.

"Travelers," the woman said. Her voice was pleasant. Warm, even. "Are you needing lodging? We've beds. The harvest's stored. You'd be welcome."

"That's kind of you," Vesper managed. "We're for the mountain. Just passing."

"The mountain." The woman smiled, and the smile fit her face perfectly and meant nothing at all. "Work is good this year. The leases are fair."

"Thank you," Sable said smoothly, stepping half in front of Vesper. "We won't keep you from your fields."

The woman nodded, turned, and walked back to her furrow with the same fluid calm, taking up her work exactly where she'd left it, as if conversation were a stone she'd set down and could pick up again whenever required.

Vesper stood at the fence a long moment. "What was taken from her?"

"Everything you'd call *her*." Sable's voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice of someone handling something sharp-edged in the dark. "Memories. Desires. The part that wants anything. The court calls it shearing. They take the self whole and leave the body able to work. Then—" she gestured at the terraces, the mended walls, the neat stacks of firewood, the children playing a clapping game in perfect unison by a well, laughing at exactly the right intervals— "they lease the bodies back to the surface. Labor, guaranteed docile. No hunger strikes. No runaways. No grief when one dies; the neighbors just fill in the row."

"They laughed," Vesper said. "Those children."

"Court craft leaves the shape of things. A smile is muscle. Laughter is breath and sound. It costs them nothing to leave the shell of joy if the joy itself goes into the vat." Sable watched the woman in the field. "She has children somewhere, I'd guess. She might even be one of those children's mothers. But ask her if she loves them and she'll say love is a fine thing, the leases are fair, would you like lodging?"

Vesper's hand had found her mark without her noticing—the old scar along her forearm, the one she'd spent years passing off as a rope burn. Under her glove, it ached dully, the way it had ached for three years of silence. Now the ache sharpened, almost like attention.

"We're leaving," she said.

"Yes."

"And we're not staying at any inn in this valley. We camp cold."

"Yes," Sable said again, softer. And then, because she apparently couldn't help herself: "Now you understand a little better why the peace held. Look at how well everything runs."

* * *

They camped that night in a stand of birch above the road, cold by choice, a small fire screened by rock because Vesper refused the alternative and Sable hadn't argued. Vesper took first watch because she didn't trust her own head enough to sleep in it yet.

The images wouldn't stop arranging themselves. The woman's smile. The children clapping in time. A granary they'd passed at noon, full to the roof beams, tended by men who worked without a single spoken instruction. The peace Vesper had grown up under—the long decades since the last border war, the safe roads her company had grown rich running—she had always thought of it as weather. Just the way the world was. Now she could see the machinery under it, and the machinery was made of people.

Her mother had brokered the binding. Sable had said it plainly on the road out of Greyhollow: *Maerin Halloran wrote the terms.* Which meant her mother had known about this. Had priced it. Had signed her name under it.

"Ask me," Sable said from across the coals. She wasn't sleeping either; Vesper doubted she truly did, or needed to. "I can hear you building the question. Ask it now while it's still honest."

"What did my mother know?"

"Everything." Sable said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "She negotiated the exemptions. Certain bloodlines exempted from levy. Certain villages bought free with payments in gold instead of souls. Your family's name is written in the front of that ledger, Vesper, not the back. Maerin Halloran wasn't trapped by the contract she inherited. She helped write the one your generation inherited."

"She was twenty-three years old and trying to stop a war."

"She was twenty-three years old and she chose who paid. That's what power is. I don't say it to wound you." Sable fed a twig to the fire. "I say it because tomorrow we pass through Ashenford, and Ashenford is one of the villages your mother *bought*. Exempt. Free. Its people are loud and greedy and alive and they will thank the Halloran name if you give it, and you will have to decide whether to let them. That's the answer you actually came down this road to find. Not whether Maerin knew. Whether knowing changes what you do."

Vesper stared into the coals until her eyes burned. Somewhere below in the dark, faintly, she could hear the village of Merrow's Bend—not voices, never voices—but the rhythmic knock of wood being split, unhurried, tireless, patient as dripping water. Working through the night. There was no reason to split wood in the dark unless the body simply did not know how to stop.

"I prayed to the Veil for three years," she heard herself say. "Every night. Silence. I thought I'd done something wrong. I thought I'd been unworthy." Her thumb moved over the scar. "All that time, this is what the world looked like underneath. Maybe the god went quiet because there was nothing left worth saying."

"Don't." For the first time since Greyhollow, something moved behind Sable's composure—quick, fierce, gone again. "Don't you dare make the god's silence your guilt too. You'll drown in it, and I need you walking." She paused. "The Veil didn't go silent because it stopped caring. Remember that much, whatever else you decide to believe about me."

* * *

Ashenford was everything Merrow's Bend was not, and it was worse.

It was loud. Market-day loud, dogs and haggling and a fiddle badly played in a doorway, children shrieking through the lanes with the specific lawless joy of children whose parents were too busy to care. Smoke rose fat from chimneys. An old man argued cheerfully with a fishmonger about the honesty of trout. Every face Vesper passed was animated, opinionated, gloriously, wastefully alive.

And over the well in the square hung a carved wooden plaque, painted in faded blue: *By covenant and gratitude — the freedom of Ashenford, purchased and renewed, in the year of the Binding.* Below the words, in older paint, a name. *M. Halloran.*

"They renew it every seven years," Sable said quietly beside her. "A payment goes south, into the mountain, and a new seal comes up. Gold, not souls. Your mother's terms. As long as the payment's met."

Vesper barely heard her. A woman at a stall of dyed wool had caught sight of the name on her face—the way Vesper was staring at the plaque—and followed her gaze, and laughed.

"Halloran girl? You've the look of her, the jaw. Gods' teeth, come here—" The woman bustled around her stall, wiping her hands on her apron, beaming. "Maerin Halloran. My grandmother used to tell us about her. Came through the year I was born, they say, riding a half-dead horse and arguing with court brokers twice her size. Bought our names out of the dark with her own coin, some of it. My gran kissed her boots and Maerin made her stop, said boots were clean that day." The woman's eyes shone. "You'll take bread. Don't argue. House rule for Hallorans."

Vesper took the bread. It was warm, and it weighed nothing and everything, and she stood in the market square holding it while people pressed close—someone wanted to shake her hand, someone else had a story about the year the payment nearly failed and riders came up from the canyon mouth and how the whole town had stood in this square holding candles until word came that the seal was renewed. Alive. All of them so loudly, stubbornly alive, and every one of them alive because a ledger elsewhere had their village's name in the *safe* column.

Which meant somewhere else, in the columns that weren't safe, other names balanced theirs out. Merrow's Bend's names. Names nobody had ridden a half-dead horse for.

She ate the bread because refusing it would have been its own kind of lie. It tasted like nothing she'd ever eaten before. It tasted like a price.

That night, camping outside Ashenford's bright window-glow rather than accept its beds, she sat sharpening a knife she didn't need sharpened and finally said what had been sitting in her chest since morning.

"My mother saved this town. And she did it by agreeing to the machine that emptied Merrow's Bend. Both true. Both her." The whetstone whispered. "Sable. When you loved her—which you did, don't bother rearranging your face—did she ever talk about it? About the ones who weren't exempt?"

Sable was silent long enough that Vesper looked up. In the firelight the exiled noble looked, for once, exactly her age—old in the way stone is old, worn smooth by water that never stops falling.

"She talked about nothing else, near the end," Sable said. "She kept a second ledger. Unofficial. Every village that paid in souls, she recorded the number. Not to balance it—there was no balancing it. She said someone should at least know the count. She called them pilgrims, the hollowed ones. Said they were the only honest worshippers left in the world, because they'd given everything and gotten nothing back." Her voice roughened. "She made me promise to remember the count if anything happened to her. I remembered. That's most of why I'm exiled, if you want the truth of it. Asking questions about the vats is poor politics for a house already falling."

"The count," Vesper repeated. "Where is it?"

"In the cache. With the official ledger. She hid them together—your mother had a taste for irony."

*Two days,* Vesper thought. Two days' hard travel to the cache, then the long climb to the gate of teeth. She looked east, where Thornfell's massif shouldered up against the sky, blacker than the night around it. Above its highest ridge, where stars should have been thick as spilled salt, there was only void. Three nights now, and she still flinched at it. The whole sky wounded, and everyone pretending not to see.

Then, because the night was very still and the mountain was very far and something in her blood had begun listening whether she willed it or not, Vesper heard it.

Not with her ears. With the scar.

A thread of sound rising from beneath the earth—thin as wire, sweet as a voice through a wall, a melody in a mode no surface music used, repeating and repeating like something singing to keep from forgetting itself. Her mark burned cold and hot at once. Across the fire, Sable had gone rigid, head tilted, and Vesper realized she heard it too.

"It's the god," Vesper breathed. "It's *singing*."

"No," Sable said softly, and her face in the firelight was terrible with knowledge. "That's not the god, Vesper. That's what's left of the voices it lost. And if we can hear them from here—" she looked east at the starless mountain, and finished, very quietly, "—then the vaults are fuller than even I believed. Move faster tomorrow. Whatever's left of your brother is in there learning that song."
