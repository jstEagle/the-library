## 9. The Singing Cave

The rain came down the mountain like a verdict.

Vesper had watched it build all afternoon—a bruise-black wall crawling down the western face of Thornfell, swallowing the terraced villages one by one until it swallowed the road itself. By the time Sable pointed her riding crop at a cleft in the rock above the treeline, the light had gone the color of old pewter and Vesper's oilskin was losing its argument with the weather.

"There," Sable said. "A pilgrim shelter. Old."

"Old how?"

"Old enough that the stone remembers what it's for." Sable urged her grey mare up a switchback of loose shale, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had walked these mountains before they had names. "Which is more than I can say for most things up here."

Vesper followed. She'd learned three days ago that arguing with Sable about the route wasted breath she might need later. The woman navigated like water finding a downhill—no hesitation, no second-guessing—and every time Vesper asked how she knew, Sable answered with some variation of *I lived below* or *my house kept these roads* or simply a look that said *you are not ready for that answer.*

The cleft opened into a cavern mouth tall as a chapel door, its rim carved with figures worn nearly smooth by centuries of wind. Vesper ran her gloved fingers over one as she led her horse inside: a human shape, arms raised, mouth open. Singing, maybe. Or screaming. Weather made no distinction.

Inside, the cavern went back farther than her lantern could reach. Someone had built dry-stone walls across part of it once—a hermit's cell, long abandoned—but the heart of the space was raw mountain, ceiling lost in darkness that drank their lamplight whole. The air smelled of wet granite and something else, faintly mineral, faintly sweet, like the inside of a bell.

"Pilgrims used to stop here on the way down," Sable said, unsaddling her mare with brisk efficiency. "When pilgrimage to the Undercourt was still permitted. Before the Regent decided petitioners were more useful as decoration." She hung the saddle over a shelf of stone that had been polished smooth by generations of saddles before it. "There will be niches in the back wall. Offerings. If any silver remains, don't touch it. Dead men's coin has poor manners."

"You say things like that," Vesper said, driving her picket pin into a crack in the floor for her own mare, "and then refuse to explain them."

"I explain everything eventually. It's one of my flaws."

"It's not one of your flaws."

"No," Sable agreed, and smiled without warmth. "It isn't."

* * *

They made camp against the hermit's ruined wall because it held heat better than open stone. Vesper got a fire going from damp wood through sheer stubbornness—three years of mountain winters taught you that patience and a knife could coax flame out of almost anything—and set water boiling while Sable laid out their supper of hard bread, smoked goat, and a wedge of cheese so aged it had opinions.

Outside, the storm hammered the mountainside like a creditor at a locked door.

Vesper ate with her back to the wall and her mark turned inward, pressed against her side where Sable couldn't see it. Habit. The scar along her left forearm—the one the company believed came from a barbed arrow at Redwater Ford—had been quiet for three years, but quiet wasn't nothing. Quiet was a presence. She felt it the way an amputee feels a hand: a constant low ache of absence, worse at night, worst when she prayed.

She still prayed. That was the humiliating truth of it. Every night since the stars died, curled into whatever shelter the road provided, she'd pressed her lips to the scar and whispered the old words her mother taught her before Maerin Halloran disappeared—*Veil who walks behind the world, veil who keeps the door, hear your servant walking in the dark*—and every night the silence on the other end had been total. Not the silence of distance. She knew distance; she'd carried messages across two hundred miles of pass and felt the god's attention flicker at the far edge of it, faint as warmth through a wall. This was the silence of an empty room.

Or, she thought now, watching Sable feed the fire with the economy of someone who never did anything twice, the silence of a room with someone standing behind the door.

"Ask," Sable said, without looking up.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your jaw does this thing when you're composing a question you're afraid of. You get it from her."

Vesper's hand closed around the bread until it crumbled. "Don't."

Sable looked up then. Firelight moved across her face and for once didn't find any irony there. "Maerin never asked questions either, until the end. Then she asked all of them at once, and there wasn't time." She turned a flat stone over near the flames, adjusting the heat. "So ask the small ones now, while we have small hours to waste. That's not cruelty, Halloran. That's arithmetic."

Vesper chewed on the question she'd been carrying since Greyhollow, turning it over like a coin she suspected was clipped. Finally: "You loved her. My mother. You said it on the road—you said it like a fact, the way you say everything. What I want to know is whether she loved you back, or whether you were just another debt collector wearing a kinder face."

The question landed. Vesper watched it land—watched Sable's hands go still on the flat stone, watched something move behind her eyes like a fish under ice.

"She loved me," Sable said quietly. "For eleven months, which below is a season and above is a lifetime. And then the contract came due, and she chose her daughter over her lover, and she was right to." She resumed turning the stone. Her knuckles were white around it. "I've had sixty years to decide how I feel about being right. I'm still deciding."

"Sixty years." Vesper stared. "You don't look—"

"Below, we measure differently. Time pools. Don't change the subject."

"That *was* the subject."

"The subject," Sable said, "was your god."

And there it was—the thing Vesper hadn't asked, circling back like a hawk. She fed another branch to the fire and made herself hold the silence until it became a weapon instead of a wound.

"Fine," she said at last. "Here's what I can't make fit. You told me the court devours voices. You told me the Regent wears a crown of stolen starlight. But my mark—" she almost showed her, almost pushed back her sleeve, and stopped herself with the words half-swallowed. "My god went silent three years ago. Three years. If the Regent stole its voice, why did it take so long for everything to break? Why do the wards only fail *now?*"

"Because theft takes time," Sable said. "And because a voice doesn't die when you swallow it. It drowns." She looked into the fire. "You've heard a singer drown, haven't you? On the surface. Water in the lungs. They keep trying."

Vesper's spine went cold despite the fire.

She had heard that. A smuggler named Corvin, pulled from the Whitemelt ford, coughing river water for an hour and singing snatches of a ballad between spasms—his body remembering music his mind had drowned in. The company had laughed about it afterward, gently, the way you laugh about the dead.

"Why would you tell me that?" she whispered.

"Because tonight you'll want to know it's possible," Sable said, "to be drowning and still singing."

* * *

The storm worsened after dark.

Vesper took first watch because sleep had become a country she could visit but not live in. She sat near the cavern mouth with her cloak around her shoulders and her sword across her knees, watching lightning stitch the black peaks together, listening to rain fall in sheets and thunder roll down the valleys like boulders loosed from a height.

The scar ached.

Not the old dull absence-ache. Something sharper. Warmer. She rubbed it absently through her sleeve and frowned, because the warmth was increasing, degree by degree, like a hand laid over hers.

She told herself it was the fire. The fire was fifteen feet away and behind her. She told herself it was her blood. Her blood didn't pulse in rhythm.

Because that was the thing—once she noticed it, she couldn't unnotice it. The warmth rose and fell. Slow. Steady. Patient. Like breathing. Like—

Like a refrain.

Vesper got to her feet. Her body moved before her mind authorized it, drawing her deeper into the cavern, away from the storm-noise, past the dead fire where Sable lay sleeping with one hand on her belt knife even in dreams. The warmth in her arm strengthened with every step, and underneath it, so faint she couldn't have sworn to hearing it rather than imagining it, came a sound.

She stopped at the hermit's ruined cell and pressed her palm flat against the back wall. Cold stone. And through the cold stone, rising from somewhere impossibly far below—from under the roots of the mountain, under the bedrock, under the world—

*singing.*

It wasn't a melody, exactly. It was the memory of one. It was what a melody becomes after years underwater: the bones of a song, the shape of it, notes surfacing one at a time like bubbles from a drowning throat. A woman's voice, or something that had once been a woman's voice, or a thousand voices worn down to a single thread of sound, rising through solid rock the way water rises through chalk.

Vesper slid down the wall until she was kneeling.

"*Veil,*" she breathed, and her lips found the scar through the fabric of her sleeve, and the words came out cracked and childish, the prayer of a child at her mother's knee: "*Veil who walks behind the world. Veil who keeps the door.*"

The song stuttered.

Changed.

Swelled toward her—not louder, exactly, but *closer*, the way a candle flame leans toward a draft—and the warmth in her arm blazed, and for one impossible moment Vesper felt the wall against her palm grow warm as living skin, felt the stone itself lean into her touch like a starving animal into a kind hand, and the thread of song wound around her name—

*ves-per*

—her name, in her mother's cadence, in a voice she had spent three years mourning, sung from beneath a mountain—

Then it was gone. Snuffed like a pinched wick. The stone went cold. The cavern went silent except for the storm and her own ragged breathing and the sound of Sable's boots on rock behind her.

"How long," Sable said softly, "have you been able to hear it?"

Vesper didn't turn around. Her eyes were burning and she refused to let this woman watch them burn. "Three years it's been silent. Three years I prayed into a well with no bottom. Tonight it sang my *name*, Sable. My mother's voice sang my name from inside the rock, and if you tell me that was a trick of the acoustics I will—"

"It wasn't a trick." Sable crouched beside her. In the dark her face had lost all its polish; she looked suddenly ancient, suddenly tired, suddenly like someone carrying sixty years of a decision she was still making. "It was leaking. Three years ago the Regent finished sealing the Veil's voice into the crown—sealed it so completely that not even a whisper escaped. But seals crack, Halloran. Everything cracks. Especially things built on stolen foundations." She reached out, hesitated, and touched the back wall with two fingers, the way you'd check a fevered brow. "The crown is failing. The stars going out—that wasn't an attack. That was the first shudder of a machine tearing itself apart. And tonight, through the deepest stone, through the oldest shelter, the prisoner started singing through the cracks."

"The prisoner." Vesper's laugh came out broken. "You're saying my god is a prisoner."

"I'm saying your god is drowning," Sable said. "And still singing. Just like I promised you it was possible to do." She paused. "And I'm saying that a drowning voice grows quieter every year, and a seal that cracks once will crack wider, and whatever window exists between those two facts—" she met Vesper's eyes, and her voice dropped to something raw and urgent, "—is closing. Faster than either of us would like."

Vesper pressed her forehead against the cold stone. Above her, the worn carving of the figure with the open mouth seemed suddenly less like a screamer and more like a listener—someone bent close to the rock, drinking in whatever leaked up from below.

"They can hear it down there," she whispered. "The hollowed. In the villages. Can't they. That's why they stand in the fields at night. That's why they turn their faces to the mountain. It's not worship. It's—they're listening for the same thing I just heard."

"Now you understand what the peace cost," Sable said. "Now you understand what she paid, and why, and what's still owed."

Vesper lifted her head. Through her sleeve, the scar still glowed faintly warm, fading now, like embers under ash—but not gone. Never quite gone.

"Then we leave at first light," she said. "Storm or no storm. I don't care if we have to climb the teeth off that canyon with our fingernails. There is a door down there, Sable. There's always a door. Show me which way."

Sable studied her for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted—something that might have been grief, or pride, or fear for her.

"There's a canyon," she said finally, "two days east, where the mountain opens its mouth. The locals call it Gate of Teeth." She rose, pulling her cloak around her shoulders. "Sleep, Halloran. Tomorrow you'll need your voice."

Vesper slept sitting up against the singing wall, one hand pressed flat to the stone, and if the god spoke again in the dark hours, she heard it only in dreams—a thread of song, rising, patient, waiting for her to come down and listen properly.

Outside, the storm broke at dawn, and the eastern sky over Thornfell cleared to show the place where the mountain's ridgeline jagged downward like a jaw full of stone teeth, waiting.
