## 10. Gate of Teeth

The mountain had been growing in the window of the world for two days, and by the third morning Vesper stopped pretending she wasn't afraid of it.

It didn't loom the way mountains were supposed to. It *waited*. The massif rose out of the high moorland like a jaw set into the earth, black granite streaked with veins of something that caught the light and refused to give it back. The locals called it the Crown, or sometimes just Him, as if it were a person, and Vesper understood why. From a distance it had the posture of a seated figure—broad shoulders of scree, a head of snow that never melted, a throat of shadow where no river ran.

"That's not a pass," she said, reining up at the edge of the last village. "That's a mouth."

"Accurate." Sable sat her grey mare like she'd been born in the saddle and hated every minute of it, spine straight as a lance, gloved hands light on the reins. Three days on the road had worn the polish off her exile's elegance but none of her edges. She still spoke like each word had been weighed on jeweler's scales before release. "The old maps call it the Ammon. The gate itself is called the Teeth. You'll see why."

"I've seen enough already."

"No," Sable said quietly, "you haven't. That's rather the point."

Vesper let the silence sit between them while she checked the girth strap—a habit, since there was nothing left to steal this far north and no one left to steal from. The hollowed village behind them was already shrinking into the morning haze: forty souls' worth of bodies sweeping streets no one visited, tending gardens they would never taste, smiling at nothing with mouths that moved like puppet jaws. One woman had paused in her weeding as they rode past, and for half a heartbeat her empty eyes had *focused*, and her cracked lips had shaped a single word—*down*—before the emptiness closed over her again like water.

Vesper hadn't slept since.

She'd slept less after the cave. Two nights ago, sheltering under stone that hummed like a struck bell, she'd heard the song—the first thread of the Veil's voice in three years of dead silence—and her mark had warmed against her skin like a hand remembered. Hope was a worse torment than grief. Grief let you sleep.

"Tell me again what happens at the gate," she said. "All of it. No jeweler's scales."

Sable's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You declare yourself. Your name, your line, your claim. Aloud, before the Teeth, so that the gate—and everything behind it—hears you and cannot pretend otherwise afterward. The Undercourt runs on declaration. Words spoken at the threshold are binding. Older than the Regent. Older than most of what lives down there."

"And once I've said it?"

"Then you're not a smuggler poking at a locked door. You're a petitioner of the Halloran line, come to contest Maerin Blackwood's contract, and the court must receive you. Must give you audience. It's the one law they've never dared break, because their whole edifice stands on the sanctity of bargain." Sable's eyes, pale as frost over slate, held hers. "It also means every noble, every broker, every thing with ears in those vaults will know your name before nightfall. Including the ones who sold Tam. Including the Regent."

"Including whatever's waiting to collect my mother's debt."

"Yes."

Vesper looked at the mountain. Somewhere inside that dark throat, Tam was being eaten one memory at a time—his laugh first, probably, he'd have fought hardest for that—and every hour she spent gathering courage was an hour shaved off whatever remained of him.

"Good," she said.

Sable raised an eyebrow.

"Let them hear me coming." She kneed her horse forward. "I'm done knocking politely on doors that eat people."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, following a track that wasn't a road so much as an argument the mountain had lost—switchbacks stitched across the lower slopes, cairns marking a path that pilgrims had worn smooth centuries ago and the world had since agreed to forget. The air changed as they rose. Warmer, somehow, though the snowline climbed above them, carrying a smell Vesper couldn't place until Sable named it.

"Ozone and honey," the exile said, nostrils flaring. "Court-scent. The vaults breathe it up through the stone. You'll smell it stronger below."

"It smells like my mother's trunk." The words came out before Vesper could stop them. "The one she kept locked. I used to press my face against the seam when I was small and breathe it in. She'd catch me and go white as salt and lock it somewhere new."

Sable said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped its careful lacquer. "She kept the contract in that trunk. And a dress I liked very much, and a bottle of Thornfell red she swore she'd share with me when the debt was done." A pause. "She never opened the bottle."

"You loved her." Not a question. Vesper had assembled it over three days of roads—from Sable's flinches, her silences, the way she spoke Maerin's name like a bruise she kept pressing to prove it still hurt.

"I loved her," Sable said, "and I let her walk into that court alone because my house told me to, and I have spent twenty years telling myself obedience was the price of survival." Her gloved hands tightened on the reins. "So understand what I am offering you, Halloran, and what I am not. I am not offering you clean help. Everything I do serves two purposes, and one of them is mine. When you learn what it is—and you will—I want you to remember that I warned you here, first, honestly."

"There it is," Vesper said. "The jeweler's scales."

"The truth weighs what it weighs."

"Then weigh this too." Vesper turned in the saddle, close enough to see the fine exhaustion under Sable's eyes, the exile's beauty gone gaunt with purpose. "When your second purpose shows its teeth—and it will—I'll cut around it. I won't hate you for it. But I won't thank you either, and I won't follow you past the point where Tam's life ends up paying for your house's future. Say yes to that, and we ride on."

The wind came down off the snowfield, cold and mineral. Sable held her gaze for five heartbeats, six, seven.

"Yes," she said. "Agreed."

Vesper nodded and faced front. Above them, through a ragged notch in the ridge, she saw the Teeth.

* * *

The canyon opened like a wound.

No—like a mouth. That was the honest image, and the gate demanded honesty; she understood that now. Two walls of black stone parted ahead of them, fifty feet tall and closing overhead, jagged along their upper edges where the rock had shattered and healed and shattered again. Between them ran a floor of pale gravel that crunched with a sound like teeth grinding. The court-scent rolled out thick as fog, ozone and honey and beneath both something older—candle smoke, grave-dirt, the ghost of incense burned at funerals for gods.

And ringing the mouth of it, in tiers carved into both walls, stood the petitioners.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Figures of grey stone frozen mid-gesture—kneeling, arms raised, faces upturned, hands pressed flat against the rock as if shoving against a door. They lined the approach in ranks, each on its own plinth, each wearing the eroded remains of clothes and armor and crowns. Weather had taken their features, mostly. Enough remained.

A soldier with his sword driven into the ground before him, both hands still resting on the pommel, head bowed—as if surrendering had been the last honest act of his life. A merchant woman clutching a ledger to her chest, her stone fingers sunk into pages that would never turn. A child, small on a low plinth near the path's edge, one arm stretched toward the dark, and Vesper made herself look away because looking was a wound.

"They're not dead," Sable said softly. She had dismounted; her voice echoed strangely between the walls, arriving twice, once from her lips and once from everywhere. "That's what the surface stories get wrong. Petrification is the court's mercy—or its cruelty, depending which end of it you stand on. Petitioners who make their declaration and then lose their nerve are held. Preserved. Until they find the courage to finish speaking, or the mountain decides they never will." She touched the soldier's stone boot, almost gently. "Some of these have been deciding for nine hundred years."

Vesper dismounted too. Her legs felt borrowed. "And if I freeze?"

"Then in nine hundred years, some other Halloran's daughter will climb this road and wonder who you were." Sable met her eyes. "But you won't freeze. I watched your mother speak her own declaration on this exact spot, terrified out of her mind, and she did not freeze, and you are more hers than you know."

"You watched?"

"I stood where you're standing." Something flickered across Sable's face—grief, or its fossil. "The gate remembers voices. If you listen, it might play hers back to you. Some petitioners find that kind. Others find it unbearable. I don't know which you'll be."

Vesper walked the gravel alone. She'd told Sable to wait at the threshold's edge—not out of drama, but because whatever came next had to be said by one voice only, unaccompanied, the way prayers are said and confessions and vows. Her boots crunched. The stone figures watched her pass with their weathered eyes, and she found herself nodding to them, one by one, soldier and merchant and the child she couldn't quite look at—an old caravan habit, acknowledging the dead so they wouldn't think themselves unseen.

Halfway up the approach, the gate began to murmur.

Not wind. Voices. Layered and thin, like sound leaking through a wall—fragments of declarations spoken across centuries, overlapping, dissolving. *...come to reclaim what was pawned... ...swear by my house and its nine generations... ...my brother's body, return my brother's body... ...I withdraw, I withdraw, I withdr—*

That one cut off. Vesper glanced sideways at a figure she'd passed without seeing: a young man, stone arms still raised in the shape of speech, mouth open on a word nine hundred years unfinished. *I withdraw.* He'd tried to take it back. The gate was holding him to the grammar of courage.

Her mark woke.

Warmth spread up her forearm from the scar-shaped sigil she'd passed off as battle damage for fifteen years, and with the warmth came a pressure behind her sternum, like a hand pressing flat, like the god leaning close. Not words. Never words, not anymore. But a presence, attentive, the way a listener leans in when the story reaches the part about them.

*Say it true,* the warmth seemed to say. *Whatever else you do in there, say it true.*

"Yeah," Vesper murmured. "Working on it."

She reached the center of the approach, where the gravel widened into a circle of pale stone worn concave by centuries of standing feet, and turned to face outward—back the way she'd come, toward the grey sky and the moorland falling away and the whole ordinary world she'd spent her life moving contraband through. Behind her, the dark of the canyon breathed its funeral-honey breath.

The murmur of old voices hushed. Every petrified face seemed to lean in.

Vesper Halloran, who had lied to ward-keepers and tax collectors and customs men and captains and gods, stood in the ring of stone and found her throat full of glass.

Here was the trap in it, she thought. Not the danger—danger she knew how to carry, danger was just weather. The trap was that the words were *true*. She'd built a life on lies that fit like good boots. But this—her name, her line, her blood-price—was the one cargo she'd never chosen to carry, handed down sealed and unlabeled from a woman who'd died owing, and declaring it aloud meant admitting she'd been freight all along. Contraband. Inherited. Sold forward against her will.

Her mark burned.

"Fine," she whispered to it. "Together, then."

She filled her lungs with the cold court-scented air, and spoke, and her voice came out rougher than she intended and she did not soften it:

"My name is Vesper Halloran. Daughter of Maerin Blackwood, daughter of Ilsette Halloran, of the line that bound the wards and paid the wards and bled for the wards across four generations. I come before the court beneath to contest the contract of my mother—to audit it, to challenge it, and to claim what was promised under its terms. My blood is the collateral. My name is the bond. Hear me, gate. Hear me, court." Her voice cracked on the next words, and she let it. "And hear this too: I know what you took. I've come to take him back."

Silence.

Then the gate answered.

Sound rolled down from the walls above her—a deep, grinding chord, stone moving against stone, the two great jaws of the canyon flexing once as if testing the weight of her words and finding them sufficient. Along the tiers, the petrified petitioners did not move, could not move—but the murmur of trapped voices rose all around her, and this time she could pick out the notes within it: approval, or hunger, or both, the way a crowd sounds when the fight is about to start.

The darkness ahead of her shifted. Not lightening—organizing. Far down the throat of the canyon, torches were kindling in sequence, one after another, descending into the earth like a lit fuse running the wrong direction.

Someone had heard her name. Someone was sending an escort.

Sable appeared at her shoulder, having ignored the instruction to wait exactly as Vesper had known she would. Her face in the torchlight was unreadable and very still.

"Well spoken," she said. "Better than your mother's, if it comes to that. She led with the legal language. You led with him." A pause. "They'll have heard that last part especially. 'I know what you took.' The Regent does not enjoy being accused of theft in front of nine hundred years of witnesses."

"Good."

"It isn't good, Vesper. It's *interesting*. And in that court, interesting is the most dangerous thing a petitioner can be." Sable's gloved hand closed briefly on her arm—above the mark, deliberately, as if in salute. "Escort's coming. When they ask who vouches for you, you say my name and my house, and you let the shock do its work. Exiles returning cause commotion. Commotion is cover. Cover is time."

"Time for your second purpose, too."

Sable smiled, thin and true. "Everything serves two purposes down there. That's the first lesson. Here's the second—" The torchlight reached her eyes, and for the first time since Vesper had known her, the exile looked genuinely afraid. "Whatever they offer you at audience—and they will offer, it's how they hunt—they will offer you the thing you most want at the price of the thing you least suspect you value. Your mother took the deal. Understand that. Whatever you decide she was, she *took the deal*."

The first escort torch rounded the bend below, and with it came footsteps—many, unhurried, wrong. Too even. Like things walking to a count.

Vesper straightened her shoulders, touched the warm mark on her arm once, and stepped forward into the mouth of the mountain, toward the light coming up to meet her.

The gate ground shut behind her like a swallow.
