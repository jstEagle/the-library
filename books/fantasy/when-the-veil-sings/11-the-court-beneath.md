## 11. The Court Beneath

The gate closed behind them with a sound like a swallowed word.

Vesper had expected a tunnel. What she got was a throat—a descending passage wide enough to ride six abreast, its walls veined with light that pulsed in slow rhythms, as though the mountain itself were breathing. The stone was not gray here. It was the deep blue-black of a night sky before dawn, and threaded through it ran filaments of pale silver, branching and rebranching like frost grown on glass.

"Starlight," Sable said, without turning. She walked ahead, her exile's traveling cloak traded for something older: a long coat of midnight silk that caught the vault-light and held it, so that she seemed to carry her own small constellation at her back. "Or what's left of it. The court hoards what it steals."

"You said the Regent wears a crown of fused starlight."

"I say many things. You believe very few of them." Sable glanced back, and in the bioluminescent glow her eyes had lost their surface color entirely—just dark, and depth, like looking down a well. "Believe this one. Everything that shines down here shines because something up there stopped shining. Keep your anger close, Halloran. You will need it before you need your sword."

The passage sloped downward for hours. Vesper counted her steps out of old habit—mercenary discipline, Tam's voice in her head saying *a fighter who stops counting stops noticing*—and somewhere past four thousand the walls opened.

She stopped counting.

The first vault of the Undercourt spread beneath her like a city seen from a mountaintop, except there was no mountain above it, only stone worked into a ceiling so distant and so dark that it might have been sky. Terraces cascaded away below her, carved from the same blue-black rock, each level hung with gardens of luminous fungus in colors she had no names for—violet like bruises, gold like honey held to flame, a green so pale it seemed remembered rather than seen. Water fell in threads from terrace to terrace, and where it fell, it glowed, tracing slow rivers of light through the dark.

It was beautiful.

That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: it was beautiful, and every gorgeous inch of it was lit by theft.

"The soul-vats," Sable said quietly, watching her face. "You've found them already, haven't you? With your mark."

Vesper looked down at her forearm, where the Veil's mark lay quiet under her sleeve—the scar she'd spent years passing off as a knife wound, the brand of a god who had gone silent three years ago. It wasn't quiet now. It was warm. Not painfully, not yet, but warm the way a hearthstone is warm an hour after the fire dies, and it warmed in pulses, and the pulses matched the rhythm of the light in the walls.

"It can feel them," she said. Her voice came out flat, which surprised her; she'd expected it to shake. "The souls."

"They are not hidden. That is the cruelty of this place—or the honesty, depending on how bitter you've become." Sable descended the great stair that spiraled along the vault wall, and Vesper followed because following Sable was still the only plan she had. "The court does not pretend the splendor is free. It simply assumes no one down here remembers what free cost."

* * *

They walked the terraces like pilgrims, hooded against attention, though Vesper noticed quickly that no one paid them any. The people of the Undercourt moved through their glowing streets with a smoothness that took her too long to name, and when she named it she wished she hadn't.

Graceful. They were all graceful. They walked without hesitation, without the small stumbles and second thoughts of living people, and their faces—

Their faces were calm. All of them. Every face she passed wore the same serene, untroubled blankness, like water after wind has died. A woman arranged luminescent fruit in a market stall with movements precise as clockwork. A child—*a child*, and Vesper's hand went to her knife hilt before she could stop it—sat on a stair and watched the light-rivers flow with eyes that never blinked quite often enough.

"Hollowed," Vesper said.

"Leased back to the labor lines, mostly. These are the ones whose extraction went deep." Sable didn't soften it. She never softened anything; it was one of the few things about her Vesper had come to trust. "The shallow cuts keep most of themselves. Enough to work, enough to smile when overseers pass. The deep cuts—" she gestured at the market, the streets, the serene crowds, "—are more useful. They don't tire. They don't grieve. They don't ask why the gardens need tending at all."

"And the court calls this peace." Vesper thought of Greyhollow, of the border wards she'd bribed ward-keepers to maintain, of thirty years of quiet trade through Thornfell while villages like these fed the machinery underneath. She had smuggled salt and steel through a peace built on this. Everyone had. The whole surface world stood on a floor of harvested souls and called it stability.

"The court calls it *order*. Peace is the word the surface uses to avoid asking questions." Sable paused at the edge of a terrace overlooking a broad plaza, where a fountain played light instead of water—threads of silver rising and falling in patterns almost like language. "You should know I did not bring you here to grieve. Grief comes later, or never, depending on whether we succeed. Right now I brought you here to show you the shape of what you're walking into. So look. Really look."

Vesper looked.

Beyond the plaza, the terraces rose again toward the far side of the vault, and there—built against the vault wall itself, half architecture and half growth—stood a structure that made her mark flare hot enough that she hissed and pressed her sleeve over it.

It was a palace. Or the idea of a palace, rendered by something that had never needed doors or windows. Towers of translucent stone, filled from within with drifting light. Bridges spanning gaps no mortal engineer would dare. And woven through all of it, running up its spires and along its bridges like sap through a tree, the same silver filaments she'd seen in the passage walls—but thicker here, brighter, moving.

Moving like things alive. Moving like things *struggling*.

"What am I seeing?" she asked, and hated how small her voice came out.

"The Court Proper. The Regent's seat." Sable's jaw tightened, the first crack Vesper had ever seen in that composed face. "And the filaments are what the crown eats. Starlight and soul-song, braided together. The towers are vats, Vesper. The whole palace is a vat. The Regent lives inside the harvest."

* * *

They could not go straight to the Court Proper—Sable was emphatic on this, and for once Vesper didn't argue, because everything in her wanted to run at those towers right now, blade drawn, and everything in her also knew that would be dying stupidly in a place built to make dying easy.

So they took the long way, through service galleries where the hollowed moved goods in silent processions, and it was in those galleries that Vesper saw the vats up close.

She had imagined—without letting herself imagine it—that souls would look like ghosts. Wisps. Candle-flames. Something soft.

They were not soft.

The vat-halls were long chambers lined with vessels of clear stone, tall as men, each one filled not with liquid but with *presence*—a shimmering density, like heat-haze made solid, and inside each vessel moved shapes. Faces mostly. Sometimes hands. Sometimes just a turn of the head, a gesture mid-sentence, frozen halfway through and repeating. Souls remembered being people, and the remembering showed.

The gallery ran along a walkway above them, and Vesper made herself look at every single one.

An old man, laughing at some joke no one would ever finish telling him. A young woman with her arms thrown wide as if dancing. A soldier, she thought, judging by the set of the shoulders, standing eternally at a watch he could no longer name.

And then—

Her whole body knew before her mind did. The mark on her arm burned sudden and bright, and her legs were moving, carrying her to the walkway rail before Sable could catch her elbow, and she was looking down into a vessel near the hall's end at a shape that repeated itself over and over:

Tam. Laughing. Head tipped back, hand raised in the middle of some joke, the one about the mule and the toll-keeper, he'd told it a hundred times on the road, and now the soul of him stood in a jar doing it forever, the laugh starting and never landing, the hand rising and never clapping her shoulder—

"TAM!"

Her voice cracked off the stone walls. Nothing changed. The shape laughed its beginning-less laugh. Around the hall, the other souls drifted on, indifferent, and two hollowed attendants turned their serene faces up toward her with expressions of mild, empty inquiry.

Sable's grip closed on her arm, iron-hard. "Stop. *Stop.* Look at me."

"He's in there—he's *right there*—"

"He is a flicker in a vat, and if you shatter that vessel, you do not free him. You spill him. Do you understand? The soul is bound to the court's ledger, not the glass. Break the jar and what's left of your brother runs out between the stones like water." Sable's voice stayed low and even, but her knuckles were white on Vesper's arm, and there was something in her face—not pity, worse than pity, *recognition*—that told Vesper she had done exactly this kind of math before. "I know. I know what it is to see someone you love in one of these. But grief with a knife in its hand is just suicide with better posture. Come away. Come *away*."

Vesper came away. She didn't remember deciding to. Her legs simply obeyed, dragging her back along the walkway while her eyes stayed fixed on that vessel until the pillars cut it off—and even then she could still see it, printed on the dark behind her eyelids. Tam laughing. Never finishing.

"How much of him is left?" she asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's. Distant. Very controlled, the way she got before violence.

Sable considered lying. Vesper watched her consider it, watched the lie assemble and get discarded, and respected her for the discard.

"Enough to be restored, if the ledger dissolves soon. Not enough to survive another season of drawing-down." A pause. "The Regent knows you're coming, incidentally. The vats sing to the crown. Your brother may already have been... sampled."

"*Sampled.*"

"Choose your battles, Halloran. We are deep in enemy country, and the only reason you are still breathing is that the Regent wants something from you. Which means every hour you spend alive is an hour you spend as merchandise." Sable drew her toward a side gallery, away from the vats, into a corridor where the light dimmed to embers. "There is an audience chamber beyond the Court Proper. The Regent will summon us—me as the returned traitor, you as the debt come due. When that happens, you will be offered a bargain. You will want to refuse it immediately, loudly, with your knife. Don't. Hear it. Bargains have seams. Seams have edges. Edges can be cut."

"You sound like you've cut one before."

"I sound like I failed to." For a moment, in the ember-light, Sable looked old—not in years but in wear, like a road worn smooth by too much travel. Then the composure sealed over again, and she was only the exile noble, cool and unreadable, guiding Vesper deeper. "Rest while you can. Eat. The food down here is real; the court still needs its guests functional. Tomorrow we climb to the Court Proper."

Vesper ate mechanically. She slept in a niche behind a curtain of glowing moss, knife in hand, and dreamed of nothing, which was somehow worse than dreaming of the vat.

* * *

They climbed at what Vesper guessed was morning, though the Undercourt kept no mornings. The galleries gave way to grander passages, the passages to ramps of veined silver-stone, and everywhere the light grew brighter and the filaments in the walls grew thicker, until they ran like cables overhead, humming faintly, all flowing in one direction: upward, toward the Court Proper.

Toward the crown.

The mark on her arm had stopped being warm. Now it ached, deep and rhythmic, and Vesper realized with a cold clarity that it ached in time with her heartbeat—as if the god's remnant in her flesh was trying to speak in the only language it had left, the language of a body.

*I'm here,* the ache said. *I'm still here.*

"Yeah," she murmured under her breath, so low even Sable couldn't hear. "I know. Hold on. I'm coming."

The final ramp opened onto a bridge of pure silver filament, wide as a road, spanning a chasm filled with slow-drifting motes of stolen light. Across the chasm stood the heart of the Court Proper: a gate of black stone flanked by statues of petitioners frozen mid-bow, and beyond it, rising tier upon impossible tier, the palace of vats with the Regent's tower at its summit, blazing with braided starlight and soul-song.

Two figures waited at the bridge's far end. Tall. Crowned in bone-white circlets. Their faces, when the drifting light touched them, were perfectly, terribly serene.

Heralds. Or wardens. It didn't matter. One of them spoke, and its voice was beautiful, which was the worst thing yet—beautiful and hollow, like a bell with the clapper removed, all resonance and no source.

"The Regent sees the daughter of Maerin Halloran."

Not a question. An announcement. Somewhere in the towers above, Vesper felt rather than heard a vast attention turn toward her, the way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth.

Beside her, Sable exhaled slowly. "Whatever happens in there," she said, barely audible, "remember the seams."

Vesper stepped onto the bridge, her mark burning, her brother's unfinished laugh hanging behind her like a wound, and walked toward the crown that had eaten her god.
