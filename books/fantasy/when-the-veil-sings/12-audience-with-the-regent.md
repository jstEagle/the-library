## 12. Audience with the Regent

They came for her at the turn of a water-clock she couldn't see, in a vault where the light never changed and so time itself seemed to have been confiscated along with everything else.

Vesper had spent three days—she thought it was three; the meals arrived with a regularity that suggested someone below kept count even if no one above did—in a guest cell that was less a prison than an argument. The walls were veined with that soft blue-green luminescence that lit all of the Undercourt, bright enough to read by, gentle enough to make you forget you were being held. A bed of woven kelp-cloth. A basin of water sweetened with something mineral and cold. No bars. Only a door of black stone that opened for the servants and closed for her, and the particular weight of knowing that if she pressed her palm against it, the mountain would simply decline to notice.

She had tried the door twice. Both times, the stone had warmed under her hand like a living thing being politely patient.

Now it stood open on its own, and two hollowed attendants flanked the threshold—men and women with faces like unfinished sculptures, eyes that tracked her without ever quite landing on anything, souls leased out and worn thin as old coins. They bowed. It was the bow you'd give a guest, not a prisoner, and that frightened her more than chains would have.

"Come," said one, in a voice like wind through a dry reed. "The Regent will see you now."

* * *

They walked her down corridors that made her feel like a coin dropped into a cathedral's offering box—small, gleaming, already spent.

The Undercourt did not hide what it was. That was the thing she hadn't expected, walking these vaults behind Sable's careful guidance and then alone: it *displayed*. The soul-vats were set into the walls of public galleries like windows into deep water, and inside them souls drifted in slow amber suspension, some whole, most not, flickering with the guttering rhythm she'd seen in Greyhollow's failing wards. Servants passed them without looking. Nobles paused to admire them the way surface-folk admired tapestries.

And threaded through everything—through the stone, through the air, through the marrow of the place—was a sound almost too low to hear. Not silence. The ghost of silence. Like standing in a room where a great bell had rung for a thousand years and had only just stopped, and the walls still remembered.

Her mark ached beneath its wrappings. She'd bound it flat with linen before they took her from the cells, and it had throbbed against the cloth ever since, a second heartbeat with opinions of its own.

*It's here,* it seemed to say. *What was taken is here.*

*I know,* she told it, silently, for the hundredth time. *Be quiet. Let me work.*

The attendants led her through a gallery of petitioners—or their likenesses, or their remains, Vesper had stopped trying to sort the categories—petrified figures frozen mid-plea along the walls, hands outstretched toward doors they would never reach. Then up a stair of white stone worn smooth by centuries of knees, and through an archway hung with banners that bore no crest at all, only darkness stitched in silver thread, and into—

Light.

Not torchlight. Not the blue-green glow of the vats. This was white and cold and faintly wrong, like moonlight remembered by someone who'd never actually seen the moon, and it fell from a dais at the far end of a hall long enough that her footsteps took forty counts to cross it.

The Regent sat upon a throne grown, not built—a single mass of pale bone-colored stone that spiraled upward like a nautilus cut open—and wore the crown.

Even at forty paces, Vesper felt it. A pressure behind the eyes. A taste like struck iron at the back of the throat. The crown was a ring of fused starlight, exactly as Sable had described it and nothing like anything Sable's words had prepared her for: dozens of stars, actual stars, crushed together into a circlet that burned without heat, each one a pinprick of the sky that had gone dark over Thornfell the night Tam signed his name. Some of them were cracked. Hairline fractures spidered through three or four of them, weeping a light that flickered, guttered, steadied, guttered again.

Like the wards at Greyhollow. Like candles. Like everything in this world that was supposed to burn forever and was quietly running out.

The Regent watched her approach with a face that was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful—smooth, luminous, and hiding the exact depth at which things drown. Ageless, or rather aged in every direction at once, young around the mouth and ancient around the eyes, and tired. Tired in a way that went past exhaustion into something geological.

"Vesper Halloran." The voice was layered, like several speakers an instant apart. "Daughter of Maerin. Sworn knife of the silent god. Smuggler of salt, steel, and secrets across my mountains." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "You've been busy."

"You've been reading my file," Vesper said.

Something moved at the corner of the Regent's mouth. Amusement, or the memory of it, worn smooth like everything else here.

"I have been waiting," the Regent said, "for eleven years, for a Halloran to walk through my gate and declare herself. Your mother made me promise I wouldn't send for you. She was very firm about it." The starlight crown pulsed once, and one of the cracks widened, almost imperceptibly, and the Regent's layered voice caught on something that might have been pain. "Maerin was always firm. It was her best and worst quality."

Vesper kept her feet moving and her face still, which was the first discipline the company ever taught you: never let them see where the wound is. But inside, cold spread outward from her sternum. *Your mother.* Spoken with the intimacy of someone who had shared a table with Maerin Halloran, maybe more than a table.

"You knew her."

"Everyone below knew her. Few above did." The Regent rose from the throne, and Vesper saw for the first time how tall she was—not towering, but present, the way a cliff is present—and how carefully she moved, as though her body were a garment fitted over something vast and strained. "Walk with me. I find negotiations go better when neither party is enthroned. Thrones encourage lying."

* * *

They walked the length of the hall, then through an arch into a garden that should not have existed this far under a mountain: a cavern vaulted high enough to hold weather, planted with trees whose leaves were translucent and faintly gold, fed by streams that glimmered with the same soul-light as the vats. The air tasted of rain that had never fallen anywhere.

Vesper counted exits out of habit. There were none worth counting. The garden was a beautiful bowl and they were at the bottom of it.

"Do you know why the stars went out?" the Regent asked, strolling beside her with hands folded, courtly as a diplomat at a funeral.

"Because your crown is cracking," Vesper said. "I have eyes."

"Mm. And do you know why the crown exists at all?"

Vesper said nothing, and the silence stretched until the Regent filled it, because monarchs always do.

"When your grandmother's generation bound the border wards—the wards that kept Thornfell safe, that kept *my* people below and your people above, that gave your surface world three generations of peace—" the layered voice flattened, "—the price was not paid once. It was structured. An annuity of souls, drawn against the Halloran line, administered by the court, enforced by the contract your mother brokered and countersigned." The Regent turned those too-old eyes on her. "I did not devour the Veil's voice, whatever Sable has told you. I *inherited* it, as one inherits a mortgage. The god's song was the collateral. The crown is the deed. Every star you see fused here is a fragment of that voice, and I wear it because someone must, because if the deed is left unclaimed the debt collapses and takes the wards, the borders, and roughly forty thousand lives on both sides of the mountain down with it."

"That's a lovely story," Vesper said. "Sable tells prettier ones, but yours has better production values."

"And yet." The Regent smiled, and it was almost warm. "You haven't denied any of it."

Vesper looked away, out over the golden-leaved trees, and hated that the Regent was right. Maerin's ledger lived in her memory now, page by page, in her mother's cramped accounting hand: *one per generation, willing or collateral, the line endures, the wards endure.* She had read it by candlelight in a smuggler's cache with her hands shaking, and she had not found a single lie in it. Only costs. Costs arranged in neat columns going back sixty years.

"What do you want," Vesper said.

"To close the account." The Regent walked to the edge of a stream and looked down into its glowing water as if consulting it. "Tam Halloran—your sworn brother, though the contract doesn't recognize oath-kinship, which is a flaw in the drafting I intend to correct—is currently collateral against the company debts he assumed. He has perhaps nine days of coherence left. After that, what's in the vat can be released back to his body, but it won't be him. You've seen the hollowed. You know the arithmetic."

Nine days. Vesper's hands curled at her sides and she made them uncurl. "Go on."

"The final payment on Maerin's contract falls due when the last Halloran of this generation stands before me and surrenders willingly. Not dragged. Not tricked. *Willingly*—the old binding requires it, and the old binding does not bend. Your mother knew that. It's why she hid you. It's why she let the world believe the line had ended." The Regent finally turned to face her fully, and up close, in the crown's fractured light, Vesper could see the toll written plainly: fine fissures in the skin of the Regent's temples, matching the cracks in the stars, as if wearing the crown were slowly splitting her open along seams no one was meant to see. "Give yourself freely, and the contract closes. Complete. Final. Tam walks out whole—today, before the ink dries. The generational clause dies with the closing; no child of yours, no child of anyone, will ever be levied again. The wards stay bound. The stars—" a pause, and the layered voice dropped to something almost human, almost pleading, "—the stars come back."

"And you get the last soul you need to keep your crown patched," Vesper said.

"I get *rest*," the Regent said, and for one stripped moment the layers fell away and the voice underneath was just tired, just a single exhausted person at the bottom of a very long hole. "Do you know what it is to hold a god's voice in your skull? It sings whether I sleep or wake. It argues. It grieves. It has been grieving for sixty years, child, and it grieves *at* me. I am not your monster. I am the lock someone built around a fire, and the fire is eating the lock, and you are the key that fits."

Vesper stood very still.

Here was the trap of it, and she saw the shape of the trap clearly, which somehow made it worse: there was no lie to catch. That was the craftsmanship of the offer. Sable lied beautifully; the Regent barely bothered. Every word rang true against Maerin's ledger, against the hollowed villages, against the ache in her own mark. The deal was probably honest down to the rivets.

That was what made it temptation instead of threat. Duty dressed as mercy. The arithmetic was flawless: one life, freely given, against a brother restored, a debt line extinguished forever, forty thousand strangers kept safe, a sky relit. Any captain she'd ever served under would call it the best trade on the table. Brannoc would call it *clean*. He'd hate it, and he'd call it clean, because that was what soldiers said about sacrifices that worked.

And underneath the flawless arithmetic, quiet as a knife slid between ribs, was the other truth the Regent had not quite said: a willing Halloran soul, surrendered at the heart of the binding, would pour straight into the crown. Would patch the cracks. Would keep the Regent alive and the deed intact and the Veil's voice caged for another sixty years, sung through another dying warden's skull.

The mercy was real. So was the cage.

"My mother brokered this contract," Vesper said slowly. "Did she know it would come to me?"

"Maerin knew everything would come to you. That was her tragedy." The Regent's gaze drifted to the golden canopy above. "She loved you the way masons love cathedrals—by building around you and calling it protection. She hid your name from every register below. She paid bribes on the surface to bury your lineage. And in the end, all of it only delayed this conversation by twenty years and cost her everything she had." A beat. "She bargained hard for your sake, Vesper. I want you to know that. When she came to amend the terms, she offered me *herself*, ahead of schedule, in your place. I refused her. The binding wants willingness, and hers was compromised—she came to me terrified for you, and terror isn't consent. The old law is strict." The layered voice softened. "So she went home and died small, in a bed, of ordinary age, believing she'd failed. I have wondered since whether telling her the delay had worked would have been kinder than silence. I chose silence. Kings usually do."

Vesper's throat was tight. She thought of her mother's hands, ink-stained, folding the ledger closed; thought of the woman only in fragments now, because fragments were all she'd kept, because keeping whole memories of Maerin hurt too much to carry on a road.

*Careful,* she told herself. *This is exactly what mercy-shaped duty is supposed to feel like. Warm. Inevitable. Pre-approved by everyone you've lost.*

"If I say yes," Vesper said, "when? How?"

"Now. Tonight. There is a rite—the same chamber where your grandmother's payment was taken. You speak the closing formula, you kneel, you surrender, and it's done before the next water-turn. Tam is decanted within the hour after. Whole. He'll remember signing the debt, and little else of the vat. They rarely do." The Regent extended one hand, palm up—an old gesture, older than courts, the gesture of someone showing they held no weapon. "I give you my word as Regent. And unlike surface kings, my word is load-bearing. Break it and the mountain knows."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the contract continues. Tam dissolves in nine days. The levy passes next to you regardless—you are the last of the line; there is no one after you, which means the debt comes due in full, unwillingly collected, and unwilling collection is *messy*, Vesper. It takes years off the wards. It cracks more stars. It hollows more villages while the collectors work." The Regent's hand stayed open between them, patient, terrible. "No is not a door out. No is just the slow version of yes, with extra suffering priced in for everyone you love."

There it was. The full architecture. No cruelty required—only arithmetic, and patience, and the absolute confidence that a woman who loved her brother and her company and her dead mother would eventually do the sums correctly.

Vesper looked at the open hand. She thought about Tam laughing in the pass, juggling a salt-cake, saying *you worry like a woman with a divine warranty*. She thought about Brannoc's face when she'd refused to burn the mark. She thought about the hollowed man in the last village, sweeping a doorstep that belonged to no house, humming a tune he didn't know he knew—her mother's tune, a lullaby Maerin used to sing, leaked out of some vat and lodged in a stranger's ruined mind like shrapnel.

And she thought about the mark under the linen, hot as a coal, and the ghost-silence threaded through the mountain's bones, and the thing the Regent had said without saying: *it sings whether I sleep or wake.*

"You want my soul," Vesper said. "Willingly. At the binding-chamber. Tonight."

"Yes."

"And in exchange: Tam whole, the line closed, the wards bound, the stars returned."

"Yes."

Vesper nodded slowly, as if weighing it. As if she hadn't already noticed the seam in the offer, the one word doing all the work, the hinge the whole gorgeous machine swung on.

"Willingly," she repeated. "The old law is strict about that. Terror isn't consent. Coercion isn't consent." She met the Regent's fractured eyes. "So tell me—does the law care *what* is surrendered? Or only that it's given free?"

For the first time, the Regent went still in a way that wasn't composed.

"The instrument binds a soul of the Halloran line," the Regent said, each word placed like a foot on ice. "Freely yielded."

"A soul of the Halloran line," Vester echoed. "Freely yielded." She let a smile rise, small and sharp, the smile she wore when a toll-keeper asked too much and she was about to show him the second ledger. "Then I accept your bargain, Regent. Take me to the binding-chamber. Bring the contract. Bring the closing formula, every word of it—I want it read aloud, in full, before witnesses, exactly as Maerin drafted it."

The Regent studied her, and behind the ancient eyes something quick and wary moved, like a fish under lake ice. "You're agreeing quickly."

"I'm agreeing *clearly*," Vesper said. "There's a difference. Ask my mother—oh. You can't."

She turned toward the archway, toward the hall and the throne and the chamber beyond, and the Regent fell into step beside her, and the crown's cracked stars guttered overhead like a sky learning how to fail.

Behind them, in the golden gloom of the impossible garden, a servant Vesper had never noticed—one of the hollowed, tray balanced, eyes fixed on nothing—set down a silver pitcher and slipped away through a side passage, moving with a purpose no hollowed thing should have had.

And in the cells below, in a vat of amber light, something that had been Tam Halloran stirred and whispered her name.
