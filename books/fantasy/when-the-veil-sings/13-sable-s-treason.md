## 13. Sable's Treason

The audience chamber was still singing when they took her.

Not with the Veil's song—Vesper knew that music now, had felt it hum through the stone of the Singing Cave like warmth through frozen fingers. This was something else: a thin, grating resonance that came from the crown itself, from the fused starlight wound around the Regent's skull. It sounded the way a kettle sounds just before it fails. Just before it scalds someone.

She stood where she'd been standing since the Regent finished speaking, her offer hanging in the vaulted air between them—*Tam's freedom for your willing surrender, Vesper Halloran. The last payment of Maerin's contract. A mercy, freely given.*

Her mouth still tasted of the word she hadn't said yet.

*No.* She hadn't said it. She'd asked for a night to consider, and the Regent—the thing wearing a face like weathered marble, eyes the color of deep water over drowned cities—had inclined its head with terrible graciousness and said, *Of course. Grief deserves an hour alone with itself.*

Now two guards flanked her, tall and gray-skinned, their faces smooth as river stones worn featureless by centuries of current. Hollowed, but hollowed so long ago there was nothing left to even miss themselves. They didn't touch her. They simply existed at her shoulders like walls that had learned to walk.

"Wait," Vesper said, and hated how small it came out. "The Regent gave me until—"

"The Regent gives nothing." Sable's voice, behind her.

Vesper turned. Sable stood at the base of the dais steps, and everything about her was wrong in ways Vesper couldn't immediately name. Her spine was straighter. Her chin higher. The careful, road-worn slouch of an exile playing at being nobody had fallen away, and what remained was something older and colder: a woman remembering she was nobility.

"Sable." Vesper's voice cracked on the name. "Tell them this is—"

"I told you every answer costs something." Sable's dark eyes were steady, and that was the worst part. No guilt in them. No flicker of the woman who'd sat across a hundred campfires sharing dried plums and stories about Maerin. "You kept asking anyway."

"What are you doing?"

"Telling the truth. Finally." Sable lifted her hand, and the guards' posture changed—some subtle shift, deference flowing toward her like water finding its level. "House Vael requests the prisoner be remanded to the deep cells pending the closing of the contract. As is our right. As it has *always* been our right."

The words landed one by one, each with its own weight.

*House Vael.*

Vesper had heard the name only once, in Maerin's ledger, in a marginal note in her mother's cramped hand: *the Vael line bound as witness, their claim dormant while the debt stands.* She'd thought it meant nothing. A formality. A dead family's ghost written into old paper.

"You're not exiled," Vesper said slowly. "You're *positioning*."

"I was exiled." Something moved behind Sable's eyes then—something that might have been pain, if pain could fossilize. "Two hundred years ago, when your mother brokered the binding and my house was stripped of standing for opposing it too loudly. We have waited generations in the dark places of this court, Vesper, watching the Regent grow fat on stolen voices, waiting for the debt to come due. Waiting for a *Halloran*."

"And I'm what? Your key?"

"You're the crisis." Sable descended one step, and the starlight crown's harsh song seemed to bend around her, wary of her. "A Halloran walks into the Undercourt voluntarily, claims her lineage at the Gate of Teeth, stands before the Regent and *refuses to kneel*. Do you understand what that does? The contract requires willing surrender. If the heir refuses—if the debt cannot be closed—the entire binding is void. The Regent's power evaporates. Every house that has ground its teeth in silence for two centuries rises at once." She spread her hands, and there was, horribly, real passion in her voice. "Your refusal is a declaration of war. I merely needed you to make it in the right room."

The chamber tilted. Or Vesper did. She gripped the guard's stone arm without thinking, cold as grave-clay under her palm.

At the Gate of Teeth, declaring her lineage—Sable had told her it would make her claim public, had warned her gravely, had held her hand almost. *Speaking truth as an act of courage,* Vesper had thought. *Point of no return.* And Sable had stood beside her, watching, and what she'd actually been doing was counting votes.

"The Singing Cave," Vesper said. "You let me hear him. You let me *hope*."

"The hope was real. I needed it to be." Sable paused on the last step, close enough now that Vesper could see the fine silver threading her temples, the exhaustion she usually hid. "I loved your mother. That wasn't a lie either. But Maerin chose the Regent's bargain over my house's honor, and I have spent two hundred years learning that love is not a currency this court honors." Her voice softened, which somehow made it crueler. "You have her stubbornness. Did you know that? She also believed people could be walked out of burning buildings by telling them true things."

"You used me."

"I *guided* you. There's a difference, and history will—" 

"*There's no history!* There's Tam!" Vesper lunged forward and the guards caught her arms, stone-firm, immovable. She didn't care. She was shouting now, and the sound went up into those vast bioluminescent vaults and came back down multiplied, ugly. "He's in your vats! He's *fading*, Sable, I saw him, I saw pieces of him floating in that light like—like ash—and you stood next to me on the road and taught me court etiquette!"

"Yes." Sable didn't flinch. That was the unforgivable part. She received every word like tribute. "And when House Vael sits the throne that follows, the vats will be emptied. All of them. I swear it on my house's name—which, unlike yours, has never been spent."

Above them, the Regent stirred on its throne of black glass. It had watched all of this in silence, and its silence had weight, pressing down like deep water. When it finally spoke, its voice was the thin grating song made into language.

"House Vael speaks boldly of thrones." The Regent rose. The crown crackled, and a hairline flare of white light spat from its surface, and the Regent's left hand—the one resting on the throne's arm—crumbled at the fingertips. Just crumbled, like chalk, like it had been dead a long time and only pretending. It didn't look at it. "*Dormant* houses do not inherit. Dormant houses wait to see whether the debt closes or breaks. Take the prisoner below. Both outcomes require her intact—for now."

The guards pulled. Vesper twisted once, found Sable's eyes, and held them.

"My mother deserved better than both of you," she said.

For half a heartbeat—less—something in Sable's composure shivered, like a reflection disturbed by a dropped stone. Then the guards dragged Vesser through the side arch, past curtains of glowing lichen, down.

* * *

The deep cells were older than everything above them.

Vesper felt it in the walls—a different kind of stone, pre-court, maybe pre-mountain, veined with mineral light so faint it was more memory than glow. The corridor sloped down in long spirals, and the temperature dropped with each turn until her breath fogged and her wet clothes stiffened against her skin. The guards deposited her in a cell whose door was a slab of stone carved with the same spiral sigil she'd seen on the Gate of Teeth's petrified petitioners. Then they left, footsteps receding upward into silence.

She sat with her back against the wall and shook.

Not from cold. Or not only. The shaking came up from somewhere beneath thought, the body's honest arithmetic: *you trusted her, you slept in shifts with her for nine days, you told her about the scar, you told her about the night your mark first went silent, you let her touch the mark—*

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until sparks bloomed.

Brannoc had told her. That was the bitter joke of it. *Trust the company, burn the mark, vanish.* She'd chosen Tam over that advice, and she'd been right to, and she'd still walked blind into a stranger's story because the stranger had known her mother's name and her mother's smile and the exact way Maerin used to hum when she mended sails. Sable had handed her truths one at a time, each one priced, and Vesper had paid without ever asking who set the exchange rate.

"Stupid," she whispered to the dark. "Stupid, stupid—"

"Ves?"

The voice came from the cell adjacent. Faint. Scraped. Wrong.

Every muscle in her body locked.

She knew that voice the way you know your own pulse. It had called her *Ves* across twenty years of passes and taverns and narrow escapes. It had sung bawdy songs off-key on watch. It had promised, three nights ago under a sky with no stars, that he'd find a way to fix what he'd broken.

"Tam?" Her voice came out shredded. She scrambled to the wall between the cells, palms flat against the cold stone. "Tam, I'm here. I'm here, brother. Talk to me."

A long pause. Then a sound that was almost laughter and mostly not.

"Heard you... upstairs. Shouting." Another pause, longer, like a man surfacing from deep water between words. "Couldn't tell if you'd won."

"No." Her forehead dropped against the stone. "No, we lost. I lost. I'm in the cell next to you because I was an idiot and I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, Tam, this whole thing started because—"

"Don't." Sharp, suddenly, his old fire flickering through the ruin of his voice. "Don't you dare apologize. I signed. *Me*. My hand, my seal, my stupid arithmetic about cargo losses." A breath, ragged. "You want to feel guilty, feel guilty about the dice game in Greyhollow. The rest isn't yours."

She laughed, and it came out wet, and she let it.

"Talk to me," she said. "What's it like? In there. What are they doing to you?"

Silence stretched. When he answered, he'd gone somewhere else in his head; she could hear it, the careful distance of a man describing his own wound from very far away.

"It's quiet. Mostly. Like being... a house with the furniture being carried out room by room. I remember the company. I remember Brannoc's terrible stew. I remember you falling in the Kestrel ford and swearing me to secrecy." A faint huff. "But I tried to remember Mother's face yesterday—our landlady, back home, not yours—and it was just... a shape. A warm shape. There's a light in here, Ves, and when it gets bright, things go missing."

Her stomach turned over. She'd seen the soul-vats from the gallery above—great vessels of liquid luminescence where harvested selves drifted like pale fish. She'd seen a flicker that might have been Tam's laugh suspended in that light.

"How much is left?" she asked. "How much of you?"

"Enough." And then, firmer, gathering himself: "Enough to know you'll get us out. You always get us out. Even the Kestrel ford."

"I don't know how." Honest, because lies cost more than truth in the dark. "The Regent wants me to surrender myself. Sable wanted—I don't even know what Sable wants anymore except a war. And the god, Tam, the god is *here*, I heard it, but it's trapped inside the crown, inside the Regent, and I can't—"

"Ves." Gentle now. "Breathe."

She breathed. The mineral veins in the wall pulsed, very slow, like something sleeping.

"There's a servant," Tam said quietly. "Comes with the water ration. Hollowed, but not all the way—not like the guards. Sometimes when she thinks no one's watching, her face does things. Real things. Yesterday she looked at me and her eyes *focused*, and she said—" his voice dropped, conspiratorial, fraying at the edges, "—she said, 'The crown is cracking. He knows it. That's why he's afraid of the song.'"

The words settled over Vesper like snowfall.

*A sympathetic hollowed servant.* A thread, thin as spider silk, dangling in the absolute dark.

"Tam," she said slowly, "when she comes again—"

"I know." And beneath the ruin of his voice, she heard it: her sworn brother, still in there. Still hers. "Get her talking. One of us still remembers how."

Outside the cell door, far up the spiral corridor, footsteps began to descend. Soft ones. Unarmored. Unhurried.

Carrying something that clinked gently, like a water jug.

Vesper pressed herself to the bars of the stone doorway, heart hammering, and watched the faint light strengthen—and the figure within it resolve into a slight silhouette, a woman's shape, moving toward the cells through the sleeping dark of the mountain's oldest bones.
