## 14. Tam, Half-Gone

The cell had no door. That was the first thing Tam noticed, in the long grey time between thoughts: there was nothing to be locked *behind*. The Undercourt did not need doors for what it kept. It needed only patience.

He sat with his back against a wall that was not stone—not exactly—and tried to remember his own name.

*Tam.* It came to him the way a coin comes up out of a well: late, and cold, and only because someone was calling for it. He clung to it. Tam Halloran—no, not Halloran, never Halloran, that name belonged to Vesper and to debts he hadn't understood when he signed—but Tam, yes. Sworn brother of the Grey Company. Card-sharp. Terrible singer. The one who always took the first watch so Vesper could sleep, and who told her it was because he liked the quiet, which was a lie, because he liked listening to her breathe slow instead of fast.

Those were his things. He stacked them up inside himself like stones against a flood.

But the flood came anyway.

It came without malice. That was the horror of the hollowing—he'd expected pain, or hunger, some teeth to fight. Instead there was only a soft tide going out of him, gentle as sleep, taking pieces of him down to wherever the court kept its treasures. He could feel the gaps now. There had been a dog, once, when he was small. He knew this the way you know a room has a window even after the shutters are closed—the shape of the absence, not the thing itself. A brown dog? A loud one? Gone. The vat-keepers below had his dog now, or the memory of it, folded away like linen in a nobleman's chest.

Somewhere beyond the wall, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be natural. Counting. Everything down here counted something.

*Tam.*

He opened his eyes. He hadn't known they were closed.

The voice was thin, scraped raw, unmistakable. He turned his head, and it cost more than turning a head should cost—it felt like dragging an anchor through sand—but she was there, three cells down the row, her fingers hooked through the gap where wall met floor, her face pressed sideways against the stone as if the dark were a thing you could push through by wanting hard enough.

"Vesper." Her name came out of him like blood from a reopened wound. "You shouldn't—you gave yourself up. You were supposed to run."

"I don't run." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "You know that about me. You've complained about it for six years."

"Seven," he said, because correcting her was the oldest habit he had, older than the company, older than the debt. "Six years and four months. I keep accounts. You know I keep accounts."

Something in her face went strange and soft at that—*keep accounts*, of all the phrases to survive the hollowing intact—and he realized a half-second too late what he'd said, and what she must be thinking: that the brokers had taken everything but the ledger-keeping, that the court had left him nothing but arithmetic. Maybe that was true. Maybe that was all that was true anymore.

"Tell me something," she said. "Anything. Tell me something only you would know."

So he did. Because asking was her way of holding on, and holding on was the only prayer either of them had ever really believed in.

* * *

"The winter we met," Tam said, and his voice was a guttering thing, "you'd been on the road alone for two years, and you ate like a person who expected every meal to be her last. You'd take bread off my plate when I wasn't looking. Not steal it—*take* it, right in front of me, daring me to object. And Brannoc asked why I put up with you, and I said—" His breath caught. The gap where the next part should be yawned open, and he groped across it like a man crossing ice. "I said... I said..."

"Take your time."

"No. No time. That's the whole problem here, Ves, there's no *time*—" He pressed his palms flat to the floor, steadying himself. "I said: she eats like that because she's been hungry longer than she's been anything else. And Brannoc laughed and made you a sworn sister on the spot. Do you remember?"

"I remember," she whispered. "I remember the bread."

"The bread," he agreed, absurdly, wonderfully, and felt one stone stack itself firm against the flood. "Good. Keep that. If I lose it, feed it back to me."

She crawled closer along the wall. In the dark he couldn't see her mark, but he could feel it—or imagined he could—a faint warmth in the air between them, like a banked fire behind wet stone. The silent god's scar. She'd hidden it from everyone for years, and now it was the only light left in either of them.

"Tam," she said carefully. "How much of you is still here?"

He considered lying. He was good at lying; he'd lied to the whole company for a season about the contract, smiling over cards while the brokers' ink dried on his soul. But lying to Vesper in a cell beneath the world seemed like the one theft he couldn't commit.

"I don't know how to measure it," he said. "It's not like losing a limb. It's like... being read aloud by someone who skips the parts they find boring. I reach for a year of my life and find the binding of the book instead. I know I loved my mother's cooking. I can't taste it anymore. I know I once cried at a funeral and swore everyone to secrecy. I can't remember whose." His voice thinned. "They started with the small things, Ves. They're working inward. Like rot in a beam—you don't see it until the house leans."

Her hand found his through the gap between cells. Her fingers were cold and calloused and the most real thing in the world.

"Then we lean back," she said.

* * *

They played the game all night, or what passed for night in a place where the dark had texture and the dripping never stopped. She would give him a memory, and he would try to hold it, and sometimes it slipped through him like water through a net and they had to start again.

"The ferryman at Coldbrook. You paid him double to sing badly, so no one else would hire him and we'd have the crossing to ourselves."

Tam laughed before he remembered why, then the remembering arrived, warm and whole: the old man's tuneless droning, Vesper's wicked grin, the mist on the river like breath held. "You said bad music was a form of territory marking. Like wolves."

"Like wolves," she confirmed. "Did it stay?"

"It stayed." Another stone. "Keep going."

"You hate olives. You once picked them out of a stew at a duke's table and hid them in your boot rather than insult him, and limped for two days."

"Gone," he said, and heard his own voice go flat with grief. "Wait—no. Not gone. I have the *limping*. I just don't have the olives. What kind of monster takes the olives and leaves the limp?"

"The kind that works for the Regent," Vesper said, and her voice changed, went hard and bright as a drawn blade. "Tam. Listen to me. While we do this—I need you to tell me what you've seen. What they say around you. You've been in their vats, their halls. Hollowed men are furniture to them. Furniture hears everything."

He wanted to say *I'm not furniture yet*, but the words caught on something sharp and true, so he swallowed them and did what she asked. Because that was the other oldest habit he had: doing what she asked, especially when it was dangerous, especially when it was stupid.

"They talk above the hollowed," he said slowly, dredging each piece up like a diver hauling rope. "We're supposed to be empty. Empty things don't count as witnesses. So I listened. I got good at listening—empty men make excellent ears." He closed his eyes. "There's a woman. Sable. They brought her in yesterday, in chains of green light. The guards were angry. She'd led you straight into an ambush, but the Regent didn't thank her for it—that's the part they couldn't understand. The Regent was *furious* that she'd come at all. Kept saying—" he frowned, reaching—"kept saying, *the Halloran girl was not to be touched until the crown is mended.*"

"Mended," Vesper repeated. "The crown's broken?"

"It sings wrong," Tam said simply. "That's what the vat-keepers mutter. Every soul they pour in goes first to the crown, to patch it, and every soul comes up short. The Regent drinks starlight and starlight keeps leaking out through the cracks. They're scared, Ves. Down here they don't say the god's name—they won't even shape the silence where its name should be—but I've seen the Regent's face." He opened his eyes and found hers in the dark, or the idea of her eyes, the warmth of her attention. "You want to know what frightens the throne of the Undercourt? Not the surface kingdoms. Not armies. Not debtors with knives."

"Tell me."

"The Veil." His voice dropped to almost nothing, and the dark leaned in to listen. "The Regent fears the Veil waking more than it fears any debtor, any war, any succession. Because every soul it stole was also a nail in the god's mouth, and nails pull loose. The wardens whisper that when the song comes back, everything the crown is built on comes apart. The contracts, the vats, the peace the surface pays for without knowing—all of it is notes in a stolen song. And the Regent is the only one who knows how the song ends."

Vesper was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her thumb was moving over his knuckles, slow and steady, the way she used to calm horses.

"Then we'd better learn the ending first."

* * *

Later—hours later, or what he decided to call hours, because keeping time was another stone for the wall—he felt the tide come for him again.

He knew the signs now. A softness at the edges of thought. The sense of being gently, lovingly unlaced. The vat-keepers called it *tithing*, as if his selfhood were grain in a granary, owed and collectible. He'd signed for this. Ink on paper, in a smoky room, to cover losses that were never his to cover—that was the poison in it, the thing he'd carried alone for a season: the debt was hers, her family's, her mother's, and he'd paid it with himself because he'd rather be devoured than let them come for her.

He'd do it again. That was the terrible, clean truth at his center, and it was the one thing the hollowing could never take, because it wasn't a memory. It was a choice, and choices apparently weighed too much for the tides to lift.

"Tides coming?" Vesper asked. She always knew. Six years—seven years and four months—of reading him across campfires, and distance meant nothing to it.

"Yeah."

"Then hold onto my voice. Just my voice. Let the rest go under if it has to."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan I've ever had that worked," she said, and began to speak.

She talked about the road. About the pass above Greyhollow where the wind sang through a split crag and Tam had named it the Whistling Widow. About the night he'd taught her dice and she'd lost three silver pieces and accused the dice of treason. About the morning the company stood in the snow and swore each other in, breath steaming, Brannoc's big hands on their shoulders, and Tam had looked over and seen Vesper trying not to cry and had pretended not to see, because that was the gift he could give her: the mercy of being unobserved.

And Tam drifted, and the tide rose around him, and he held to her voice like a drowning man holds a rope—not climbing it, just refusing to let go. Pieces of him went out on the water. The dog. The funeral. His mother's kitchen, already gone. He felt each departure like a small death, and grieved each one honestly, and did not let go of the rope.

Because here was the thing he understood, floating in the dark with less and less of himself to float with:

Grief was proof. Whatever they took, the grieving remained, and the grieving was *his*. They could harvest the harvest, but the wanting-it-back was a root too deep for their tongs. As long as he hurt over what was missing, some part of him was still standing guard over the door.

"Tide's passing," he managed, when the softness receded. "Ves. Ves, listen. While it's quiet—the Regent will call for you soon. Before the crown fails completely. There's a servant, hollowed, brings the water. She looks at things. Counts things. She's counting the guards, Ves. Someone's teaching the furniture to count."

Vesper's grip tightened on his hand. "A riot?"

"A hope," Tam said. "Down here those look the same."

Above them, far above, through leagues of mountain and vault and stolen sky, something vast and cracked shifted in its sleep—and for one impossible instant, the dark of the cells filled with a sound neither of them had heard in three years.

Faint. Broken. Rising up through the roots of the world like water finding a crack in a floor.

Singing.

Tam felt Vesper go rigid beside him, felt the mark on her arm wake like an ember blown upon, and heard her breathe one word into the dark, half prayer, half promise:

"There you are."

The song faded. The dripping resumed its counting. But somewhere in the vaults above, Tam knew, a servant was counting guards, a crown was cracking, and a god with a smuggler's voice for a herald had just remembered its own name.

"Hold on," Vesper whispered—to him, to the dark, to both of them. "Hold on. We're going to make some noise."
