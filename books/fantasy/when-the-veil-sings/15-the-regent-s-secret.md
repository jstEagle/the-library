## 15. The Regent's Secret

The cell had no door. That was the first cruelty of it—a mouth of black stone open to the corridor, as if the Undercourt considered walls beneath its dignity, or as if it wanted Vesper to see everything that happened outside her cage and be unable to look away.

She saw plenty. Hollowed servants drifted past at intervals, carrying trays of food she couldn't eat, water she barely touched. Their faces were smooth as river stones, their eyes lit from within by a faint, borrowed glow—the residue of whatever souls had been skimmed off them, like light trapped in amber. They moved without haste and without weariness. Once, that would have looked like peace to her. Now it looked like a room with the furniture removed.

She'd tried talking to them. The first dozen ignored her completely, gliding by like fish past a rock. The thirteenth—a small woman with gray-streaked hair and hands scarred from years of laundry work, if laundry work left scars like rope burns—had paused. Just for a heartbeat. Her luminous eyes had flicked to Vesper's face, to the mark on her arm, and something behind all that emptiness had *twitched*.

Vesper had spent three days cataloguing that twitch.

Now she waited for the woman's next pass, sitting with her back straight against the cold wall, arms loose, posture deliberately unthreatening. Beside her, Tam slept—or what passed for sleep in a man who was half-unmade. His breathing came shallow and irregular, and when he dreamed he made small sounds, like a child lost in a market crowd calling for a mother who'd turned away a moment too soon.

"Hey," Vesper said softly, as the woman drew level with the cell's open mouth. "Laundry woman. Greyhollow? You have soap-burns. I know those burns."

The woman didn't stop. But her step hitched—barely, a stutter in the rhythm of her walk—and she was gone down the corridor before Vesper could say more.

*Good.* A thread, thin as spider silk. You didn't yank threads. You waited.

She settled back against Tam's shoulder, careful of the places where his body had gone translucent at the edges—his fingertips, the rim of his left ear, a patch above his collarbone where she could see the faint pulse of light that wasn't quite blood anymore. She talked to him anyway, low and steady, because Chapter Fourteen had taught her that memory was an anchor and she intended to throw him every rope she could find.

"Do you remember the winter we hauled salt through the Wolf's Teeth passes," she murmured, "and your boots froze so solid you had to break them off your feet like ice sculptures? And you cried about it for a week, and I told Brannoc you were crying about the company's losses, not your toes?" A breath. "He believed me. Brannoc believes anything if you deliver it with enough confidence and a straight face."

Tam stirred but didn't wake. The light under his skin pulsed once, twice, like a moth against glass.

"You owe me for that lie, by the way. Interest accruing. When we get out of here, you're buying drinks until spring."

*When.* Not *if*. She'd made the decision somewhere in the last three days, quietly, the way you decide to keep walking through a blizzard—not because you can see the far side, but because stopping is the only thing guaranteed to kill you.

The corridor beyond the cell had been quiet for hours when the laundry woman came again.

This time, she stopped.

She stood at the edge of the cell's threshold like someone standing at the lip of deep water, her smooth face tilted toward Vesper, her glowing eyes fixed on the mark on Vesper's arm. For a long moment nothing happened. Then her lips moved, and the voice that came out was rusty, cracked, like a hinge that hadn't swung in twenty years.

"They take the voice first." The words cost her visibly; her whole body shuddered around them. "Did you know that? Before the rest. The singing part of a soul. They take it first because it's the loudest."

"I didn't know that," Vesper said carefully.

"It's why the hollowed don't scream." The woman's hands twisted together, wringing themselves the way laundresses wring cloth. "You can't scream without a voice in you. We make sounds. The Regent doesn't like the sounds. So we learn to be quiet even without trying." Her eyes lifted. "You're loud. In there." One scarred finger tapped her own chest, over her heart. "I can hear you from the corridor. Like a struck bell. It hurts. I came back anyway."

Vesper's throat tightened. "Why?"

"Because it hurts *good*." The woman took one step closer, into the cell's shadow, and up close Vesper could see the fine tremor running through her, the effort of holding herself present. "Like a limb waking up. My name was Marenet. I had a daughter. I think—I think her name was Issa. I'm not sure anymore. But when you talk to your brother, I remember what remembering felt like." Her glowing eyes were wet now, though the tears fell without expression, sliding down a face that couldn't shape grief. "You asked me why I stopped. Here's why I stopped: they watch the ones who pause. And I've watched you for three days, Halloran, and I need you to understand what you're standing inside of before you do something brave and stupid and die."

"I'm listening."

Marenet glanced out at the empty corridor, then closer, dropping her voice to that ruined whisper. "You want to know how the wards fell. Why the stars went out. Everyone up top thinks it's the court rising, the old powers breaking loose. It's simpler than that." A pause, and then the words came faster, tumbling, as though she'd been carrying them so long they'd grown heavy enough to crush her. "The Regent *ate* the Veil."

Vesper went very still. Beside her, Tam murmured in his sleep.

"Not killed. Not imprisoned. *Ate*," Marenet said. "Three years ago, the Veil's voice grew too strong for the binding Maerin Halloran brokered—the contract your mother wrote gave the court rights to harvest, yes, but it also bound the god's song to the mountain, and the song was getting louder every year. Leaking. Waking things below that even the Regent feared. So the Regent did what our kind does when something is more beautiful than we can bear." Her ruined voice dropped lower. "It swallowed the voice whole. Took the song into itself, into the crown, thinking it would become the power. Thinking it would hold the wards and the god both, and rule surface and Undercourt as one throne."

"And instead?"

Marenet laughed. There was no humor in it. There was almost nothing in it—it was the ghost of laughter, performed by muscles that remembered the motion but not the meaning. "Instead it poisoned itself. The Veil's song isn't power, Halloran. It's *truth*, and truth doesn't sit quietly in a thief's belly. The crown is cracking. Have you seen it? Of course you haven't—they hide the Regent now, behind veils of ceremony, behind distance. But I served in the high vaults, before. I saw the light leaking out of the seams. The stars didn't go out because the wards failed." Her luminous eyes bored into Vesper's. "The stars went out because the crown *broke*, and everything the Regent stole spilled out through the cracks—and most of it bled upward, into the sky, where it burned itself out looking for its god."

Vesper looked down at her arm. At the mark that had been silent for three years—the same three years, she realized with a cold rolling certainty, since the Regent had swallowed the Veil's voice. Three years of silence she'd mourned as abandonment. Three years of praying into a well and hearing nothing.

It hadn't abandoned her. It had been *choking*.

"The Regent is dying," she said slowly.

"The Regent is dying *badly*," Marenet corrected. "Slowly enough to terrify everyone who serves it, fast enough that it can feel the end coming. And that makes it more dangerous than it has ever been, because a drowning creature doesn't bargain, Halloran. It grabs. It will offer you your brother's freedom, your mother's absolution, the whole glittering weight of this court—and every word will be true when it says them, and none of them will survive the moment it has what it needs. It needs a vessel. A soul strong enough, marked enough, to hold the stolen song while it sheds its own failing body." Her trembling hand rose and pointed, unerringly, at Vesper's arm. "It's been waiting three years for you to come down here. Why do you think the cells opened for you? Why do you think Sable's little treason succeeded? You weren't captured, child. You were *invited*."

The floor seemed to tilt. Vesper gripped Tam's hand—carefully, avoiding the translucent places—and anchored herself to the warmth still living in his fingers.

"Sable," she said. Her voice came out flat. "Sable brought me here knowing this."

"Sable brought you here believing her house could catch what falls when the Regent breaks." Marenet shook her head slowly. "Exiles always believe that. It's how courts eat their exiles." She stepped back toward the corridor, toward the smooth-faced safety of the hollowed, and the tremble in her was worsening—Vesper could see the effort costing her, the borrowed light in her eyes guttering like the wards of Greyhollow had guttered. Whatever allowed her this lucidity, it was spending itself fast.

"Marenet," Vesper said. "Your daughter. Issa. If we burn this place down—if the voices go free—"

"Then maybe some of us remember ourselves when the light comes back." The woman's stone-smooth face achieved, briefly, something that was almost a smile—a cracking of the surface, like ice on a thawing pond. "That's why I spent myself on you, Halloran. Not for the court. Not for the god. For the chance that my girl's name might mean something to me again before the end." She turned, then paused at the threshold. "One more thing. The Regent will come to you soon—it can't wait much longer, the cracks are spreading daily. When it offers you the bargain, and it will dress the bargain in mercy, look at its hands. Not the crown. The crown is beautiful; the crown is a lie. Look at the hands."

"Why the hands?"

"Because that's where the song is tearing it apart," Marenet said. "And because hands tell you what a creature does with what it holds."

Then she walked out into the corridor, spine straightening as she went, face smoothing over, becoming once more a piece of furniture in the court of the devourer—and Vesper sat alone in the dark with a half-gone brother and the full weight of the truth.

* * *

She talked to Tam until her voice went raw. She told him about the salt runs and the frozen boots, about the time he'd cheated at cards against a Thornfell clan-mother and they'd both run for their lives through a hail of thrown crockery, about the night he'd carried her three miles through a fever because she refused to let the company doctor bleed her. She told him everything she could remember, and when her memory ran dry she invented stories in his voice, filling the dark with the shape of him, anchoring the anchorless.

Somewhere in the small hours—if hours meant anything down here, in the bioluminescent gloom where time was measured in servant-drifts and the flicker of soul-light—he woke.

Not fully. His eyes found hers and held on with visible effort, like a man gripping a ledge.

"Ves." The word was mostly breath. "You're still... here."

"Where else would I be?"

"Don't..." His translucent fingers twitched toward her. "Don't take any deal. Whatever it offers. I heard—" His gaze slid sideways, unfocused. "They talk, in the vats. The taken ones. We're all... mixed together, near the surface of ourselves. I heard the servants. I heard the woman." His eyes refocused, sharp with sudden urgency. "Ves, the Regent isn't offering mercy. It's offering *lunch*."

"I know." She squeezed his hand, gentle around the fading parts. "I know, Tam. I'm not going to feed anyone's lunch."

"It's scared," he whispered. A strange, awful awe crept into his thinning voice. "Do you understand? It's got a crown of stolen starlight and a whole court kneeling, and it's *scared*. Scared things down here don't negotiate. They consume. It's how they pray." His grip tightened with strength he shouldn't have had—borrowed, brief, burning like a match in wind. "Whatever it offers—count the cost *after*, not before. Promise me. Promise me you'll count after."

"I promise."

He smiled then—the crooked, lopsided smile that had gotten them thrown out of four taverns and one temple, and for a moment the light under his skin steadied, and he looked like her brother instead of a debt being collected. Then his eyes rolled back and he sank again, breathing shallow, and Vesper sat alone with the promise and the dark.

*Count the cost after.*

* * *

They came for her before the next sleep-cycle. Two honor guards of polished chitin and hollow light, flanking a herald whose robes shimmered with woven shadow, who unrolled a scroll Vesper suspected was pure theater—the court loved its ceremonies, Marenet had said, loved veiling decay in ritual.

"Vesper Halloran, daughter-line of Maerin, bearer of the mark." The herald's voice was rich and rehearsed. "The Regent grants audience. Come, and bring your questions. Leave your weapons."

"I don't have weapons," Vesper said, standing. "You took them."

"Leave your *other* weapons, then." The herald's smile showed too many teeth. "The sharp ones you carry in your chest."

She followed them through corridors of vaulted bone-white stone and veins of blue-green light, past vats where souls swam like lantern-fish, past petitioners' alcoves where hollowed nobles knelt in perfect rows. The court was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it—all this splendor, lit by the harvested dead, gorgeous as a jewel and rotten as a corpse beneath.

And at the heart of it, behind doors of fused starlight, the dying thing that had eaten her god.

Her mark burned against her arm—not painfully, not yet. More like a held breath. Like something pressing its ear to the inside of her skin, listening for what waited beyond those doors.

*Look at the hands,* Marenet had said.

The doors opened onto a hall of shattered constellations, and upon a throne of cracked glass and drifting light, the Regent raised its crowned head and smiled at her with the weary tenderness of a host welcoming family home.

Vesper looked at its hands.

Even across the distance, even through the ceremonial gloom, she could see them: split along every seam, weeping thin trails of silver light, the stolen song bleeding out of them like sap from a wounded tree. The Regent followed her gaze down to its own ruined fingers, and its smile widened—and beneath the widening, for just a heartbeat, she saw the terror underneath, vast and starving and cornered.

"Ah," it said, in a voice like a choir played backwards. "The daughter comes. Sit, child. We have so little time, and so very much to give you."

Behind her, the starlight doors began to swing shut.
