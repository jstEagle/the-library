## 16. Riot of the Hollowed

Vesper had learned in the cells that despair had a texture. It was not the cold—she'd been cold before, on passes where breath froze in her beard-stubble and Tam's jokes were the only fuel worth burning. It was not even the dark. Despair was damp. It crept into the mortar of you, softened everything until a person could be shaped like wet clay by whoever pressed hardest.

The hollowed pressed hardest.

She heard them before she saw them: a shuffling rhythm in the corridor beyond the bars, dozens of feet moving without urgency, without anything at all. When the servant—the one who'd whispered the Regent's secret through the door crack, the one whose name might have once been Ilsa or Ilsabet or nothing at all—ushered her out under pretense of carrying water to the vats, Vesper saw them properly for the first time.

They filled the great vault like sediment fills a bottle. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, arrayed along the catwalks and the terraced shores of the soul-vats, tending pipes and valves and braziers with movements so smooth they looped back on themselves, work becoming rest becoming work. Their faces were the worst part. Not empty—emptiness would have been a mercy—but *thinned*, like cloth washed too many times, the pattern of them still faintly there beneath the fading. A woman stirring a vat with a long glass rod paused mid-motion for no reason a living person would pause. A man stacked ingots of pale light with hands that never trembled and never hurried and never stopped.

"Where are their souls?" Vesper asked quietly.

Ilsa's mouth did something that was almost a flinch. "In the vats. What's left after the Regent feeds. The court leases the bodies back to the surface—they fetch better wages hollowed. No complaints. No dreams." Her eyes flicked sideways, quick as a bird's. "You said you could sing."

"I said I *heard* it. In the cave above. A fragment, leaking up through stone." Vesper flexed her fingers against the cold weight of the water-yoke across her shoulders. The mark on her forearm ached beneath its wrappings, a low banked heat she hadn't felt since the night the stars died. "That's not the same as singing."

"It is if you mean it." Ilsa took the yoke from her shoulders with hands surprisingly strong and set it down beside a brazier whose flame burned the wrong color—green-gold, starlight-colored, harvested. "The Regent tore the Veil's voice out whole and swallowed it. But a voice isn't a thing you can hold. It's a thing that happens *between*." She tapped Vesper's sternum with one knuckle. "It happens here. And between is the one place the crown can't reach."

"And when it reaches me instead?"

"Then we'll know how loud you really are," Ilsa said, and there was something in her face then—not hope exactly, but the scar tissue where hope had been cut away and healed wrong—that made Vesper understand why the woman had risked the whisper through the cell door. She wasn't brave. She was already ruined, and ruined people gamble differently.

The vault was vast enough to have weather. Mist drifted down from the bioluminescent ceiling in slow curtains, and somewhere far above, water dripped in a rhythm that was almost, almost musical—a mockery of song, or perhaps a memory of one. The vats themselves stretched away in ranks, each one tall as a granary, their surfaces glowing with that same green-gold light. Faces surfaced in the glow sometimes. Not reflections. *Faces.* Mouths opening around soundless syllables.

Vesper looked for Tam's face among them and made herself stop looking, because if she found it she would do something stupid and loud right now instead of something clever and louder later.

"The labor shift changes at the deep bell," Ilsa murmured, guiding her toward the central terrace where the greatest concentration of hollowed stood in their patient rows. "They'll all be still for a hundred heartbeats. Changeover. It's the only time their minds aren't full of task." Her grip tightened on Vesper's arm, brief and desperate. "Sing then. Whatever you have. Sing it *loud*."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then the Regent will have your throat before the bell rings twice, and I'll have lost nothing I wasn't going to lose anyway."

The deep bell sounded.

It came up from below the floor itself, a note so low Vesper felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the old scar-tissue of the mark. Across the vault, the hundreds of hollowed went still—not resting still, not sleeping still, but *paused* still, like puppets whose puppeteer had stepped away to wipe his hands. The woman with the glass rod stood frozen mid-stir. The man with his ingots held a bar of light suspended in the air.

A hundred heartbeats.

Vesper closed her eyes. She thought about the cave above Thornfell, the one where she and Sable had sheltered while patrols circled the mountain's knees. She thought about the sound she'd heard there—or hadn't heard, exactly; had *felt*, the way you feel a distant avalanche through the soles of your boots. A thread of melody rising through miles of stone, thin as spider silk and older than any kingdom on the surface.

She thought about her mother, whom she had hated a little and loved enormously and understood, only now, barely at all.

And she opened her mouth and sang.

* * *

Tam had counted four hundred and eleven drips since the last time he remembered his own name.

He kept a tally because the counting was a rope thrown across a chasm, and as long as he walked along it hand over hand he didn't fall. The trouble was that the rope kept fraying. He would count to two hundred and realize he couldn't remember what number came before one, or what one meant, or that numbers were a thing at all—and then some vast slow pressure would lean on him from inside, gentle as snowfall, and whisper: *you don't need this. You don't need any of it. Isn't it lighter without?*

It *was* lighter. That was the horror of it. Every memory the vats took left him easier, smoother, more convenient. He could feel himself being sanded down toward something useful. Something that wouldn't grieve. Somewhere in the fog of what he'd been, there was a woman who laughed at his jokes and called him an idiot with real affection, and holding onto her face was like holding water in cupped hands, and God—the silent one, the one Vesper prayed to on watch when she thought nobody saw—it hurt less every day to let her go, and that was the most obscene thing Tam had ever known.

*Her name,* he told himself, gripping the rope. *Say her name.*

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He wasn't sure he had a mouth anymore. He wasn't sure—

Then the world sang.

It began below him, or above him—he had no orientation left, he was a point of attention drifting in warm dark—but it came, unmistakably, a woman's voice raised in a melody with no words and all words, and the mark of it went through Tam like a spear of clean cold air through a room that had been shut for years.

He remembered winter. He remembered the pass above Greyhollow, white silence, Vesper swearing creatively at a mule. He remembered her singing—badly, off-key, on purpose, because she claimed the god preferred honest noise to pretty lies. He remembered the fire, the company, Brannoc's gruff blessing at midwinter, the taste of burnt stew. He remembered his own name.

*Tam.*

He screamed it, and discovered he had a voice, and discovered the voice worked, and laughed—laughed until the laughter broke into something else, because four hundred and eleven drips of dissolving and he was still *here*, still himself, still the man who owed Vesper Halloran his life twice over and intended to make good.

Around him, impossibly, others were waking too.

* * *

Vesper sang, and the vault caught fire.

Not with flame—with selves. It moved through the crowd like wind through wheat, visible as a ripple, audible as a swelling murmur that became gasps that became weeping. The woman with the glass rod dropped it and clutched her own face and made a sound Vesper would hear for the rest of her life: the sound of someone meeting themselves again after years apart. The man with the ingots let them fall, all of them, a cascade of pale light shattering on the terrace stones, and he sank to his knees saying a name over and over—his own name, tasting it, a man returned from a long voyage.

"Halloran blood sings true," Ilsa breathed beside her, tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face. "Look. *Look.*"

Vesper looked. The ripple was spreading outward through the whole vault now, terrace to terrace, catwalk to catwalk. Hundreds of faces lifting. Hundreds of mouths remembering speech. Some of them were screaming. Most were weeping. All of them were *looking at her*—and she understood, with a lurch, that she had just done the most dangerous thing possible in a place built on stolen souls. She had reminded the stolen what they were.

Somewhere overhead, an alarm began to toll—not the deep bell, but a high shrieking note, and doors she hadn't noticed in the vault walls began to grind open.

"They'll send the wardens," Ilsa said, suddenly all business, hauling Vesper behind a vat-support pillar. "Hollowed enforcers—the ones they emptied deepest, so nothing wakes in them. Blades, chains, no hesitation. We have minutes."

"We?" Vesper managed. The song had cost her more than she expected; her throat felt scoured, and the mark on her arm was burning now, visibly glowing through its wrappings like a coal wrapped in linen.

"You think I whispered secrets through your cell door planning to die in obscurity?" Ilsa's grin was feral. "Girl, I've been hollow for eleven years. You just gave me back my sister's face—I haven't seen it in my mind since they took it. I intend to *use* this."

Across the vault, the newly-waking were doing what newly-waking people do when they realize where they are and what was taken: they turned on the machinery. Valves were wrenched open. Pipes were torn free. One enormous man—still half-hollow, waking in patches, roaring fragments of himself—wrapped his arms around a soul-vat's support strut and *heaved*, and the vat tilted, and the green-gold light within sloshed and screamed, a chorus of voices briefly audible as the surface broke, and the sound of it drove the crowd past fury into frenzy.

"The cells," Vesper shouted over the rising chaos, grabbing Ilsa's shoulder. "There's someone in the cells I came for—"

"Your brother. I know. The warden post sits between us and the stair." Ilsa pulled a length of pipe from the wreckage, tested its weight like a woman who had once known weapons. "Stay behind me and keep singing. Every note you spend buys ten of them back."

So Vesper sang.

She sang walking, sang fighting, sang the fragment over and over as they pushed through a riot that was becoming a revolution. Wherever her voice reached, hollowed straightened like plants finding sun—some of them taking up tools, some simply standing in the path of the wardens, unarmed and unmovable, daring the enforcers to cut down people who had just remembered their children's names. The wardens' line wavered. Chains slipped. And in the gaps, the crowd flowed like floodwater, carrying Vesper and Ilsa toward the cell block in a current of rage and grief and impossible, unreasonable joy.

This, Veper thought wildly, ducking a wild swing, hauling Ilsa clear of a collapsing scaffold. This is what the Regent feared. Not armies. Not bargains. *A voice.* The god hadn't needed warriors. It had needed only to sing, and people remembered they were people, and no throne ever forged could survive that.

The cell block doors loomed ahead—and between them and freedom, a wall of wardens, twenty deep, shields locked.

Vesper drew breath for the loudest thing she had ever done.

* * *

Tam hit the cell door with his shoulder and felt, for the first time in days, something answer besides silence: the lock was old, the frame rotten, and he was—miraculously, gloriously—*strong*. His body had been kept fed and rested, a vessel in good repair, and whatever the vats had leeched from his mind they'd left his muscles mercifully intact.

The door burst. He staggered into the corridor, into chaos—alarms, running figures, and underneath it all, threading through everything like gold wire in dark cloth, *her voice*.

He followed it.

Not because he knew the way. Because following Vesper's voice was the first skill he'd ever learned, back when they were children of the same bad winter in the same mercenary camp, and some skills live deeper than souls. Left at the junction where the screams were loudest. Down the stair where the light ran green-gold. Through a doorway choked with fleeing servants—

And out onto a terrace overlooking the central vault, in time to see her.

She stood on the steps of the warden platform with a mob at her back and wardens before her, and she was singing with her arm raised high, and the wrappings had burned away from the mark, and it *blazed*—a line of silver-white script coiling from her wrist to her elbow, alive, luminous, writing and rewriting itself in letters older than the mountain. Where the light fell, wardens stumbled. Where it fell further, they *woke*—half of them must have been hollowed too, Tam realized, watching armored figures drop their shields and sink down clutching their heads as decades of stolen self came home like birds to a roost.

The shield wall broke from the inside.

The crowd surged forward, and Tam surged with it, shouldering through bodies, shouting her name against the roar—"VESPER!"—and whether she heard him or whether the mountain itself parted for her, the space between them collapsed, and then he had her by both arms, real and solid and blazing, and she had him, and for three seconds neither of them said anything at all because there were no words with enough room in them.

"You're supposed to be soup," she finally got out, wrecked and laughing and crying all at once.

"You're supposed to be quiet," he shot back. "Your god has terrible taste in miracles."

Above them, the alarm changed pitch—deeper now, angrier, and a new sound rolled down through the vault's high darkness: a grinding, groaning groan of vast weight shifting, like a throne adjusting, like a crown cracking another degree further.

Isla appeared at Vesper's elbow, bleeding from a scalp wound and grinning like a war god. "The Regent's coming. All of it. Everything it has." She pointed upward, to where the vault's distant ceiling had begun to pulse with sick red light. "Whatever you two are going to do next—do it climbing."

Vesper looked at Tam. Tam looked at Vesper. Four hundred and eleven drips ago he had been dissolving in the dark, and now he was grinning like an idiot in the middle of a revolution, and there was nowhere else in any world he would rather stand.

"To the vats," she said. "We end the bargain at its source."

And the mountain shook, and the riot roared, and they ran.
