## 17. Sable's Choice

The vaults of the Undercourt had been built to withstand gods.

Sable had known this since childhood, when her grandmother first led her down the Processional Stair and showed her the soul-vats glowing in their tiers like orchards of captive suns. *This is our strength,* her grandmother had said, one cold hand on Sable's shoulder. *This is why no house rises above ours.* The glass had been grown, not made—coaxed over centuries from the mountain's own marrow until it could hold a screaming soul without so much as a tremor. Nothing broke it. Nothing had ever broken it.

Tonight, a mercenary woman with a dead god's mark on her arm was breaking it with a song.

Sable stood at the vault gallery's upper rail, her white court-silks torn at the shoulder where a hollowed laborer had seized her in passing—not in malice; the man had simply not seen her, because for the first time in his shorn life he was seeing something else—and watched the chaos she herself had spent two centuries engineering.

It was going wrong. That was the truth of it. It was going magnificently, gloriously wrong.

Below, the great tiered amphitheater of vats blazed with unrest. Vesper Halloran stood on the central causeway, small and fierce as a struck flint, singing the fragment Sable had taught her—three notes of the Veil's stolen song, nothing more, barely enough to hum around a campfire. But three notes were enough. Three notes were always enough; that was the horror of what the Regent had done, stealing a voice that lived in everything, because a voice like that could never be fully silenced, only scattered, only hidden, only held down by weight of crown and contract and fear.

Where the song passed, the hollowed woke.

Sable watched it happen again and again across the tiers: a laborer hauling soul-slurry would stumble, straighten, and look at their own hands as though meeting an old friend after a long imprisonment. Some wept. Some screamed. One woman near the western stair began laughing, high and helpless, and did not stop. And every soul that woke reached for its neighbor, and the song passed along the causeway like fire through dry grass, and the vats themselves—the unbreakable vats—began, very softly, to ring.

They resonated. Of course they did. They were full of voices.

*You planned for a riot,* Sable told herself. *You planned for exactly this.* She had spent decades cultivating the discontent of the shorn, mapping the guard rotations, seeding the rumor that the Regent's crown was cracking—that the starlight fused into it flickered now at odd hours, that the Regent's own shadow had begun to stutter. She had brought Vesper down the Gate of Teeth knowing full well what the girl's blood would ignite. A succession crisis. That was all it was ever meant to be. The Regent destabilized, the throne vacant, and House Vael—her house, ruined, exiled, name scraped from the Processional records—restored by acclamation of a grateful court.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan.

The plan had not included Maerin Halloran's daughter standing in the middle of the burning vaults with tears cutting through the grime on her face, singing to people whose names the court had sold a hundred years before she was born.

The plan had not included the way Vesper looked up, just then, and found Sable at the rail—as if she'd known all along exactly where Sable would be standing.

Their eyes met across the blazing amphitheater, and Sable felt two hundred years of careful exile tilt beneath her feet.

* * *

The guards found her within minutes. Of course they did. She had made certain her part in the night would be discoverable—she had left the vault keys' impression in wax on her desk, had spoken the gate-words aloud in a corridor she knew was watched. A traitor's trail, laid neat and legible, pointing back toward House Vael. Let them come. A restored house needed a martyr's story, and there was no better story than the exiled noble who struck the blow that toppled the tyrant.

What she had not planned for was the Regent's captain finding her calm, and reading something in that calm that made him smile.

"Lady Sable," he said, backing her against the rail while his squad sealed the gallery doors behind him. Around them the vault-song swelled, those ringing harmonics rising through the floor like water coming up through stone. "The Regent bids you watch."

"Watch what?" she asked, and heard her own voice do what it had been trained to do since birth: stay smooth, stay bored, stay above.

The captain gestured downward.

At the far end of the amphitheater, past the tiered orchards of light, a smaller vault stood apart—the collateral vault, where debts in progress were kept. Where Tam's soul had been poured six days ago, already half-drained into the Regent's reserves. And now, as Sable watched, the Regent itself descended the spiral stair to stand before it—tall and terrible in its robes of woven dark, the crown of fused starlight guttering visibly even at this distance, cracks crawling its circumference like frost on a windowpane.

It pressed one hand flat against the collateral vault's glass.

And it *drank*.

Sable had seen feeding before. Every noble child saw it once, as instruction: the measured siphon, the courtly portion, the Regent taking only what contracts permitted. This was not that. This was gluttony. This was terror. The light in Tam's vat dimmed in long, desperate gulps, and the Regent's cracked crown flared brighter with each swallow, and Sable understood—with the clarity that arrives too late and all at once—what she had actually set in motion.

The Regent wasn't putting down a rebellion.

The Regent was *feeding*, because the stolen voice inside the crown was tearing loose, and the only thing that quieted the tearing was more soul, more light, more borrowed song—and the vaults were ringing, the whole harvest was waking, and soon there would be nothing left in this mountain quiet enough to swallow.

"Your mercenary," the captain said conversationally, "sings very prettily. The Regent wonders whether she'll still be singing when her brother is slurry." He turned to Sable, and his smile widened. "And the Regent wonders about you, my lady. It remembers your grandmother. It remembers what your house tried, last time. So here is its offer, which I am instructed to deliver before witnesses: open the master seal. Quell the vats. Sing the counter-hymn and put the shorn back to sleep, and House Vael will be restored tonight—by decree, above ground, with lands and name and title entire. The Regent is generous to useful nobility."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can watch the brother finish draining while the mercenary watches you watch. And House Vael will be struck from memory itself. There are ways. We have the tools. Your grandmother's name is already half-gone; you've read the Processional rolls."

Below, the Regent lifted its dripping hand from the glass. Tam's vat had gone grey as old moonstone.

Sable looked at the master seal.

It sat in its alcove twenty paces to her left: a disc of black glass the size of a wagon wheel, ringed with the sigils of every noble house that had ever signed the binding—most of them dead names now, including her own, her family's crest worn nearly smooth by two centuries of other hands refusing to touch it. One word from her, one note of the counter-hymn her grandmother had drilled into her before exile, and the vats would still. The waking would end. Ten thousand souls would sink back under, dreamless, obedient, and the Regent would have its quiet mountain back, and Sable would have everything she had crawled through darkness two hundred years to reclaim.

She thought of her grandmother's hands on her shoulders. *This is our strength.*

She thought of Maerin Halloran, thirty years ago, standing in a snowfield above Greyhollow with her collar open and her mark bare, saying: *You want me to sign my daughter's future into your glass, Sable, and you call that an alliance.*

*I call it arithmetic,* Sable had answered.

Maerin had signed anyway. To save the wards. To save the border towns, the caravans, the world above—because someone had to pay for the peace, and Maerin had decided it would be her line rather than ten thousand strangers. Sable had watched her sign, and admired her, and said nothing, and gone home to her vaults and her ledgers, and told herself for three decades that love was a currency like any other and hers had simply been spent unwisely.

Now the daughter of that signature stood below in the firelight, singing her throat raw for souls she didn't owe and couldn't save alone, and the master seal waited beside Sable like an open mouth.

The captain watched her. The guards watched her. Somewhere below, the Regent turned its cracked crown slowly toward the gallery rail, and even across the distance Sable felt its attention land on her like a physical weight, patient and enormous and hungry.

*Choose,* said the weight.

*Yes,* Sable thought. *That is precisely what I am doing. I have simply been choosing for longer than you know.*

* * *

"I'll need the gallery cleared," she said. "The counter-hymn is house-secret. No ears but mine and the seal's."

The captain hesitated. Hunger made courts stupid; that was the first law of them. He waved his squad back to the doors.

Alone—or near enough—Sable walked to the master seal. Her reflection swam in the black glass: a thin woman in ruined silk, two hundred years old and looking forty, wearing an expression she did not recognize on her own face until she named it. Hope. Ugly, late, undeserved hope.

"You knew," she said quietly, to the reflection, or to Maerin, or to no one. "All those years ago. You knew what you were signing, and you signed it anyway, and you never once asked me to stop it. You just left your ledger where your daughter would find it." Her breath fogged the black glass. "You trusted me to fail, Maerin. You trusted me to fail *loudly enough*. Is that it? Was that the arithmetic?"

The seal gave no answer. Seals never did. That was the whole disease of this court: everything down here spoke except the things that mattered.

Below, the Regent began to climb toward the gallery.

Sable pressed both palms flat against the cold disc, felt the old sigils bite her skin, and did the one thing her grandmother had forbidden above all others. She did not sing the counter-hymn. She sang the *opening*—the deep house-song of Vael, the vault-key melody, the tune that had opened every door her family ever built, back when House Vael had been the Undercourt's keepers of thresholds rather than its exiles. Her voice caught on the first note. Two hundred years of disuse. Then it steadied, and the black glass drank the sound, and the sigils lit one by one—dead house after dead house, waking in sequence like lamps down a buried road—and the master seal turned.

Every vat in the amphitheater opened at once.

The sound was not a sound. It was the *absence* of the silence the court had been marinating in for a thousand years—a flood, a thaw, ten thousand voices released from glass in a single chord, and the chord had words in it, and grief, and names, and mothers, and the mountain itself groaned on its roots like a ship coming off the rocks. Guards dropped their weapons to cover their ears and then, worse, lowered their hands, because they recognized what they were hearing. Everyone recognized it. It was the thing the crown had been carved out of. It was home.

Sable gripped the rail and laughed, or sobbed, or both.

"There," she said, to the ascending Regent, to the cracking crown, to the grandmother whose strength had been theft all along. "There is our strength. Look at it."

The Regent stopped on the stairs. For a moment—only a moment—the vast attention pulled away from Vesper, from the vats, from everything, and fixed on Sable with something almost like bewilderment, as if it genuinely could not compute a noble choosing ruin.

Then the crown flared, cracks racing, and the Regent screamed—a sound with a thousand stolen voices in it—and came on.

Sable turned her back on it. She had one thing left to do, and it was not fighting; she had never been a fighter. She went down the gallery stair into the flood of freed souls, against the current, walking toward the central causeway where a mercenary woman stood blinking in the new light with her dead god's mark shining like a coal.

Vesper saw her coming and raised her voice over the roar. "The seal! Was that you? Sable—was that *you*?"

"It was the house my family built and betrayed!" Sable shouted back. She caught Vesper's arms—found them shaking, found the girl real and solid and alive, and something in her chest tore cleanly along an old scar. "Listen to me. The Regent fed on your brother—it's nearly finished with him. The collateral vault. If his soul scatters before the song reaches it—"

"Which way?"

"Past the eastern tier. But Vesper—" Sable tightened her grip. "The Regent knows the vaults now. It will defend the collateral glass with everything left in the crown. You cannot fight it. You were never meant to fight it. Whatever your mother wrote in that ledger—whatever inheritance you've been carrying down this whole cursed mountain—now is the hour you spend it."

Vesper's eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "And you? What will you do?"

Sable looked back the way she had come—at the gallery, at the ascending Regent, at the smoking ruin of two hundred years of patient, poisonous work. At the restoration of House Vael, dissolving in the same tide that was freeing everyone else.

What remained of her house was this: a name worth striking from the record, a key worth turning once, and a debt to a dead woman in a snowfield, thirty years overdue.

"I'm going to buy you time," Sable said, "with the only coin I have left. Go. *Go.*"

And as Vesper sprinted for the eastern tier, Sable turned to face the stair, planted her feet in the flood of freed souls, and began to sing—not the Veil's stolen fragment, not the counter-hymn, but her own house-song at full voice, loud as a beacon, drawing the Regent's cracked and starving crown away from the collateral vault and toward *her*.

Toward the last daughter of Vael.

Who had finally, at the very end, chosen correctly.
