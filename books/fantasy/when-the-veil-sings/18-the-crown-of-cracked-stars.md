## 18. The Crown of Cracked Stars

The soul-vats stretched below her like a field of buried moons.

Vesper stood on the catwalk above them—iron grating slick with condensation, swaying faintly with the breathing of the mountain—and looked down into light that should not have existed in a place like this. Ten thousand vats. Ten thousand vessels of glass and bone, each one holding a soul the Undercourt had skimmed from a living body like cream from milk. They glowed in colors she had no names for: a blue like the hour before dawn, a gold like honey held to the sun, a green so tender it hurt to look at. Tam was down there somewhere. She had seen him—*a flicker* of him—in vat nine hundred and forty-one, guttering like the wards at Greyhollow had guttered, before everything ended.

And rising out of that sea of stolen light, at the center of the vault where the catwalks converged, stood the Regent.

They were smaller than Vesper expected. That was the first obscenity of it—that something so vast should wear such a slight shape. A figure in robes the color of deep water, gray-haired, almost grandmotherly, hands folded at the front of them as if in patient prayer. Only the crown betrayed them. It sat upon their brow like a wound that had learned to be jewelry: a circlet of fused starlight, jagged shards of white fire bound together by threads of darkness, pulsing out of rhythm. *Crack. Mend. Crack again.* With every pulse, the Regent's shoulders hitched, a small involuntary flinch, as if the crown were biting deeper into the skull beneath it.

"You came up my stair," the Regent said. Their voice was gentle. It carried across the vault without effort, without echo, as though the mountain itself repeated it for them. "Most who reach this place crawl. You climbed."

"I had a reason," Vesper said.

"Everyone does." The Regent turned, slowly, and Vesper saw their face properly for the first time. It was kind. That was the second obscenity. Lined, weary, human-seeming—the face of someone who had once worried about harvests and grandchildren. But the eyes behind it moved wrong, too many reflections deep, like looking down a well that was looking back. "Your brother's reason was love. Your mother's was fear. Yours, I think—" the well-eyes flicked to Vesper's arm, to the mark hidden beneath her sleeve, "—is older than all three."

Vesper kept her hand away from her knife. It would have been an insult to both of them.

The riot was still burning somewhere above. She could hear it even here, muffled through miles of vaulted stone—the roar of the hollowed, thousands of them, souls briefly rekindled by the fragment of song she'd loosed in the labor-halls. Sable had opened the soul-vault for her; Sable was holding the gate behind her, buying her these minutes with whatever ruin remained of her house's hopes. The whole mountain was shaking itself apart, and here at its heart, the Regent stood calm as a stone in a flood.

"They're coming for you," Vesper said.

"Yes." No denial, no flinch. "I have perhaps an hour. Perhaps two. Long enough, I believe, for one honest conversation." The Regent smiled, and it was almost warm. "You've been lied to by everyone who ever loved you, Vesper Halloran. Your company wanted you gone before your name cost them coin. Your brother signed his soul away rather than tell you he'd failed you. Sable—" a flicker of something old and tired crossed that kind face, "—Sable loved your mother and let her die alone with a ledger full of sins. I am the only creature in this mountain who has never pretended to be anything but what I am. So let me offer you the truth entire, and then let me offer you everything else."

"Start with the truth, then."

"The Veil did not abandon you." The Regent began to walk—not toward her, but along the crown of the vault, unhurried, the starlight crown throwing broken shadows against the glass vats below. "Your god has been screaming for three years. Did you know that? Not silent. *Screaming.* Every night you knelt and prayed into that silence, you were praying into a mouth that was already full." The Regent paused beside a vat near the walkway's edge, and inside it a soul turned over like a sleeper, pale gold, restless. "I took its voice. All of it. I took it because the binding your mother brokered required a voice to anchor the border wards—a god's voice, sung into stone, holding back everything that wants to pour through the Thornfell passes. And I thought: why should a god hold that burden? Why should anyone hold it, when power is only ever wasted on those too weak to use it?"

"And it's killing you." Vesper nodded at the crown. At the way the Regent's hand had drifted, unconsciously, toward it—as if to steady it. As if it might fall off. "The servant told me. The song doesn't fit inside you. You swallowed a god and it's cracking you open from the inside."

The Regent laughed. It was a terrible sound, because it was genuine—cracked and wheezing and delighted, the laugh of someone who had not been surprised by anything in centuries and found it refreshing. "The hollowed girl told you. Of course. Yes, child. I am dying. I have been dying since the night I devoured the Veil's voice, and every year the dying gets louder. The stars went out because the crown can no longer hold the shape of the sky. When I end, the song ends with me, and the wards with the song, and everything your mother sold her bloodline to protect comes pouring through the passes like water through a rotted dam." The Regent spread their hands, grandmotherly, reasonable. "Unless, of course, someone takes it from me."

"No," Vesper said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"I don't need to. I watched you build a kingdom on other people's souls. I walked through villages of hollowed farmers who smile at their children because there's nothing left inside them to weep with. I saw my brother floating in your glass like a firefly in a jar. Whatever you're offering, it comes wrapped in that." She jerked her chin at the vats. "No."

"Then watch closely," the Regent said softly, "while I offer it anyway. Because you will want to say yes, Vesper Halloran. That is the cruelty of honest bargains—they only work when they are real."

* * *

The Regent descended to her.

It should have been majestic and was instead pitiful—the ruler of the Undercourt picking her way along the catwalk with one hand on the rail, breathing carefully around the pain of her own crown, each step deliberate as an old woman crossing ice. Vesper did not move to meet her. She stood at the convergence point where three walkways met, above the deepest row of vats, and waited.

"Here is what happens if you refuse me," the Regent said, stopping six feet away. Close enough that Vesper could see the hairline fractures running through the crown's light, fine as spider silk, spreading even as she watched. "When I unravel—and I will unravel tonight, child, your little song made certain of that—the crown breaks. The stolen voices scatter. Some will find their bodies again. Most won't; the hollowed have been empty too long; there's nothing left in them to come home to. Your brother has been in the vats for five weeks." The Regent tilted their head, almost apologetic. "He is mostly memory now. If the crown shatters violently, he scatters with it. He will not suffer—he will simply stop, the way a candle stops when the wind finds it. Is that the freedom you climbed down here to buy?"

Vesper's jaw ached. She realized she was clenching it. "And if I take the crown?"

"Ah." The Regent's whole face changed—lit from within, the way faces do when hope enters a room. "If you take the crown, I give you the keys to the vats, and you may pour every soul home before the mountain finishes falling. Your brother wakes whole. Not a flicker. *Whole.* The hollowed wake. The stars come back—young Halloran blood holds the song better than mine ever did; your mother's line was bred for it, chosen for it, marked for it since before either of us was born. You could reignite the sky from the top of this mountain and watch Thornfell burn bright all around you while every soul you saved remembers whose hand lit it." The Regent stepped closer. The crown pulsed. *Crack.* The Regent flinched, and covered it, and kept smiling. "Power, absolution, and love. Everything the mercenary life never gave you. Everything you've spent your whole wretched, loyal, hungry life pretending not to want."

"And what does it cost?"

"Nothing you haven't already paid." The Regent's voice dropped, went intimate, went *gentle*, and that gentleness was the sharpest blade in the room. "A little more each year. The song eats the singer—I know; I have been its meal for three centuries. You will forget things. Small things first. The name of your first horse. The taste of your mother's cooking. Then larger things. Faces. Reasons. One day you will look down at these vats and feel nothing at all, and the day after that, you will refill them, because refilling them will seem *sensible*. That is all the crown asks. Not your soul—your soul stays exactly where it is. Just the warmth of it. Slowly. Over years. Until you become me." The Regent opened their arms, wide, welcoming, the fractured starlight streaming off the crown like blood off a blade. "I was a midwife once, Vesper Halloran. In a village above the passes, four hundred years ago. I took this crown because I believed I was strong enough to bear it and still be myself. Tell me honestly: do you believe you are stronger than a midwife?"

The vault breathed around them. Below, ten thousand souls glowed and turned and glowed, and somewhere in that light was Tam, and Vesper felt the offer sink into her like cold water into cloth.

Because that was the obscenity of it. It was a good bargain.

She had spent her life watching people make bad ones—smugglers trading years for coin, captains trading honor for survival, her own mother trading a daughter's unborn soul for the safety of everyone else's children. This was not that. This was clean. Take the crown, save Tam, save the hollowed, save the world above, pay for it in installments of herself, decades away, deniable, survivable-looking. Every mercenary instinct she owned leaned toward it like a plant toward light. *Take the deal. Worry about the interest later. You've always been good at surviving yourself.*

And underneath that lean, quiet as a stone in a riverbed, lay the truth the Regent had just handed her without knowing: *I was a midwife once.*

Three hundred years ago, this thing had been a person who caught babies and believed in her own strength. And now it wore kindness like a mask bolted to a skull, and called devouring a god *stewardship*, and could not remember—if it had ever known—that what it had built was not salvation but a larder.

That was the payment. Not the forgetting. The becoming. The slow, comfortable, reasonable slide into being the thing she had climbed down here to kill.

"You're dying faster than you told me," Vesper said.

The Regent's smile didn't move. Their eyes did. "The song is leaving the crown. Your doing, herald. Every soul you woke with that fragment loosened another thread of it. I have perhaps minutes of coherence left, not hours, which is why I am being so very generous with the truth." The well-eyes glittered. "So. Decide. Take it now, willingly, or watch it break and take your chances with the dark."

Vesper reached up and pushed back her sleeve.

The mark showed black against her skin—the scar-shaped brand she'd passed off to smugglers and wardens for fifteen years, the oath-sign of the silent god. Except it wasn't silent anymore. Since the singing cave it had been warm, and since the riot it had been *loud*, thrumming under her skin like a plucked string that refused to finish its note, and now, standing six feet from the thing that had eaten her god's voice, it burned.

And she understood—suddenly, completely, with the clarity that only arrives at the edge of a cliff—that the mark was not decoration. It was inheritance. Her mother's line hadn't just owed the court a debt. Her mother's line had been the *collateral on the contract itself*, written into the binding, and contracts could be read aloud, and reading aloud was a kind of power, and she was standing in a vault full of witnesses made of glass.

The Regent watched her bare the mark and misread it entirely. Triumph softened the ancient face. "Yes," they whispered. "Yes, child. Reach for it."

"I'm going to," Vesper said. "Just not the way you mean."

She closed her hand around her own forearm, over the burning mark, the way you'd grip a rope, the way you'd grip a brother's wrist over open dark—and began, quietly, to speak her name into the vault.

Behind the Regent, unnoticed, the door at the far end of the catwalk swung open, and Sable stood in it, bleeding, holding it wide.

* * *

The first words of Maerin Halloran's ledger rose up through Vesper like water finding an old channel.

She had read them by candlelight in her mother's cache, days and a lifetime ago: *Let the record show what was given, and by whom, and to what end.* She had thought they were accounting. She knew better now. They were law. The Undercourt ran on contracts the way the surface ran on sunlight, and a contract read aloud by the blood that secured it was not a recitation—it was a summons.

The mark blazed white under her palm. The nearest vats flared in answer, ten thousand colors brightening at once, and the Regent's head snapped toward the sound, and the triumph on that ancient face curdled into something four hundred years younger.

"*What are you doing.*"

"Reading the terms," Vesper said, and did not stop.
