## 19. The Bargain Rewritten

The crown hung in the air between them, turning slowly, shedding light like a wound sheds blood.

Vesper felt its pull the way she'd once felt the pull of a high cliff edge—not a desire to fall, exactly, but an awareness of how easy falling would be. The cracked stars embedded in its band pulsed with a rhythm almost like breathing. Almost like begging.

"Take it," the Regent said again. Its voice had lost whatever courtly polish it had worn at their first audience; now it was raw silk dragged over gravel, beautiful and wrong. It stood atop the soul-vats on a bridge of black glass, and beneath its feet the harvested dead churned in their luminous thousands, faces surfacing and sinking like fish rising to feed. "Tam Halloran walks free within the hour. Your company's debts burn to ash. The wards of Thornfell answer to you instead of to me. Everything I have built, everything your mother helped me build—you could hold it all in one hand."

"And what would it cost me?" Vesper asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Her arm ached where the mark of the Veil sat beneath her torn sleeve, glowing faintly now, warming against the cold of the vaults. "Just my name. Just my self. Just everything I am."

"The crown wears *you*, not the reverse." The Regent spread its hands—too many fingers, she noticed for the first time, joints bending in directions hands should not bend. "That is the secret no ruler confesses. I did not devour the god out of hunger, mercenary. I did it out of fear. Something must hold the wards. Something must pay the price. If not me, then someone. If not a monster—" its mouth curved, "—then a hero."

"There's a third option."

"There is always a third option," the Regent said wearily. "And it is always worse."

Behind Vesper, Sable stood very still at the edge of the bridge. During the riot she had opened the soul-vault, had confessed her love for Maerin in front of half the hollowed laborers of the Undercourt, and Vester had not yet decided what that confession was worth. Redemption purchased with ruin, Sable had called it. Ruin was a currency the Undercourt respected above all others.

"Vesper." Sable's voice was low. "The crown is cracking because the stolen song doesn't fit inside it. If you take it while it breaks, the shrapnel goes into *you*. That's what happened to the Regent. That's why it looks—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. The Regent's face had begun, over the course of this audience, to come apart at the seams: hairline fractures of white light running from eye to jaw, from throat to collarbone, as though something inside were trying to hatch out.

"I know what it looks like," Vesper said. "I've seen it before."

She had. In Greyhollow, three years ago, when the mark on her arm went silent. She had thought then that the god had abandoned her, that silence was abandonment—and she had spent a thousand nights praying into a void that never answered, hating herself for praying, hating herself more for stopping. But the silence hadn't been absence. It had been theft. This creature on the bridge had swallowed the Veil's voice whole, had worn it as a crown of starlight, and the voice had been eating its way outward ever since, patient as water, certain as stone.

The Regent was dying. And dying things made the most dangerous bargains in any world, above or below.

"Last chance, daughter of Maerin," the Regent said. "The vats are stirring. Your brother has perhaps an hour of himself left. After that, even I cannot un-hollow him." It extended the crown on both palms, an offering, a trap, a mercy. "One soul closes the contract. Yours willing is worth ten taken. Do the arithmetic of love. It always favors surrender."

Vesper looked at the crown.

And she did not reach for it.

Instead she reached into her shirt and drew out the thing she had carried down the whole length of the mountain: a scrap of oilcloth wrapped around a book small enough to fit in two hands. Her mother's ledger. She had found it in the cache beneath the old Halloran hearthstone, in a chapter of her life she was still learning to read, and she had carried it past the Gate of Teeth, through the singing cave, down through the bioluminescent vaults where splendor grew like mold on suffering. Every step, it had beaten against her ribs like a second heart.

"You keep talking about the contract," Vesper said. "Maerin's contract. The debt of the Halloran line. One soul per generation, in perpetuity, securing the border wards." She unwrapped the oilcloth. "But you've never actually let anyone *read* it. Not Tam. Not me. You just tell people what it says and expect them to kneel to your version."

The Regent's fractured eyes narrowed. Light leaked through the cracks in its face. "Carefully, mercenary."

"I read it." She let that land. Let the silence stretch across the bridge of black glass until even the churning souls below seemed to still and listen. "All of it. Every clause, every margin note, every amendment in my mother's handwriting. And here's what your version leaves out, Regent."

She opened the ledger.

The pages caught the light of the cracked crown and held it, and for a moment the little book glowed as if lit from within—the way the mark on her arm glowed, the way the singing cave had glowed, the way everything touched by the buried god seemed to remember what light was for.

"The contract names its payment precisely," Vesper said. "*In lieu of souls, the bound party may tender the inheritance of memory: the recollection of the contracting ancestor, held in trust by her line, surrendered freely and in full.*" She looked up. "My mother wrote that clause herself. Did you know that? Or did you skim past it, all those years ago, because souls were easier to take than truths?"

Sable made a sound behind her—small, strangled. When Vesper glanced back, the exiled noble had one hand pressed over her mouth, and her eyes were wet and wide with something that looked terribly like recognition. Like memory.

"She told me," Sable whispered. "Gods forgive me, she *told* me, the night before she sealed the wards. She said, *If they ever come for the blood, give them the remembering instead. Memory is the only wealth that grows by being given away.*" Her hand dropped from her mouth. "I forgot. I buried it so deep I forgot. Maerin, I'm sorry—I forgot *again*—"

"You didn't forget," Vesper said gently. "You were made to forget. That's what the Regent does. It's the only trick it knows."

The Regent moved.

It was fast—faster than anything its ruined body should have allowed—and the bridge shook as it took a stride forward, cracks blazing, too-many-fingered hands reaching not for Vesper but for the ledger. "That clause was struck! Struck by decree of the court, by right of the regency—"

"By right of a *usurped* regency," Sable said, and her voice rang off the vault walls with a authority Vesper had never heard in it, the old blood of the Deep Court rising up through exile and shame like groundwater finding its ancient channel. "The succession was broken when you devoured the god's voice. A regent who steals the throne cannot strike clauses from the founding contracts. Every amendment since is ash. The original terms stand." She drew herself up, and for one moment she was not a smuggler's contact or a traitor or a woman drowning in old guilt—she was nobility, cold and bright and terrible. "And the original terms, Regent, include the memory-tender."

"*There are witnesses required,*" the Regent snarled. "*Two of unbroken soul—*"

"I'm exiled, not hollowed," Sable said. "I count."

"And I count twice," said another voice.

Vesper turned. At the far end of the bridge, limping, scorched, half-carried by two hollowed laborers who should not have had the will to carry anyone—stood Tam.

He was a wreck of himself. His eyes were wrong, filmed with gray where the vats had begun drinking him, his left arm hanging loose as a cut rope. But he was walking. He was *there*. Somewhere in the riot's chaos, someone had pulled him free—a fragment of the Veil's song restoring just enough of him to run—and he had crossed half the burning vaults on legs that barely answered him, because of course he had. Because he was Tam, who had sold his own soul to cover debts he thought were hers, and who would crawl across a mountain's corpse to stand beside her.

"Witness number two," he rasped, and managed something like his old grin. "Took you long enough to get to the good part. I hate missing the good part."

"Sit down before you die, you idiot," Vesper said, and her voice cracked on the word *die*, and she hated that too, and kept going anyway.

The Regent stood frozen on its bridge, and in the blaze of its cracking face Vesper saw—for the first time, clearly—fear. Not the fear of a monarch facing rebellion. The fear of a thief cornered with the stolen goods in plain view.

"You don't understand what you're holding," the Regent said softly. "Memory of the contracting ancestor, surrendered in full. Do you know what lives inside your mother's memories, Vesper Halloran? Do you know what she *saw* when she brokered that binding? What I showed her? What she agreed to never speak of?" Its many-fingered hands spread wide. "Her mind holds the true shape of the Veil's song. All of it. Take that inheritance and tender it, and the song pours out of you into the contract—into the wards, into the mountain, into every ear in this court. There will be no putting it back. No crown will ever hold it again. Not mine. Not yours. The age of the Regency ends, and something *older* begins, and neither of us knows what that is."

"Yes," Vesper said.

The Regent blinked. Cracks of light shifted across its face like ice breaking on a river.

"That's the whole offer," she said. "Yes."

"You would give up everything you remember of her." The Regent's voice had gone quiet now, almost gentle, the gentleness of a knife sliding in. "Every kindness. Every lesson. The sound of her voice. You would not so much as recall her face. You would make yourself an orphan twice over—once in flesh, once in memory—to save one man and free a few thousand strangers. Is that the arithmetic of love? Because from where I stand it looks like arithmetic of grief."

"It's not arithmetic," Vesper said. "That's the difference between us. You count everything. My mother counted nothing. She signed away a generation of her own blood because someone had to hold the wards, and she trusted the future to find a better way than she could see. Everyone in this mountain thinks inheritance means receiving." She lifted the ledger higher, and the mark on her arm flared, and she let the words come the way the god's song used to come—unbidden, inevitable, true. "But that's not what she taught me. Inheritance means *answering*. And here is my answer."

She spoke the words from the ledger's final page aloud.

She did not fully understand them—they were written half in the surface tongue and half in something deeper, a language of covenants older than the court, older maybe than the mountain—and she suspected understanding wasn't the point. Contracts of this kind were not read. They were *meant*. So she meant them. She meant them with Tam bleeding at the bridge's end, and Sable standing witness with tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face, and the hollowed thousands pressing against the vault doors drawn by a sound they could not name, and the mark on her arm singing heat up into her shoulder, her throat, her teeth.

*"In lieu of souls, I tender the inheritance of memory. The recollection of Maerin Halloran, contracting ancestor, held these twenty years in trust by her line. Surrendered freely. Surrendered in full. Let the debt be dissolved. Let the voices be unbound. Let the contract close as it was written to close—with truth outbidding duty."*

The ledger began to burn.

Not with fire. With *remembering*.

Light poured off the pages in threads—silver, gold, a green like summer oaks on a hill Vesper suddenly realized she could see, oh gods, she could see it, a hill above a house with blue shutters, a woman kneeling in the garden with dirt on her knuckles looking up and laughing—

"No," Vesper whispered.

The memory came gently. That was the worst of it. Nothing tore. Nothing ripped. The hill and the laughing woman simply rose out of her like breath leaving the body, and she felt herself reaching for them even as she understood that reaching was the one thing she must not do, and she watched her mother's face grow brighter and thinner and more distant, a window fogging from the inside, a star going out—

*Vesper. Sweetheart. Look at you.*

The voice was inside the light. Inside her, and leaving her.

*Mom.*

*I'm proud of you. I need you to know that, wherever this goes, whichever side of forgetting I end up on. You were never the debt, Vesper. You were always the payment's opposite. Now give them what I couldn't keep, and don't you dare grieve longer than—*

The memory crested like a wave.

Vesper stopped grieving before it finished. She stood on the bridge of black glass and let her mother go a second time—this time entirely, this time forever—and she did not look away, and she did not beg, and when the last thread of silver-green light lifted from her chest and streamed into the burning pages, she heard herself say, clear and steady across the trembling vaults:

"The debt is paid. The contract is closed."

The ledger's light went white.

Then the white light *moved*—outward, downward, everywhere at once. It ran along the bridge like dawn running along a ridge. It fell into the soul-vats, and the vats lit from beneath like lanterns, and the churning faces below stopped churning and turned upward, all of them, thousands upon thousands, mouths opening in a single silent inhale.

And then the mountain *sang*.

Not the faint leak of the singing cave. Not the fragment Vesper had used to spark the riot. The whole of it—the true shape of the Veil's song, which Maerin Halloran had once held in trust and which no crown could hold again—rose through the vaults like floodwater through a breached dam. Vesper felt it pass through her, harmonizing with the mark on her arm until she could not tell where her pulse ended and the music began, and all around her the sound took hold of the hollowed and did not let go.

Gray film slid from Tam's eyes. He gasped like a man surfacing from deep water, sagged against his carriers, and laughed—wet, disbelieving, whole. Across the vaults, laborers straightened out of their eternal stoop. Names returned to mouths that had forgotten them. Somewhere below, Vesper knew, ward-stones were waking along the whole spine of Thornfell, drinking in a song they had been built to carry.

The Regent screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a vessel breaking—which was, Vesper understood as she watched, exactly and only what the Regent had ever been. The cracked crown blazed, then dimmed, then *emptied*, the stolen voices pouring out of it like light out of a shattered lamp, each one flying free to rejoin the song. And as the crown emptied, the thing wearing it unraveled. Fractures widened into seams, seams into gaps, gaps into absence. The too-many fingers went first, dissolving into drifting motes of fading starlight. Then the arms. Then the robes, the shoulders, the terrible beautiful face, which in its last moment wore an expression Vesper would carry to her grave—not rage, not despair, but relief.

"Finally," the Regent whispered, in a voice suddenly small and human and old. "Oh. Oh, it's *quiet*."

And then there was no Regent at all. Only the crown, falling.

It struck the black glass of the bridge and rang like a bell and rolled to a stop at Vesper's feet, dark now, ordinary, just metal and dead stone. The last mote of stolen light rose from it, hesitated—and dove into the mark on her arm.

The world whited out.

When Vesper could see again, she was on her knees and Tam was shouting her name and Sable was gripping her shoulder, and through the vast open height of the Undercourt's central shaft, visible even from this depth, something impossible was happening:

Stars.

Faint, at first—pinpricks of silver showing through the mountain's roots like sunlight through a keyhole. Then dozens. Then hundreds. The freed souls of the hollowed were rising, streaming upward through the vaults in rivers of light, pouring toward the surface, toward the sky, toward the empty heavens above Thornfell that had been dark for one long terrible night.

And underneath it all, beneath the song, beneath the riot and the weeping and the ringing of a hundred waking bells, Vesper heard something new begin.

A voice. Hers—and not hers. Rising up her throat like water finding its level, wearing her voice the way a river wears a valley.

The Veil, singing through her, for the first time in three years.

"Vesper," Tam breathed, staring at her. "Your arm—"

She looked down. The mark blazed like a brand of living silver, and it moved as she breathed, and when she opened her mouth to answer him, music came out instead of words.

Above them, the first freed souls broke the surface of the world—and the night sky over Thornfell caught fire with returning stars.
