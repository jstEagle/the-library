## 20. The Veil Sings

**Vesper**

The song left her throat and did not stop.

That was the first thing Vesper understood, standing on the rim of the soul-vats with the Regent's crown dissolving into ash-light at her feet: she had not ended the song by giving it away. She had started it.

Her memories of Maerin poured out of her like water finding a crack in a dam—her mother's hands braiding salt-grass, her mother's voice counting debts in the dark of their rented room, her mother's face the last time Vesper saw her, turned toward the mountain as if listening. The court took them all, every memory she offered, and instead of consuming them the way the Regent had consumed souls, the deep places *sang them back*.

The vaults lit from beneath.

The soul-vats—those slow, terrible pools where stolen selves had drifted like drowned lanterns—began to rise. Not the liquid itself; the souls in it. Thousands upon thousands of them, flickering up through the bioluminescent dark like sparks climbing a chimney, and each one carried a voice. Vesper heard them all at once and could not distinguish any single one: a woman laughing, a child naming stars, an old man swearing cheerfully at a mule, a hundred thousand small ordinary sounds that together made something so vast it stopped being sound and became weather.

The Regent came apart in the middle of it.

Vesper watched, and found she could not look away, and found too that she felt no triumph—only a kind of terrible pity. The crown of fused starlight cracked along its seams, and what spilled out was not power but *grief*, centuries of hoarded voices tearing free of the thing that had eaten them. The Regent's body unraveled like rope ends, like thread off a spindle, and in the last instant before nothing remained, Vesper would have sworn the Regent's face went young. Went relieved. Went, for one heartbeat, like someone setting down a weight carried far too long.

Then the crown fell, and struck the stone floor, and rang like a bell.

And the Veil sang.

It came up through the mountain the way dawn comes over a ridge—not arriving so much as being revealed, suddenly everywhere, having been there all along behind the dark. The god's song filled the vaults, filled the canyons, filled the petrified petitioners at the Gate of Teeth until Vesper half-expected stone faces to soften back into flesh. It poured through her mark, through the scar-shaped thing on her forearm, and the mark *blazed*, white-gold light running under her skin like veins of ore, and it did not hurt. That surprised her most. Three years of dead silence, and now this, and it felt like being remembered.

"Freesoul."

The voice was everywhere and nowhere and also, unmistakably, inside her own chest. The Veil had never spoken to her in words before. Even now it barely did—it gave her the sense of a name, hers, held up to the light like a coin.

"I hear you," Vesper said aloud, because after three years of silence she was not going to waste the chance on silence herself. Her voice cracked. She let it. "I hear you. Where were you? No—" She pressed her palm flat against the warm stone of the vat-rim. "Never mind. I know where you were. You were *in here*. All of you, all this time."

The song answered without words, and the answer was grief and gratitude braided together, and Vesper stood in it and wept without shame while freed souls streamed past her upward through the vaults like a river deciding, after centuries, to run home.

She found Tam at the edge of the last vat—the shallow one, the holding pool where the nearly-consumed waited. He floated there, or what remained of him did: a shape like a man drawn in fading ink, his edges translucent, his face smooth as an egg where memory had been sanded away. When the flood of returning voices reached him, he shuddered once, hard, like a man coming up from drowning.

Then he opened his eyes.

They were his eyes. Grey-green, crooked-tempered, *his*. Vesper dropped to her knees on wet stone and hauled him out of the pool with arms that shook, and he coughed vat-water that smelled of cold iron and starlight, and then he said, in a voice rusty as a gate hinge:

"Ves. You look terrible."

She laughed. It came out broken and loud and alive, and somewhere above them the mountain caught the sound and passed it upward, and the whole Undercourt rang with one mercenary's ugly, sobbing laugh as if it were a note in the song.

"You're welcome," she managed.

"Did you pay my debt?" Tam asked. His hands were shaking. He looked down at himself—at hands gone solid again, at the color crawling back into his skin—and Vesper watched him remember, piece by piece, what had been taken. "Ves. What did it cost?"

"Nothing," she lied.

He looked at her. He'd known her eleven years; he knew every lie she owned, and which ones she used when the truth would break her.

"It cost everything," she said, "and I'd pay it twice," and before he could argue she pulled him upright, and the two of them stood swaying in the rising tide of freed souls, brother and sister by oath if not blood, while overhead the first crack of true daylight split the vault-ceiling far above like an egg opening.

"Stars," Tam whispered. "Ves. Look. *Stars.*"

Through the fissure overhead, thin as knife-cuts, points of light were appearing in the grey of morning. One. Ten. A hundred. The sky over Thornfell, empty for three nights, was filling again—and not filling dimly, not cautiously, but blazing, star after star reigniting as the Veil's song climbed out of the mountain and touched them one by one like a mother relighting candles in a house after a long illness.

Vesper tipped her head back and watched the stars come home.

**Tam**

He had dreamed of nothing, and the nothing had been almost kind.

That was the part no one warned you about, Tam thought afterward, sitting wrapped in a borrowed cloak on a ledge overlooking the great vault, watching hollowed laborers weep and laugh and shout each other's names as their selves flooded back into them. The hollowing didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. You simply got quieter and quieter, and your reasons got smaller and smaller, until one day you couldn't remember why you'd ever wanted anything, and that felt like peace. He had been three days from the bottom of that peace when the song reached him.

He remembered fragments now, surfacing like debris after a flood: Vesper's voice in the dark of the cells. *Do you remember the winter we ate only partridges and Brannoc pretended they were pheasant? Do you remember the ford at Marlow, the one that stole your boot? Do you remember your mother's kitchen, the blue door, the smell of onions?* She had sat beside what was left of him and fed him memories like a nurse spooning broth, hour after hour, and each one had caught somewhere in him—a hook, a handhold—and he had clung to them the way a drowning man clings to floating timber, without understanding why, without knowing even whose hand was feeding him, only knowing that the hand was *hers* and therefore good.

"You're staring," Vesper said, dropping onto the ledge beside him.

"I'm allowed. I was dead-ish. Staring privileges." He pulled the cloak tighter. Below them, the Undercourt was transforming in real time. Without a Regent to feed, the machinery of consumption had simply—stopped. Soul-brokers who'd spent centuries weighing human lives in ounces stood bewildered amid their own abandoned scales. Hollowed servants, restored, were walking out of the noble houses carrying whatever they liked, and no one stopped them, because half the nobility were hollowed themselves and the other half had fled into the deep tunnels to wait out whatever came next.

Except one hadn't fled.

Sable stood below them in the great vault, in the wreckage of her house's ambitions, directing the restoration of the vats with the crisp authority of someone who had been born to command and exiled from using it. She'd torn the silver from her sleeves—Tam noticed the bare forearms, the stripped-down black—and she was arguing with a cluster of newly-restored laborers about water rights, of all things, and losing, and looking delighted about it.

"She stays, then," Tam said.

"She stays." Vesper's voice did something complicated on the second word. Tam had heard the story by now—all of it, pieced together from Sable's confession and the servant's testimony and Vesper's own tight-lipped account. Loved Maerin. Failed Maerin. Guided Vesper down partly for Tam's sake and partly for her own house, and had chosen, at the last, in front of everyone, to say so out loud. Tam understood better than most what it cost to be sold and bought back. He suspected Sable was learning it too.

"Are you going to forgive her?" he asked.

Vesper was quiet a moment. "I'm going to write to her," she said finally. "Forgiveness is a longer road than a letter. But I'll start the road." She glanced sideways at him. "You'd have done worse. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were composing the argument. Your eyebrows do it."

He laughed, and the laugh hurt his ribs, and he laughed harder because of it, because hurting meant being in a body, and being in a body meant being *him*, wholly, entirely, debt paid and self returned. Around them the freed souls were still streaming upward—slower now, gentler, a migration rather than a flood—and where they passed, the bioluminescent vaults brightened as if the mountain itself were waking from a long fever.

Above, through the great fissure, the sky had gone fully dark with the natural arrival of night, and it was *full*—thick with stars, more stars than Tam had ever seen, as though three years of extinguished light had been banked somewhere and now spent all at once. The Thornfell constellations his grandmother had named. The Shepherd. The Salt Road. The Drowned Queen, whom sailors cursed and smugglers loved.

"There," Vesper said softly, pointing.

High in the vault of the sky, in the place where old maps marked nothing at all, a new constellation was assembling itself—star after star kindling in a pattern no astronomer had charted, a curve like a cupped hand, like a mouth open in song.

"What is it?" Tam asked.

Vesper flexed her right arm. Beneath her sleeve, the mark glowed steady and gold, and when she spoke again her voice had harmonics in it that were not entirely hers.

"That's me," she said. "Apparently."

* * *

**Vesper**

They walked out of the mountain through the Gate of Teeth at dawn, and the teeth had softened.

Not literally—the petrified petitioners still ringed the canyon mouth, centuries of stone—but the Veil's song moved through them constantly now, and where it passed, moss grew in the cracks of their faces, and small flowers opened between their fingers, and Vesper chose to believe that somewhere inside each statue, whatever remained of each petitioner was listening. Some of the freed souls, she'd learned, had refused to leave. Had curled up inside the stone like seeds, waiting for a spring no one could promise them. She had promised it anyway. She was apparently in the business of making promises to impossible audiences now.

Sable met them at the gate's threshold, where the mountain's shadow ended and the sunlit world began. She stopped exactly there, one foot in each country, as if the line mattered.

"The court will need years," Sable said. "Decades. The ledgers alone—" She shook her head. "There are contracts older than kingdoms down there. Debts written in blood on vellum that screams. Someone has to unwrite them honestly, or the next Regent will simply write them dishonestly again."

"You're volunteering," Tam said.

"I'm volunteering," Sable agreed. Then, to Vesper, quieter: "Your mother once told me that the difference between a prison and a house is whether anyone sings in it. I intend to find out if she was right."

Vesper studied her—this exile, this traitor, this woman who had loved Maerin Halloran across whatever distance the living are permitted to love the dead. "If you ever need—"

"You'll know," Sable said. "You're the god's voice now. I suspect knowing is the job." Something moved across her face, quick and old. "She'd have been proud of you. And furious. Mostly proud."

"Mostly," Vesper said, and found that it was true enough to carry, and that carrying it felt lighter than carrying the anger had.

They parted there—no embrace, because some histories don't fit in arms—but Sable pressed something into Vesper's palm as they separated: a small key, dull iron, shaped like none of the locks Vesper had ever seen. "For when the letters come," Sable said. "You'll understand eventually. Or you won't, and it will make a fine story to tell drunk."

Then Vesper and Tam climbed out of the canyon into morning, and behind them the mountain hummed, and ahead of them the world was unbearably, ordinarily bright.

They made Greyhollow in four days. Vesper sang as they walked—couldn't help it; the songs rose in her like water rising in a well—and wherever the music touched, things grew. Grass thickened along the trail. A lame crow they fed flew off crooked but flying. On the third evening Tam fell asleep to her voice and woke claiming he'd dreamed of his mother's kitchen, the blue door, the smell of onions, and had woken crying and glad of it.

Greyhollow's walls appeared on the fourth dusk, and Vesper's heart climbed into her throat, because the last time she'd seen these walls the company had scattered and Brannoc had told her to burn her own skin and disappear, and family, once broken that way, did not always mend.

But the gates were open. And the company was waiting.

Brannoc stood at the head of them—grayer, leaner, his sword conspicuously sheathed—and behind him, in ranks loose as a harvest crowd, were the Thornwood Company: forty-one faces she'd expected never to see again, every one of them watching her come down the road with the stars igniting overhead in her new constellation, her mark glowing gold through her sleeve like a coal that wouldn't die.

Brannoc crossed the ground between them slowly. Stopped. And then Captain Brannoc, who had never knelt to any god or king or creditor in twenty years of selling his blade to the highest bidder, went down on one knee in the dust of the road—not to the Veil, whose song hung faint and sweet in the evening air, but to Vesper, to her choice, to the road she had walked when every sane road ran the other way.

"Halloran," he said, and his voice was rough as gravel. "The company owes a debt."

"No," Vesper said. She reached down and hauled him up by the collar, the way he'd hauled her out of tavern brawls a dozen times, and she was grinning and crying and her mark was singing. "No, Captain. This one's paid. But the roads are open, the borders are broken, and there's a whole world up here that hasn't heard the news." She looked past him at the company, at Tam already trading punches and insults with the twins, at the stars wheeling bright and whole above Thornfell. "Who wants to do some honest smuggling?"

Somewhere behind the mountains, in a court learning to be a house, a letter was already being sealed with dull iron and sent up toward her by strange couriers—and whatever it carried, Vesper suspected, would change the shape of every road she had left.

But that was tomorrow's weight. Tonight there was music, and family, and a god who answered.

Tonight, the Veil sang, and Vesper Halloran sang with it.
