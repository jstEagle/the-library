## 21. Marked

The mountain let her go gently.

That was the strangest part. Vesper had braced herself for the climb back to be a fight—for stone to clutch at her boots, for the dark to resent losing what it had held so long. Instead the passages lit as she passed, veins of pale gold waking in the rock like a body remembering its own blood, and the air grew warmer with every turn upward, until she emerged through the Gate of Teeth into morning light that made her stagger.

The stars were still there.

Not in the sky—the sun owned that—but in everything else. Dew on the canyon stones caught it and scattered it. The petrified petitioners ringing the gate stood exactly where they'd stood for centuries, but their faces had changed: the terror frozen into them was softening, feature by feature, into something like rest. One of them—a woman in robes older than any kingdom Vesper knew—had a single tear track down her cheek that gleamed like a seam of silver ore.

Sable stayed below. Of course she did. Someone had to teach a court of the soul-shorn how to be people again, and no one alive knew the vaults better than an exile who'd spent two hundred years memorizing every corridor she'd been banished from. She'd pressed a letter into Vesper's hand at the last fork—"For when your company asks questions you can't answer"—and then turned back into the gold-lit dark without goodbye, because Sable of the Deep Court had never once in her long life said a plain thing when an elegant silence would do.

Tam walked beside her the whole way up. Whole. His footsteps had weight again; his shadow kept pace instead of flickering; and every hour or so he would stop, put his palm flat against the rock, and laugh—actually laugh—at nothing, at everything, at the simple outrageous fact of being one person instead of a fading echo in someone's vat.

"You're staring," he said, without turning around.

"I'm checking."

"For cracks?"

"For you," Vesper said. "Making sure you're still there."

He stopped then and faced her on the narrow ledge, wind pulling at his hair, and his eyes were wet and furious and alive. "Three days I've been whole and you've asked me that forty times."

"Forty-one," Vesper said. "You lost count during the singing cave. You do that now. Lose count."

"I lost count because I was dead for a week, Ves." His voice cracked on it, and he laughed again, that unsteady new laugh. "I remember it, you know. The vat. Like dreaming underwater. And then—" He touched his own chest, wonderingly. "Then a song came down through the floor of the world and pried me open. Your song."

"Not mine."

"Yours." He said it with the flat certainty he used for blade-maintenance and dice odds, the tone that ended arguments. "I know voices, Vesper Halloran. I've heard you sing over campfires for nine years and lie about the words every time. That was you."

Vesper didn't answer. Her arm ached where the mark sat, and it had been aching differently ever since the mountain—no longer the cold scar-seam of a silent god, but something warm and full, like a held breath. Like a throat waiting to be used.

They came down out of the high passes on the fourth day, following the old salt road toward Greyhollow, and from the ridge above the valley Vesper saw the smoke first—cookfire smoke, a lot of it, rising from the walled town in orderly columns—and felt her stomach drop through the mountain behind her.

"That's not refugees," Tam said quietly. "Refugees burn messy."

"No," Vesper said. "That's a camp. That's *our* camp."

* * *

She knew it before they reached the gates: the picket lines set at the angles Brannoc favored, the sentries walking the wall in the overlapping arcs he drilled into every green recruit, the company banner—the grey heron of the Halcyon Blades—limp and faded above the gatehouse but flying nonetheless. Flying when it should have been scattered across half a dozen border towns by now, if the vote in chapter all those miles below had gone the way votes usually went.

The vote had been to scatter. She'd heard it counted. She'd watched Brannoc call it himself, watched him say *we bury the colors and run*, and she'd told him no, and they had parted with his order burning in her ears—*burn the mark, change your name, never come back*—and the whole company watching her walk away from the only family she'd had since Maerin died.

Now the family was camped outside the town whose wards had shattered the night the stars went out. Waiting.

"They came back," Tam murmured.

"People don't come back from things like this."

"These people do. These people followed *you* into Thornfell with contraband strapped to mules for nine years." He bumped her shoulder. "Brannoc's stubbornness has to have come from somewhere, and I refuse to believe it's original to him."

The gates were open. That was the second strange thing. Greyhollow's ward-keepers had sealed them for a generation after the Night Without Stars—you could still see the scars of the shattering in the stonework, hairline fractures spidering out from the archway like frost on glass—but today the gates stood wide, and folk moved in and out freely, and Vesper saw why before she was close enough to make out faces.

The wards were relit.

Faint—candleflame-faint, a shimmer along the gate-arch like heat-haze—but *lit*. And standing beneath the arch, one hand raised toward the stone, was a hollowed woman. Soul-shorn. Vesper remembered the villages on the road down: the flat eyes, the hands that worked without ceasing, the terrible patience of people missing the part of themselves that wanted anything. This woman had been one of them; Vesper was almost sure she recognized the rope-burned wrists, the miller's stoop. But the eyes looking up at the ward-light weren't flat anymore. They were squinting. Frowning. *Trying.*

"She can't hold it," Tam whispered. "Not alone. Not with most of herself still in the ground."

"No," Vesper said. "But she's trying anyway."

And then the woman's head turned, and the frown became something else, and she pointed one rope-scarred finger straight at Vesper on the road and said, in a voice like a hinge that hadn't moved in years:

"There. There's the singer."

Every face in the gateyard turned.

Vesper had walked into ambushes with more grace than she walked into that yard. She wanted her sword hand free and her hood up and the particular anonymity of being one armed stranger among many, and instead she got three hundred people going quiet in a spreading ring, and the Halcyon Blades pushing through the crowd from the far side with Brannoc at their center like the axle of a wheel.

He looked worse than she'd ever seen him. Grey had taken his beard wholesale since the parting; there were new lines around his mouth that hadn't finished deciding whether they were grief or fury. His sword hung at his hip, unbuckled—which from Brannoc was practically a written apology—and his eyes went from her face to her uncovered arm, where the mark lay visible along her forearm, and stopped.

Because the mark was glowing.

Not blazing. Not the vulgar radiance of a relic or a saint. It glowed the way coals glow under ash: a deep, patient gold, pulsing very slightly, in time—with her pulse? with something larger?—and as the yard fell utterly silent, Vesper realized she could hear it. A note. Thin and constant underneath the wind, underneath her own heartbeat, the same faint song that had leaked up through the Singing Cave, except that now it wasn't leaking. It lived in her skin.

"It sings," someone said hoarsely. One of the veterans, Dellis, who had once bet a month's pay that Vesper's scar was from a bear. "Gods' teeth, the scar *sings*."

"The Veil," said the hollowed miller-woman, and her ruined voice cracked on the name like river ice. "It's the Veil. It's *back*."

And then the yard broke—not into panic, which Vesper had braced for, but into something far harder to defend against. People knelt. Not everyone, and not at once, but in patches, like frost forming: the miller-woman first, folding down onto her knees in the dirt with her forehead bowed; then Dellis and the veterans near him; then a knot of Greyhollow townsfolk who had surely never prayed to anything but the ward-stones and the price of salt. A child peered out from between her mother's skirts and stared at Vesper's arm with the frank, bottomless awe only children and cats are permitted.

"Get up," Vesper said. It came out too harsh. She tried again. "Please. Get up. That's—I'm not—I'm a smuggler. I'm a mercenary. Two months ago I was running blacksalt past your walls and lying to your ward-keeper about it."

"You were running blacksalt while the god was dying," said a voice from the crowd—an old man, a carter she vaguely remembered bribing, "and nobody else did anything at all."

"I didn't do it to save anyone. I did it for Tam."

"Then you're honest," the carter said, shrugging. "First honest holy person I've met."

Laughter rippled through the yard—nervous, real, human laughter—and Vesper clung to it like a rail in a storm, because the alternative was the pressure building behind her sternum, the mark warming, the song pressing up her throat wanting *out*.

And through all of it Brannoc had not moved. Had not knelt, had not spoken, had simply stood at the center of his blades with his jaw working, watching her the way he watched a bridge before trusting it with the whole company's weight.

Finally he said, "Walk with me," and turned toward the gatehouse stairs, and that—that gruff, ordinary, captain-shaped command after all the kneeling—was such a relief that Vesper nearly laughed out loud.

* * *

The gatehouse room still bore the ward-keeper's abandoned comforts: a cold stove, a map of the border passes pinned with rusted nails, dust thick enough to write in. Brannoc shut the door, looked at the map for a long moment as though it might tell him what to say, and then gave up on it entirely.

"You went down," he said.

"You voted to scatter."

"I voted to scatter. Then I lay awake all night listening to thirty people pretend to sleep, and every one of them was thinking about you." He pulled out the ward-keeper's chair, considered sitting, didn't. "We broke camp the next morning. All of us. Nobody ordered it. I want that understood—nobody ordered it. They just started packing, and I stood there like a fool, and then I helped them."

"Why?" Vesper asked. "I defied you. In front of everyone. I chose a debt-contract over the company."

"You chose your brother over a strategy." Brannoc's voice was gravel. "There's a difference, and every soul in that camp knows it, because every soul in that camp would have made the same choice and been ashamed of wanting to." He finally looked at her directly. "I told you to burn the mark off. Do you know why?"

"Because a Halloran alive is a debt the creditors will collect."

"Because I thought the mark was a wound." His hand rose, hovered near her forearm without touching, tracing the glow's edge. "Maerin showed me hers once, years ago, drunk on plum wine after the Kettleford job. Said it was the family curse. Said the god had gone quiet and left them holding the receipt." His jaw tightened. "I've spent three years watching you pray to a thing that never answered, Vesper. Watching you sit up past every watch, lips moving, getting nothing. I told you to cut it out because I couldn't stand watching you talk to a grave."

"It wasn't a grave."

"No. It was a prison. And my best smuggler was the key, and I sent her toward it with orders to run the other way." He exhaled slowly. "Dellis told me what the pilgrim caravans are saying. The whole mountain sang. The Regent of the Undercourt is ash. Ten thousand souls walked out of the dark and went home—or went wherever souls go. And the stars came back."

"All true."

"And you're marked forever."

"That part's also true."

Brannoc nodded, once, twice, the way he absorbed bad terrain reports. Then he did the last thing in the world Vesper expected, which was to sink into the dusty chair and put his face briefly in his hands—a man, for one unguarded second, letting the weight show—and when he looked up his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"When you walked out of camp," he said, "I gave the order to strike your name from the rolls. Oath-breaker, deserter, dead to the Blades. It's the law of the company and I'm bound by it same as any man." He reached into his coat and drew out a strip of parchment, the roll-entry itself, ink still sharp: *VESPER HALLORAN — STRICKEN.* "So before anything else happens in this room, I need to fix the record. Because the law also says a name struck in error may be restored by unanimous oath of the officers, and I'll be damned—" his voice cracked clean through, and he pushed on anyway, "—I'll be *damned* if the woman who gave the sky back its stars gets buried in my ledger as a thief."

"Brannoc—"

"Hear me." He stood. "I'm not going to kneel to your god. I don't know your god. I fought beside you for nine years and I never once saw the Veil lift a finger for you, and whatever changed under that mountain, I'll take it on faith the way I take weather." He crossed the small room and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that she could see every silver thread in his beard, and then—slowly, deliberately, the proudest man she had ever known lowering himself joint by joint onto one knee on the filthy floor of a gatehouse—

—he knelt. Not to the mark. Not to the glow. To *her*.

"But I know you," he said. "I know you carried Tam's share of every march when his knee was ruined, and never told him. I know you took the Kettleford contract knowing the pay was short because the widow hiring had children. I know you lied to me for three years about the mark because you were protecting us from the debt, the same way Maerin did before you, and I called it deceit, and it was love wearing armor." His hand closed into a fist over his heart, the old salute of the Halcyon Blades, given now from his knees. "I kneel to your choice, Vesper Halloran. It was better than mine. Restore your name yourself, if the law won't stretch that far. Take the company, if you want it. Or walk away and I'll spend what's left of my life making sure every border town between here and the sea knows your name and sings it right."

The room was very quiet. Through the arrow-slit window, Vesper could hear the camp below: hammers, cookfires, Tam laughing at something, the ordinary machinery of a family reassembling itself.

"You absolute ox," she said, and her voice broke on it. "Get up. Get *up*, you great grey fool, before I start crying in front of the ward-map and it goes in the company songs forever."

"Too late," said Dellis cheerfully, from the doorway, having evidently been listening the entire time. "Already composing it."

Brannoc rose, and Vesper gripped his forearm, hard, soldier-fashion, and neither of them said anything else because nothing needed saying, and it was the finest conversation of her life.

* * *

They stayed in Greyhollow six days, because there was work. There was always work; it was the one prayer the world answered reliably.

The wards needed tending. The hollowed were coming up out of the ground in ones and twos now, climbing the old pilgrim roads from the Gate of Teeth with their souls only partly returned, blinking in daylight like deep fish, and the towns along the border had to decide whether to fear them or feed them. Vesper walked the gate-arch each evening with the miller-woman—her name was Odile, it turned out, and her hands shook less every day—and taught her the anchoring verse Sable had scribbled in the letter: four lines, no more, enough to hold a ward together when sung by someone still learning to be whole. Odile learned it in three nights. By the fifth, the Greyhollow wards burned steady as hearth-fire, and folk had begun leaving bread and candles at the gate-arch, and Vesper pretended not to see because correcting them would only make it worse.

On the sixth night the company built a proper fire in the yard, and someone produced a fiddle, and the singing started—first the old marching songs, then, inevitably, inevitably, the new one, the one Dellis had been polishing all week, which was embarrassing and inaccurate and rhymed *Halloran* with *morale-man*, and Vesper sat in the firelight enduring it with her ears burning while Tam harmonized gleefully and Brannoc pretended to inspect his bootlaces.

And here is the thing she discovered that night, the thing that would reshape every road she walked afterward: when the company sang, her mark listened. And when *she* sang—even humming along, even half under her breath—the mark answered. The gold under her skin brightened. The thin constant note deepened and spread, and the fire flames leaned toward her like flowers toward sun, and every voice in the circle found the melody easier, truer, sweeter, as though her throat were lending the song a floor to stand on. She felt it happen. She felt the Veil moving through her like water through a channel, and for the first time in three years of praying into silence, the channel ran both ways.

*Thank you,* the warmth said, without words, the way a hearth says it.

"Don't you dare get sentimental," she muttered at her own arm.

"Talking to the scar again?" Tam asked, dropping down beside her.

"It talked first."

"Mm." He handed her a cup of the terrible spiced wine the quartermaster brewed, and they drank, shoulder to shoulder, watching the fire. After a while he said, quietly, under the fiddle: "The letter. From Sable. You haven't opened it."

"I know."

"You're going to open it tonight, aren't you."

"I'm going to open it tonight."

He nodded, satisfied, and said nothing more, because that was Tam: he knew which silences were doors.

Later, when the fire had burned to amber and the company slept in sprawls and tangles around it, Vesper sat alone on the wall with the letter in her hands. Sable's seal was a pressed moth-wing of dark wax. The paper inside was deep-court vellum, soft as skin, and the writing was elegant and unhurried and smelled faintly of the gold-lit vaults below.

*Vesper,* it began.

*Your mother used to sit on this exact thought the way you're sitting on that wall, and I loved her for it, and I failed her, and I have finally said so aloud to a court of ten thousand witnesses, which is more penance than pride.*

*Three matters. First: the hollowed recover unevenly. Some will need singers. Send them south when they're ready; the vaults hold echoes that only music can clear. Second: your mother's ledger is balanced—truly balanced, the old bargain dissolved root and branch—but the court will need a new covenant with the surface within the year, and I would rather negotiate it with the Veil's voice than with kings. Think on it. No hurry. I have become, at long last, patient.*

*Third:* —and here the handwriting changed, slower, the letters spaced like careful footfalls— *your mother left something with me the night before her last crossing. She said, "If my daughter ever comes down, give her the rest of the song." There is a verse, Vesper. The one Maerin wrote into the original binding and then struck from every copy, including her own memory, so the Regent could never steal it. I kept it two hundred years. It is yours by every law of inheritance I know.*

*I would have given it to you at the gate, but some things must be chosen freely, and I have watched enough bargains in my life to know the difference.*

*Come down whenever the roads allow. Or don't, and I will send it up by stranger couriers than these. Either way—*

*—sing loud. The deep places are listening, and for the first time in an age, they are listening kindly.*

*S.*

Below the signature, in a different ink, older, worn nearly to invisibility—as though it had been traced and retraced across centuries—ran a single line of verse. Just the first line. An invitation. A door left unlocked.

Vesper read it once. Then she lifted her head, filled her lungs with the cold clean star-thick air of the surface, and sang it aloud to the mountains, and the mountains—every pass, every peak, every listening fold of the sleeping Thornfell range—sang it back.

Her mark blazed like dawn.

Beside her, Tam opened one eye. "New song?"

"Old song," Vesper said, smiling, already reaching for her boots. "Older than the war, older than the wards, older than the debt. Apparently it's mine." She stood, and the stars wheeled overhead, and every one of them was burning. "Finish your wine, brother. We've got a delivery to make."
