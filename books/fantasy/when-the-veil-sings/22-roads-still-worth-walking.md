## 22. Roads Still Worth Walking

The first snow of the season had come down the night before, and by morning the Thornfell passes wore it like a fresh bandage over an old scar. Vesper Halloran walked ahead of her mule with her hood back and the cold biting her cheeks, and she was humming.

That was the thing that would have made old Vesper laugh—new Vesper, the one from three years ago who walked these same passes with a contraband manifest sewn into her coat and a dead god's mark hidden under a leather bracer. That woman had kept her voice low and her prayers lower. This woman sang in the open air where anyone could hear, because now when she sang, something answered.

The mark on her forearm glowed faintly beneath her sleeve, a warm gold thread winding through the old scar tissue. It wasn't pain. It was more like standing near a hearth after years of sleeping in ditches. The Veil's voice lived there now—not the whole of it, not even most of it, but enough. A herald's portion. A listening post on the surface of a world that was learning, slowly, to trust its own sky again.

She paused at the switchback above Greyhollow and looked down. Smoke rose from the town's chimneys in straight, untroubled columns. The ward-towers stood clean and bright, their stones no longer guttering but burning steady as lanterns. And above it all—the thing she still stopped to check every morning, the way some men checked a wound to make sure it hadn't reopened—the stars were out.

All of them. Every single one, right up to the horizon's edge, sharp and white and indifferent in the way stars were meant to be. She'd stopped flinching when she counted them about two months ago. Some mornings she still counted anyway. Habit, Tam called it. Grief learning new manners, Sable would have said.

"You're doing the star-count again." Tam came up the trail behind her, leading the mule, his breath pluming. He looked better than he had any right to look. The hollowing had taken nearly everything—his laughter for a while, then his name, then the memory of why he'd ever sold himself—and what the Undercourt gave back when the vaults opened had come home slightly rearranged. He forgot small things: the name of a tavern keeper in Saltmere, which boot he liked to lace first. He remembered the important ones. He remembered her mother's face, which was more than Vesper could honestly say anymore, since she'd traded those memories herself. "You know they're going to stay lit."

"I know they're going to stay lit," Vesper agreed, and kept counting anyway. Forty-one visible from this ledge on a clear autumn morning. Last month it had been thirty-nine. The sky was still settling, like a house creaking back onto its foundations.

"Brannoc says the caravans are moving again on the east road. Full loads. Merchants actually sleeping at night instead of posting double watches."

"Brannoc says a lot of things." But she smiled when she said it. Six months ago Brannoc had knelt in the mud of Greyhollow's market square, sword in the dirt beside him, and told her—not asked, told—that the company would follow wherever she walked, because a man who'd been willing to drag her home by force owed her at least that much honesty. It had been the least graceful reconciliation in the history of mercenary companies, and Vesper treasured it precisely for that. Nothing real was ever graceful.

They came down into Greyhollow through the south gate, past the ward-keeper's tower where Vesper had once bribed a tired functionary to look away from salt smuggled under hay. The functionary was still there—older, grayer, honest now or pretending to be—and he tipped his cap to her without being asked. Word traveled strangely in the mountains. They knew what she was here, or thought they did: the god-touched woman, the one whose arm sang. Half the stories were wrong and half were worse than the truth, and Vesper had learned to let them be. A herald's job, Sable had written once, is not to correct every lie. It is to give people a truth worth lying about.

Their business in town took until midday. There wasn't much of it—that was the joke of her new trade. Vesper Halloran, once the best smuggler on the western passes, now carried nothing worth stealing. News, mostly. Songs, always. The names of the freed souls who'd clawed out of the Undercourt's vats and were trying to find their families on the surface; the court's new edicts, gentler than anything Maerin's generation had bargained for; word of which villages still needed singers to walk their hollowed neighbors back toward themselves, one fragment of melody at a time. She delivered these things door to door and tavern to tavern, and in exchange people fed her, sheltered her, told her their own news, and sometimes—this was the part that never got old—asked her to sing.

Today a widow in the tannery quarter asked. Her husband had come back from below three months ago, soul mostly restored, but he sat by the fire some evenings looking at his own hands as if he couldn't remember whose they were. So Vesper sat in their kitchen with tallow smoke stinging her eyes and sang the fragment the Veil had given her in the riot-vaults, the one that had turned a crowd of soul-shorn laborers back into people for eleven shining minutes. The widow's husband wept into his supper. Not sadly. Vesper recognized the shape of it. It was the crying of a man hearing his own name spoken in a voice he trusted.

The mark on her arm warmed all the while, steady as a banked coal.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a shrine—one of the old wayside shrines to the Veil that had gone dusty and forgotten during the silent years, and which people were quietly cleaning up again, sweeping out leaves, leaving offerings of bread and candle-stubs. This one had a fresh wreath of rowan berries on the lintel. Tam set the kettle on while Vesper checked the mule's feet, and neither of them spoke until the water boiled, because they were the kind of friends who didn't need to.

"He wrote again," Tam said finally.

Vesper didn't have to ask who. "I haven't gotten mine yet."

"You will. You always do. Mine came with the wool merchant out of Deepvale." He dug in his coat and produced a letter—thick paper, expensive by mountain standards, sealed with grey wax pressed with a sigil like a closed eye. Sable's seal. Sable's absurd, aristocratic, exile-of-a-great-house seal, deployed now on letters that mostly contained complaints about municipal drainage in the rebuilt Undercourt.

Vesper held out her hand. Tam pulled it back.

"Mine first," he said, grinning. "Yours is coming with the salt caravan tomorrow, I'd wager. And you always read hers slower than I read mine, so really I'm doing you a favor—you'll have time to savor."

"You're insufferable."

"I died a little and came back. I've earned insufferability." But he handed the letter over, because under the grin his hands were still, even now, a little too careful with things given freely.

Vesper broke the seal and read by firelight.

*Halloran*—Sable had never once called her Vesper in writing, though she used it freely in person, and Vesper had decided long ago not to ask why—*the eastern vats are drained. Drained, mind you, not merely emptied; my cousin's faction wanted to keep the infrastructure intact against future need, and I want it recorded in whatever ledger the Veil keeps that I called them sentimental idiots to their faces. The stone will take a generation to forget what it held, but stone forgets faster than flesh. We are converting the lowest vaults to granaries. There is poetry in that if you squint.*

*The Regent's crown has been interred. Not destroyed—we tried; starlight does not burn, it only waits—but buried under a mile of granite and a legal instrument forty pages long, which my colleagues assure me is stronger protection than the granite. I signed it. My hand shook. I am told that is normal.*

*A request, and I dislike requesting, so attend: the village of Marrow's Bend has petitioned the court for a singer. Their hollowed are stubborn cases—leased out twice over, worked hard, sung at by no one competent. I could send a court cantor, but the court cantors sound like court cantors, and your people trust you. Come south when the roads allow. Bring the loud one.*

*I remain, against all evidence and expectation,*
*Your servant in the deep places,*
*Sable*

P.S. *Tell your brother the drainage complaint was resolved. He will know what I mean. He always knows what I mean, which remains deeply suspicious.*

Vesper laughed—a real laugh, startling a fox somewhere upslope—and passed the letter across the fire. Tam read it twice, snorted at the P.S., and folded it with the reverence of a man handling scripture.

"She likes me."

"She tolerates you. There's a difference, and she'd be happy to enumerate it."

"She loved our mother," Tam said, quieter now. "It's in every line. Even the drainage ones. Especially the drainage ones."

Vesper looked into the fire. She had traded away her memories of Maerin—the laugh, the smell of ink and road dust, the particular way she'd hummed while cooking—all of it poured into the bargain that dissolved the generational debt and unbound a thousand stolen voices. It had been the right choice. She would make it again. But right choices leave holes, and she had learned to live in hers the way you live in a house missing a wall: carefully, and with a good view of what's gone.

What she had instead was secondhand Maerin, filtered through Sable's dry, grieving prose. Maerin, who argued contract law for sport. Maerin, who once rode into the Undercourt alone to renegotiate a binding and came out having saved everyone and damned her own bloodline, and never told a living soul which cost her more. Maerin, whom Sable had loved and failed and spent a century in exile paying for.

"She did love her," Vesper said. "And she loves us by extension, and it terrifies her, and that's why the letters are forty percent insults." She reached across and tapped the folded page. "Keep that one. I'm building an archive."

"An archive of what?"

"Her. Of them. Everything I can't carry myself anymore." She shrugged, and found the shrug cost less than it used to. "An inheritance isn't just what you're owed, Tam. Turns out it's also what you decide to keep."

Tam looked at her for a long moment over the fire. "When did you get wise?"

"Somewhere between the gate of teeth and the bottom of the world. It snuck up on me."

* * *

The salt caravan arrived two days later at the crossroads shrine above Deepvale, exactly as Tam had predicted, and with it came Vesper's own letter. She read it standing in the trampled snow of the road while the merchants unloaded crates around her, and this one was shorter, and different, and she read it three times before Tam noticed her face and came over.

"What is it?"

"She's found something." Vesper held the page out, but Tam waved it off—he'd wait, he always waited—and so she read the relevant lines aloud, her voice steadier than she felt. "*In clearing the Regent's private archive, my clerks uncovered correspondence predating the original binding. Maerin's hand appears throughout. Halloran, your mother did not merely broker the ward-contract. There are drafts. Revisions. Pages in which the terms are struck through and rewritten in her own blood, apparently in protest.*"

Vesper stopped. The wind moved through the pass, carrying the smell of snow and pine, and somewhere down the valley a cart bell rang, ordinary as bread.

"*We believe,*" she continued, "*that Maerin attempted to rewrite the bargain once before, and was overruled—or perhaps simply outranked. If the drafts are complete, they may contain terms the current court has never seen. Terms that might explain why the debt fell due the way it did. Or might, frankly, contain nothing but a dead woman's anger. Either way. Come south. Bring the loud one. Bring your singing voice and your suspicion, in that order.*

*There is also a second matter, which I will say plainly because you have taught me that plainness is not fatal:* 

*One of the draft pages bears a signature that is not Maerin's. It is yours. Your name, Halloran, in ink older than you are.*

*I did not believe it either. Come and see.*

*S.*"

Tam read the letter over her shoulder—she let him, this once—and when he finished, the silence between them was the old kind, the trail-silence, the one they'd used for years to mean *trouble ahead, how many horses do you think we have left.*

"That's not possible," Tam said. "You weren't born when the binding was made."

"No," Vesper agreed. Her mark was warm under her sleeve, warmer than it should have been, the way it got when the Veil was paying attention. "But names travel strange roads. Ask any smuggler."

She looked south, down the long fall of the passes toward the deep country, where the mountains went down and down into the dark that was no longer devouring anything, where an exiled noble was digging through a dead regent's papers and finding Vesper Halloran's handwriting in a century-old ink. Somewhere in those vaults, her mother had fought this fight before her and lost it in a way that had cost a daughter she'd never meet. Maybe the drafts held nothing but grief. Maybe they held the other half of the song.

Either way, the road went south, and it was a good road, and for the first time in her life Vesper Halloran was walking it carrying exactly what she chose to carry.

"Well?" Tam shouldered the pack and grinned at her, snow starting again, fine and dry, catching in both their hair. "Marrow's Bend first? Or the deep places?"

Vesper rolled her shoulders, felt the mark hum against her skin like a string tuned and waiting, and started walking.

"Marrow's Bend," she said. "Then the deep places. We've got roads still worth walking—and this time, brother, I intend to sing the whole way down."

The stars came out behind them, all forty-one and more, and followed.
