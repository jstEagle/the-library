## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The nightshade in the pheasant was, frankly, lazy work.

Freya Underhill swirled the wine-dark sauce around her tongue and identified it before she'd finished swallowing: nightshade cut with foxglove to mask the bitterness, and something else underneath — hemlock, perhaps, or a cheap imitation of it. Whoever had brewed this poison for Lord Aldous Vane had read perhaps two books and understood neither.

She set down her fork, dabbed her mouth with the linen napkin embroidered with the Vane family's sad little crest — a stoat rampant, or possibly falling — and waited the customary count of one hundred heartbeats.

At heartbeat forty, Lord Aldous began watching her with the particular hopefulness of a man who has paid good coin for a murder and would like his money's worth. At heartbeat seventy, Lady Vane stopped pretending to eat. At heartbeat ninety-nine, Freya smiled at them both, warm as fresh bread.

"The sauce is lovely," she said. "A touch heavy on the foxglove, but I imagine that was intentional."

The color drained from Lady Vane's face so completely that Freya briefly worried *she* might need a taster.

"You're meant to be dead," Lord Aldous said, not even bothering to lower his voice. The dining hall of Vane House was small and drafty, and its walls had ears that gossiped worse than the servants.

"Yes," Freya agreed pleasantly. "I do apologize."

* * *

Everyone in the Veiled Valley was exiled, which made for excellent company if you liked regret and terrible company if you liked ambition. There were disgraced ministers, disinherited dukes, one archbishop who insisted he'd been framed (he hadn't), and a rotating cast of courtiers whose crimes were too embarrassing even for the royal dungeons. The king sent them here because the Valley sat inside a border no magic could cross. No curses, no hexes, no enchanted daggers sliding between ribs in the dark. Just honest, mortal murder, like the gods intended.

And so the court also sent tasters.

Freya had come to the Valley eleven years ago, at fourteen, with a letter of introduction, an empty trunk, and a secret she hadn't fully understood herself: poison didn't kill her. It barely inconvenienced her. Nightshade gave her a mild headache. Arsenic made her sneeze. Once, in her apprenticeship, old Master Corvin had dosed her soup with enough wyrm's-bile to fell a plow horse, purely to test her, and she'd asked for seconds.

He'd gone white as milk and told her never to speak of it. She hadn't. Much.

"Eleven years," said Master Corvin now, pacing her narrow kitchen while she washed dishes. He was eighty-three, mostly deaf in one ear, and had survived three kings by the simple method of agreeing with all of them. "Eleven years without incident, and tonight you announce to the Vanes that you've tasted their treason."

"I announced their *seasoning*."

"You announced their seasoning *with knowledge*." He flung up his hands, sending flour everywhere. He baked when anxious; the kitchen looked as though it had snowed indoors. "Do you know what happens now? Now they wonder. And wondering turns to asking, and asking turns to writing letters south, and letters south turn into *inquiries*, and inquiries—" he pointed a floury finger at her "—turn into you, on a cart, heading to the capital, where people with soft voices and hard eyes will want to know why poison slides off you like rain off wax."

Freya scrubbed a plate harder than it deserved. "What would you have me say instead? That the dish was fine? They'd have poisoned Aldous tomorrow with something better, and I'd be tasting my own incompetence."

"That," said Master Corvin, "is called discretion, and it is the entire profession."

She almost smiled. Almost. But there was a stone sitting under her ribs tonight that laughter couldn't shift, because he wasn't wrong, and she'd known it when she opened her mouth. Something in her was tired of being a lock that everything passed through. Eleven years of surviving other people's murders, one dish at a time, waiting for someone to ask the question she couldn't answer.

*Why are you still alive, girl?*

She didn't know. That was the truth of it. She'd been left at a convent kitchen door with nothing but a blanket and a name stitched in thread that no scholar could place. Underhill. A name for someone who lived beneath a hill, beneath notice, beneath the world. She'd worn it like a borrowed coat for thirty years.

The knock came at midnight.

Master Corvin answered it, grumbling, expecting a neighbor with a sick goat or the watch with a complaint. What stood in the doorway instead was a man held together mostly by mud and stubbornness.

He wore courier's leathers, the royal sigil scraped off the chest as though with a knife. His left side was soaked black, and the smell coming off him was wrong — not blood-smell exactly, but blood-adjacent, the way thunder smells of lightning. Freya's skin prickled. Every hair on her arms stood up and pointed at him like a jury delivering a verdict.

"*Magic*," she heard herself whisper. "He's touched by magic."

Which was impossible. Nothing magical crossed the border. That was the whole point of the Valley, the one law of the place, older than any of the exiles' crimes. Magic stopped at the ridgeline like weather against glass.

The courier's eyes found hers over Corvin's shoulder, and they were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic with what remained of his life.

"Underhill," he rasped.

Her stomach dropped through the floorboards. "Who's asking?"

"You're—" He coughed, and the sound was wet and awful. "Last heir. Last of the covenant line. They burned the others, do you understand? Burned them and salted the ashes, and the king's sorcerers are coming, and I ran it *here*, across the border, because it can't follow me over, it *can't*—"

"It just did," Freya said quietly. "You did. You're carrying it in your blood."

He laughed then, a terrible cracked sound, and staggered past Corvin into the kitchen, into the flour-dusted light, and collapsed against her table like a puppet with its strings cut. From inside his coat he dragged a leather roll, stained dark at one corner, and pressed it into her hands with the last of his grip.

His fingers were cold. Colder than they should have been.

"The map," he breathed. "Don't read it with your eyes. Read it with—"

Whatever she was supposed to read it with, he didn't say. His hand slid from the leather. His head tipped back against the chair. And the strange prickling in the air went out of him all at once, like a candle pinched, leaving only an ordinary dead man in an ordinary kitchen, and Freya standing there holding something that hummed faintly against her palms, warm as a sleeping bird.

Corvin crossed himself with flour. "Sweet saints," he whispered. "Girl. What have you brought into my kitchen?"

"Nothing," Freya said. Her voice sounded far away. "It followed me home."

Then the sky above the valley split open.

Not lightning — she knew lightning, had watched storms walk the ridgeline all her life. This was a seam of green-white light tearing down the center of the night, and through it came shapes on horseback, tall figures in grey, moving down the mountain path at an unhurried trot, as though the border were a curtain and they'd simply decided to walk through it. As though a hundred years of law were a thing you stepped over.

The king's sorcerers. In the Veiled Valley. Where magic could not go.

Could not, apparently, until tonight.

"They'll start with the houses near the pass," Corvin said, very fast, very quietly, the way he talked when he was terrified. "They'll question everyone. They'll find the courier's body. They'll find you, and you'll have that in your hands, and there isn't a lie in this valley big enough to cover it."

Freya looked down at the map. Up close she could see lines etched into the leather, fine as spider silk, and they seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them — rivers rearranging themselves, roads breathing. Somewhere beneath her fear, beneath the cold sweat and the hammering of her heart, something deep in her chest leaned toward those lines like a plant toward sun. Like it recognized them. Like it had been waiting thirty years to be introduced.

"Corvin," she said. "Pack me bread. And whatever poison you have in the back room."

He stared at her. Then, blessedly, he didn't argue. He was a coward, old Master Corvin, but he was an experienced one, and experience had taught him precisely which battles were already lost.

While he filled a satchel with the speed of a man who'd fled things before, Freya wrapped the map against her chest, beneath her coat, where its warmth beat against her heart like a second pulse. Through the window, the green seam in the sky still glowed, and the grey riders were closer now, and behind them the mountains of the border — the mountains that had been the edge of her whole world — looked suddenly less like walls.

They looked like a door.

She had spent her life surviving other people's poisons. Tonight, it seemed, she would finally taste her own fate.

It did not, she noted grimly, pulling her hood up, appear to be poisoned at all.

That was somehow worse.
