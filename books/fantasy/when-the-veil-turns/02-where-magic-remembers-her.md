## 2. Where Magic Remembers Her

The border did not announce itself.

Freya had expected thunder, perhaps — some great tearing of the world, given that she was committing treason against two realms at once. Instead she simply walked up the eastern ridge at dawn, the bloodstained map pressed flat against her ribs beneath her coat, and the air changed the way water changes when you swim past a cold spring. One step: the thin, gray magiclessness of the Veiled Valley, where her whole life had happened. The next step: green so violent it looked wet even from a distance, and a hum in her teeth like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

She stopped. She waited to feel afraid.

What she felt instead — and this frightened her more than any sorcerer could have — was *welcome*.

The path ahead of her had not been there when she'd scouted the ridge two nights ago. Now a track wound down through ferns tall as doorways, and wildflowers turned their heads toward her as she passed, all of them, all at once, like a room of courtiers noticing the queen has entered. Freya pulled her collar up and walked faster.

"Stop that," she muttered at a patch of bluebells that had begun, unmistakably, to lean after her. "I'm nobody. I taste soup for a living."

The bluebells did not look convinced.

By midday the track had led her to a river — wide, silver-scaled, moving fast — with no bridge and no stones for fording. Freya stood at its bank doing the arithmetic of a practical woman: cold water, strong current, a map that must not get wet.

Then the river *paused*.

There was no other word for it. The current slowed, thickened, gathered itself into something almost like attention, and the surface flattened to glass. And in that glass Freya saw not her own reflection but a woman's face rendered in gold leaf and river-light, wearing a crown of woven reeds, and beneath it a name rose up through the water in letters of pale foam:

**UNDERHILL.**

Her own name. Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's grandmother's, back through generations of exiles who had told their daughters nothing except *never speak of the old country.*

"Right," said Freya, in a voice that only shook a little. "That's enough of that."

She stepped onto the water. It held her. It held her the way a hand holds a thing it has been waiting a hundred years to hold again, and she crossed the river dry-shod while every hair on her arms stood up and whispered *this is wrong, this is wonderful, this is yours.*

She did not let herself think about how easy it had been. Not yet.

* * *

Captain Aldric Vane had commanded the king's sorcerers for eleven years without once being surprised, and he found the sensation so unfamiliar it sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

"The border is open," said Tam, his youngest, staring at the ridge line. "Sir, the border is — we've never —"

"I can see that."

"But it *can't* be. The Valley eats magic. That's the entire point of the Valley."

Aldric said nothing. He was looking at the place where the world changed color, and thinking about the courier they had chased for three days — the courier who had bled out in an exile's kitchen with a map pressed under his dying hands. The king wanted the map destroyed and the woman who carried it silenced, and those were orders Aldric had carried out before without losing sleep.

Except the king had used the word *consume*. And the king had smiled when he said it.

"Mount up," Aldric said. "We cross."

Tam stared at him. "Into Aethermoor? Sir, no one comes back from Aethermoor."

"No," Aldric agreed, and thought: *which means whatever happens in there, the king cannot verify my report.* He nudged his horse forward and tried not to examine too closely why that thought felt like relief.

* * *

The ruins began on the third day.

They rose out of the forest like bones out of sand — arches of white stone, columns wrapped in ivy, staircases climbing to doorways that opened onto sky. And everywhere, everywhere, carved into lintels and fountains and broken bells: the same word, worn soft by centuries of weather but still legible to anyone who knew how to read a family name in stone.

UNDERHILL. UNDERHILL. UNDERHILL.

"They built all this," Freya said aloud, to no one, because there was no one. "My family built all this." Her voice cracked on *family*, which had always meant one draughty cottage and a mother who changed the subject whenever the old country came up.

She reached out and touched a doorway — just touched it, meaning nothing by it — and the door swung open.

It had been sealed for a century. She could see the seam of dust, unbroken as fresh snow. And it opened at her fingertips smooth as breath, warm air spilling out smelling of cedar and candlewax, as though someone inside had only just stepped away to put the kettle on.

"Oh," said Freya softly. "Oh, that's — "

*Easy,* finished a voice behind her. "It's easy, isn't it? That's how they get you."

* * *

The archivist lived in the ruin's cellar, or perhaps the cellar lived around the archivist; it was difficult to tell. She was small and gray and surrounded by towers of books that leaned at angles geometry disapproved of, and she introduced herself as Wren, "formerly Chief Archivist of the Covenant Court, currently mad, formerly tidy."

"You're Underhill blood," Wren said, squinting at Freya over spectacles mended with twine. "I can smell it. Like rain on hot stone. Sit down. No — not that chair, that chair remembers the last person who sat in it and he didn't enjoy it. There."

"Wren." Freya perched carefully. "I need to know what the covenant is. All I have is a map and a dead man's last words."

"A map and dead man's words. That's more than most heirs get." Wren pulled a book from a tower with the practiced motion of someone who knew exactly which books would fall and had made peace with them. "Sit still. This is the short version, and it will take until supper anyway.

"A thousand years ago, your ancestors were the Keepers. The covenant bound Aethermoor's magic to the mortal world — shared it out, thread by thread, so that neither realm could hoard it. Magic isn't a treasure, girl, it's a *current*. Dam it up and everything downstream dies. Your family kept the flow honest. Every heir came here, to the Spire, and renewed the binding. And then—"

Wren's mouth twisted.

"And then an heir decided the Spire shouldn't bind the magic. The Spire should *be* the magic. One throne-shaped well of power, and one person sitting in it. He took the mantle. His descendants kept taking it, each a little greedier, until the mortal king's line broke the covenant entirely and sealed your family in the Valley to die out forgotten." She peered at Freya. "You didn't die out."

"We make excellent soup-tasters," Freya said. "Poisons don't touch us. Nobody ever wondered why."

"Hah! Immunity is inheritance, child. Your blood remembers what it's for." Wren leaned forward, and her eyes were suddenly, terribly clear. "So listen to me, because everyone who tells you the next part will tell it differently. You can reforge the covenant. The Spire will offer itself to you — all of it, the whole throne-shaped hunger, enough power to save both realms and rule them too. It will feel like coming home. It will feel like being *loved*. And it will be the easiest thing you have ever done."

"And the price?"

"That's the trap in the question. Everyone thinks there's a price you pay *after*. There isn't. The price is paid first, quietly, in installments. Every time you use the power, it uses you back. Your grandfather-heir started with small mercies — healing a village, ending a drought. By the end he was burning cities to keep them obedient, and he remembered each step as kindness." Wren tapped Freya's chest, right over the hidden map. "You've already tasted it, haven't you? The doors opening. The river holding you."

Freya said nothing. But she remembered the bluebells leaning, the water going glass-still at her wish, the ease of it, the sweetness of being *known* by a whole country after a lifetime of being suspected in one. She remembered thinking: *I could stay here. They want me here.*

"Yes," she admitted.

"Good. Honest. That's rarer than immunity." Wren pushed the fallen book across the table — a map older and vaster than the one under Freya's coat, all roads converging on a single black spire. "Three days east. Rest tonight. And girl — when the Spire offers itself, remember that belonging bought with a crown is just ownership with better lighting."

* * *

That night Freya slept in a guest chamber whose fire lit itself when she shivered, whose basin filled when she was thirsty, whose bed warmed exactly to the temperature of comfort. She lay awake cataloguing these small obediences, telling herself she hadn't asked for any of them.

But she had thought, just once, walking the corridor: *it would be nice if the lamps were brighter.* And the lamps had bloomed like sunrise.

She should have felt afraid. Instead she had felt — pleased. Capable. *At home.*

In the morning she stood at the ruin's eastern gate, the road to the Spire unspooling before her through hills that glittered faintly, like the land itself was gilded with waiting.

Behind her, far off, she heard horses — the king's sorcerers, come through the border that should have been impossible to cross.

Ahead of her, the road bent toward the black shape of the Spire, and every flower along it turned to watch her go.

Freya pulled her coat tight and started walking, and tried very hard not to notice how much she wanted them to keep watching.
