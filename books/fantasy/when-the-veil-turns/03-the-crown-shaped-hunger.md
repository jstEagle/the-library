## 3. The Crown-Shaped Hunger

The Spire did not appear so much as it *insisted*.

Freya had been climbing for three days through a forest where the trees grew in spirals and the moss glowed faintly when she apologized to it — Wren had insisted on the apologies, muttering something about grudges older than kingdoms — and then the trees simply stepped aside, all at once, like courtiers making room for someone they'd been expecting.

The Spire stood in a bowl of white grass, taller than any tower she'd seen, made of no stone she could name. It was less a building than a held breath. Light moved beneath its surface the way blood moves under skin.

"Underhill," the wind said. Not her name, exactly. The family name, spoken by a place that had been waiting to say it for three hundred years.

"I know," Freya muttered. "Everyone's very excited. I haven't even eaten anything suspicious today, so let's not get carried away."

She put one hand against the Spire's surface. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything in Aethermoor was warm to her now — the rivers parted around her ankles with something that felt embarrassingly like affection, doors unbarred themselves before she touched them, and last night a fox had followed her for two miles carrying a lantern in its mouth, which even here she suspected was showing off.

The wall opened. It didn't swing or grind; it simply stopped being a wall where she wanted there to be a doorway, which struck her as the most arrogant piece of architecture she had ever encountered.

Inside: stairs. Thousands of them, spiraling upward around a column of light so dense it seemed almost solid, humming a note she felt in her teeth rather than heard. And along every landing, carved into the walls, names. Hundreds of names. Underhill. Underhill. Underhill. Some of the carvings were deep and proud. Others were scratched shallow, hurried, as if the hands that made them had been shaking.

Freya climbed, and the light hummed louder with each step, and somewhere around the two-hundredth landing she noticed that the climb had stopped being tiring. That the light was feeding her strength back into her legs, generous as a grandmother pressing seconds on a child who hasn't asked.

*Easy,* something whispered, in a voice that sounded like her own but kinder. *It was always meant to be easy for you.*

"That's what worries me," she said out loud.

The voice laughed, warm and delighted, and did not deny it.

By the time she reached the top chamber, her heart was hammering — not from exertion but from recognition. She knew this feeling. She'd spent her whole life watching exiled nobles at their tables, learning to read the moment a man's eyes went soft over a dish he shouldn't trust. This was that look. She was wearing it.

The chamber was round and open to the sky, and at its center stood the source itself: a throne-shaped absence in the light, shaped exactly like a person sitting down. Shaped exactly like *her*, if she sat down.

It spoke without speaking, the way the map had, the way the rivers had.

*I can save them all. The valley. The exiles. Even your sorcerer, if he runs fast enough. Take the mantle, heir, and no poison will ever touch another tongue. Take it, and be home everywhere.*

And oh, that was the cruelty of it — it wasn't lying. She could feel the truth of it in her blood, the same blood that had never once been poisoned, the blood that had been *made* for this, generation after generation of Underhills bred and bound and broken into a key. Every ancestor whose name was carved on those walls had stood exactly here. Had felt exactly this. Had believed, sincerely, that they were saving everyone.

That was how inheritance worked, she realized. Not as an order. As an invitation, extended until someone accepted.

Her knees actually bent. An inch. Two.

* * *

Aldric had served King Maldric for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had learned that the king's silences were more informative than his speeches. So when His Majesty summoned him to the war tent on the border of the Veiled Valley — the border no sorcery could cross, the border Aldric's own company had breached only days ago, bleeding magic into mortal soil for the first time in a century — and the king said nothing at all for a long while, Aldric waited, and listened, and understood that he was about to lose something.

"Captain." The king turned from the map table. On it lay Aethermoor, rendered in silver thread, and threaded through the silver — Aldric had assumed it was decoration — ran veins of gold, converging on a single point. The Covenant Spire. "Do you know why the covenant bound us out of our own realm? Why my ancestors accepted exile?"

"A punishment, Majesty."

"A *harvest schedule*." The king smiled, and Aldric's stomach dropped through the floor of his composure. "The girl's ancestors didn't break the covenant out of greed. They broke it because they discovered what the throne-shaped power truly is. Not a well, Captain. A stomach. It eats magic — ours, theirs, everything — and for three hundred years it has been starving behind that border, kept alive on crumbs smuggled in by bloodlines we thought we'd erased." He tapped the Spire. "The girl will reach it. Her blood will wake it fully. And when it opens, we don't take Aethermoor's magic, Aldric. We take the eater itself. I will sit where her ancestors refused to sit, and I will digest this realm down to its roots, and I will still be standing when the mountains have worn to sand."

There it was. Nineteen years of service, laid out on the map table like a dish at a tasting.

Aldric found, to his mild surprise, that he was thinking of Freya Underhill. He had hunted her across two realms, and somewhere in those weeks he had catalogued her the way he catalogued everything: competent, stubborn, prone to talking to animals. But also — he remembered her at the border crossing, pausing to move a beetle off the road before running for her life. He had told himself it was tactics. Covering tracks. Nobody covered tracks like that.

"You want me to bring her to the Spire alive," Aldric said.

"I want her to *open* it. Whether she arrives willing or carried is immaterial. Your company departs within the hour."

"And if she refuses to sit?"

The king looked at him with genuine puzzlement, the puzzlement of a man who has never once needed the answer to a question. "Then she sits anyway. Bring restraints."

Aldric bowed, because his body had been doing that longer than his conscience had been awake. Then he walked out of the tent, gave his second-in-command an order he had no intention of verifying, took the fastest horse in the line, and rode — alone, deserting, treasonous, and for the first time in nineteen years entirely certain — toward the white grass and the Spire that stood over the horizon like a needle about to be threaded.

He caught up with the king's vanguard too late to stop them, but early enough to see them fail: forty sorcerers against a Spire that recognized Underhill blood and nothing else. Their spells broke against it like waves on a lighthouse. They fell back to regroup, and Aldric slipped past them in the dark, and climbed.

* * *

He reached the top chamber at dawn, breath ragged, sword still sheathed — he'd left it sheathed deliberately, though he couldn't have explained why — and found her standing before the throne-shaped light, knees half-bent, face lit gold, looking exactly like every portrait of every ruler he'd ever studied and nothing like the woman who moved beetles off roads.

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not going to take it." Her voice came out strange, strained. "I'm deciding *how* not to take it. There are apparently several options and most of them end badly."

"The king isn't behind me. Yet. But he's coming, and he doesn't want you dead, Underhill. He wants you seated. You're not the prize — you're the corkscrew."

"I gathered." She laughed, a thin, cracked sound. "You know what the worst part is? It would work. I can feel it. I could save the valley, feed the exiles, end the poisonings, fix everything wrong with both realms — all I have to do is become the thing that ate my family for three hundred years and call it mercy." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "My great-grandmother probably stood right here thinking the exact same sentence."

"Every heir thinks they'll hold it better than the last."

"Yes. That's rather the point, isn't it?" She faced the light again. "So the question isn't whether I refuse it. Refusing is easy. Refuse, walk away, and the Spire waits for the next Underhill, and the next, and some poor descendant of mine stands here in another hundred years with their knees bending. The question is how to make sure nobody ever gets offered this again."

The light pulsed, and its voice turned coaxing, wounded, enormous: *Without a keeper, the bond frays. Without a crown, the realms drift apart. Choose, heir. Choose, or everything unravels.*

"There's a third option," Freya said softly, and Aldric watched her raise her hand — not toward the throne, but toward the humming column of light beneath it, the root of the thing, the stomach of the world. "Wren told me what the covenant was before my ancestors corrupted it. Not one bond. Not one will. *Thousands.* A thread between every living thing, shared consequence, no keeper at all. My family didn't inherit that covenant, Captain. They *collapsed* it. Gathered ten thousand threads into one rope and called it a crown."

"And if you scatter them again?"

"Then no one rules the magic. Everyone shares it. Tyranny becomes self-defeating — starve the land, and you starve with it." Her jaw set. "It will also, I suspect, hurt rather a lot, and change me in ways I can't preview, and leave me belonging nowhere at all."

"That sounds like a terrible bargain," Aldric said.

"It's the best one anyone's ever offered me."

The light screamed — a soundless, pressure-behind-the-eyes scream — and the throne-shape blazed brighter, offering harder, showing her visions now: the valley green, the exiles feasting, herself crowned and beloved and *home*. And Freya Underhill, who had spent her life tasting other people's poisons, looked directly at the sweetest lie ever brewed and said, in the flat professional tone of her trade:

"Sugar over arsenic. I know the finish. It's always bitter at the end."

She drove her hand into the light.

Far below, through every winding stair and carved name, the Spire began — gently, terribly, like a knot coming undone after centuries — to unravel. Aldric braced himself against the dais and watched the throne-shape flicker, fracture, and burst apart into ten thousand motes of gold that poured out through the open sky, streaming east toward the mortal world and west into Aethermoor alike, threading themselves into rivers, roots, wolves, wheat, kings, and tasters.

The light went out. The silence afterward rang.

Freya swayed. Aldric caught her arm — she was cold, suddenly, ordinary, the warmth gone out of her skin — and she looked up at him with an expression he'd never seen on a face that wasn't exhausted.

"Well," she said. "I believe that makes me unemployed."

Below them, far across the white grass, the vanguard of the king's army crested the hill — and stopped, every soldier at once, as the first scattered threads of the new covenant settled into their bones and showed each of them, unmistakably, what their king intended to eat.

One of them dropped his sword. Then another. Then, in a long silver ripple across the hillside, all of them — and Aldric felt the truth settle into his own chest like a coal, warm and undeniable, and knew the king's war was already over.

What came next, neither of them yet knew — only that Freya stood at the center of it, no longer heir, no longer hunted, no longer wholly of either world, holding in her empty hands the smallest, largest thing either realm had ever owned:

A promise, kept together by everyone, and ruled by no one.
