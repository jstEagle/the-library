## 2. Wards Like Broken Teeth

The wards broke at dusk, which Theo would later decide was the most insulting part of the whole affair.

Not at dawn, when a proper catastrophe announces itself. Not in the middle of the night, when everyone is already braced for bad news. Dusk — the exact hour when Brother Anselm rang the evening bell and the whole of Thornfell temple settled into its comfortable rhythm of soup, scripture, and sleep. The hour when a man's guard is lowest and his stomach fullest. Whatever had broken through the border wards, it had chosen its moment like a pickpocket choosing a festival crowd.

Theo was in the scriptorium when it happened, hunched over his forty-seventh page of tithe rolls, when the light coming through the window turned wrong.

He noticed it before he understood it. He always noticed things first and understood them later; it was the arrangement he and the world had come to over thirty years of acquaintance. The light through the east window went from gold to a bruised violet, then to something that wasn't a color so much as an absence wearing color's clothes. His quill stopped mid-stroke. A drop of ink fell and bloomed on the parchment like a small dark flower.

Then the bells began.

Every warding bell in the valley — the temple's own bronze voice, the shrine bells up on the ridge, the little cracked bell in the miller's yard that mostly served to annoy crows — all of them screaming at once, a sound Theo felt in his teeth and behind his eyes. He had lived in Thornfell all his life and had heard those bells tested exactly once, at midday, for the span of three polite chimes. This was not three polite chimes. This was the sound of a valley being told, in no uncertain terms, *you are no longer protected.*

Brother Anselm appeared in the scriptorium doorway, breathless, his robes buttoned crookedly, which for Brother Anselm was roughly equivalent to running through the halls tearing out his hair.

"Theo! Thank the saints you're still here." He pressed a leather satchel into Theo's hands before Theo could say anything about it. "Warning letters. Three of them. One to the garrison at Coldmere, one to the reeve at Harrow Ford, one to the waystation at Miller's Rest. Sealed and everything. You're the fastest walker we have."

"I'm the only walker we have," Theo said. "Everyone else has a horse or a mule or, in Father Ovid's case, strong opinions about walking."

"Which is why it must be you." Anselm gripped his shoulder. "The roads will be chaos by morning. Go tonight. Take the short ways."

*The short ways.* Theo looked down at the satchel, then back at the old monk. Everyone in Thornfell knew Theo Marchetti knew the smuggler roads — the goat paths, the dry creek beds, the holloways sunk below sightline of the highway — because everyone knew Theo knew everything and no one ever asked him what he'd do with any of it. It was, he reflected as he tucked the satchel under his cloak, a strange kind of trust: *we don't ask questions precisely because we trust you.* Trust built entirely on the assumption that he would never use what he knew.

Tonight, apparently, the assumption was going to be tested.

"What happened?" he asked. "The wards—"

"Failed." Anselm said the word like a man spitting out a rotten tooth. "All of them. Along the whole Thornfell line, near as anyone can tell. The wardens at the border tower sent one signal and then nothing. Nothing, Theo. Towers don't go quiet."

Towers don't go quiet. Theo carried that sentence out into the violet dusk like a stone in his chest.

* * *

The temple steps were crowded with frightened people looking toward the border, which lay eleven miles south and invisible beyond the hills. Mothers held children. Old men stood very straight, the way old men do when they're terrified but remember a time when they weren't allowed to show it. Nobody paid attention to the thin scribe slipping past them with a satchel — which was, as always, exactly how Theo preferred it.

He had spent his whole life perfecting the art of being overlooked. It wasn't shyness, exactly, though people called it that. It was more like a craft, practiced daily: the way he stood just outside a conversation's arc, the way his footsteps landed soft without his deciding they should, the way his face arranged itself into something forgettable whenever anyone glanced his direction. Debt-bound men learned early that invisibility was survival. The temple held the paper on his father's debts, and debts passed down like heirlooms in the Marchetti family, and a debt-bound scribe who attracted attention attracted trouble, and trouble collected interest.

So he moved through the crowd like smoke through a keyhole, and no one stopped him, and part of him — the tired, honest part — noted that this too might become useful tonight.

The smuggler road began behind the tannery, where a gap in the wall wide enough for a slim man led down into the old holloway. Theo took it without hesitation. His feet knew this ground better than his head did; he'd walked these ways since he was nine years old, first hauling contraband letters between lovers separated by feuding families (his first professional commission, two coppers per letter), later carrying goods for men whose names he'd been careful never to learn. The holloway smelled of earth and elderberry and the faint ghost of every fire ever lit beneath its overhang. Above him, the sky stayed that bruised, unnameable color.

He made good time. He always made good time; it was the one thing about himself he'd never managed to make small.

The first checkpoint sat where the holloway met the Coldmere road — a warden's post, stone-built, with a signal lantern and a chain across the track. On any ordinary night there would be two wardens there, one awake and one pretending to be, both glad of company and willing to trade gossip for a pinch of pipeleaf.

Tonight the chain was down. The lantern was out. And the post was empty.

Theo stood in the shadows and listened. Wind in the gorse. A fox somewhere, complaining about the state of the world. No voices. No boots. No snoring warden, which was the detail that finally frightened him, because wardens at checkpoints slept the way other men breathed — constantly, reliably, and in defiance of their duties.

He crossed the road quickly, keeping low, and saw through the post's open door a table, two stools, a cold hearth, and a half-eaten bowl of stew. The spoon was still in the bowl.

Wardens didn't leave stew. Wardens would leave their mothers before their stew.

Theo did not go inside. He adjusted the satchel on his shoulder and kept walking, faster now, and told himself that perhaps there'd been a call-up, an alarm, a muster at the border tower. Men left posts for good reasons. Men left stews for—

He heard them before he saw them, and flattened himself into the hedge shadow beside the road without thinking, the way water finds a crack.

Soldiers.

They came up the Coldmere road in column, dozens of them, then scores, boots striking the packed earth in a rhythm too even, too synchronized, like a single enormous heart beating in many bodies. Theo watched from four feet away as they passed, close enough to see the stitching on their sleeves. They wore armor — plain, gray, unmarked — and they marched with their faces forward and their eyes fixed on nothing, and there was something wrong with the way they walked that Theo couldn't name and couldn't stop cataloguing. Their stride never varied. Not once. Not when the road dipped, not when a stone rolled under a boot, not when the column passed the abandoned checkpoint and not one helmet turned toward it. Soldiers notice things. These soldiers noticed nothing, including, apparently, the road itself.

And above them floated a banner.

It wasn't the king's. Theo knew the king's banner the way everyone did — the silver stag on green, stitched into every tapestry and stamped on every tax notice he'd ever copied. This banner was the color of wet ash. Its device, if it had one, was a shape that Theo's eyes slid off of, the way a tongue finds the hole where a tooth used to be. He tried to look directly at it and got a flicker of something — a suggestion of depth, of a fold, of a dark opening in fabric that had no business having openings — and then his gaze skated away, sweating, to the safety of the roadside weeds.

It wasn't a rebel banner either. Theo had copied enough sedition trials to know the heraldry of every disgraced house within three hundred miles. This banner belonged to no one. That was somehow worse than treason. Treason, at least, came from somewhere — a grievance, an ambition, a rival claimant with a name and a face and a motive you could write down in a neat column. This came from nowhere. This was a flag for the country of nowhere.

The column passed. The even heartbeat of their marching faded southward, toward Coldmere, toward the broken line of wards. Theo stayed in the hedge until the last echo died, and then he did something he almost never did: he spoke aloud, to nobody, in the empty dark.

"That's not an army," he whispered. "That's a *weather pattern.*"

His voice sounded very small and very human, and he found, to his surprise, that he needed it to be.

* * *

Miller's Rest was closest, so Miller's Rest it was — a waystation in a bend of the river, run by a widow named Marta who fed travelers and asked no questions and watered her ale just enough to be respectable about it. Theo reached its gate an hour past full dark, and his heart sank before he reached the door, because the windows were shuttered and the yard was full.

Full of people. Dozens of them, huddled under carts and eaves, wrapped in blankets, staring south. Refugees, though from what direction Theo couldn't say — the border was south, and these folk had come from south, and yet the soldiers had been marching north. Something was squeezing the valley from both ends, and the people caught in the middle were doing the only sensible thing, which was standing very still and hoping the squeeze missed them.

Marta herself was in the yard, distributing bread with the brisk ferocity of a woman who copes by working. She recognized Theo instantly — everyone recognized Theo eventually, after the third or fourth meeting; recognition just refused to stick to him in the meantime.

"Scribe Marchetti." She shoved a heel of bread at him as if feeding him were also an accusation. "You've picked a night for deliveries."

"Temple business." He held up the satchel. "Is the waystation master here? I have letters for—"

"You can put those letters in your satchel and keep them there." Marta's voice dropped, and she pulled him aside behind a hay cart, glancing around with the wariness of a woman who'd survived three husbands and intended to survive whatever came next. "Roads are closed. Wardens are gone — ran, or worse. Folk coming up from the ford say the river's wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Won't reflect." She said it flatly, daring him to laugh. "Starlight won't sit on it. Candlelight neither. You look down at the water and it's just — black. Like a hole cut in the world." Her jaw tightened. "And there's no stars tonight anyway, have you noticed? Whole sky, and I count maybe six of them, and those look scared."

Theo looked up. She was right. The sky above the valley was nearly bare — a handful of stars clustered in the west like the last guests at a party who've realized something's off about the host. The rest were simply gone. He felt the absence like a physical pressure, a great dark ceiling lowering by degrees.

"Marta," he said carefully, "the letters. People need warning."

"People need *sense*, is what they need." But she was already pulling a lantern from under the cart, lighting it with hands that shook only slightly. "If you're set on being a fool with a satchel, take the river path north of the ford, not through it. And give me one of those letters."

He blinked. "You?"

"My sister keeps the tollhouse at Harrow Ford. If the reeve's too busy panicking, she'll want to know." Marta thrust the lantern at him and took a letter from his satchel with the efficiency of a woman reclaiming a debt. "There. Now we're both fools. Go."

He went. And it was somewhere along the river path, alone with the lantern and the letterless satchel and the starless sky pressing down, that the dread in Theo's chest finished hardening into something else.

It surprised him, the shape of it. He had expected fear to stay fear — a soft thing, a thing that made you hide, and hiding was his oldest skill. But what settled into him instead as he walked was harder-edged. Anger, maybe, or its quieter cousin, resolve. Someone had broken the wards that kept this valley safe. Someone had put soldiers with no banner on the roads and stolen the stars out of the sky and emptied the checkpoints and frightened Marta, who feared nothing except dying embarrassed. And the temple — his temple, his cage, his home — had handed the warnings to the one man who knew every hidden path in three valleys, and had done it without asking why he knew them.

Well. They'd asked now, in their way. And Theo Marchetti, overlooked and debt-bound and deliberately small for thirty years, had said yes, and taken the letters, and walked out into the dark on purpose.

"There you are," he murmured to himself, and wasn't entirely sure whether he meant the resolve or himself.

The ridge shrine rose ahead against the bare sky — a circle of old stones on the hilltop, older than the temple, older than the king, older probably than the idea of kings. Travelers left offerings there. Theo had always liked it; stones didn't care about your debts.

He was twenty paces from the shrine when he saw that someone else was already there.

A figure crouched among the standing stones, hooded, doing something quick and precise with tools that clinked. Beside her — he thought it was a her, from the build and the impatient economy of movement — lay a bundle of papers weighted with rocks against the wind. And as Theo's lantern-light touched the edge of the stone circle, the figure went absolutely still, in the manner of a person who has been hunted before and knows exactly what lamplight means.

"Don't," the figure said, without turning. "Whatever you're about to shout, don't. And whatever you're about to do with that lantern, think very carefully."

Theo stopped. Behind him, miles south, the last few stars in the western sky went out — all at once, together, like candles pinched by a single unseen hand.

The darkness that remained felt, distinctly and unmistakably, like it was looking back.

The hooded figure rose slowly and turned, and Theo saw that she was holding something in each hand: in the left, a wardbreaker's pick, delicate and wicked; in the right, a folded paper sealed with wax.

"Well," she said. "You're the one they're looking for."

Theo's mouth went dry. "I'm sorry — who's looking for—"

"No time." She tossed him the sealed paper, and he caught it on reflex, thirty years of catching dropped inkwells serving him faithfully. "Read that. Then tell me, scribe, because I've stared at it until my eyes bled — tell me what a royal decree signed in handwriting that predates the king's birth is doing sitting in a border vault that shouldn't exist."

Above them, the sky was utterly, perfectly, hungry dark.

And Theo, hands trembling, looked down at the seal — and felt the ground tilt beneath everything he thought he knew. Because he had seen that seal before. He had seen it every day of his adult life.

It was pressed, in identical wax, on his own debt contract.
