## 3. The Night Without Stars

Theo Marchetti had a theory about running away: it went better if you didn't call it that.

He called it *delivering correspondence*. He had letters in his satchel—real ones, warning missives he'd copied three times over before slipping the originals into his coat—and a route in his head, and a story ready for any checkpoint: *Temple courier, urgent dispatches to Harrowmere, don't delay me, I'm nobody.* It was the same story he'd been telling the world his whole life, only now it came with faster walking.

The smuggler roads helped. They were carved into the hills below Thornfell like the veins of some old animal—deer paths widened by generations of men who preferred not to be counted. Theo knew them the way other people knew their own kitchens. He'd learned them at fourteen, running tithes for a factor who never asked where the extra coin went, and he'd kept them, the way you keep anything precious and slightly shameful.

By late afternoon he was two valleys past the temple, moving along a shelf of stone above a river the maps didn't name. The wards had fallen at dusk yesterday, and the world since then had been wrong in small accumulating ways—the way a room is wrong when someone has moved all your furniture one hand's width to the left.

The birds, for one thing. Birds should have been everywhere after the bells. Instead the valley was quiet in the specific way a room is quiet when someone has just stopped breathing.

And the soldiers. He'd seen a column of them at midday, marching up the far road under a banner of grey silk with nothing on it at all. No stag, no sunburst, no sigil of any house Theo could recall from six years of copying heraldic rolls. Just grey. An army wearing no name was somehow worse than an army wearing an enemy's.

*Don't think about it,* he told himself, which had never once worked, but he said it anyway, out loud, because talking to himself was one of the few indulgences of being invisible. "You're a courier. You're boring. Boring is armor."

A marmot whistled from the rocks as if mocking him.

The ridge shrine appeared around a bend near dusk—a low cairn of stacked stones with an iron star nailed to the top, one of hundreds scattered across the borderlands. Farmers left bread here. Smugglers left coins. Theo, out of habit older than thought, left nothing but a pause.

It seemed polite. And tonight, standing beneath a shrine to stars, he looked up.

The first stars were coming out—Veshel's Lantern low in the west, the Shepherd's Three strung above the ridge. Ordinary. Comforting. Theo felt something in his chest unknot by a single degree.

Then every star in the sky went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out*, all at once, like a lamp snuffed between finger and thumb. One moment the heavens were doing what heavens had done since before there were ledgers to record them, and the next the sky was a black so complete it had texture—velvet, oil, the inside of a closed mouth.

Theo stood very still.

His mind, which remembered everything whether he wanted it to or not, helpfully supplied the fact that he had seen this exact sky exactly never. In twenty-three years of nights, the stars had flickered, been veiled, hidden behind storm—but they had always come back, always in the same places, faithful as interest accruing. There was no entry in any roll he'd ever copied for *the sky simply stopping.*

"No," he said conversationally, to the marmot, to the shrine, to whatever was listening. "No, thank you."

Because it wasn't just dark. That was the part he would struggle later to explain, when anyone asked, which no one would. The darkness had *attention*. It lay over the valley like a lid, and beneath the lid something had turned toward him—not eyes, he was almost sure, not eyes yet, but the way a room changes when someone enters it. The way the hair rises on your arm before you hear the door.

The darkness was looking back.

Theo did what his whole life had trained him to do. He made himself small. He stepped back against the cairn, tucked his shoulders in, became a shadow among shadows, a scribe among stones, a man whose talent had always been *not being perceived.*

The attention lingered a moment longer—curious, patient, like a creditor checking a ledger he already knows is good—

Then it moved on. Down-valley. Toward the roads. Toward, Theo realized with a lurch, *people*.

He ran.

He ran the way he hadn't run since he was a boy caught stealing bread heels from the tithe kitchen—badly, arms pumping, lungs burning, dignity abandoned somewhere back at the shrine. He scrambled down the goat track below the ridge, took a smuggling cut he knew through the blackthorn, and got thoroughly, comprehensively lost, which had also not happened since he was fourteen.

That was how, in full dark under a starless sky, he walked directly into a campfire.

*

"Sit down before you fall down," said the woman by the fire, without looking up. "You've been crashing through my brushline for ten minutes. You walk like a cart with one wheel."

Theo froze at the edge of the light, doing rapid arithmetic. One woman, forty-ish, grey-streaked black hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like copper wire. A bedroll. A kettle. A satchel that clinked in a way satchels shouldn't. She sat with her boots off, which either meant she trusted this hollow completely or she wanted him to underestimate her, and Theo's instincts—usually so reliable—offered nothing useful except *run*, and his legs were already spent.

"I'm a courier," he managed. "Temple business. Urgent—"

"You're Temple-something," she agreed. "I saw the ink stains from thirty paces. Sit. If I wanted you taken, there are patrols half a mile east who'd love to meet a Temple man right now, and I didn't whistle for them. Work that through."

That was unfortunately sound logic. Theo worked it through, decided that a woman alone with a kettle was statistically less likely to kill him than the attention-shaped dark behind him, and sat. His legs folded with gratitude.

She poured tea into a tin cup and handed it over. It was terrible tea—bark and something medicinal—but it was hot, and Theo drank it while studying her sideways. She had the forearms of a laborer and the hands of a jeweler, scarred knuckles and precise fingers, and around her wrists she wore thin braided cords that shimmered faintly wrong, like heat-haze trapped in thread.

*Ward-threads.* His stomach dropped through the hillside. He was sitting with a wardbreaker. Wardbreaking carried a tithe-fine, a public marking, and in bad years, hanging. Every temple clerk learned the statutes. Theo had copied them four times.

"You're Isolde Vane," he blurted, then immediately wished he hadn't.

Her eyebrows rose, genuinely interested. "Well. The invisible little mouse knows my name. Go on, then. How?"

"The guild rolls. Before the purge. I—I copy documents. I copied yours. Your expulsion hearing." He winced. "Sorry."

To his astonishment, she laughed—a short, rough bark of real amusement. "Copied my own disgrace. That's a new one. Most people flinch." She topped up his cup. "Isolde Vane, disgraced wardbreaker, at your service. And you're the scribe from Thornfell. Don't look surprised; patrols are asking after 'a Temple clerk, slight, forgettable face.' Which describes half the order, but they're paying well, so everyone's suddenly a witness."

"I'm not—" Theo began automatically, *I'm nobody, I'm not important, please stop perceiving me*—and then heard himself, and shut his mouth.

Isolde Vane watched him with sharp grey eyes over her cup. "There it is," she said softly. "That flinch. You do it like breathing. Someone taught you to be unseen, boy, and you got *good* at it." She set the cup down. "Good enough that I nearly missed you twice tonight. And I don't miss things. So here's my offer, since we're both fugitives now whether you've admitted it or not."

From inside her coat she drew a leather tube, wax-sealed, royal blue. She held it out across the fire.

"Read this."

Theo didn't move. "What is it?"

"A royal decree. Stolen from a courier I'd rather not discuss. Sealed with the king's own signet." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go around dangerous facts. "I can't read the cipher-script on the second page. Guild-trained wardbreakers get court-cipher, not clerk-cipher. But you—you said you copy documents. Everything. So read it, and tell me I'm mad, and I'll let you go your way and I'll go mine."

"And if I tell you you're not mad?"

"Then," she said, "neither of us sleeps again, probably ever, and we figure out what to do about it together."

The fire popped. Somewhere down-valley, too far to be near and too near to ignore, a horn sounded—one long note, patient as the dark.

Theo took the tube. His fingers, which could reproduce the curve of any letter ever set before them, shook slightly as he cracked the seal.

The first page was ordinary enough—an order of ward-maintenance, schedules, garrison rotations, the dry furniture of governance. He read it aloud in the level tone of his trade, and Isolde nodded along until he reached the signature block, and then she said, "Stop. Read the signature. Slowly."

"*Given under our hand and seal at Whitespire, in the ninth year of our reign—Edric, by grace, King of Thornfell and the Marches—*"

"Now the date," Isolde said. "Look at the date, scribe."

Theo looked. His memory laid it beside everything else it held, all the thousands of pages, and the comparison assembled itself in his head with the horrible smoothness of a completed sum.

"This says ninth year," he whispered. "The king has reigned thirty-one years. This decree is dated *twenty-two years before his coronation.*"

"Keep going."

"The ink's aged right. The seal impression matches the deep-cut die they only used in the early reign—I've copied a dozen examples. Whoever forged this forged it *perfectly*, down to the paper stock." He turned the page, and the cipher-script swam up at him, and his photographic mind simply—read it, the way it read everything, unasked. "Second page is a grant of authority. 'The bearer of this instrument may open, amend, or dissolve the border wards of Thornfell, wholly and permanently, without further consent of the crown.'" His voice thinned. "It's signed *by the king*, authorizing someone to take down his own kingdom's walls. Twenty-two years before he was crowned."

The fire crackled. Isolde Vane was watching him with an expression he'd never received before in his life—not indifference, not the polite blindness he cultivated, but full, undivided, *seeing* attention, and it was almost worse than the dark had been.

"So either," she said quietly, "there's a forger alive who can beat time itself, or the man on the throne signed away his kingdom before he ever wore the crown—which means the thing wearing Edric's face isn't Edric." She leaned forward. "Your turn, scribe. Tell me I'm mad."

Theo wanted to. He wanted it desperately. But his traitor memory was already flipping through its endless archive, and it stopped—of course it stopped—on a page he'd copied eleven months ago in the temple undercroft, a debt-bond with his own name at the bottom, and the sigil pressed into the wax beside it.

A sigil of interlocking circles, empty at the center.

The same sigil was stamped in the decree's seal, small and neat, beside the king's.

"The mark," he said. His mouth had gone dry as old vellum. "Under the royal seal. I've seen it before. On my—" He stopped. Some instinct, deep and old, closed his mouth around the word *contract* like a trapdoor.

Isolde's eyes narrowed. "On your what?"

Above them, through the bare branches, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath. The horn sounded again, closer. Somewhere in Theo's chest, the vertigo finally finished its work: the rules were gone. Kings before their own crowns. Skies that watched. Debts with ancient seals. The ledger of the world had been opened and someone had written in it, and every entry Theo had ever trusted was subject to revision.

"On my way here," he said, which was true, in the way most of his life's sentences were true.

Isolde Vane looked at him for a long moment, and he could see her deciding—could see, clearly, the exact weight of the choice, the same choice she'd made when she hadn't whistled for the patrols.

"Well," she said at last, kicking dirt over the fire and reaching for her boots. "Then neither of us is sleeping, and we'd best be gone before that horn gets curious. Pick up the kettle, scribe. You know these hills better than I do—that much is obvious from the state of the bracken."

"That's it?" Theo said, scrambling up. "You steal a royal decree, learn the king might be—might be *nothing*—and your plan is *walk east*?"

"My plan," Isolde said, shouldering her satchel with its guilty clink, "is to stay ahead of the grey banners until I find someone who'll believe us, which is a short list, and getting shorter." She paused at the edge of the trees and glanced back at him, and for one unguarded instant she looked tired all the way through. "Unless you'd rather turn yourself in and explain why you can read court-cipher and recognize a dead dynasty's sigil. They're offering a pardon to witnesses, I hear. Of a sort."

Theo thought of the darkness at the shrine, turning toward him like a page being marked. Thought of the sigil on his contract, empty at its center, waiting.

He picked up the kettle.

"East," he said. "There's a quarry road past the second ridge. Patrols won't use it—they think it's haunted."

"Is it?"

"No," said Theo Marchetti, and found, to his surprise, that he almost meant it as a joke. "But it's about to wish it were."

* * *

They walked east through the blind dark, two small figures on roads only thieves remembered, and overhead the sky stayed empty, and stayed attentive, like a ledger waiting for its next entry.
