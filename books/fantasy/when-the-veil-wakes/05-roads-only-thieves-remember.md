## 5. Roads Only Thieves Remember

The first thing you learn on the smuggler roads is that they don't want to be found.

The second thing you learn is that they don't much care whether *you* find them either. The road decides. It's less like walking a path and more like being tolerated by one.

I'd been using the deep network since I was fourteen, back when my debts were small enough to fit in a coat pocket and my talents were small enough to hide under a table. Scribes who copy tithe rolls for the temple are paid in coin, in bread, and in the particular silence of people deciding you aren't worth noticing. That last payment was the most valuable. It bought me access to places the temple didn't know it had: the Hollow Stair behind the tannery, the culvert under the old wool market, the door in the shrine wall that opened only if you knocked in the rhythm of a drinking song nobody sober remembered.

Isolde Vane did not tolerate any of this gracefully.

"You're telling me," she said, three days out from the ridge shrine where we'd made our uneasy alliance, "that the way past the checkpoint at Marrow Bridge is to climb down into a ravine, wade through a stream that smells like a dead god's laundry, and come up inside a *barrel cellar*."

"Two barrels of it is wine," I said. "Technically."

"And the rest?"

"Turnips. Fermented turnips. The vintner uses the cellar because the tax assessors won't go near the smell." I paused. "Neither will most reasonable people."

She looked at me the way she looked at everything lately — as though the world had handed her a document and she was still checking it for forgery. Her wardbreaker's kit rattled faintly against her hip as we walked, what remained of it after she'd spent half her tools buying us out of trouble that hadn't even found us yet. She kept touching it anyway. People do that with things they're losing.

"Reasonable people," she muttered, "are currently being conscripted by an army marching under no banner at all. I'll take the turnips."

* * *

The trouble with the deep roads — one of the many troubles, Isolde would say, and she'd be right, and I'd hate her a little for it — is that they only work if they stay secret. And secrets, since the night the stars went out, had been leaking like a cracked jug.

We came up out of the ravine at dusk, smelling like the underside of a river, and I knew before I saw them that something was wrong. The barrel cellar's trapdoor should have been invisible from the yard above — hidden under stacked crates, behind a fence, in the shadow of the smokehouse. Instead there were torches. Four of them, maybe five, and voices with the flat, rehearsed calm of men reading from orders they didn't write.

"—the scribe. Medium height, brown hair, forgettable face—"

"Forgettable," Isolde whispered beside me, crouched among the wine racks. There was genuine offense in her voice. "They wrote *forgettable*. Someone described you in detail."

"That's the problem," I whispered back. My heart was doing something unpleasant against my ribs. "Nobody describes me in detail. Nobody describes me *at all*. I've spent twenty years making sure of it."

"Well, someone's broken your spell, Marchetti. They've got posters." She said the word like it was a disease. "You're famous. Do you understand how dangerous that is? Out here, famous means *findable*, and findable means—"

"I know what it means." I pressed my eye to the gap between two barrels. Below us, in the cellar proper, three soldiers were turning over casks with the bored efficiency of men who had searched a hundred cellars this week. Their armor was wrong. No crest, no colors, just dull grey plates that seemed to drink the torchlight rather than catch it. One of them had a paper pinned to his chest — my paper, my description, copied in a hand I recognized because I'd written a thousand documents in that same official clerk-hand myself.

Someone in the temple administration was hunting me with my own typography. There was a joke in there somewhere. I couldn't find it.

"The roof," I said. "Old cellars have vents for the fermentation gases. This one vents through the smokehouse flue. If we can get up the rack and through—"

"If we can get up the rack without bringing four hundred bottles down on our heads."

"Three hundred and eighty," I said. "I counted once. Bored afternoon."

She stared at me. Even in the dark, even covered in ravine water, her stare had weight. "You counted the wine bottles in a smuggler's cellar during a temple inventory."

"It was that or listen to Deacon Harrow explain grain tariffs."

"*Climb*, Marchetti."

We climbed. Isolde went first because she weighed less and argued more, which I've since learned is her natural order of operations. The racks groaned. Somewhere below, a soldier kicked a cask and swore about turnips. I got my fingers around the vent grate and pulled, and it came away easier than it should have, easier than it ever had before, and for half a heartbeat I had the strange sliding feeling that the grate had been oiled for me. That the whole cellar was holding its breath to let me pass.

Then the feeling curdled into something else — a pressure behind my eyes, like a finger pressed against the inside of my skull. Like something leaning close to read over my shoulder.

I'd felt it twice already since the ridge shrine. Once crossing a field where the wards had failed; once in a dream I declined to describe to anyone. Each time, it arrived when I used the hidden ways. When I slipped through a crack only I knew.

I told myself it was exhaustion. I was getting very good at telling myself things.

"Move," Isolde hissed, and I moved.

We came out through the smokehouse chimney like a pair of extremely undignified spirits, dropped into the yard behind the woodpile, and walked — walked, not ran, because running attracts eyes — out past the fence and into the treeline while the soldiers below continued their methodical, doomed search of a cellar we'd already left. Behind us, faintly, I heard one of them say, "He was *here*. The trail says he was here."

The trail said he was here because I had led him here. Because every hidden way I used left a mark somewhere, in some ledger I couldn't see, and whatever hunted me could read it.

I didn't tell Isolde that part. Not yet. I told her the vent had been loose.

She gave me a look that suggested she'd been keeping her own list of things I wasn't telling her, and that mine was now officially longer than hers.

* * *

For six more days we went down, and down, and down — not into the earth exactly, but into the layers beneath the world's attention. The smuggler network wasn't really roads at all. It was a consensus among thieves, going back generations, about which gaps in the world could be trusted. A sheep track here. A dry wash there. A ford that appeared only in autumn. A chapel with a false floor and a true tunnel, maintained by priests who took vows of poverty very seriously and vows of legality not at all.

I knew them all. That was the thing nobody understood about me — not the temple, not Isolde, not even me until recently. A scribe who copies everything remembers everything, and a boy who learned early that invisibility was safer than speech had spent his whole life memorizing the shape of the shadows instead of the light.

On the fourth day, Isolde caught me reciting, under my breath, the sequence of turns through the Kettlewash tunnels — forty-one turns, two of them false, one of them lethal if you took it in the dark.

"You do that a lot?" she asked.

"Do what?"

"Recite. Like a prayer. Or a shopping list."

I shrugged, which is a full sentence if you're built like me. "It keeps the routes fresh. Routes rot. A tunnel floods, a door gets bricked, a contact dies. You have to keep checking the map in your head against the world, or you end up trusting a memory that killed you three years ago."

"Hm." She walked a few steps. Then: "My guild master used to say something like that. About wards. *A ward you stop checking is a door you've opened.*" Her jaw tightened around the word *guild*, the way it always did. Around the word that had cost her everything.

"What happened?" I asked. "With the wards. You never—" 

"Don't."

"All right."

"I *didn't build them,*" she said anyway, three minutes later, because Isolde Vane was constitutionally incapable of leaving a debt unpaid, including conversational ones. "The Thornfell border lattice. I was offered the commission eleven years ago. Chief wardbreaker, full guild honors. I looked at the specifications and I said no." She kicked a stone off the path with unnecessary violence. "The specs weren't defensive. They were *permissive*. Built to fail on command. Somebody wanted doors in the border that could be opened from one side, and I refused to be the hand on that latch, and the guild stripped my marks and put me out, and everyone said I was mad and proud and finished."

We walked in silence for a while. Somewhere above us, muffled by forty feet of limestone, an army marched on roads that were supposed to be empty.

"They weren't mad," Isolde said quietly. "The specifications. Whoever wrote them knew exactly what they were doing." She glanced sideways at me. "Your contract, Marchetti. The sigil on it. You said it matched the seal on the decree."

"It matched."

"Have you thought about why a debt-bond on a temple scribe carries the same seal as a royal decree signed in handwriting older than the king?"

"Yes," I said. "And then I think about something else, very loudly, until it goes away."

To my surprise, she laughed. It was a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose, but it was real. "That," she said, "is the most honest thing anyone's said to me in eleven years."

* * *

The second save came at the Weeping Gate, and I'm still not entirely sure how we survived it, except that I suspect we didn't survive it so much as we were permitted to pass, and I liked that possibility even less.

The Weeping Gate is a fissure in a cliff face where groundwater bleeds through the stone year-round, freezing in winter into curtains of ice that look, in the right light, like a grieving face. Smugglers called it that long before anyone mapped it. On the far side of the fissure runs a ledge path, wide enough for one, that drops two hundred feet into the Greywater gorge. It bypasses the toll-bridge at Sorrel's Crossing entirely — which matters, because the toll-lords of Sorrel's Crossing have long memories and short mercies, and one of them has particular reasons to want Theo Marchetti delivered to them in pieces.

But the ledge was occupied.

We heard them before we saw them — boots on wet stone, the clink of grey plate, that same flat command-voice. A patrol, coming toward us along a ledge with nowhere to step aside. In fog. At night.

"There's a cutback," I breathed. "Forty paces ahead. Old quarry niche. It's hidden behind a curtain of ivy — or it was, eight years ago."

"Eight years ago."

"The ivy doesn't retire, Isolde. It's ivy."

The ivy, as it turned out, had not retired. It had *flourished*, and when I pulled its curtain aside it did so willingly, almost eagerly, revealing a niche deep enough for two thin, frightened people to press themselves into while five soldiers walked past close enough to touch.

Close enough to touch. That was the thing. The lead soldier turned his head as he passed — I saw the movement, inches away, through the ivy — and his eyes passed across the niche, across us, and slid off. Like water off wax. Like we weren't there.

And in that moment I felt it again, the pressure behind my eyes, but stronger now, intimate now, and underneath it — I swear this, I would swear it on any ledger you like — underneath it I heard something almost like satisfaction. Almost like a voice too large to be a voice saying, in the tone of a merchant confirming delivery:

*There he is. Going the right way.*

The patrol passed. The boots faded. Isolde and I stood pressed together in the dark, breathing each other's air, and neither of us spoke for a long time.

When we finally came out onto the open ledge, she grabbed my arm. Not gently.

"They looked *at* us," she said. "The lead man. He turned his head. He looked straight into that niche and walked on. Wards don't do that. Concealment charms don't do that. What did you *do*, Marchetti?"

"Nothing. I didn't do anything. I don't *know* how to do anything. I'm a scribe. I copy documents. My greatest talent is being ignored."

"Your greatest talent," she said slowly, "is apparently being ignored *by things that should not be able to ignore us.*" Her grip tightened. "Every hidden way we've taken, you've known it like you were born to it. Every danger, you've slipped past like the dark was making room. And every time, you come out looking like a man who's just heard bad news and filed it under later." She shook me, once, lightly. "What's under 'later,' Theo?"

The use of my given name landed harder than the shaking. I looked out over the gorge, at the starless sky doubled in the black water below, and I tried to answer honestly, which is a skill I was still learning.

"Lately," I said, "it feels less like I'm hiding from something. And more like I'm... following instructions I never received. Like the roads know me. Like something paved them ahead of my feet and is pleased about it." I swallowed. "Like I'm not sneaking through the dark, Isolde. Like the dark is *escorting* me."

She let go of my arm. For a moment she just studied my face in the lightless air, and I braced for mockery, or worse, for belief.

Instead she said, "Then we'd better figure out whose escort it is before it delivers you wherever it wants you." She shouldered her pack and started up the ledge. "Because I've read that decree, Marchetti. Things that escort people to their destinies usually expect payment. And in my experience, the interest rates are *murder*."

* * *

Two days after the Weeping Gate, the tunnels ended and the world resumed — abruptly, the way the smuggler network always hands you back to reality, like a midwife slapping a newborn. We climbed a final stair cut into a hillside and emerged into grey morning light and the smell of coal smoke and cabbage, and there below us lay Cindermarrow: a mining town stitched into the flank of a dead mountain, all slate roofs and slag heaps and chimneys breathing thin grey threads into a sky that still, after nine days, held no stars.

I'd passed through Cindermarrow twice on temple business. It had been loud then — hammer-song from the pits, dogs, children shrieking in the lanes. Now it was quiet in a way towns shouldn't be quiet. Doors shut. Shutters drawn. And everywhere, chalked on walls and lintels and well-covers, the same crude symbol: a circle with a slash through it.

"What is that?" Isolde murmured.

"I don't know. But somebody's scared enough of something to mark every doorway in town."

An old woman was drawing the mark on her own door as we passed, her chalk stub worn to a nub. Isolde asked her, gently, what it meant. The woman looked up, saw strangers, and her eyes went first to the shadows behind us, then to Isolde, then — and this I noticed, this I filed under *later* with everything else — directly to me, and lingered.

"Keeps the hollow ones guessing," she said. "Doesn't always work." Then her gaze fixed on me properly, and her chalk hand began to tremble. "Oh," she said softly. "*Oh.* You're one of the marked ones, aren't you. I can see it on you, dear. Like frost on a window." She stepped backward through her own doorway. "There was another one here. Little girl. Eyes like the night sky used to be. Soldiers came for her yesterday. Soldiers with no faces worth remembering." The door began to close. "They're taking the marked children to the pit. You'd best not be seen—"

The door shut. The latch fell.

Isolde and I stood in the empty lane, listening to the thin grey threads of smoke rise from a town that had learned not to make noise.

"To the pit," she repeated. "Theo." She was already unclasping her kit, checking what was left of her tools, her jaw set in the shape it got when she'd stopped calculating odds and started spending herself. "How many ways do you know into a mine?"

I closed my eyes. And the map unfolded behind them — every shaft, every air-hole, every forgotten adit and collapsed gallery, the whole buried skeleton of Cindermarrow, drawn there by some hand I was increasingly afraid to name.

"More than I'd like," I said. "Less than we'll need."

Behind us, at the far end of the lane, a chalked circle-with-a-slash seemed to watch. Above us, the starless dark pressed down like a lid. And somewhere below, down where the miners had dug too deep and found something waiting, a little girl with stars in her eyes was about to learn whether the world contained anyone willing to break every rule they had for her sake.

I was already moving before I decided to.
