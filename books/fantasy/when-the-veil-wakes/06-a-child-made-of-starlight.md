## 6. A Child Made of Starlight

Isolde Vane had broken wards in nine kingdoms and built them in four, and she could say with professional authority that the mining town of Copperslack had never possessed a single one worth the chalk it was drawn in. The town's protection had always been simpler: nothing here worth stealing except copper dust and coughing men, and even the coughing men were mostly past stealing.

Now it had something worth taking. She could feel it before they reached the well square—a thin, bright wrongness in the air, like a hairline crack in dark glass. Since the stars went out, Isolde's senses had been ringing constantly, every wardline in the world either dead or screaming, but this was different. This was small. This was *local*.

This was, she would soon learn, about four feet tall.

"Theo," she murmured. "We're being watched."

Theo Marchetti did what Theo always did when trouble appeared: he became furniture. It was genuinely remarkable to watch. One moment there was a tall, tired scribe walking beside her; the next there was simply a man-shaped absence that your eyes slid off like rain off slate. Isolde had spent three weeks on the road with him and still couldn't decide whether it was a talent or an affliction.

"Where?" he asked quietly.

"Not where. *What.*" She tilted her chin toward the well square ahead, where evening market stalls were folding up for the night. "There. By the baker's awning. Do you see it?"

He looked. He squinted. He performed the full theatrical repertoire of a man searching a crowd. And then, because Theo was Theo, he said, "No," while his hand drifted—casual as anything—to the knife at his belt, which meant yes.

The thing by the baker's awning was a child. A girl, perhaps eight years old, sitting cross-legged on a turned-over crate as though she'd been assigned the post by some municipal committee. Her clothes were miner's castoffs two sizes too large, her boots had been patched so many times they were more thread than leather, and her hair was the color of old rope. Ordinary. Unremarkable. Except—

Except her eyes glowed.

Not blazingly. Not the hellish furnace-glow of Veil-touched things Isolde had seen in her worst months. Just a faint silver shimmer, like moonlight caught in deep water, like someone had taken two stars—the last two in the whole black sky—and set them gently behind a child's face.

She was watching them. Had been watching them since the bridge, probably. And when Isolde met her gaze, the girl smiled, wide and gap-toothed, and raised one grubby hand in cheerful greeting.

"Well," Isolde said faintly. "That's inconvenient."

"You see glowing children everywhere, then?"

"I see *wells*, Theo. I see ley-lines and ward-seams and the occasional cursed hearthstone. I do not—" she lowered her voice further, "—I do not see children who glow like the sky used to."

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. The stars had gone out eleven days ago, all at once, like candles snuffed by a passing hand, and the darkness that remained had weight and appetite. Isolde had felt it pressing on the world ever since. But this girl's eyes weren't Veil-dark. They were the opposite. They were the memory of light.

Which meant the soldiers who'd been sweeping these valleys would want her very much indeed.

"We keep walking," Theo said. It wasn't cowardice in his voice—it was arithmetic. Isolde had learned to hear the difference. "We don't know her, she doesn't know us, and stopping to admire strange luminous orphans is how fugitives get hanged."

"Mm." Isolde kept walking. And then stopped walking. Because the girl had hopped down from her crate and was following them.

Not stealthily. Not at all stealthily. She trailed them through the market lane at a distance of perhaps ten feet, humming a tuneless little song, and when Isolde glanced back, the girl waved again.

"Theo."

"I know."

"She's following us."

"I'm aware."

"She's *humming*."

"I am *aware*, Isolde."

The girl caught up at the mouth of the alley behind the smithy, where Theo had been angling them out of town with the smooth unconscious competence of a man who'd spent his life learning which streets swallowed people. She planted herself in front of him and looked up, glowing eyes enormous.

"You're the ones the dark doesn't like," she announced.

Isolde blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"It keeps trying to look at you and you keep not being where it looks." The girl said it the way other children reported the weather. "It's very annoyed. I think it's funny." She stuck out a hand. "I'm Pip."

Theo stared at the offered hand the way a man stares at a trap he can't locate. Isolde watched him run through it—the cost-benefit ledger of a lifetime's caution, every entry written in the ink of *stay small, stay unseen, stay owed to no one*. She'd seen him do this calculation over shared firewood and over stolen horses. He always arrived at the same answer: touch nothing, owe nothing, be nothing.

Pip's hand hung in the air between them, patient as a stone.

And then Theo Marchetti, debt-bound scribe, invisible man, took the child's hand and shook it, solemn as a treaty signing.

"Theo," he said. "This is Isolde. She's crosser than she looks."

"I'm exactly as cross as I look," Isolde muttered, "and we are leaving, because if those eyes catch torchlight from across the square, we'll have the garrison down on us before you finish introductions."

Pip's grin faded into something older and wearier than any eight-year-old's face should hold. "I know," she said. "That's why I found you first."

* * *

Her story came out in pieces over the next hour, in the cellar of a tannery whose owner owed Pip's mother money and had decided, in the absence of the mother, to pay the debt in cheese rinds and silence. Pip sat on a barrel among the hanging hides and talked with her feet swinging.

She'd lived in Copperslack all her life. Mother dead of lung-rot two winters back, father unknown, raised loose and communal by the mining families the way orphan children are raised in towns too poor to be cruel. When the stars went out, she'd been on the ridge above town with three other kids, daring each other to shout at the dark.

"And then they went out," Pip said, "and I screamed, and everyone screamed, but after—" she frowned, hunting for words, "—after, I could still see where the stars had been. Little lines. Like the sky is a coat and somebody unpicked the stitching, and I can see the holes where the thread was."

Isolde went very still. "Show me."

Pip held up one hand, fingers spread, and pointed it at the cellar wall. And Isolde saw—faintly, at the edge of sight, the way you see your own breath in cold air—a web of silver filaments spring up against the stone. Not wardwork. Not quite. Older. The seams of things. The places where the world was stitched together, and the places where the stitching had been picked loose.

"There," Pip said softly, tapping the air near the ceiling. "That one goes all the way to the mountain. And there's one under the temple floor in the city, big and heavy, and it's *tied* to something, and the something breathes." She shuddered, a quick whole-body flinch. "I don't like looking at that one."

Theo had gone pale in the way of a man recognizing handwriting. Isolde noted it, filed it, and let it lie—for now. The scribe had secrets stacked on secrets, and prying them out one at a time was the only way to keep him from bolting.

"The patrols," Isolde said instead. "How long have they been hunting glowing children?"

"A week. They come at night, in wagons with no markings. They take kids who shine. Old Marta hid me in her root cellar twice already." Pip shrugged, elaborately casual, fooling nobody. "But Marta says they're getting closer to finding me, so when I saw you two coming over the bridge and the dark couldn't find *you*, I thought—I thought maybe you were going somewhere, and maybe going somewhere is better than hiding in cellars forever."

Silence, except for the drip of tanning liquor somewhere in the dark.

Isolde looked at Theo. Theo looked at the floor. And Isolde recognized the expression on his face, because she'd worn it herself once, years ago, standing in front of the Guild Council with her tools confiscated and her name struck from the rolls. It was the expression of a person doing math that always ended badly no matter how the numbers were arranged.

Take the child: slower travel, brighter trail, a target strapped to their backs through country crawling with unmarked soldiers.

Leave the child: a glowing orphan handed over to whoever wore no banner and answered to no crown, in a world where the stars themselves had been eaten.

"Theo," she said quietly. "Walk with me."

They climbed the cellar steps into the yard behind the tannery, where the starless sky pressed down like a lid. Theo stood with his arms crossed, jaw tight, staring at nothing.

"Say it," Isolde said.

"If we take her and they catch us, she dies with us. If we leave her, she dies alone, but we live to reach Aurelia, and Aurelia might save thousands. That's the arithmetic. You know it is."

"I know it is," Isolde agreed. "Now tell me why your voice sounds like that when you say it."

He didn't answer for a long moment. Above them the empty heavens glittered with nothing at all.

"Because I've done the sums my whole life," he finally said. "Stay small. Owe nothing. Be worth nothing to anyone, and no one comes looking for you. It kept me alive. It kept me *safe*. And I'm standing here telling you we should leave a child in a root cellar because the numbers work, and every part of me that made those numbers is the same part of me that let—" He stopped. Swallowed. "There's a version of this where I walk away, Isolde. I've walked away from smaller things and slept fine. That's the problem. I'd sleep fine."

Isolde studied him. Somewhere in these three weeks on smuggler roads, sharing cold camps and worse food, she had stopped thinking of Theo Marchetti as a useful stranger with a photographic memory and started thinking of him as—well. She hadn't found the word yet. But it was a word that made his current misery matter to her, which was its own kind of dangerous arithmetic.

"For what it's worth," she said, "when I refused to build the border wards, the Guild called it treason and I called it the only sum that added up. Both of us have been doing forbidden mathematics our whole lives. Might as well do it together." She jerked her chin toward the cellar door. "Go get the girl."

Something in his shoulders unlocked. "You're certain?"

"No. Absolutely not. Go get the girl before I regain my senses."

* * *

They left Copperslack before dawn, by the drainage road along the old sluice channel—a route Theo knew from his smuggling days, because of course he did; the man carried an entire criminal geography in his head alongside his tithe rolls. Pip walked between them, wearing Isolde's spare hood pulled low, her hands jammed in her pockets like a small convicted criminal.

"The trick," Theo was explaining to her in a low, patient voice, "is that nobody looks at boring people. If you slouch and complain about your feet and argue about bread, you become scenery."

"I'm very good at arguing about bread," Pip said seriously. "Marta says I could argue the crust off a loaf."

"That," said Theo gravely, "may be the single most valuable skill you possess."

Isolde brought up the rear and let herself, just for a moment, enjoy the absurd picture they made: a disgraced wardbreaker, a fugitive scribe, and a luminous child playing at being dull, marching down a drainage ditch under a sky with no stars, fleeing an army that didn't officially exist.

It lasted until midmorning, when they crested the ridge above the mill ford and Isolde's wardsense lit up like a struck bell.

"Wagons," she breathed. "Two. Coming up the valley road. No markings."

"They're early," Pip whispered. Her glow was visible even through the hood, flickering like a candle in wind—not fear exactly, though fear too, but the light responding to proximity. To whatever rode in those wagons. "They're *looking* now. Really looking. It's like the dark leaned down and put its eye to the ground."

Theo pulled them off the road into a stand of birch, and they crouched in the bracken and watched the wagons roll into view below. Soldiers in them, a dozen at least, in armor with no sigil and faces with no particular expression at all—which was worse than cruelty, somehow. Cruelty had reasons. And standing in the bed of the second wagon, holding a lantern that gave off no light Isolde could name, was a figure in grey robes whose outline didn't quite agree with itself. Her eyes kept sliding off it. Every time she looked directly, it was somewhere slightly else.

In its free hand it held a net of black mesh, and the mesh was *moving*, gently, like something breathing.

"It catches the shiny ones," Pip whispered. Her small hand had found Isolde's sleeve without her noticing. "That's what the others said. The taken kids. Nobody comes back, but the ones who got away saw the net."

The wagons slowed at the mill. A soldier spoke to the miller, gesturing. Questions. The miller shook his head, hands spread wide, the universal gesture of *no glowing children here, sir, only flour*. And then—one of those small, terrible accidents that decides lives—the wind shifted, and the grey-robed thing lifted its head, and its attention swung around and fixed on the birch stand on the ridge like a compass needle finding north.

Pip's glow flared silver through the hood, bright as a signal fire.

"*Run,*" Isolde said.

They ran. Down the far side of the ridge, through bracken and scree, Theo hauling Pip onto his back when her short legs failed, Isolde crashing through the trees behind them and hearing, horribly, the wagons turning off the road below—the creak of wheels on forest loam, unhurried, certain. Things that hunted didn't need to hurry.

"There's a ravine!" Theo gasped. "Half a mile north—old mine adit—the shafts go under the river, they can't follow with the wagon—"

"You're sure?"

"I grew up in these hills, Isolde, I'm *sure*—"

The grey thing stepped out of the trees ahead of them.

Not running. Not even walking quickly. Just suddenly *there*, between two birches, robes agreeing with itself for one awful moment, and beneath the hood there was no face, only a depth, a soft dark like deep water, and Isolde felt her knees try to buckle under the sheer weight of its attention. Beside her, Theo made a sound she'd never heard from him—not fear. Recognition. Grief, almost.

The black net rose in the thing's hand, unfolding, hungry.

And Pip slid off Theo's back and stepped forward.

"No," she said, in a voice that was not a child's voice at all, that was silver and vast and briefly filled the whole forest. Her glow blazed, and the seams of the world lit up around her in a web of light—and for one heartbeat the grey thing *flinched*, recoiled, robes writhing—

And Theo moved.

Later, Isolde would try to reconstruct it and fail. The man she'd traveled with for three weeks, the man whose entire soul was built on staying small and touching nothing—he stepped *between* the child and the dark, unarmed, hands open, and said, in a voice shaking but clear:

"You don't get to have her."

The forest held its breath. The net hovered. The void-face regarded Theo Marchetti, and something in its regard changed—curiosity, hunger, *interest*, the way a collector looks at a piece that has wandered unexpectedly into reach.

Then the horn sounded. From the ridge above—two notes, sharp, human. Riders on the skyline, half a dozen, in the mud-and-iron livery of the border clans, bows drawn. The grey thing's attention snapped upward, and in that flicker of divided focus, Theo grabbed Pip and Isolde grabbed Theo and they threw themselves down the slope into the ravine as arrows hissed overhead.

They ran the old mine adit until the light behind them died, until the only sound was water dripping in the dark and Pip's ragged little breaths against Theo's shoulder. Finally, in a junction chamber where three dead shafts met, they collapsed against the timbers.

For a long time, nobody spoke.

Then Pip, muffled, from inside Theo's arms where she'd apparently decided she lived now: "You stood in front of me."

"Yes," Theo said.

"Nobody stands in front of me. People hide me. People run with me. Nobody ever—" She pulled back and looked at him, and her glow had gone soft and steady, like banked coals. "Why?"

Theo opened his mouth. Closed it. Isolde watched him search for the careful answer, the deflecting answer, the answer that owed nothing—and watched him, deliberately, set it aside.

"Because you're ours now," he said simply. "I've decided. I hope that's acceptable."

Pip burst into tears and laughed at the same time, which is a thing only children and the newly bereaved manage, and clung to him again, and Isolde Vane—who had not cried since the Guild stripped her name—found she had to look away and study the mine timbers for a considerable length of time.

When she had command of her face again, she said, "Those riders. Clan colors. Someone tipped them to the wagons' route."

"Mm." Theo was quiet a moment. Then: "Isolde. There's something you need to know. About why that thing looked at me the way it did." His hand went, unconsciously, to the inner pocket where he kept the contract he never talked about. "About the seal on my debt. It matches the king's decree. And I think—" his voice dropped to barely a whisper, "—I think it knows my name."

Above them, muffled through fifty feet of rock, something began to sing. Low. Patient. In a language older than names.

Pip's glow flared, and she whispered, "It's saying yours."
