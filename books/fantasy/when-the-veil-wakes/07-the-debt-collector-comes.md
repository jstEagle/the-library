## 7. The Debt Collector Comes

Theo had always believed that the surest sign of trouble was quiet arriving where noise should be.

The smuggler road out of the mining town ran along a dried riverbed for three miles before climbing into the pines, and for those three miles there should have been birdsong, wind, Pip's endless questions about whether stars had bones. Instead there was nothing. Not even the wind. The trees stood still as painted scenery, and the silence pressed against Theo's ears like water.

He stopped walking.

Isolde stopped two paces ahead without turning around, which was a trick of hers he'd stopped questioning sometime around the second day. She'd been a wardbreaker for thirty years; she said she could feel a broken circle the way a cook felt a burnt pot. "Well," she said. "There it is."

"There what is?" Pip whispered, pressing close to Theo's side. Her eyes were dimmer than usual—she'd been spending her glow sparingly since the soldiers came to the mining town, hoarding it like a miser's coins—but they still caught the light strangely, the way a cat's do.

"The thing I've been dreading since we left," Isolde said cheerfully. She said most terrible things cheerfully. It was one of the many things about her that Theo found both infuriating and, increasingly, load-bearing. "They've been behind us for half a day. Temple enforcers travel quiet because they're paid to, and because shouting is beneath their dignity." She sniffed. "I once knew an enforcer who wouldn't sneeze in front of a witness. Held it in until his eyes watered."

"That's not dignified," Pip said gravely. "That's sad."

"Pip, my darling, that is the temple entire in one anecdote." Isolde turned at last, and Theo saw that her hands were already moving through the small preparatory gestures she made when working—thumb to forefinger, forefinger to thumb, a rhythm like counting rosary beads. "Theo. How much do you remember of your contract?"

"All of it," he said, because he did. Every word, every clause, every cramped notation in the margin. He remembered everything he'd ever read or seen, which was precisely why he'd built his life around being overlooked, and precisely why being hunted now felt like the universe breaking its own rules. "Clause nine is the enforcement provision. If the bondholder flees service, the debt may be collected *in kind*—meaning me. My person, handed over to whoever holds the claim."

"And who holds your claim?"

"The Thornfell temple treasury. Which answers to the diocese. Which answers to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Which answers to the crown."

"To the crown," Isolde agreed pleasantly, "which currently has a vacancy sign hanging off it, if you follow my meaning. They don't want you for debt, love. Debt's just the hook they're legally obliged to use. They want the scribe who saw. You're going to be questioned by whatever wears the king's face, and I promise you the questioning will not involve tea."

The silence deepened. Somewhere up the slope, a pine branch creaked though no wind touched it.

"They'll have a collector with them," Theo said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which surprised him. Perhaps fear, like everything else, eventually became routine if you survived enough of it. "A bonded officer. They can compel compliance within a certain radius—the contract language allows for *persuasive proximity*."

"Persuasive proximity," Isolde repeated, delighted despite everything. "Gods, lawyers. Give me a collapsing ward any day—at least those are honest about wanting to kill you." She rolled her shoulders, cracked her neck left then right, and looked at the two of them with an expression Theo would later think of as the moment she decided. "Right. Here's how this goes. When they come—and they will come, they're already circling—you let me talk. You say nothing. Nothing, Theo. Especially not your name."

"Why not my name?"

"Because names are anchors, and right now you are the loosest end in three provinces." She said it lightly, but something flickered across her face—an expression Theo couldn't read, gone too fast. "Now walk. Quickly, quietly, and if anyone asks, we're pilgrims going to mourn at the ridge shrine. Grieving people are invisible. Take it from an expert."

* * *

They didn't make the shrine.

The enforcers came down out of the trees a mile short of it, six of them in grey robes with the temple's ledger-sigil stitched at the hem, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once been told no. At their center walked a woman Theo recognized, because recognizing things was what he did: Magistrate Corvane, who had administered his debt-bond eleven years ago, who had watched him sign with a dry quill and drier eyes, who had never once learned his name because she didn't need to. To Corvane he had always been *the asset*. Asset number four-seventeen.

"Asset Marchetti," she said now, and the old name settled over him like wet wool.

"Magistrate," Theo managed. Beside him, Isolde went very still, which from her was louder than screaming.

"You have fled your bondhold," Corvane continued, consulting a slate as though reading from a menu. "Under clause nine, your debt is hereby converted to collection in kind. You will accompany us to the capital, where you will render full account of what you witnessed at Thornfell. Your cooperation is not required. Only your presence." She looked up, and her gaze slid past Pip entirely—of course it did, nobody ever saw Pip when there was anything else to look at—and fixed on Isolde. Her thin mouth compressed. "*Vane.*"

"Magistrate." Isolde spread her hands, all innocence. "What a coincidence, meeting you on this road. We were just off to grieve at the shrine. Terrible business, the wards."

"Do not." Corvane's composure cracked, just at the edges, and Theo saw—for the first time—that underneath the slate-cold surface she was afraid. Actually afraid, in the specific way of someone who had seen something in the capital and could not unsee it. "Do not play games with me, Vane. The wards fell. I was *at Thornfell* when they fell. I have filed reports. I know what answered them." Her voice dropped. "Give me the scribe. Whatever you're doing, whatever rebellion you imagine you're building—it ends faster if you give me the scribe."

"See, here's my problem with that," Isolde said, and her voice had changed. All the levity had drained out of it, leaving something old and hard. "You said 'whatever you imagine you're building.' But I'm not building anything. I'm just refusing to help tear it all down again. It's the only thing I've ever been any good at."

The lead enforcer stepped forward. "Magistrate, shall we take them all? The child registers on the—"

"No," Theo said.

It came out of him before he knew he was going to say it, loud and clear in that terrible still air, his first act of open defiance in twenty-six years of careful smallness. Every head turned. Six grey-robed enforcers, one magistrate, all looking at *him*, seeing *him*, and Theo discovered that being visible felt like standing in a river current—terrifying, and clarifying.

"You don't touch the child," he said. "She's got nothing to do with my bond. Clause nine covers the asset. One asset. Me."

"Theo," Isolde murmured, "what are you—"

"Buying time," he breathed. "Talk, remember? You talk. I'll keep talking."

And he did. It was, absurdly, the thing he was best at: words, clauses, the architecture of obligation. He cited sub-clauses. He noted that the conversion order lacked the countersignature of a second magistrate. He observed that transporting a minor without guardianship documentation violated the same temple code that bound his own debt. Corvane's enforcers shifted uncomfortably; lawyers hated being lawyered, especially by someone they'd spent a decade failing to notice existed. For ninety seconds—Theo counted them, counting was another of his afflictions—he held six trained men motionless with nothing but procedural pedantry.

Then Corvane said, tiredly: "Take them."

The enforcers moved, and Isolde Vane finally did what she'd been doing with her hands this whole time.

* * *

Later, Theo would try to describe what a wardbreaker's work looked like when it was done in anger, and he would fail every time. The nearest he ever got was this: imagine someone unpicking the world's stitching, quickly, expertly, with a bone needle and no apologies.

Isolde's last tools came out of a leather roll worn soft as cloth—a set of needles, a spool of thread that wasn't thread, three chalk sticks of colors that hurt slightly to look at, and a small brass compass whose needle pointed not north but at *people*. She dropped to one knee in the pine needles and drew a circle around the six enforcers before they'd crossed ten feet toward her. Then she stood, dusted off her skirts, and said, conversationally:

"Gentlemen. You are currently standing inside a containment lattice keyed to your own oath-oaths. Step outside it and your tongues will forget every word of temple liturgy for a month. Try to break it and your tongues will forget considerably more." She smiled at them, warm as a hearth. "Please don't try to break it."

One of them tried to break it.

His mouth opened and produced a sound like a bell falling downstairs. He sat down hard in the pine needles and did not get up, and his five colleagues regarded him with the profound resignation of men watching a colleague learn something the hard way.

Corvane alone hadn't moved. She was staring at Isolde with an expression that had traveled a long way past anger into something almost like grief. "You were the best of us," she said quietly. "Thirty years ago. You were the best wardbuilder the temple ever trained, and you threw it away rather than work on the border project. Do you know what I thought, all these years? That you were a coward. That you were afraid your work would be judged."

"I was," Isolde said simply.

"Afraid?"

"Afraid it would hold." Isolde's hands had begun shaking now that the work was done—Theo noticed, the way he noticed everything, the fine tremor she was hiding by gripping her own wrist behind her back. "Corvane, listen to me, because I'll only say this once. Those wards weren't walls. They were a *lid*. And I read the specifications, and I understood what we were being asked to lid, and I said no. So they took my guild-mark, burned my name from the rolls, and put me out. And eleven years later the lid came off anyway, because you cannot imprison a hunger forever with geometry. You can only postpone it." She looked at the frozen enforcers, at the road, at the sky that had been empty of stars for days. "I've been running from that *no* for thirty years. Turns out it followed me. Turns out it was right."

Corvane was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman stepping carefully across ice. "The capital knows you're with him. There's a price on both your heads now. Go east, Vane. The coast roads. Get out while—"

"No," Isolde said. "I'm done getting out. I spent thirty years getting out." She reached into her coat and drew out the last item from the leather roll: a single key, black iron, unremarkable except that it hummed faintly, the way a struck tuning fork hums long after you can't hear it anymore. She pressed it into Corvane's hand and closed the older woman's fingers over it. "When the lattice releases at dawn, take your people home. And when someone asks you what happened on this road, tell them the truth. It's the only weapon left that works."

Then she turned her back on the temple, on thirty years, on everything, and walked away up the slope, and Theo and Pip fell in beside her because there was nowhere else in the world either of them would rather be.

* * *

They climbed until the light failed, and made camp in the hollow beneath a lightning-split oak, because Isolde said split oaks held warmth oddly well and Theo had learned not to check her claims—he just wrote them down in his memory and waited to be proven right, which he always was, eventually.

It was Pip who broke the silence, as it usually was.

"Those were your last tools," she said. Not a question. Children always heard the important part, adults' deflections notwithstanding. "All of them. Now you don't have any."

"I have my hands," Isolde said. "And my enormous brain. And a truly magnificent supply of opinions."

"But the tools were how you did the *magic*."

Isolde was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved across her face, and Theo watched her decide, once again, to tell the truth—which was, he was coming to understand, a decision she made fresh each time, each time costing something, each time paying more than it cost.

"The tools were how I did magic *safely*," she said. "From far away, through prepared circles, with everything measured twice. A wardbreaker with tools can fight a battle and go home for dinner. Without them..." She flexed her fingers, and for just a heartbeat the firelight bent toward them, hungry, like iron filings finding a lodestone. "Without them, every working comes out of me. Out of the middle. And the middle doesn't grow back quick."

Pip absorbed this with the grave practicality of the very young. "So you spent all your magic on us. On saving us. And now you might not be able to do it again if they come back tomorrow."

"That's roughly the shape of it, yes."

"Was it worth it?"

And here Isolde did something Theo had never seen her do. She laughed—not her usual dry bark, but something softer, startled out of her—and rubbed a hand over her face, and looked at the two of them sitting together in the firelight, the scribe and the star-child, the two loose ends she'd tied herself to.

"Ask me again when they come back tomorrow," she said. "But yes, darling. Yes. Worth it. Don't tell anyone I said so. I have a reputation as a selfish old woman and it's taken decades to build."

Theo should have laughed. Some part of him even wanted to. But the guilt had been sitting in his chest since the road, heavy and specific, and it chose that moment to find its voice.

"It shouldn't have been yours to spend," he said quietly.

Both of them looked at him. He kept his eyes on the fire, because looking at people directly had never come naturally to him, and because tonight the fire was easier.

"The debt was mine. The danger was mine. You bought our way out with the last of your life's work, and I—I hid behind procedure while you did it. I talked clauses at them while you burned thirty years of craft keeping us free." His hands twisted together. "I've spent my whole life making sure no one ever had to pay anything for me. Stay small, stay useful, stay invisible, and no one gets hurt on my account. That was the whole design. And in one week I've gotten you exiled twice over, hunted, and emptied out. I'm not—" His voice snagged. He pushed through it, because Pip was watching him and he refused, absolutely refused, to teach her that men folded. "I'm not worth that trade. I need you to know I know that. What you gave was worth more than what I am."

For a moment nobody spoke. The fire popped. Far off, too far off, something in the dark woods sighed.

"Oh, Theo." Isolde leaned forward, and to his horror her eyes were bright. "You absolute ledger of a man. Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I'm only saying this once and then I'm going back to being insufferable." She pointed one finger at him, and the firelight bent faintly around it. "Worth is not a column in a book. It doesn't balance. Nobody sits down with an abacus at the end and works out whether the friendship came out ahead. You don't *earn* people. You don't owe your way into a family. Someone hands you a bowl of soup, you eat the soup, and the debt is that someday you hand somebody else a bowl of soup. That's the whole economy. Everything else is temple arithmetic, and we've both seen where temple arithmetic gets us."

"The soup economy," Theo repeated weakly.

"The soup economy," Isolde confirmed with dignity. "First principle: no one keeps score."

"I keep score," Theo said. "I can't help it. I remember everything. Every kindness anyone's ever done me, it's all just... filed. I can't make it stop."

"Good," said Isolde. "Keep it. Just stop trying to pay it back to the people who gave it. Pay it forward instead. That's how you honor a gift without insulting it." She settled back against the oak's split trunk, pulling her coat closed, and her voice gentled into something almost sleepy. "Besides. You want to talk about debts? I owed the world a *no*, Theo. Thirty years I carried it, undelivered, like a letter I was too frightened to post. Today I finally sent it. You didn't cost me my tools." She closed her eyes. "You gave me somewhere to spend them."

Pip, curled between them under the shared blanket, was already half asleep. But her voice drifted up, small and certain, from the edge of dreams:

"We're not a trade, Theo. We're a family. Families aren't worth anything. They're worth *everything*. There's a difference."

Theo lay awake a long while after they slept, staring up through the lightning-split crown of the oak at a sky with no stars in it, turning Isolde's words over like a coin of unfamiliar currency. No one keeps score. He tried to imagine it. Twenty-six years of ledgers in his head, and she was asking him to close the books.

And that was when he felt it.

Faintly, distantly, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm: a tug behind his sternum, gentle as a hooked line, patient as interest accruing. The contract, waking. Somewhere south, in a capital with a hollow king, something had opened a new page in his account—and begun, with infinite leisure, to write.

Theo sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and beside him Pip's eyes flew open, glowing softly, already looking at his chest.

"It's writing," she whispered. "Something's writing on you."
