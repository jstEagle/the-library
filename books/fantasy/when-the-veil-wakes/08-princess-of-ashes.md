## 8. Princess of Ashes

Pip counted the ways the world had gone wrong since her eyes started glowing, and she'd gotten very good at it. Wrong thing number one: stars, gone. Wrong thing number two: soldiers who liked to grab girls whose eyes lit up like lanterns in a cellar. Wrong thing number three—and this was the newest one—Theo Marchetti, who had once been so quiet you could lose him behind a coat rack, now kept looking at her like she was something worth keeping.

"You're staring again," she told him as they climbed the last ridge before the valley. Her breath came out in little clouds. The morning was cold enough to bite.

"I'm not."

"You're doing it right now. Your eyebrows go all worried when you do it. Like I'm a pot about to boil over."

Isolde Vane snorted from up ahead. She walked like a woman arguing with the ground and winning. "Leave him be, spark. Scribes stare. It's what they're for. Staring at things and writing them down." She paused at the crest of the ridge, and her voice changed—the way it always did when she saw something that made her want to swear professionally. "Well. There she is. Or what's left of her."

Pip climbed the last few steps and looked down into the valley, and for once she forgot to count wrong things, because there was nothing wrong here at all. The summer palace sat in its bowl of dead gardens like a candle burned down to the socket. Its towers were broken off at odd heights, as if a giant child had snapped them. Ivy had eaten most of the walls. A fountain stood in the middle courtyard, dry for years by the look of it, its stone maiden holding out an empty jug forever.

"It's beautiful," Pip said.

Both adults looked at her.

"It is," she insisted. "It's all broken but nobody swept it away. It's just *there*. Being ruined. Not pretending to be anything else." She hugged her arms around herself. "I like things that aren't pretending."

Theo made a small sound, like he wanted to say something and decided against it, which was his second-favorite kind of sound. His first favorite was the one he made when Isolde swore.

"Coronation night," Isolde said quietly. "Princess Aurelia was supposed to stand beside her father while they crowned him. Instead she ran. They say she fell from a cliff on the coast road. They buried an empty box and the whole kingdom wept for three days." She squinted down at the ruin. "Empty boxes are very useful that way. I've attended two funerals for people I knew were alive, and both times the wine was excellent."

"How do you know she's here?" Theo asked.

"Because I know where everyone goes when they don't want to be found. Ruins. Nobody guards ruins. Nobody taxes ruins." Isolde started down the slope. "Also, a birdkeeper in Millbrook sold me three pigeons last month and complained that someone kept buying his feed in silver old enough to be illegal. Old silver means old money means palace money. Come on. And Pip—if anyone's home, let me do the talking."

"That's your rule for everywhere," Pip said.

"And has it ever once failed me?"

Pip thought about the checkpoint outside Hollowmere, where Isolde's talking had nearly gotten them all arrested until Theo's hidden road saved them. "Don't answer," Isolde said, before Pip could. "Your face answers for you. It's unsettling."

* * *

The palace was bigger on the inside than it wanted them to believe. Corridors opened onto corridors. Doors stood open onto rooms full of birds' nests and drifted leaves. Pip's eyes did their strange work as they walked—she saw the seams of everything now, faint bright lines running through walls and floors and air, like the whole world was stitched together and only she could see the thread. Here in the palace, the seams were tangled. Knots of them clustered in certain doorways, in the dry fountain, along one staircase that glowed so brightly she refused to touch the rail.

"This place is full of old wards," she said, before she could stop herself. "Dead ones. Mostly. That stair's still awake a little bit."

Isolde stopped mid-stride. "The grand stair? Still keyed after—" She turned and looked at Pip with an expression Pip had learned meant *recalculating everything.* "What else can you see?"

"Doors that lie," Pip said. "People, sometimes. You've got a seam right under your collarbone that's all knotted up. It hurts you when you breathe deep. Theo doesn't have any seams at all." She said it simply, because it was simply true. "He's like a page with nothing written on it."

Theo went very still. He'd been doing a lot of going-very-still lately, ever since the night the stars went out and Isolde caught him on the shrine ridge and everything stopped being a story about other people.

"Charming," Isolde said lightly, but Pip saw her hand drift to her collarbone. "Right. The kitchens should be this way if the layout matches the old plans. Palaces keep their kitchens far from the throne room so the screaming doesn't carry."

"Why would there be screaming?"

"Because kings are people, spark, and people scream. Come along."

They found the kitchen by following the smell of woodsmoke, which was the single hopeful thing in the entire ruin. Someone was alive down there, and someone alive meant fire, and fire meant—Pip's stomach growled loud enough to echo, and Theo pressed his lips together in the way he did instead of laughing.

The kitchen door was barred from inside. Isolde raised her fist to knock, considered, and then simply called out in a voice pitched to carry: "We're not soldiers. We're not temple. We're three hungry travelers and one of us is nine and will cry, which is worse than soldiers."

A long silence. Then a voice from within—low, careful, a voice used to being quiet: "How many?"

"Three. As advertised."

"The child's eyes. Are they—"

"Glowing? Yes. Bright as a chapel window. She thinks it's very rude of us to keep mentioning it."

"It IS rude," Pip muttered.

Another silence, longer. Then the bar scraped back, and the door opened on a woman holding a kitchen knife in a grip that said she knew exactly how to use it and regretted that she'd have to.

She was younger than Pip expected, though Pip hadn't known what to expect. Thin as a winter branch, with ash-brown hair cut short and unevenly—as if she'd done it herself with a knife and no mirror. She wore a servant's plain wool dress, mended many times. But her face—

Her face was on every wall in Thornfell. Pip had seen it in painted portraits, in coins held in her palm, in the great stained window of the cathedral that showed the royal family standing under painted stars that no longer existed overhead. Princess Aurelia. Alive. Standing in a ruined kitchen with a knife and flour on her sleeves.

"You're supposed to be dead," Pip said helpfully.

"Pip," Theo whispered, mortified.

"Well, she IS."

Something flickered across the princess's face—not anger. Something rawer and more tired. "So I'm told. Frequently. By myself, mostly, whenever I think about going back." She lowered the knife but didn't put it away. "Who sent you?"

"No one," Isolde said. "That's rather the point of us."

* * *

They ate stewed barley and dried apples at a scarred wooden table, and Aurelia watched them eat with the particular attention of someone who had been alone long enough to forget how noisy company was. Pip ate fast. Theo ate slowly and neatly. Isolde ate like she was interrogating the bowl.

"You came through the pass at Wren's Tooth," Aurelia said at last. "There's no other approach that wouldn't have been seen from the east tower."

"Isolde knows roads that don't exist officially," Theo said.

"I know roads that exist unofficially," Isolde corrected. "Which is different and better."

"You're Vane." Aurelia set down her spoon. "Isolde Vane. The wardbreaker. My father's guild exiled you the year I was ten. Mother said it was political. Cook said you refused a commission and told the Guildmaster exactly where he could put it."

"He remembers it more poetically than I delivered it," Isolde said. "But yes. And you're meant to be bones at the bottom of the sea, Highness, so I'd say we're even on the matter of reputations."

"Don't call me that."

"Highness?"

"*Any* of it. There's no crown to be highness OF. The man wearing my father's face sits on the throne and the kingdom salutes him every morning." Aurelia's hands had curled into fists on the table. "You didn't come all this way through Wren's Tooth to trade titles with me. Why are you here?"

Isolde reached into her coat and brought out the folded decree—the stolen royal paper she'd made Theo read on the shrine ridge, the one with handwriting that predated the king's birth. She laid it flat between the bowls.

Aurelia read it. Pip watched her read it. Pip couldn't read much—letters swam unless they held still—but she could read faces better than either of the adults suspected, and what crossed Aurelia's face went through grief and arrived somewhere colder.

"This is his seal," she said. "His actual signet. And this isn't his writing." Her fingertip hovered over the ink without touching it. "I used to copy letters for him when my tutor was ill. I know his hand better than my own. This is—it's close. It's his hand the way a mask is a face." She looked up. "Where did you get this?"

"Stolen from a courier who no longer needed it," Isolde said. "Along with several other documents suggesting the border wards weren't broken. They were *released.* From inside. By order of the crown." She leaned forward. "So I'll ask plainly, since plainly seems to be your preferred currency. What happened on coronation night? What did you see that made you run and die so convincingly?"

For a moment Pip thought Aurelia wouldn't answer. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the broken towers with a low moaning note, like the palace itself humming an old song it half remembered.

"I saw my father stop blinking," Aurelia said.

Pip felt the words go through her like cold water.

"It was during the crowning. The archpriest lifted the crown and everyone bowed, and I looked up—I was the only one who looked up, I think, everyone else had their heads down—and Father was smiling. He was smiling at the crowd. And then he stopped. Not closed his eyes. *Stopped.* The way a painting stops. And the smile stayed exactly where it was, frozen, like something wearing his face had forgotten faces were supposed to move." Her voice was steady in the way Pip recognized from children in the mines who had learned to be steady or be punished. "Then he blinked, and smiled wider, and the bells rang, and everyone cheered. And I knew—I didn't understand, but I *knew*—that whatever stood up from that throne wearing my father's robes, it wasn't him. So I ran. I've spent two years telling myself I was a coward. That maybe I imagined it. That if I'd stayed, shouted, done *something*—"

"You'd be dead," Isolde said flatly, "and useless. Cowardice and wisdom wear the same coat, Highness. Only hindsight knows which is which."

"Does it never sleep?" Theo asked quietly. It was the first thing he'd said since the stew. "The king. Have you heard anything—from traders, refugees—"

"He never sleeps." Aurelia said it immediately, as if the answer had been waiting in her chest for two years. "The servants who fled the capital talk about it like a ghost story. He holds court at midnight. He receives petitioners at dawn. The chamberlain resigned rather than keep arranging audiences at hours no living man is awake for. And he never blinks in public. People have started betting on it. Little wagers, in taverns, whispered. How long can you stand in the audience hall and watch the king's eyes before he blinks. No one has won yet." She laughed—a short, awful sound with no floor under it. "My father used to fall asleep in council meetings. He snored. He once drooled on a treaty from Callowmere and we had to pretend the stain was a wax seal."

The laugh broke halfway through, and then Aurelia, heir to a stolen throne, put her face in her flour-dusted hands and cried in front of three strangers in a ruined kitchen.

Pip didn't think. Pip never thought—that was her whole trouble according to every adult who'd ever managed her. She slid off her bench, went around the table, and put her arms around the princess's shoulders. They were thin shoulders. Sharp. Like holding a bird that had flown too long in winter.

"He's still in there," Pip said. "Underneath. Things that get worn still have somebody inside them. I know because I used to pretend to be invisible too. Doesn't mean you stop."

Aurelia went stiff, then, slowly, not-stiff. "You're a strange child."

"Everyone says that."

"She's also correct," Theo said softly, "more often than anyone gives her credit for."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned low. Isolde laid it all out—the decree, the sigil that matched Theo's debt contract (at which point Theo studied his boots with tremendous scholarship), the collapsed wards, the starless sky, the patrols hunting a scribe who saw too much and children whose eyes had gone bright. Aurelia listened the way stone listens, absorbing everything, giving back questions sharp enough to draw blood.

When Isolde finally ran down, Aurelia stood, walked to the kitchen window—cracked and grimy but still a window, still showing the dead gardens and the empty fountain—and was silent for a long time.

"I promised myself I would never go back," she said. "I had a whole speech prepared for the next person who found me. About how one girl can't fight a thing that wears kings. About how survival isn't shame." She turned around. In the firelight, with tear-tracks drying on her cheeks and flour still on her sleeves, she looked less like the princess in the stained glass and more like someone real, which Pip thought was probably an improvement. "And then a nine-year-old told me that worn things still have somebody inside them, and now I can't find a single word of my speech."

"So you'll come?" Pip asked.

"I'll come." Aurelia straightened, and something shifted in her spine, some weight rearranging itself from burden into armor. "Not because I believe we can win. Because my father drooled on treaties and sang badly at festivals and once gave me a puppy he'd smuggled into a diplomatic dinner inside his hat, and that man is sitting in the dark inside whatever wears him, and I am *done* leaving him there alone." She picked up the kitchen knife, weighed it, and set it down deliberately, choosing instead a short sword leaning in the corner—an old thing, well kept. "Besides. You'll need me. None of you can walk into the capital. I can. Every guard in that city grew up on my face. It's the one disguise I can't remove."

Isolde grinned, slow and wolfish. "Oh, I like her."

"Everyone does eventually," Aurelia said. "It's exhausting."

Pip tugged at Theo's sleeve and pointed, subtle as a landslide, toward the doorway.

Standing there, half in shadow, was a boy. Maybe twelve. Filthy, wide-eyed, breathing hard like he'd run the whole valley. One of Aurelia's pigeons—no. Not a pigeon. A messenger, a real one, with a courier's satchel and a look of pure animal terror. He stared at Aurelia, and his mouth worked, and what came out was barely a whisper:

"Your Highness—they said—someone had to climb the pass and warn you—we couldn't send a bird, they read the birds now—" He swallowed. "The new moon. They're moving it to the new moon. Whatever the king's been waiting for. It's happening in eleven days."

Nobody spoke. Above them the broken towers moaned in the wind, and far beyond the mountains, in a city none of them could see, something on a throne that never blinked turned its head toward the west—toward the summer palace, toward the ashes, toward them—and smiled.
