## 9. Confessions at the Cold Hearth

The hearth had not been swept in a year, and neither, Aurelia decided, had she.

They had reached the old gamekeeper's cottage an hour past dark—a low stone thing swallowed by ivy on the palace grounds' far edge, the sort of place her mother used to call "the kingdom's forgotten pocket." Pip had found it by walking straight to it, which was becoming less surprising and more unnerving every day. The girl simply looked at the treeline, tilted her head as though listening to something behind the world, and said, *that way.* And that way it always was.

Now the fire caught at last—Isolde muttering at it in wardcant until the damp wood surrendered—and the four of them arranged themselves around it like survivors of a shipwreck, which, Aurelia supposed, they were.

She sat with her back to the wall out of habit. A year of hiding taught you to keep stone at your spine and doorways in your sight. But the fire was warm, and the cottage smelled of old pine and older smoke, and for the first time since she'd fled her father's coronation with ash still in her hair, no one was hunting her tonight. Only the Veil's soldiers were, and they didn't count. They hunted everyone.

Pip sat cross-legged closest to the flames, her eyes catching the light with that faint silver shimmer that made Aurelia's chest ache when she wasn't careful. Theo sat where he always sat: at the edge, half in shadow, a book open on his knee that he hadn't turned a page of in ten minutes.

"We should rest," Isolde said. "Dawn's a long way off."

"Rest," Pip repeated, in the tone of a child who had never once been tired in her life.

Aurelia watched Theo watch the fire, and something in her—the part that had spent a year learning silence, learning how grief could be carried quietly or not carried at all—decided that tonight would not be quiet.

"My father," she said, before she could stop herself, "used to burn porridge."

Three heads turned. Theo closed his useless book.

"Every morning," she went on, staring into the flames because it was easier than staring at them. "He was king-in-waiting, then king, and he could negotiate a trade treaty with three hostile clans in an afternoon, but he could not make porridge. He refused to admit it. He'd scrape the black layer off and serve the middle and say, *there, you see? Perfectly fine,* and my mother would eat it without blinking, and I would eat it, and the whole court pretended for eleven years that the king made excellent porridge."

Pip laughed—a bright, startled sound, like a bell rung by accident.

"He burned it again the morning before the coronation," Aurelia said. Her voice did something strange in the middle. She let it. "I keep thinking—I fled that night. I ran through the kitchens, and there was the pot, still on the hob, burnt as ever. And I thought, *when this is over, I'll go home and he'll have burnt it again,* and that thought is what kept me moving for a year. Not hope for the throne. Hope for terrible porridge."

The fire popped. No one spoke, which was its own kind of kindness.

"So." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way her mother used to. "That's what I lost. Not a crown. Breakfasts." She looked around the circle. "Your turn. All of it. We're going to be marching into the dark together soon; I want to know who I'm marching with. Not the useful parts. The true parts."

* * *

Isolde Vane held out against confession the way she held out against most things: with crossed arms and the expression of a woman auditing your soul and finding it short.

"You've all seen enough of my history," she said. "Guild records are public."

"The guild struck your name from their rolls," Aurelia said. "That's not history, that's erasure. Undo it."

For a long moment Aurelia thought the wardbreaker would simply stand up and take first watch. Then Isolde sighed, the sigh of a woman surrendering a fortress she'd defended too long, and pulled something from inside her coat: a small wooden badge, scorched around one edge. The sigil of the Wardwrights' Guild, third rank, worn smooth by years of a thumb rubbing over it.

"I was nineteen when I took my oaths," she said. "You swear to build wards that protect. That's the whole creed. Protect. Not *obey*, not *serve the crown*. Protect." She turned the badge over. On the back, scratched deep enough to scar the wood, someone had carved two words: *NOT MINE.*

"The crown commissioned the border lattice thirty years ago. Grand work. The pride of the guild. My master designed it. And when I rose high enough to see the full schematics—" her jaw tightened— "I saw what the lattice actually fed on. Wards don't run on nothing, Highness. They run on bound things. Anchored souls, mostly. People sold at birth, consecrated in temple vaults, their whole lives siphoned into keeping the borders bright. Nobody knows. Nobody's *meant* to know. The wards shine and everyone sleeps safe and no one asks what the light costs."

Theo had gone very still. Aurelia noticed, and filed it away, and hated herself a little for noticing.

"I refused to sign the maintenance charter," Isolde said. "Told the Guildmaster exactly what I thought of feeding children to fence posts. He told me I could recant or lose everything. Rank, name, workshop, the lot." She tucked the badge away. "I gave a speech. It was magnificent. Three people clapped. Then they took my tools and put me out the gate, and I spent twenty years doing illegal wardwork in smuggler towns, which pays better and thanks you more."

"And the words on the badge?" Pip asked softly.

Isolde was quiet long enough that the fire filled the silence.

"When they expelled me, the Guildmaster said, *this failure is yours alone.*" She smiled, thin and sharp. "So I carved the truth where I'd see it daily. *Not mine.* The lattice was never my failure. It was theirs." Her eyes flicked to Theo, just once, quickly. "Some debts aren't owed by the people carrying them. Remember that. All of you."

* * *

Pip's turn came easiest and hardest, the way children's truths do.

"What I lost?" She considered this with the seriousness of a judge. "Everything. But also nothing, if that makes sense."

"It doesn't," Isolde said gently.

"Well." Pip hugged her knees. "I never had anything, so I couldn't lose it. See? Ma died when I was small. Da went down the mine and didn't come up, and the company said his debt died with him so we shouldn't grieve twice, which I think was meant kindly but wasn't. So I lived under the tannery steps with the other gutter-mice, and we had a system, and it was a good system, and nobody starved worse than usual." She said it lightly, the way children narrate horrors they haven't yet learned are horrors. "Then the stars went out, and my eyes started glowing, and the mice decided glowing attracts soldiers, so they left me. Which was fair! I understood. Glowing *does* attract soldiers."

Aurelia's throat hurt.

"But here's the nothing part," Pip continued, warming to her theme. "When the stars went out, I could suddenly see—" she waved a hand vaguely at the air, at the seams of the world— "*everything underneath.* Like the world's a coat and I got turned inside out. And underneath everything, there's threads. Threads between people. And do you know what I saw when I looked at us? At this fire?"

"No," said Theo, who rarely spoke at these hours, and whose voice sounded rougher than usual.

"A thread." Pip pointed at each of them in turn, drawing invisible lines across the firelight. "Four ends. One knot. I don't know what kind of knot—it's not a ward-knot, Isolde, I asked, stop looking worried—it's a different kind. A family-shaped one." She shrugged, matter-of-fact as weather. "So I didn't lose everything. I lost everything I *had*, and then I got everything I *am*. That's a fair trade, mostly. Except for the stars. I'd like the stars back."

"We'll get them back," Aurelia said, and was surprised to find she believed it—not as hope, but as plan.

Pip beamed, silver-eyed, satisfied, and stole the last of the bread.

* * *

Which left Theo.

He had gone quieter and quieter as the night wore on, folded into his corner of shadow like a man trying to become furniture—which, Aurelia was beginning to understand, was his native talent. Now the silence around him had weight. Everyone felt it. Even the fire seemed to lean toward him.

"Theo," Pip prompted, mouth full of stolen bread.

"I don't have one," he said.

"A loss," Aurelia said. "Everyone has one."

"That's the problem." He set the unread book down carefully, aligned with the floorboards, a small precise gesture that told Aurelia more about him than any speech could. "I've been sitting here listening to all of you, and—Isolde lost a guild. You lost a father. Pip lost—well. Everything and nothing. And I kept waiting for mine to come up. And it won't. Because I can't name it." He laughed, short and unhappy. "Do you know how frightening that is, for a man with a memory like mine? I remember everything. Every page I've ever copied. Every face. I can recite the tithe rolls of Thornfell for six years running. And somewhere in all of that, there should be something I lost. A person. A place. A grief. Everyone has at least one. And there's just—" his hands opened, empty— "nothing. A smooth spot. Like a river stone where a life should be."

The wind moved through the ivy outside. No one interrupted.

"I used to think it was a gift," Theo said. "Being overlooked. Being forgettable. It kept me alive on smuggler roads and in temple corridors. Nobody robs a ghost. But now I'm starting to wonder—" he looked at the fire, and Aurelia saw fear in him plainly, the clean honest terror of a man examining his own foundations— "what if it isn't a temperament? What if it's a design? What if the reason I have no losses is that someone arranged my life so there'd be nothing in it worth losing?"

"Whoa," said Pip.

"Yes," Theo agreed. "Whoa."

Isolde leaned forward. Whatever she was going to say, Aurelia could see her choosing it with a craftsman's care. "Theo. Listen to me. I've handled anchored souls before—in the bad years, after exile, I cut a few loose. There's a look they get. A hollowness. A man scraped down to function." Her voice gentled into something Aurelia had never heard from her. "That's not you. You sit wrong for a tool. Tools don't feel guilty about being afraid. Tools don't break their own rules to save gutter-children, or memorize tithe rolls out of sheer stubborn thoroughness, or—" she gestured at him— "sit at the edge of fires. Whatever made you invisible, it didn't manage to make you empty. There's a great deal worth losing in you. We'd know. We're losing sleep over it."

Theo stared at her. Then at the rest of them. Something moved behind his eyes—some locked door rattling on its hinges.

"You realize," he said at last, unsteadily, "that none of you had any reason to say that."

"Families don't require reasons," Pip said. "I checked. There's no paperwork."

And Theo Marchetti—who had spent his whole life believing he was a smooth stone where a man should be—laughed. It cracked in the middle and turned into something else, and Aurelia reached over and put her hand on his shoulder and left it there, and Pip scooted over and leaned against his side without asking, and Isolde pretended to inspect the fire with tremendous professional interest while absolutely failing to hide that she was smiling.

They stayed like that until the fire burned low. Aurelia found herself cataloguing it the way she'd once catalogued her father's court: *the scribe laughing, the wardbreaker humming, the star-child asleep against a stranger's ribs.* This, she thought, is what he's fighting to take from us. Not our lands. Our hearths. And this is what we fight with.

* * *

It was near dawn when Theo spoke again, and the warmth had gone hard in his voice, forged into something with an edge.

"I've been thinking about what I *can* name," he said. "Not a loss. An error." He drew from his coat the folded copy of the royal decree, and beside it, from an inner pocket, a much-folded scrap of yellowed vellum—his own debt contract, Aurelia realized, the paper he'd been bound by since birth. He laid them side by side in the firelight. Two seals. Identical.

"I told you the sigils matched. Tonight I finally worked out *why* I recognized it so fast." His finger traced the coiled design pressed into both waxes. "Because I've copied this seal before. Hundreds of times. In the Thornfell vaults, on documents I was told never to speak of. Tithe redirects. Gold moving where grain should be. Payments routed to accounts that don't exist, sealed with *this*—" he tapped the Veil's mark— "going back further than my records reach. The debt system and the Veil aren't connected. They're the same machine. Someone's been buying something with the kingdom's gold for longer than anyone alive can remember, and every purchase was signed for in a script older than the king."

"Buying what?" Aurelia asked.

Theo looked up at her, and his photographic memory had clearly already assembled the answer from a hundred fragments he'd never known were pieces.

"An anchor," he said. "Or anchors. Plural. Your Majesty—Highness—we need to go to the temple vaults. Not the ones in Thornfell. The originals, under the capital, beneath the throne itself. Because whatever I am, whatever was done to me, the record of it will be there. And I think—" his voice dropped— "I think the reason the void in that throne room knew my name is written on those shelves."

Isolde was already pulling her boots back on. Pip woke mid-motion, eyes flaring silver, saying only, "Under. Yes. Under."

Outside, above the black hills, the sky remained naked and starless—but the horizon east had begun to turn the grey of a blade's edge, and Aurelia rose, ashes and porridge and all, and reached for her sword.

"Then we ride for the capital," she said. "Before the new moon."
