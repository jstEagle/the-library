## 10. The Hollow Court

The plan, if you could dignify it with the word *plan*, was Isolde's. It had three parts, which she counted off on her fingers the night before while Pip slept against Aurelia's shoulder and the fire cracked low.

"One: we get you into the capital wearing a tithe clerk's badge, because nobody in the history of the world has ever looked twice at a tithe clerk. Two: you get into the throne room during the quarterly accounting presentation, because even hollow kings need their ledgers read to them. Three—" she'd paused here, and her face had done something complicated, "—you look at him, and you tell us what you see."

"That's not a plan," I said. "That's a list of ways for me to die."

"It's a *short* list," Isolde said, with the particular warmth of a woman who had once been exiled for honesty. "Be grateful."

So now, three days later, I was Theo Marchetti, junior tithe clerk of the Eastern Reaches, third from the bottom of a procession of eleven clerks shuffling through the capital's Iron Gate, and I was discovering that being invisible on purpose felt very different from being invisible by design.

For one thing, it itched.

The clerk's badge was real—Isolde had acquired it from a man named Ferro who owed her money and would never collect the favor back—and it sat against my chest like a coal. For nine-and-twenty years, people had looked through me as though I were furniture with opinions. That had been the Veil's doing, I understood now, or at least I suspected it, the way you suspect a draft without ever finding the window. My whole life had been built in the shadow of that forgetting. And now, walking through the capital's gates with a forged badge and a forged purpose, I kept bracing for the moment someone would squint at me and say *wait*.

Nobody said wait. A gate sergeant glanced at my papers, stamped them, and called me "clerk" in the tone most men reserve for mud. A fruit seller ignored me so thoroughly she nearly knocked me into her own cart. An actual tithe clerk marching three rows ahead turned around once, looked directly at me, and asked if I had a spare nib.

I gave him the nib. He nodded like we were brothers.

"See?" Isolde had said. "You're made for this."

I wasn't sure whether to be flattered or horrified. I settled, as usual, for both.

* * *

The capital had changed since I'd last seen it, two years ago, copying contracts in its temple annexes. The change was hard to name at first. Then I realized: there were no birds.

Pigeons still strutted the gutters—I could hear them, fat and shameless—but nothing flew above the rooftops. No gulls off the river, no swallows stitching the evening air. The sky over the palace district was a clean, empty blue, the way a plate is clean after everything on it has been eaten.

And the banners. Every flagpole in the city flew the king's colors—or what had been the king's colors, the silver stag on deep green. Except the green was wrong. Not faded, not dyed badly. *Wrong*, the way a word is wrong when you've stared at it too long, all its letters present and arranged correctly and yet somehow not itself. I caught myself staring at one until a guard told me to move along, and even then I couldn't have described what was off about it. Only that looking at it felt like looking at a stranger wearing a friend's coat.

"Quarterly accounting," our escort announced at the palace steps, in the bored voice of a man who has said it forty times. "Clerks proceed in single file. You will present your rolls. You will not speak to His Majesty unless addressed. You will not approach the dais. You will not linger."

We proceeded. We presented. Somewhere ahead of me, behind doors tall enough to swallow a chapel, the King of Thornfell waited to hear about grain.

Grain that, according to every roll I'd memorized since the wards fell, was no longer grain at all. Gold moving where grain should be. The same wrongness I'd found that first night in Thornfell, multiplied across every province, every ledger, every line. The kingdom's wealth was flowing somewhere, in quantities that should have emptied the treasury thrice over, and no one—not the treasurers, not the clerks beside me, not the guards—seemed able to see it. The numbers sat right there in ink and everyone read past them.

Everyone except a debt-bound scribe nobody bothered to forget properly.

* * *

The throne room was colder than it had any right to be in late summer.

They lined us up along the eastern wall, twenty-odd clerks with our rolls held before us like offerings, and I got my first clear look at the dais, and the throne, and the thing sitting on it.

My first thought, absurdly, was that he looked *well*. The king had been old for as long as I'd been alive—a gray, weathered presence on coins and proclamations, more institution than man. This figure was upright. Composed. His hands rested on the armrests with the ease of long practice. From thirty feet away, in the flat light falling through the high windows, Aldric IV looked exactly like the portraits.

Then the light shifted as a cloud crossed the sun, and I stopped being able to pretend.

It was the eyes first. They didn't move. Not the stillness of an old man resting them, not the fixed stare of a portrait—they simply did not *do* anything. They didn't track the chamberlain reading the grain totals. They didn't blink. And beneath the skin of his face, something was... absent. I can only describe it the way I'd describe a copied page that has been copied so many times the letters are perfect and the meaning is gone. Everything was where it should be. Nothing was home.

He's a mask, I thought, and the thought arrived with the calm of a fact already known. He's been a mask for a long time.

Behind the mask—if you could call a king's living face a mask; behind the *shape* of him—there was dark. Not shadow. Shadow is just absence of light, and it behaves politely. This was a darkness with weight, with appetite, coiled up inside the royal body the way smoke is coiled inside a bottle, patient, filling every corner of the space it's given.

And I knew it.

Not the way you know a story. The way you know your own handwriting. Something in me leaned toward that darkness like a plant toward sun, and the horror of that leaning was worse than any fear I've ever felt, because fear at least comes from outside.

I gripped my roll of accounts until my knuckles went white and made myself count clerks instead. Eleven. Twelve with me. Breathe. Count again.

The chamberlain droned: "Eastern Reaches, quarterly tithe, wheat, barley, oats—" and I heard the gold underneath the words, silent as a heartbeat under cloth.

* * *

Here is what I will say about terror: it is much easier to carry when you are busy. The presentation took two hours. Clerks stepped forward one at a time, recited their figures, bowed, withdrew. When my turn came, I walked to the marked spot on the floor, opened my roll, and read the Eastern Reaches' quarterly accounts aloud to a dead man.

My voice did not shake. Nine-and-twenty years of being overlooked had taught me how to be steady in front of power; it turns out that skill transfers perfectly to reading lies in front of a monster. I recited the false numbers clearly, and as I did, I watched him.

I shouldn't have. I knew I shouldn't have. But some part of me—the part that had spent a lifetime studying faces because faces never studied me back—needed to know if anything behind those unmoving eyes would react to the lie.

Nothing reacted. And then, everything did.

It began as a pressure, like the moment before thunder when the air goes thick. The torch flames along the walls leaned, all together, toward me, though no wind moved. The chamberlain faltered mid-word, frowned at his notes as though they'd betrayed him, and continued. None of the other clerks seemed to notice anything at all.

But the king turned his head.

Slowly. Smoothly, the way oil moves, with none of the small hesitations a human neck performs without thinking. Those unblinking eyes came around and settled on me, and I learned in that instant what it means to be seen after a lifetime of not being seen, and I would give almost anything to unlearn it.

There was no cruelty in that gaze. That was the worst of it. There was recognition—vast, unhurried, almost tender, the way a farmer looks at a field he planted himself.

*There you are,* said a voice, and it did not come through my ears. It came up through the floor, through the soles of my boots, through the bones the boots stood on, and it also came from inside my own skull, from a room in my head I had never known was furnished.

*Little anchor. Little lamp left burning in an empty house.*

My mouth kept moving. Some trained, obedient fragment of me went on reciting the barley totals while the rest of me stood in the path of that attention like a rabbit under a hawk's circling. I finished the line. I bowed. My knees performed the bow correctly, which I consider among the great achievements of my life.

*I know you,* the voice said, warm as a banked fire, cold as the space between stars. Both at once. *Of course I know you. Who do you think taught you to be small?*

I walked back to my place along the wall. One step. Two. The torches straightened. The chamberlain recovered. The world resumed pretending to be ordinary, and I stood among the other clerks with my heart slamming against my ribs and sweat crawling down between my shoulder blades, and I thought, absurdly, *don't faint, scribes who faint get carried out and examined, don't faint.*

*Theo,* said the voice, gently, tasting the syllables.

Except—it didn't say Theo. That was the thing. That was the thing that unraveled me, standing there in the cold throne room trying to breathe through my nose like a calm person. The voice said a name, but not *my* name, not the four plain letters I had answered to my whole life. It said something older, rounder, a name with weight and edges, a name that fit me the way a key fits a lock it was cut for, and I had never heard it before and yet my whole body answered to it, the way a dog answers to a whistle pitched below hearing.

*That is what you are called,* the darkness murmured, pleased, proprietary. *That is what you were called before they gave you the little name. Before they wrote you down in the small books. I wrote you first.*

The presentation ended. The king rose—smoothly, terribly, like water pouring upward—and processed out through the far doors, and the entire court bowed, and I bowed, my spine bent double over the roll of accounts that proved the kingdom was bleeding gold into the dark.

*Come back, little anchor,* the voice said, fading, fond. *You always come home. Ask your mother.*

* * *

Afterward I remember pieces. Walking out through the corridors on legs that belonged to someone else. Presenting my exit papers to a guard who called me "clerk." The fruit seller near the gate ignoring me with tremendous consistency. Finding the safehouse—an abandoned dyer's loft that smelled of old mordant and vinegar—and climbing the stairs, and finding them waiting.

Isolde took one look at my face and put down whatever she was tinkering with. "Talk."

"He knows me," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like paper. "Not—not the way he'd know anyone. Isolde, he called me by a name. A true name. Mine. And I'd never heard it, and I knew it anyway. It fit." My hands had started shaking now that it was safe to shake, the way they always did. "He said he wrote me before anyone else did. He called me—"

I stopped. Saying it out loud felt dangerous, like speaking a spell backward.

"He called me *anchor*."

Pip, curled in the window seat with her star-flecked eyes half closed, sat bolt upright. She looked at me with an expression I'd come to dread from her—the expression of someone seeing a seam in the world that everyone else's eyes politely skip.

"Theo," she said slowly, "when you talk, sometimes the dark talks back. Did you know that? It's been talking back since the throne room. Real quiet. Like it's waiting for you to finish a sentence."

Aurelia went pale in the way of someone learning her father's fate has another terrible layer. Isolde swore, long and fluently, in three languages and one language that predated languages.

And I stood in the middle of the dyer's loft, holding a forged badge and a stolen nib, and understood that the door I'd opened today did not close behind me. The Veil hadn't merely recognized me.

It was listening.
