## 12. The Price of the Bridge

The gorge announced itself long before they saw it—a low, grinding roar like the world clearing its throat, growing louder with every mile of the old shepherd track they followed east.

Theo knew the sound. He had hoped, absurdly, that he might have forgotten it.

They crested a ridge of gorse and broken slate in the late afternoon, and there it was: the Serpent's Throat, where the river Vell cut a wound three hundred feet deep through the heart of the borderlands, and the only bridge for forty miles in either direction was the Tollbridge at Karrek's Rest—planks and rope and iron, strung across the chasm like a stitch holding a body together, guarded by men who charged for passage the way priests charged for salvation.

Pip leaned over Theo's shoulder to look. "It's pretty," she said.

"It's expensive," Theo said.

"Everything's expensive."

"Not everything." He thought of the cold hearth, of Aurelia laughing at Isolde's terrible singing, of Pip asleep against his ribs like something he'd been carrying all his life without noticing. "Some things you just have."

Isolde came up beside them, squinting at the bridge through the gray light. Her expression did the thing it always did when she was doing sums she didn't like. "Guards on both ends. Four visible. Probably six more under the toll-house. That's not a patrol, that's a garrison. Why would toll-lords staff up like that unless someone told them to watch for travelers?"

"Because someone did," Theo said.

The words came out flat, and everyone heard the flatness, because by now they all knew what his flatness meant—the same way they'd learned that when he went quiet, he was remembering, and when he remembered too hard, his hands shook slightly until he folded them still.

Aurelia looked from him to the bridge and back again. She had gotten very good at reading him these last weeks. He wasn't sure how he felt about that. Being read was supposed to be the one thing that never happened to him.

"Theo," she said gently. "Whose bridge is this?"

He watched the planks sway in the wind. Somewhere below, the river ground away at its ancient work.

"Theo," Aurelia said again. "Whose?"

* * *

He told them that night, in the lee of an abandoned drover's hut with a fire small enough to hide in your fist. He made himself sit where they could all see his face, which was the hardest part. He had spent twenty-six years arranging himself so no one would look. Now he arranged himself to be looked at, and it felt like standing naked in a market square.

"You should know what I am before we get any closer to that bridge," he said. "So you can decide if you want me along."

"You're a scribe," Pip said confidently.

"I was a thief first." The fire popped. He kept his voice level. "When I was sixteen. Before the temple took me in."

Aurelia sat very still. Isolde fed another stick to the flames and said nothing, which from Isolde was a kind of courtesy.

"There were four of us," Theo said. "Smuggler kids, running goods down the Vell—salt, mostly, sometimes medicine, once a shipment of ward-ink that I didn't ask enough questions about. We worked the Serpent's Throat because the toll-lords took a cut and left us alone. Ten percent. It was robbery, but it was honest robbery. Predictable. There are worse things than predictable."

"Wren," Pip said suddenly.

Theo blinked. "What?"

"That's what I'm going to call this story. Wren. It's my game. I name all your stories now." She hugged her knees. "You look like a wren when you talk about the past. Small and quick and hiding in hedges."

Despite everything, something in Theo's chest loosened. "All right. Wren." He took a breath. "There was a man named Doss Karrek who ran the toll then. Big man, loud, mean to his own people but fair in his accounting—that mattered more than kindness, in the Throat. Fair accounting was the law. And I was good with numbers. Better than good. I could hold a ledger in my head, whole pages, whole years. Doss noticed. He started letting me check his tallies. Sat me by his fire. Gave me the corner seat near the warmth."

He heard his own voice go distant, the way it did when memory turned photographic and merciless, every detail preserved in amber whether he wanted it or not.

"And I used it," he said. "I found where he kept the winter take—not the toll money, the *real* take, the years of skimmed silver he kept off the books even from his own captains. Three hundred crowns. Enough for four starving kids to disappear forever. Buy names. Buy boats. Buy lives."

"What happened?" Pip whispered.

"I told the others it was just sitting there. Unclaimed. I told myself the same thing." His hands had started their faint tremble; he folded them in his lap. "We took it. All of it. And Doss Karrek—" Theo stopped. Started again. "Doss had a daughter. Sefa. Twelve years old. When the theft was discovered, his captains decided a man who couldn't guard his own strongbox couldn't guard the toll. They put it to the crews, the way they did things in the Throat. Vote of knives."

The fire cracked. Nobody moved.

"They gave him to the river," Theo said. "Weighted. And Sefa watched it happen, because making the family watch was part of it, so no one would ever forget what failure cost. Then the crews scattered us—hunted us, actually, because Doss had been popular underneath the meanness. Two of my friends didn't run fast enough." His voice stayed level. He had practiced staying level for ten years. "One drowned trying to swim the Vell in flood season. The other was caught. I don't know what they did to her. I've never let myself work it out, and I can do almost any sum, but not that one.

"I got to Thornfell and sold the only thing I had left, which was obedience. The temple bought it with room and board and a debt-bond I'll be paying until I'm dead or worse. I told myself it was penance. Mostly it was hiding. You can't be found if no one sees you. I built a whole life out of not being seen." He finally lifted his eyes from the fire. "And now the road to the vaults runs straight across Doss Karrek's bridge, held by the same crews, and every mile I've walked with you has been built on top of a lie I let you believe—that I was just a scribe. Just small. Just harmless."

Silence. Long enough that the river's roar filled the hut like weather.

Then Pip uncurled, crossed the space between them, and put her starlit hands on either side of his face.

"You're not harmless," she said sternly. "You're *ours*."

* * *

It was Aurelia who spoke next, after Pip had retreated satisfied and Theo's eyes had stopped burning in a way he refused to call crying.

"Three hundred crowns," she said. "Where is it now?"

"Gone. Spent within a month. Boats, papers, bribes, funerals. Nothing left to return."

"Then returning it isn't possible. Good. That simplifies things." She said it the way she said most things—as though justice were a puzzle she intended to solve rather than a grief she intended to perform. "What's possible instead?"

"Nothing," Theo said. "That's the point of what I did. Some ledgers can't be balanced. Only paid into."

"Then we pay into it." Aurelia leaned forward. "Tell me about the crews. Who holds the toll now?"

"Doss's old first captain. Maret Voss. She voted for the drowning—she led it. She's held the bridge eleven years. She's fair in her accounting too, which somehow makes it worse." He swallowed. "If she sees my face, there won't be a vote. There won't be anything. And she has every right. I want to be clear about that—I'm not asking you to protect me from consequences. I'm telling you I'll offer myself at the toll-house tomorrow. Myself as payment. Passage for the three of you in exchange for whatever justice the Throat decides I owe. It's the first entry in ten years I can actually make good on."

"No," Isolde said.

Theo turned. "You of all people—"

"I of all people know what happens when good people hand themselves to crowds with knives." Isolde's voice was quiet and absolutely final. "I refused to build the wards that fell, and I paid for it with my guild, my name, my livelihood. It nearly destroyed me, and I had *allies*. You'll have a rope and a river." She jabbed a finger at him. "Guilt is not a currency, scribe. Stop trying to spend yourself."

"Then what would you have me do? Walk across and pretend?"

"No," Aurelia said. "You walk across and *tell the truth*. Out loud. To Maret Voss. And then you let me negotiate."

"Aurelia—"

"I negotiated trade treaties between clans who'd been killing each other for six generations. I was twelve. One of them tried to poison the tea." A ghost of her mother's smile crossed her face. "Trust me. And Theo—if it goes wrong, if they take you, Isolde will pull you out of any cell they put you in, because she owes you, and Pip will make the walls glow until they beg for quiet, and I will stand in front of the crews and invoke river-law, which predates the kingdom, which predates the Veil, and which says a debt may be answered by service as well as suffering. Do you understand? You don't get to buy our safety with your body. We're a family. Families negotiate *together*."

Pip raised her hand. "I vote we also threaten them."

"Pip."

"Fine. I vote we *seem* like we might threaten them."

* * *

Morning came gray and windless. They walked down to the toll-house side by side, and Theo made himself walk in front, which was its own kind of unbinding.

Maret Voss was exactly as memory had preserved her—broad, iron-haired, with a scarred jaw and a ledger-clerk's squint. She looked at the four of them coming up the switchback road, and her eyes went past the princess, past the wardbreaker, past the glowing child, and stopped on Theo with the slow, terrible certainty of a hawk finding a vole in open grass.

"Well," she said. "The little counter."

"Maret." His mouth was dry. "I've come to pay a debt."

"Have you." She stepped down from the toll-plank, and behind her the guards drifted closer, hands loose near knife-hilts. "Eleven years, and you walk up my road on your own feet. That's either courage or stupidity, and you were never the stupid one."

"He's ours," Pip said, stepping forward, starlight pooling in her palms.

Maret looked at the glow. Something flickered across her face—not fear. Recognition. "Throat-child," she said softly. "Born under the dark sky. We've had word about your kind." Her gaze returned to Theo. "Inside. All of you. Bridge stays shut till this is settled."

* * *

The toll-house smelled of tallow and wet rope. Maret sat them at a table older than all of them and laid a knife flat on the wood between them—point toward herself, which Theo understood, and which terrified him more than the point aimed anywhere else.

"Say it," Maret said. "All of it. The crews will hear it true or not at all."

So Theo said it. Again. Worse, this time, because he added the parts he'd spared his family—Doss's laugh, the corner seat, the night he'd traced the strongbox route in charcoal on a scrap of floorboard and burned the scrap and memorized it anyway. He watched Maret's face do nothing at all, and understood she was holding herself the way he held himself.

When he finished, she was silent a long moment.

"Sefa lives," Maret said finally. "Westreach. Married a cooper. Two children." She turned the knife slowly. "She comes to the Throat every spring, stands on my bridge, and looks at the water. Every year I think she'll ask me why I led the vote. Every year she doesn't. Maybe because she knows I ask it loud enough for both of us." Her eyes lifted. "Here's Throat-law, scribe, older than your temples, older than your crown: a stolen debt can be answered in coin, in pain, or in *service*—and service outweighs the other two, because anyone can bleed but not everyone builds. You cost this bridge a keeper. So you'll build for it. Winter's coming, the east anchorage is rotting, and I need every plank replaced before the ice. You and your hands and that famous head of yours, working my bridge until it's sound. That's the price. Pay it, and the ledger between you and Doss Karrek closes in the eyes of the crews. Fail, and the river collects the old way."

"That could take weeks," Isolde said sharply. "We don't have weeks. There's a new moon coming and a king being eaten and—"

"Then you'd best all pick up hammers," Maret said, "because Throat-law says the debtor serves, and his sworn kin serve beside him if they choose." Her gaze swept the four of them, and something dry moved at the corner of her mouth. "Or they can watch him work alone. Kinship's a choice here too."

Aurelia stood, pulled off her rings—one of the few things she had left of her mother—and set them on the table. "Materials," she said. "Consider it an investment. And I'll want the crew tallies, Mistress Voss. If I'm swinging a hammer, I'm managing the accounts."

For the first time in eleven years, Maret Voss laughed.

* * *

They worked nine days.

Theo learned that rope burns heal slower than guilt, and that Pip could find dropped nails in the dark by their faint reflection of her own light, and that Aurelia, princess of a hollowed throne, was terrifyingly good at getting free timber from farmers by simply telling the truth about why they needed it. Isolde grumbled, warded the scaffolding against the wind, and secretly re-engineered the anchorage so well that Maret quietly asked if she was available for other projects and received a look that ended the conversation.

On the ninth evening, the last plank went down, and Maret called the crews to witness. She stood Theo at the center of the bridge, over the black water, where his legs wanted to buckle—and instead of a knife, she pressed into his hands a small iron token, stamped with the mark of the Throat.

"Debt witnessed. Debt paid," she said, loud enough for the gorge to carry it. "Walk free, little counter. And if you ever steal again—" the dry smile returned "—steal from people who deserve it, and count better."

As they crossed at dawn the next morning, Pip walking backward ahead of them so she could grin at everyone at once, Theo paused at the exact middle of the span and looked down. The river roared on, indifferent, ancient, forgiving nothing and punishing nothing—just moving, the way rivers do, toward the sea.

"Come on, Wren," Pip said. "Stories don't wait."

He caught up. He was learning to catch up.

But two miles past the far anchorage, Isolde—who had been quiet even by her standards—suddenly stopped on the road and held up a hand.

"The token," she said. "Give it here."

Theo handed over the iron stamp. Isolde turned it in her fingers, frowning, then held it up against the gray sky.

Stamped into the reverse side—too fine to see by eye, but plain as daylight to a wardbreaker's sight—was a sigil he knew better than his own handwriting. A spiral, closing inward. The seal from his debt contract. The seal from the king's decree.

"It's been on the toll since before Doss Karrek died," Isolde said quietly. "Maybe longer. This bridge isn't theirs, Theo. It never was." She looked east, toward the temple vaults waiting beyond the hills, and her jaw tightened. "Someone wanted us to cross here. Someone's been leaving doors open along this road—and I'm starting to wonder if we've been walking through them, or walking *in*."

Far off, at the bottom of the gorge, the river seemed to change its note—just for a moment—almost like a voice.
