## 13. Beneath the Temple Floor

The stair down had no name.

Theo knew this because he had spent three days memorizing every scrap of the temple's under-records before they came, and in all those scraps—the mason's ledgers from four hundred years ago, a surveyor's complaint about damp, one scandalized abbot's letter regarding "the improper storage of certain relics beneath the tithe hall"—not once did anyone write down what to call the staircase that spiraled below the Thornfell temple's foundations.

Which meant, in Theo's professional opinion as a scribe of eleven years, that it was either unimportant or unspeakable.

"Cheerful thought," Isolde muttered behind him, her wardbreaker's lamp throwing long shadows up the walls. "Keep talking, Marchetti. I like knowing you're still between me and whatever's down there."

"You're behind me because you insisted on being behind me."

"I insisted on not leading. There's a difference." Her boots scraped on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet that had never been recorded. "Pip, you all right?"

"I can see the stairs humming," Pip said cheerfully, from somewhere in the middle. "They hum in old gold and tired brown. Like bees that gave up."

Theo glanced back at her. In the lamplight her eyes were two faint coins of silver, and the seams of the world—she'd shown him once, pressing his hand flat against a wall so he could feel where her gaze snagged—writhed down here like roots under a road. Whatever was below them, the Veil had threaded through it thickly.

"That's reassuring," Isolde said dryly. "Bees that gave up. Wonderful."

"They're *tired* bees," Pip said, as if this were an important distinction. It probably was. Everything with Pip was.

The stair ended at a door.

Not a grand door. Not an iron-banded thing with warnings carved into it. Just a plain oak door, small, the kind you'd find on a pantry or a priest's privy, and Theo felt something in his chest go tight and strange, because he *knew* that door. He had never seen it in his life, and he knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

"The ledger room," he said. His voice came out flat. "I dreamed about it when I was six. I told my mother there was a little door under the temple and she laughed and said I'd never been to Thornfell."

"You hadn't?" Isolde asked sharply.

"I was born here."

The words dropped out of him like stones down a well. He heard them land far below, echoing.

Isolde was quiet for a moment. Then: "You said you grew up in Marrowdale."

"I said I was raised in Marrowdale." He put his hand on the door. The oak was cool and slightly damp, patient as everything else down here. "There's a difference. Apparently there's been a difference my whole life and I only just now learned to say it out loud."

He opened the door.

* * *

The vault beyond was not a treasure chamber. It was a library that had given up pretending.

Shelves of ledgers ran back into the dark farther than the lamp could reach, and the air had that particular smell Theo associated with the happiest hours of his life—old paper, binding glue, dust settling on ink—and it hurt, actually physically hurt, to find that smell down here, attached to this place, as if someone had taken the best part of him and used it as bait.

"Well," Isolde said. "This is either very good news or the worst archive in the kingdom."

"It's organized by year," Theo said numbly. His feet were already carrying him down the first aisle, his eyes already reading spines without his permission. That was the curse of him—he couldn't *not* read. Every word that crossed his vision stayed there forever, filed and cross-referenced, a memory that never slept. "Tithe rolls, consecration registers, bond indentures... it's a full scriptorium. Underground. Unlisted."

"Why would a temple keep a whole archive nobody admits exists?"

Theo stopped walking.

Because he knew the answer. He'd known it since chapter and verse of his apprenticeship, since old Brother Callow had rapped his knuckles for asking why some records went to the deep shelves and some went to the fire. Records weren't hidden because they were false, Callow had said. Records were hidden because they were *load-bearing*. Pull one out and see what falls.

"Because the debt system sits on top of it," Theo said slowly. "Every bond in the valley. Every tithe. Every contract. They're all copies of copies of things written down here first. If anyone ever read the originals—"

"They'd ask who the debts really belong to," Isolde finished.

Somewhere above them, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the temple bell rang the hour. Pip's head tilted. "The bees got louder," she whispered.

"We're on a clock," Isolde said. "Marchetti. Find yourself."

It should have been a simple sentence. He stood in an aisle of centuries and didn't know which shelf held the answer to what he was.

But his hands knew.

His hands had always known things before he did—that was how he'd survived childhood, how he'd learned to copy documents in a single pass and never miss a stroke. His hands walked him past forty years of registers, eighty, a hundred, and stopped at a shelf low near the floor, in the corner where damp had bloomed the leather green, and drew out a ledger with a spine labeled in a dead clerk's careful hand:

**Consecrations — Living Bonds — Restricted**

"I don't want to open this," Theo said honestly.

"Open it," said Isolde and Pip, in perfect unison.

He opened it.

* * *

The entries were brief. That was the worst part, later, when he tried to describe it to Aurelia around a fire he couldn't feel the warmth of—the worst part wasn't horror. Horror is loud. The worst part was how *brief* it was. How administrative.

*Thirdday, Sowing Moon. Child presented by Marchetti, Aldous, wheelwright, and Marchetti-Sorrel, Wren, weaver. Male, three days. Fee waived per standing arrangement. Consecrated to the Standing Debt. Anchor designation: Veil-Tithe, Seventh of the Cycle.*

*Note (clerk's hand): parents wept. Standard.*

*Note (different hand, older ink): Seventh holds longer than Sixth did. Recommend same household placement protocol. Subject must remain overlooked. Visibility is failure.*

Theo read it twice. His memory did what his memory always did—it took the page entire, every water stain, every smudge of the clerk's thumb, and set it in the permanent collection of his mind, where he could never, ever stop looking at it.

"Anchor designation," Isolde read over his shoulder. Her voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when her guild training took over and feelings were escorted from the room. "Theo. Do you know what an anchor is, in binding-work?"

"A fixed point," he said. His mouth felt full of cotton. "Something the working ties itself to so it doesn't drift."

"In great workings," she said, "a fixed point isn't enough. You need a living anchor. Something with a soul, because souls resist dissolution. You tie the working *through* them." She paused. "And if the working is vast—if it's meant to hold across generations—you don't pick a strong soul. Strong souls strain the tether. You pick an invisible one. A soul that the world has already agreed not to look at."

"Subject must remain overlooked," Theo repeated. The words tasted like the temple's Communion wine, which he had always secretly thought tasted like punishment. "Visibility is failure."

"Your whole life," Isolde said gently, "every time you've slipped through a crowd unnoticed, every time guards looked straight at you and saw nothing, every time you called it a talent—"

"It was the working." He sat down on the floor. It seemed important to be closer to the ground. "It wasn't me. I built my whole life on it. I got *good* at it. I made it my craft—being small, being missed, staying under—" He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I thought I was clever."

Pip crouched next to him. Up close, her starlit eyes were unbearable and beautiful, like looking at the night sky from underneath. "You are clever," she said firmly, with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old. "The bees didn't make you clever."

"Pip—"

"They needed someone who could remember everything," she said, tapping his chest, right over his heart. "So they found somebody who was *already* going to be that. My mama said the stars don't make people. They just find the ones who were always going to shine and stand next to them." She shrugged. "The dark does the same thing. It just stands next to you meaner."

Theo looked at her. Out of the mouths of babes, as his mother—his *Marrowside* mother, the one who chose him after, or maybe didn't choose, maybe was assigned—used to say.

"Turn the page," Isolde said quietly.

He turned the page.

* * *

There were six of them. That was the thing he kept circling back to, later, the detail his grief kept using as a handhold. Six children across sixty years, each consecrated in the same brief, tidy way, each noted with the same clerk's phrase—*parents wept; standard*—and each assigned to the same purpose.

*Veil-Tithe, First of the Cycle.* Died at nine. Fever. Replacement initiated.

*Second.* Died at eleven. Drowned; body recovered from a well the family swore had been dry. Replacement initiated.

*Third through Sixth*, various, none past fourteen. And beside each entry, in that older hand, the same clinical note:

*Anchor failed early. Tether redistributed. Recommend longer observation period prior to next consecration. Subject must reach majority for stable anchoring. Emotional attachments accelerate failure—assign to remote household. Limit affection.*

Limit affection. Theo stared at the words until they swam. Somewhere in Marrowdale, a wheelwright named Aldous and a weaver named Wren had handed over a three-day-old infant and been paid nothing—*fee waived per standing arrangement*—and then, presumably, had gone home and grieved, because the note said they wept, and then they had been instructed to stay away. To love him from a distance, or not at all, because love wore holes in tethers.

And the Marchettis of Marrowdale—the ones who raised him—had been kind in their distant, busy way, and he had spent his whole childhood wondering what he'd done wrong to be loved so lightly.

Nothing. He'd done nothing wrong. He'd been *designed* to be loved lightly.

"Oh," Theo said. It was a very small word for such an enormous falling. He said it again anyway. "Oh."

Isolde knelt across from him. For a woman who claimed sentiment was a design flaw, she had a remarkable way of putting herself exactly where a person needed her without touching them. "Say what you're thinking, scribe. All of it. I've found the unsaid parts are what rot."

"My parents sold me," he said.

"No," said Isolde, instantly and fiercely, and Theo blinked, because he'd never heard that tone from her except toward tax assessors. "Read it again. *Fee waived per standing arrangement.* They weren't paid. Which means they weren't selling. They were *complying*—with the temple, with a debt of their own or a threat or a doctrine, I don't know yet, but look at the note. *Parents wept. Standard.* The clerks wrote 'standard' because it happened every time, because every one of those families broke. Your parents didn't sell you, Theo. They were robbed, and the robbery was filed under 'consecration' so it would sound holy."

Theo's throat worked. "They still gave me away."

"Yes," Isolde said, because she was honest even when kindness would have been easier. "They did. Both things are true. They were robbed *and* they gave you away, and you get to be angry about both, and neither one of them is the truth of what you are." She tapped the ledger, hard. "This book tells you what they *made* you. It says nothing about what you made yourself. So let's audit it, shall we? Professional habit." She held up fingers. "One: the working made you unnoticed. But you turned being unnoticed into reading everything—which is how you caught the gold moving in the tithe rolls, which started all of this, which is why we're here instead of hiding in a ditch. Two: the working made you isolated. But the moment you had a choice, you broke your own rules to save a glowing child from soldiers, and now she won't leave. Three—" her voice roughened slightly, "—three: the working made you forgettable. And I have known you eight weeks, and I will tell you plainly that forgetting you is going to take work."

Silence, in the deep of the earth. Pip had climbed into Theo's lap at some point without asking, the way she did, and was holding his hand with both of hers, and her palms were warm. Warm as a hearth. Warm as that night around the fire when he'd said *I have nothing to mourn and that frightens me,* and Aurelia had looked at him like he was a puzzle worth solving, and Isolde had poured him the last of the whiskey.

"Seventh of the Cycle," Pip said suddenly. "That's you. You're number seven." She frowned at the ledger. "Six failed. Why didn't you fail?"

Theo looked at the page. *Subject must reach majority for stable anchoring. Emotional attachments accelerate failure.*

And he was twenty-seven years old. He had reached majority and beyond. By every rule in this book, he should have burned out at fourteen like the others—should have drowned in a dry well, died of a convenient fever, been replaced by an Eighth while the valley slept on.

Unless.

Unless somewhere along the way, against all instructions, against the *limit affection* note in his own file, someone had loved him properly. The Marchettis, in their awkward rationed way. Old Brother Callow with his knuckle-raps and his smuggled honeycakes. And lately—a hearth, a princess, a thief-turned-guardian, a child made of starlight.

His attachments should have destroyed the tether.

Instead, somehow, they seemed to have been holding *him* together.

"Isolde," he said slowly, "if emotional attachment accelerates anchor failure... and I'm still here..."

She was ahead of him; he could see it dawning in her face, the wardbreaker's mind seizing the implication with both hands. "Then either the Veil has been patiently waiting sixty years for its seventh anchor to mature—"

"—or the attachments are doing something the original binders never planned for," Theo finished. "Something they didn't put in the notes."

Pip squeezed his hand. Down the endless aisles of forbidden ledgers, in the humming dark, the seams of the world shivered—and far above them, faint but unmistakable, the temple bells began to ring out of turn.

Not the hour. Not any hour.

An alarm.

Isolde was on her feet, lamp snatched up, all business again. "Company. Someone knew we'd come." She hauled Theo up by the arm. "Scribe—did you get what you needed?"

Theo looked back once at the open ledger, at six short lives and one impossible survival, at the word *overlooked* written in a dead man's hand about a living man's whole existence.

"Yeah," he said. "For the first time in my life, I know exactly what I am." He pulled Pip close and turned toward the alarm. "Now let's go tell it that's not all I am."

Above them, the bells screamed on—and beneath the screaming, threading up through the stone, through the soles of his feet, into the permanent collection of his memory where he could never stop hearing it, something ancient and patient whispered his true name.

And this time, it wasn't whispering to call him home.

It was whispering to warn him that *it* had company coming too—and it knew exactly whose side Theo was now on.
