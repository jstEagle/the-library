## 14. Unbinding the Scribe

Isolde Vane had broken a great many wards in her life, and she had learned early that the work was less like picking locks and more like talking people down from ledges. A ward, properly made, was an argument. Someone had built it out of conviction—*nothing gets past me*—and her job was to find the flaw in that conviction and speak to it gently until it let go.

Theo's bond was not going to be gentle.

She knelt on the stone floor of the millhouse loft, chalk in hand, and studied the circle for the fourth time. Outside, rain worked at the roof tiles with the patience of something being paid by the hour. Pip sat cross-legged at the edge of the chalked ring, watching Isolde with those faintly luminous eyes that had stopped startling her about a week ago and now simply felt like home. Aurelia stood by the window with a sword across her knees, sharpening it out of what Isolde suspected was pure nerves.

"You're frowning at it," Pip said. "Frowning doesn't fix circles."

"Frowning is how I *check* them," Isolde said. "If I stop frowning, worry."

"Then you should be very worried."

"I am, thank you."

Theo came up the ladder with an armful of firewood and the particular careful quiet he carried everywhere, like a man perpetually apologizing for taking up space. He set the wood down, looked at the circle, looked at Isolde, and said nothing—which was itself a kind of eloquence. Theo could fill a silence the way other men filled a room.

"Sit down before you vibrate apart," Isolde told him.

He sat in the center of the ring, folding himself into it neatly, hands on his knees. "Before we begin," he said, "I'd like to register my objections."

"Noted."

"I registered them last night too."

"Loudly. It's on record." Isolde set down the chalk and picked up her knife—a wardbreaker's knife, edgeless, its blade inscribed with more argument than steel. "For the benefit of anyone who wasn't listening at midnight: this ritual doesn't cut the bond. Nothing cuts the Veil's work; believe me, people have tried, and what's left of them fits in a shoebox. What we're doing is *loosening* it. Like loosening a knot instead of cutting the rope. If it works, you get slack enough to breathe, to think your own thoughts without wondering which of them were planted. If it half-works—" she paused, "—we improvise."

"And if it fails completely?"

"Then we run, and I spend the rest of my life feeling terrible, which will be brief."

"That's very comforting."

"I do my best." She met his eyes. "Theo. You know I wouldn't do this if there were another way."

"There's always another way. That's the trouble with ways."

Aurelia crossed the room and put a hand briefly on his shoulder—the princess had a way of touching people as though granting them a small knighthood—and then retreated to the window again. Pip scooted forward until her toes touched the chalk line.

"Ready?" Isolde asked.

"No," said Theo.

"Good. Honest patients survive longer."

She began.

The ritual was old guild-work, older than her disgrace, older than the guild itself maybe—words in a language that predated the kingdom, that predated most things, that the Veil had presumably heard sung around whatever passed for campfires before the world had names. She spoke them slowly, and as she spoke, the chalk lines began to drink the light. Not shadow exactly. More like the circle became a hole shaped like a circle, and the room poured slightly into it.

Theo's breath went shallow. "It's warm," he murmured. "The bond. I can feel it now—it's usually so quiet I forget it's there, but—"

"Don't narrate," Isolde said. "Breathe."

She turned the blade flat against the air just above his heart, where the contract's anchor-point lived—not in his flesh, she'd realized long ago, but in his *name*, which was worse, because names don't bleed, they only fray. She pressed the words against it. The knot resisted, smooth and vast and patient, like trying to argue with the sea.

But every knot has a first turn. Every binding starts somewhere. And Isolde Vane had spent thirty years finding starting places.

She found this one beneath his left shoulder-blade, where some temple clerk with a steady hand had written him into servitude before he could walk. She wedged the ritual into that seam and pulled.

The sound Theo made was not loud. It would stay with Isolde longer than loud sounds do.

* * *

Afterward—she never could say precisely how long afterward—the loft settled, and Theo lay breathing hard on the floorboards with the chalk ring scorched black around him, half of it crumbling to ash.

"It half-worked," Isolde said hoarsely. "That's—I want to be clear—that's better than failing."

"Is it?" Theo whispered. His eyes were open, fixed on the rafters. "Because I can hear it, Isolde. The Veil. Right now. It's thinking."

The temperature of the room changed. Not colder, exactly. Just—*attended*. As though the dark between the rafters had leaned in.

"What's it saying?" Aurelia asked, hand on her sword hilt, though there was nothing yet to fight.

"Nothing. Everything. It doesn't think in words, mostly. It thinks in—" Theo frowned, groping. "Appetites. It thinks about the king the way I think about supper when I've missed two meals. Except it's been hungry since before hunger was invented." He shuddered. "And it knows I'm listening. Oh—it *likes* that. It likes that I'm—"

He went rigid.

Pip moved fast for a small person. She scrambled over the ruined chalk and took his face in both hands, and the starlight in her eyes brightened from ember to lamp.

"Hey," she said. "Hey. Scribe. Look at me."

His pupils had gone wrong—wide, drinking the lamplight the way the chalk had drunk it. But Pip's light got in somehow. Isolde watched it happen: the starlight caught in those blown-dark eyes and held there, a fixed point, and slowly Theo's breathing remembered its own rhythm.

"It talks to you," Pip said. "I know. It talked to glowing kids back home too, before the soldiers came. You have to have something louder in your head than it is." She tapped her own chest. "So borrow mine. Stars are louder."

"Pip," Theo managed, "that's—"

"Shut up and borrow it."

He shut up and borrowed it. Isolde watched his shoulders come down from around his ears, watched his hands unclench finger by finger. When he spoke again his voice was his own, tired and wry and unmistakably Theo's.

"All right," he breathed. "All right. It's still there. But it's—farther. Like hearing a river through a wall."

"The shield won't hold forever," Isolde said quietly. "Starlight's strong, but the Veil is patient. We use this while we have it. Theo—if you can hear it, can it hear *you*?"

He hesitated. Closed his eyes. And when he opened them, he looked like a man standing at the top of a staircase he'd rather not descend.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, it can."

* * *

They tested it that same night, because waiting felt worse than knowing, and because Aurelia argued—correctly, Isolde hated to admit—that intelligence gathered under controlled conditions beat intelligence gathered mid-battle.

They sat in a ring around the cold hearth. Pip kept one small hand on Theo's wrist, her light banked low, a nightlamp rather than a beacon. Isolde kept the wardknife across her knees. Aurelia kept watch at the window out of habit, though the nearest soldier was forty miles away.

"Start small," Isolde instructed. "Think of something harmless. Something it couldn't possibly use."

Theo thought. His face did something complicated.

"What?" Aurelia demanded.

"He's laughing," Pip reported. "Inside. He's thinking about the tithe rolls and how Brother Cassian used to lick his thumb to turn pages, and the Veil has no idea what to make of it."

"It genuinely doesn't understand ink," Theo said, and there was wonder in it. "It's older than ink. It ate whole civilizations and it cannot comprehend why anyone would press symbols onto dead trees to keep numbers straight. It keeps turning the thought over like—" he laughed, actually laughed, wet-eyed, "—like a dog with a walnut."

"Good," Isolde said. "Feed it walnuts. Now—can you find anything useful? Anything about its plans? The new moon?"

His expression smoothed out. Went distant. Pip's grip tightened on his wrist.

"It's... thinking about the capital," he said slowly. "About the throne. About the king—not the thing wearing him, the *king*, the real one, there's still a piece of him down inside the dark, curled up small, holding onto—" Theo's voice cracked. "Holding onto a name. A girl's name. It can't dig that memory out. It's tried for months. There's a splinter of him it can't swallow, and it hates it, and it's saving the whole consumption for the new moon because it needs the dark to finish the job."

"Aurelia," Pip said softly.

Aurelia didn't turn from the window, but her shoulders told Isolde everything. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way that cost her. "Then we have until the new moon. Eighteen days."

"There's more." Theo's brow furrowed. "It's worried. Genuinely worried, the way I've never felt it worry. About us. About *me*. It keeps circling back to the vaults beneath the Thornfell temple—to the place where my debt was forged. It doesn't want me going there. Which means—" he opened his eyes, "—that's where the answers are."

"It also means it's a trap," Isolde said.

"It's definitely a trap."

"We're going anyway," Aurelia said.

"We're going anyway," Theo agreed, and Isolde marveled at how quickly he'd stopped sounding surprised when he agreed to such things.

* * *

Later, well past midnight, Isolde found she couldn't sleep. She climbed to the loft with a candle and found Theo awake, sitting against the wall, watching the rain. Pip was asleep against his side, her glow dimmed to almost nothing, rising and falling with his breathing.

"You should wake her," Isolde said. "Carrying someone's mind all evening is heavy work, even for starlight."

"She won't let go if I try." He glanced down. "First time in my life anyone's held on to me on purpose."

Isolde lowered herself onto the floor beside him, joints complaining, and said nothing for a while, because she'd learned that Theo opened up in silences the way other people opened up in questions.

"You heard me tonight," he said eventually. "Through the link. Didn't you. During the ritual. When it hurt."

"Yes."

"What did you hear?"

Isolde considered lying. Considered kindness. Chose truth, because he'd had enough of both weaponized against him. "I heard a man who believed, honestly believed, that if he vanished tomorrow, the world's arithmetic would come out exactly the same. And that he'd made peace with it. And underneath that—" she paused, "—that he was wrong, and desperately hoped somebody would prove it."

Theo looked at the rain for a long time.

"When it speaks to me," he said quietly, "it offers me a self. A big one. Seen. Named. Important. And the horrifying thing, Isolde, is that part of me wants it—not because it's powerful, but because it's *witnessed*. Everyone in my life looked through me. My parents sold me and probably couldn't pick me from a crowd. The temple kept me forty years and half of them never learned my face." He shook his head. "And then three impossible people break into my hiding life and refuse to leave, and I don't know what to do with that except—"

"Except let us," Isolde finished.

"Except let you." He exhaled. "The Veil says nobody loves what they can see all of. It says visibility is a wound. And then I sit here with a sleeping child leaking starlight on my sleeve and a disgraced wardbreaker sharing my insomnia, and I think: it's lying. It's been lying since before names, and it's never once been loved itself, so how would it know?"

Isolde smiled and knocked her shoulder against his. "You know what I heard from you tonight, second thing? Underneath the despair. You thought, clear as church bells: *if I'm tied to this thing anyway, then let the tie be useful. Let it save the king.*" She shrugged. "That's not an anchor talking, Theo. That's a bridge."

"Isolde." He said her name carefully. "When we get to the vaults. Whatever we find there about me, about what I was made to be—you'll tell me the truth?"

"I'll tell you the truth."

"Even if it's terrible?"

"*Especially* if it's terrible. Terrible truths are the only ones I've ever trusted." She stood, joints protesting, and gathered her candle. At the ladder she paused. "Sleep, scribe. Tomorrow we plan an impossible march, and I need your walnut-thoughts fresh."

He laughed softly. Below them, the rain eased, and far off toward the capital, beneath a sky scrubbed bare of stars, something ancient and hungry seethed around the fact that its anchor had begun, impossibly, to hope.

It began, for the first time in an age, to hurry.
