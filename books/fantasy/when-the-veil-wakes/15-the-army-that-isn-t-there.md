## 15. The Army That Isn't There

Aurelia had learned to read her mother's letters the way other people learned to read weather. There was a tilt to the pen when her mother was amused, a crowding of ink when she was afraid, and a particular flourish on the word *ally* that meant *someone I do not entirely trust, but who owes me a favor large enough to be useful.*

Now Aurelia stood in the longhouse at Dunmarrow with eleven of those favors arranged around a fire, and she missed her mother so fiercely it was difficult to breathe.

The border clans had come because of those letters. Not because a princess asked—princesses were, in the clans' considered opinion, decorative—but because Maren of House Vael had once sat in this same longhouse for three days and three nights, drinking with their grandmothers, settling blood-debts with her own hands, and laughing at jokes that would have gotten any other courtier exiled. Her daughter had inherited the letters, the seal, and apparently enough of the laugh to be tolerated.

"You're telling me," said Old Brannoc, chief of the Ashvale clans, working a pipe that smelled like it had been lit during her grandfather's reign, "that the king's army is marching on Holdfast Ferren. And you're telling me it isn't the king's army."

"It wears no colors," Aurelia said. "No banners. No badges. The men don't speak. They march at night."

"They march at night," repeated Brannoc's granddaughter, Sefa, who had a scar across her jaw and the air of someone who found most conversations beneath her. "Soldiers hate night marches. Blistered feet, broken ankles, horses lame by morning. Only two kinds of soldiers march at night. Desperate ones, and dead ones."

"These," Aurelia said, "are neither."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, Pip was teaching clan children a clapping game that involved glowing faintly, which the children found *tremendous* and their mothers found deeply alarming until they got used to it.

"Then what are they?" Brannoc asked.

Aurelia looked at Theo.

He sat near the wall where he always sat, in the spot least likely to catch anyone's eye—a talent she'd watched him deploy for weeks now, folding himself into shadows and corners as though furniture had agreed to vouch for him. He looked up, caught her glance, and gave the small nod that meant: *go ahead.*

"There is something under the crown," Aurelia said. "Something old. It hollowed out my father and took his shape, and it is building an army out of people whose names have been struck from every record in the kingdom. Deserters. Debtors. Smugglers. The erased." She let that sit. She saw several faces around the fire go carefully still—the clans kept their own share of people the law preferred not to count. "It cannot use anyone who is *seen*. Anyone with a name someone loves. So it takes the ones nobody wrote down."

"That's why your scribe matters," Sefa said, jerking her chin at Theo.

Theo winced. "I'm mostly here for the handwriting," he said, and there was a ripple of reluctant laughter, because even frightened people liked a man who made fun of himself before they could.

"He hears them," Aurelia said quietly. "Through a bond he didn't choose and doesn't want. When the army moves, he knows."

*Mostly,* Theo had told her privately, the first time it happened. *It's like hearing a conversation through a stone wall. I get shapes. Directions sometimes. And lately—" lately it had started getting back. Lately, some nights, Theo woke with the taste of ash in his mouth and a certainty that something on the far side of the link was listening right back, patiently, the way a spider listens to a fly testing its web.*

She hadn't shared that part. Not yet. Hope was a weapon; you didn't hand your allies the cracked hilt.

"Show me," Brannoc said.

Theo sighed, sat forward, and closed his eyes. The room went quiet. His fingers twitched once against his knee, tracing something—a road, Aurelia guessed, or a column's length. Then he opened his eyes.

"The Ferren road," he said. "Three thousand, maybe more, moving in columns of five hundred. They'll reach the holdfast in four days. They're not hurrying." His voice went flat in a way she'd learned meant the words weren't quite his own cadence. "They don't need to hurry. Nothing runs from them. They know that."

"And how," asked a grey-bearded woman named Maera, "does a debt-scribe know what an army knows?"

"Because," Theo said, and here he smiled, small and tired and honest, "the thing that owns that army tried to own me first. It's still holding the other end of the rope. I've just gotten very good at pulling information through it instead of letting it pull me."

Silence. Then Sefa laughed—one short bark—and said, "I like him. He lies beautifully and tells the truth uglier."

"Aye," said Brannoc. He knocked out his pipe against the hearthstone, an old man's punctuation. "Maren Vael drank my grandmother under the table and then paid her debts for a year without telling her. If her daughter says the dark is coming up the Ferren road, then the dark is coming up the Ferren road." He looked around the longhouse. "Who stands?"

One by one, fists struck the table—not applause, but oath, the clan way. Eleven chiefs. Four hundred spears between them, maybe six hundred with the young ones who'd sneak along regardless of permission.

Six hundred, Aurelia thought, against three thousand things that did not bleed.

*Hope with teeth*, Isolde had called it once, watching Aurelia drill clan fighters in a frozen field. *Better than hope alone. Worse on the stomach.*

* * *

They had nine days to make six hundred people into something that could survive an army, and Aurelia spent all of them wishing her mother were alive, because Maren Vael would have known exactly which lie to tell.

Instead, Aurelia stood on the ridge above Holdfast Ferren with a map weighted by four stones and a strategy built on one honest truth: *you cannot fight three thousand. You can only embarrass them.*

"They don't scout," Theo said, crouched over the map with charcoal-stained fingers. He'd been drawing for an hour, reproducing from memory terrain features he'd seen once, years ago, on tithe rolls and grain-route surveys. Watching him work was like watching a man unfold a house from his pocket. "That's the flaw. They don't need to—they move on the Veil's knowledge, and the Veil thinks it already knows everything worth knowing." He glanced up, and there was something almost wicked in it. "So we teach it otherwise."

"Ambush at the narrows," Sefa said. "Where the road pinches between the scree and the river."

"They'll expect ambush at the narrows," Theo said. "Not from me—from *it*. It remembers every war this kingdom ever fought, and we always defend the narrows. So we don't. We give them the narrows empty. We let the vanguard walk through and feel clever." He tapped the map above the holdfast itself. "And while they're feeling clever, the real welcome is waiting at their campsite. Because here's the thing about an army that never sleeps—" he looked around the ring of faces, "—it never *rests*. It camps where it stops, but it doesn't fortify. Why would it? Nothing has ever attacked it at rest. It doesn't believe in being attacked at rest."

"Arrogance," Maera said.

"Habit," Theo corrected. "Worse. Habits can be broken. Beliefs have to be *shattered*."

Isolde, who had arrived two days earlier smelling of ozone and bad decisions, unrolled a bundle of copper wire and chalk that made the clan fighters edge away. "I can't ward six hundred people," she said flatly. "Don't ask. What I *can* do is key a lattice to the ground itself—here, at the campsite. When they bed down on it, the lattice wakes. It won't hurt them. It will do something worse." Her mouth twisted. "It will make them *noticeable*. For about ninety heartbeats, every one of those hollow soldiers will cast a shadow with edges. Visible. Named, if you like. And things built out of being unseen—" she snapped the wire taut, "—don't much care for being seen."

"Ninety heartbeats," Aurelia said. "Then what?"

"Then," said Pip, who had appeared at Aurelia's elbow with the soundless suddenness she'd perfected, "they get very angry." The girl's eyes glowed softly in the dusk, starlight behind glass. She pointed past the ridge, toward the dark line of the Ferren hills. "The big quiet one is already looking this way. It does that more lately." She paused. "It's mostly looking at Theo."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Theo studied the map very intently, as though it might contain instructions for becoming a different person.

"Then we hit them fast," Aurelia said finally, "and we hit them where it hurts, and we are gone before it finishes being angry." She swept her hand across the high ground east of the campsite. "Clans fight the way water fights—fall, gather, fall again. We don't hold ground. We hold *time*. Ninety heartbeats of chaos, arrows into the supply train, fire into the tents, and away. No glory. No last stands." She met each chief's eyes in turn. "Anyone who wants glory should stay home. Glory is how the Veil collects names."

Brannoc grinned around his pipe. "Maren would've said it prettier."

"Maren would've drunk you all under the table first."

"Aye," he said fondly. "She would. Get on with it, princess."

* * *

The night of the battle, the sky was clear and utterly black, and Aurelia hated it. Every night since the stars had gone out felt like standing inside a held breath.

She waited on the eastern slope with Sefa and two hundred Ashvale spears, close enough to hear the army come. It did not march like men. There was no jingle of gear, no low murmur, no coughing or cursing or singing—the ordinary noise of soldiers that Aurelia hadn't known she loved until she heard its absence. Just footsteps. Thousands of them, in perfect unison, soft as snow falling on snow.

"They don't even breathe loud," Sefa whispered beside her. In the dark, her grin showed teeth. "I've fought goblins with better manners."

Below, the vanguard reached the narrows and found them empty, exactly as promised. Aurelia watched the hollow columns flow through the pinch of land without slowing, without posting scouts, without hesitation—and understood Theo's word completely. *Belief.* They believed nothing could touch them, and belief moved through the world like oil moves through water.

They came up the valley and bedded down around Holdfast Ferren's walls in silent ranks, and Isolde, hidden in a streambed half a mile off, pressed her palms to the earth and spoke three words in a language that predated the kingdom.

The ground lit.

Not brightly—it wasn't fire, it was more like the world briefly remembering these soldiers existed. Shadows bloomed beneath them with hard, sharp edges, and in that same heartbeat, every hollow face turned upward at once, thousands of heads moving together, and the silence broke into a sound Aurelia would hear for the rest of her life: a vast, thin *wailing*, like wind through a keyhole the size of a cathedral.

"Now," Aurelia breathed, and blew the horn.

Two hundred spears came down the eastern slope, and the clans screamed—not war cries, exactly, but the specific insult-shout of border folk, informing the enemy of their parentage and hygiene. Arrows fell into the supply train. Fire arcs arced into the tents. And the hollow army, blind with rage at being *perceived*, surged toward the eastern slope—

—which emptied, because the clans fell back the way water falls, gathering on the western rise, loosing again, falling again. Theo's voice carried over the link to Aurelia through Pip, who relayed it breathless and gleeful: *"Left flank's turning! They're chasing the noise, they can't help it—tell Maera to stop taunting them personally, she's going to get—"*

Maera, in fact, did not stop taunting them personally. It worked disturbingly well. Hollow soldiers, it turned out, had no defenses against mockery, having never been mocked.

By the time the ninety heartbeats stretched to two hundred—Isolde holding the lattice open through gritted teeth and a bleeding nose—the army had broken formation for the first time in its existence. It milled. It chased shadows. It burned its own supply wagons in its fury to reach the spearmen who were no longer there.

And when dawn finally came—grey, starless, but coming all the same—the road below Holdfast Ferren was littered with collapsed uniforms and abandoned baggage, and the army that wasn't there had withdrawn north, in disorder, making sounds like grief.

The clans roared. Sefa lifted Aurelia off her feet. Somewhere downhill, Theo sat on a rock with both hands pressed flat over his ears, shaking.

Aurelia reached him first. She knelt, took his wrists gently, and pulled his hands away. His eyes were wet.

"What did it say?" she asked.

He laughed—a cracked, terrible little laugh. "It said, '*Clever, little anchor. Enjoy the victory. You've just taught me where to aim.*'" He looked up at her, and behind the fear in his face was something steadier. "But Aurelia—it *felt* that. Pip saw it. The lattice hurt it. Six hundred people with rocks and arrows hurt something older than the kingdom." He gripped her arm. "It can be beaten. It knows it can be beaten now, and it hates that more than anything."

Above them, the starless sky pressed down, patient and enormous. But for one morning, on one broken road, the dark had blinked first.

"We'll need more than six hundred next time," Aurelia said.

Theo nodded slowly. "Then we'd better go find an army everyone forgot exists." He paused, and his expression shifted into something she'd learned to dread. "Because it just told me where it's going next. Aurelia—the new moon is in twelve days. And it's taking my father's throne room by force of numbers, whether we're ready or not."

Aurelia rose, brushing ash from her knees, and turned to face her ragged, victorious, furious family of border clans.

"Then we'd better be ready," she said.
