## 16. What the Veil Offers

The link had been quiet for three days.

Theo had almost convinced himself it was over—that the half-ritual Isolde had performed in the vaults had frayed the connection, that the whisper at the edge of his skull was only his own exhaustion wearing a stranger's voice. He had almost managed to sleep through a whole night for the first time since Beneath the Temple Floor. He had even laughed twice, once at Pip's impression of Isolde muttering over her ward-chalk ("*no, no, NO, who drew this line, a drunk badger could—*"), and once at Aurelia's expression when she'd discovered that smuggler rations included something called "traveler's joy," which turned out to be dried fish and honey pressed into a cake that tasted like regret.

Almost. That was the operative word, and Theo had learned to distrust it. Almost safe. Almost invisible. Almost free.

On the third night, camped in the ruins of a shepherd's cot with rain needling the broken roof, the Veil spoke to him again—and this time it did not whisper.

*You are awake,* it said, warm as a hearth he'd never sat beside. *Good. I have been waiting to show you something.*

Theo sat up so fast he cracked his head on a low beam. Across the dead fire, Isolde stirred but didn't wake; she slept like a woman who had learned that sleep was a resource to be rationed. Pip, curled against Aurelia's side under one threadbare blanket, glowed faintly in her dreams—a soft blue pulse, like a lantern breathing.

*I'm not interested,* Theo thought back, deliberately, testing whether intention alone carried down the link.

It did. The presence chuckled. It was the worst sound he had ever heard, because it sounded exactly like his own laugh, the small private one he made when he found an error funny and no one was around to see him enjoy it.

*That is not what your heart says. Your heart says:* what if. *Everyone's heart says* what if. *I am simply the first thing honest enough to ask.*

"I'm not talking to you," Theo whispered aloud, because saying it out loud made it feel more like defiance and less like madness.

*You already are. You have been since the night you were born. But very well—we don't have to talk. Let me just show you.*

And the world folded.

---

He was standing in a kitchen.

Not any kitchen he knew, and every kitchen he had ever wanted. It was morning—the kind of thick, golden morning that Thornfell got perhaps four days a year—and the windows were open, and someone was frying eggs, and the smell of bread came from an oven that had clearly been tended by hands that loved it. Rain on the roof, but the good kind, the kind that meant the crops would come.

A woman stood at the stove. She had dark hair going grey at the temples and flour on her cheek, and she was humming.

Theo knew her.

He had never seen her face before—he had been three days old when they left him at the temple steps, and no portrait survived, and the temple records listed his mother only as *consigned, fee paid*—but he knew her the way you know a voice through a wall. Something older than memory reached up through him and said, quietly, unbearably: *Mama.*

"Theo," she said, without turning around. "You'll be late."

His name in her mouth. Not *the scribe.* Not *boy.* Not *you there.* His name, said the way people say names of people they are glad exist.

"Late for what?" he heard himself ask. His voice was steady. Inside, he was coming apart like wet paper.

"Your father needs those ledgers checked before market." She glanced over her shoulder then, and smiled, and the smile was aimed at him, at *him*, with nothing behind it but gladness. "And I need my son to eat something before he drowns himself in ink. Sit."

There was a place set for him at the table. Three places. A chipped blue cup that was, somehow, unmistakably *his* cup, the cup that had always been his, that everyone knew was his, that no one would ever use because everyone knew it belonged to Theo.

He sat. He ate the eggs. They were slightly overdone, the way eggs always are in dreams of home, and they were the best thing he had ever tasted.

*This is the life you were owed,* the Veil said, gently, from everywhere and nowhere, from the sunlight itself. *This is what was taken from you. Your parents did not abandon you, Theo. They were deceived, same as everyone. Sold a lie about a temple stipend and a better life for their boy. If the Veil had never touched this world, this kitchen would be real. This woman would be real. Every morning of your small gray invisible life that you spent copying other men's numbers—every one of them was stolen from this.*

"Mama," Theo said, and his voice broke on it, and she laughed and came around the table and wiped flour off her hands and hugged him, and she smelled like bread and rosemary, and he was crying into her shoulder, and she held him the way he had spent thirty years not knowing he'd been starving to be held.

*Do you want to know the cruelest part?* the Veil murmured. *She loved you. She searched for you. She died two winters ago still writing letters to a temple that never answered, asking after her son. I can show you the letters. I keep everything. I forget nothing.*

"You're lying," Theo said into her shoulder.

*I have never needed to lie. That is the difference between me and your god-kings. Ask me anything. Check my arithmetic. You're a scribe—you know how to check arithmetic.*

The kitchen flickered—

---

—and became a debt ledger.

But not his ledger. A different one, bound in green leather, water-stained, the handwriting round and patient. *To the Temple of Thornfell, again, regarding my son, Theo Marchetti, consigned 14th of Harvestmonth...* Page after page. Letters written every season for eleven years. *Has he grown? Does he like the cold? Please tell him we think of him.* And each letter returned unopened, stamped in red: *DEBT-BOND PRECLUDES CORRESPONDENCE. INQUIRY CLOSED.*

Theo had copied hundreds of such stamps in his career. He had never once wondered whose letters they closed.

"They told me no one came looking," he said aloud. The kitchen was gone now; he stood in a gray space that was not a place, holding the green ledger, and the weight of it was the weight of thirty years.

*They told you no one came looking because the truth was inconvenient to the bond. An anchored soul must believe itself unwanted, or it might reach for others, and reaching tugs at the anchor-chain. Your invisibility was not an accident, Theo. It was engineering. But engineering can be undone. I can give you back every year they stole. I can give you the kitchen. All I ask—*

"There it is," Theo said. "*All I ask.*"

*—is so small,* the Veil continued, smooth as poured cream. *The king. Only the king. A hollow old man who has already been eaten, mostly, whose remnant clings to one word like a drowning man to driftwood. You would not even be killing him. I would be finishing what was started on coronation night. And in exchange: your name restored. Your debts struck. Your parents' love, real and remembered and yours. A self that is finally, finally seen.*

The gray space bloomed again—

---

—and now he was old.

That was the first thing he understood: he was *old*, sitting in a garden chair with a blanket over his knees, and his hands were spotted and steady, and children were running through the grass calling "Grandda! Grandda, tell the one about the toll-lords!" and he was telling it, embellishing shamelessly, and they were laughing, and someone brought him tea without his asking because they knew how he took it, because they had known him his whole life, because everyone here knew him—

Pip was there. Older, tall, her eyes still star-flecked, laughing at his embellishments and correcting them. Isolde, white-haired and terrifying, arguing about ward theory with a grandchild on her hip. Aurelia, crowned, rolling her eyes at all of them with royal precision. A whole orchard of people who knew him. Who had *always* known him. Whose lives were braided with his so thoroughly that pulling one thread would unravel the lot.

*No one will remember you if you refuse,* the Veil said softly. *That is what unbinding means. You know this. Isolde knows it. She has read the ritual. To cut me loose is to cut yourself loose, and a nameless man leaves no more mark than spilled water. But accept, and this garden is yours. Accept, and you are the most remembered man in the world.*

The children ran past him and one of them—a girl with flour on her cheek, flour, where had that detail come from—grabbed his hand and said, "Grandda, you're crying."

"I know, little star," old Theo heard himself say. "I'm happy."

---

He came back to himself gasping, flat on his back on the cot-room floor, rain hammering the roof, his face soaked with tears that had fallen in the real world while he'd been away.

Three days. Had it been three days? The fire was ash. The light through the broken roof was gray and mean, dawn or dusk, impossible to tell.

Isolde crouched over him, gripping his shoulders hard enough to bruise. Her eyes were red-rimmed. "Second day," she said. "You've been under two days. We couldn't wake you. Pip's been pouring light into you since yesterday noon and it slides off like water off goose-grease." Her voice cracked on the last words, and Isolde Vane's voice cracking was a thing Theo had witnessed exactly once before, and both times had frightened him more than soldiers.

"He offered you something," she said. "What did he offer?"

Theo opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"A kitchen," he said, stupidly.

Isolde stared at him. Then, slowly, she sat back on her heels. "Ah," she said. "Ah, hells. It went for the mothers, did it? Mine got a forge. My father's forge, with my guild sigil back on the wall and my master alive to apologize." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old chalk. "It showed me ten years I didn't live. I nearly said yes. I want you to understand that, scribe—I, who built half the wards on this border, I sat in my own skull for six hours with my hand hovering over *yes*, and the only reason I let go is that my master, in the vision, apologized, and the real man never apologized for anything in his life. That's how I knew it was false. That was all I had." She gripped his wrist. "What did you have?"

Theo thought about the chipped blue cup. About *Grandda.* About flour on a child's cheek.

"Pip," he said hoarsely. "Get Pip."

She was already awake—of course she was; she'd probably been awake the whole time, glowing faintly in the corner like a worried candle. She padded over and took his hand in both of hers, and her palms were cool and full of that faint hum he'd felt once before, the night she'd shielded his mind.

"It showed you Mama-and-Papa stuff," she said. Not a question. Children who grew up in mining towns learned early what grief looked like, and they learned not to dance around it.

"Yes."

"And it was nice."

"It was—" Theo's throat closed. He tried again. "It was everything. It was the exact shape of everything I ever wanted and never let myself want, because wanting things gets you noticed, and getting noticed gets you hurt. It showed me being *loved*, Pip. Specifically. In detail. With correct seasoning on the eggs."

Pip nodded gravely, as though correct egg seasoning were a known hazard of infernal temptation.

"But here's the thing," she said, and climbed up onto the bench beside him so they were eye to eye, and her glow brightened until it filled the ruined room like a small stubborn dawn. "It had to *show* you. Right? It couldn't just tell you. Because telling doesn't work. It had to build you a whole fake world with a fake mama in it, because the true thing—the real thing—it hasn't got anything. It's got nothing, Theo. It's a big empty hunger wearing a crown, and all it can do is copy."

"That's true," Isolde murmured. "The Veil creates nothing. It only reflects. Every vision it shows you is assembled from fragments it stole out of your own head. Which means—" her eyes narrowed, working it through the way she worked through ward-lines, "—which means somewhere in that kitchen, in that garden, is material it pulled from *us*. From memories of you. It can't invent love, Theo. It can only remix what it has observed."

Theo frowned. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying," Pip said, with the patience of a seven-year-old explaining weather to a scholar, "that the grandkids in your dream had your laugh. I've heard your laugh. You did the snorty one when Isolde called the badger line. And the girl had flour on her face like Aurelia does after she bakes, which she thinks nobody notices. And the garden had *our* crooked fence, the one at the cold-hearth house." She squeezed his hand. "It stole us, Theo. It built your heaven out of *us*. Because we're the realest thing it ever saw you have."

Theo looked at her. Then at Isolde. Then at Aurelia, who had appeared in the doorway at some point—she moved quietly for a princess—with flour, actually, on her sleeve, and an expression of fierce, alarmed tenderness that she was trying to disguise as royal composure and failing at completely.

"So the question," Theo said slowly, feeling his way along the edge of it like a man walking a dark stair, "isn't whether the vision was beautiful. It was. It was the most beautiful lie ever told. The question is whether I trade a stolen picture of a family for the actual family, who are currently covered in ash and fish-honey crumbs and keeping watch over my unconscious body for two days."

"Don't phrase it like a contract," Isolde said. "You'll make it sound appealing to yourself. Scribes."

But she was smiling. Wryly, tiredly, but smiling.

Theo exhaled, long and shaking. And then, deliberately, down the link—not hiding, not flinching—he sent a single thought, clear as a bell:

*No.*

The silence that followed had texture. It had *weight*. Somewhere far beneath the world, something vast turned its attention fully upon him for the first time, and Theo Marchetti, debt-bound scribe, anchor of an ancient hunger, did not look away.

*You would choose the ash over the garden,* the Veil said, and for the first time there was something in its voice besides warmth. Something thinner. Something that might, in a creature less vast, have been called hurt.

"I'd choose the real over the reflection," Theo answered aloud. "Every time. That's the whole difference between us, I think. You keep everything and have nothing. I have almost nothing, and it's *mine*."

Another silence. Longer.

Then the warmth drained out of the link entirely, and what remained was cold and enormous and utterly without pretense, and Theo understood that he was hearing the Veil's true voice for the first time:

*Then you leave me no choice. The new moon comes in nine days. I will finish consuming the king, and I will take this world's nameless and forgotten—all of them, every soul like you, every invisible one—and I will make them mine, and you will watch, anchor, and remember that you were offered the garden and chose the rain.*

The link snapped shut like a door.

Theo sat in the gray dawn light, shaking, Pip's hand still in his, Isolde's grip still on his wrist, Aurelia already moving toward the map spread on the table with her jaw set in a way he was learning meant *someone was about to have a very bad week.*

"Nine days," Aurelia said. "To the capital. With whatever army we can scrape together." She looked up, and there was no despair in her face—only the grim, bright arithmetic of a woman counting what she had instead of what she lacked. "Then we ride tonight. Wake the clans. Send word to the deserters. Isolde—how fast can you build a lattice?"

"Faster than a drunk badger," Isolde said, already reaching for her chalk. "Barely."

Theo looked down at Pip. "Thank you," he said. "For the flour."

She grinned, starlight in her teeth. "Anytime, Grandda."

Outside, the last stars were fading into a sky that had begun, very faintly, to pretend at dawn—and somewhere south, beneath a throne that never blinked, something ancient stopped pretending to be patient.
