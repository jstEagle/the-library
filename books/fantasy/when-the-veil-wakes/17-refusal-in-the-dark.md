## 17. Refusal in the Dark

Pip knew something was wrong before anyone said a word.

She'd gotten good at knowing things lately. Since the stars went out, her eyes had learned to see seams—thin silver cracks in the world where the Veil pressed against reality like a thumb testing bread dough. Most days the seams were quiet. That morning, standing in the yard of the loyalist holdfast with a heel of bread in one hand and flour still ghosting her sleeves from yesterday's prank on Theo (he had taken it remarkably well, for a man who'd spent thirty years being invisible), she saw them all at once.

Every seam in the valley. Lit up.

"Pip?" Aurelia was crossing the yard, sword belt buckled over a borrowed coat that didn't quite fit her. The princess had a way of wearing other people's clothes like they were armor anyway. "You've gone white as your own joke."

"Theo," Pip said. Her voice came out small. She hated when it did that. "Where's Theo?"

"Isolde took him down to the cellar an hour ago. The half-severance. She wanted to finish what the ritual started while he could still—" Aurelia stopped. Pip watched her face do the thing it did when she was remembering that Pip could see things the rest of them couldn't. "What is it? What are you seeing?"

Pip pointed at the well.

The seam above the well—a crack no wider than a hair, the kind of crack you could walk past every day of your life without noticing—was open. Not wide. Just enough. And through it, faint as smoke, came a thread. A long, gray, hungry thread, running south, running down into the earth beneath their feet, running to the cellar where Isolde chalked circles and Theo sat with his sleeves rolled up and his eyes closed.

The Veil wasn't shouting anymore. It was whispering. And Pip was the only one who could hear which door the whisper went through.

"It's talking to him," she said. "Right now. Through the link."

Aurelia moved. Pip had seen the princess run drills, spar with clan fighters twice her size, duel a Veil-general's lieutenant at the ford two weeks past—and none of it looked like this. This was a daughter moving. "Isolde," Aurelia called across the yard, and her voice cracked like a whip. "ISOLDE!"

* * *

They went down the cellar stairs too fast, all three of them—Aurelia first, Pip wedged between her and Isolde's elbow, because nobody had thought to tell Pip to stay behind and Pip had never once in her life waited to be told.

Theo sat in the center of the chalked circle, legs crossed, hands loose on his knees. He looked peaceful. That was the horrible part. Theo never looked peaceful. Theo looked like a man perpetually braced for someone to notice he existed and demand back-rent. Right now his face was smooth as an unwritten page, and his lips were moving, just barely, shaping words too quiet for the room.

"He's not hearing me," Isolde said grimly. Chalk dust covered her to the wrists; the circle around him was layered three deep with script Pip couldn't read but could *see*, glowing faint gold where the wardbreaker's craft still held. "I cut the channel like I planned. Half-severed. He should have been able to hear it only if he listened hard." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, leaving a gray smear. "It's not waiting for him to listen anymore. It's *pulling*."

"What's it saying?" Aurelia crouched at the circle's edge, close enough that the chalk light caught the scar along her jaw—the one she'd gotten the night she fled her father's coronation, though she only told them that part around the fire, weeks ago, with her voice very steady and her hands very much not.

"I can't hear it," Isolde said. "That's the problem. The lattice is keyed to his blood, not mine. It's a conversation between the two of them."

"Not the two of them," Pip said.

They both looked at her.

Pip stepped forward, right up to the edge of the outermost ring, and squatted down on her heels the way she used to squat outside the mine office window in Coppervein, listening to men talk about wages she'd never be paid. Listening had always been her best skill. Nobody guards against a listening child.

Because here was the thing about the link, the thing Isolde hadn't known when she built the ritual, the thing that had surprised even the Veil: it ran through Pip too. Not strong. Not direct. But when the Veil spoke to Theo through the severed-half connection, its voice passed *near* her starlight on its way, the way water passes near a lit lamp. And starlight, as it happened, reflected.

She closed her eyes and leaned in.

* * *

The cellar fell away.

Pip stood in a kitchen.

Not this kitchen. An old one, small, low-ceilinged, with a hearth built into the wall so the stones held heat long after the fire died. She knew it instantly, the way you know a place from a story told at night. The cold hearth. Their hearth—the one from the night they'd all sat around a fire in the ruined summer palace and told each other what they'd lost. Isolde must have warded the memory into the ritual somehow, or maybe Theo had carried it with him into the dark, or maybe love just worked like that, leaving marks on a person the way boot-heels mark a threshold. Pip didn't care which. She was inside his head now, and his head had kept the room.

But the room was wrong.

The hearth was cold. The chairs were empty. The walls were gray and going grayer, and the whole scene was fading at the edges like a page left in the rain, and standing in the middle of it was the Veil.

It wore a shape it had clearly stolen from somewhere—tall, robed, faceless, the way people imagine a god when they're frightened of it. But its edges kept coming apart into threads, thousands of gray threads, reaching for the furniture, the walls, the memory itself, unmaking the grain of the wood, the soot-stain above the fireplace, the little burn mark where Isolde had set her tea too close to the coals and refused to admit it.

And across from it sat Theo. Real Theo, young and tired and ordinary, sitting in a chair that wasn't there, watching his own memory get eaten.

"You were never given anything," the Veil was saying, and its voice was the worst part—it sounded like everyone and no one, like a crowd heard through a wall, like the particular silence of a room where everyone has decided to ignore you. It sounded, Pip realized with a sick lurch, like the temple offices where Theo had worked for fifteen years. It had learned to speak in the accent of being overlooked. "Look at what they gave you. Debt. Dust. Fifteen years of copying other people's names while yours went unwritten. I offer you what they never could."

It gestured—or the suggestion of a gesture—and the gray kitchen peeled back like a curtain, revealing another life underneath.

Pip saw it too, because she was seeing through the Veil's offering now, and stars help her, it was beautiful.

A shop. A scribe's shop with Theo's name painted over the door—*Marchetti & Sons*—and ink-stained windows, and sunlight. His mother stood in the doorway laughing at something he'd said, and she had his same crooked smile, and there was no debt-bond on his wrist, no ledger, no contract. There was a wife who looked at him like he was worth looking at. Children who shouted *Da!* when he came home and meant it. Neighbors who nodded to him by name. A whole town that had never once failed to see him.

"All of it, yours," the Veil murmured. "The life you should have had, before they bound you, before they sold you, before they made you small. I can give it back. I can make them remember. I can make the whole world remember you, Theo Marchetti. You need only stop fighting me. You need only let go. Let me wear your hand for a moment, speak with your voice for a season, and everything you have ever wanted—"

"*No.*"

The word came out of Theo cracked and ragged, and Pip's heart broke a little, because she could hear how much it cost him. He was gripping the arms of the not-chair so hard his knuckles had gone bloodless. His eyes were wet.

"It's not real," he whispered. "None of it is real."

"Real is a matter of appetite," said the Veil gently. "You of all people know that. You have been fed on unreality since birth. At least mine is honest."

And Theo—Pip watched it happen—Theo wavered. His grip loosened. His chin came up, and his eyes went distant, and some deep part of him leaned toward the beautiful lie the way a freezing man leans toward a fire that will eat him.

Pip did the only thing she could think of.

She walked into the memory.

* * *

Moving inside someone else's mind felt like wading upstream through warm honey, but she was made of starlight now, more or less, and starlight does not ask permission of the dark. She planted herself directly between Theo and the Veil, spread her arms, and shouted with everything she had:

"THEO! IT'S ME! IT'S PIP AND YOU'RE DOING THE FACE!"

He blinked. "...What face?"

"THE GUILTY FACE! THE ONE YOU MADE WHEN YOU ATE THE LAST OF THE HONEY BREAD AT THE HOLDFAST AND BLAMED THE CAT!" She jabbed a finger at him. "THERE IS NO CAT HERE, GRANDDA. THERE IS NO CAT *ANYWHERE*. YOU ATE THAT BREAD AND YOU KNOW IT."

The gray kitchen shuddered. For a heartbeat, color flickered back into it—the brown of the beams, the orange ghost of old fires in the hearthstone.

"Pip?" Theo said, and his voice wobbled horribly. "How are you—"

"Doesn't matter. Listen to me. Look at the kitchen." She grabbed his sleeve—she could touch things here, sort of, if she believed hard enough, and Pip believed things harder than anyone alive. "This is your memory. You kept it. Why would you keep it if it was nothing?"

"It's just a night," Theo said. "Around a fire. We were telling each other what we lost. And I said—" His throat worked. "I said I couldn't name what I'd lost. Because I'd never had anything. And everyone went quiet, and I thought, *of course, even here, even with them, I'm the hole in the middle of the table—*"

"That's not what happened." Pip's voice shook. She didn't care. "I was there, Grandda. I was sitting right next to you, and I remember *exactly*, because I remember everything, that's my whole stupid gift, and here's what actually happened."

She turned to face the Veil, and she made her voice big, big as the mine shafts she'd grown up in, big as the sky with its missing stars.

"When Theo said he had nothing to name, Isolde swore. Actually swore, actual curse words, first time any of us heard it. And Aurelia reached over and put her hand flat on the table—flat, like she was taking an oath, because princesses take oaths with their hands, that's how they're built. And she said: *'Then we'll name it for you. You lost a childhood. You lost parents who should have burned the contract instead of signing it. You lost fifteen years of being unseen. And you'll lose none of those things again, because from tonight, you're seen. By us. Permanently. It's non-negotiable; I'm still technically royalty.'*"

The kitchen flared. Warm. Gold. The hearth lit itself with a sound like a struck match.

"And then," Pip went on, relentless, marching toward the Veil now, starlight pouring off her shoulders, "then Isolde poured us all tea she'd stolen from the palace cellars and said, 'To Marchetti. May his debts rot and his name grow teeth.' And then YOU laughed, Theo. You laughed like a person who had just found out he'd had a family for months and only just been told. And then I fell asleep on your shoulder because I hadn't slept somewhere safe in two years, and you didn't move your shoulder all night, not once, not even when it cramped, Isolde told me. So don't you DARE sit there and let a walking shadow tell you that nobody ever saw you."

The offered vision—the shop, the mother, the sunlight—flickered like a candle in wind.

"They saw you," Pip said, softer now, stepping right up to the faceless dark, small and enormous at once. "Not with your name. With your *tea*. With your shoulder. With your terrible jokes and the way you check on me three times every night pretending you're checking on the fire. The Veil can give you a whole world that knows your name. It can't give you one single soul who'd sleep on your shoulder. It doesn't know how. It's never been loved. It's only ever been *hungry*."

Silence.

Then the Veil spoke, and for the first time its voice was not gentle, was not many-voiced, was not patient. It was thin, and furious, and afraid.

"*Little star,*" it said. "*You should not be able to stand in here.*"

"Yeah," Pip said. "Everybody keeps saying that."

"*You are one spark. I am the dark between all stars. When I take your king, I will snuff you first, and I will enjoy—*"

"See, that," said a new voice, hoarse and steady, "is exactly the wrong thing to say to her."

Theo stood up. The not-chair dissolved behind him. He looked wrecked—eyes red, hands trembling—but he looked *himself*, and he put his hand on Pip's head, right between her glowing eyes, the way you'd rest a hand on a lantern.

"You offered me a family," he said to the Veil. "Here's mine. They found me covered in flour and called me Grandda. They named my losses when I couldn't. They're currently, I'd wager, screaming at each other somewhere above us about whose fault it is that I'm down here, and when I wake up, Aurelia is going to lecture me, and Isolde is going to pretend she wasn't worried, and Pip is going to demand honey bread as tribute. That's my inheritance. Keep yours."

He drew a breath.

"I refuse."

The word hit the gray like a hammer hits glass. The false shop shattered. The false mother, the false children, the false sunlight—all of it came apart into threads, and the threads shrieked, high and silent, a sound Pip felt in her teeth rather than heard. The Veil recoiled, its borrowed robes unraveling, its borrowed shape collapsing, and for one instant Pip saw it plain: vast, starving, *old*, older than names, and wounded—not in body, but in the place where its hunger met something it could not digest.

Love, apparently, was indigestible.

"*You will regret this,*" it hissed, retreating, the kitchen dissolving around them, the link snapping taut and then fraying. "*The king wears thin, scribe. Thinner than you know. I meant to savor him until the new moon. Now I will simply take him. All of him. Everything he is, gone before the dark comes—and your wards, your armies, your little family of forgotten things will watch the crown go hollow and know that you chose this. Sleep badly. I will not be slow anymore.*"

The cellar slammed back into being.

* * *

Theo gasped like a drowning man surfacing and pitched sideways out of the chalk circle. Aurelia caught him before he hit the floor—an armful of scribe and guilt and trembling—and Isolde dropped to her knees beside them both, scrubbing at the circle with her palm, breaking her own careful lattice without hesitation because the ritual was done, done, *done*.

"I heard the last of it," Isolde said quietly. "Through the lattice. Your refusal. It rang through the whole wardline like a bell." She looked up, and Pip saw, for the first time ever, that the disgraced wardbreaker's eyes were shining. "Foolish, brave, impossible man. It *felt* that."

"It talked about the king," Theo rasped. He clutched Aurelia's sleeve. "Your father. It said it would stop waiting. It said it would take him fully before the new moon—days, Aurelia. Days, not weeks. It's done savoring."

Aurelia had gone very still. Then, slowly, she helped Theo upright, and when she spoke, her voice was the queen's voice, the one she kept hidden under the borrowed coats and the bad jokes and the grief: level, clear, and absolutely certain.

"Then we march sooner."

Pip sat down hard on the cellar steps, suddenly exhausted down to her bones, starlight guttering low. Theo crawled over and sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and after a moment he said, without looking at her:

"Honey bread. As tribute. I heard that part."

"You ate the last piece and blamed a cat," Pip said. "There's a ledger of your crimes now, Grandda. It grows daily."

Above them, muffled through stone, the holdfast bells began to ring—not alarm, but assembly, the deep rolling call that meant riders were being saddled and maps were being unrolled and an army of the erased was about to become unstoppable. Somewhere far south, beneath a throne that never blinked, something ancient nursed its wound and abandoned patience forever.

Pip leaned her head against Theo's shoulder, the good one, and closed her eyes.

Three days to the capital. One king left to save.
