## 18. March of the Nameless

Aurelia had learned to count armies differently since she'd stopped being a princess in any way that mattered.

The royal musters of her childhood had been columns and banners, ten thousand men moving like a single glittering animal. What followed her now was nothing so tidy. It was a river of people the ledgers had never bothered to record: deserters who had walked away from garrisons that no longer paid them, smugglers who knew every goat track between here and the capital, clan fighters from the border hills who still spoke her mother's name with something like reverence. They came in ones and twos and small ragged dozens, and by the third day of marching they were not ones and twos anymore.

She rode at the head of it on a borrowed mule, because her horse had died crossing the Ashwater and because, as Isolde had put it with characteristic tact, "You look less like bait on a mule."

"A princess should ride something dignified," Pip had said solemnly from her perch behind Isolde's saddle.

"I'm not a princess," Aurelia said. "I'm dead, remember? Dead women ride mules."

"Dead *princesses*," Pip corrected, "ride very fast horses."

It was hard to argue with a child whose eyes held faint constellations. Aurelia had stopped trying around the time the girl had started calling her "Ash" — short for Princess of Ashes, which Pip considered a great honor and Aurelia had decided to consider one too, eventually, once she'd finished grieving everything the ash used to be.

Behind them, the road bent along the ridge, and Aurelia turned in the saddle to look at what she was building.

Perhaps four hundred souls, if you counted generously. Farmers with billhooks. Two wardens from Thornfell who had seen what marched under that blank banner and chosen, quietly, to walk away from their posts. A company of miners from the pits where they'd found Pip, still wearing their pit-lamps on their belts like little stubborn stars. Clan fighters in grey and green with their faces painted in the old spiral patterns that hadn't been lawful to wear for sixty years. They wore no uniform. That was rather the point. The law had erased every one of them — struck their names from rolls, burned their warrants, declared their marriages void and their children bastards and their gods illegal — and now all those erased people were walking toward the capital in broad daylight, and there was nothing on any ledger anywhere that could tell the Veil who was coming.

"They're singing," Theo said, reining up beside her. He always found her at dusk. She suspected he thought he did it invisibly, which was the most Theo thing about him. "Is that— is that the toll-lord's drinking song?"

"It's your fault," Aurelia said. "You told the gorge clans how we bought justice instead of vengeance. They've been telling the story at every campfire since. Now there's a verse where I threaten a man with his own ledger."

"That's not in the version I told."

"The version you told didn't rhyme. Everything rhymes when four hundred people are bored."

He smiled — that small, startled smile he still wore whenever anyone included him in anything, as though inclusion were a clerical error someone would soon correct. Aurelia had spent nine days watching him be astonished by kindness and had privately resolved that when this was over, whatever remained of the world, she was going to spend a great deal of effort teaching him to expect it.

If there was an afterward. If there was an *after*.

The new moon hung above the western hills like a closed door. Three nights away. Maybe two.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of an old waystation, and Isolde Vane laid out her tools on a wagon tailboard and began, for the fourth time, to build a lattice out of almost nothing.

Aurelia brought her tea, because watching Isolde work without tea nearby made everyone nervous. The disgraced wardbreaker sat cross-legged amid a scatter of copper wire, chalk stubs, bone needles, and three vials of things Aurelia chose not to ask about, muttering calculations under her breath in the particular tone of a woman arguing with mathematics itself.

"Tell me again why it has to be blood," Aurelia said, settling onto the wagon step.

"Because wards are oaths," Isolde said, not looking up. "An oath needs a speaker, a witness, and a price. Ink can fake the first two. Blood doesn't fake anything." She held up a strip of linen marked with Theo's signature in rust-brown — he'd signed the working that afternoon with a cut thumb, flinching only a little, which by Theo standards was heroism. "Every thread of the lattice will key to him. When we tear open the throne room, the wards will know whose hand is pulling."

"And the Veil will know too."

"Yes." Isolde tied off a knot with her teeth. "That's the other reason. We're not hiding from it anymore, Aurelia. We're ringing the dinner bell." She said it lightly, but her hands were steady in the way of hands that had decided steadiness was a professional obligation. "Sixty years ago my guild built the border wards. Beautiful work. I helped design the anchor system before I understood what the anchors were *for*. Then I read the full specification, asked one question in a meeting, and got exiled for heresy." She snorted. "My question was 'anchored to whom?' Nobody wanted the answer then either."

"You could have stayed quiet," Aurelia said. "Kept your guild. Your name."

"I could have kept being a tool." Isolde threaded copper through a bone ring, and for a moment the lamplight caught the fine white scarring on her fingers — the mark of a woman who had handled live wards for thirty years and paid for every one. "Tools don't get to choose what they cut. I've spent my whole exile learning to choose." She glanced up, and her crooked grin surfaced. "Besides. Look at us. A dead princess, a debt-scribe who's actually a cosmic linchpin, a glowing child, and the worst wardbreaker of her generation. You think any of us were ever going to have quiet respectable lives?"

"Speak for yourself. My life was extremely quiet until recently. Quiet and miserable."

"There it is. Perfect qualifications."

From across the campfire came a shriek of laughter — Pip, teaching a knot of clan fighters some clapping game that involved cheating outrageously. One of the fighters, a granite-faced woman named Bren who had lost two sons to the warden purges, was losing the game with tremendous dignity and laughing anyway. Nearby, Theo sat surrounded by scribes' children from the river towns, showing them how to fold paper birds, his hands moving with the unconscious grace of a man doing the one thing he'd never been allowed to believe mattered.

Aurelia watched him and felt the familiar tightness in her chest that had started somewhere around the cold hearth and never quite left.

"He still doesn't see it," she said quietly. "What he is to them."

"No," Isolde agreed. "And when this is done, assuming we survive, our first act as citizens of whatever comes next is going to be sitting that boy down and reading him his own inventory." She held up the blood-marked linen to the firelight, studying it. "There. Lattice holds. Four hundred threads keyed to one scribe." She paused, and her voice dropped its humor. "You understand what happens at the throne room door. He walks in unarmed. The lattice opens the way because his blood commands it — and because whatever's waiting wants him close. We're using both truths at once, and only one of them is ours."

Aurelia looked at the new moon. Closed door. Three nights.

"Then we make sure ours wins," she said.

* * *

On the second-to-last night, the army stopped being a rumor and became a fact.

It happened at a ford called Miller's Crossing, where a Veil patrol — forty soldiers under that blank grey banner, faces smooth as eggs — had dug in to hold the bridge against exactly the kind of gathering Aurelia was leading. Her scouts reported it at midday, and her war council consisted of herself, Isolde, Bren, and a smuggler captain named Osk who chewed cloves and had opinions about everything.

"We go around," Osk said. "There's a ford upstream. Takes half a day."

"We don't have half a day," Aurelia said. "We have a moon that's dying by inches. And if we go around, they report us, and the capital closes its gates, and we trade a battle for a siege we cannot win."

"So we hit forty entrenched soldiers with farmers."

"With people they can't see coming," Aurelia said, and then heard herself, and laughed despite everything. "No — listen. That's nearly true, and I'll tell you why. Every soldier in that patrol is bound to the Veil. Bound things perceive the world through their binding. And the binding—" she tapped the map, at the smudged ink where weeks ago Theo had copied out the original ritual from memory, "—was written to make certain kinds of people invisible. Scribes. Debtors. Smugglers. Everyone the temple rolls erased. Their own master wrote them blind to us."

Silence around the map. Then Bren said, slowly, "The thing on the throne built its army to hunt kings and wardens and lords."

"And it forgot the rest of us exist," Aurelia said. "It forgot us so thoroughly that its forgetting is armor. So we don't march down the bridge road in column. We come out of the ditches, the reed beds, the culverts — every hole the law said we belonged in. Let's show it its mistake."

Osk spat a clove stem into the fire and grinned like a knife. "I've waited thirty years to be somebody's tactical advantage."

They went in at dusk, when the light favored people who knew how to be unseen even when standing in plain sight. Aurelia watched it happen from the reed bank with her mother's old sword in her hand: farmers rising out of drainage ditches ten feet from the entrenchments; smugglers stepping from culverts the patrol's maps didn't contain because maps were made by lawful men; clan fighters flowing down from the high ground in spirals, silent, painted, patient. The Veil soldiers turned and turned, finding enemies already inside their lines, already behind them, already — as far as their bound perception could tell — nowhere and everywhere.

It was over in the length of a hymn. Not one of the four hundred died. Seven of the forty surrendered, and when the blankness left their eyes — when Isolde's counter-wards cut them loose from the binding — they wept and asked how long they'd been gone, and Aurelia sat with them a while and told them the truth: not their fault, not yet forgiven, but welcome to try earning it.

Afterward, Theo stood on the bridge looking at the abandoned grey banner crumpled in the mud. His face had the expression he got when the link with the Veil whispered to him — distant, listening, a little cold.

"It's angry," he said. "It's telling its general about us now. Correcting the... the blind spot." He looked at her. "We won't get that trick twice. It learns."

"Good," Aurelia said. "Let it learn our names."

"It doesn't want names. It eats them."

"Then let it choke on ours."

He blinked at her, and then — wonder of wonders — grinned, quick and real, and for one moment he looked less like a man carrying the end of the world and more like a scribe who'd just gotten away with something. Pip appeared between them, took each of their hands, and pronounced the bridge "conquered," which apparently entitled them all to jump up and down on it, so they did, an army of the erased stomping victory on old planks while the last sliver of moon thinned overhead.

* * *

The final night before the capital, they camped within sight of the city walls — a black mass against blacker sky, the palace towers crowned with that wrong, unwavering light that never flickered, because nothing up there slept or blinked or breathed.

Four hundred had become six hundred along the road. Deserters had joined in twos and fives. A whole tithe-village had walked out of its fields to follow them, led by a grandmother who informed Aurelia that she'd been striking unfair debts from her village roll for forty years and was frankly insulted it had taken a revolution to get her noticed. Word ran ahead of them down the smuggler roads Theo had grown up in: *the invisible are marching.* People came out of their houses at night just to watch them pass, holding candles in doorways, saying nothing, lighting the way.

Aurelia walked the picket lines before dawn and found Theo awake, as she'd known she would, sitting on the crest of the hill with the lattice-wrapped staff across his knees and the whole army spread below him like scattered stars.

"You should sleep," she said.

"So should you."

"I'm dead. Dead women are famously well-rested." She sat beside him. Below, watch fires burned in rings, and somewhere Pip was asleep in a pile of clan fighters who had adopted her completely and would fight dragons for her, probably tomorrow, if asked. "Theo. Tomorrow we go through the wall, and you walk down that throne room floor, and I need you to hear one thing before any of it. Whatever happens in there — whatever it offers you, whatever it says about what you were born to be — you come back up those steps. That's not strategy. That's not hope. That's an order from a dead princess with an army, and I'm given to understand my word carries weight these days."

He was quiet a long moment. The city glowered on the horizon.

"When I was small," he said finally, "I used to imagine that someday someone important would need me for something. Just once. Just enough that I'd matter in the record somewhere." He turned the staff in his hands, and the lattice wires caught the nearest firelight. "Now six hundred people are marching on a god because of me, and I keep thinking — I never learned how to be needed. I only learned how to be overlooked. What if I do it wrong?"

"Then we'll do it wrong together," Aurelia said. "That's rather the family specialty. Isolde does wardbreaking wrong, I do grief wrong, Pip does childhood wrong. We're spectacularly bad at everything except each other."

That earned the smile — the real one, the one that reached all the way up. "When did you get wise?"

"Somewhere between the mule and the bridge."

Below them, a horn sounded from the pickets — three notes, urgent but not afraid. A rider was coming up the dark road at a gallop, one of Osk's smuggler scouts, and even before the man drew rein and slid half-falling from his saddle, Aurelia saw the answer in his face.

"The gates," the scout panted. "The capital gates — they're *open*, Highness. Standing open. All of them. No guards on the walls. Nothing on the streets but candles." He swallowed. "Thousands of candles. Like it's expecting guests."

Theo's hand found the lattice staff. In the silence, faint as a breath, he said words none of them had taught him:

"*Come home, anchor.*"

Aurelia rose, and drew her mother's sword, and the dawn behind them found six hundred erased people already forming up — visible, at last, and done with hiding.

"Well," she said. "We wouldn't want to keep it waiting."
