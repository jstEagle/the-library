## 19. Storming the Starless Court

I had copied a thousand battle reports in my years at Thornfell temple, and every one of them lied.

They lied about the weather—no battle ever happened under clean skies. They lied about the numbers, always rounded to something dignified. And they lied most of all about how it felt, because not one scribe who wrote those reports had ever stood on a field where people were actually trying to kill each other. I know this now with certainty, because I am standing on such a field, and I can tell you: it is nothing like the reports. It is louder, and colder, and it smells like wet wool and terror, and there is a great deal more running than anyone admits.

The capital of Veymar does not look like a city under siege. It looks like a city that has forgotten what light is for.

Above the walls, where stars should be wheeling toward dawn, there is only that flat, hungry blackness—the Veil's own sky, pressing down like a lid. The new moon is two nights away. We came early because Isolde said we would never survive arriving on time.

"Left," she snaps at my elbow, her wardbreaker's staff crackling faint blue against the dark. "Theo, *left*, or I swear on my guild sigil I will haunt you personally."

"I'm going left." I am going left. The smuggler's gate beneath the tannery district—the one I'd mapped in my head eleven years ago while copying a customs ledger nobody thought a debt-bound boy would memorize—is exactly where I remembered it being, which is fortunate, because my memory is currently the only thing in this army that works reliably.

Our army. That word still catches in my throat like a fishbone. *Army.* Two hundred deserters who'd stopped believing in orders, forty clan fighters who followed Aurelia because her mother had once saved their gran's village, sixty smugglers Pip had charmed out of the river Warrens with a smile and a promise of "proper adventure," and Isolde, who insisted she was only here to retrieve her confiscated tools and would absolutely not be dying for any of us.

Two hundred-odd souls against whatever the Veil had grown in the dark.

"Gate's barred from inside," calls Bramble, one of the smuggler captains, a woman built like a door with a voice to match.

"It won't be," I say, before I can stop myself. Everyone looks at me. I feel my ears go warm. "There's a—there's an old toll-clerk's passage. Behind the third drying shed. The bar mechanism is on our side; they sealed the outside, not the inside, because no one who worked the tannery was ever important enough to matter."

Isolde grins at me, sharp as a drawn blade. "Invisible man knows the way invisible things work."

"That's the whole trick of it," I say, and push open the shed door.

* * *

The lattice goes up at moonset—or what would be moonset, if the moon were anything tonight but a rumor behind the Veil's black.

We're crouched in the aqueduct shadow below the palace hill, all four hundred of us packed into stone corridors that smell of centuries of rain, and Isolde stands at the center of a ring of chalked sigils with her sleeves rolled up and her hair escaping its braid like she's fought it and lost.

"Understand what this is," she says, and her voice carries strangely, bouncing off the wet stone until it seems to come from everywhere. "A ward lattice is a sentence. You write it once, in blood and intent, and reality has to read it aloud. What I've keyed into Theo's blood—" she glances at me, and something in her face softens by perhaps a degree, which from Isolde is practically weeping— "is a sentence with exactly one verb. *Open.*"

"Open what?" asks one of the clan fighters.

"The throne room doors," says Aurelia quietly. "The wards around them. The Veil's grip on the palace itself. Everything my father's kingdom built to keep people out—" her jaw tightens— "and everything the thing wearing him built to keep us in. Isolde is going to make all of it read itself as an invitation."

"And it'll cost her," I say. Because I did the arithmetic weeks ago, copying her draft sigils by firelight while she pretended not to watch me do it. A lattice this size, keyed through a living caster—

"It'll cost me *some*," Isolde says briskly. "Don't make that face, scribe. I've been exiled, bankrupted, hunted, and struck from three separate registries. Do you know what I have left to lose?" She holds up her hands, flexing fingers scarred with old burns. "These, possibly. And they've been nothing but trouble since I was nineteen."

Pip makes a small sound beside me—not quite a laugh, not quite a sob. Her eyes are bright enough now to cast shadows, twin points of starlight in the dark, and when she reaches over and takes Isolde's hand the old woman lets her, just for a moment, before shrugging her off with a gruff "concentrating, starling."

Aurelia draws her sword. It's her mother's—I know this because she told us at the cold hearth, the night she finally spoke of the coronation, of running barefoot down palace stairs while something that used to be her father turned its head too slowly to watch her go. The blade is plain steel, unornamented, the sword of a queen who'd cared more about using it than displaying it.

"When the lattice opens the throne room," Aurelia says, "the Veil will send everything it has at us. Its general first. I've seen it in dreams since I was small—it wears the shape of the captain of my father's guard, or it wore him once. It's fast. It doesn't tire. It doesn't stop." She looks around at the faces in the dark, and I watch her choose to say the next part honestly rather than bravely. "Some of us are going to die tonight. I won't pretend otherwise, and I won't order anyone forward. If you follow me, follow because you choose it. That's the only command I have left worth giving."

For a moment, silence, and rain, and the far-off sound of the Veil's nameless soldiers shifting along the walls above.

Then Bramble the door-built smuggler captain spits into the aqueduct channel and says, "Chose it the day the little glowing one bought me dinner. Forward, then."

And that, absurdly, becomes the war cry. Four hundred voices, ragged and quiet so the sentries don't hear: *forward, then.* Not a king's name. Not a god's. Just two words that mean *we decided.*

Pip squeezes my hand. "Walk careful, Theo."

"You too, starlight."

"I don't walk anywhere," she says, with the fierce grin that means someone is about to have a very bad night. "I *glow* places."

* * *

The lattice opens the world like a book falling apart.

I'm fifty paces behind Isolde when she drives her staff into the mud and speaks the sentence she bled onto the chalk, and I will spend the rest of whatever life I have trying to describe what happens next, and I will fail. The best I can manage: imagine every lock in the world deciding, all at once, that it had been holding the wrong thing shut.

The palace gates swing inward without being touched. The walls' guardian wards flare violet and then gutter out like candles pinched between fingers. Above us, along the battlements, the Veil's soldiers turn toward something none of us can see—and hesitate. Just a heartbeat. Just long enough.

"FORWARD, THEN!" Aurelia's voice cracks across the hill like a whip, and we go.

I run badly. I want that recorded somewhere honest: I run badly, arms pumping wrong, lungs burning, flinching at every clash of steel ahead. I am a scribe. My body has spent thirty-one years learning to sit. But I run anyway, because courage turns out to be less like a fire in your chest and more like putting one foot after another when every sensible part of you is screaming about furniture you could hide behind instead.

The courtyard becomes a killing floor. I see it in flashes, the way memory keeps things—Bramble's axe meeting a soldier whose armor has no crest and whose helmet has no face; clan fighters moving in pairs, covering each other with a wordless grace that must be generations old; Pip blazing overhead—actually *overhead*, carried aloft in a shimmer of borrowed starlight, her glow washing across the enemy like a tide, and where it touches them they falter, they *remember themselves*, some of them, and drop their weapons and stand weeping inside their hollow armor until their own comrades cut them down.

And everywhere the dark presses back. It knows we're here. Somewhere beneath the palace, the thing that ate a king has lifted what serves it for a head, and it is paying attention, and the weight of that attention falls on me like a hand.

*Little anchor,* whispers the link at the base of my skull, silk over broken glass. *You brought them to me. How generous. How *predictable*.*

"Not for you," I mutter, and keep walking.

Because that's the thing. While everyone else fights, I have one job, and it requires no weapon, no skill, nothing but the willingness to cross a courtyard full of death on my own two feet. Isolde's lattice tore the throne room open—but the tearing is temporary, a wound in the Veil's defenses that closes as fast as her strength drains. I have perhaps ten minutes. Maybe eight. And the throne room is straight ahead, up the great stair, past the dueling ground where Aurelia is about to meet the general.

*Aurelia,* I say through the link—the half-made connection that lets me hear her now, faintly, like a voice through a wall. *It's coming to meet you. Left side of the fountain court, it's fast on the—*

*I know,* comes her answer, calm as ledger ink. *I've been dreaming of its footwork since I was nine. Let it come.*

* * *

I see the duel begin, because fate is a cruel editor and refuses to let me look away.

The general steps out of the shadow of the colonnade wearing Captain Aldous Vairn's body—the man who taught Aurelia to ride, she told us once, the man whose funeral she wasn't allowed to attend. It moves wrong, that body. Too smooth. As though grief has been scooped out of it and replaced with efficiency.

Aurelia meets it in the open ground before the stairs, plain steel against whatever that blade of its is—a length of darkness that eats the torchlight around it.

"You wear my friend," she says, loud enough for the whole courtyard to hear. "So I'll tell you what I couldn't tell his grave: he taught me that a guard's duty isn't to win. It's to *stand*. And I have been standing since the night you took him. Every day since. Every step of every road." She raises her mother's sword. "You should have left me dead, general. Being believed dead was the freest I've ever been."

Then they blur together, and I stop watching, because watching costs time I do not have, and because I trust her—God help me, I trust a princess I met three weeks ago in a ruin with ash in her hair—and trusting her means letting her fight her fight while I walk mine.

Up the great stair. Alone.

The killing floor spreads out below me, and I cross it unarmed, hands empty at my sides, and every step is a decision made fresh. That's what no battle report ever captured, I think wildly, ducking as a stray bolt of ward-light shrieks past my ear—that courage isn't a thing you *have*. It's a toll you pay per step, forever, and you never finish paying it, and you keep walking anyway because behind you are people who paid it first. Isolde, spending her last strength to hold a wound open in the world. Pip, burning herself hollow to shield minds. Bramble, the clans, the deserters, the thieves—every one of them invisible to the law, every one of them choosing to be seen tonight, fatally, gloriously seen.

If they can pay that toll, I can pay mine.

The throne room doors hang open ahead of me—torn wide by a dead woman's stubborn sentence written in blood and chalk—and beyond them, the dark breathes.

I walk through.

* * *

The throne room is exactly as I remember it from the tithe rolls' floor plans, except that the floor plans failed to mention the ceiling has become a sky with no stars in it, a dome of pressed black that ripples like the skin of something swallowing. The banners are gone. The portraits are gone. Even the dust has been consumed. There is nothing left in this room but the throne, and the shape upon it, and me.

The king's body sits slumped, chin sunk to chest, and for one wild hopeful moment I think: asleep, merely asleep—

Then the head rises.

There are no eyes. Where eyes should be there are two apertures into the same starless sky that hangs overhead, and they find me across forty feet of empty air, and I feel them the way you feel a hook finding flesh.

*There you are,* says the Veil, in a voice made of every voice it has ever eaten. *My little anchor. Come home at last.*

"I'm not yours," I say. My voice sounds thin and human and absurd in all that dark. "I'm here for the man underneath you. The real king. Give him back."

Something that might be amusement moves through the room like a cold current. *Give? Little scribe, I have held this vessel for twenty years. I have worn his face and signed with his hand and slept in his sleep. He is a coat I have grown fond of.* The shape on the throne leans forward, and the darkness leans with it, filling the space between us. *But you—you I have waited for. Born bound. Raised unseen. Shaped like a key all these years and never once shown the lock. Do you know what you are, Theo Marchetti?*

"No," I lie, and take another step forward, because the remnant is down there somewhere—I can feel it through the link, a flicker like a candle behind thick glass, a man holding one true memory in two cupped hands while the flood rises. *Hold on,* I think at it, desperately. *Hold on. I'm coming.*

*You do not need the remnant,* the Veil murmurs, softer now, almost tender, and oh, that tenderness is the worst weapon it has. *You need only kneel. Take your place. Anchor and entity, joined as we were always meant to be, and no more walking unarmed through courtyards, no more debts, no more being overlooked. You will be the most *seen* creature in this world.*

My feet slow. I hate that they slow.

And then, through the link, small and fierce and glowing like a coal wrapped in paper, Pip's voice: *Cold hearth, Theo. Cold hearth. Remember who already saw you.*

I remember. Firelight. Four people telling their losses to each other like passing bread. *Theo—nothing he can name—which frightened him most.* But that wasn't true anymore, was it? I could name it now. What I'd lost, all those years, was this: a table, a fire, people who knew my footsteps and waited for them.

They're waiting now. Below me, on a killing floor, at terrible cost.

I start walking again—one step, another, courage paid per pace, forever—and the throne grows closer, and the dark grows louder, and somewhere beneath the thing that wears him, a drowned man clutches his daughter's name like a stone.

Twenty feet. Ten.

The Veil rises off the throne to meet me, unfolding like night given joints, and its shadow pours across the floor toward my feet, and I hear myself say—calmly, absurdly, like reading a ledger entry:

"I've come to discuss the terms of my bond."

The whole room goes still.

* * *

Behind me, far below, a plain steel sword rings against a blade of darkness, and holds.
