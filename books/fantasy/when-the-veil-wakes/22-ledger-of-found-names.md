## 22. Ledger of Found Names

The temple scribes of Thornfell had a word for what they did to the debt rolls in the weeks after the Veil fell: *amnesty*, which was a lovely way of saying that grown men and women sat at long tables with knives and scraped away the ink of other people's suffering, line by line, until the parchment came up blank and could be used again for something kinder.

Aurelia watched them do it from the doorway of the tithe hall on the first morning she felt like herself again.

She had not meant to stop. She had been walking—she walked everywhere now, having developed a deep and permanent suspicion of thrones—and the hall's doors stood open against the autumn cold because the fires inside were lit and the work was warm. Inside, forty scribes bent over their desks with little blades, and every few minutes one of them would pause, blow shavings from the page, and smile at nothing.

"Your Highness." The head scribe, a stooped woman named Maren with ink permanently staining her fingers to the second knuckle, rose and bowed too deeply. "We weren't told you'd come."

"Don't get up. I'm only watching."

"It unsettles some of them," Maren admitted. "Being watched while they do it. Like the debts are still shy."

"I can imagine." Aurelia stepped inside, drawn despite herself to the nearest desk, where a young man was scraping at an entry with careful strokes. She read the half-erased line upside down before she could stop herself—a habit of a lifetime of reading state documents she'd been trying to talk her father out of.

*Theo Marchetti, scribe, bound for services rendered, sum outstanding—*

She looked away.

"You knew him," Maren said quietly. It wasn't a question. Everyone in Thornfell knew by now; Aurelia had made sure the story was told properly, in temples and taverns and the great hall of the summer palace where her mother had once danced. The scribe who saw. The name he gave away so the rest of them could keep theirs.

"He copied my father's correspondence for six years," Aurelia said. "I never once spoke to him. He held a door for me once, I think. Or maybe he was just standing near it." Her throat tightened around something that was not quite grief and would never quite be anything else. "He was very good at being near things without being seen. That was the whole tragedy of him, really. And then he made himself seen in the loudest possible way, and now none of us can look away."

Maren followed her gaze down the rows of desks. "There's a strange thing about the amnesty rolls," the old woman said. "Every debt we strike, we find his name attached somewhere. Not as debtor—he paid everything, or rather, he paid the only coin the contract couldn't refuse. But he *copied* half these bonds. His hand is in the margins. Little corrections. Dates fixed. One poor widow's interest recalculated because someone had entered it wrong, and it turns out that someone was him, twenty years ago, and he couldn't fix it then because fixing things gets a scribe noticed." She shook her head slowly. "He was breaking the ledger from inside it the whole time. Small kindnesses in the margins. We're finding dozens."

Aurelia stood very still among the scratching knives.

Of course he had. Of course a man whose entire existence had been engineered to be overlooked had spent that invisibility the only way it could be spent—quietly, secretly, in the margins, where no one would ever thank him because no one would ever know.

"We should publish them," she said. "The marginal corrections. All of them. Let people see what he was doing all those years."

"He wouldn't have wanted—" Maren began.

"No," Aurelia agreed. "He wouldn't have. Which is exactly why we will." She smiled, and found that the smile didn't hurt, which was new. "He spent his whole life refusing to be seen by anyone who might love him for it. He doesn't get to keep refusing now. It's the least respectful thing we can do."

Maren laughed—a startled, bright sound—and around the hall, scribes looked up from their scraping, and more than one of them was smiling too.

* * *

The borders were strange now, in the way that open windows are strange after years behind bars.

No wards. Isolde had dismantled the lattice-work along the Thornfell march herself, stone by stone, muttering apologies to each one like a woman burying old friends. What replaced them was nothing at all—just the border stones, mossy and patient, and beyond them the clans of the high country coming down to trade without toll or fear for the first time in living memory.

Aurelia rode out to one of the crossing points on a grey morning and found a market already happening. Clan folk and valley folk, arguing cheerfully about wool prices over carts of apples, children running between the wheels with the specific lawless joy of children who had never known a checkpoint.

An old clan woman—Brenna of the Ashvales, whom Aurelia's mother had once called *the sister I chose badly*—flagged her down with a cup of hot cider.

"Your mother would have hated this," Brenna said, handing it over.

"My mother would have pretended to hate this," Aurelia corrected, "while drinking your cider and renegotiating every treaty in our favor."

"Aye." Brenna's eyes crinkled. "She'd have been proud of you, girl. Unwarded borders. Half the council fainted when you announced it."

"The other half resigned, which was tidier."

They drank in companionable silence. Below them, a clan child and a valley child were engaged in a solemn negotiation over an apple, conducted entirely in gestures, neither speaking the other's tongue.

"Do you miss them?" Brenna asked. "The wards. Some folk say they feel naked."

Aurelia considered it honestly. "I feel like I've been holding my breath since the coronation night I ran," she said. "And I finally let it out." She looked east, toward the capital, toward the palace where her father woke every morning weeping and every morning remembered a little more, and where the throne room stood empty now by decree—*let the room be empty*, she had written, *until we learn to fill rooms with better things than fear*. "The wards were never keeping the dark out, Brenna. They were teaching us the dark was all there was. My father signed them away because something wore him like a coat. But some part of me thinks some part of him was tired of walls too."

"And the scribe?" Brenna asked, blunt as all her kind. "The one who bought it all back. Folk leave offerings for him, you know. At the ridge shrine, where the stars went out. Candles and bread and little written prayers. They call him the Margins Saint." She snorted. "First saint in history canonized by smugglers."

Aurelia laughed, and the laugh caught on something halfway through, and she had to look out at the mountains for a moment until her eyes agreed to behave.

"He'd have hated the statue," she said. "Isolde commissioned one anyway. It's deliberately bad. She says it's so he'd have something to complain about."

"That woman grieves like a mule kicks."

"She grieves like someone who loved him," Aurelia said softly. "It comes out sideways sometimes."

* * *

Pip kept the ledger.

That had been the girl's own idea, announced at dinner one night with the ferocious gravity only a star-touched child of ten can muster, eyes glowing faintly gold in the lamplight even now, months after the stars returned—as if a little of the sky had decided to stay in her.

"If everyone writes down remembering him," Pip said, stabbing her spoon at the table for emphasis, "then he can't be gone. Names are how the Veil ate people. So memories must be the opposite. So we write them down and then he has to exist, because the book says so."

Isolde had opened her mouth—no doubt to explain, in her exiled-wardbreaker fashion, that memory was not metaphysically binding—caught Aurelia's eye, and closed it again.

"That's the soundest magic I've heard all year," Isolde said instead.

So the ledger existed. A fat, ugly, wonderful book with a cracked spine that Pip had liberated from a stationer's in the capital, its cover stamped with the arms of some minor house that had probably gone extinct centuries ago. *PROPERTY OF NOBODY,* Pip had lettered across the front in enormous careful capitals. *EVERYBODY MAY WRITE IN IT.*

Aurelia went to read it on quiet evenings, the way other people went to shrines.

The entries were everything and nothing. Isolde wrote in a fierce slanting hand: *He complained about my ward-lattice design for four straight hours once. Four hours. He was right about two of the points, which made it worse.* Then, beneath, smaller: *He stayed up with me the night before the assault on the city. Neither of us slept. Neither of us said why. I think he knew I needed company more than comfort.*

Her father—the real one, the waking one, moving through his convalescence like a man relearning his own house—had contributed a single trembling line in a hand that was still recovering from being worn hollow: *I do not remember him. I am told he saved me. I am told I clung to one true thing in the dark, and that it was Aurelia's name, and that he carried it out for me. I intend to spend the rest of my life deserving that.*

Aurelia's own entries were harder. She kept starting them and striking them out. What did one write about a man one had known barely a season and mourned like a brother? Finally she wrote: *He hummed when he thought no one was listening. Badly. Some smuggler's song about a river with no banks. Pip learned it. Now we all hum it, badly, on purpose.*

Because they did. That was the strangest grace note of the whole strange year: somewhere between the gorge and the vault and the starless dawn, the humming had become theirs. Aurelia had caught a kitchen boy doing it last week, a boy who had never met Theo Marchetti at all, and had decided not to correct him. Songs spread the way light does. Nobody owns them afterward. Everybody keeps them.

Tonight she climbed to Pip's little room under the eaves of the summer palace, where the girl had claimed territory with the ruthlessness of a general, and found her sitting cross-legged on the bed with the ledger open on her knees and a candle burning low.

"Reading or writing?" Aurelia asked, settling onto the window seat.

"Writing." Pip's tongue poked out the corner of her mouth in concentration. "But I got stuck." She turned the book around.

The page was nearly full—Pip filled pages faster than anyone, her entries tumbling over each other in enormous letters: *he let me hold the lantern in the vaults even though it was dangerous. he said my glow counted as a second opinion. he called the Veil 'a big boring bully' right to its face and it got SO mad—*

But the bottom of the page held a single unfinished line:

*I wish I remembered his voice better. I keep getting it wrong. I make it too—*

"Too what?" Aurelia asked gently.

"Loud," Pip said miserably. "In my head he's loud now. But he wasn't. He was quiet. Like the space between words." Her glowing eyes came up, wet at the edges but furious with determination. "That's the whole point of the book and I'm *losing* him anyway. Slowly. Every day it gets a little quieter and a little wronger."

Aurelia crossed the room and sat beside her, taking the pen.

"Then we do what scribes do," she said. "When a copy degrades, you go back to the oldest manuscript you have. You recopy from source." She thought for a moment, then wrote, in her clearest court hand:

*His voice was low and dry, like a door closing on a joke. When he was scared—which was often—he got politer. When he was brave—which was always—he got quieter still. On the last morning, walking into the killing floor unarmed, he turned around and said, "Tell Isolde the third anchor point was misaligned. She'll know. She'll be insufferable about it. Good." That was the whole speech. It was enough.*

Pip read it twice. Then she leaned her head against Aurelia's shoulder, and they sat in the candlelight, two survivors of a war most of the world was already telling wrong.

"He'd have hated the statue," Pip murmured.

"So Isolde tells me. Daily."

"Good." A yawn overtook her. "Can we read the whole book tonight? Start to finish? Isolde added a new one, I saw. It's got a drawing."

* * *

They read the whole book.

Word traveled, apparently, because by the time they reached the middle chapters, Isolde appeared in the doorway with two cups of tea and no apology, took the window seat, and listened with her arms crossed and her face carefully neutral in the way that meant she was feeling everything. By the end, Aurelia's father was there too, wrapped in a blanket, listening to his daughter read aloud the story of a debt-bound scribe and a disgraced wardbreaker and a star-touched child and a dead princess who refused to stay buried.

"—and then the stars came back," Aurelia read, from Pip's entry, "all at once, like someone relit a thousand candles, and Theo was gone, except not gone, because Pip could still see, just barely, the faintest seam of light where he'd been standing, and it looked like a person-shaped piece of sky."

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the household settled into sleep.

Isolde cleared her throat. "I have an entry," she said gruffly. "Haven't put it in yet. Been drafting it for three weeks."

"Read it," Pip demanded.

Isolde stared into the fire for a long moment. "It's one sentence," she warned. Then, quietly: *"Theo Marchetti, you were never invisible to us—we simply hadn't finished looking."*

Nobody spoke. Pip slid off the bed, took the ledger, and copied it out in her enormous letters beneath Isolde's dictation, and when she finished she blew on the ink, and turned the page to find fresh paper for tomorrow's remembering—

And stopped.

"Pip?" Aurelia's father asked. "What is it, little star?"

Pip didn't answer. She was staring at the bottom of the new page, where—faint, pale, as though the paper itself were dreaming it—the ghost of an inscription was surfacing, letter by letter, in an ink that was not ink and a hand that was familiar the way a song is familiar, the way the space between words is familiar.

One word.

*remembered.*

Pip's eyes flared gold, brighter than they'd been since the stars returned, and she looked up at her family with tears streaming down her face and a grin breaking through them like dawn.

"He's writing back," she whispered.
