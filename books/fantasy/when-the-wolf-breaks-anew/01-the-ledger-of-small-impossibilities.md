## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when August Underhill reached the bottom of the day's column and found an entry that did not balance.

He sat in the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things, a long stone chamber beneath the signless door on Candlemaker's Row, where the shelves went back farther than the building had any right to be deep. The air smelled of tallow and old vellum and the faint green tang of things kept past their season — bottled storms, pressed shadows, the preserved last word of a man who had died saying something no one else ever heard. August knew all of it by heart, or believed he did, which in his experience amounted to nearly the same thing.

The troublesome entry read, in his own careful hand: *One small impossibility, returned by anonymous donor. Condition: weeping. Shelf 44.*

He turned to Shelf 44 and found it empty except for dust and a small brass tag gone green with age. Somewhere in the guild there was a weeping impossibility unaccounted for, and August Underhill had been keeping these books for eleven years without losing so much as a button.

He dipped his pen. In the margin, in the small tidy script he used for corrections, he wrote: *Search premises at first light. Do not alarm the apprentices.* Then he sat back and looked at the words he'd written and felt the familiar ache settle into him like weather — the ache of being the only man in a city of two hundred thousand who still believed the books mattered.

Because that was the truth of the Guild of Impossible Things, the truth nobody said aloud anymore: nobody came. Oh, they came to *look*, sometimes, in the way people look at the stuffed birds in a naturalist's window, with polite pity. But no one came to trade. No one brought a memory of their grandmother's kitchen to swap for a thimbleful of borrowed luck. No one needed a jar of courage or a knot of untied grief or a name spoken backward to undo a curse. The world had stopped believing in oaths, and when the oaths went, the impossible things went inert on their shelves one by one, like lamps going out along a road at dusk.

August kept the ledgers anyway. That was the whole of his religion.

* * *

He worked until the candle was a puddle and then lit another from the stub, because the guild's rules were plain on this point: *the books are never left in darkness.* He had asked the Guildmaster once, years ago, why that rule existed, and the old man had looked at him for a long moment with eyes like coins left too long in water and said, "Because things grow strange in the dark, boy, and strangeness is our inventory." It had seemed profound at the time. Lately it just seemed true in the most literal and uncomfortable way.

At the third hour past midnight he heard footsteps on the stair, unhurried, and knew them before the door opened. Only one person in the guild walked as though the stairs were a formality she tolerated.

"Still awake," said Maren, the night steward, appearing in the doorway with her shawl over her arm and her gray braid over her shoulder. She was sixty if she was a day and moved like a woman who had made peace with every joint in her body individually. "I told the Guildmaster you'd be down here. He said, 'Of course he will,' in that voice of his."

"That voice means he approves of me," August said.

"That voice means he's stopped expecting anything else from you. There's a difference, and I wish you'd learn it before it costs you." She set a covered bowl on the corner of his desk — barley soup, from the smell, with the burnt edge of the pot in it, which meant she'd made it herself rather than let the cook do it wrong. "You've eaten nothing since noon."

"I ate at noon."

"A roll. Standing up. While reading." She pulled the second chair around and sat, which she never did unless she intended to stay, and looked at the ledger open before him. "How is the inventory?"

"Dying," August said, because lying to Maren was like lying to a wall; the wall didn't mind but you felt foolish. "Two more jars went flat this week. The luck from the miller's daughter — remember, the good luck, the kind that arrives as a coincidence rather than a catastrophe? Flat as pondwater. And the shadow we keep for the widow Fenwick went feral in its frame. I had to pin it back with iron wire. It bit me."

"Shadows bite?"

"This one does now. Everything's getting hungry, Maren. The things on the shelves, I mean. Like they know something we don't." He hadn't meant to say that last part aloud, and hearing it in his own voice made the hair rise on his forearms. He laughed it off, badly. "Or like I'm tired."

Maren studied him the way she studied soup — patiently, with an expectation of finding something wrong. "When did you last sleep a whole night?"

"Tuesday."

"It's Friday."

"Tuesday of next week," August amended, and she snorted, and for a moment the room was warm in the ordinary human way, and he was grateful for it the way a drowning man is grateful for a plank — completely, and while knowing it changes nothing about the sea.

She rose at last, took up her shawl, and paused at the door. "There's a trader come tonight. Down in the exchange room, waiting on you. Wouldn't see anyone else. Says he has something to sell and something to buy, and only the ledger-keeper will do."

"At this hour?"

"He says memories keep better at night. Fewer dreams to bump against." Maren's mouth twisted. "I don't hold with the memory trade, August. I never have. But he's paid the door-fee honest, and the rules say—"

"The rules say any soul may trade with the guild, at any hour, in any condition, save drunk or desperate beyond consent." August was already closing the ledger. "I know the rules. I wrote half of them out fair copy myself."

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "That's what worries me about you." And she went up the stairs, and the door swung shut behind her, and August sat alone a moment longer with the soup cooling and the candle guttering, feeling the sentence land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

* * *

The exchange room lay at the heart of the guild, a round chamber walled in shelving from floor to unseen ceiling, where the trades were made and witnessed and written down in triplicate. The trader waited by the central table, a narrow man in a traveler's oilskin with his hood back, revealing a face like a folded map — creased, brown, hard to read at a glance. A lantern burned low beside him. On the table between them sat two objects: a clay jar sealed with wax, and nothing else at all, which was how memories looked when they were properly presented. Nothing. That was the trick and the terror of them.

"Ledger-keeper," the man said. His voice was road-worn. "They told me you're the one who writes it all down."

"August Underhill. I keep the books." He took his seat across the table, laid out the trade-forms, uncapped his pen. "State your goods."

The man set his hand flat on the table between them, palm up, and closed his eyes, and August felt the air change the way it changes before a storm — a pressure, a hush, a taste of wet stone. When the trader opened his eyes again, the memory hung above his palm where anyone could see it: rain. Not a description of rain. Rain itself, seen from below, from the angle of someone lying on their back in a field, watching it fall out of a sky gone the color of pewter, feeling it strike their face, hearing it drum on — on what? A roof? A coffin lid? The memory flickered at its edges the way memories do, refusing to be entirely known by anyone but its owner.

August's breath caught. He hadn't stood in rain in — he searched himself and found, to his mild surprise, that he couldn't say. Years. He could not remember the last time he had stood in rain, and the absence had a smooth, polished feel to it, like a stone worn by water, which should have told him something and did not.

"Rain," the trader said simply. "A whole summer afternoon of it. I was fourteen. We were haying at my uncle's farm when the storm came up the valley, and we all ran for the barn, and I stopped halfway. Just stood there. Got beaten blue by the drops and loved every minute." His jaw tightened. "I want to sell it."

"Why?" August asked, though the forms didn't require a reason. The forms required a price, a witness, and a signature given freely. Reasons were the guild's luxury, in the old days. Now reasons were all August had left to collect.

"Because I'm going to war," the man said, "or something shaped like one. They're mustering men in the river towns, and they pay a signing bounty, and my family needs the money. And a man going to fight doesn't want to remember standing still in the rain doing nothing. It'll shame him. So I'll sell it now, while it's still worth something, and buy—" he touched the clay jar "—that. Borrowed courage. One jar. Enough to walk onto a muster field with my chin up."

August looked at the jar. It was guild stock, older than his tenure, sealed by hands dead before he was born. The wax held the faint warmth of what slept inside — courage, genuine and borrowed, the kind that lets a trembling man do the trembling thing anyway. It was one of perhaps nine jars left in the guild's keeping that still answered when you knocked.

"You understand the terms," August said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Memories sold to the guild cannot be repurchased at the same price. The guild charges interest on the self. If you come back in ten years wanting your rain, it will cost you more than you sold it for. Sometimes much more. Sometimes it costs things you haven't earned yet."

"I understand."

"And the courage is borrowed. It doesn't make you brave. It makes you able to be brave, once, in one direction, for one hour. Afterward you'll be empty for days. Some men find the emptiness worse than the fear was."

"I understand," the trader said again, gentler now, as if August were the one who needed steadying. Maybe he was. "Ledger-keeper — do you know what they're saying in the river towns? They're saying the border lights have gone strange. My cousin drives cattle near the Gray Marches. He says the ward-fires burn green some nights, and the stars above the towers flicker like candles in a draft. He says the king hasn't been seen in a year, and the proclamations come out in handwriting that's almost the king's but not quite." He shrugged. "Maybe it's nothing. Farmers say a lot of things. But a man can feel a storm coming in his knees, and mine have been singing for a month. So. Courage. And in exchange, rain."

August wrote it all down. Seller: unnamed by request, per statute seven. Goods tendered: one memory, sensory, non-critical, freely given. Goods received in trade: one jar, courage, borrowed, single use. He read the terms aloud in full, as the law required, and watched the man listen to every word with his eyes open, which was rarer than you would think, and signed, and pressed his thumb in the wax, and it was done.

The memory passed across the table like a cool weight settling into August's keeping, and he guided it — as he had been taught, as he had done a thousand times — into a clean glass vessel, stoppered it, labeled it in his neat hand: *Rain, summer, age fourteen. Donor: a man going to war. Handle gently.*

And then, because the room was private and the hour was late and no one would ever know, August lifted the stopper a fraction and let himself feel it. Just a breath of it. Rain on an upturned face, fourteen years old, the whole sky falling and nothing to do about it and nothing needing to be done. It went through him like cold water through cloth, and when it faded he found his eyes stinging and could not entirely blame the lamp smoke.

"Good trade?" the trader asked, shrugging his oilskin on.

"No," August said honestly. "It never is. That's rather the point of the guild, though I'm not permitted to say so."

The man laughed — a short, surprised bark — and clapped him on the shoulder at the door, and then he was gone up the stair and out into the city with his borrowed courage knocking softly against his ribs, and August stood alone among the shelves holding a jar of rain and feeling like a man who had buried something alive.

* * *

He climbed back to the counting room afterward, though the books were done, because there was one more habit left in his night and he was too tired to pretend he could skip it.

His sword hung on its pegs behind the ledger desk, where weapons were technically forbidden and August's had hung unmolested for eleven years, because the Guildmaster understood that some inheritances are not negotiable. It was an old blade, plain-hilted, its scabbard leather cracked like dry mud, its maker's mark worn to a ghost. It had belonged to the last swordspeaker before him, and to one before that, back down a line that thinned into legend. Once, the oath-bound swordspeakers had been the realm's guarantee — blades that spoke, literally spoke, oaths made audible and binding, steel that testified. There had been hundreds. Then dozens. Then, for thirty years and more, one: a man who kept accounts in a cellar and talked to a sword that never answered.

"Evening," August said to it, taking it down, drawing it a hand's width to check the oil on the blade, as he did every night. The steel caught the candlelight and threw it back unchanged. "Quiet shift. Traded a man his rain for a jar of nerve. Filed a weeping impossibility as missing. Maren thinks I work too much, and she's right, and I'll ignore her tomorrow same as always."

The sword said nothing. It had never said anything, not once, not in all the years he had carried it and cleaned it and slept with it near his head. The old texts said a sword spoke to its speaker when the speaker's oath was true and the moment was needful. August had long ago concluded that either the texts were wrong, or the oaths were dead, or — the thought he kept in the deepest drawer of himself, the one he opened maybe twice a year and always regretted — he was not what he thought he was. That his line had ended and the sword knew it, and stayed silent out of courtesy, the way you don't correct a grieving man who speaks to an empty chair.

"Habit," he told the blade, sliding it home. "That's all this is. Habit, not need. You'd speak if you could, wouldn't you. If there were anything in you to speak with."

The lamplight ran along the steel and settled, and for just a moment — a heartbeat, no more — the light seemed to hesitate on the blade, the way a bird hesitates on a branch before deciding to fly. August stared at it. The moment passed. Steel was steel, and he was a tired man past the third hour of the morning, seeing what he wished to see.

He hung the sword back on its pegs, and that was when he noticed it: through the counting room's high, slit window, the sky over the northern hills — the direction of the Gray Marches, the direction of the border — had lost its stars. Not clouded. Clouds move, breathe, thin. This was a patch of perfect black sitting among the constellations like a hole cut in cloth, and even as he watched, it widened, very slightly, the way ink widens in water.

August Underhill stood with his hand still resting on the sword's pommel and felt the hair rise along his arms, and heard, far off beneath the edge of hearing, a sound like the first note of something vast beginning to break.

Somewhere below him, on Shelf 44, something began to weep.
