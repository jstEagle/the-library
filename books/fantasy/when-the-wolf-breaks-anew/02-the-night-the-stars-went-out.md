## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The sound came again before August reached the bottom of the stairs.

He had heard it first as a child hears thunder — distant, unformed, belonging to some other country of the sky. Now, descending past Shelf 44 with his lamp held high and his hand still resting on the sword's pommel out of habit rather than courage, he understood that it was not distant at all. It was beneath him. It was in the walls. It was in the mortar of the guildhouse itself, which was older than any kingdom currently claiming to own the ground it stood on, and which had been holding its breath for three hundred years without anyone thinking to ask why.

On Shelf 44, the weeping thing had stopped.

That was worse. August had catalogued the weeping thing four years ago, when it arrived in a crate of salt from the Fenmarch coast: a jar of grief that belonged to no one, sealed with wax stamped by a guild long dead. It wept on schedule — every seventh night, between the hours of second and third bell, a thin and terrible sound like a child crying through water. The guild's ledger recorded its schedule the way a farmer records rain. *Entry 4,411: jar of borrowed sorrow, fen-salt preserved, weeps nightly on the sabbath. Do not open. Do not comfort.* He had written that last instruction himself, after the night young Pell the apprentice had opened it out of pity and spent a week unable to stop mourning a mother he had never had.

Now it was silent, and silence from an impossibility was not rest. Silence from an impossibility was a held breath.

August set his lamp on the audit table and looked down the length of the vaulted cellar where the guild kept what could not be kept anywhere else. Shelves rose into shadow on either side, forty-four rows of them, each one crowded with jars and boxes and cages and things wrapped in oilcloth that shifted when you weren't looking directly at them. A bottled storm that grumbled in its glass. A door that opened onto a room that did not exist, propped shut with a chair. Seven eggs of something the founding guildmaster had declined to name, kept warm under a copper hood because letting them go cold was apparently the only mercy worse than letting them hatch.

And everywhere, threaded through all of it like veins through marble, the wards — old oaths spoken into the stone by swordspeakers who had died believing their words would hold forever.

The sound came again. Closer now. And this time August named it for what it was, because naming was the first discipline of his trade: glass. Somewhere impossibly far away and impossibly close, something vast was breaking like a dropped goblet, and the breaking had a rhythm, and the rhythm was slowing, and between each shatter there was a silence longer than the last.

*The border wards,* he thought, and did not let himself finish the sentence.

He climbed back up instead of climbing down. There were procedures. The Guildmaster would want to be woken; the Guildmaster was always awake these days anyway, sitting in her tower room with the ledgers of the last century spread around her like fallen leaves, though she claimed she slept like the dead and August had never once caught evidence of it. There were forms to begin. There was a contingency ledger — Ledger Nine, bound in grey calf, chained to the desk in the counting-room — that existed solely for nights like this one, and which no one had ever been permitted to open, and whose chain August had dusted around every week for eleven years without once letting his fingers linger.

He was halfway up the stairs when the stars went out.

It was not gradual. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in all the years that followed, when people asked him — and they asked him for the rest of his life — what it had been like. They expected darkness creeping, dusk arriving early, some slow tide going out. But it was nothing like that. One moment the arrow-slits along the stairwell showed him the ordinary scattered constellations of late autumn, the Shepherds and the Broken Crown and the pale scatter of the Wolf's Eye; and the next moment there was nothing there at all. Not cloud. Not fog. Not even the honest black of a moonless night, which is never truly black but always full of the faint suggestion of light somewhere behind it. This was a darkness with weight. A darkness that pressed against the slits like water against a hull.

The sound of breaking glass crossed the whole sky then — not far away anymore, not a note beneath hearing, but a single vast concussion that passed through the guildhouse and through August's body and rang in his teeth like he had bitten a bell. Dust sifted from the ceiling. On the shelves below him, four hundred impossible things made a sound he had never heard them make: they all moved at once.

He stood very still on the stairs with his lamp shaking in his hand and listened to the inventory of the Guild of Impossible Things wake up.

The bottled storm began to throw itself against its glass, hard, rhythmic, furious — not grumbling now but *attacking*. The door on Shelf 12 swung open on its nonexistent room and something inside breathed out, a long slow exhalation that smelled of snow and iron and made the lamp flame lean away as if flinching. The seven eggs under their copper hood began, very softly, to tick. And the weeping thing on Shelf 44 — silent for an hour, silent as the grave — began to laugh.

August took the stairs two at a time.

* * *

The counting-room was lit and full when he burst into it, which meant everyone else had felt it too. Pell stood white-faced by the window with his hands pressed flat against the sill as if steadying himself against the movement of the world. Old Maren the registrar had her arms wrapped around the great index book as though it might bolt. And at the head of the long scarred table sat the Guildmaster, Veyra Coll, who was not an old woman so much as an old building — grey and load-bearing and impossible to imagine collapsed — and she was writing.

She was writing while the sky broke. That was the detail that lodged in August and stayed. Her pen moved with the same unhurried precision it carried on market days, laying down figures in the margin of Ledger Nine, the grey-calf book with its broken chain lying open beside her like a dead snake, and she did not look up until August's boots stopped moving.

"You heard it," she said. Not a question.

"The wards." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Guildmaster, the border wards — all of them? The northern line, the—"

"All of them. Every ward on every border of the realm, gone within the space of nine heartbeats." She said it the way she announced quarterly losses, flat and exact, and somehow that was more frightening than shouting would have been. "I have been counting. Nine heartbeats. Which means they did not fail. Wards fail one at a time, fraying at the edges like rope. These shattered *together*, root and branch, which means whatever anchored them—" and here her pen stopped, just barely, a hesitation shorter than a breath, "—whatever anchored them has been pulled out from underneath us."

"Anchored?" Pell said from the window. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Guildmaster, I was taught the wards were anchored in the mountain-stone. In the old king-stones along the marches. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows wrong," Veyra said. "Everyone has known wrong for thirty years, and the knowing-wrong has been very carefully maintained, which should tell you something about how much trouble we are in." She closed Ledger Nine and put both her hands flat upon its cover, and only then did August see that her knuckles were white. "The wards were oath-work. You know what oath-work anchors to."

August knew. Every keeper of the guild knew, the way you know a thing you've been told so often it stops meaning anything. Oath-work anchored to living memory — to the sworn word held fast in a mind that remembered why the word was given. It was the whole reason swordspeaking had died out, the whole reason the guild traded in smaller currencies now: memories, hours, names, the taste of a summer, the face of a mother. Small coin for small magics. Nobody spoke the great oaths anymore because nobody could afford them.

But nobody had spoken the border oaths in thirty years either. And they had held until tonight.

"Whose memory?" he asked, and heard his own voice go quiet, the way voices go quiet in a sickroom. "Guildmaster. Whose memory were the borders standing on?"

Veyra Coll looked at him for a long moment. Lamplight moved across her face, and August — who had served her for eleven years, who had audited her entries and fetched her ink and learned to read her silences the way sailors read weather — saw something in her expression he had never seen there before. Not fear exactly. Something older than fear. The look of a woman doing arithmetic she had done many times before and always hoped would come out differently.

"That," she said, "is not in any ledger I am free to show you."

*Free.* He marked the word. Eleven years in the guild taught you that the Guildmaster chose her words like a moneylender choosing coins, and *free* was not the word of a woman with no answer. It was the word of a woman with a debt.

Before he could press it, the window went dark.

Not the ordinary dark of the starless sky outside — Pell was standing right there, and the reflection in the glass showed the lamplit room behind him plain as noon — but something crossing *past* the glass. A shape. Winged, vast, silent, blotting out the streetlamps below for the length of one heartbeat, two, and then gone northward toward the palace hill with a sound like wet silk tearing. Maren made a small noise and gripped the index book harder. Pell stepped back from the window as though the glass might bite.

"What," he whispered, "was that."

"That," said the Guildmaster, rising at last, "was a messenger. And we are going to ignore it, because we are going to be busy." She turned to August, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, formal, the voice she used for oaths and dismissals, the voice under which eleven years of his life had been arranged like goods on a shelf. "Underhill. Fetch your coat. Fetch your sword. Fetch the satchel with the guild seal."

"Guildmaster—"

"You are going south." She was already moving toward the tower stair, and he followed because his legs had decided before his mind did, because eleven years of duty is a kind of gravity and he had never once escaped its pull. "Tonight. Before the roads fill with whatever comes off those borders now that there's nothing holding the wild things back, and before the crown's riders decide that guild-keepers know too much — which, as of tonight, they do."

"South to where? What am I carrying?"

She stopped on the landing. Below them, through the floors, they could both hear the guildhouse shifting — shelves settling, cages rattling, the bottled storm hammering its glass in a rhythm almost like speech. Somewhere on Shelf 44 the laughter had stopped, and the silence it left behind was the loudest thing in the building.

Veyra Coll drew a page from inside her coat. A single leaf, folded thrice and sealed with black wax — not the guild's green seal, which August had known since childhood, but another mark entirely, a circle broken by a sword, and looking at it made the hair rise on his arms the way the sword's presence did, the way the first note of breaking had.

"When the stars return," she said, "you may read this. If they do not return, burn it unread and consider your life well spent not knowing." She pressed it into his hand, and her fingers were cold as ledger-iron. "You will go south along the Ember Road to the shrine of the Emberwrights. You remember the Emberwrights."

"Fire-keepers. Dying order. Last entry in their account is twenty years old."

"They have one entry left to make." Her eyes held his, and for a moment — one strange, unguarded moment — August saw the arithmetic behind them finish, and hated the sum. "There is a fire in that shrine older than the guild, older than the crown, older than the lies we told to keep both of them standing. If anything in this world can undo what broke tonight, it is that fire, and it is dying, and you are going to carry the reason to save it or watch it gutter out. Those are your instructions. The rest—" she touched the folded page against his chest, over his heart, "—the rest is in the wax."

"Guildmaster." His throat was tight. Eleven years of ledgers and audits and quiet lamplit nights, and this was what all of it had been leading to, and he found he could not even be angry, only afraid in a way that felt almost like grief. "What broke tonight? Tell me that much. Whose memory were we standing on?"

For the first time in the eleven years August Underhill had served her, Veyra Coll lied to his face. He watched her do it. He watched her mouth form the words and her eyes say otherwise, and both of them knew he saw, and neither of them said.

"No one's," she said. "Go south, Keeper. Quickly."

* * *

He was in the courtyard saddling the grey mare when the first feral thing hit the guild's outer gate.

He heard it rather than saw it — a sound like a cart crashing, then a shriek that bent wrong in the middle, the cry of something that used to be held in a shape and wasn't anymore. Pell shouted from the wall. Lanterns bloomed along the battlements. And August stood with the mare's girth-straps half-buckled and his hand, again, of its own accord, on the pommel of his sword.

*Speak,* he thought at it, absurdly, the way he had thought at it a thousand idle times across eleven years. *Say something. Tell me what's coming.*

The sword stayed silent, as it had always been silent, as it had been silent since the day a dying stranger had pressed it on him with the words *keep it till it wakes* — and yet beneath his palm, for the first time in all those years, he felt it. Warmth. Faint as a coin left in sun, brief as a held breath, gone almost before he noticed it. As if something inside the blade had turned over in its sleep.

Then the gate-shriek came again, closer, and there was no time for wonders. He swung up into the saddle. At the gate, Pell hauled the bar aside with shaking hands, and beyond the iron the night lay open and utterly, perfectly black — no moon, no stars, no Wolf's Eye, no Broken Crown, just darkness stacked on darkness like pages of a closed book — and the road ran south into it straight as a ruled line.

"One more thing, Keeper," Veyra Coll called down from the wall, and her voice carried strangely in the starless air, too clear, as if sound itself had lost its distance. "When you get where you're going — if you find what I think you'll find — remember that the guild raised you. Remember what we gave you."

He looked up at her silhouette against the blind sky, and eleven years of loyalty sat in his chest like a stone, and beneath the stone, small and cold and newly born, something else stirred that he did not yet have a name for.

"I always remember, Guildmaster," he said. "It's my trade."

He put his heels to the mare, and the gate yawned, and August Underhill rode south into a sky with no stars in it, carrying a sealed page against his heart and the first unanswered question of his life:

If everything the realm forgot had to be kept somewhere —

who was keeping him?
