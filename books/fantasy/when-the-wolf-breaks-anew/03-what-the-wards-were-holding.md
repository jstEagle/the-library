## 3. What the Wards Were Holding

The north gate had become a mouth, and the city was being spat through it.

August Underhill went with the flood of them — carters and cooks, a lamplighter still carrying his pole as if the dark were merely an outage he could fix, a woman clutching a birdcage in which a pigeon sat utterly calm, being the only creature in Ashfall that did not understand what had happened. Behind them the wards had come down. Ahead of them the road unspooled southward into a night that was no longer night at all, merely the absence of everything that had ever hung above the world to make it legible.

There were no stars. He kept looking up anyway. It was like running his tongue along the socket of a pulled tooth.

He had done this once before, he realized — looked up expecting stars and found nothing. The thought arrived without context, a splinter working loose from some sealed place in him, and he pushed it back down because there was no time for it. There was only the road, and the weight of the satchel against his ribs, and inside the satchel beneath three ledgers and a jar of borrowed courage he was suddenly very glad he hadn't returned, a single page folded four times and sealed with the Guild's grey wax.

*Open it when you are beyond the walls,* the Guildmaster had said. *Not before. Sworn is sworn.*

August had been beyond the walls for an hour when the press of refugees thinned enough that he could stop pretending he wasn't about to be sick.

He stepped off the road into the ditch-shadow of an old milestone, one hand flat against the cold stone — *ASHFALL, 3 LEAGUES* worn nearly smooth by weather and hands — and breathed until his stomach agreed to hold still. Around him the dark was total in a way dark had never been before. Even on moonless nights, even in cellars, there had always been starlight leaking under the edges of things. Now the darkness had no edges. It sat on the land like a lid.

He took out the page.

His fingers knew what to do with seals; they had broken a thousand of them over nineteen years, wax cracking under a thumbnail with a sound like a small opinion being offered. This seal fought him. Grey wax, stamped with the guild's sigil — the open book with seven impossible things flying out of it — and when it finally gave, it gave all at once, and August unfolded the page by feel because he could not have read it in that dark anyway.

Then he walked half a league more, until the refugee fires began to dot the roadside ahead, and stood at the edge of the first of them, close enough to see, far enough not to be spoken to, and read.

He read it twice. Three times. His lips moved on the third pass without producing sound, which was its own kind of irony, because August Underhill was one of perhaps five people alive who could have read the page aloud in the old cipher and meant something by it, and the page deserved better than a man mouthing it like a prayer he'd forgotten how to finish.

*Re: the Border Warding, its anchorage and maintenance.*

*Let it be recorded, under the Seventh Seal and the Silence-Oath entire, that the Great Ward which stands upon the borders of the realm is not anchored in stone, nor ley, nor any working of craft, but in the living memory of Aldric, third of that name, King in Ashfall.*

*It was bound so at his own petition. The Guild performed the binding in the year the dragons went. Payment was rendered as per contract. The nature of the payment is recorded elsewhere.*

*Understand what follows. A ward anchored in living memory draws its strength from the remembering. So long as the anchor remembers himself — his name, his reign, the shape of his own soul — the ward stands. Should the memory fail, the ward fails. The Guild's obligation is therefore maintenance of the anchor's continuity, and this duty has been discharged quarterly since the binding, per ledger entries cited herein.*

August stopped reading and looked at the fire.

A family was huddled around it. A grandfather was telling the children something — August could see the gestures, the broad sweeping arms of a story about wolves or weather — and the children were laughing, and the mother was not laughing, she was looking south, toward the capital, where her king sat, and August watched the thought move across her face like cloud-shadow: *is he even alive? What happens to us if he isn't?*

*Discharged quarterly,* the page said.

And August Underhill had signed the receipt for the last quarter's discharge himself. Entry 44,912. He remembered it now with a physical lurch: a courier in undyed wool, a payment of forty marks and a sealed letter he had been told not to wonder about, entered in the ledger under *Maintenance, Misc.* He had written *Misc.* He had written it in his own careful hand, and gone to supper.

Because that was the thing, that was the thing that made his vision swim and the milestone-stone tilt under his palm: the ledgers had *told* him. Nineteen years of entries, and somewhere in them the truth had been sitting in plain sight wearing a clerk's dull clothing, and he had catalogued it and shelved it and never once asked the question that would have split the grain.

*What does the Guild of Impossible Things do, every quarter, for the King in Ashfall?*

It kept him remembering. That was the answer. The whole impossible apparatus of the guild — the traded memories, the borrowed courage, the jars of other people's rain — all of it orbited, all these years, around one central transaction conducted in a palace August had never seen: they paid the king, quarter upon quarter, to go on remembering who he was, because his remembering was the only thing holding the sky together.

Except.

Except that the stars were gone, weren't they. Except that tonight the wards had shattered across the whole northern sky with a sound like the world's window breaking, and the page was still warm in his hands, and if the maintenance had been discharged — entry 44,912, *Misc.*, his own hand — then the failure was not the king's death, which was the first thing anyone would think, the comfortable explanation. The failure was worse. The failure was that the anchor still lived, and no longer remembered.

*Meaning,* August thought, with the vertigo rising through him like water filling a boot, *meaning the king has none left to give.*

Someone had been drinking the well. Someone had been drawing down the king's living memory, year after year, faster than the guild's quarterly payments could replenish it — and the ward had stood all this time on whatever dregs remained, and tonight the cup had finally run dry, and the sky had gone out like a lamp.

And the guild had known. Had to have known. You did not keep ledgers for thirty years without watching the numbers trend. The Guildmaster had put a sealed page in August's hands and pointed him south and said *sworn is sworn*, and August understood now that he had not been sent to carry news. He had been sent to carry evidence. Or an apology. Or an accounting — and gods, that was the word that broke him, *accounting*, because that was all August had ever been to anyone: the man who totted up what the world owed and never asked why the invoices kept coming.

He was still standing there when the riders came.

He heard them before he saw them, which saved him, though he wouldn't know it until later. Hooves on the packed road-mud, but wrong — too many moving as one, no driver's cursing, none of the ragged rhythm of refugees' exhausted animals. This was cavalry. Cavalry rode like a sentence: deliberate, punctuated, going somewhere.

August did not think. Later he would be grateful for that, because thinking would have cost him the four seconds he needed. He simply moved, folding himself down behind the milestone with the satchel crushed to his chest, into the deep seam of shadow that the missing stars had opened like a wound beside the road.

They came up the rise at a walk, deliberately slow, the way men ride when they are hunting something that hides.

There were six of them. The firelight reached them in pieces — a breastplate here, a horse's foam-flecked chest there — and what it showed turned August's blood to well-water. Royal black. Not the black of mourning or the black of rich cloth, but the true royal black, dyed with inkroot, the color of the king's own household guard, cut in the long riding coat of the crown's messengers. And on the breasts, picked out in thread so dark it was visible only when the firelight caught it exactly: the sigil of the crown entire. Not the prince's mark, not a seneschal's badge. The full arms of Aldric III.

The king's men. Riding out of a capital whose king had, by every account, just lost the sky.

They drew rein at the fire. The grandfather's story died mid-word. August, pressed into the dirt with his heart hammering against the satchel's buckles, heard everything with terrible clarity — the way you hear well when you are trying not to breathe.

"Ledger-keepers," the lead rider said. He had a voice like dry paper turning. "Anyone pass through with guild marks? Satchels, seals, grey wax?"

"No one," the mother said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, in the firelight, were white-knuckled around her youngest's shoulders.

"We're looking for clerks." Another rider, younger, and there was something wrong with his voice too — a doubled quality, as if two men were speaking in unison slightly out of step. "Men who carry books. They'll have ink on their fingers. They may not seem dangerous." A pause, and then the doubled voice again, softer, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the worst thing August had ever heard. "They are the most dangerous men alive."

Silence from the fire. Then the grandfather, quavering: "The stars, m'lord. Our stars. Is it the king? Has something happened to—"

"The king," the lead rider said, "is in excellent health."

And August, crouched in the ditch-dark with the sealed page digging a corner into his palm, felt the words go through him like a blade finding the gap in armor — because that was the confirmation, wasn't it, laid out neat as a ledger line. The wards had failed while the king lived. Something wore the crown and drank the memory, and now it wanted the witnesses tidied away, the men whose books might prove the arithmetic of thirty years of theft. It was sending the king's own black to hunt the guild's clerks.

It was tying off the loose ends of the oldest fraud in the realm, and August Underhill, keeper of ledgers, oath-bound swordspeaker, was item number one.

The riders moved on. Hooves in mud, receding southward — toward the next fire, and the next, asking their question at each one. August stayed where he was long past the point of comfort, until the cold had soaked through his coat and into the marrow-place where courage lived, and then he rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood in a fire.

He did not go back to the road. He struck out east across the dark fields instead, away from the fires, away from the questions, letting the land swallow him. South was still the direction. The Guildmaster had said south. But south was also where the riders were going, patient as arithmetic, and August meant to be a great deal harder to find than entry 44,912 had been.

Somewhere behind him, Ashfall burned nothing — the city had no light to spare. Somewhere ahead, past the forgotten places, south lay the capital and the crown and the answer. And somewhere in his satchel, against his ribs, three ledgers rode with him that six men in royal black would kill a family's grandfather to find.

August walked, and did not look up, because there was nothing above him anymore to look at — and because he had begun, very quietly, in the cellar of himself where he kept the thoughts he didn't sign for, to wonder:

*Nineteen years I kept those books. Thirty years they've paid the king to remember.*

*So why can't I remember the day I first walked through the guild's door?*

The dark had no answer. But miles behind him, at the top of the rise, a seventh rider had appeared on the skyline — motionless, hatless, watching the spot where the milestone stood empty — and did not follow, and did not leave, and turned his horse east.
