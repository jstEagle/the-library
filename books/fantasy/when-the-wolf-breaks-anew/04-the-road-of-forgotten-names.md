## 4. The Road of Forgotten Names

Sera Vane knew the faces of four hundred and eleven people.

She kept them the way other women kept buttons — in a jar, in a tin, strung on twine across the rafters of her memory. The miller's wife at Harrow Ford with the burn scar shaped like a moth. The toll-keeper's twin sons, one born with a cowlick that never lay flat. The peddler woman who came through every autumn selling needles and lies about her age. Four hundred and eleven faces, each with a name stitched to it like a label on a preserves shelf, because names were what people paid her in and she had learned early that a witch who forgets her clients does not eat through winter.

She could not have told you her mother's face to save her own life.

That was the joke the road had played on her, or God, or whatever it was that decided these things: Sera Vane, who could summon the face of any soul who had ever crossed her threshold, woke one morning eleven years ago to find a hole in her own history shaped exactly like a mother. She remembered the cottage they'd shared. She remembered the smell of tallow and feverfew. She remembered being loved — that was the cruelest part, she could remember *the feeling* of it, warm as bread — but the face above that feeling was smooth as an egg, blank as a coin worn featureless by ten thousand hands.

Everyone else's mothers. Never her own.

So when the stars went out three nights past and the sky turned to the inside of a closed coffin, Sera did not panic the way the villagers did. Panic was for people whose memories were still attached to things. She simply packed her satchel — dried nettle, willow bark, the good knife, the bad knife — and walked north out of Harrow Ford before anyone could ask her to stay and fix something that could not be fixed.

The road between Harrow Ford and the river towns had gone strange in the dark. Without stars, without even moonlight, the world after dusk became a country of sounds: hooves where there should be none, doors slamming in farmhouses that had stood empty since the plague years, and once — she told herself she had imagined it — a voice calling her name from a stand of birches, in her mother's voice, except that she had no memory of her mother's voice and so could not say whether the thing calling was lying or not.

By day she walked with her hood down and her eyes on the ditches, because in the dark nobody could see anything, and in the dark, things had begun to move that the wards used to keep out. She found the first body two days into the dark. A courier, royal black livery scorched off him in patches, his horse gone. She did not rob him. She took his boots, because dead men's boots did a living woman more good than dignity did, and she said his name over him — she had read it off the tag sewn in his collar, Wilkin Arras, and she said it twice so it would stick — and then she walked on.

It was on the third day, in the grey hour before dawn that no longer promised anything, that she smelled blood and old smoke riding the wind off the eastern hills, and followed it, because a hedge-witch follows trouble the way crows do: half for grief, half for whatever's left after.

* * *

He was in the wreck of a way-shrine at the crossroads where the Marrowgate road split from the river road — one of the little stone shelters that had once held a lantern lit from some saint's flame, though the lantern now hung cold and the saint's name had been chiseled off the lintel so long ago that only the shape of the letters remained, like scars where writing used to be.

The man was folded against the shrine wall as if someone had tried very hard to make him fit there and he had refused. Blood had dried black down the whole left side of him, matting his coat to his ribs. His right hand, even unconscious, even half-dead, was curled around the strap of a satchel like a child around its mother's skirt.

Sera crouched at the shrine mouth and listened. No birds — there had been no birds since the stars went, another wrongness she kept in a list at the back of her mind — but no breathing either that wasn't his, slow and wet and stubborn. Whoever had done this had left in a hurry, or thought their work finished. There were hoofprints in the frost, shod, heavy, turning east toward the river. And there, dropped near the door of the shrine, a scrap of royal-black cloth, burnt at the edge.

Riders in black. Hunting something. Hunting *him*, most likely.

Any sensible woman would have kept walking. Sera Vane had been called many things in her life and sensible was occasionally among them, but she knelt in the frost and put two fingers under the man's jaw anyway, and found a pulse going like a moth against glass.

"Right," she told him. "Don't die while I'm deciding whether to save you."

Up close he was younger than she'd guessed — thirty, perhaps, with the kind of face that stops aging early from worry rather than weather. Brown hair cut short and practical. Ink stains on the fingers of his ruined left hand, the hand of a man who wrote for a living, or had, until whoever worked him over had broken three of those fingers with what looked like care. His breathing gurgled. At least one rib had gone through something it shouldn't have.

She got her flask against his lips — willow and poppy and a measure of something stronger — and worked the satchel free of his grip only after peeling back his fingers one at a time, apologizing to them under her breath the way she apologized to everything, chickens and corpses and strangers' knuckles alike. Witches' habits. You talked to what couldn't answer. It kept your own voice alive.

The satchel was leather, old, scarred, and stamped on its flap with a seal in faded red wax.

Sera went very still.

She knew that seal. Not from seeing it often — she had seen it exactly once, eleven years ago, on the letter that arrived the morning after her mother's face went missing. A circle bisected by a key, the key's teeth formed from small impossible shapes: a stair that climbed inside itself, a bottle wider than its own mouth, a door drawn open onto empty air. Beneath it, in script fine as eyelashes, the words she had never forgotten because forgetting them was the one thing she could not forgive herself for:

*The Guild of Impossible Things. Your account is settled.*

She had taken the letter to three priests and two wise-women and one very drunk scholar, and none of them could tell her why a guild of thaumaturges and wonder-brokers would write to a hedge-witch about her account, or why the letter made her hands shake, or why — this was the part she never told anyone — reading it had felt like standing at the grave of someone whose name she couldn't recall, weeping without knowing for whom.

And here was that same seal, red wax gone the color of old blood, strapped to the ribs of a man the crown's riders had left for dead on the Marrowgate road, three nights after every star in the sky went out.

"Well," Sera said softly. "Aren't *you* a piece of the puzzle."

She looked at his face again, at the worry-etched brow, the ink-stained fingers, and felt the familiar ache open behind her breastbone, the ache that lived where her mother's face should have been. This man knew things. This man had been carrying the guild's leather against his body, and the guild had written her a letter once, and the sky had gone dark, and all of those facts were pulling on each other like magnets under a table.

She was going to save him. She already knew it. She was furious about it, and she saved him anyway.

* * *

It took the better part of the day.

She splinted his fingers, bound his ribs with linen boiled that morning over a fire she built low and shielded, because smoke drew eyes and eyes these days were dangerous. She packed the wound below his shoulder blade with moss and cobweb and a poultice of yarrow that she prayed over in the old fashion, which is to say she threatened it. She fed him broth of bone and nettle drop by drop when he surfaced enough to swallow, and when fever took him near dusk he muttered — names, mostly, and numbers, and once, clearly, terribly:

"—page forty-one, the seal is *wrong*, the seal has always been—"

Then he wept without waking, quietly, the way men do when they've taught themselves to do it silently, and Sera sat in the dark of the cold shrine with her knife across her knees and watched the road and thought about seals and letters and accounts settled, and did not sleep at all.

Near midnight the fever broke. Near dawn, his eyes opened.

They were grey, and they were afraid, and they found her face across the small fire and fixed on it with an intensity that made her spine prickle — not the look of a man seeing a stranger. The look of a man checking a ledger entry against reality.

"You're not who I expected," he rasped.

"Disappointing," Sera said. "I'm usually exactly who people expect. Hedge-witch, middle years, bad temper, good broth. Drink this." She put the flask to his mouth before he could argue. He drank. That, at least, told her he wasn't a fool. "You've been asleep a day and a night. Three of your fingers are broken and set. Two of your ribs are cracked and one of them is worse than cracked. The riders in black went east toward the ford, and if you tell me they weren't looking for you, I'll know you for a liar, and liars don't get more broth."

Something moved behind his eyes — the flicker of a man counting, weighing, deciding what truth cost versus what silence cost. She knew that arithmetic. She'd done it her whole life.

"August," he said at last. "My name is August Underhill. And yes. They were looking for me." He shifted against the wall and winced with the particular discipline of a man practiced in pain. "You have my satchel."

"I have your satchel." She let that sit. Then, slowly, she lifted it and turned the flap toward the firelight, toward him, and tapped the seal with one finger. "Tell me about this."

Whatever he expected her to ask, it wasn't that. She watched him go careful.

"It's a guild seal," he said. "The Guild of Impossible Things. I keep their ledgers. Kept." A shadow crossed his face, some fresh grief too large to be about fingers. "In Underhill. The city. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "Why?"

"Because eleven years ago," Sera said, "a letter came to my door bearing that seal, telling me my account was settled, and the next morning I couldn't remember my mother's face. So." She smiled, and knew from long experience that the smile was ugly. "You'll understand if I don't hand it back just yet."

The silence that followed had weight. In it, somewhere far off across the starless hills, something howled — long, low, wrong, an animal sound that ended in a register no animal owned. Neither of them flinched anymore. They were both past flinching. That, too, was something they had in common, she supposed, added to the pile.

"The ledgers," August said finally, very quietly, as if confessing to a crime. "The guild keeps ledgers. Everything anyone trades us. Memories. Years. Names. Faces. It all goes in the ledgers." His grey eyes came up to hers, and she saw the moment he understood what she'd told him, truly understood it, and she watched something break behind his face and get filed away to be dealt with later, the way careful men do. "Your mother's face would be in a ledger somewhere. If it was traded. It would be *written down*. It could be—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she meant. She softened it with effort. "Don't give me hope in that tone of voice. Hope with paperwork attached is just debt wearing a ribbon."

But her hands had gone unsteady, and she hid them in her skirts, and hated herself for it.

* * *

They stayed in the shrine two more days, because he could not walk and she would not carry him and leave the satchel, and because — though neither said it — the road alone was death now, and two watchful people slept in shifts better than one desperate one did.

They talked the way travelers talk in bad times: sideways, in pieces, trading truths like coins tested by bite. She learned he had served the guild since he was a boy; that the city of Underhill had emptied in a single terrible night; that the border wards had shattered with a sound like the sky breaking, and every star had gone out at once, and the Guildmaster had put a sealed page in his hands and said one word: *south*. She learned that he spoke to his sword — the sword was gone now, lost or taken in the ambush, and when he mentioned it his voice did something complicated — and she did not laugh, because she had spent her whole life talking to things that couldn't answer, and she knew the difference between madness and loneliness better than most priests did.

In return she gave him smaller coin. That she was born somewhere in the vale of the Netherwater and could not find it on a map anymore, because the map of her childhood had a hole in it. That she remembered four hundred and eleven faces and could draw any of them from memory but not the one that mattered. That she charged honest prices and cheated no one and had buried thirty-one clients in the plague year and remembered every face at every graveside, and had stood at her mother's grave — she was nearly sure it was her mother's grave — dry-eyed, because you cannot mourn what you cannot picture.

August listened to all of it with the attention of a man doing sums, and on the second evening he said, abruptly, "You're a ledger."

She looked up from the fire. "I beg your pardon."

"A ledger," he repeated, wondering at it, turning it over the way he must turn over everything. "Four hundred and eleven entries, indexed, preserved. You've been keeping accounts of people your whole life. People pay you in memories and you hold them safe and give them back true." He shook his head slowly. "The guild could have built an entire chapter-house around a talent like yours. Instead you're sitting in a ditch eating nettle broth."

"Instead I'm free," Sera said. "Which is what your guild charges the rest of us not to be." Then, because the fire crackled and the night was vast and lightless and his face in the firelight was young and hurt and trying so hard to be brave about it, she relented by a degree. "Besides. Ledgers can be stolen. Ask your ribs."

He laughed — a short, surprised bark, immediately regretted, one hand pressed to the bound ribs — and the sound startled a fox-something away from the shrine mouth, a quick slink of eyes and shadow. When he had his breath back he said, seriously: "Come south with me."

"I haven't agreed you're going anywhere yet."

"I have to reach the capital. Or beyond it. The sealed page—" his hand moved toward the satchel, hovered, withdrew; he had not asked for it back and she had not offered, and both of them understood this was now a negotiation conducted nightly in silence — "names things I don't understand yet. But riders in black are killing ledger-keepers, which means the crown thinks what's in our books matters, which means it matters. And you—" he met her eyes, and did not look away, which she gave him credit for, "—you want to know what your account was. What they took. Why. So do I, now. We're both owed, Mistress Vane. The debtors are the same. It seems foolish to collect separately."

Sera Vane looked at him for a long time. At the guild seal on the satchel between them. At the hole in her own history, eleven years old and aching as always, and at this ink-fingered stranger who carried the guild's leather against his heart and spoke of debts like a man who had spent his life on the creditors' side of the desk and was only now learning what it was to be owed.

Two people carrying other people's memories, walking the same dark road. It was either fellowship or folly, and she had survived long enough to know the difference was mostly a matter of who was holding the knife.

"All right, August Underhill," she said. "South. But three conditions. One: I hold the satchel at night. Two: when we find your ledgers — and we will find them, because men like you always go back to their ledgers — I read the page with my mother's account on it first. Before the king, before the guild, before God." She held up a third finger. "And three: you tell me why a grown man talks to a sword, and you tell me true, before we reach the river."

August looked at her across the small defiant fire, in a world with no stars, and something eased in his grey eyes — the particular relief of a lonely man recognizing, across a great distance, another keeper of impossible accounts.

"Done," he said. "Though I warn you, the answer to the third is longer than the road."

"Good," said Sera Vane, and banked the fire for the night. "I've nowhere else to be but forward."

Far to the east, toward the river and the capital beyond it, the wrong-howling rose again — closer now, and answered, this time, by a second voice. August's head came up sharply, and his hand went to his empty hip where a sword no longer hung.

"That," he said quietly, "is a problem for tomorrow."

"No," said Sera, watching the darkness between them and the road, and reaching for her knives. "That's a problem for however long it takes them to get here."
