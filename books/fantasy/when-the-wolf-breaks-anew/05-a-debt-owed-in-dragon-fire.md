## 5. A Debt Owed in Dragon-Fire

The mountain announced itself before Sera saw it — a smell of hot iron and old smoke rolling down the valley like a rumor of something that had happened long ago and never quite finished happening. She walked behind August, who rode the mule because his wounds demanded it, though he kept trying to dismount whenever the path steepened, out of what she suspected was a lifetime's habit of not asking anything of anyone.

"You'll open those stitches," she said.

"They're your stitches."

"Which is why I know how badly you'll open them."

He subsided. Three days since she had pulled him out of the ditch below the ruined toll-house, three days of fever and riddles, and already they had developed the rhythm of an old married couple in a story neither of them wanted to be in. Behind them, Tam Broadfoot carried Ivet on his shoulders, the child asleep with her cheek against his cropped hair, her small hands knotted in his collar as if even dreaming she feared being set down.

Sera counted her own steps because it was something to do that was not thinking. Four hundred and six steps to the next bend. At the bend, four hundred and seven more. It was a hedge-witch's trick for keeping panic at bay: give the mind a ledger of its own, small and honest, and it would not go rummaging in the drawers where worse things were kept.

The trouble was that her mind kept returning to one drawer in particular, and prying it open no matter how many steps she counted.

*Your mother's face,* the road whispered. *You have forgotten your mother's face.*

She remembered everyone else's. That was her curse and her craft both: she could hold a stranger's grief in her cupped hands like water from a stream, carry it a mile or a year, pour it out whole when they needed it back. Faces came to her the way birds came to a hedgerow. The miller's wife with her burn-scarred jaw. The dead soldier at Wetherford whose eyes she'd closed and whose name she still said some mornings, unasked. Ivet's small pointed chin. Even August Underhill, gray as ash and twice as stubborn, she had already memorized down to the seam of the scar over his left eyebrow.

But her own mother — the woman who must have held her, fed her, taught her the first knot of the first charm — was a shape without features. A voice with no words attached. Sera had stopped telling people this years ago, because the looks they gave her were worse than the lack itself.

"Smoke," Tam said behind her. "Fresh."

"I smell it." August swung down from the mule and immediately regretted it; Sera watched him decide not to show that he regretted it. "The shrine should be above the tree-line yet."

"Maybe someone lights fires against the cold."

"Maybe." August's hand drifted to the sword at his hip — not to the hilt, Sera noticed, but to the scabbard, resting his palm against it the way another man might touch a sleeping dog. He had done it all three days, at every fork in the road, every rustle in the bracken. Speaking to steel out of habit, he'd admitted finally, when she asked. Oath-habit. The sword hadn't answered him since the stars went out.

They climbed through pine into cloud, and out of the cloud into evening, and there the shrine stood: not the grand edifice Sera had half-expected, but a low hall of black stone built into the mountain's shoulder as if the mountain had grown around it the way a tree grows around a nail. Smoke rose from vents in the roof, thin and steady. Carved above the door, worn nearly smooth by centuries of wind, was the figure of a serpent with wings — a dragon rendered by someone who had perhaps seen one only in description, or in memory so old it had gone to geometry.

The door opened before anyone knocked.

The woman standing in it was old the way the shrine was old — not frail but weathered, reduced to essentials. Her hair was white as furnace-ash and bound with copper wire. Her forearms, bare below rolled sleeves, were mapped with burn scars in patterns too regular to be accidents: deliberate marks, a script written in healed fire.

"You bring a guild seal to my door," she said. Her voice was gravel over coals. "Three days after the sky goes dark. You have nerve, ledger-keeper, or no sense at all."

"We need shelter," August said. "And we were told—"

"I know what you were told. Everyone was told something once." She looked past him at Sera, then at Tam, then up at Ivet, who had woken and was staring at the scar-script on the woman's arms with an expression Sera couldn't read — wonder, or recognition, or hunger. The old woman's gaze snagged on Ivet and stayed a moment too long.

"Well," she said, stepping back. "The mountain doesn't turn away travelers, and neither do I, whatever else may be said of Emberwrights. Come in before the dark does. And you—" this to August, with a jerk of her chin toward his blood-crusted bandage, "—will sit down and let the witch look at you, or I'll light a fire under you myself. We are experts in that, if nothing else."

* * *

Inside, the shrine was warm in a way that had nothing to do with hearths. Heat came up through the black stone floor itself, gentle and constant, as if the mountain had a heartbeat and the hall were laid against its wrist. Along the far wall burned the strangest thing Sera had ever seen: a line of seven braziers of descending size, each holding a flame that did not flicker. They burned utterly still, like painted fire, like fire remembered rather than fire burning. Six of the flames were small — candle-small, thumbnail-small, one barely a red spark trembling in its iron bowl.

The seventh brazier, largest of all, was empty. Cold. Its basin was scorched black and glassed in places, as if something in it had burned so hot it had turned its own container partway to mirror.

"The last keepers of the dragon-fire," August said quietly, reading the hall the way he read everything, top to bottom, left to right, like an entry awaiting audit. "There were stories. When I was—" He stopped. Sera watched him reach for the memory and find the shelf where it should stand. "When I was younger," he finished, and she marked the hesitation and filed it away.

"There were hundreds of us once," the old woman said. She had brought bread and smoked goat and a pot of something dark and bitter, and she set these down with the economy of someone who had cooked for crowds and now cooked for ghosts. "Ysolde Emberwright. You will call me that or nothing. This is my daughter's hall, and my granddaughter tends the flame, and that is the whole of us. Three Emberwrights. When my granddaughter was born, the mountain wept sulfur for a week. It knew." She sat, heavily, and looked at each of them in turn, and Sera felt the look go through her like a lancet. "Now. You will eat, and then you will tell me why a guild-ledgerer, a hedge-witch, a deserter, and a child with an unbaptized gift are walking south through a starless country toward my door."

"How do you know what we are?" Tam demanded, his hand flat on the table.

"Deserters always sit facing the door. Hedge-witches count their herbs before they eat anything. The child dreams loud enough that I heard her from the ridge. And him—" Ysolde nodded at August. "No one carries a satchel that carefully unless it matters more than his own skin. Eat."

They ate. The bitter drink turned out to be a tea of some mountain root that settled Sera's nerves like a hand smoothing cloth. Ivet ate three helpings and fell asleep against Tam's arm mid-bite, which told Sera more about how afraid the child had been than anything she might have said.

Then Ysolde folded her scarred hands, and the warmth in her went out like a shutter closing, and she said: "Tell me what you believe happened to the sky."

August told her. He told it plainly, without ornament — the wards breaking, the stars going out all at once, the sealed page, the king's living memory anchoring the border-wards, the meaning of that. Sera watched the old woman's face while he spoke and saw there no surprise at all. Only a slow, deep settling, like a house easing off its foundations after long expectation of an earthquake.

"Thirty years ago," Ysolde said when he finished, "a delegation came to this shrine from the capital. Courtiers, and behind them, men whose faces I have spent thirty years trying to forget and cannot, which for me is unusual." Something moved behind her eyes; Sera recognized the effort of a memory pushing uphill. "They made an offer. The guild of ledger-keepers stood surety for it. In exchange for certain favors — certain blindnesses, let us say — the crown would leave the Emberwrights our shrines, our traditions, our lives. We refused." She paused. "My son did not refuse. He negotiated. He believed there was room between refusal and surrender for a family to survive. He signed a provisional agreement. Within a season he was dead of a fever that burned him from the inside, and the provisional agreement became permanent, and the favors began."

"Favors," August repeated. His voice had gone very careful, the voice of a man reading a figure that doesn't balance.

"The fire dimmed," Ysolde said simply. "Year by year. Our dragons died out centuries ago — you know that, everyone knows that, the way everyone knows rain is wet — but the fire itself, the heart-flame, the fire that does not need feeding because it feeds on meaning, on oath, on true things spoken over it — that fire began to gutter. My grandmother tended it and lost a decade of her own life in a winter. My mother lost more. Each generation of Emberwrights has poured itself into keeping the last flame alive, and each generation has gotten less for its pouring. Seven braziers, seven splittings of the heart-fire. There is one left." She gestured at the sixth brazier, the trembling red spark. "That."

Sera looked at the tiny flame. It seemed almost obscene, that the fate of anything should rest in something so small. "You're saying the crown did this deliberately."

"I am saying that when a fire dims everywhere at once, in a shrine warded by its own heat, it is not accident. I am saying my son signed a piece of paper and the paper was stronger than the fire, which tells you what kind of paper it was and who wrote it. I am saying—" and here Ysolde's gravel voice roughened into something older and rawer, "—that I have known for thirty years that the thing sitting on that throne in the capital is eating the realm, because I have been watching it eat our fire at the same table. What I did not know, until tonight, was that it had grown bold enough to swallow the sky."

Silence. The still flames burned without sound. Up through the floor, the mountain's slow pulse continued, indifferent.

Then August leaned forward, and Sera saw him assemble the question with the care of a man handling a blade by both edges.

"The page the Guildmaster gave me says the wards were anchored to King Aldric's living memory. If the fire can unmake a false crown—"

"It can." No hesitation. "It is the only thing that can. A false crown is not gold and jewels, ledger-keeper; it is a claim. A lie so old and well-fed it has become a law of the world. Laws cannot be argued with, assassinated, or outlived. They can only be unmade by something older and truer. Dragon-fire is the oldest true thing left in this world. Spoken over a liar, it burns the lie and leaves the man. Usually." Her mouth twisted. "Usually."

"But?" Sera said, because there was a but; she could feel it hanging in the warm air like smoke before the flame shows.

Ysolde looked at the spark in the sixth brazier for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice was quieter, and somehow that was worse.

"But fire is fire. It spends. The heart-flame has been dying for thirty years, starved of what it needs — and what it needs is exactly what has been stolen from your king. Memory. Meaning. True names. The thing wearing Aldric has been drinking from the same well our fire draws from, and it has been drinking first, and deepest. What remains to us is—" she spread her scarred hands, "—enough. Perhaps. For one unmaking. One. If the flame is carried intact, if it is spoken over truly, if the one who bears it does not falter on the stairs of that place — and the stairs of that place, I promise you, were designed by something that understands faltering."

"And if we're too slow," Tam said. "If it takes months."

"Then the spark goes out," Ysolde said, "and there will never be another. Not in your children's time. Not in your children's children's. The dragons are gone. The oaths are broken. The Emberwrights are three old women and a girl. When this flame dies, the world loses its last word for *no*." She let that sit. "Weeks. I will not insult you with months. Weeks, ledger-keeper, and fewer of them than you would like."

Hope, Sera thought. Hope with a deadline attached. It was a peculiar sensation — like being handed a lamp on a night road and told simultaneously that it was the last lamp in the world and that its oil was measured. She found she could not decide whether to laugh or weep, and suspected that either would be inappropriate, and so drank her bitter tea instead.

August had gone very still. She knew that stillness now; she had seen it in ditches and on roadsides. It was the stillness of a man doing sums. "The capital is five weeks' hard travel. Less if we push. But we won't be traveling as four pilgrims. The hunters wear royal black, they've found us twice already, and we're carrying a child and a flame we don't yet have."

"You'll have the flame," Ysolde said. "I have not finished telling you what I can give."

"And the cost?"

"The cost is mine to reckon, not yours." The old woman rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and crossed to the braziers. Up close, Sera saw that the sixth flame cast no heat at all — or rather, that its heat was somewhere else entirely, in some other register; standing near it was like standing near a bell that had just been struck, a fullness in the ears, a pressure behind the sternum. Ysolde cupped her palms around the spark the way one cups a moth, protective and reverent and a little hopeless.

"My granddaughter will carry it," Ysolde said, not turning around. "She is sixteen and she has never left the mountain and she is the strongest Emberwright born in four generations. Do not argue with me about it. I have buried a husband, a son, and two daughters to this fire, and I will not bury the last of us in a dying shrine waiting for the world to end politely."

* * *

Later — after the plan had been sketched and argued and sketched again, after Tam had been shown to the sleeping alcove with Ivet, after August had withdrawn to a corner with his sealed page and his ledgers and his habit of speaking softly to a sword that no longer answered — Sera found herself in the shrine's small dispensary with Ysolde's granddaughter, re-stitching August's side under supervision.

The girl worked in silence, quick and sure, her fingers stained faintly copper at the tips. Ash-and-copper, like her grandmother. Sera held the lamp and tried not to think of drawers.

"He doesn't sleep," the girl said, nodding toward the corner where August sat. "I've watched. He pretends to, but his breathing stays awake."

"People like him don't trust the dark anymore. It took the stars."

"People like him." The girl tied off the last stitch, snipped the thread with a small hot knife, and looked up. Her eyes were unsettling — the color of banked coals, and lit similarly, from within. "Grandmother says you remember other people's things. Faces. Names."

"I do."

"And you've lost one of your own."

Sera's throat closed. She managed, eventually: "She talks to everyone about everyone, doesn't she."

"She listens to the fire. The fire hears things." The girl stood, gathering her tools, then paused at the doorway. "Witch. The flame needs true things near it. It feeds on them. Grandmother didn't say that part, because it frightens people. Whatever you've lost — if you get it back, don't waste it. Nothing is wasted here. Not anymore."

She left. Sera stood alone with the lamp and the sleeping man and the six still flames, and across the hall the seventh brazier waited, cold and glassed and hungry, and she understood suddenly that she had walked into a debt being collected — that the whole world, perhaps, had been living on credit for thirty years, and the bill had come due in dragon-fire, and someone had written her name on it in a ledger she had never agreed to sign.

From the corner, very softly, August said: "I know you're awake, witch. You counted four hundred steps coming up that last stretch. You only count when you're frightened."

Sera blew out the lamp.

Before the dark closed fully, she saw August rise and cross to the seventh brazier and lay his palm flat against its cold rim, and she heard him speak to it as he spoke to his sword — quietly, hopelessly, out of habit rather than need.

And the cold glass under his hand, briefly, warmed.
