## 6. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The king of Vhalderan woke, as he did every morning, in the dark behind his own eyes.

It was not true waking — he had not truly woken in thirty years — but there was a hour, just past dawn, when the thing that wore him grew quiet and drowsy with the weight of its feeding, and in that hour Aldric could press his face against the inside of his skull the way a prisoner presses his face to a cell window, and watch.

He watched through eyes that were no longer his. They were good eyes still, sharp and grey, set in a face the courtiers called grave and kingly. The hands that lifted the water-glass were steady. The voice that greeted the chamberlain was measured, warm at the proper intervals, cold at the others. It had learned all of this from him, once — had drunk his manners down like wine before it began on the vintages underneath.

*Good morning, Your Majesty,* said Chamberlain Ossel, bowing so low his chain of office swung forward and clicked against the marble.

*Good morning, Ossel.* The mouth shaped it perfectly. Thirty years of practice. *What is the day?*

*The fourteenth of Harvestmonth, sire. Greyweather brings the border tallies at midmorning, and there is a petition from the weavers' guild regarding dye tariffs—*

*We will see the tallies first.*

Aldric knew the ritual by heart, because he had written most of it himself, long ago, when his mornings were his own: the tallies first, always, because a realm that knows its borders sleeps well. He had been a careful king. He had believed in ledgers and tallies and wards renewed each season, in oaths spoken aloud and kept in writing. He had believed a kingdom was a memory agreed upon by enough people.

Now something agreed upon him instead.

* * *

The feeding happened at night, though the court never saw it. The court saw only what the crown wished: a king who retired early, rose late, and forgot nothing of consequence — or so they thought. In truth he forgot everything, nightly, methodically, the way a cellar is emptied bottle by bottle by a patient thief who does not wish the owner to count what remains.

Aldric had learned to feel it coming. There was a pressure first, like deep water filling the ears; then a warmth behind the breastbone, spreading upward along the spine, settling into the skull like a cat arranging itself on a cushion. Then the presence would turn its attention inward — inward toward *him*, the small dark room where the true king sat — and begin.

It did not tear. That was the horror of it, the part no one could have warned him about because no one had survived to describe it. It did not tear memories out. It *unpicked* them, stitch by stitch, with a delicacy that could only be called loving. His mother's kitchen — gone over three nights, the smell of bread one evening, the sound of her humming the next, her face last, so gently taken that for a full day afterward he had known he loved someone whose name he could no longer find. His coronation — unpicked over weeks, the archbishop's blessing, the weight of the crown, the crowd roaring his name until even the roar was gone and only a shape remained where the memory had been, smooth as river stone.

He had fought, in the early years. He had raged against the inside of his own body like a man beating on a door in a burning house, and the thing had paused in its feeding once — once — and considered him with an attention like moonlight falling through a keyhole, and said, in the voice that was his voice:

*Spend your strength as you like. I have time. I am very old, little king. I have eaten kingdoms older than the word for kingdom.*

After that he had stopped raging and started rationing.

That was the war now: not swords, but shelves. Each night the presence came to the cellar of him, and each night Aldric chose which bottles to place within reach and which to bury. He could not stop the feeding. But he had learned — slowly, over decades, the way a prisoner learns the exact length of his chains — that the thing took what was offered most readily. What lay loose. What he himself reached for.

So Aldric had done the unthinkable. He had begun to forget on purpose — or rather, to let go, loosening his grip on whole years of himself, pushing them to the front of the cellar like a shopkeeper arranging goods for a customer he hated. Let it take his wedding day. Let it take his father's funeral, his hunting trips, his mastery of the northern dialects, his love of chess. Let it take them quickly, cleanly, while he hoarded the rest.

And at the very bottom of the cellar, wrapped in oilcloth and iron, buried deeper than anything else he owned, he kept the one thing he refused to surrender.

A boy with a ledger.

* * *

Midmorning came, and Greyweather brought the border tallies, and the thing wearing the king received them with every appearance of a monarch's attention.

Aldric watched through the window of himself. This was the other cruelty — that the presence did not merely use his body but performed his office with competence. The tallies were correct. The tariffs would be judged fairly. Under the false crown, grain moved, roads were mended, justice — mostly — was done. A kingdom could be run by a hollow thing wearing a dead man's manners, and run *well*, and that was perhaps the deepest treachery of all: the realm had never been happier than under the thing that was eating it.

*Your Majesty approves the western figures?* Greyweather asked.

*I approve the western figures.* A pause, masterfully human. *The eastern garrisons report the wards renewed?*

*Renewed at midsummer, sire. Strong as ever.*

Strong as ever. Aldric felt something twist in the small dark room of himself. The wards. He remembered — no. He *had* remembered, once, before the cellar was emptied of that shelf too. He had stood on the walls at the founding, an old swordspeaker's hand on his shoulder, and sworn his living memory into the border stones: *while I remember my realm, my realm will be defended.* A young king's boast. A young king's mistake. He had not understood then what it meant to anchor a wall to a mind — that a mind could be emptied without dying, that a wall anchored to forgetting does not fall like a wall.

It thins. Like light through fog. Like a voice going quiet in another room.

Thirty years the thing had fed, and the wards had thinned with every swallowed year, and somewhere far away, in some guildhall or counting-house, men who kept accounts of impossible things must have watched the numbers change and wondered. Or not wondered. Aldric had trusted institutions once. Institutions were how the crown had gotten into his head in the first place — he was nearly sure of that, though the memory itself was long since eaten. There had been a favor asked. There had been a price. There had been a night when the wine tasted of ash.

He clung to the shape of that night the way a drowning man clings to the shape of a plank, knowing it was a plank because his hands remembered holding wood.

*You seem distant, sire,* Greyweather ventured.

*I am considering the east,* said the thing, in his voice, with his gravity. And then, softly, almost tenderly, in a tone Aldric alone could hear and understand: *And you are considering the boy again. Little king, you waste your hoard.*

Aldric said nothing. Inside, he folded his arms around the memory the way a man folds his coat around a candle in wind.

The boy with the ledger. He was not sure anymore why that memory, of all the thousands the thing had left him — why that one it seemed unable to take, or unwilling, or perhaps simply hadn't found yet, tucked where he'd hidden it beneath layers of deliberately cultivated boredom. A counting-room. Candle-smoke and the smell of ink. A thin boy, maybe twelve, maybe thirteen, with ink-stained fingers and a look of ferocious seriousness, reciting entries back to him without looking at the page. *Three favors owed by the crown to the Emberwrights, interest accruing. One ward-stone cracked at the ford of Ellow, replacement pending. One apprentice entered into service, August, surname withheld per custom.*

*August.* The name was a splinter he refused to let the flesh close over.

He did not know where the boy was now. He did not know if the boy lived. He knew only that on some forgotten afternoon thirty years ago, a child had looked up from a ledger at a young king and said, with the solemnity only children and accountants can manage: *Everything written here stays true, Your Majesty. Even when people don't.*

Even when people don't.

The thing wearing him smiled at Greyweather — his smile, wasted on Greyweather — and Aldric, in his cell behind the eyes, held the splinter and bled around it and called it hope.

* * *

Night came. The candles in the royal bedchamber were snuffed one by one by servants who bowed to a king and never looked long at his face; the court had learned, in the way courts learn things without knowing they learn them, that meeting the king's gaze after dark was unwise. The great doors closed. The guards withdrew. Silence pooled in the room like spilled water.

And the presence settled in to feed.

Aldric felt it turn inward, that familiar deep-water pressure, that warm arranging weight. Tonight it was unhurried. It had eaten well lately — he could tell by its languor — and tonight it browsed rather than devoured, moving along the shelves of him like a scholar in a library it owned outright.

It paused at the shelf where he kept the recent years, the ones he fed it freely, and took a little. His twentieth winter — gone. A horse he had loved — gone. The taste of honeyed pears — going, going, the sweetness thinning out of his tongue even as he tried to hold it, until he could remember that he had liked them without remembering how liking tasted. Each loss was small. Each loss was nothing. That was the design of it: a kingdom of small nothings, emptied one coin at a time, so that the treasury never once rang hollow in the hearing of the world.

*You are quiet tonight,* the presence remarked. It spoke inside his skull, in his own voice, and after thirty years he still could not make himself hate the sound, and he hated that most of all. *No struggle? No rattling of your little cage? You've grown philosophical, little king.*

*I've grown economical,* Aldric answered, because even now, especially now, he would not give it the pleasure of despair.

A ripple of amusement, cold and vast, like a lake turning over. *Economical. Yes. You hide things from me. You think I haven't noticed the empty corners you keep sweeping clean. You arrange your cellar so carefully.* The attention drifted lower, browsing, idle — and then stopped.

Aldric went absolutely still.

For a long moment there was nothing but the dark and the pressure and the sense of a vast patience bending close, closer, reading the dust on the shelves, reading the swept floors, reading the one small iron-bound box at the bottom of everything that did not match its surroundings because it was polished, daily, by hands that had nothing else left to polish it with.

*There,* the thing said softly. *There is something you love.*

*There is nothing I love,* said Aldric. *You've seen to that.*

*Nothing you love,* it repeated, tasting the words. And then, with terrible gentleness: *But something you keep.*

It turned away. It let him go. It fed elsewhere in him, casually, taking a summer and half a friendship and the name of his childhood dog, and it gave no sign that it would return to the bottom of the cellar — but Aldric had been a careful king, and careful kings know the difference between a wolf passing a fold and a wolf marking it.

Tonight, for the first time in years, it had marked the fold.

He pressed his face to the window of his own eyes and looked out at the dark bedchamber, at the crown glinting faintly on its stand across the room, black metal drinking the starlight that came through the shutters. No stars, he noticed dimly, through the gap. No stars tonight. He could not have said how he knew there should be — that shelf had been eaten long ago — but some ledger-line in him insisted: *the sky keeps accounts too, and tonight the columns are blank.*

The presence yawned in his chest, drowsy with its meal, and murmured as it sank toward its daylight sleep, in his voice, almost fond:

*Soon, little king. I'll find what you're keeping soon. Everything written stays true — didn't some earnest child tell you that?*

Aldric froze.

It laughed once, low and long, the laugh of something that has eaten thirty years and found them sweet, and slept.

And in the dark behind the dark, the last free king of Vhalderan held his splinter of a memory — a counting-room, a candle, a boy saying *everything written here stays true* — and understood, with a certainty that felt like a bell tolling, two things at once.

That the thing had heard the boy's voice somewhere, recently, carried in someone else's borrowed thoughts.

And that somewhere south of the city, in the wide world he could no longer see, August was coming home.
