## 7. Heirs of the Broken Oath-Lines

Marrowgate smelled of tallow, wet stone, and the particular stubbornness of a town that had decided, long ago, not to belong to anyone.

August had heard of it the way one hears of all free towns — in guild records, filed under *anomalies*. A river-port wedged between three lordships that had each claimed it and none had held it, Marrowgate had simply refused. No garrison. No banner. A council of quarrelsome merchants and a wall that no siege engine had ever breached, though every engine in living memory had tried at least once.

He understood why when he saw the wall.

It was not beautiful. It was not even particularly tall. But it rose from the river mud like something grown rather than built, its stones fitted so tightly that mortar seemed an insult, and where older towns showed their scars as crumbled breaches patched with newer masonry, Marrowgate's wall bore no scar at all. Every assault ever made upon it had simply... failed. Stones struck by catapults had been found afterward unchipped, on the far bank, arranged in neat rows, as if the wall had declined them politely.

*An oath-line*, August thought, and his chest tightened around a guilt he had carried south for two hundred miles. *A living one.*

The Guild's records called the oath-lines *assets*. August had copied the word into ledgers a hundred times without ever letting himself feel its weight. Now, walking Marrowgate's crooked streets with his satchel heavy against his hip and Sera Vane a half-step behind him, he felt it. Assets. People who had been born carrying something the realm needed, catalogued and counted by men like him, in books like his, while the world forgot what those people were owed.

"You keep looking over your shoulder," Sera said quietly. She wore her hedge-witch's gray hood down despite the drizzle; she said hoods made people remember they were hiding, and remembering was the whole point of her now. "The hunters?"

"The hunters follow ledger-keepers," August said. "We're past that."

"We are the ledger-keepers."

"Yes," August said. "But I think they've stopped following. I think they're waiting to see where we go." He looked up at the wall again, at the gray sky pressing down on it. "Which means wherever we go next matters more than anything we've done yet."

Sera said nothing to that. In the weeks since she'd pulled him half-drowned from a ditch outside Harrow Fen, dug the crossbow bolt out of his shoulder with her own hands and a prayer to no god in particular, she had developed the habit of saying nothing precisely when silence would cut deepest. It was, August suspected, a hedge-witch's discipline. When you remembered everyone else's griefs and not your own, you learned to let other people's silences speak first.

They found Tam Broadfoot where the whole town could point them to him: at the wall.

Not guarding it. Guarding implied duty, and Tam Broadfoot had famously abandoned his. He sat on a stool at the base of the western gatehouse with a mug of small beer and a whetstone he never used, and he watched the stones the way old men watch the sea — not with love, but with the weary attention of someone who knows exactly how large and patient and indifferent a thing can be.

He was younger than his reputation. Thirty perhaps, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had carried things heavier than soldiers should carry, with a deserter's beard grown careless and a deserter's eyes that went flat when August gave him the guild salute out of habit and then wished immediately he hadn't.

"Don't do that," Tam said.

"Sorry." August lowered his hand. "Habit."

"Everything you people do is habit." Tam took a pull of beer. "You came about the line. Everyone comes about the line eventually. The crown sent a man two years back, very polite, wanted me to sign something renewing my *service*. I told him what I tell everyone." He set down the mug. "I don't have a gift. I have a debt. And I'm done paying it."

"It isn't a debt," August said. "It was never meant to be—"

"Wasn't it?" Tam stood, and up close he was taller than August, and broader, and there was a stillness to him that made the busy market street seem to arrange itself around his quiet. "Then explain it to me, swordspeaker. Explain it slow, because everyone who's come to explain it has used words like *duty* and *inheritance* and *the realm's need*, and not one of them has explained why the gift lives in my bones and the cost lands there too."

August opened his mouth. Closed it. Because here was the thing he had rehearsed the whole road south, the speech he'd built in inns and ditches and the hollow dark before dawn: *the wards have fallen, the stars are gone, the king wears no crown but is worn by one, and we need the heirs of the old lines to stand together one last time.* He had imagined delivering it like a summons. Like history.

Standing in front of Tam Broadfoot, he found he couldn't deliver it as anything but a confession.

"Sit down," August said. "Please. What I have to tell you isn't short, and most of it shames me."

Tam looked at him for a long moment. Then, to August's surprise, he sat, and waved the stool beside him at Sera, and said, "Beer for the lady," to a passing boy, because even deserters, apparently, kept manners somewhere under everything else.

And August told him.

He told him about the night the stars went out — the sound like glass breaking across the whole sky, the guild's impossible inventory going inert or feral around him, the jar of borrowed courage in the third cabinet cracking like an egg. He told him about the sealed page: the wards anchored to King Aldric's living memory, which meant the king had none left to give, which meant something had been *drinking* a king for thirty years while the realm forgot itself village by village, name by name. He told him about the Emberwrights and the guttering dragon-fire, weeks from dead, the only flame in the world that could unmake a false crown. He told him about the riders in royal black hunting ledger-keepers on the roads, and about the second ledger Sera had found evidence of, the hidden one, the one that recorded what the crown had paid to have erased.

"And here is the part that shames me," August said, and made himself say it plainly, because he had learned on this road that shame spoken plainly was the only kind anyone could use. "I kept accounts for thirty years. Names of heirs. Locations. Strengths. Costs incurred. I wrote your name in a book, Tam Broadfoot, when you were six years old, entered under *asset, wall-line, dormant*. I didn't question it. I didn't question any of it until the sky broke. The institution I served helped build the forgetting. And now I'm asking the people in those books to stand up and bleed for a realm that sold their names, because the alternative is that everything ends in the dark."

The street noise went on around them — carts, gulls, a fishwife arguing about eels. Tam stared at the wall.

"My father held the line before me," he said at last. "His mother before him. You know what the line actually is? Not the wall. The wall's just what it does when it's working." His jaw worked. "It's the promise. *Nothing gets through.* That's the whole oath, the first one, the one the Broadfoots swore when Marrowgate was a camp of refugees and somebody had to say *nothing gets through these people, not hunger, not raiders, nothing*. And it doesn't live in the stones. It lives in us. My grandmother stood a siege of nine months and walked away bent like a bow. My father held the ford at Callowmere for a day and a night and never straightened again. And me—" he laughed, and it was an ugly sound, "—me, I ran."

"From what?" Sera asked gently.

"From the levy war. Crown wanted the line for its own walls. Wanted Broadfoots standing at the capital gates, being *assets*. My father said no and died for saying it — officially of a fever, officially. And I put on the black anyway, because I was nineteen and stupid and proud to be asked." His hands curled around the empty mug. "First time I felt the oath take hold of my bones and *bend* them toward the crown's purpose instead of the town's, I deserted that same night. Walked out of my boots. They flogged the man who covered for me. So yes, swordspeaker. I know exactly what oaths cost. I'm paying it off in my spine every hour of every day, because the line doesn't care whether I serve it or not. It just *is*. And every time some fool attacks this town, it uses me whether I will it or not."

"That's why I'm here," August said quietly. "Not to commandeer the line. To ask the man."

"Ask him what, exactly?"

"To come south. To stand, once, deliberately, for something chosen instead of something inherited. The false king's hunters burned a shrine of the Emberwrights' kin two weeks ago. They're erasing the last keepers of the fire. When the fire dies, there is no unmaking the crown. There is no return of the stars. There's just whatever wears Aldric, wearing the world after." August met his eyes. "I won't pretend it's your fight. But it's going to be everyone's fight soon, and right now there are maybe six people in the world who know that, and you could be one of them."

Tam was silent for a long time. A gull screamed somewhere. The wall stood over them, patient as geology.

"I'll want to know one thing first," he said finally. "You said the second ledger recorded what the crown paid to erase. Was Marrowgate in it?"

August's stomach dropped, because he had read the sealed pages Ysolde's messenger had smuggled him, and he knew. "There was a payment recorded," he said carefully. "Forty years ago. For the *quieting* of a border claim. A sum to the guild for... administrative forgetting. The entry doesn't name the town. It names a valley."

"The Vale of Serrow," Sera said softly. "That's this valley. That's why no lordship can hold Marrowgate — everyone forgot they'd already agreed to give it up. The town's freedom isn't freedom. It's a hole in the record."

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them, something had gone out of them — the last ember of the man who might have said no.

"When do we leave?" he said.

* * *

They might have left that same evening, if not for the child.

August noticed her first at the market well the next morning: a girl of eight or nine, thin as a heron, sitting apart from the other children with the stillness of someone listening to something very far away. Her hair was the brown of river water and her dress had been let down twice at the hem by someone careful, and she was watching August with an expression he could not parse until she spoke.

"You're the one from the book," she said.

The market noise did not stop, but August's heart did. "What book?"

"The one with everyone in it." She tilted her head. "I dream the book sometimes. Pages and pages. Your name's in it a lot. August Underhill." She pronounced it carefully, the way children pronounce words they intend to keep. "Except in the dream, some of the pages where your name is have been torn out. And you walk around looking for them without knowing."

Behind him, he heard Sera's breath catch. He did not turn. "What's your name?"

"Ivet."

"Just Ivet?"

"Just Ivet." She shrugged, a child's shrug, too thin to carry what it carried. "Nobody remembers giving it to me. The ferryman found me on his boat five years ago with my name stitched in my collar and nothing else. But that's not the strange part." She leaned forward, and her eyes were gray as the sky over the wall, and old as the stones of it. "The strange part is I know things. Everyone the crown erased — I dream their names. Waking up screaming, mostly. There were four hundred and eleven in the last village. I can say all of them."

August knelt slowly, joints protesting, until his eyes were level with hers. Up close he could see the sleeplessness written under them, the fine tremor in her hands that he had seen before — in Ysolde Emberwright's youngest granddaughter, in every vessel asked to hold more than vessels should.

"Ivet," he said. "Has anyone ever told you what you might be?"

"Auntie Maren says touched. Old Cobb says cursed. The priest says I make it up." She considered. "I think they're all scared of me, which is silly, because I'm the one who has to carry it."

"Yes," August said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "Yes, you are. And it isn't fair, and I'm not going to pretend it is." He took a breath. This was the confession again, the one he seemed doomed to repeat on this road, each time costing more. "Ivet, I served the people who did this to you. Whatever named you, whatever filled your dreams with other people's stolen names — the machinery of it, the ledgers of it — I oil that machinery. I kept it running. If you come with me, I can't promise to make it stop hurting. I can only promise that what you carry is the only weapon in the world that the thing wearing the king is afraid of. Names are its food. You're full of names."

Ivet looked at him for a long moment with those old gray eyes, and then, horribly, kindly, she smiled.

"You tore the pages yourself," she said. "In the dream. You just didn't know it was you." She stood, brushing off her dress with the brisk finality of someone twice her age. "I'll come. Auntie Maren will say I shouldn't. I'm going anyway. Someone should carry them home."

"Carry whom home?"

"All the names." She said it as though it were obvious, and to her, August realized, it was. "That's what they're for."

* * *

They gathered at the western gatehouse at dusk, when Marrowgate's lamps came up yellow against the gray, and Tam stood waiting with a pack on his shoulder and his face turned toward the wall.

"I've got a condition," he said. "Two, actually. First: nobody writes my name in any book ever again. Yours included. If we win, I retire into a story nobody tells."

"Agreed."

"Second." He nodded upward, at the wall, at the town behind it glowing in the lamplight. "If it comes to a choice between the mission and civilians, we choose the civilians. Every time. I've watched institutions choose the other way my whole life. I'd rather fail clean than succeed dirty."

August thought of the guild, of the Guildmaster's face, of thirty years of ledgers balanced neatly against human lives. "Agreed," he said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much it cost and how much lighter he felt for the paying.

Sera appeared out of the dusk with bread, cheese, and the particular expression of a woman who has spent a day arranging departures without telling anyone. Ivet trotted at her heel like a small grave bird, clutching a bundle that August suspected contained every possession she owned and possibly several things belonging to others.

They passed under the gatehouse arch as the first true dark of evening settled. And it was there — in the tunnel of stone beneath the unscarred wall — that the wall itself seemed to notice them.

August felt it as a pressure, faint and vast: the old oath waking in Tam's bones as he passed beneath the promise his family had made, *nothing gets through*, and Tam grunted once, low, like a man accepting a familiar weight, and the stones above them hummed a note too deep to hear.

Then they were through, and on the south road, and the town fell away behind them.

They walked in the dark of a starless sky, four travelers on an empty road: a swordspeaker who could not remember himself, a witch full of other people's memories, a deserter whose bones were a fortress, and a child full of the dead.

It was Ivet who stopped first, halfway down the mile-long causeway from the gate, her small hand shooting out to catch August's sleeve.

"Lights," she whispered. "Behind us. On the wall."

They turned. Along the top of Marrowgate's unbreakable wall, where no watchman had climbed, torches were lighting one by one — a dozen, two dozen, silent, orderly, wrong — and standing among them, black against the flames, figures in royal livery who had no business inside a wall that nothing got through.

"They're already in," Tam breathed, and August felt the words land in his gut like stones.

The wall had let something through.
