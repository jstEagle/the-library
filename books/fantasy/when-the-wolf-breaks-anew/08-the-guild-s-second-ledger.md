## 8. The Guild's Second Ledger

The rain came back to Marrowgate the night after Ivet dreamed her first dead name, and Sera Vane stood in the doorway of the safehouse watching it fall and thought, as she always thought, that she could not remember whether her mother had liked rain.

She remembered other people's mothers easily enough. That was the shape of her gift and its cruelty both: she held faces the way a wall holds moss, catching whatever blew against it. The baker's mother in Hollowfen, who sang off-key and kneaded grief into her bread after the father died. Tam's mother, whom Tam himself barely recalled, though Sera carried her laugh like a coin in a pocket, rubbed bright with handling. Even August's mother — or the woman he supposed must have been his mother, from a single fragment he'd admitted to over the fire: flour on her hands, a door lintel marked with his height in knife-scratches. Sera kept all of them, an orchard of borrowed mothers, and her own was a bare branch.

"You're doing it again," Tam said behind her. He sat propped against the wall with his ribs wrapped, because a man whose oath cracked his bones every hour it held did not simply stop aching when the wall came down. "The looking-through-rain thing."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"Your eyes are working very hard for a rest." He shifted, winced, and gestured at the room with his chin. August had gone out at dusk to meet a contact among the river-ferrymen; Ivet slept in the loft, twitching through another dream, and Ysolde's heir Brenna had taken the girl's boots to re-sole them by candlelight. "He left the satchel," Tam said, meaning August, meaning the guild-leather bag that never left his shoulder except when he expected not to come back. He always expected not to come back. It was, Sera had decided, his most annoying form of prayer.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've been staring at it since sundown like it owes you money." Tam's grin was crooked and tired. "And because you're the one of us who reads things. I break things. Brenna burns things. Ivet—" he glanced up at the loft, softening, "—remembers them. But reading's yours."

The satchel sat on the table where August had left it, squat and patient as a toad. The guild seal was stamped into its flap in grey wax: a circle crossed by three lines, which August had once explained were a sword, a quill, and a scale, though which was which depended, he said, on the year. Sera had spent two weeks on the road with him now, long enough to know that everything about the Guild of Impossible Things depended on the year. Long enough to notice how he spoke of it — fondly, loyally, the way a man speaks of the house that raised him, without ever once asking who had paid for the raising.

She hadn't meant to open it. She told herself that afterward, and knew it for the polite lie it was. She had meant only to move it nearer the fire, out of the damp, and the flap had fallen loose where the wax seal had already been broken and rejoined so many times the wax had gone soft as bread. Inside lay the ledgers — August carried three, always, the current audit-book, the oath-line registry, and a slim black volume of guild correspondence — and beneath the ledgers, wrapped in oilcloth that smelled of lamp-oil and old decisions, something heavier.

A book bound in iron-grey leather, clasped shut with a hasp of the same dull metal, unmarked by any seal at all.

"Tam," she said quietly.

"Mm."

"If I look at this, and it turns out to be nothing, you are never allowed to mention it again."

"And if it isn't nothing?"

Sera lifted the book. It had weight the way a grave has weight. "Then we have a different conversation."

* * *

The hasp had no lock. That was the first wrongness — a guild that sealed its jam jars with wards and its doors with spoken oaths had left its most guarded-looking book open to any curious hand, and Sera understood why before she understood that she understood it: a lock invites breaking, but boredom is inviolable. No one steals what no one wants. No one reads a ledger twice.

Except that someone had read this one many times. The spine was cracked soft at particular pages, the way a priest's psalter cracks at the penitential psalms.

She opened it by candlelight while Tam watched the door and pretended not to watch her, and the first page bore a heading in a hand so fine and regular it might have been typeset:

*Second Ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things. Being a true account of all commissions accepted from the Crown, with payment received and goods delivered. Not for circulation. Not for confession.*

*Goods delivered.* Sera read the phrase three times. Then she began at the first entry, and the room got colder around her without changing temperature at all.

The entries were dated. They went back one hundred and forty years.

*Winterfall, Year 312. Commissioned by the Office of the Interior Wards, under seal of the King's Regent. To erase from common memory the village of Ashenmill, its situation, its population (two hundred eleven souls), and the cause of its removal. Cause: plague of the speaking kind, escaped containment. Payment: remission of the guild's tithe in perpetuity, plus consideration.*

*Consideration,* Sera thought. *What does consideration mean, in iron-grey ink?*

She turned pages. The hand changed over the decades — four different clerks, maybe five, each one neat, each one anonymous — but the entries did not change in substance, only in appetite. A village here. A scandal there. A noble line expunged from record when its claims inconvenienced a regent's succession. An entire harvest failure, forgotten so thoroughly that the famine that followed it seemed to arrive from nowhere, an act of God, none could say why the granaries stood empty. Each entry closed with the same chilling bookkeeping: *Delivered. Confirmed clean. No residue detected.*

No residue detected. Except there was residue. Residue was hedge-witches who couldn't remember their mothers' faces. Residue was towns where everyone paused at the same crossroads and felt sad without knowing why. Residue was a child in a loft above her head, waking up screaming names.

"I found Marrowgate in it," she said. Her voice came out flat and far away.

Tam's head came up. "What?"

"Not erased. Almost." She turned the book toward him, and her finger shook, and she hated it for shaking. "Year 341. Commissioned by the Crown, Office of the Interior Wards. To erase the free town of Marrowgate's charter-oath, its founding line, and the names of its oath-heirs, such that no lawful wall-binding may be renewed within its bounds." She looked up. "They didn't erase the town, Tam. They erased its ability to defend itself. Someone paid to make sure that when the king's riders came, there would be no walls worth the name."

Tam was quiet a long moment. His hands, resting on his wrapped ribs, curled slowly into fists. "That's why my grandmother taught me the binding in secret," he said. "Off the books. In a barn. She said the words had been taken out of the town records and she'd copied hers from her mother, who'd heard it sung." He laughed, one dry note without any humor in it at all. "I thought she was being quaint."

"There's more."

"There's always more. That's the trouble with books."

She kept turning. The ledger grew darker as it grew recent — the last twenty years filled page after page, the crown's commissions swelling like a tumor finding blood supply. Names bought by the dozen. Memories harvested wholesale during the Unremembering Feast, itemized like grain receipts. And then, wedged between a commission concerning a rebellious harbor-master and one concerning the suppression of a ballad — *the ballad of the Emberwright favor*, her mind whispered, remembering Ysolde's face when she'd told them what the guild had done to the dragon-fire — she found an entry dated the spring of her twelfth year.

*Spring, Year 355. Commissioned by the Office of the Interior Wards, countersigned in the Regent's own hand. To erase from the memory of Sera Vane, hedge-witch of Fenmarsh, aged twelve, all recollection of her mother, Maren Vane, her person, her calling, and the circumstances of her disappearance. Reason: subject witnessed the collection of Maren Vane under commission 2,207 and retained evidence useful to no one. Note: daughter exhibits early signs of the keeping-gift. Recommend monitoring. Do not erase the gift itself — a keeper who keeps nothing is useless, and the crown may have future use for one.*

Payment received: *consideration, ongoing.*

Sera did not remember standing up. She was standing, though, and the candle had guttered, and Tam was saying her name in the careful voice men use near ledges and wild animals.

"Commission 2,207," she said. "It should be in here. If they numbered it—"

"Sera."

"They numbered everything else. If they numbered my mother there will be an entry, there will be a *reason*, there will be—"

"Sera, sit down, you've gone white as whey—"

She found it four pages back, because of course it was earlier than the daughter's erasure; the collection preceded the cleanup, cause preceded effect, even the forgetting had an order of operations. Commission 2,207, autumn of the same year. Her eyes moved down the entry and her heart moved down with them, step by step, into water that got deeper and darker until it closed over her head.

*Maren Vane, keeper-hedge-witch of Fenmarsh, retired. Collected alive per standing arrangement with the Office of Interior Wards. Goods delivered: her memories of the Emberwright favor, the true terms of the dragon-fire dimming, and the identity of the guild clerk who brokered it. Payment: as agreed. Disposition of subject: transferred north, archive intake. Viable.*

*Viable.*

Alive. The word hit her sideways, from an angle she hadn't braced for, because she had spent fifteen years grieving a woman who had, apparently, walked into a wagon under her own power — or been made to, or been paid to, or been *arranged*, the word was *arrangement*, as if her mother were a market stall. Transferred north. Archive intake. Somewhere in the capital, in whatever vault fed the crown its nightly meal, her mother's memories sat in glass. Perhaps her mother sat too. *Disposition of subject: viable.*

"Sera," Tam said again, and this time he had gotten up despite his ribs and put a hand on her shoulder, and she realized she was shaking the ledger the way you shake a person who won't wake.

"He knew," she said. "August."

"No. Sera, no — listen to me — he audits the front ledger. This thing was hidden in his own bag—"

"In *his bag*." She set the book down with exaggerated gentleness, because if she did not be gentle with it she would throw it into the fire, and some part of her, the part that was still a keeper, still a witch, understood that this book was the most dangerous object in Marrowgate and must not burn until it had been read to its last line. "Who carries a bag for fifteen years and doesn't know every stitch of it? Who audits a guild for a living and never asks what the second column spends?" She heard her own voice rising and could not stop it, and hated that too, because Ivet stirred in the loft and Brenna looked up from the boot she was mending, and grief with an audience curdles into performance. She lowered it. "I don't blame him. That's the worst of it, Tam. I want to and I can't. He's been robbed same as me, probably worse, and he doesn't even know what was taken. But the guild — the guild he loves, the guild he *keeps* —"

"—sold your mother to the crown," Tam finished quietly. "Yes."

Brenna had risen without a word. Now she took the candle and turned the wick up, and light flooded back into the room, and Sera saw the ledger properly for the first time: not a book but an inventory, not a history but a warehouse manifest, decades of stolen lives shelved and numbered and *confirmed clean*. Her whole life she had believed forgetting was weather — something that happened to people, wind and rot and time. It wasn't weather. It was commerce. Somebody had sat at a desk like August's desk and ruled a line beneath her mother's name and written *delivered* in a fair round hand.

"I need to see the rest of it," she said. "All of it. Tonight, before he's back."

"He'll help you," Brenna said. She was young, Ysolde's heir, steady-handed; she had carried fire in her cupped palms across half a mountain range. "You know he'll help. He's not them."

"He's the last person who can help," Sera said. "Because he'll want to protect me from the worst pages. And because if the guild comes looking for this book — and they will, Brenna, they kept it hidden in his satchel precisely because he travels, because moving things are harder to find and easier to lose — then whoever holds it becomes a target, and I will not watch them take anything else from anyone at this table." She drew a breath that tasted of tallow-smoke and rain coming under the door. "So tonight it's mine. I'm the reader. That's the gift. Everyone forgets it's a weapon too."

They let her. Tam went back to the door with his fists unclenched now, jaw set, a wall in miniature even sitting down. Brenna trimmed the wick. Above, Ivet murmured through some new dream, and Sera sent a silent apology upward — *not tonight, little one, don't dream my mother tonight, I couldn't bear to hear her name in a child's scream* — and turned back to the ledger.

She read until the candle burned down to a thumb's width and the rain stopped and the sky over Marrowgate stayed black and starless, as it had every night since the wards fell, a ceiling instead of a heavens. She read the commissions for erased ballads and erased borders, erased heirs and erased debts, erased witnesses, erased midwives, erased a whole island fishing-culture because its rights-of-way annoyed a duke. She read the payments — always *consideration, ongoing*, always vague, always pointing like a compass needle toward one word that appeared in the margins more and more often as the years went on, in the regent's countersigning hand, a single italicized term that the clerks never expanded upon:

*the Patron.*

She read the final entries, dated within the month just past. Commissions rushed through in the days surrounding the fall of the border wards. One stopped her cold enough that she read it aloud, twice, to be sure:

*Autumn, Year 357. Urgent, per the Patron's instruction. To erase from guild records and from the memory of the undersigned clerk all knowledge of the following persons: apprentices of the Guild of Impossible Things, capital chapter, intake years 340–344. Disposition of subjects: various. Payment: forgiven.*

*Forgiven.* Forgiven what? She flipped forward. There was nothing — the ledger ended there, mid-page, the ruled columns running empty to the bottom like a road stopping at a cliff. Apprentices, intake years 340 to 344. She did the arithmetic slowly, the way you walk out onto ice. August would have been a boy in the capital in those years. August, who remembered flour on his mother's hands and almost nothing else before the guild. August, who had said, laughing awkwardly into the fire, *I suppose I was always theirs.*

They had erased his fellow apprentices. All of them. Around him, in the years he was small and learning his letters beside them, and then erased the erasing, so that no one noticed the empty desks, the empty beds, the empty names. So that he would grow up in a dormitory that felt merely sparse, a life that felt merely lonely, a loyalty that felt merely earned.

*Various,* the ledger said, of what had become of them.

Sera closed the book. The hasp clicked shut with a sound like a small bone breaking, and in the silence after it she heard hoofbeats on the wet cobbles outside — one horse, moving fast, then slowing at their street. Tam straightened at the door. Brenna pinched out the candle, and the iron-grey ledger sat on the table like a heart that had been cut out of something and was somehow still beating.

The knock, when it came, was August's knock — three, pause, two, the ferryman's contact signal he'd taught them all. Tam looked at her. Brenna looked at her. Sera slid the ledger off the table and into her own cloak, feeling its weight settle against her hip like something she would carry now for the rest of her life, and nodded once at the door.

She had decided, in the dark, exactly what she would say to him.

What she had not decided — what she discovered only when she opened the door and saw his face, pale as paper, rain streaming off his hair, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe as if the world had tilted under him — was what to do when he said, before she could speak a single word:

"Sera. Something followed me from the river. Someone's asking after a second ledger." His eyes went to her cloak, to the weight against her hip, and widened. "They described it. They described it exactly."
