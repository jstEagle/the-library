## 9. What August Cannot Remember

The rain came down on the road to Marrowgate like an apology no one had asked for, and August Underhill walked through it with his hood up and his hand never far from the hilt of a sword that hadn't spoken in three days.

He told himself it was sulking. Swords did that sometimes — went quiet after hard use, the way a throat goes hoarse. He'd carried the blade for eleven years and it had spoken perhaps two hundred times: a word here, a warning there, once — memorably, on the night the stars went out — a single syllable that sounded like *no*. But since he'd fled the burning guildhall, since the sealed page and the riders in royal black, the sword had been silent as river stone.

"You could at least tell me if I'm walking the wrong way," he said to it.

The sword said nothing. The rain kept falling, patient as a creditor.

Sera walked ahead of him, her hedge-witch's bundle of charms clinking faintly under her oilcloth. She had not spoken much either. She was still turning over what she'd read on the sealed page, he knew — the wards anchored to a living king's memory, the realm's forgetting fed nightly from a throne. She kept touching the satchel where the page rode, the way a woman touches a wound to check if it's still bleeding.

"The ford's ahead," she said without looking back. "There'll be a toll-house. Or there was. Whether anyone remembers manning it is another question."

"Ledgers remember," August said. It was an old comfort, an old creed. "Even when people don't."

She glanced back at him then, and in the grey light her face had something in it he couldn't name — pity, maybe, or its close cousin. "That's what worries me about you, Underhill."

* * *

They made the ford at dusk, and that was where the hunters found them.

Later, August would try to reconstruct those minutes and find them full of holes, but the shape of it was this: the toll-house stood open and gutted, its roof fallen in, and Sera went first because she always went first into ruins, and August followed because following her had become a habit he didn't examine too closely. And in the dark interior, among the wet char and the collapsed beams, three figures rose out of the shadows like ink rising through water.

Royal black. Visored helms with no sigil — the crown's hunters wore no sigil now, which itself was a kind of signature. The one in front held a long hooked blade; the other two carried nets weighted with cold iron rings, the kind used for catching oath-bearers whole.

"Swordspeaker," the lead hunter said. The voice was muffled by the visor and yet somehow amused. "The Guildmaster sends regards. You've been carrying things that aren't yours."

August drew.

Eleven years of drawing. His body knew the motion the way it knew breathing, and the oath-line woke in him the way it always woke — that pressure behind the breastbone, that gathering of words that were older than words, the sense that the blade was a door and something on the other side was leaning against it, waiting to be let through.

*Speak*, he thought at it. *Speak for me.*

The sword said nothing.

The silence was absolute. Not sulking, not hoarseness, not the tired quiet of an overworked tool. It was the silence of a door with nothing behind it anymore. August felt the pressure in his chest build and build and go nowhere, like a wave breaking against a wall that had been quietly removed, and for one terrible heartbeat he stood in the ruined toll-house holding a length of honest steel that was only steel, and he understood — in the body, before the mind caught up — that something had been taken out of him.

Then the net came down.

* * *

Cold iron doesn't hurt, exactly. What it does is *insist*. The rings pressed against his shoulders and back and every one of them insisted: *you are ordinary, you are ordinary, you are ordinary.* The words in his chest guttered like a candle in a draft. Sera was shouting somewhere — he heard glass break, smelled burnt rosemary, one of her charms detonating in a flare of green fire — and one of the hunters reeled away clutching a smoking arm. But the lead hunter moved with terrible economy, hooked blade flicking, and August, net-bound and wordless, did the only thing left to a man who is only a man: he fought dirty.

He drove his shoulder into the hunter's knee. They went down together in the wet char. The hook opened a line of fire along his scalp. He got a thumb into the visor's slit and pushed, and the hunter made a sound like a startled dog and rolled off him, and then Sera was there with her knife at the man's throat and her other hand blazing green, and she said, very calmly, in the voice she must have used on sick animals and dying neighbors:

"Drop it. All of it. Now."

Iron rings clattered on stone.

The lead hunter knelt in the wreckage, disarmed, unhelmeted — and young, August saw with a shock. Nineteen, twenty. A farm-boy's jaw under the pallor. He looked up at them not with fear but with something worse: recognition.

"I know you," the young hunter said. "You're the one from the poster. *Underhill.* They said—" and then he stopped, and his brow furrowed, and August watched him reach for something and fail to find it. "They said you were... I had it a moment ago. Why can't I—"

"Get out," Sera said.

The young hunter scrambled up, gathered his companions' weapons, hesitated at the broken doorway with the rain silvering behind him. He turned once more.

"They paid us in memories," he said, as if confessing to a priest. "Did you know that? For hunting ledger-keepers. Whole years of them. Good ones." His voice cracked. "I can't remember why I wanted any of it."

Then he was gone into the dark, and the rain closed over the road like water closing over a stone.

* * *

They didn't make a fire. Sera bound August's scalp by touch and charm-light, her fingers quick and impersonal, and all the while the question sat in him growing teeth.

His sword hadn't spoken. Not refused — *couldn't*. He had felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth: not the pain of it, but the wrongness of the space where it lived. And the young hunter had looked at him with recognition and reached for a reason and found a hole.

August sat with his back against the toll-house wall and took out the sealed page.

He had read it a dozen times since the night the stars went out. The wards were bound to King Aldric's living memory. The king had none left to give. Something ancient wore the crown and fed. Standard horror, well-documented, the guild's own hand confirming what the darkened sky already screamed.

But there was a line near the bottom. He had read past it every time, the way you step over a loose floorboard in a house you trust.

*Inventory note, appended by the Second Ledger: disbursements from the royal account include, inter alia, one (1) swordspeaking bond, Underhill line, matured and collected in full. See archive.*

*Matured and collected in full.*

He read it now in Sera's charm-light, and the floorboard gave way beneath him.

The Underhill line. His line. The last oath-bound swordspeakers in the realm, and their bond — the thing in his blood, the thing that let his sword speak, the inheritance eleven generations deep — listed as a disbursement. Collected. Paid out. Which meant someone had sold it, and someone had bought it, and the transaction had passed through the guild's books with the tidy precision August himself applied to jars of borrowed courage and phials of bottled thunder.

And when had it been sold? When had he—

He tried to remember learning the oath. That was the natural place to start. Every swordspeaker remembered the day the bond woke; it was the hinge of a life, the story you told at fires until everyone begged you to stop.

August reached for it and found smooth, featureless dark.

Not forgetting. Forgetting has edges — you feel around the hole, you know the shape of what's gone. This was different. This was a room in his memory that had been furnished so carefully with plausible things — a master's voice saying *the bond comes late in some lines, boy, be patient*; a winter of practicing forms alone; a quiet pride when the sword first whispered — that he had never once thought to look behind the furniture. Someone had built him a childhood around an absence and he had dusted it gratefully for thirty years.

He started counting the gaps and could not stop.

His mother's face — a warm blur above a hearth, no features he could swear to. The city he'd grown up in — he knew he'd grown up somewhere before the guild, and the somewhere had the texture of a painting seen through fogged glass. His first year at the guildhall: present, vivid, complete. Everything before it: a corridor of doors painted on a wall.

"Ivet asked me once," Sera said quietly. She had settled across from him, knees drawn up, watching him read. "Back at the shrine. She asked why you never talk about before the guild. She says children see holes better than we do. Nobody's taught them to walk around them yet."

"What did you tell her?"

"That it was grief." Sera's mouth twisted. "It's what I tell myself about my mother. Grief is kinder than theft."

"It's not grief." His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "It's an invoice. Somewhere in the capital there's a shelf with my life on it, filed and labeled, and somebody drew against it. My oath. My mother's face. Whatever city raised me." He laughed, and hated the sound. "You know the worst part? I audited that ledger for eleven years. I checked every entry against every receipt. I was proud of how clean the books were. Clean books, my old master used to say, mean honest hands." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "My hands were the honest ones. I just never counted myself among the assets."

"You couldn't have known."

"No," he agreed. "That's rather the point of erasure, isn't it? It's the only crime where the victim certifies the books." He looked at her. "But here's what keeps circling, Sera, and I want you to hear it plainly, because you deserve it plainly: the guild sold my bond to the crown. Before the wards were ever built on Aldric's memory, or after, I don't know — but the crown paid, and the guild delivered, and the delivery was *me*. And I spent eleven years keeping those accounts. Polishing them. Defending them to anyone who called the guild a den of thieves." He shook his head slowly. "I wasn't the loyal servant of a guilty institution. I was the guilty institution's best argument for itself."

Sera was silent a long moment. Rain ticked in the ruins. Somewhere far off, a wolf began to sing — a long, rising note, thin as wire.

"When we get to Marrowgate," she said at last, "and you find your heirs of the oath-lines — what will you tell them? About the cost?"

"The truth. That oaths are currency and someone's always trading yours while you sleep."

"And your sword?"

August looked down at the blade across his knees. Honest steel. Dead steel. He thought about the pressure he'd felt in the toll-house — the gathering of words going nowhere — and understood it differently now. The bond hadn't died. It had been *collected*, moved to some vault, some shelf. Which meant it existed. Which meant it could, in theory, be returned.

Or it meant the thing that spoke through his sword had been sold to the crown too, and was at this moment wearing a royal harness, speaking in someone else's service, and he would meet his own stolen voice across a battlefield someday, wearing black.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I know someone who might. There's an archivist in Marrowgate — half-legend, even by our standards — who claims to keep records the guild doesn't know exist. A second ledger. If the guild sold me, there's a bill of sale. Bills of sale name buyers." He stood, wincing, and slung the satchel across his chest. "And they name sellers."

Sera rose with him. In the doorway of the ruin, the rain had thinned to mist, and beyond it the road south ran black toward the hills, toward Marrowgate, toward whatever version of himself was waiting in the guild's hidden columns.

"One more thing," she said, as they stepped out. "The young hunter. He said they paid him in memories. Years of them." Her eyes found August's in the dark. "Whose years, do you think? Where does a crown get memories to spend?"

August had no answer. But as they walked, he found himself listening to the wolves again — that long rising note, doubled now by another voice, a third — and thinking, with a calm that frightened him more than panic would have, that the song sounded almost like a summons. Like something old in the hills had heard a bond break open, thirty miles away in a ruined toll-house, and was calling across the dark to ask:

*Who let this one go loose?*

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, the last of the cold iron rings lay in the char of the toll-house floor — and etched into its underside, small and precise as everything the crown touched, was a single stamped mark: the seal of the Guild of Impossible Things.
