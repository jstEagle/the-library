## 10. Marrowgate Stands

The wall woke before Tam did.

That was the first thing no one had warned him about — that an oath-line did not sleep when its bearer slept. It lived in his marrow like a second heartbeat, and in the grey hour before dawn, as the royal vanguard came down out of the hills with torches banked and banners furled, it began to hum against his ribs like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

He lay on the cot in the gatehouse of Marrowgate and felt every one of them arrive. Two hundred riders, maybe more, fanning out along the town's northern approach. The oath translated them into pressure: each soldier a small weight settling onto his chest, each horse a heavier one, each iron-shod hoof a knock against a door he had sworn to keep shut. He had held walls for an hour once, for a night during the retreat at Callow Ford. He had never tried to hold one for days.

He got up because lying down made it worse. The floorboards took his weight grudgingly, as if he were asking them to carry two men.

Below the window, Marrowgate was stirring. Lamplight in the fish-smoke lanes. The clatter of the market square being cleared, awnings folded, carts dragged into barricades. A free town with no lord to protect it and no garrison worth the name — just militia with pikes older than they were, and a mayor who had spent the whole night arguing that surrender was the sensible course until Sera Vane had stood up in the council chamber and said, very quietly, *they will not let you remember having surrendered*, and the argument had died there, in the cold air of what everyone knew was true.

Tam dressed. His hands shook, which was new. He flexed them until the shaking stopped, or mostly stopped.

In the common room below, August Underhill was already awake, seated at the long table with the sealed ledger page spread before him and four more pages fanned beside it, reading by candlelight with the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he does not want the answer to. He looked up when Tam came down. His eyes went first to Tam's hands.

"How bad?"

"Two hundred," Tam said. "Give or take. Cavalry, some foot behind them. They'll want the gates intact, so they'll try the ram before they try fire." He lowered himself onto the bench; even that cost him something now, the oath registering every strain on the man who bore it. "I can hold the north wall through today. Maybe tomorrow if I sleep between waves."

"And after?"

"After, we find out what happens when a Broadfoot's bones run out of patience."

August folded the pages away into his satchel, unhurried, and Tam realized the man had already decided something. There was a quality to guild-keepers when their ledgers balanced — a settledness. "Then we don't spend you all at once," August said. "We hold the wall only while it matters, and we make it matter."

"Matters how? We're six people and a child."

"We're six people and a child the crown has sent two hundred soldiers to kill." August rose, shouldering the satchel. "They know what Ivet is. That makes this ground worth holding."

From the stair came the soft pad of small feet, and Ivet appeared, wrapped in a blanket too big for her, hair sleep-wild, her dream-eyes still half-full of whatever she'd been seeing. She looked at Tam the way she always did lately — not afraid of him, which unnerved him more than fear would have. Children were supposed to flinch from a man whose knuckles cracked like green wood whenever the wind picked up.

"They brought the tall ones," she said.

"The what?"

"The ones who eat oaths." She said it simply, the way she said everything now, names and horrors alike delivered without inflection, because she carried so many that none of them could be given special weight. "Not yet. They're coming behind. But they're coming."

Tam looked at August. August's face did the thing it did instead of going pale: it became very organized.

"Then we hold fast," August said, "and we hold briefly, and then we are gone over the south road before the eating begins." He glanced toward the window, where the sky was turning the color of old tin. "Marrowgate doesn't need us to win. It needs us to make the price high enough that the crown learns towns like this bite back."

* * *

They came at midmorning, and Tam learned what two hundred men weighed.

He stood on the walkway atop the north gate with his palms flat against the ancient stone, and when the first rank of cavalry put spurs to their horses, the oath took the charge into his body and gave it somewhere to go — down through his legs, into the wall, into the deep foundations laid by masons four hundred years dead who had never known they were building for him. The stone did not move. The horses broke like water on rock, screaming men tumbling, and Tam screamed too, silently, because the impact passed through him on its way into the masonry and for one white instant he understood exactly what a horse felt at full gallop meeting a wall.

His left radius cracked. He heard it — a sound inside himself, intimate as a snapped twig under snow — and kept his palms where they were.

The second wave came with a ram, a great larch trunk slung on chains, forty men walking it forward in rhythm. The oath sang through Tam like a plucked cable. Every blow of the ram against the gate arrived in his spine. He counted them. Eleven blows, twelve, and on the thirteenth something in his right knee gave with a grinding crunch that dropped him to one hand, still pressed to the stone, and the wall *held*, because he was touching it, because as long as a Broadfoot touched a wall and willed it whole, the wall was an extension of his refusal.

*You swore,* his grandfather's voice said in his memory, from a kitchen that no longer existed, in a house erased from every map. *A Broadfoot's word is mortar.*

*I'm aware,* Tam told his grandfather, and spat blood where he'd bitten his cheek, and pushed himself upright.

The ram withdrew. Arrows came instead, loosed in lazy arcs from beyond bowshot of the walls, and the militia raised their shields and Tam crouched behind the merlons shaking, both forearms splintered beneath the skin though nothing showed, the breaks living somewhere deeper than bone — in the oath itself, which was wound around his skeleton like ivy around a fence post and broke when he broke.

Sera found him like that when the arrows paused. She climbed the ladder with her hedge-witch's bag banging against her hip and knelt and took his wrist without asking, and he felt her magic slide along the fractures — not healing, nothing healed oath-breaks but rest, but easing, numbing, wrapping the pain in wool.

"You're killing yourself by inches," she said.

"I'm holding a wall by inches. Same thing." He managed something that was nearly a grin. "Go check on the boy-swordspeaker. He's been standing in the square for an hour looking like a man composing a letter."

"He has been." Sera tied off the numbness with a knot of murmured words. "And here's the strange part, Tam Broadfoot. An hour ago he told Mayor Corren the company stays until the siege lifts."

Tam stared at her. "He what?"

"Said the south road will still be there in three days. Said the Emberwrights' flame has burned thirty years; it can burn a fortnight longer. Said—" she paused, and her voice did something complicated, "—said that a man can't ask other people to keep faith with oaths while running from every bill that comes due."

Through the arrow-slit, Tam watched August Underhill in the square below, talking with the militia captain, pointing at the barricades, sleeves rolled, utterly ordinary, utterly unremarkable, a clerk among pikemen. The last oath-bound swordspeaker in a realm that had forgotten what oaths were, choosing to stand in a town that wasn't his, for people who weren't his, against soldiers wearing the black of a king neither of them had ever met.

"Well," Tam said. His cracked ribs ached when he laughed, so he did it anyway. "Damn me. The ledger-keeper's learning arithmetic."

* * *

They came again at dusk, and a third time past midnight, and between assaults Tam slept on the walkway itself, palm against the stone, because moving to a cot meant re-establishing contact meant fresh pain like a debt collected twice. Ivet brought him water. She sat beside him in the dark between the merlons and told him, quietly, the names of the defenders who had died in the day's fighting — a miller named Osk, a girl with a pike named Brenna who had stood her ground at the breach-gate — and saying them aloud seemed to steady her the way counting coins steadied other children.

"They'd be forgotten," she said. "If the crown wins. I have to say them now, while I'm sure."

"Say them," Tam said. "I've got them."

On the second night the tall ones came.

He felt them before he saw them — a wrongness in the pressure against his chest, soldiers who weighed *nothing*, whose absence of weight was worse than any mass. Seven figures in royal black walking ahead of the fourth assault wave, unhurried, carrying between them on a litter an engine like a harp the size of a cart, strung with wires that gleamed wet in the torchlight. Where they passed, the militia's courage audibly drained; he heard men begin to mutter old grievances, forget orders mid-word, stare at their own hands.

And Tam felt the wires begin to drink.

It started as an itch in the mortar of his teeth. Then the wall itself grew *thirsty* — the oath humming in him suddenly pulled taut like a rope over a pulley, hauling something out of him and out of the stone, feeding it upward into those wet gleaming strings. He tasted his grandfather's kitchen. He tasted the retreat at Callow Ford. He tasted his own name, going thin—

"No," Tam said aloud, to the engine, to the night, to whatever ancient bargain had built such a thing. "This wall isn't yours to drink."

He did the thing every Broadfoot swore never to do lightly: he *leaned*. Not held — leaned, threw his whole failing weight of will into the stone, and the wall answered him the way a loyal dog answers a master falling, surging up against the pull. The nearest wire snapped with a report like a musket. A tall one staggered. The engine shrieked a note that cracked shutters along the street below.

And Tam Broadfoot's left femur broke.

He went down behind the merlons, white-eyed, breath whistling, and still — still — he reached up and kept his hand flat against the parapet, because the assault wave was coming and the gate was behind him and there was no one else. He heard Sera shouting his name. He heard August's voice, clear and carrying across the square, calling orders in a language that was not quite language — the swordspeech, the old binding-tongue, ragged and half-remembered and *working*, the barricades rearranging themselves, a chain hoisting a cauldron of burning oil above the breach-gate as though the metal itself had decided to serve.

The assault broke against the wall a fourth time. And a fifth. And sometime before dawn, horn calls sounded from the royal camp — not the advance, but the recall — and the pressure against Tam's chest began, slowly, to lift.

He was still conscious when they carried him down. He remembered Ivet taking his broken hand in both of hers, careful as a bird-handler, and saying names into his ear — his grandfather's name, his mother's, names he hadn't spoken in years, names he'd thought were gone — giving them back to him like coins returned from a thief who'd found he couldn't spend them.

"You kept the wall," she said. "So I keep you."

* * *

Three days later, the siege lifted entire. Word came by pigeon and by fleeing peddler both: the crown's column was recalled south at speed, all haste, no explanation — summoned back toward the capital by some urgency greater than one stubborn free town. The tall ones and their oath-eating engine went with them, and Marrowgate's militia cheered from the walls like men who had personally defeated an empire.

Tam sat propped in the gatehouse doorway with his leg in Sera's woven splints and watched August pack in the yard below — methodical, economical, the satchel squared away, the ledgers wrapped in oilcloth. The company would leave within the hour. South. Toward the shrine, the guttering flame, the weeks-not-months that had become rather fewer weeks.

August paused in his packing and looked up, and caught Tam watching him.

"You stayed," Tam called down. His voice was rougher than he intended.

August considered the accusation. "The road was there when I needed it," he said finally. "It seemed poor manners not to return the courtesy." He swung the satchel up. "Can you travel?"

"I can lean on a horse and complain. It's practically my family trade."

"Good." August hesitated, and then said, in the tone of a man entering something into a ledger whether or not there was a page for it: "For what it's worth — the wall held because you held. Whatever else gets written about these days, that line goes in true."

He turned to go, and it was then that Ivet came running across the yard, barefoot, breathless, her eyes enormous — not with nightmare this time, Tam saw, but with something else entirely, something that had her glowing like a lamp.

"I dreamed the shrine," she said, skidding to a stop in front of August, gripping his sleeve. "I dreamed the mountain. And August — the flame's not weeks." Her voice dropped, small and terrible. "It's days. Someone lit something in the capital, and the fire felt it, and it's *hurrying to die*."
