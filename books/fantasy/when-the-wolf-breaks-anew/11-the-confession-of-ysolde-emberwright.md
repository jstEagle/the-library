## 11. The Confession of Ysolde Emberwright

Ysolde Emberwright had kept her death waiting in the doorway for three days now, and it was beginning to sulk.

She felt it the way she felt everything lately — as a coldness at the edge of her hands, a dimming behind her eyes that no tallow candle could argue with. The shrine of the Emberwrights had seen nine generations of keepers born beneath its soot-black rafters, and it would see her die there too, among the copper braziers and the ledgers of flame, while the last fire of the dragons guttered in its iron cradle like a sick man breathing.

But not yet. Not before she confessed.

The strangers had come up the mountain out of a world gone starless — a swordspeaker with hollow eyes, a hedge-witch who flinched at her own name, a deserter whose bones cracked audibly when he laughed, and a child who woke screaming names. They had asked their questions gently, as pilgrims do, but Ysolde was old enough to know that gentle questions were still knives. *How long has the fire been failing? Why does it fail? What can be done?*

And she had lied to them for two days. She could feel the lie sitting between them all at supper like an uninvited guest — August Underhill watching her across the brazier-light with the particular patience of a man who spent his life reading what people wrote down, including the things they didn't mean to say.

Tonight, then. Tonight she would open the third ledger.

* * *

Forty years ago — no. Sixty. Time moved strangely near dragon-fire; it always had; that was half the reason the Emberwrights lived apart — sixty years ago, Ysolde had been a girl of seventeen with singed eyebrows and her grandmother's temper, and she had stood exactly where August Underhill stood now, on the worn flagstone before the Fire Cradle, and watched her grandfather do the unforgivable thing.

She remembered it in pieces, the way old memories come back: not whole cloth but patches, stitched wrong.

*Her grandfather, Maren Emberwright, kneeling before the Cradle with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, his forearms bare and marked with the keeper's brands — seven lines, one for each generation sworn to the flame.*

*A stranger standing in the shrine doorway. That was the part she could never make right in her mind, no matter how many times she turned it over. The stranger wore grey. Plain grey, travel-worn, the clothes of nobody. And yet every candle in the shrine leaned toward him as he entered, every one, flames bending like wheat in wind, and the great fire itself — the dragon-fire, which had never once flickered in Ysolde's lifetime — drew back from him the way a dog draws back from a man who has kicked it before.*

*Her grandfather's voice, steady as a ledger line:* "The Guild of Impossible Things acknowledges the debt. Speak the terms."

*And the stranger in grey smiling — she remembered the smile most of all, because it did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were not eyes at all if you looked too long but something older, something patient and hungry, a darkness with weather in it — and saying:* "Only this. Dim the flame. A little. Enough that I may pass unburned where I need to go. And when I am crowned—" 

*Crowned. He had said it then, sixty years before any crown sat on any head, said it casually, the way a man says* when I'm home for supper.

"—when I am crowned, your guild will look away. Once. Only once. And the Emberwrights will keep their fire small and obedient, and in exchange I will forget this mountain exists."

*Her grandfather had bowed his head. And Ysolde, seventeen years old, hidden in the smoke-shadows above the Cradle where children were not supposed to be, had understood with a clarity that would haunt her for six decades that her grandfather was not selling the fire.*

He was selling the world.

* * *

"You were there." August's voice came quiet across the brazier-light. "You saw it happen."

They had gathered in the inner shrine, the four of them plus Tam propped against the wall with his bandaged ribs, and Ysolde had brought out the third ledger — the one bound in salamander hide that no Emberwright opened more than once in a lifetime, because opening it meant either confessing or dying, and often both.

"I was there," she said. "Seventeen years old, hiding where my grandmother would have skinned me. I heard every word. And when my grandfather died, the duty of keeping the secret passed to me, the way the fire passed to me, and I kept it. Forty years I kept it after he was ash. I told myself the bargain was done, sealed, harmless. The stranger had his favor. The guild looked away once. The fire burned on." Her hands shook around the ledger's cover. "Then thirty years ago the stars began going out at the borders. Then the wards began to thin. Then, three nights past, the sky went black as a dead hearth, and I knew. I knew what 'once' had purchased. It purchased everything."

Sera Vane sat very still, the way she always did when memory was being handled in her presence — as though other people's pasts might spill on her and stain. "What was he?" she asked softly. "The stranger in grey. What did your grandfather think he was?"

"My grandfather thought he was a devil. My grandmother thought he was a god. They were both wrong, and both right, which is usually how it goes." Ysolde opened the ledger. The pages inside were not paper; they were thin sheets of hammered copper, and the writing on them had been done in fire, each letter seared permanent by a dying dragon's breath. "He called himself nothing. But my grandfather wrote down what the fire called him, afterward, in the days when the flame still spoke to keepers plainly." She traced a line of scorched script with one trembling finger, not quite touching it. "It called him *the Crown Unworn.* A hunger that had eaten kingdoms before ours and worn them until nothing was left, and then slept, coiled under mountains, waiting for someone foolish enough to give it a shape again. A king's body is a fine shape. A king's memory is a finer meal."

August had gone the color of old bone. Ysolde watched him — she had spent seventy years watching fire, she knew how living things burned, and she could see this man burning from the inside now, quietly, terribly.

"The guild," he said. It wasn't a question. His voice sounded like a man reading his own death warrant aloud to check for clerical errors. "My guild. The Guild of Impossible Things made a bargain with — with the thing that is wearing King Aldric."

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Ysolde said, "made a bargain with it *generations* before Aldric was born. Understand that, boy. This is not a sin of your masters' youth. It is the foundation stone of the house. Your guild was built on that favor. Every impossible thing it has ever brokered, every miracle it keeps in jars and cages and ledgers — all of it rests on the one time it sold the light of the world for the price of looking away." She let that sit in the smoky air. "Your guild is not a victim of the crown, August Underhill. It is a co-signer."

* * *

She read to them from the copper pages, because some truths are too heavy to speak freehand and must be quoted, weighed, certified.

"*In the year the comet wept,*" she read, "*Maren Emberwright, Seventh Keeper of the Last Flame, did bind the Cradle to a lesser burning. By the stranger's term the dragon-fire shall burn no brighter than a forge-flame, shall warm no farther than these walls, shall wake no wing nor voice nor wrath. In exchange the stranger swears by his own hunger — and his hunger is older than his name — that the Emberwrights and their mountain shall be forgotten by him forever.*"

"Dimmed deliberately," Tam said hoarsely from the wall. His face was grey with pain and greyer with disgust. "Not dying. *Crippled.* On purpose."

"Not merely dimmed." Ysolde turned another copper page, and here the fire-writing changed character — angrier strokes, letters gouged rather than written, her grandfather's hand losing its composure as the years advanced. "Read the margin. Go on, hedge-witch, you're closest."

Sera leaned forward. Ysolde watched her lips move over the scorch-marks, and watched the blood leave her face.

"It says — *the flame remembers what it was,*" Sera read. "*Each year it burns smaller and hates us more. Each year it asks me why. I have begun to answer it.*"

"My grandfather answered it for eleven years," Ysolde said. "Do you know what a dragon-fire asks a keeper who has betrayed it? It doesn't rage. Dragons don't waste themselves on rage. It *grieves.* It showed him visions, every night, of what the world would lose — the wards unborn, the oaths unhung, the dark coming down like a lid. And on the eleventh year he walked into the Cradle." Her voice went flat and careful, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this sentence for forty years without ever once managing to make it hurt less. "The fire refused to take him. Refused. It carried him out and laid him on this floor and left him alive, which was crueller than burning, and he lived nine more years knowing exactly what he had bought with his honor. Nine years of answering the flame's question honestly. *Why. Why did you do it.*"

"Why did he?" August asked. It burst out of him almost involuntarily, and Ysolde heard what was underneath the question — not curiosity. Grief. The grief of a man watching the floor of his life crack open. "What could the guild have needed so badly? What favor—"

"That is the last page," Ysolde said. "And it is the one I have dreaded showing you, boy, because I think it will kill something in you that you will want to keep."

She turned the final copper sheet and held it toward the brazier-light, and read it herself, because she would not ask anyone else to carry the sound of it.

"*The favor was a forgetting,*" she read. "*The guild had made an error beyond mending. An oath had been sworn that should never have been sworn, a binding so dangerous that the guild's own founders wept over it, and it could not be undone by any art the guild possessed. So the guild sought out the one power that eats anything — even truth, even the past — and bargained. Look away once, when I come wearing a king. And I will eat the oath entire. Root and branch. Witness and word.*"

She lowered the page.

"They fed the world to the wolf," August whispered, "to save themselves from one mistake."

"They fed the world to the wolf," Ysolde agreed, "and the wolf took payment in advance, and has been collecting interest for sixty years. That is the architecture of it, boy. See it whole. The guild dimmed the only fire that could destroy the thing it had invited in. The guild erased the only knowledge that could warn the realm. And when the thing finally came wearing Aldric's skin, thirty years past — well." She smiled, and hated her own mouth for it. "The guild looked away. Once. As promised. Have you never wondered why your Guildmaster's records of the coronation year are so thin? Why the sealed archives go silent for exactly that season, like a held breath?"

August made a sound that was not a word.

"And there is worse, and you will have it all, because I am dying and I will not leave this debt half-paid." Ysolde closed the copper ledger with both hands, as if shutting a coffin. "The bargain had one escape clause. One. My grandfather carved it into the binding himself, in defiance, the night before the stranger's terms were sealed — a flaw planted in the contract like a splinter under skin. The crown cannot be unmade while the flame is dimmed. But the flame can be *rekindled.* Not by keepers. We are bound; our hands are forfeit; the moment an Emberwright feeds the Cradle past its leash, the bargain breaks and the stranger is freed to come collect this mountain first. No. The rekindling must come from outside. From oath-blood. From those who never signed."

Every eye in the shrine turned — to Tam, broken-boned and oath-sworn; to Sera, who carried other people's memories like stolen goods; to Ivet, asleep upstairs and dreaming names.

To August.

"The last oath-bound swordspeaker," Ysolde said, "walks into my shrine carrying dragon-quest in his pockets, and tells me he never wondered why the fire was dying." She shook her head slowly. "Either the guild believed you too loyal to ever turn on it, or it believed you too broken. Perhaps both. Perhaps that is the cruellest entry in the whole ledger: they sent the one man who could undo everything, dressed in chains they thought he'd never see."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone — Tam limping, Sera walking like a woman with a ghost's hand between her shoulders — August remained. He knelt by the Fire Cradle itself, close enough that the guttering flame threw its weak gold across his face, and Ysolde watched him the way she had once watched her grandfather watch it: with pity, and dread, and the terrible tenderness one feels for someone standing on ice that is already singing.

"It spoke to me earlier," August said, not turning. "When we arrived. I thought it was just — heat, or imagination. But it said a word. It said *Underhill.*"

The cold in Ysolde's chest, the death-cold she had been politely ignoring for three days, spread suddenly through her ribs.

"Fire remembers," she managed. "That is the first lesson of keeping and the last. It remembers everyone who has ever stood on that flagstone."

"Has someone named Underhill stood here?" He turned then, and his eyes in the firelight were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a hole in his own history, hearing water at the bottom. "Because I don't remember. I should remember, shouldn't I? There's a gap — there have always been gaps — and I told myself it was grief, or age, or the road, but your ledger says the guild erases witnesses, it eats oaths root and branch, witness and word — Ysolde. *Was I here?*"

The old keeper looked at the last swordspeaker, at the last hope of the world kneeling in the ashes of her family's shame, and found that the truth she had carried sixty years was not yet done giving birth.

"Before I answer that," Ysolde said, "you should know that my grandfather's ledger has one more margin-note. The last thing the fire ever told him." She reached out, and with fingers gone steady at last — the steadiness of the already-lost — she touched the guttering flame, and it bent toward her like a grieving hound, and she gave it the words it had waited three generations to have spoken aloud:

"It said: *the witness was here. The witness will return. Ask him what he saw.*"

The flame leapt — a hand's breadth, the tallest it had burned in thirty years — and in its light Ysolde watched August Underhill's face come apart, quietly, like a seal breaking on a letter no one was meant to read.

Above them, far to the south, the black sky hung empty where the stars had been. And somewhere in that dark, something ancient turned its attention toward a mountain it had promised to forget.
