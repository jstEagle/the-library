## 12. Ivet Dreams the Old Names

The dreams began on the third night out of Marrowgate, in a shepherd's barn with a leaking roof and a smell of old wool that clung to everything like a second skin.

Ivet had not wanted to sleep. She was nine years old, and she had learned in the last month that sleep was a door, and doors, in her experience, opened onto things. But Tam had carried her on his shoulders through the last mile of the pass because her feet were bleeding inside boots two sizes too big, and Sera had washed the blood off with water warmed over the fire, and August had sat beside her while she ate, saying nothing, which she had decided was his way of being kind. You could not refuse people who did things like that. It would be like refusing the sun.

So she slept.

And she dreamed of a village called Hallowmere.

She had never heard of Hallowmere. She knew this the way you know your own hands — absolutely, without needing proof. In the dream she stood at the edge of it, in a wheat field gone gold and heavy-headed, and the village lay before her with its crooked chimneys and its geese in the lane, and every single person in it turned to look at her at once, and every single one of them said her name as if they had been waiting for her all their lives.

Not *Ivet*. They said something else. Something long and warm and syllabled like a bell being struck underwater. And when they said it, Ivet understood — the way you understand things in dreams, whole and entire, no learning required — that this was what she was for. This was the oath-line running in her blood like a bright thread: not walls, not swords, not fire. *Remembering.*

She woke screaming.

* * *

Sera reached her first. That was how it always went now; Sera slept light as a bird, one ear open for children crying, and she came across the barn floor with her braid half-undone and gathered Ivet up before the dream had even finished draining out of the air. Tam lit a lamp. August stood in the doorway with his hand on his sword hilt, scanning the dark outside for hunters, and only when he saw there was nothing to fight did he come and sit down on the straw, slowly, like an old man.

"Tell us," Sera said. She said it every time now. This was the fourth night of dreams, though the first three had been small ones — a name whispered into a well, a road sign dissolving, a woman's voice singing a lullaby in a language none of them knew. "Tell us what you saw."

Ivet told them about Hallowmere. The wheat. The geese. The way everyone had turned at once.

"How many people?" August asked. His voice was careful, the way it got when he was doing sums in his head. He was always doing sums. She had watched him do them over the ledgers he kept in his satchel, columns of figures in a hand so neat it looked printed.

"I don't know. Lots. A hundred? Two hundred?"

"Hallowmere," August repeated. He said it strangely, tasting it. Then he reached into his satchel and drew out a ledger — not the great main one but a slimmer volume, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling — and wetted his thumb and turned pages by lamplight until he found what he was looking for. He read for a long moment. Then he closed the book very gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

"It's not in here," he said.

"What isn't?" Tam asked. He was sitting against the barn wall with his arms around his knees; since Marrowgate he moved carefully, favoring his left side, and his bones made a sound like river ice when he shifted wrong. Nobody mentioned it. That was another thing the family had learned — the things nobody mentioned.

"The village." August's thumb moved along the ledger's spine. "I've audited every page of this book twice. Every erased entry the crown ever paid for is recorded somewhere in the guild's holdings — I thought. Hallowmere isn't. Which means either my ledger is incomplete—"

"Or the erasure was complete," Sera said quietly.

Nobody answered that. The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the broken roof, righted itself, leaned again.

It was Ivet who broke the silence, because nine-year-olds have not yet learned that some silences should be left alone. "They said my name," she said. "But it wasn't Ivet."

Sera went very still.

"What was it?"

Ivet tried to hold onto it. It slipped like a fish. It had been long, and warm, and it had sounded like bells underwater, and it had been *hers*, more hers than the name she'd had at the foundling house, which was itself a name given by strangers who hadn't known what to call a child left in a doorway with no note. She squeezed her eyes shut and reached for it and felt it dissolve, and to her horror she felt tears come instead, hot and furious.

"I lost it," she said. "I just got it and I lost it."

"No." Sera took her face in both hands. Her palms smelled of yarrow and woodsmoke. "Listen to me. Names aren't lost. Not really. They're *kept* — sometimes by the wrong people, in the wrong places, but kept. And you—" her voice caught, steadied — "you are going to be the best keeper this realm has ever had. Do you hear me? Whatever they took, whatever they buried, you are going to dig it all back up again. Every name. Starting with mine."

* * *

It happened on the sixth night, in the shrine of the Emberwrights, high in the mountains where the air tasted of iron and snowmelt.

Ysolde had given them a cell near the fire-shrine itself, a stone room that was never quite warm and never quite cold, humming faintly at all hours with the presence of the last dragon-fire guttering in its iron cradle two chambers away. Ivet could feel it in her teeth, like a word held unspoken. The others had stopped noticing. She never would.

That night she dreamed of a woman kneading bread.

It was a small dream, domestic, ordinary — flour to the elbows, a cottage with geraniums on the sill, a cat asleep in a basket by the oven. The woman sang while she worked, tunelessly, a song about a fox and a goose. And Ivet stood in the corner of the kitchen knowing — with that whole-and-entire dream-knowledge — that this woman had been erased. Root and branch. Name and grave and the memory of her from every mind that ever held her. The crown had paid for it. The guild had done it. And the woman had a daughter who grew up not knowing her own mother's face, carrying a grief with no shape to it, like a coat cut for someone else.

The woman turned from her bread and looked at Ivet, and smiled, and said her name.

And this time Ivet did not lose it.

She woke with it whole in her mouth, blazing, and she did not scream this time. She got up in the dark, barefoot on cold stone, and walked down the corridor past Ysolde's cell and the ember-store and the chamber where the dragon-fire breathed its slow dying light through the seams of its iron door, and she pushed open the door of the room where Sera slept, and she stood over her, and she said:

"Maren."

Sera surfaced from sleep like a diver, slow and confused. "What? What is it, love, are you—"

"Maren Voss," Ivet said. "Of Thistledown Hollow. She baked bread on Thursdays and sold it at market on Fridays, and she sang about a fox and a goose, and she had a cat named Duchess who slept in a basket by the oven, and she grew geraniums because your father brought her the first one from a fair the year you were born, and she died in the winter of the gray fever, and they erased her three years after that because she had once hidden a ledger-keeper in her cellar during the Purge of the Marsh Roads, and hiding him was the most ordinary brave thing anyone ever did, and they erased her for it."

The words came out of Ivet steadily, in a voice that did not sound entirely like her own — older, or deeper, or simply *fuller*, a cup filled to the brim. She stood in the moonlight from the narrow window with her arms at her sides and her eyes open and shining, and she delivered Maren Voss back into the world name by name, detail by detail, the way you carry water from a well, one careful handful at a time.

Sera had gone white. Then grey. Then she made a sound Ivet had never heard a grown person make — a small, cracked noise, like a hinge on a door that hasn't been opened in thirty years — and then she was out of bed and on her knees on the cold stone floor with her arms around a nine-year-old girl, shaking, weeping without any sound at all, her whole body saying what her voice could not.

*Mama. Mama. Mama.*

Ivet held her head and let her.

After a long time Sera pulled back and looked at her, and her face was wrecked and transfigured at once, tear-tracked and lit from within. "Say it again," she whispered. "Please. Say her name again."

"Maren Voss."

"And again."

"Maren Voss. Of Thistledown Hollow."

Sera laughed then, wet and broken and real, and pressed her forehead against Ivet's, and stayed like that until the moon moved off the window and the light in the stone corridor turned the color of ash.

* * *

By morning the whole shrine knew.

Tam cried, which surprised no one and embarrassed him terribly; he blew his nose on a sleeve already stiff with old salt and said, gruffly, "Good. Good. That's — good," and then had to go outside and chop wood that didn't need chopping for an hour. August wrote the name in a new ledger — a slim one, unbound, pages loose between covers of plain board — and when Ivet asked him what it was, he showed her the heading on the first page, written in that printed-neat hand:

*A Ledger of Returned Names.*

"Not debts," he said. "Names. Everything you give back, we write down. So it can't be taken twice."

"You'll run out of pages," Ivet said.

August looked at her for a moment. Something moved behind his eyes — she was getting better at reading him, the flickers, the places where the man and the gaps in the man met — and then he said, "Then we'll get more pages."

Ysolde Emberwright came to her at midday, when the mountain light came flat and white through the shrine windows. The matriarch was old the way mountains are old, folded and weathered and patient, and she lowered herself onto the bench beside Ivet with the care of someone whose joints had opinions.

"My grandmother dreamed too," Ysolde said. "Before the fire dimmed. The Emberwrights kept the dreams of the dead — it was part of the keeping, once. We'd write them down and burn the pages in the shrine-fire so the names went up whole instead of rotting in the ground forgotten." She looked toward the iron door, behind which the last flame guttered. "We haven't been able to do it in generations. The fire won't take them anymore. Won't take much of anything, soon."

"I'm sorry," Ivet said, because it seemed like the thing to say.

"Don't be sorry." Ysolde's hand, dry and strong as bark, closed over hers. "Be *hungry*. That's what the line wants — I've read the old fragments, the ones the guild didn't find. Remembering isn't a gift, child. It's an appetite. It grows by feeding. Every name you speak makes room for ten more. Every grief you carry makes you strong enough to carry worse." Her grip tightened. "Which is why I need to ask you something hard, and I need you to answer me honestly."

Ivet nodded.

"When you dream tonight — and you will dream, and it will be worse than the woman with the bread, because the appetite has woken now — when you dream of the *big* ones, the villages, the roads, the whole towns paid into darkness like Hallowmere..." Ysolde's voice dropped low. "Can you bear it? Because a Rememberer who breaks becomes a hollow, and hollows are what the crown hunts easiest. I have seen what happens to keepers who take in more than their vessel holds. I need to know if you will tell us when it's too much. Or whether you'll be proud, and quiet, and break where we can't catch you."

Ivet thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything, turning it over like a stone to see what lived underneath.

"I'll tell Sera," she said at last. "When it's too much. Not because I'm not proud. Because she's my family now, and family means you don't get to break alone."

Ysolde laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut — and pressed Ivet's hand one final time. "Then the line chose well," she said, and rose, and went to tend her dying fire.

* * *

That night the dream was Hallowmere again, but this time Ivet did not stand at the edge of the wheat field. She walked down the lane between the crooked chimneys, and the geese parted before her, and the people came out of their houses and their fields and their graves — the graves too, opening gently, the dead sitting up in the dark earth with wheat in their hair — and they gave her their names, all of them, one by one, pressing them into her hands like loaves of warm bread.

There were two hundred and eleven.

She woke at dawn with all of them arranged in her mind in perfect rows, shelf upon shelf upon shelf, and she did not scream, and she did not break, and she did not feel hollow. She felt *vast*. She felt like a granary in autumn. She lay still in the straw and listened to the shrine hum its iron hum and the wind move over the mountain, and she waited for the fear to come, and it did not come, and in its place was something else — something steady and burning, kin to the dying fire two chambers away.

When the others woke, she was already sitting up, cross-legged, waiting.

"I have them," she said. "All two hundred and eleven. And I know where Hallowmere was — a day south of the Ashford crossing, under what's now a crown grain depot. And I know why they're not in August's ledger." She paused, and the vastness in her narrowed to a point, sharp as a needle. "Because Hallowmere wasn't erased by the crown."

August looked up sharply from his ledgers. "What?"

"They erased themselves," Ivet said. "Or — no. Someone erased them *for* them. A whole village, paying everything, so that one person could be hidden so thoroughly that even the erasure couldn't find her." She frowned, reaching, the dream shifting its weight. "A woman. Young. Carrying something. There was a box, and the box was important, and the village fed itself into the dark so the box would stay lost, and the woman went north, and—" the needle slipped, the dream closing like a hand — "and I almost have it. I almost have her name. Tonight. I'll have it tonight."

The barn — the shrine cell — had gone very quiet. Tam had stopped mid-motion, a heel of bread halfway to his mouth. Sera's hand had risen unconsciously to the satchel where her mother's name now lived written on a scrap of paper she never let leave her body. And August had gone pale in a way Ivet had never seen, whiter than ambush-pale, whiter than hunter-pale, holding his grey ledger so tightly the soft leather creaked.

"A box," he said. His voice came out strange. Flat. Careful. "Carried north. By a young woman."

"Yes," said Ivet. "Why? Do you know it?"

August did not answer at once. He looked down at the ledger in his hands as if seeing it for the first time — this book he had audited twice, this book he had trusted the way other men trust their own hearts — and when he finally spoke, his voice was barely above the hum of the shrine.

"Because I think," he said, "that I may have been the box."
