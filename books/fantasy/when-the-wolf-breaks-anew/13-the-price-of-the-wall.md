## 13. The Price of the Wall

Tam Broadfoot had never been a brave man. He wanted that understood, at least by himself, in the honest dark of his own skull where no one else could hear it. Bravery was a thing for men with something to prove. Tam had deserted an army and broken a betrothal and run from every fight that offered him a choice, and he had told himself for eleven years that this was wisdom, not cowardice — that a man who knew exactly how much he was worth did not gamble with the coin.

But then August Underhill had sat across a Marrowgate tavern table and told him what oaths were for, and Tam had felt the old wall-oath stir in his marrow like a dog hearing its name after years of silence, and he had said yes. Not because he was brave. Because for the first time since he'd walked away from the walls of Carrenholt and left them to burn behind him, saying yes had seemed easier than carrying the weight of having said no.

Now he stood on the eastern rampart of Marrowgate at dusk, and the hunters had come back.

They brought the engine up the old drover's road in pieces, on wagons drawn by teams of oxen whose eyes had been sewn shut with black thread. Tam watched through a gap in the parapet as forty men assembled it in the field below — a frame of pale wood that looked almost like scaffolding, almost like a gallows, rising joint by joint into something with the proportions of a bell tower built sideways, all mouth and no throat. At its center hung a lens of smoked glass the size of a cartwheel, and around the lens, in rings, were set hundreds of small iron hooks, each one holding a scrap of parchment, a knotted cord, a strip of cloth embroidered with names.

"Gods below," whispered Serl, the tanner who commanded the town's ragged watch. "What is it?"

Tam knew. He knew the way he knew the taste of blood in his mouth when he'd bitten through his cheek falling off a wall he'd sworn to hold. The oath-eater. He'd heard about them in his soldiering days — rumors only, stories told in low voices around fires, the kind of story you laughed at so you wouldn't have to believe it. A machine that didn't throw stones or fire. A machine that threw *want*. It found the binding between a person and their word, the golden thread of an oath-line, and it pulled, and it did not stop pulling until either the oath broke or the man did.

"It's for me," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "They've finally worked out what's been holding their siege."

* * *

The first night, nothing happened. The engine sat in the field like a patient animal, and the hunters made camp around it in perfect geometric silence, and Tam lay awake in the guardhouse listening to Ivet sleep-talk names three rooms away and wondering which one would be his.

The second night, they lit it.

Not with flame — with sound. A horn, deep and wrong, blown once from the heart of the machine, and Tam felt the note go through the stone of the rampart and into the stone of him, and somewhere beneath his ribs the wall-oath turned over in its sleep and *noticed*.

He was holding the east gate's inner bar at the time. He felt his hands tighten on the oak without his telling them to, felt his shoulders square, his boots settle, his whole body become a thing planted — because that was what the oath was, at its root. *I will hold.* Not I will fight, not I will win. Hold. And the horn's note slid along that root like a hook seeking purchase, and Tam gasped, and let go of the bar, and staggered back until his shoulder blades hit the wall behind him.

"Tam?" One of the watchmen — a boy, Fennick, fifteen and freckled. "You all right?"

"I'm fine." He wasn't. Something in his chest had begun to ache, low and constant, like a tooth that hadn't decided yet whether to rot. "Get some sleep, lad. Long day tomorrow."

There would be long days. That was the arithmetic of an oath-eater: it did not break you at once. It *pulled*, patiently, hour upon hour, and every hour you held against it cost you. Tam had seen men hold against things before. He had never been one of them. He had always, always found the door.

That was the joke of it, he thought, lying awake while the ache deepened by slow degrees. All those years of running, and in the end he'd volunteered for the one fight there was no walking away from. Because the wall was not just his gift. It was the town. Behind him slept four thousand people who had taken in a deserter and fed him and asked nothing, and behind *them*, further south than any of them could march in time, went August and Sera and a child full of screaming names and a jar of fire that was guttering out, and if the wall fell, all of it fell.

*I will hold.*

The ache answered him, sweetly, like a voice in a dream: *then hold.*

* * *

On the fourth day the pulling began in earnest, and Tam learned what his bones were for.

It started at dawn. The horn sounded — they'd learned its rhythm now, three notes at first light, three at dusk, and once at midnight to keep the wound from closing — and Tam climbed the east rampart because that was where the oath put him, the way water finds a channel. He took his place at the gate tower and set his palms flat against the stone, and when the note came he *held*, and the holding was —

He had no words for it, that first morning, and he was a man who talked too much when he was frightened, so the lack of words frightened him worse. It was like being a bridge. It was like being load-bearing. Every ounce of force the engine exerted on Marrowgate's walls came down through the oath into Tam's body, and his body distributed it the way a keystone distributes weight — outward, downward, into the earth. He could feel the engine's pull as pressure along his spine, his pelvis, the long bones of his legs. He could feel his teeth meeting, jaw locked, molars taking a share of the burden because the body shares everything among itself when the sharing is survival.

By noon his fingernails had gone white where they pressed the stone. By dusk there was blood in his boot from where the toenails of his right foot had lifted away from the bed. By midnight, when the third sounding came, Tam Broadfoot screamed — briefly, quietly, through closed lips, the scream of a man who has decided not to give the thing the satisfaction — and held anyway.

Sera found him at dawn, still standing, still touching the wall, and she did not ask him if he could stop. She was a hedge-witch; she could read the truth of a body the way August read a ledger. She knelt at his feet instead, and cleaned the ruined toes, and packed them with moss and a poultice that smelled like winter, and said, "Ysolde told me once that the Emberwrights kept a list. Of everyone the dragon-fire ever warmed. Your name isn't on it, Tam, but I'm writing it in myself. In my head. Where nobody can sell it."

"That's the stupidest comfort anyone's ever given me," Tam said.

"I know," said Sera. "I'm out of practice."

He laughed, and the laugh hurt, and holding hurt, and he held.

* * *

August came to him on the seventh day, when Tam's hair had gone grey at the temples — actually gone, not figuratively; he could see the strands when Sera tied his hair back from his sweating face — and sat down beside him on the rampart stones with the careful deliberateness of a man approaching a debt he intends to pay.

"You should know what I've worked out," August said. "About the engine."

"Tell me it can be burned."

"It can be burned. Everything can be burned. But we'd have to get to it, and the field between here and there is—" He stopped. Recalibrated. August always recalibrated; Tam had come to think of it as the man's version of blinking. "What I mean is, Ivet had a dream last night. About you. She dreamed your name being spoken by a wall, and the wall letting go of it, and she woke up crying. She thinks—"

"She thinks I'm going to die."

"She thinks you're going to *choose*," August said. "She's seven. Her dreams don't distinguish yet."

Tam looked out over the field. The engine stood black against the afternoon, its lens drinking light, its hooks heavy with other people's promises — he could see new scraps added daily, oaths bought or stolen from villages along the hunters' road, and the thought of all those little bindings hanging there like washing made him want to weep, though he couldn't have said whether from pity or recognition. "August. Can I tell you something? Since you're the confessor type."

"I'm the accountant type."

"Same thing, worse hours." Tam shifted his weight, and his hip ground audibly, and they both pretended it hadn't. "When I deserted Carrenholt — eleven years back — I told myself it was sense. Walls were falling everywhere, the war was lost, staying was death with extra steps. And maybe some of that was even true. But underneath it, the true true thing, was that I was afraid of what the oath would ask me to pay. I could feel it, even then, young as I was. I could feel that the line ran all the way down, and I didn't want to find out what was at the bottom of me." He flexed his fingers against the stone. They cracked, all ten, like green branches. "Well. Now I know."

"And?"

"And it's me," Tam said. "At the bottom. Just me. Turns out that was enough."

August was quiet a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and took out the ledger — the guild ledger, the sealed page still folded inside it like a splinter — and tore a blank leaf from the back, and wrote something on it with the stub of pencil he carried, and folded it, and tucked it into Tam's breast pocket, over the heart.

"What's that?"

"An entry," August said. "'Item: one wall, unbreakable, held seven days against an army. Value: beyond assessment. Status: owed.'" He stood, knees popping. "I keep accounts, Tam. When this is over, I intend to pay this one. So don't die before I've figured out how."

Tam laughed again, and this time it didn't hurt quite so much, and after August had gone he touched the folded paper through the cloth of his shirt, and held.

* * *

The ninth day was the day the engine changed its tune.

Tam knew before the horn sounded. He woke at midnight with the ache in his chest transformed — sharpened, focused, *aimed* — and crawled to the arrow-slit and looked out, and saw lanterns moving around the engine in patterns, and heard, thin on the wind, the voices of the hunters chanting in a cadence that made his fillings hum. They had stopped trying to break him by force. Force had nine days to prove it wasn't enough. Now they were trying something older.

They were trying to make the oath *doubt*.

The chant came through the stone and into him, and it was made of truths, which was the filthy genius of it. *Carrenholt fell,* the voices said, in the old tongue of oath-lines, and it had fallen, and Tam had run. *The Broadfoots were wall-breakers before they were wall-makers* — and they had been, generations back, turncoats who'd sold a gate for gold, the family shame, the reason Tam's grandmother had bound her children's children to the line so tight. *A deserter holds nothing. A deserter IS nothing. What does a nothing swear TO?*

And the oath stirred, and wavered, and Tam felt the walls of Marrowgate shift under his palms — a fraction of an inch, a settling, a groan of masonry that four thousand people heard and four thousand people screamed about — and he understood, with terrible clarity, that he could not outlast this. The doubt would grow. Doubt always grew. You could feed courage into a wound but doubt fed itself.

Unless.

He sat back against the gate tower in the freezing dark, cradling his ruined feet, and he thought about doors. He had spent his whole life finding doors. Exits. The clever angle, the early departure, the graceful withdrawal. And the whole time, the oath had been in him like a second skeleton, waiting, asking only one question, over and over, patient as stone: *will you hold?*

There was one answer left that he had never given. There was one door that opened only from the inside.

"Tam?" Ivet stood at the top of the tower stair in her nightshirt, hugging herself against the cold, her eyes enormous in the starless dark. "The wall's crying. I can hear it. It sounds like you."

"I know, sprout." His voice was gentle. His hands were shaking. "Go wake the others, would you? Bring them to the east gate. All of them. Everyone you can find who'll stand in the cold."

"Why?"

"Because I want them to hear it from me first," Tam said, "and not from the wall coming down."

* * *

They gathered in the gate's shadow: Sera with her witching kit clutched like a weapon, August with his ledger held to his chest, Ivet wrapped in someone's cloak, and behind them a hundred townsfolk in whatever they'd slept in, faces upturned, breath steaming. Above them the sky was a smooth black lid. No stars. There had been no stars for weeks. Tam had stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a wound, and only now, looking up, understanding that this was the last sky he might ever argue with, did he feel properly angry about it.

"The engine's got my number," he told them. No preamble. He'd never been much for speeches and he wasn't going to start dying elegantly. "It can't break me, but it can make me doubt, and doubt spreads, and when it spreads far enough the wall goes with it. So I'm going to do the one thing it can't use." He looked at August. "You keep accounts. Here's mine."

"Tam," August said, very quietly. "Wait."

"I break the line myself. Tonight. On my terms, not theirs. The oath comes home to roost and the engine catches nothing but an empty hook." He smiled, and it cost him more than the nine days had. "Walls are for holding, right? Well. This is me, holding."

"The cost—" Sera began.

"I know the cost. I've had nine days to read the invoice." Breaking an oath-line deliberately didn't just end the gift. It ended the shape the gift had made of you. Eleven years of the oath living in his marrow would come out all at once, and what was left standing afterward — if anything stood — would be a man with a hole where a load-bearing wall used to be. He'd seen it happen once, to an old knight who renounced his sword-oath. The knight had lived thirty more years and recognized no one, not even his own face in the glass.

"But not tonight," Tam said. "Tomorrow at dawn, when the horn sounds and the pull is strongest — that's when the breaking does the most damage to their engine. An empty hook, snapped back down the line. Might crack the lens. Might not. Worth doing properly." He looked around at their faces, at the fear in them, and found — to his lasting surprise — that their fear was the warmest thing he'd felt in nine days. "So. Anyone want to say anything sentimental, tomorrow's your last chance. Tonight I'm going to sit here and memorize what you all look like, so the next man wearing my face has something to work with."

Ivet pushed through the crowd and hugged him around the knees, hard, the way children hug, without any of the adult machinery of permission. "You'll still be you," she said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't entirely a statement either. Children of the Remembering-line spoke sometimes in the grammar of dreams.

"Yeah, sprout," Tam said, and put his hand on her head, and lied, and knew she knew, and knew she forgave him, which was somehow the worst part.

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless. The horn sounded its three notes, and Tam stood at the gate tower with his palms flat against the stone for the last time, and felt the whole architecture of himself come taut — the line running down through him into the foundations of Marrowgate, the golden thread of the Broadfoot oath humming with nine days of strain — and he said the words.

Not aloud. Oath-lines were not broken aloud. He said them in the place where the oath lived, in the marrow-dark, in the language of walls: *I release. I let go. Whatever I was holding, I set it down. Go on without me. Stand on your own. Be a wall for somebody braver.*

The thread parted.

Later, Sera would describe what happened next, because Tam remembered none of it. She said the sound was like a bell the size of the sky cracking down its middle. She said the engine shrieked — actually shrieked, iron and glass voicing together — as nine days of accumulated pull came back down a suddenly empty line and shattered the lens into a spray of black glitter. She said half the hunters dropped their weapons and simply sat down in the mud, released from something they hadn't known they were carrying. She said the walls of Marrowgate sighed, settled, and became — just walls. Good stone. Nothing more.

And she said Tam Broadfoot stood at the gate tower for one long minute after the breaking, upright, palms still flat against the stone, and then his eyes went white as milk, and he sat down very slowly, and began to hum a song no one recognized, and did not know his own name.

* * *

They made a litter of two spears and a door taken off its hinges, because it seemed fitting, and they carried him south.

He weighed almost nothing now, or seemed to; the gift had been most of his mass in some way none of them could measure, and what remained was a lean, grey-streaked man with cracked lips who hummed and occasionally said, in a voice like a child reciting, fragments of things. *Hold fast. Third stone from the left is loose. Mind the gate in winter, ice gets in the hinge.* Instructions to no one. A wall talking to itself after the house was gone.

"He'll come back," Ivet said, walking beside the litter with her hand resting on Tam's ankle, the way you'd steady something. "Some of him. I dreamed it. He's in there like a name written under paint. Somebody just has to remember him at himself."

"Then we'll remember him," Sera said fiercely, "every mile of the road."

August walked at the litter's head, silent, and Tam — in whatever country he now lived, behind whatever painted-over door — turned his face toward the sound of August's footsteps, and hummed louder, and for one moment the humming resolved into words:

"*Entry's open,*" he said clearly. "*Write me down.*"

August stopped in the road. The others looked at him. And August, who had torn a page from his ledger six days ago and never explained it, took out the pencil stub, and said, "I already did, old friend. Item one. Owed."

They carried him on. Behind them Marrowgate's walls stood whole against the horizon, ordinary stone in ordinary morning light, and ahead the road bent south toward the crown's city, toward a feast of forgetting, toward a king who remembered a boy with a ledger — and above them the sky stayed black, and stayed black, and waited.
