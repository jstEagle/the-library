## 14. Into the Crown's City

The road to Vaelcrest ended at a checkpoint that no longer checked anything.

August saw it from the ridge an hour past dusk: a tollgate of pale stone, lanterns burning blue-white along its length, and beyond it the capital spilling down the valley like spilled mercury — ten thousand roofs catching no starlight because there was no starlight left to catch. The sky above Vaelcrest was a perfect, seamless black, so complete it looked painted, and against that darkness the city's lamps burned with a hard, festive brilliance, as if the whole place were trying to shout down the silence overhead.

"They're not stopping anyone," Tam said beside him. His voice had been quieter since Marrowgate — not weaker, exactly, but careful, the way a man speaks when he has learned how much noise his own bones can make. He leaned on his staff and squinted at the gate. "Look. Wagons going through. Pedestrians. Nobody's being questioned."

"Nobody needs to be," Sera said. She stood a little apart from them, her hedge-witch's cloak swapped for a plain grey traveling dress, her hair bound back. "It's Feast night. The gates haven't closed on the Unremembering in thirty years. The crown *wants* the roads full."

Ivet crouched at the edge of the ridge, chin on her knees, watching the distant glitter of the city with an expression far too old for her face. "There are so many names down there," she murmured. "So many empty ones." She had been like this all day — drifting into stillness, her lips moving faintly, counting. Since Marrowgate, since the wall, since Tam had shattered his oath to save them all, Ivet's dreams had come more often and cut deeper. She dreamed names now even with her eyes open.

August shouldered his satchel — his ledgers wrapped in oilcloth against his back, the sealed page riding over his heart where he had carried it since the night the stars died — and looked at the four of them in the last light of a sunset with nothing above it.

"Then we go in with the crowd," he said. "And we remember everything they've paid to forget."

* * *

They bought their masks from a peddler whose cart had followed them the last two miles of the road, creaking under racks of lacquered faces.

"Feast masks! Feast masks!" the peddler called, though everyone within a mile was already masked or buying one. "Wolves and wrens! Kings and candles! Wear a face that isn't yours and leave your sorrow at the gate!" He was a small man with a voice worn smooth by repetition, and when August paused before his cart, the peddler looked up at him with eyes that held nothing festive at all. Just exhaustion. Just a kind of practiced cheer laid over something hollowed out. "For you, master clerk? You've the look of a man who'd wear a wolf."

"Why a wolf?" August asked.

The peddler shrugged, already turning to another customer. "Everyone forgets differently. Wolves remember too long, folk say. So they wear 'em to remind themselves to stop." He pressed a mask into August's hands without waiting for payment — carved wood, dark-grained, the wolf's face half-snarling and half-sad, its paint faded as though it had been worn by many owners across many years. "Paid for already," the peddler said, not looking at him. "Long ago. Go on."

August stood holding it a moment too long. Something about the weight of it. Something about the grain of the wood under his thumbs, which felt — absurdly, impossibly — familiar, the way a stair feels familiar underfoot in a house you've never entered.

"August." Sera's hand light on his elbow. "We need to move."

He tied the wolf-mask over his face, and behind its eye-holes the world went dim and close and strange, and he followed his companions down toward the gates of Vaelcrest.

Tam wore a mason's mask, square-jawed and stern. Sera had chosen a wren, delicate-beaked, that made her look sharper and wilder than usual. Ivet had wanted no mask at all, and Ysolde's heir — young Bren Emberwright, who had carried the shrine's dwindling flame in its iron lantern every mile of the journey and slept with it chained to his wrist — had argued with her only briefly before giving her the plainest one in the cart, a blank white face with no features but two eyeholes. "Better than nothing," Bren had said. "If the hunters are using true-seeing tonight, nothing is harder to read than nothing." So Ivet wore blankness, and walked between them with the lantern-light glimmering through the gaps in Bren's coat, and no one spoke of what would happen if the flame guttered before they reached the throne.

The checkpoint guards did not ask a single question. They stood in royal black with silver trim, faces bare — unmasked, which struck August wrong until he understood: the hunters and the watch wore no masks because they were the only people in Vaelcrest permitted to keep their own faces tonight. Everyone else was required to surrender identity at the gate. That was the theology of the Feast, if a feast could be said to have theology: for one night a year, the realm celebrated its forgetting by making the forgetting voluntary. Come to the capital. Put on a face that isn't yours. Drink to the dead you can no longer name, and be glad, and be lighter for it.

A guard waved them through with two fingers. Behind his shoulder, August glimpsed a table stacked with papers — lists, manifests, pages bearing the guild's own seal — and forced himself to keep walking, one foot then the other, through the gate and into the city of his king.

* * *

Vaelcrest at Feast-time was beautiful the way a fever is bright.

The streets were rivers of masked bodies, all flowing the same direction, uphill, toward the Palace Square where the great Unremembering bonfire would be lit at midnight. Every window hung with white banners. Every doorway dripped candlelight. Musicians played on corner platforms — but the music was strange, all of it in minor keys, all of it built on the same slow falling phrase, over and over, like a lullaby for something dying. And everywhere, threading through the crowd, vendors sold small wax tapers stamped with the royal seal.

"For the names," a woman beside August explained to her child, kindly, automatically, in the cadence of something recited ten thousand times. "You buy a taper for someone you've lost, and you carry it to the fire, and when it burns, the ache burns with it. Isn't that nice? No more ache."

"But I don't ache," the child said.

"You will, sweeting, when you're older. Everyone aches for someone. That's why the King gives us the Feast."

August moved through them like a ghost through a harvest festival, and the wolf-mask hid whatever his face was doing.

Because here was the thing — the thing that had been tightening around his ribs since the ridge, since the gate, since the moment he first smelled the city's woodsmoke and river water: *he knew these smells.* Not the way a traveler knows any city's smells, the generic grammar of hearth-smoke and fish-market and horse. He knew *these* smells. The bakeries of Vaelcrest used ash from a particular willow grove upriver; he could taste the willow's sweetness under the bread-smell, and he knew, without knowing how he knew, that the grove was called the Widow's Teeth. The river fog off the Vael had a mineral tang in autumn that the fog of no other river had, and his body recognized it the way a tongue recognizes its own blood.

He stopped, once, at a crooked alley-mouth where the crowd thinned, and stared down its length at a shuttered shop with a sign hanging by one chain. The sign was too dark to read. But his feet had turned toward it before his mind caught up, and he stood there swaying slightly, a large man in a wolf's mask angled down a dead-end alley like a compass needle finding north, and his heart was going very fast, and somewhere behind his eyes something knocked — softly, patiently, the way a prisoner knocks on a wall — and said: *this one. This is one of mine.*

"August." Sera again. She had learned, in the weeks on the road, to find him whenever he went quiet too long. She came down the alley after him, the wren-mask pushed up on her head, and followed his gaze to the sign. "What is it?"

"I don't know," he said. It was almost the truth. "I think — I think I've been here before."

"Half the realm's been to the capital."

"Not like this." His voice came out low and rough. He touched the satchel strap over his shoulder without meaning to, the gesture of a man reaching for a ledger to check a figure. "Sera. When we crossed Tanner's Row — the bridge with the seven arches — I counted the arches before I saw them. Seven. I told you we'd come to a bridge. I didn't know I knew that. And the fountain district, the one with the drowned statue — I flinched before I saw it. My body knows this city. My ledger—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "My ledgers hold twenty years of entries and there's no gap in them. No missing years. A life doesn't stay continuous unless someone kept it that way."

Sera was silent a moment. Down at the alley's mouth, the crowd flowed on, singing the falling phrase, carrying their little tapers toward the fire.

"You think they edited you," she said at last. Not a question. She had found her mother's name in the second ledger; she knew better than anyone alive what the guild's pen could do to a person.

"I think I'm standing in the proof." He turned away from the shop with its unreadable sign, deliberately, the effort of it visible in his shoulders. "Later. Tonight we have work."

* * *

They lodged the daylight hours — what remained of them — in a chandler's cellar rented from a woman who took Bren's coin without counting it and asked nothing, which itself was a kind of testimony: on Feast night, no one in Vaelcrest asked anything of anyone. Questions were for other nights. On this night the whole city conspired to be uncurious.

August did not sleep. He sat by the cellar's single high window with the wolf-mask in his lap and watched the sky.

Even here, deep in the capital, the absence of stars was a wound. He had grown up — wherever he had grown up, whichever of his seamless memories were real — believing the night sky was simply *dark*, the way most of the realm now believed it. But he had seen the wards break. He had heard the glass-crack of them going, and he had spent every night since lying awake reconstructing what the sky must have held before: not a few scattered points but a *flood*, a river of light, a depth you could fall into. The old ledgers mentioned it constantly, casually, the way ledgers mention weather. *Starlight poor for the south-road shipment. Starlight fine, the moon full, the crossing easy.* Twenty years of clerical notes treating the stars as ordinary as rain.

And the crown had taken them. The thing wearing Aldric had drunk the sky.

Near midnight, footsteps on the cellar stairs: Ivet, bare-footed, blanket over her shoulders, her blank white mask dangling from one hand. She didn't cry anymore when she woke from the dreams. That was somehow worse.

"There's a door," she said, without preamble. "Under the palace. I saw it again. Glass doors, and cold coming through them, and rows and rows of—" Her hands made a shape in the air, small and curved, holding something invisible. "Little bottles. Thousands. And behind the glass doors, August, there's a shelf with writing on it, and I couldn't read it in the dream but I know it's a name. I know it's *yours*."

The cellar went very quiet. From the street above, muffled through stone, the falling phrase of the Feast music rose and fell, rose and fell.

"The archive," Sera said from her bedroll, already sitting up. "Beneath the throne."

"I don't dream directions," Ivet said. "I dream names and doors. But tomorrow — tomorrow night, when the fire's lit and the whole court is drunk on forgetting — the door will be open. Doors always are, on the Feast. That's part of it. That's how they bring the new ones down." She hugged the blanket tighter. "I don't want to see what's on your shelf, August. But I think you have to."

August looked at the wolf-mask in his hands. Half-snarl, half-sorrow. Worn by many owners, the peddler had said. Paid for long ago.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "Tomorrow night."

* * *

They joined the procession at the turn of midnight.

By then the whole city was one slow river of candleflame, tens of thousands of tapers converging on Palace Square, and August walked in the middle of it wearing his borrowed face, and the strangeness of it closed over him like water. On every side, citizens lifted their little flames and spoke aloud — not to each other, never to each other, but upward, to the starless sky — the names they were choosing to burn.

"For my mother, whose face I gave back to the King, bless him."

"For my brother, dead at the border, whose letters I cannot read anymore but whose handwriting I still love."

"For the village of Harlow's Bend." A pause. A puzzled silence from the speaker, a man in a fox mask, standing very still amid the flow. "For... I had a reason for that one. For Harlow's Bend, whatever it was."

August listened, and his blood ran colder with every voice, because this was the architecture of it, the full horror laid open in festival colors: the crown hadn't merely stolen the realm's memory. It had taught the realm to *celebrate* the theft. To queue up year after year and hand over what remained willingly, gratefully, with music playing, and call the growing emptiness inside them peace. Somewhere ahead, in the palace above the square, the thing wearing Aldric's skin would be presiding over all of it — and feeding.

Bren walked closest to August, the iron lantern swinging at his side, shielded by his coat. Inside it, the last dragon-fire of the Emberwright line burned no larger than a thumbnail, trembling with every step, and August had stopped letting himself estimate how many days it had left. Weeks, Ysolde had said, weeks ago now. The flame had opinions about the city; August could feel it through the lantern's seams, a faint restless heat against his hip whenever the procession passed a royal banner. It wanted the crown. Fire remembers what it was forged for.

At the square's heart stood the Unremembering pyre — forty feet of white-pale timber, already stacked, already crowned with effigies: cloth figures sewn in the shapes of the dead, thousands of them, fluttering in the night wind like a field of ghosts waiting to be harvested. And above and behind it, floodlit by ten thousand candles, the palace itself rose against the absolute black of the sky — black towers, black walls, windows burning gold, and at its summit, faintly, a light that was not a light. A shimmer. A wrongness, like heat-haze in winter, coiled around the highest tower's peak.

The crown. Or the thing that wore it.

August felt Ivet's small hand slip into his. She was staring up at the same shimmer, her blank white mask tipped back, and her fingers in his were ice-cold and absolutely steady.

"It sees us," she said calmly. "It doesn't know our names yet. But it sees us."

Below them, the first torch touched the pyre, and the effigies of the realm's stolen dead began to burn while the music fell and fell, and August Underhill stood in the crowd of his king's city, in streets his bones remembered and his ledgers denied, and made himself one silent promise:

*Before that fire dies down — I'm coming for my shelf.*

* * *

They split at the edge of the square, as planned: Bren and Sera working counterclockwise along the palace's service wall to find the delivery gate Ivet had dreamed, while August took Tam and Ivet through the press of revelers toward the eastern colonnade, where the Feast's oldest rite would open the inner ways. Tam moved slowly, leaning hard on his staff, his oath-broken body aching with the cold, but his eyes were clear, and when August glanced at him he managed something like his old grin beneath the mason's mask.

"Don't you dare count me out of this, Underhill," he said. "The wall held. I can lean on a door."

Ahead, trumpets sounded from the colonnade — the signal August had memorized from a Feast program nailed to a tavern post, three rising notes and one long fall. Along the eastern gallery, a rank of doors began, one by one, to swing inward, admitting the masked procession into the palace proper, into the gold and shadow where the court waited with its glasses raised.

August drew the wolf-mask tight, settled his satchel of ledgers against his spine, and stepped through the doors of the palace he suspected he had grown up in — into light, into music, into the teeth of the thing that had eaten the sky.

And in the great hall beyond, on a dais beneath a canopy of black silk, a figure in the king's crown turned its head — slowly, precisely, like something that had all the time in the world — and looked directly at the wolf in the crowd.
