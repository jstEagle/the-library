## 15. The Archive Beneath the Throne

The dream came to Ivet three times before she would speak it aloud.

Sera woke to the girl's screaming on the second night, in the cellar of a dyer's house two streets from the palace walls, and held her while the child shook and named names — dozens of them, a litany of the erased pouring out of her small body like water from a split cask. But on the third night Ivet did not scream. She sat up in the dark with her eyes open and said, in a voice too old for her mouth:

*Under the throne there is a stair that was never built. It goes down past the roots of the mountain into a room where the dark is kept in glass.*

Then she lay back down and slept like any child, and Sera sat awake until dawn with her hands pressed flat against her own sternum, as though she could hold something in that wanted very badly to get out.

They went down through the city in festival dress.

The Unremembering Feast had swallowed Marrowgate's caution whole — no, not Marrowgate; they were far past Marrowgate now, three weeks and two broken men south — and the capital wore its masks like a second skin. Every face Sera saw was lacquered or veiled or hidden behind carved grins of gilded wood. That was the point of the feast, Ysolde's heir had told them before the wall took him: *on the night of Unremembering, everyone forgets someone willingly, and the crown drinks what is freely poured.* The whole city was a chalice tonight. The whole city was toasting its own hollowing.

August moved through the crowd like a man walking through a house he'd once lived in and could no longer find the doors of. She watched him pause at a corner where a fountain stood dry, watch his fingers drift toward the worn lip of the basin where generations of children had rubbed the stone smooth — watch him stop, frown at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger, and walk on. He did that perhaps a dozen times between the dyer's cellar and the palace district. Each time, something behind his eyes strained toward a shape it couldn't hold, and each time it slipped away, and each time his jaw set a little harder.

"Say it," Sera murmured, falling into step beside him beneath a paper lantern the color of old wine.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you almost remember."

He was quiet long enough that they crossed an entire square. "There was a woman," he said finally, "who sold candied plums near this fountain. I don't know how I know that. I don't know her face. I just know the taste." His laugh was thin and terrible. "That's all I have of her. A flavor."

"I know," Sera said, because she did know, better than anyone breathing. "Keep walking."

Ivet walked between Tam's crutch and August's shadow, wearing a mask of a sleeping fox, and wherever she turned her head, Sera marked the way the crowds seemed to part around the child without knowing why — as if some deep animal instinct in them recognized a Rememberer and flinched. Once, a masked noblewoman stopped dead in their path, stared at Ivet with naked horror through the eyeholes of a peacock mask, and then bowed. Deeply. Without knowing why she was doing it. Ivet patted the woman's gloved hand and said, "You had a brother. He sang. You're allowed to miss him," and the woman wept behind her feathers and let them pass.

The palace itself rose out of the feast-light like a cliff of black basalt, and every window burned gold, and none of that gold reached the ground. The guards at the servants' postern were drunk on ceremony and bribed with coins Tam pressed into their palms along with a name — a name he shouldn't have known, an under-steward dead eleven years, and Sera watched the older man flinch as the name left his mouth, watched the cost of it move through him. Since Marrowgate, since the oath-eating engine and the deliberate breaking of his line, everything cost Tam. Speaking felt sometimes like paying tolls.

But it worked. The postern opened. And inside, in a corridor that smelled of tallow and wet stone, Ivet took off her fox mask, looked at nothing at all, and pointed at the floor.

"There," she said. "Down."

* * *

The stair that was never built was behind the third wine-rack in the undercellars, and it was exactly as wrong as Ivet's dream had promised.

It hadn't been built. That was the only word Sera's hedge-witch senses could find for it. The steps descended in a smooth spiral through solid rock that showed no chisel-marks, no seams, no history — stone simply *parted* here, the way a throat parts, the way memory parts when you decide not to have it. The air grew warmer as they went down, which was wrong twice over: cellars should be cold, and this heat had no source. It came from everywhere. It felt like standing inside a held breath.

Lanterns appeared in niches as they passed, lighting themselves with a soft sound like an exhale, and Sera understood that the vault knew they were coming. Had perhaps always known. She thought of saying so and decided against it; August's face was already grey as ash.

"How deep?" Tam asked. His voice echoed wrong — too flat, as if the stair were swallowing the sound.

"Past the roots," Ivet said placidly. "I told you."

At the bottom was a door of glass, and through the glass was the room where the dark was kept.

Sera had imagined shelves. She had imagined, without quite admitting it, something like the guild's archive — orderly, clerical, a bureaucracy of loss. What she saw instead made her press her palm to the cool glass door and feel her heart stutter.

The chamber below was vast beyond reason, a cavern hollowed in the mountain's root, and it was filled — filled — with light. Thousands upon thousands of vials hung in great slow constellations from the ceiling, suspended on silver threads at every height, each one holding a swirl of luminescence that was not fire and not lightning but something softer, older, the color of the sky just before dawn. They drifted. The whole room drifted, a slow galaxy of stolen moments turning on invisible tides, and the light they cast was the warmest, kindest light Sera had ever seen, and every drop of it was taken from someone.

"That one's mine," Ivet whispered. She raised one small hand toward a vial drifting low near the entrance, glowing a fierce stubborn gold. "The day my mother gave me her name to keep. That's what it looks like."

"And those?" Tam nodded upward, at the dim ones. Whole regions of the constellation had gone grey, the vials within them clouded like dead stars, empty husks swinging gently in the dark.

"Eaten," Ivet said. "Those are the ones the crown finished. There's nothing left in them to give back."

Sera counted the dead ones. Then she stopped counting, because the number was doing something to her mind that counting should not do.

The glass door opened at August's touch. No lock. Of course no lock — who would steal from this place? Who would even find it? The warmth rolled over them, carrying a scent that nearly buckled Sera at the knees: woodsmoke and bread and river-water in summer, rain on dust, a hundred homely smells that were not smells at all but memories leaking faintly through glass, exhaled by ten thousand stolen days.

"Spread out," August said, and his swordspeaker's voice — that strange resonance he carried even now, even diminished — rang strangely intimate in the drifting light. "We're looking for—"

He didn't finish. None of them needed him to. Ivet closed her eyes, turned slowly in place like a compass needle finding north, and began to walk.

She led Sera leftward first, through aisles of hanging glow, past vials close enough to touch. Sera didn't dare. Even passing near them, she caught fragments — a wedding, a funeral, a man teaching his daughter to whittle, a woman's last sight of a burning village — each one brushing her mind and sliding off, and each brush left her more certain that touching these things uninvited would be like drinking from a river of other people's blood.

Then Ivet stopped, and reached up on her toes, and took down a vial.

It was small. Smaller than Sera had imagined. The light inside it was the color of late afternoon in autumn, and it swirled lazily, patiently, and Ivet held it out in both hands the way one holds a wounded bird.

"This is yours," the child said. "She wants you to have it back. She's been waiting such a long time."

Sera took it, and the glass was warm, and through it she heard — not words, nothing so kind as words — a hum. A tuneless, humming hum. Someone humming while they worked.

Her knees went.

She sank down right there among the drifting lights, in the archive of everything the realm had lost, and clutched the vial to her chest, and wept without sound, because the humming was *familiar*. Not the tune — there was no tune — but the act. Someone in her life had hummed like that, endlessly, absent-mindedly, while kneading bread or mending hems or grinding herbs, and Sera had forgotten her, had forgotten her face and her voice and her name, and the guild's ledger had recorded the sale in neat clerical ink, and here was what remained of her mother: a half-inch of warm glass and a hum.

"She hummed," Sera said stupidly, to no one, to everyone. "She hummed when she worked. I didn't — I forgot that I—"

Tam knelt beside her, awkward with his ruined bones, and put one heavy hand on her shoulder, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and his silence was the kindest thing anyone had given her in years.

It was Ivet who broke it, and gently. She touched Sera's wrist and pointed across the chamber.

"He's found his," she said.

* * *

August stood at the far side of the vault, beneath a shelf.

That was the other wrongness Sera hadn't yet absorbed: amid all the drifting constellations, there were also *shelves* — great banks of dark cedar rising in places where the threads grew thick, and on those shelves the vials were ordered rather than hung, ranked in rows like jars of preserves, labeled in a hand so elegant it looked like scripture.

The shelf above August's head bore a single word in that beautiful script:

**UNDERHILL**

And it was full. Shelf after shelf after shelf, decades deep, hundreds of vials, each one glowing with the same warm light, each one, Sera understood with a sickness that started in her stomach and climbed, containing a piece of the man standing beneath them.

"Oh," Tam breathed. "Oh, gods. August."

August wasn't listening. He had gone very still, the particular stillness of a man reading his own death warrant, and his eyes moved along the labels — for each vial was dated, catalogued, annotated in that same lovely hand. Sera drifted closer despite herself and read over the stillness of his shoulder.

*Underhill, A. — age 9 — the harbor road, the red kite.*
*Underhill, A. — age 11 — his mother's voice, singing (partial).*
*Underhill, A. — age 13 — the name of his home street.*
*Underhill, A. — age 16 — the face of M—*

The label stopped there. The ink was smudged, as if written by a hand that hesitated.

"The guild sold me," August said. His voice was perfectly level, which was worse than screaming. "My whole life. In installments. Like — like a haunch of meat." He reached up, and his fingers hovered a hair's breadth from the nearest vial, the one holding a nine-year-old boy's joy about a red kite, and did not touch it. "Do you know what the Guildmaster told me when I took my vows? He said the guild dealt only in what was freely given. Freely *given*, Sera. I signed. I was nine years old and they put a pen in my hand and called it an apprenticeship contract, and I've been signing myself away ever since, entry by entry, year by year, and calling it loyalty."

"You didn't know," Tam rumbled.

"I audited the ledgers." August's hand dropped. He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "For twenty years I audited the ledgers. Do you understand? The entries were in front of me. My own name was in the columns, and I read it as work. I read my own life as *bookkeeping*."

Sera looked at the shelf. All those shelves. Decades of a man, filed and shelved, and the thing that frightened her most was not the theft of it but the *orderliness* — the care, the patience, the long slow siphoning of a person by people who believed themselves decent.

"Take them," she said.

His head turned. In the vial-light his eyes were wet and furious. "Take them. Just like that."

"Yes. Just like that. It's yours."

"It's *poison*, is what it is." He gestured at the whole glittering dark. "Ysolde warned us — the Emberwrights' oldest records say the returned memory can burn the vessel that receives it. Thirty years of a self, poured back into a man in one moment? I would come apart, Sera. Or worse—" he touched his temple, "—worse, I'd come apart *correctly*, and be someone else afterward, someone I can't plan for, someone who might not choose to carry this flame to the throne. Right now, tonight, I am useful. I am intact enough to finish. If I take myself back—"

"You'd be *whole*," Sera snapped. "You'd stop being a ledger with legs."

"And if wholeness breaks me on the threshold of the throne room, then Aldric stays a prisoner, Ivet carries that flame alone, and everyone in this room has bled for nothing." He turned back to the shelves, and his voice dropped to something raw. "You think I don't want it? There's a vial up there with my mother's singing in it. Partial. *Partial.* I have spent thirty years not knowing what I lost, and here it all is, labeled, and you want me to believe I'm strong enough not to drink?"

"No," Sera said quietly. "I think you're exactly as weak as any of us. I think that's why it has to be your choice, and no one else's."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, faintly, through a thousand feet of rock, the feast-hall music throbbed — the city dancing as it emptied itself. The vials turned on their threads. On the lowest shelf of the UNDERHILL bank, one small vial glowed brighter than its neighbors, pulsing softly, insistently, like a heartbeat quickening, and Ivet cocked her head at it and frowned.

"That one knows him," she said. "That one's been waiting longest."

August stepped toward it. Stopped. His hand rose again, trembling now, and hovered over the little vial's warmth, and Sera watched a man stand at the exact center of himself and try to decide which direction was forward—

The light in the vault changed.

Every vial in the chamber flickered at once — a single ripple of dimming rolling outward from the far end of the cavern, where a door of black iron stood in the living rock. A door that had, Sera was certain, been shut.

It was open now. And on the threshold stood a figure in guild-grey, lantern in hand, and though thirty years and half a lifetime of forgetting stood between them, Sera knew that silhouette the way you know a scar.

The Guildmaster lowered his lantern, surveyed the four of them amid the stolen stars, and sighed the sigh of a clerk finding a misfiled page.

"August," he said. "Put that back. We need to talk about the terms of your restoration."
