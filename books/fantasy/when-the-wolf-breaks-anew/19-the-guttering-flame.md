## 19. The Guttering Flame

The feast-hall was dying the way old things die — not all at once, but in groans.

Sera felt it through the soles of her boots before she heard it: a deep, tectonic shudder running up through the marble floor, through the bones of the building, through the bones of her. Somewhere above, one of the great crystal chandeliers — each one worth more than Marrowgate's whole harvest, each one hung with a thousand candles that had never been allowed to gutter — tore loose from its moorings and came down in a slow, glittering catastrophe. She heard August shout something and hauled Ivet against her side, turning them both away from the fall. Glass rained across the flagstones like a season of ice compressed into three seconds. When Sera looked up again, half the hall's eastern windows were gone, and the night beyond them was still starless, still black as the inside of a closed eye.

"Left," Tam croaked from behind her. He was leaning on Bram's shoulder now, both of them moving at the pace of the wounded, which is to say the pace of grief wearing boots. "They'll funnel us right if we let them. Go left."

He was right. Of course he was right; he had spent his whole life learning how walls worked, how spaces could be made to betray the people inside them. The king's guardsmen — no, not guardsmen, *hunters*, the ones in royal black with the sigils sewn over their faces — were pouring in through the shattered doors at the hall's far end, and they were trying to drive the company toward the grand stair where their numbers would matter. Left was the long gallery, the servants' corridor, the narrow throat of stone that led to the dais and the throne.

To Aldric.

Sera ran. There was no elegance in it anymore. Her hedge-witch's craft had burned down to embers hours ago somewhere in the archive beneath the throne room, and what remained of her magic was a dull ache behind her eyes and a name she kept saying over and over in her head like a rosary — *Maren, Maren, my mother's name is Maren* — because if she stopped saying it she was afraid the crown would notice and take it back.

"Emberwright!" August's voice, ragged, ahead and to the right. "Brannoc! With me!"

She risked a glance sideways. Brannoc Emberwright was running at August's flank, and in his cupped hands he carried the fire.

Even now, even here, her breath caught at the sight of it. It did not look like fire ought to look. It was small — absurdly small, a coal no bigger than a sparrow's heart — and it did not flicker or dance but simply *burned*, steady and orange-gold and utterly silent, casting light that seemed to fall on things the way memory falls on things: gently, and making everything it touched look older and truer than it was. The last dragon-fire in the world. Ysolde had carried it for sixty years. Ysolde had died for it two nights ago in the smoke below Marrowgate, and her grandson had taken it up without a word, the way his family had always done, generation after generation, keepers of a flame everyone else had forgotten existed.

It was dimming. Sera could see it even at a glance, even mid-flight — could feel it the way you can feel a candle about to drown in its own wax. Weeks, Ysolde had said. But weeks had become days, and days had become *now*, and the little flame sat in Brannoc's scarred hands looking less like a weapon and more like a patient in its final fever.

"How far?" Brannoc shouted. His voice was hoarse; he'd breathed too much smoke in the underhalls.

"The dais!" August called back. "Past the gallery — there's a door —"

The door exploded inward before he finished the sentence.

Not opened. *Exploded*, hurled off its hinges by a wave of force that sent August staggering, that knocked Sera to one knee with Ivet clutched beneath her arm. Through the smoking doorway came the thing the hunters had been protecting all this time — the reason the feast-hall had not yet emptied of soldiers, the reason the crown had bothered defending a building that was already coming down around its ears.

It wore armor, or something shaped like armor, black plate etched with the same sigils the hunters wore over their faces. But nothing human moved inside it. The joints bent wrong. The helmet had no eyeholes, only a seam that glowed faintly, and when it took its first step toward them the floor tiles cracked in a spreading web like frost forming in fast-forward.

"What is it?" Ivet whispered against Sera's ribs. The girl's face was gray with exhaustion — she had dreamed names all night and all morning, hundreds of them, thousands, waking screaming and then standing up anyway — but her eyes were wide open and fixed on the armored shape with an expression Sera had learned to dread. Not fear. *Recognition.*

"It's empty," Ivet said. "It's — it's like the vials. Someone poured themselves out and something else poured in."

"Swordspeaker." The voice that came out of the armor was wrong in a way Sera couldn't immediately name, and then she could: it was layered. A dozen voices speaking in unison, none of them quite aligned, like a choir rehearsed by something that had read about music but never heard it. "You carry the ember. Give it here. The crown remembers your guild kindly. The crown will remember *you*."

"I've heard better offers," August said.

He drew his sword. Even now — even knowing what Sera knew, that the blade had spoken with his own stolen voice, that every word it said cost him something he couldn't name — hearing it wake made the hair rise on her arms. The steel drank the light of Brannoc's ember and gave back something stranger, a pale silver gleam, and along the fuller of the blade letters surfaced like fish rising in dark water. Old words. Oath-words.

"*Hold the line,*" the sword said, in a voice that was almost August's and almost no one's.

"Hold the line," August repeated, and charged.

* * *

Later — much later, when she had time to be anything but terrified — Sera would try to reconstruct those minutes and find they refused order. Memory, she thought bitterly, was supposed to be her trade now, and it betrayed her like everything else. What remained came to her in fragments:

August meeting the armored thing head-on, sword ringing against black plate, and the sound of that contact not like metal on metal but like a bell struck underwater — a vast, muffled *bong* she felt in her teeth.

Tam, broken-backed and half-blind, planting himself in the gallery mouth with his ruined oath-line flaring one last stubborn time, shouting "*Not past me*" at six hunters who had learned in Marrowgate exactly what that vow meant, and watching two of them actually hesitate.

Bram dragging Tam backward, step by step, refusing to leave him even as the hunters pressed in.

And Brannoc, running.

That was the thread she held onto through all of it: Brannoc, running low and fast along the wall with the ember cupped against his chest, using August's battle as cover, going for the door at the gallery's end — the narrow servant's passage that wound up and around to the back of the dais. That had always been the plan. Everything since the archive had been in service of that single geometry: get the fire to the throne while the crown's attention was elsewhere. While August was elsewhere. Being loud.

Sera ran with him. She told herself it was tactics — Ivet needed to reach the dais too, Ivet with her terrible gift, and someone had to keep the child alive long enough to use it. She told herself that. But some quieter part of her knew she ran beside Brannoc because sixty years of Ysolde's stories had taught her that the Emberwrights never carried the fire alone, and someone should be there, at the end, to witness what keeping it cost.

They were ten paces from the door when the crossbow bolts came.

Not many. Three, perhaps four, loosed from the gallery rail above by hunters who had abandoned the melee for the smarter prey. Sera saw the first one take Brannoc high in the shoulder and spin him halfway around; saw him stagger, correct, keep running, the ember still sheltered in his hands like a man carrying water across ice. She screamed a warning — a useless, wordless scream — as the second bolt caught him in the back, below the shoulder blade, angling in.

He fell the way careful men fall: turning, curling, protecting the thing in his hands with his whole body, so that he hit the marble in a crouch and then simply stayed there, kneeling, as though in prayer.

"No," Sera said. "No, no, get up, get *up*—"

She reached him and went to her knees beside him and saw everything at once, the way the dying let you see everything. The blood already pooling beneath him, shockingly black in the ember-light. The second bolt's fletching trembling between his shoulders. And his hands, opening.

"Take it." His voice was calm. That was the worst part. He sounded like Ysolde. He sounded like every story she'd ever told, every evening in the mountain shrine, about the generations who had kept the flame and handed it on. "It doesn't — it won't burn you. It knows why it's here."

"I can't carry it, I don't have your line, I'm nobody, I'm just a hedge—"

"Sera Vane." He said her full name, and something in it steadied her, the way a hand steadies a shaking cup. His eyes were finding it harder to focus, but they found her. "Nobody carries it alone. That's the whole — that was always the whole secret. Sixty years Ysolde carried it and she wasn't alone either. She had the dead with her. Every keeper. You have her too." A wet, painful laugh. "And you have her heir."

Sera looked up.

Ivet was standing over them.

When had she come? Sera had left her with Bram and Tam at the gallery mouth — she was certain of it, certain — but here the child was, small and singed and streaked with soot, staring down at the ember in Brannoc's opened hands with that same terrible recognition. And the ember was *rising*. Lifting off his palms by a finger's width, drifting, the way a moth drifts toward the one lit window in a dark house.

"It wants her," Brannoc whispered. Wonder broke through the pain in his face. "Look at it. Look — it's been waiting. All this time, it's been waiting for someone who remembers."

"Ivet, no—" Sera grabbed for the girl's wrist and the heat that came off the little flame was gentle, gentle as sun through spring glass, and her hand passed through the light and closed on nothing. "Ivet, listen to me, you're a child, you don't have to—"

"I dream them every night." Ivet's voice was very quiet, and very clear, the way small voices are clear in large rooms. "All the names. They scream at me until I say them. And when I hold this—" she lifted her hands and the ember settled into them, sank into her palms like a stone into snow, and did not burn her, and light came up through her fingers and lit her face from within, "—when I hold this, they stop screaming. They're just *there*. Like they're waiting to be said." She looked at Sera, and she was crying, and she was smiling, and she was nine years old and carrying the last hope of a kingdom in her bare hands. "I'm not afraid of it. It's full of people. It's the least lonely thing I've ever held."

Behind them, the bell-sound of August's blade rang again, closer now, and then a crash of splintering stone — the armored thing had thrown him, or he had been driven back, and the line was failing. Tam was down on one knee. Hunters in black were flowing around the edges of the gallery like ink around the rim of a glass.

"Go," Brannoc said. Blood at the corner of his mouth now. "Both of you. Up the passage. Behind the throne there's a — Ysolde described it, the old kings' door, the fire remembers it, ask the fire—" He coughed, and the cough cost him something he didn't have. "Tell my grandmother's ghost I kept it dry. Tell her I kept it *lit*."

"You tell her yourself," Sera said fiercely, pressing her hands uselessly over the wound in his back, feeling the awful warmth of the blood, "when this is over. You'll tell her yourself."

But he was already looking past her, up at the vaulted ceiling where stars should have been visible through the high clerestory windows and weren't, and his lips were moving, and she understood with a lurch that he was reciting the keeper's litany, the one Ysolde had murmured over the shrine's hearth every dawn — the counting of the keepers, name after name after name, all the way back to the first woman who had pulled a dying dragon's heartfire from the ashes and sworn to carry it until it was needed.

He got through eleven generations before his voice failed. Then his hands, folded in his lap, completed it in silence.

Sera knelt there one heartbeat longer — one heartbeat she could not afford — and then she stood, and took Ivet's free hand, and ran.

* * *

The servants' passage behind the dais was everything Ysolde's descriptions had promised and worse: narrow, steep, hot. Hot, though no forge had been lit in the palace for thirty years. The deeper they climbed, the more the air took on the quality of the moment before lightning — a pressure, a waiting, a taste of copper on the tongue. The walls sweated. Torches in iron brackets flared as Ivet passed them and leaned toward her like plants toward sun.

"He feels us," Ivet whispered. Not the hunters. *Him.* The thing in the crown. "He's — oh." She stopped on the stair, one small hand flat against the sweating stone. "Oh, he's *scared*."

"Good," Sera said.

"No." Ivet turned and her eyes were enormous, ember-lit, ancient with other people's memories. "No, you don't understand. He's scared, and underneath the scared he's — hungry. So hungry. And under *that*—" the child's voice dropped to hardly more than breath, "—under that there's a man. Still. There's still a man in there, and he's holding on, he's been holding on for thirty years, and he's almost gone. We have to hurry. Whatever we're going to do, we have to do it *now*, before there's nothing left of him to save."

Above them, muffled through yards of ancient stone, came the sound of the feast-hall's far wall giving way — a roar and a rush and a rain of rubble, the whole rotten edifice of the Unremembering Feast collapsing toward its own foundations. The palace was coming down. The crown's long night was ending one way or another, and the thing wearing King Aldric knew it.

Sera put her hand on the small of Ivet's back, above the small burning hands.

"Then we go save him," she said.

They climbed, small and burning, into the dark.
