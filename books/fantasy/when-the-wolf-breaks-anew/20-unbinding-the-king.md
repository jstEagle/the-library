## 20. Unbinding the King

*Aldric*

He had been a boy once. He was almost certain of it.

The thought arrived the way all his thoughts arrived now — small and wet and gasping, like a fish pulled from deep water — and he held it in the dark behind his eyes while the thing that wore his body presided over its feast.

Below him, through his own eyes, he watched the Unremembering Feast glitter and swirl. Ten thousand candles. Ten thousand masks. His hands — no, *its* hands, resting in his lap like a king's hands should — lifted a goblet of wine that he could not taste. He had not tasted anything in thirty years. Taste had gone early, along with the smell of bread and the weight of rain and the sound of his mother's voice saying his name with the *r* softened, the way only she said it.

*You are Aldric,* he told himself, nightly, the way a man counts coins to be sure none have slipped from his purse. *You are Aldric, you were crowned at nineteen, your father built the winter road, your daughter's name is—*

The name was gone. The thing had taken it at midsummer, sipping it out of him like marrow from a bone while he screamed in the locked room of himself. It always took the sweet ones last. It liked to savor.

Tonight it was pleased. He could feel its satisfaction the way a house feels an earthquake far away — a low tremor running through everything he was. It looked out over the feast-hall and saw ten thousand citizens raising their masks in toast to forgetting, and it thought: *mine.* It thought: *another decade. Another century. There is still so much of him left.*

There was not. Aldric knew the arithmetic better than the thief did. He was down to one memory now, one coin clutched in a fist grown thin as parchment. It was a strange one. Not a coronation, not a love, not a face. A boy.

A boy standing in a doorway with a ledger under his arm, ink on his fingers, watching Aldric being crowned and knowing — somehow knowing even then, with the terrible clarity children have — that something was wrong with the shape of the day. The boy's eyes had met his across the coronation floor for exactly one heartbeat. And in that heartbeat the boy had looked *afraid*, not of the king but *for* him, and then the guild-men had come and taken the boy away to make him forget, and Aldric had spent thirty years holding onto that one frightened, faithful pair of eyes because they were the only proof left in all the world that anyone had ever seen what was done to him.

*A boy with a ledger,* he thought. *I am Aldric. I remember a boy with a ledger.*

The doors of the feast-hall burst open.

For one glorious, impossible instant, the thing wearing his crown felt surprise — and surprise was a crack in a wall, and light came through it.

* * *

*August*

The vials were heavier than they had any right to be.

August ran through the burning feast-hall with Sera's satchel-sling cutting into his shoulder and thirty years of stolen lives sloshing against his back, each glass vial glowing faintly with the color of the person it had been poured out of. Amber for a farmer who had forgotten his fields. Blue-grey for a woman who had forgotten her songs. One small vial, hardly bigger than his thumb, that Ivet had named aloud three days ago in a voice like a bell cracking: *Sera Vane's mother, Maren Vane, of the village of Hollowmere, erased in the eleventh year.*

Behind him the hall was coming apart. Tam stood in the great doorway — Tam, whose bones were powder held together by stubbornness alone since the oath-eater had come — and held the line with nothing left but the ghost of his gift, his fists raised, and royal guards simply stopped, confused, unable to advance past a man who had sworn so hard at them that reality believed him. Ysolde's heir lay dying under a banner stand. And ahead, up the dais steps, through smoke and falling embers, sat the king.

The false crown burned on his brow with a light that cast no warmth. August had seen a great many impossible things catalogued in his years at the Guild, and he knew this one: it did not shine. It *drank*. Every candle flame in the hall leaned toward it like grass toward the sun.

*Ivet.* His voice cracked. She was beside him, small and singed and carrying the dragon-fire in a lantern of hammered bronze, and she looked up at him with eyes that had dreamed ten thousand names and refused to forget a single one. "When I say."

She nodded once. No fear in her. That frightened him more than the crown did.

They reached the foot of the dais. The king rose from the throne — rose wrong, joints moving a half-beat after the will that drove them, like a puppet remembering its strings — and the voice that came out of the beloved mouth of Aldric the Restorer was layered, ancient, patient as rot:

"*Ledger-keeper.* You brought me more to eat. How thoughtful the Guild has always been."

"You're not eating tonight." August shrugged the sling off his shoulder and wrenched open the first vial with his teeth. "Tonight we settle accounts."

He threw it.

* * *

*Aldric*

Memory struck him like a wave striking a swimmer too long in deep water.

Rain — real rain, warm summer rain on the roof of his father's hunting lodge, and the smell of it, gods, the *smell* of it, wet stone and green things — his mother's voice, the softened *r*, *Aldric, come inside before you drown* — the taste of blackberries, purple-stained fingers, his sister laughing — his sister, *Liss, her name was Liss* —

He screamed, and for the first time in thirty years the scream was his own.

The thing reeled on the throne, clawing at a brow already crowned, because the memories were pouring in past it, around it, *through* it — every vial a life returning to the world through the only door the crown had ever left open: the king himself. It had spent decades drinking him dry. It had never considered that a drained vessel could be *filled again*, that the archive beneath the throne was not a treasury but a wound, and that wounds could be answered.

Another vial shattered against his chest. Fields. A farmer's whole lifetime of dawn-labor and harvest-song, flooding home. Another — songs, a woman's voice singing her child down into sleep, and somewhere in the hall below a masked stranger tore off their mask and began to weep without knowing why.

"*Stop,*" said the thing in the king's voice, and the voice broke in the middle of the word, and both halves of it were screaming.

"No," said August Underhill from the foot of the dais, and there was ink still on his fingers, thirty years later, and his voice was steady as a ruled line. "No. This entry stands."

*I am Aldric,* the king thought, drowning gloriously. *I am Aldric and my daughter is Elenora and I buried her mother in the spring and I was afraid on my crowning day because I saw a boy with a ledger and the boy was right, the boy was right about everything—*

The crown began to burn.

Not to shine — to *burn*, truly burn at last, dragon-fire rising up the dais steps in Ivet's small determined hands, the last flame of the Emberwrights, guttering and golden and utterly unimpressed by thirty years of theft. The thing wearing the king felt it come and understood, finally, with the vast slow horror of the very old meeting the very simple, that it was going to end.

It tried to leave. That was the worst part — Aldric felt it try to leave, felt the ancient presence scrabbling at the inside of his skull like a rat in a collapsing house, seeking some other skin, some other memory to wear, some corner of the world to crawl into and begin again.

But there was nowhere. Every vial August broke gave the flame more to hold to. Every returned memory was a nail. The fire walked up the throne, and the crown drank at it desperately and found that dragon-fire cannot be drunk, only borne, and the thing that had worn a kingdom like a coat for thirty years opened the king's mouth and screamed with a sound that was not human and had never once bothered to learn how to pretend.

* * *

*August*

Later — much later, when he tried to write it down, which he would, because writing things down was the only prayer he knew — August would struggle to describe the unmaking. The nearest he ever came was this: it was like watching a lie die in front of everyone who had believed it.

The false crown blackened. Cracked. The light that drank went out of it all at once, like a debt forgiven, and what remained fell from Aldric's brow in pieces no larger than coins and lay smoking on the dais. The thing inside the king made one final attempt to speak — August felt the words form in the air, felt them press against the hall like a hand pressing on glass, some last bargain, some ancient offer, *I can give you back your voice, swordspeaker, I can give you everything the Guild promised* — and then Ivet set the lantern down beside the ruined crown and the last of the dragon-fire went up in a pillar of gold, and the words burned away unfinished, and the silence afterward was the cleanest sound August had ever heard.

Then the king collapsed, and August caught him, and the king was *weeping*.

"Thirty years," Aldric gasped, gripping August's sleeves with fingers that had signed laws and pardons and death-warrants in another man's stead, fingers that now shook like an old man's. "Thirty years in my own — I heard everything, do you understand, I heard every decree it spoke with my mouth, I watched it feed, I remembered — oh gods, I remember *everything*, why did I want to remember, why did any of us—"

"Because it's yours," August said. He held the king the way you hold a ledger open — firmly, honestly, letting it fall where it falls. "All of it. Even this. Especially this."

Around them the feast-hall was quieting. Masks lay everywhere on the marble like dead birds. Here and there people knelt or wandered or clutched strangers, weeping names they had not spoken in decades — mothers, villages, sons lost to the erasure-tithes, all of it coming home through ten thousand broken vials' worth of returned truth, flooding the city like the thaw after the cruelest winter in history. It would hurt. August knew that already, knew it the way he knew arithmetic. Memory kept was not memory healed. But it was theirs. That was the whole of the new law, and it fit in a single sentence, and he intended to write it on the first page of whatever came next.

Sera reached the dais, smoke-streaked, carrying Tam's arm across her shoulders. Behind her, Ivet stood over the burnt-out lantern, the last dragon-fire spent to its final ember, and she wasn't crying. She was listening — head tilted, eyes wide — and when August looked at her she smiled the oldest smile he had ever seen on a child's face.

"The stars are coming back," she said. "I can hear them remembering their names."

And above the shattered skylight, above the smoke, above the crown that was no longer anything but cinders — the first star kindled.

Aldric lifted his head from August's shoulder and looked at the boy with the ledger, the boy grown grey at the temples, and said, in a voice that was only one voice, hoarse and human and entirely his own:

"I know you. By all the returned stars — I've carried you thirty years and I only now know your face. What is your name, son?"

August opened his mouth to answer.

And found, to his horror and his wonder and the beginning of a grief he would carry the rest of his days, that he did not know.
