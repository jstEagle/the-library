## 21. The Stars Return

August Underhill did not sleep.

He sat on the steps of the ruined feast-hall with his back against a fallen pillar, the way he had once sat in the guild's counting room on nights when the ledgers would not balance, and he waited for the sky to do something. Around him the capital breathed in its sleep — or what passed for sleep in a city that had spent thirty years celebrating the loss of its own dead. Smoke still rose from the broken gates. Somewhere in the lower quarters a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, or at the absence that had hung over the world since the night the wards collapsed and every star went dark.

The dawn came grey and uncertain, the way dawns had come for a year now, as if the sun itself had grown unsure of its welcome. August watched the eastern sky pale and thought, with the detached precision of a man who has spent his life auditing the impossible: *if the crown is unmade, and the wards were bound to the king's living memory, and the king is free —*

The first star returned at 4:47 by the guild's reckoning, which was to say, by the reckoning of a man who no longer had a guild.

It did not announce itself. There was no fanfare, no thunder, no second shattering of glass across the heavens. It was simply *there*, one point of white light low in the east where the grey was thinnest, so small and so ordinary that August might have mistaken it for a spark off a cookfire if he had not spent the last year of his life learning exactly what the sky was supposed to hold. He knew that star. He had audited it. Entry eleven thousand and four, *The Ledger of Small Impossibilities: one star, Veth's Needle, the sailors' star, the one the fisherfolk of the Grey Coast steer by, currently absent, presumed consumed.* He had written *presumed consumed* in his careful clerk's hand and had not let himself think about what it meant, because a man who thinks about what his entries mean cannot keep writing them.

"Come back," he said to the sky. His voice came out as a rasp, a ruin, a whisper of what it had been. The unmaking had taken it — the swordspeaking voice, the voice that had commanded oaths awake and unbound the wolf of his own binding, the voice that had spoken the final word with Ivet's small flame cupped against the false crown while Aldric screamed beneath him in a flood of returning memory. He had known the price going in. Ysolde had told him, or rather she had told them all, in her flat Emberwright way, the way one reads a line item: *the fire takes what carries it, and what it takes from a swordspeaker is the speaking.*

He had signed for it anyway. Metaphorically. He had not signed anything at all, in the end, and that was the point.

The second star came at 4:52. The third and fourth together, a pair of twins over the northern wall, and August heard a woman in the street below begin to cry — not the ragged weeping of grief but something stranger, a slow, disbelieving sound, the weeping of a person remembering. He heard her say a name. *Marta. Marta, oh, Marta, I had a daughter, I had a daughter and I set a place for her at table every year and I never knew why—*

By the time the sky over the eastern hills had gone from grey to rose, there were forty-one stars visible, and August had counted every one of them, because counting was the only prayer he had left.

* * *

They found him on the steps an hour after full light.

Sera came first, as she always did, moving through the wreckage of the feast-hall with her hedge-witch's unhurried tread, her satchel — his satchel, returned to her, or perhaps never taken — bumping at her hip. She had ash in her hair and her mother's name written on a scrap of parchment tied around her wrist, because Ivet had named her aloud two nights ago in the collapsing hall and Sera had sworn she would never again trust a name to memory alone. Behind her came Tam, walking carefully, the way a man walks when the walls he built for ten years have left their cracks in his bones and his gift is gone and his mind is only mostly his own. His hand rested on Ivet's shoulder, and the child walked beside him with the last dragon-fire — no, not the last anymore, August corrected himself; the fire was spent, truly spent now, its flame given wholly to the unmaking, the shrine on the mountain dark for the first time in nine hundred years — and Ivet walked with empty hands and did not seem diminished by it. The child looked, if anything, taller.

"You're counting," Sera said. It was not a question. She lowered herself onto the step beside him with the groan of a woman whose body had finally been permitted to register everything it had survived.

"Forty-one as of dawn. Forty-three now." Two more had kindled while she spoke, high in the fading west. "The fisherfolk will be able to find their way home by week's end. The caravans will move again. The tide-tables in every port in the realm are wrong by a finger's width, and every navigator who ever learned the sky is going to have to relearn it, and they will weep doing it, and they will do it anyway."

"You're making a list," Tam said. He eased himself down on August's other side, joints cracking, and August noticed — filed it away, the way he filed everything — that Tam's hands had stopped trembling sometime in the night. The breaking of the wall-oath had nearly unmade him. It had not. There was a difference, August was learning, between a thing that is destroyed and a thing that has been spent. He was, he supposed, an authority on the subject now.

"I'm opening a ledger," August said.

Sera turned to look at him. Her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce and tired, and in them he saw the question she was too kind to ask: *whose ledger? The guild's? The guild is ash. The guild was ash the moment Ysolde finished her confession and you chose not to sign.*

"Not theirs," he said, answering it anyway, because he had spent thirty years — or however many years he had actually spent; the number was one of the things the archive vials had given back and he was still learning to trust it — keeping accounts for institutions, and he was done. "Mine. Ours, if you'll have it that way. Not a ledger of debts. Not of trades. Not of what was bought and sold and erased." He looked down at the book in his lap. It was a plain thing, bought yesterday — no, three days ago, time had gone strange — from a stationer in the lower city who had survived the feast-night barricades by hiding in his cellar with his stock, and who had sold August the last unruled ledger in his inventory with the bewildered courtesy of a man who did not yet understand what had happened to his world. "A ledger of restorations. Names being given back. One line per name. No price column."

"No price column," Sera repeated softly.

"That's the whole point of it." August opened the book to the first page. The paper was good paper, unruled, blank, patient. His pen-hand ached to rule it into columns, to impose the old discipline, debits and credits, what was owed and to whom. He made himself leave it open and free. "The guild kept two ledgers. The one everyone saw, and the one the crown paid for. I spent my life in the first one believing it was clean, and the second one existed to prove it wasn't." He turned the page. "This is the third kind. The kind that just *watches*. Writes down what's true and doesn't touch it. The stars are coming back one at a time, and every one of them is a name, and someone in this city is remembering a daughter or a brother or a street they grew up on, and I intend to write down every single one."

"That will take the rest of your life," Tam said.

"Yes," said August.

* * *

The work began before noon, and it began, as most of his work had always begun, with other people's grief arriving at his door.

They had taken a room — the ground floor of a chandler's shop on Candlemaker's Row, its owner dead in the feast-night riots with no one left to remember him until Ivet had dreamed his name two nights past and spoken it over his abandoned stock, and the neighbors, hearing it, had wept and opened the doors and said *yes, yes, sit here, use his tables, he would have wanted his candles to light something.* So the Guild of Impossible Things, in its newest and smallest and most honest incarnation, opened for business among tallow and wicks, and the first customer came before August had finished sharpening his pen.

She was a baker, broad and flour-dusted, with the wrecked look of a woman who had not slept. "They're saying there's a man here writing down the names," she said. "The names that are coming back. My husband—" Her voice failed. She stood in the doorway gripping her apron with both hands as though the apron were the last solid thing in the world.

"Sit," August said, and his ruined voice made the word a rasp, and she flinched at it, and he did not blame her. He pushed the open ledger across the table. "Tell me the name. Only the name. And what he made, or what he loved, or where he sat of an evening — one true thing, so the line isn't only letters. A name alone is a debt. A name and one true thing is a person."

She sat. She told him: *Aldous Merrin, baker, this shop's street, who sang to his ovens.* And August wrote it in his careful clerk's hand, in the first ruled line he had permitted himself — a single line, because a ledger needed *some* structure or the names would drown — and beneath it he wrote the true thing, and the baker read it and put her flour-white hands over her mouth and wept, and when she left she pressed a loaf on him that no one had asked for, and that was the first entry in the ledger of the Guild of Possible Things, though August did not yet know to call it that.

By evening there were thirty-one entries. Sera had taken over the interviewing by then, because she had a gift for it — the gift of a woman who remembered everyone else's dead and had lost her own, who could sit with grief without flinching because grief was her oldest acquaintance. Tam sat in the doorway like a wall, because that was what Tam did now even without his oath, and the desperate and the weeping seemed to steady themselves in his shadow. And Ivet slept in the back room and dreamed, and woke two, three times an hour, and each time woke saying names, and each name was a bell tolling somewhere in the city, and each bell was answered by weeping, and the weeping was — August listened to it as the night deepened — the weeping was not only grief. Underneath the grief of it, in the way that a ledger's columns have a bottom line, the weeping was *relief*. Thirty years of a city mourning without knowing what it mourned. Thirty years of empty chairs and unexplained rituals and streets named for people no one could recall. It was all coming back, and it hurt like a wound reopening, and the wound had needed to reopen to heal.

* * *

The king came on the fourth night, and he came the way he had promised: incognito, hooded, alone, in a plain grey cloak that would have fooled no one in the capital a month ago and fooled no one now, because the whole city knew their king had come back to himself and was walking his own streets learning them like a stranger.

August was closing the ledger when the door opened and the hood came back, and there was Aldric — older than his years, thinner, with the hollowed look of a man who had been a house occupied by something else for three decades and was now learning to live in his own rooms again. His eyes were the worst of it and the best of it: they held everything, all of it, the captivity and the crown and the boy with the ledger, and he did not look away from any of it.

"You're not sleeping either," August said.

"I remember sleeping," Aldric said. "That's the difficulty. I remember everything now, and most of it is not fit for a man to lie down inside of." He lowered himself into the customer's chair with the careful movements of a man relearning the weight of his own body, and for a while he said nothing, and August let him, because there were debts between them that no ledger could hold and both of them knew better than to pretend otherwise.

Finally the king looked at the book. "May I?"

August turned it toward him. Aldric read. August watched him read — watched the king's lips move faintly with each name, watched him arrive at the true things beneath them, *who sang to his ovens, who kept a garden of blue flowers though no one could say why, who was kind to horses,* — and watched a tear fall onto the page, and watched the king not wipe it away, as though the salt were part of the record now and ought to be preserved.

"There were nine hundred of us," Aldric said quietly. "In the throne room, at the crowning. The thing wanted witnesses. It wanted the witnessing *consumed*. You were the only one it made to forget, because you were the only one who saw what it truly was at the moment of the crowning — and it couldn't risk you." He looked up. "It kept you for thirty years, August. Kept your memories in glass on a shelf labeled with your own name, and let you keep its books. I have wondered, in the nights since — and I have nothing now but nights — whether it kept you close because it was proud of you. Things that wear crowns grow fond of their instruments."

"I've had the same thought," August said. "I've been trying not to."

"Don't try." The king's voice was gentle and terrible. "You chose truth over the oath that was used to bind you. That is the only entry that matters." He rose, and stood a moment with his hand flat on the ledger, as though taking an oath upon it — and perhaps he was; August had spent enough of his life around oaths to know one being made. "I will come again," Aldric said. "When there are more names. I want to read them. All of them. A king should know what his kingdom lost, and what it is getting back, line by line."

He left the hood up when he went out into the dark, and August sat a long while with the lamp and the book.

* * *

Near midnight, when Sera had gone up to bed and Tam was dozing in his doorway like a well-disposed landslide and Ivet slept without dreaming for the first time in anyone's memory, August sat alone with the ledger and turned back to the first page.

Forty-one stars had become one hundred and ninety. He had counted them from the doorstep at dusk, and written the number in the margin, because the stars were names too, and the fisherfolk of the Grey Coast would want to know the exact hour their Needle came home. Tomorrow he would begin the star entries. After that, the villages — the erased villages, whole towns in the second ledger with their names blacked out, and Ivet dreamed them now almost nightly, and each dreamed village would need its own page, its own restoration, its own slow return. It would take the rest of his life. He had said so to Tam, and Tam had nodded as though this were the most ordinary thing in the world, and perhaps it was. A man who kept accounts for a world that had stopped believing in oaths had learned, at last, to keep accounts for one that was learning to believe again.

He dipped his pen. He turned to a fresh page, and at its head, in his careful clerk's hand — the hand of a boy who had once witnessed a crowning and been made to forget, the hand of a man who had burned a false crown and paid for it with his voice — he wrote the heading that would define everything that came after:

*The Ledger of Returned Things.*

And beneath it, the first entry of the new count, the one he had been saving, the one that was not a person or a village or a star but was somehow all of them at once — the line he had been carrying in his chest since dawn on the steps of the feast-hall, waiting for the book worthy of it:

*One star, returned.*

He sat back and looked at it for a long time. Then, because a ledger was a living thing and living things went on, he turned the page — and heard, faint and clear through the open door, Ivet's voice from the back room, small and steady, speaking a name into the dark.

He reached for his pen.
