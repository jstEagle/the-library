## 22. An Entry in the Guild of Possible Things

The frost came early that year to Marrowgate, and with it the smell of woodsmoke and new plaster. The guildhouse — for it was a guildhouse now, whatever the sign said — had been a tannery before the war, then a granary, then nothing at all, its roof open to the sky like a broken bowl. Tam had set his hands against the east wall on the first of Frostmonth and held it until the stones remembered how to be a wall, and though he could no longer do such things without weeping afterward, he had done this one gladly, and the wall stood straighter than any wall had a right to stand.

August walked the length of it each morning, out of habit, checking the seams the way he had once checked ledger-lines. Some habits were worth keeping.

The sign above the door read **THE GUILD OF POSSIBLE THINGS**, painted by Ivet's own hand in letters of slightly different sizes, because she had insisted that no two words should look alike, "the way no two names do." Beneath it, smaller, in Sera's careful script: *All memories kept free of charge. No trades. No debts. No exceptions.*

It had taken him three months to write those rules, because every rule he drafted smelled of the old guild — of conditions, of fine print, of the quiet arithmetic by which an institution decides what a soul is worth. In the end he had burned six drafts in the kitchen stove while Sera watched from the doorway, arms folded, saying nothing, until the seventh draft was short enough to fit inside a child's memory of her mother's face.

*We keep what is given us. We give back what was taken. We ask nothing in return but the truth, told plainly.*

That was the whole charter. It hung now behind the front desk, framed in oak offcuts from Tam's wall, and August looked at it perhaps forty times a day, not because he doubted it but because he still, after everything, half expected it to dissolve into small print when he wasn't watching.

* * *

The morning of the first entry began, as most mornings did, with Ivet screaming.

It was quieter now than it had been in autumn — a single cry rather than the long, ragged hours — and by the time August reached the back room she was already sitting up in her cot, hair wild, eyes wet but level, holding out her hand for the slate that lived on the nail beside her bed.

"Give it here," she said, in the voice she used only for this: older than nine years old, patient as stone. "There were two last night. A miller's wife, Grethe, from down past the Aldwater — she sang to her geese, that's how her husband knew her coming up the lane, he knew her song before he saw her." She wrote as she spoke, quick and certain, the chalk whispering. "And a soldier. No name yet. He was carrying someone's boots. He didn't know whose. He just knew they mattered."

August took the slate when she finished and read it twice. "Grethe the miller's wife," he said aloud. Names spoken aloud went into the air, and Ivet maintained — and Sera half-believed, which was nearly the same thing — that the air remembered.

"And the boots," Ivet said firmly. "Don't skip the boots. He carried them four days. Whoever they belonged to, they mattered."

"I'll make a special margin note for the boots."

"You'd better." She swung her legs out of the cot and padded past him toward the kitchen, where the smell of porridge meant Sera was already awake, already feeding people, because Sera had decided sometime during the winter that grief was best fought with breakfast and had not been argued with since.

August stood a moment in the cold back room, looking at the wall above her cot. It was papered now with slates — dozens of them, copied out fair in ink, names in columns, names in clusters, whole villages recovered name by name like bones gathered from a field. Some nights he woke and came and stood among them, reading in the dark, and some nights one of them was a name he almost knew, hovering just past reach, like a word on the tip of the tongue of a man who had sold his tongue.

His own shelf was gone. UNDERHILL, the label had said; decades of himself in glass. They had smashed the vials over Aldric's open hands in the throne room, all of it flooding back into a king who screamed and wept and laughed in the same breath — but vials, once poured, do not return to their bottles, and what had been Aldric's restoration had not come home to August. The Guildmaster had offered, in the end, one final trade, there in the ash-smelling ruin of the feast-hall: your memories, all of them, for your silence about the second ledger. August had signed nothing. He had written, instead, in the Guildmaster's own book, across the page of the offer itself: *Returned to sender. No debt acknowledged.* And the Guildmaster had looked at him with something that might have been respect, or might have been hunger wearing respect's coat, and had left Marrowgate and not come back.

Some mornings August missed himself the way you miss a house you lived in as a child — not the place, exactly, but the feeling of knowing where everything was. He had fragments. Rain on a tin roof. His mother's hands doing something with thread — or wool? Thread. Probably thread. The first time he ever balanced a column to the penny, and the strange clean joy of it. These came to him unbidden now, drifting up like silt in stirred water, and whether they were truly returning or simply invented to fill the holes, he had stopped asking. Sera had settled the question for him, one gray evening in the second month, when he'd confessed his fear that his past was a forgery he was writing himself.

"Does it matter?" she had said, stirring the pot. "You're the man who stayed. Whatever else you were, you became this one. Keep him."

So he kept him.

* * *

By midmorning the front room of the guild was full, as it was full most days now.

They came from everywhere, once the roads cleared and the word spread — and the word spread the way Ivet's dreams spread, person to person, name to name. Farmers who had found a child's shoe in their attic and no memory of any child. Old soldiers who woke weeping for comrades whose faces had gone smooth as river stones. Whole families from the erased villages, walking weeks to sit across the desk from a former ledger-keeper and say, *we think we came from somewhere, and we would like to know where.*

Sera handled the ones who needed feeding first. Tam handled the ones who needed lifting — beams, carts, spirits — and had learned to do it gently now, without oaths, the ordinary strength of an ordinary large man, which he said was harder and better. And August sat at the desk beneath the charter with the great open book — *The Ledger of Returned Things*, bound in green leather Sera had bought off a peddler at scandalous discount — and he wrote.

Not trades. That was the revolution of it, the thing that made old guild-trained hands cramp around the pen: nothing given in exchange, nothing weighed against nothing. When a woman named Marta Ossel came in with a memory she wished kept safe — her father's voice, singing, the only piece of him the crown's hunger had missed because it had hidden in a song — August wrote it down, verbatim, in her presence, and gave her the copy, and kept the fair copy in the archive, and asked for nothing, and charged nothing, and watched her leave lighter and heavier at once, the way people do when they have put something precious somewhere it will be tended.

"You're running a bank that pays out forever," the blacksmith had grumbled, the week they opened. "It'll ruin you."

"It ruined me already," August had agreed pleasantly. "This is what grew back."

At midday the bell over the door rang — Tam had hung it so it chimed rather than clanged, "because people bringing memories should be announced kindly" — and a traveler came in out of the frost.

He was tall, gray at the temples, dressed in a plain wool coat that had been good once and was now deliberately less than good, mended at the cuffs in a way that suggested either poverty or practice at looking poor. He wore no sword, which in Marrowgate marked a man more surely than any crest. He stood in the doorway a moment, letting the warmth find him, and then crossed the room to the desk with the unhurried gait of someone who had spent thirty years being watched and had finally, recently, gotten used to not being.

"Master Underhill," he said. His voice was quiet and roughened, a voice used to rooms that fell silent when it entered, now pitched carefully to rooms that didn't care. "I'm told you keep a ledger."

"We keep several," August said. "What would you like kept?"

The man drew off one glove, finger by finger, and laid his bare hand flat on the desk. It was a workingman's hand, scarred across the knuckles, and on the ring finger was a pale band of skin where a ring had been worn long enough to mark the flesh and had lately been removed.

"My name," he said. "I've had it back for three seasons now, and I still wake some mornings not sure it's mine. I thought — if it were written somewhere. In a book. By someone whose work it is to keep things true." He paused, and something wry moved through his tiredness. "I'm told you don't take payment. So instead I'll give you a story, if you want it. About a boy with a ledger, a long time ago, in a palace that has since learned how to weep."

August's pen had stopped moving. He looked at the man properly then — the gray, the scars, the careful plainness — and the recognition did not arrive like lightning. It arrived like dawn: slowly, from below, illuminating things he had already seen.

"Your Majesty," he said quietly.

"No." Aldric shook his head at once, and there was real force under the softness. "No crowns in this room. There's a boy in the back making a margin note about boots, and a woman in the kitchen who will feed me without bowing, and a man outside who once broke his own oath to save a town, and none of them will treat me as anything but a fellow creature if I have my way. I spent thirty years being a king in ways I never chose." He smiled, and it was a worn and honest smile. "Let me spend the rest being a man in ways I do. I'm told the guild takes walk-ins."

August studied him a moment longer. Then he turned the Ledger of Returned Things to its first fresh page, dipped his pen, and said, "Name?"

"Aldric." A breath. "Aldric, son of Maren. Who liked — " and here the king stopped, closed his eyes, and went fishing in the deep water of himself, and August waited, pen poised, while a man searched for the shape of his own soul. "Who liked the rain," Aldric said at last, opening his eyes bright and wet. "Before. Before all of it. I remember liking the rain. Write that down. Put it first. Everything else can come after the rain."

August wrote: *Aldric, son of Maren. Liked the rain.* And beneath it, because a ledger was a living thing: *Remembers his mother's name. Remembers why the walls were built. Carries thirty years he did not choose, and carries them anyway.*

"There," he said. "Kept."

"Kept," Aldric echoed, and touched the page once, lightly, the way you touch a wound to learn it's healing. Then he pulled his glove back on, and glanced toward the kitchen door, from which Sera's porridge-smell and Ivet's chalk-dust were drifting together into the front room like two kinds of weather meeting. "Now," the king said, settling onto the bench opposite the desk like any tired traveler, "your hedge-witch tells me you've been refusing help with the eastern archive. The one in the flooded cellar. She says your cataloguing system is, and I quote, 'a crime against memory.' As a man who was recently a nation's entire filing system, I feel qualified to mediate."

From the kitchen, distinctly, Sera's voice: "I heard my name, and I stand by it!"

And from the back room, the small steady voice, reading a name aloud into the dark — another one saved, another star kindled somewhere over a village that would never know why its people suddenly stopped mid-task and wept and couldn't say for joy.

August looked at the open book. At the first entry, drying. At the king on his bench, rolling up his sleeves, asking to be useful. At the charter on the wall, still stubbornly free of small print.

He reached for his pen — his last pen, the good one, the one he'd sworn he'd wear to a nub writing names until his hand gave out — and turned to a clean page, and at the top of it, in a hand steadier than it had any right to be, he wrote:

*One star, returned.*

Then, beneath it, the beginning of the next line — because living things go on:

*Two.*
