## 1. The Keeper's Round

There are exactly three things you need to know about walking the Hollow Gate at night, and my grandmother taught me all of them before I was tall enough to reach the third seal.

One: never hurry. The Gate can tell when you're rushing, and it sulks.

Two: always greet the stones before you touch them. Not out of politeness — though Grandmother would have said politeness costs nothing — but because a seal that knows your hand hums truer for you than for any stranger.

Three: if the humming ever stops, do not run. Running tells the mountain you're afraid, and mountains remember fear the way old women remember insults: forever, and with interest.

I have walked this circuit every night for eleven years, and I have never once needed rule three.

I was thinking about that as I laced my boots by the cold hearth — thinking that maybe it was time to retire it, the way you retire a ladder rung nobody uses or a word that's gone out of fashion. *If the humming ever stops.* Absurd. The Gate had hummed since before my grandmother's grandmother, steady as rain on slate, and it would go on humming long after I was dust and somebody else's inherited duty. That was the whole point of keepers. We were temporary. The Gate was not.

Outside, the wind was coming down off Thornfell in long grey shoves, the kind of wind that makes you walk leaning forward like an apology. I pulled the door shut behind me and stood a moment in the yard, letting my eyes adjust, letting the mountain settle around me the way you let a horse settle before you mount it.

The keeper's house sits in the throat of the pass, wedged between two shoulders of rock like a pebble in a brook. Above it, the Hollow Gate itself — which is not a gate at all, whatever the lowland maps say. No bars, no doors, no arch. Just a mile of mountain, split clean through by some violence so old the rock has forgotten it ever happened, and along both faces of that split, the seals: forty-one of them, carved into the stone in spirals no chisel could have made, each one no bigger than a dinner plate, each one singing.

Only keepers hear them. I don't know why. Grandmother claimed it was in the blood — Okonkwo blood, brought up from somewhere warm and far away four generations back by a woman who took one look at these freezing mountains and decided, apparently, to stay forever. I used to ask her why our family, why us, and she'd give me the same answer every time: "Because somebody has to listen, little wren, and the mountain got used to our ears."

Little wren. She'd have laughed herself sick to know how that name comes round again later. But that's ahead of the story.

* * *

The southern face first, always. Grandmother's order, never mine to change: south, east, west, north last, "because north is where trouble looks in from, and you want to arrive there calm, not winded." So I pressed my palm to the first seal and felt the hum come up through my bones like a cat's purr, deep and even, and I said good evening to it, and it said nothing back, because seals don't talk, they only sing, and I have spent eleven years learning to tell the difference between their songs the way a shepherd tells sheep apart — by feel, by habit, by small wrongnesses nobody else would notice.

Seal one: true. Seal two: true, though it always runs a hair sharp in dry weather, like a violin left by a window. Seal three —

Seal three had a bird's nest built against its lower curve, which was new, and I stood there for a full minute deciding whether to evict the tenants. Grandmother's rule would have been eviction; a seal wants clear stone. But the nest was empty, and the wind was bitter, and I am softer than she was, so I left it and made a note to come back in spring with a broom and a guilty conscience.

South face, seals one through nine: true. All purring. I walked the ledge with my lantern held high and my free hand trailing along the rock, and above me the stars came out in patches where the clouds tore, and below me the drop into the lowlands went down and down until it became darkness instead of distance. Somewhere down there were villages with names I knew mostly from supply ledgers. Millbrook. Fen Hallow. The ferry crossing where the smugglers ran brandy and worse on moonless nights, and where the crown soldiers pretended not to look, because the soldiers drank the brandy too.

Eleven years, and my whole world was this: a mile of stone, forty-one songs, one kettle, one bed, one chair too many that I kept meaning to burn for firewood and never did.

People ask — well. People asked, back when people still came up here. They'd say, isn't it lonely? And I'd say no, because it wasn't, not exactly. Lonely is wanting company. What I had was more like being the only reader of a very long book. Every night the same pages, but never quite the same words. The Gate changed with the seasons, with the weather, with the phase of the moon. In autumn it hummed with a dry rustle under the note, like leaves. In deep winter it went glassy and precise. Once, during the great storm eight years back, it had sung so loud I'd had to sleep with wool in my ears, and I'd lain awake half the night anyway, grinning like an idiot, feeling like the mountain was telling me secrets.

That was pride, I suppose. Grandmother warned me about pride too. "Pride in the work is fuel," she said. "Pride in yourself is frost. It gets in everything and kills what it touches." She said it while mending a net, of all things — we didn't fish, there's no water up here worth the name, but she mended nets when she thought, and she thought often.

She'd been dead six years, and I still caught myself arguing with her. Usually losing.

* * *

East face, seals ten through nineteen. True, true, true. The wind picked up here, funneled through the pass, and I turned my collar up and did the middle stretch quickly — not hurriedly, mind, rule one still held, just efficiently, the way you cross a courtyard in rain. Seal fourteen had a crack near its rim that I'd been watching for three years, a hairline thing that never grew and never healed, and I touched it the way you touch an old scar on a friend: familiar, unremarkable, noted.

It was somewhere around seal seventeen that I noticed the silence.

Not a big silence. Not the absence of the hum — that would have been rule three, and I'd have known it in my teeth before my head caught up. This was smaller. A thinning. Seal seventeen's song had always had a second voice buried in it, a harmonic, something underneath the main note like a river under ice. Tonight the river was shallower. Tonight, if I pressed my ear flat to the cold stone — which I did, which looked mad even to me, alone in the dark with my ear against a mountain — the under-song was there but faint, the way a fire is faint when it's burned down to coals and you're not sure whether it will see the morning.

Hm, I thought. And then, because I am my grandmother's granddaughter and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving a thing alone: hm hm.

I checked seventeen again. Then sixteen, eighteen, fifteen. All true. Only seventeen thinned, and only slightly, and by the time I'd walked to nineteen and back it sounded — normal? Nearly normal? The mountain does that sometimes, shifts its harmonics with the barometric pressure, and Grandmother's journals were full of entries like *seal 22 dull tonight, rain coming* followed three days later by *seal 22 restored, rain indeed.* Weather. It was probably weather.

Probably.

I wrote it in the log anyway. That was the discipline: write everything, especially the things that are probably nothing, because the day you skip writing down a probably-nothing is the day it turns out to be a something, and Grandmother had a whole grim sermon about keepers who trusted their own certainty. The log lived in the house, a shelf of them now, thirty years of her hand and eleven of mine, and my entry for that night read:

*Seal 17 under-voice shallow at moonrise. Restored by second check. Likely pressure. Watch.*

Likely pressure. Watch. Two words I would think about later, in a different life, with different blood on my floor. But that's ahead of the story, and I'm getting ahead of myself, and rule one applies to storytelling too: never hurry.

* * *

West face, seals twenty-eight through thirty-six. (Twenty through twenty-seven live in the upper cleft, reachable only by the iron ladder, and those get done at midmoon, not nightly — another of Grandmother's orders, and the only one I've ever suspected was designed partly to give her knees a rest.) The west face is my favorite, if a keeper is allowed favorites, which nobody has ever formally ruled on. It catches the last light and holds it longest, and the stone there is warm-colored even at night, a deep russet, and the seals on it sing rounder than the others. Twenty-nine sounds like a contented grandmother. Thirty-three sounds like my mother must have sounded, though I never met her — I don't know how I know that, I just do, the way you know a smell means bread.

All true. All purring. I greeted them, they ignored me, we maintained our long and comfortable non-conversation.

And then north.

Grandmother's rule: arrive calm, not winded. I sat on the ledge below the north face for a few minutes first, the way she taught me, back straight, breathing slow, letting the wind comb through me until I wasn't carrying the south and east and west up the slope with me. The north face of the Hollow Gate is different stone. Everyone sees it eventually — visitors, when we still had them, always remarked on it. Where the south and west faces are granite, russet and grey, the north face is black basalt, slick and fine-grained, and the seals cut into it are finer too, tighter-spiraled, older-looking, though Grandmother swore all forty-one were cut in the same age beyond reckoning. *The north face was dressed differently,* she wrote once, in a journal line I'd read so many times I could recite it, *because the north face answers to a different attention. Keepers south of the pass keep the Gate. Keepers north of it keep what the Gate is for. Never forget which side of the door you're standing on.*

At the time I'd taken it for poetry. Grandmother was a great one for poetry when she didn't want to answer a question directly, which was most of the time.

I climbed. I put my hand on seal thirty-seven.

And the hum was flat.

Not silent. Not thinned, like seventeen. Flat — the way a bell goes flat when it's cracked, the way a voice goes flat when the singer has stopped believing the song. The note was there, the pitch was right, the rhythm was right, and underneath all of it was a hollowness, a dead spot, like pressing your thumb on a drumskin someone had quietly slit.

I stood there a long time. Long enough for the wind to find the gap between my collar and my hat and pour cold down my spine, which it did with evident satisfaction.

Then I moved to thirty-eight. Flat. Thirty-nine. Flat. Forty, forty-one — flat, flat, all five north-face seals singing their correct notes over a core of nothing, like beautiful voices reading words they didn't understand.

Rule three says: if the humming ever stops, do not run.

The humming hadn't stopped. I want that on the record, in the log, in whatever court eventually weighs what I did next. The humming had not stopped. It had gone flat, which is different, which is subtler, which is the kind of difference that exists precisely so that a tired keeper at the end of a long round can stand in the dark and argue themselves out of alarm.

I did not run. I also did not leave. I did what Grandmother would have done, which was sit down on the frozen ledge with my back against seal forty and listen until listening stopped being a duty and became a conversation.

Here is what I heard, over perhaps an hour, in pieces, the way you hear a large animal moving in brush — never whole, never confirmed, only implied:

I heard the flatness breathe. Very slowly. In on a count I couldn't measure, out on a count longer than the in. Seals don't breathe. I know that the way I know my own hands.

I heard, twice, far under everything, a sound I can only call a swallow. As if something vast, somewhere beneath the basalt, had tasted the song and found it wanting.

And once — once only, and I have gone back and forth a hundred times on whether I dreamed it — I heard, threaded through the dead center of seal forty's flat note, a rhythm that was almost, but not quite, the rhythm of a heartbeat. Slow. Patient. Unhurried, in the way that only a thing with absolutely no need to hurry can be unhurried.

Mountains remember fear, Grandmother said. Forever, and with interest.

I got up. I walked back down the ledge at a pace I would defend as brisk and describe honestly as nearly running, and I did not stop at the yard, and I did not bank the fire properly, and I sat down at the table with the log open in front of me and my pen hovering over the page for what felt like a full minute before I could make myself write anything at all.

What I finally wrote was this:

*North face, seals 37–41: pitch true, core flat. Under-song irregular. Possible respiration. Possible pulse. Watch hourly.*

Possible respiration. Possible pulse. I stared at the words and heard how mad they sounded and knew, with the cold certain part of me that lives under the part that argues, that they were not mad at all. That the maddest thing in that sentence was *possible,* and that I had written *possible* because the alternative was to write what I actually believed, which was:

Something under the north face is awake, and it is learning to imitate the Gate.

Hourly watch, then. I built the fire up, put the kettle on out of pure stubborn habit, and settled into the chair by the window with the log on my knee, telling myself I'd doze between checks, knowing I wouldn't. Outside, the wind kept shoving. The stars tore and mended. And every hour, wrapped in every coat I owned, I climbed back up the black basalt and laid my hand on seal forty and listened to the beautiful, hollow, patient song.

Somewhere past the fourth hour — dawn still hours off, the fire burned to a red memory — I heard a sound from the direction of the pass road. Footsteps. Wrong footsteps: stumbling, dragging, stopping, stumbling again. Nobody walks the pass road at night. Nobody walks the pass road at all, this season.

I took down the lantern and the iron poker, because I am a keeper and not a fool, and I opened the door onto the storm.

A woman was falling out of it toward me — mud to the knees, an arrow shaft standing in her shoulder, another broken off high in her thigh, and clutched against her chest with both arms like a child in a flood, a wax-sealed map tube.

Her eyes found mine, and she smiled, which I will never understand as long as I live.

"Keeper," she said. "Thank the dark. The king is awake."
