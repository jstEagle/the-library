## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

I caught her, mostly.

That is to say, I got an arm under her before she hit the threshold stone, and the map tube I did not catch at all — it went skidding across my floor like a startled eel and came to rest against the leg of my grandmother's chair, which I have always suspected of having opinions. The woman herself was heavier than she looked, the way dying people are heavy. Not with flesh. With everything they're still carrying that they refuse to set down.

"Inside," I said, which was a stupid thing to say to someone already halfway there. "You're inside."

"I know," she said. Her voice had a rasp in it, like wind through a keyhole. "It's quieter than I thought it would be."

The storm came in behind us anyway, throwing sleet across the flagstones, and I got the door shut one-handed with the poker wedged under my arm and the iron cold enough to bite through my glove. Then I stood there a moment in my own hall, listening to the wind scream against the mountain, holding up a stranger who was leaking onto my floor.

There has never been blood on the Hollow Gate floor. That's not a rule anyone wrote down. It's simply true, the way it's true that the hearth draws and the seals hum. Forty years of my grandmother's rounds, and forty of mine before tonight — no, thirty-one, I'm not so old as all that, though standing there I felt every winter of them — and in seventy-odd years of keepers walking those stones, nobody had bled inside the Gate.

She chose that moment to bleed quite a lot.

"Right," I said, mostly to myself, because talking keeps me from thinking and thinking was about to become a problem. "Floor first. Floor is where you're going."

I got her down onto the rug by the hearth — the good rug, the one Grandmother wove during the long winter she broke her ankle, the one with the pattern of interlocking gates that I used to trace with my fingers as a child until she told me the pattern wasn't decoration, it was a mnemonic, and then refused to explain further because, she said, some knowledge ripens late or not at all. The rug soaked red almost immediately. I remember thinking, with the strange clarity that shock lends: *Grandmother would mind that.* And then, a heartbeat later, with worse clarity: *No, she wouldn't.*

* * *

Here is what I knew about wounds, before that night: what the herb-book said, and what I'd done for myself over the years. A gash from a slipped hatchet. A burn from a kettle. Frostbite on two fingers the winter I was nineteen and proud and stayed out past dark to listen to the north seal because I thought it sounded lonely. I could stitch, splint, poultice, and boil water. What I could not do was anything for arrows.

Arrows are meant to stay in or come out, and both options are terrible, and the herb-book is maddeningly vague on the subject, in the way of books written by people who hoped someone else would deal with it.

The shoulder shaft I left. It wasn't bleeding much — the head must have missed anything vital, lodged against bone, and every instinct I had screamed *leave it, leave it, moving it will kill her faster*. The thigh was another matter. That shaft had broken off close, and beneath the broken stub the wound pulsed dark and slow, filling the rag I pressed there, filling it again, filling it again.

"You need a surgeon," I told her. "There isn't one within three days' walk, but you need one, so we'll pretend otherwise and I'll do what I can."

"No surgeons," she agreed. Her teeth were chattering now, which the herb-book says is bad in the wet-cold sense and possibly good in the still-alive sense. "They ask questions."

"So do I."

"Not yet." She caught my wrist. Her hand was freezing and astonishingly strong, the grip of someone who had spent her life hauling reins and rope and other people's secrets. "Water first. Then listen. I don't have the time you think I have, keeper."

Everyone I have ever argued with is dead or fictional, so I don't know why I expected arguing to work. I fetched water, and the bitterbark, and the moss that packs wounds, and I worked while she talked, and if my hands shook she had the grace not to mention it.

* * *

Her name, she said, was Sefa. She'd carried dispatches on the Thornfell roads for eleven years, which she described as "the second-dumbest profession on the mountain." When I asked what the first was, she smiled again — that same smile from the doorway, cracked lips and all, aimed at me like a gift I hadn't asked for — and said, "Keeping gates."

I should have bristled. A month ago I would have. But the north seal's flat note was still sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, and instead I heard myself say, "What's wrong with keeping gates?"

"Nothing," she said. "That's the whole horror of it. Nothing's been wrong for so long everyone forgot to check whether nothing was actually something."

I tied off a compress and sat back on my heels. "You came through the pass," I said. "In this storm. With two arrows in you."

"Three." She tapped her side, below the ribs, where the coat was dark and stiff. "But the third one's shy. Doesn't like to talk about itself." She laughed, briefly, and the laugh cost her; I watched the color go out of her face like a tide going out. "I crossed the high saddle because the low road was watched. There were men on it. Not soldiers. Worse. Soldiers want things from you. These men didn't seem to want anything at all. They just walked. In a line. Through weather that should have killed them, without lanterns, without—"

She stopped. Her eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere I couldn't follow, and when they came back they were too wide.

"They didn't leave tracks," she whispered. "Sefa, old girl, you should lead with the part that matters. They didn't leave *tracks*, keeper. Snow to the knee, and nothing behind them. And their eyes—" She pressed the heel of her hand against them. "You've seen a house after everyone's gone? How the windows look? Like that."

I thought of the humming. I thought of the north face, and its note gone flat as a dead string, and how for six nights I had told myself it was ice in the mechanism, ice always, ice explains everything, ice is the honest explanation of mountains.

"What were they carrying?" I asked quietly.

"Nothing." A shudder went through her. "That's what I kept coming back to. Armed men, riding hard through the worst passes in Thornfell, and their saddlebags hung light. Whatever they were going to do, they weren't bringing tools for it. They *were* the tool."

* * *

I got broth into her, a little. I built the fire up. And at some point she made me stop tending her and sit, actually sit, cross-legged on the floor like a child, because — she said — she needed to look at my face when she told me, and looking up gives a person the advantage.

"The tube," she said.

I fetched it from beside Grandmother's chair. It was longer than my forearm, dark wood, capped at both ends with black wax stamped with a sign I didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a gate seen edge-on. The wax had cracked along one seam. Someone had opened this tube before and resealed it badly, hurriedly, with wax that didn't match.

"It can't be opened by fire or blade," Sefa said. "Believe me, I tried, the first year, out of pure nosiness. It opens for a keeper's hand or not at all. Which is why I crawled across half of Thornfell instead of dropping it down a ravine and going home to grow cabbages."

"Why a keeper? Why *me*? There hasn't been more than one keeper on this mountain in living memory."

"Because the Gate is failing, and the person who hears it fail is the person who has to decide what happens next." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report the price of oats. "And because the Ashwife said the last keeper's line was the only line worth trusting. Your grandmother. She knew your grandmother."

The floor did not move. The hearth did not gutter. But I felt something shift in the room all the same, the way snow feels a slope deciding to slide.

"My grandmother," I said carefully, "kept the seals. That's all. That was the whole of it. Round the circuit twice a day, oil the mechanisms, sing the maintenance verses, log the hums. She was very strict about the logs."

"And what did she write in the logs?"

"Hums. Dates. Weather. 'All quiet.'"

"All quiet." Sefa closed her eyes. "Eleven years I carried things for the court at Greywater, and then for whoever paid, and then for the Ashwife, and do you know what every single message came back to? *All quiet* is a sentence with a body buried under it. Somewhere under this mountain is the reason your Gate exists, keeper, and it isn't weather. It was never weather."

"The Gate keeps the old magic of Thornfell from spilling into the lowlands." I recited it the way I learned it, before I could read, before I understood that words can be walls too. "Every child in the vale knows it."

"Yes," she said. "And every child in the vale also knows the wolf eats grandmothers. Children know lots of tidy things." Her breath caught, and I moved toward her, and she waved me off with two fingers, imperious even horizontal. "Listen. Listen now, because I can feel the third arrow winning the argument. The seal on whatever sleeps under this mountain is coming apart. Not in a hundred years. By midwinter, maybe sooner. There's a thing down there wearing the king's shape like a coat, and it's starting to stretch its arms."

"That's—" I started, and stopped, because the word available was *madness* and the word true was something else, and the flat note of the north seal rang through both of them.

"There's a woman called the Ashwife," Sefa went on, faster now, spending words like a miser suddenly spending gold. "Burned watchtower, west of the Thornwater, household of strays and lunatics, smells like woodsmoke and burnt sugar. She'll be expecting the tube by the new moon. You carry it. You don't open it on the road, you don't show it to soldiers, you don't let anyone in grey ride beside you, and if you see men whose eyes look like empty houses, you run, and you don't feel ashamed of running."

"I'm not leaving the Gate," I said. "The Gate needs—"

"The Gate," said Sefa Okonkwo's successor in stubbornness, whoever she thought she was addressing, "needs someone who knows why it was built. Go find out why. Then decide."

* * *

She faded after that, in and out, and I did what I could, which was less than nothing and more than she asked. Near the end she surfaced one last time, clear-eyed, urgent, gripping my sleeve with that terrible strength.

"Your name," she said. "Give me your name. Dying's easier with a name in the room."

"Juniper," I said. "Juniper Okonkwo."

"Okonkwo." She tried the weight of it and seemed satisfied. "Good name. Heavy name. Juniper — the king is awake. Say it back."

"The king is awake."

"Again. So it goes in past the fear. Fear sits right at the top of the memory, you know. Everything important has to be pushed down underneath it."

"The king," I said, "is awake."

"Good keeper." Her grip eased. For a while I thought she slept, and I let myself hope the herb-book's optimism about cold, quiet patients might apply. Then, without opening her eyes, in a voice worn nearly to thread, she said:

"Don't bury me inside the Gate. Whatever you do. Don't let me die in it and don't put me under its stones. Bury me outside, facing west, where I can see the road. I've spent eleven years looking at the backs of doors. Let me look at the road for once."

And then she died, between one breath and the next, the way a candle goes when a door opens — not blown out, just *elsewhere*, with the smoke of her still hanging in the warm air. Her hand slid off my sleeve. The fire popped. Outside, the storm went on shouting at the mountain as though nothing whatsoever had happened.

* * *

I sat with her a long time. I am told that's what you do. Nobody tells you what to do after that.

Seventy years of keepers, and no death inside the Gate. I found myself absurdly concerned with the rug, wringing blood out of it into a basin, working the weave, doing something, anything, with my hands while the rest of me stood very still in a cold place and looked at the fact I was refusing to pick up. The first death I had ever witnessed, and it happened on my floor, in my grandmother's house, inside the walls I had given my life to keeping safe. The Gate had never once failed to protect me. It had simply never occurred to me that protection and isolation might be different things.

When I finally moved, I moved because the tube was there, leaning against the chair leg, patient as only objects can be. Black wax. Broken circle. A gate seen edge-on.

A keeper's hand or not at all.

I want to say I hesitated out of caution. Some of it was. But most of it, honestly, was that I understood — the way you understand a thing in your spine before your head consents — that once I broke this seal, there would be no unbreaking it, no morning after in which I was only a keeper walking rounds and logging hums. *All quiet.* The sentence with a body under it, and now the body was on my rug, facing nowhere, waiting to be carried out to see the road.

I took up the tube. My hands had stopped shaking, which frightened me more than the shaking had.

The wax parted under my thumb like it had been waiting seventy years for exactly this hand, and inside, rolled tight and oiled against the damp, was a map of Thornfell.

Not the map. Not the surveyor's map in the Gate office, all honest contour lines and cheerful little symbols for passes. This one showed the mountain the way a skull shows what's inside it — routes threading the rock like veins, stairways that ended in nothing, passages that ran *beneath the Hollow Gate itself*, deep, deeper than any cellar, deeper than the foundations, down and down to a mark at the heart of it all drawn in faded ink that someone had circled and circled again until the paper wore thin:

a crown.

And beneath it, in a cramped hand I did not know, four words that the candlelight seemed to make darker rather than brighter:

*It was never weather.*
