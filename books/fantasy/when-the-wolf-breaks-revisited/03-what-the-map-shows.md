## 3. What the Map Shows

I buried the courier at dawn, which is the kind of sentence I never expected to write, let alone live.

The ground above the treeline is thin and stubborn, all rock and root, and the frost had come back overnight to spite me. I dug anyway. It seemed the least I could do for a woman whose name I never learned, who bled out on my hearthstones with her hand around a map tube like it was the last warm thing in the world. I wrapped her in my spare blanket — the good wool one, the one Grandmother wove the winter before she died — because whatever else was true, nobody crosses into death on Hollow Mountain wearing nothing but someone else's blood.

I said the keeper's words over her. *What passes, passes watched. What rests, rests remembered.* They're meant for travelers coming through the Gate alive, but they were the only words I had, and I think she'd have forgiven me the improvisation.

Then I went back inside, stoked the fire, sat down at my grandmother's table with her journals stacked beside me like witnesses, and unrolled the map that had killed her.

* * *

The tube was wax-sealed, black wax stamped with something that might have been a crown or might have been a tree, depending on how you squinted. The courier's blood had soaked through the wrapping in two places, and when I peeled the leather back the smell hit me — not just blood. Old paper. Mountain air. Something underneath both, faintly sweet, like flowers blooming somewhere they shouldn't.

The map itself was drawn on hide, not vellum. That was my first clue that this wasn't crown work. The Crown surveys on parchment, neat as tax ledgers, everything squared away and labeled. This was hide, soft with handling, inked in three colors — brown for the surface roads, blue for water, and a red so dark it looked dried-in, the color of old wounds.

And the roads. Saints and stones, the roads.

I've spent my whole life on this mountain. Twenty-nine years of walking every path the Gate commands, learning the approaches the way other children learn prayers. I know where the switchbacks hide behind false scree. I know the shepherd's stair cut into the east face that doesn't appear on any chart because shepherds don't file charts. I know the frozen waterfall you can climb in deep winter if you're mad or desperate.

None of it — *none of it* — prepared me for what was on that hide.

There were roads under Thornfell. Not around it, not through the passes — *under*. A whole second geography inked in that wound-red, branching beneath the mountain range like roots beneath a tree, which I suppose should have been its own warning. Passages with names. The Drowned Stair. The Weeping Gallery. Something called only *the Long Approach*, drawn as a single unbroken line running from the lowland forests to a point directly beneath—

Beneath the Hollow Gate.

I set the map down. I picked up my tea. It had gone cold, which felt appropriate, so I drank it anyway, because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Here's the thing about being a keeper: you learn the shape of your duty the way you learn the shape of your own body. You don't question that your left hand has five fingers. You don't question that the Gate seals the pass and keeps the old magic of Thornfell out of the lowlands, and that the hum — the deep, patient note the seal-stones sing that only keepers can hear — is the sound of that sealing, steady as a heartbeat. My grandmother taught me to listen for it before I could read. She pressed my palms to the stones and said, *This is the oldest work there is, little bird. Everything else is weather.*

And now there was a dead woman's map telling me there were doors under my door. Rooms beneath my rooms. A road running straight through the heart of everything I'd guarded, marked in red, and at the end of the red line, drawn small and careful, a symbol I didn't recognize: a circle, crossed by a single vertical stroke.

I knew what it wasn't. It wasn't on any survey, any smuggler's chart, any of the forty-odd maps in my collection gathered across years of trading news with couriers and cartographers who owed the Gate favors. No living person had drawn these passages. Which meant either the map was a lie —

— or the people who drew it hadn't been alive for a very long time.

* * *

Grandmother's journals filled a crate under her bed, fourteen volumes of cramped handwriting going back fifty years. I'd read them all before, or thought I had. Mostly they were practical: weather notes, seal-maintenance schedules, recipes for the pitch that waterproofs the gate-hinges, complaints about her knees that grew more eloquent as the decades passed. Reading them again with the map open beside me was like rereading a letter from a friend and finding threats hidden between the lines.

Volume six. Winter, thirty-one years ago. I'd always skipped past the odd entries because they made no sense. Now they made a different kind of no sense.

> *The north face sings flat again. Patched it with the third formula and it held until moonset, no longer. Asked the stone outright — you can, if you press your ear close enough and ask politely — and it gave me an answer I did not care for. It says the song is not failing. It says the song is being *pulled*. I have written to the Chapter and received no reply. I begin to suspect no reply is forthcoming, and I begin to wonder whether that too is by design.*

Pulled. I read the word four times. The seal-song wasn't fraying on its own, the way everyone learned it did — the slow natural wear of centuries that keepers patched with formulas and vigilance. Something under the mountain was pulling at it. From below.

Volume nine, eleven years later:

> *Dreamed of the king again. Woke with my hands smelling of sap. I will write down what I told no one, not even myself: the Gate does not face outward. It never has. We keepers stand at a door and watch the road, and we believe we are guarding against what might come in. But a door with a lock has two sides, and I found the hinge-marks today, deep under the foundation stones, and they are worn smooth — worn from the inside.*

*I have been standing guard on the wrong side of the door my entire life, and I think my mother knew, and I think her mother knew, and I think that is why none of them ever said it aloud.*

I closed the journal with fingers that weren't entirely mine anymore.

The fire popped. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves the way it always did, ordinary as bread. Nothing in the room had changed. Everything in the room had changed. That's the trouble with disillusionment — it doesn't announce itself with trumpets. It arrives quietly, sits down at your table, drinks your cold tea, and rearranges the furniture inside your skull while you watch, helpless.

My grandmother had known. Not guessed — *known*, written it down in her own crabbed hand, and then gone on walking her rounds and teaching me the songs and pressing my palms to the stones and saying *this is the oldest work there is* without once saying what the work actually was.

Unless — and here I paused, because fairness matters even when you're furious — unless she couldn't say it. Unless the silence was part of the inheritance, handed down keeper to keeper like the pitch recipe and the bad knees. *We keepers stand at a door.* Present tense, first person plural. She wrote it like a confession, but also like a sentence she was serving. Who tells a child the floor is hollow under their whole life?

A coward, I thought, and then immediately: or someone who loved me too much to make me carry it alone, and waited too long, and died before the waiting ran out.

Both things can be true. That's the cruelest thing I know.

* * *

I worked through the night, cross-referencing. It's what I'm good at; solitude teaches you either despair or methodical habits, and I got the habits. By the time grey light came back to the windows I had laid the map over the largest of the old surveys and weighted its corners with teacups, and I had confirmed what some traitorous part of my brain had already decided was true.

The red roads matched. Wherever the hide-map showed an underground passage, the old surveys showed anomalies — sinkholes logged and explained away, springs that ran warm in winter, stretches of trail where compasses misbehaved. Three generations of keepers had recorded the symptoms and filed them under weather. The disease had been under our feet the whole time.

And there was more. In the corner of the map, in that same ancient careful hand, someone had written a line of text so faded I had to hold it near the fire and turn it to catch the light:

*The seal is a sentence. The sentence is spoken still. Find the speaker.*

Underneath, smaller, newer, in the courier's hand, in a scrawl already weakening:

*Ashwife. Burnt tower. West fork past the drowned mill. Tell her Juniper's granddaughter comes.*

I read that last part maybe ten times.

She had known my name. Or enough of it. The Ashwife — whoever that was — had known the Okonkwo name, had known to expect a granddaughter of the Gate, had sent a dying woman through a storm and an arrow-storm both to reach my door with a map no living person should possess. Which meant somebody out there had been thinking about the Hollow Gate for a long time, and had decided, recently and urgently, that I needed to be part of it.

Every instinct I owned said stay. Bolt the door. Walk the rounds, patch the north face, sing to the stones, keep the small faithful routine that had held my whole life together like mortar. The Gate was failing? Then it needed its keeper more than ever. That was the argument for staying, and it was a good one, and I wanted with everything in me to believe it.

But the journals had taught me tonight that I could be faithful to a lie without knowing it. And the map said the lie had doors under it, and the doors had a road, and the road ended at a circle crossed by a line, drawn directly beneath my feet.

*Find the speaker.*

"Grandmother," I said to the empty room, "you absolute maddening woman. What did you do?"

The journals, naturally, said nothing. But volume nine had fallen open to its final page, and on the final page, in handwriting shakier than anything before it — dated barely a month before her death — was a single line I would have sworn on the seal-stones themselves hadn't been there yesterday:

> *If you are reading this, little bird, then the door has knocked. Go west. Trust the wolf, fear the crowned man, and forgive me — I ran out of time to tell you everything, so I am telling you the only thing that matters:*

*You were never meant to keep the Gate forever. You were meant to end it.*

Outside, far off on the north face, the hum of the seal went flat — and then, for the first time in my life, I heard it answer something. A second note, rising from deep beneath the mountain, patient and sweet as flowers blooming where they shouldn't.

It sounded almost exactly like knocking.

I packed a bag.
