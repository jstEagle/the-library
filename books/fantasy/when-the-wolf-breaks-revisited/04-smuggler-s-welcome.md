## 4. Smuggler's Welcome

There are three rules to a good smuggler's life, and I'd kept all of them for eleven years: never work for free, never sleep in the same bed twice, and never, ever go back over the Thornfell border.

So naturally, on a grey morning in late autumn, I was drinking bad cider in the Drowned Otter at Millbrook, three miles from the border stones I'd sworn off, waiting to meet a Gatekeeper who wanted me to break every rule I had.

Juniper Okonkwo sat down across from me like they were settling in for a long siege. Hood up, travel-stained, eyes that had that particular keeper look — like they could hear something wrong in the room that nobody else could. Maybe they could. I'd heard stories about keepers who could hear the Gate humming. I'd also heard stories about fish that granted wishes, and I put about equal stock in both until proven otherwise.

"Wren Halloway," they said.

"Depends who's asking." I turned my cup on the table, slow. "And depends what they think they know."

"They know you owe them."

Ah. That. Two years back, a border patrol had caught me with a boat full of untaxed salt and one very illegal almanac of lowland tide-tables, and Juniper Okonkwo — young then, newer than new to the badge — had looked at my cargo, looked at my face, and written in their report that the salt was river-gathered and the almanac was mine by inheritance. A lie with a straight face. I'd bought my freedom with their honesty, and we'd both known it was a debt that didn't expire.

I turned the cup the other way. "That was a kindness, not a contract."

"It was both." They pulled the hood back. Up close they looked worse than the stories — thin, shadowed under the eyes like someone had been drawing on them with charcoal. "Someone died in my keeping two nights ago, Wren. A courier with an arrow through her and a map in her hands. She said a word before she went, and the word was *king*, and everything I've believed my whole life says there is no king. So either she was mad, or I've been guarding a lie since I could walk, and I need to know which."

The Otter was loud around us — ferry-men arguing about water levels, a card game going sour in the corner, somebody's dog under someone's table. None of it touched our little table. Juniper had the knack of talking quietly so it felt like the room leaned in.

"And your plan," I said, "is to walk into Thornfell following a dead woman's map, looking for a king who may or may not exist, and you want *me* as your guide."

"I want someone who knows the roads the crown doesn't watch. You're the best of those. The others are dead, jailed, or liars."

"Flatterer."

"I'm told it's a failing." Something moved behind their eyes — not quite humor, but the shape of where humor might live if things were different. "Name your price."

Here's where a sensible woman says no price. Here's where I finish my cider, tip my hat, and walk out into the rain toward a life that stays boring and breathing. I want it noted for posterity that I considered this. I considered it hard, with both hands wrapped around the cup and my whole clever mind shouting the obvious.

But Juniper Okonkwo had lied for me once, clean and cheap, when a lie cost them more than they let on. And now they sat across from me asking for the favor back without asking — just sitting there with their terrible honest eyes and their dead courier and their impossible map, trusting me to be the person they'd decided I was.

Damn them.

"Price is this," I said. "Full crew rate, paid on delivery. My boat where I need it. And when this is done — whatever's done — you write in your official ledger that Wren Halloway of Millbrook is a licensed salvage specialist operating under keeper authority. Legal cover. Paper that says I'm respectable."

"You're not respectable."

"No," I agreed. "But paper doesn't have to be true. You taught me that."

For a moment I thought they'd laugh. They didn't, quite, but the corner of their mouth did something complicated, and I counted it a win. "Done. When do we leave?"

"We don't. *I* leave tonight, upriver, to collect my crew. You'll meet us at the old weir below Greyford in four days. Come armed, come quiet, and come having said your goodbyes, because keeper badges and smuggler roads mix like oil and lightning." I stood, dropped coins for the cider, and paused. "One question first. This map of yours. Who else knows you have it?"

Their silence answered better than words would have.

"That's what I thought," I said. "Four days. Don't die before the weir; I hate chasing debts."

* * *

Doss took the news the way Doss takes most news: with a grunt that contained multitudes.

We were aboard the *Maggot* — her real name is *Margaret*, but no boat of mine sails under a name that polite — poling upriver through the last gold of the willows. Doss stood at the stern the way he always does, like a piece of the landscape that had gotten ideas, grey-bearded and grey-eyed and built like something a mason had abandoned mid-project.

"A king," he said, after I'd laid it all out. "Under the mountain. Awake."

"Maybe awake. Possibly a story. Definitely paying crew rates."

"Hm." He poled. The river talked to itself. Doss's conversations are like his silences, only longer, so I'd learned to wait them out. "You liked her," he said finally. "The keeper. Back then."

"I liked not being jailed."

"You lied for nobody in eleven years till that one. Then you lied twice in two years." He meant Juniper's lie and mine — because I hadn't mentioned to any authority that the keeper who falsified a report had gone missing from their post. That second lie had cost me a night's sleep, which for me is practically a religious experience.

"The keeper pays well," I said.

"The keeper's got dead eyes," Doss said. "Seen 'em before, on folk who've lost something big and haven't noticed yet." He adjusted his pole. "I'm in."

Just like that. Eleven years I'd known him and he still did that — decided things in his chest instead of his head, then let his head catch up at its leisure. It made him infuriating at cards and priceless in a crisis.

Tam was harder.

I found the boy — scholar, runaway, nineteen going on ancient — in the loft above the chandlery in Weirbridge, surrounded by stolen books and eating cold rice out of a pot like it had insulted him. Tam had come to me eight months back with a forged letter of introduction, a bag of books clearly lifted from some lord's library, and a talent for reading anything, including people, that made him useful and dangerous in equal measure. He read maps the way other men read scripture. He also asked questions, which is the occupational disease of scholars, and questions get smugglers hanged.

"A king," Tam repeated, when I gave him the shape of it. His eyes had gone bright in that way I'd learned to distrust. "Bound how? By whom? Under which mountain? Wren, do you understand what—"

"No. And neither do you, and that's the point. This isn't scholarship, it's a job. Crew rates, four days, in and out."

"In and out of *what*, though?" He set down the rice pot, and I watched him do the thing he does — assemble the whole shape of a situation from fragments faster than anyone I'd ever met. "A courier died getting that map out. Crown soldiers hunt it. Whatever's under Thornfell, someone with power wants it staying buried, and someone else — whoever shot the courier — wants it found. We're not guides, Wren. We're the move in somebody's game."

"Then we'll charge extra for being played with."

He laughed — sharp, surprised out of him — and I saw the exact moment the bright eyes won over the good sense. That's the trouble with Tam. Good sense lives in him somewhere, I've heard rumors, but curiosity keeps beating it in fair fights.

"I want the map copied," he said. "Before we go anywhere. Every line, every marking, into my own hand, so that if the original burns or floats away or gets eaten by whatever's under that mountain, the knowledge survives."

"Says the boy who steals books."

"I steal books so knowledge survives," he said, with a perfectly straight face. "It's a calling."

Doss grunted from the doorway, which in Doss means *the boy is right and also I like him*, and that settled it. Three of us. Which was one short of comfortable and two short of wise, but wisdom had left my life the moment I'd said *name your price* across a tavern table.

* * *

The old weir below Greyford looks, at first glance, like nothing — a broken comb of black stones across a narrow river, half-drowned, moss-eaten. At second glance it looks like a good place to break an ankle. Only at third glance, if you know where to put your feet, does it become what it really is: a door. Smugglers have used the weir for three generations, crossing the border water at the one spot where the patrols' sightlines fail, and I'd carried more contraband over those stones than the crown had collected in taxes that decade.

They came at dusk on the fourth day, exactly as arranged, which already told me something. Keepers, in my experience, arrive early to establish dominance or late to establish excuses. Juniper Okonkwo arrived precisely on time, alone, hooded, carrying a long knife and a satchel and the air of someone who had burned a bridge behind them — I could see it in the way they walked, the particular lightness of a person with nothing to go back to.

"Keeper," Doss rumbled, by way of greeting.

"Okonkwo," I corrected. "No more keeper. They took the badge back when I resigned." Juniper said it flat, but flat costs effort, and I heard the effort. Resigning the Hollow Gate — that wasn't quitting a job, that was cutting off a limb. Their grandmother had held that Gate before them, and theirs before that, all the way back into fog. I filed the observation away next to the dead-eyes comment and resolved to be careful with this one. Careful, and maybe kind. In that order.

"Show me the crew," Juniper said, businesslike, and I performed introductions on the wet stones with the river muttering below.

"Doss. Ferries things. Hasn't drowned a client since spring."

"Once," Doss said.

"Told you he takes it personally. Tam — reads anything, steals selectively, asks fatal questions."

"I don't—" Tam began.

"He absolutely does. Okonkwo, formerly of the Hollow Gate, currently of nowhere, in possession of a map that gets people killed." I spread my hands. "Crew assembled. Now show us why we're freezing on a weir instead of warm in a tavern."

Juniper hesitated. I watched them weigh trust against necessity — a familiar arithmetic, and one I'd been doing myself for four days — and then they drew out the map tube, cracked the wax, and unrolled it across a flat stone with Tam holding the corners against the wind.

I've seen a lot of maps. Good ones, forged ones, beautiful ones, ones worth killing for. I'd never seen one like this. It wasn't drawn so much as grown, the lines fine as roots, and it showed Thornfell entire — not the safe cartographer's version with its blank spaces marked *here be mountains*, but the true bones of the place. Roads that weren't roads. Passages threaded under the range like veins under skin. And there, at the heart of it, a mark like a crown drawn in a single unbroken line, ringed about with script none of us could read.

Except that Tam, leaning close, made a sound like a man finding a knife between his ribs.

"This is Third Dynasty notation," he whispered. "This is — Wren, this hasn't been readable in six hundred years. Where did—"

"The courier bled on it," Juniper said. "Read what you can."

He read. We stood in the wind and watched him read, his lips moving, his finger tracing root-lines, and gradually his face went through wonder into something colder. "These routes pass *under* the Gate itself," he said. "Whoever drew this knew passages the keepers themselves don't—"

"My grandmother's journals mention a lower road," Juniper said quietly. "One line. I thought it was a story."

"Well, the story has a map." I rolled the thing up before the wind or my own greed could make decisions for me. "Here's how this works, all of you, so listen. We move by night on the hidden ways, day-camp in the deep folds where smoke doesn't show. Doss handles water, Tam handles paper, I handle everything else, and Okonkwo—" I looked at them, standing straight-backed on the broken stones with the border country dark behind them, "—you handle the truth. All of it. Whatever you know, whenever you learn it, it goes in the common pot. No private hoards. Hoarded truth is how crews die."

Something flickered across their face — guilt, maybe, or its cousin — and I made a mental note that there was already something in the pot they weren't serving. But they nodded. "Agreed."

"Then we're contracted." I spat in my palm, an old crew custom, and offered it. Doss spat in his. Tam looked horrified and then, deciding this was apparently a thing people did, spat in his with tremendous dignity. Juniper looked at my hand for a long moment — at the ritual of it, the small human foolishness of it — and then clasped it, dry-palmed and firm.

"One more thing," they said as we shouldered packs and started up the smugglers' path into the darkening hills. "On the road here. I felt someone watching. Twice. Not soldiers — soldiers I'd know. Something else."

"Bandits?"

"No." They glanced back the way we'd come, at the treeline going blue-black with evening. "It felt like being *read*. Like something deciding whether I was interesting."

I laughed, because the alternative was shivering. "Welcome to the smuggling life, Okonkwo. Everything out here is either hunting you or pricing you."

Behind us, high on the ridge we'd have to cross tomorrow, something moved between the trees — too large for a deer, too fluid for a bear — and was gone before any of us could swear we'd seen it at all.

Only Doss kept looking at that empty ridgeline, pole over his shoulder, with the expression of a man adding a figure to a sum.

"Hm," he said.

Which is Doss for *trouble coming*, and Doss is never wrong about trouble. He's only ever wrong about cards.
