## 5. Down the Smuggler's Road

I want it on the record — whatever record survives this expedition, which given current trends may be a single damp page found clutched in my dead hand — that I did not want to come on this journey. I was *recruited*. There is a difference, and the difference matters to scholars.

Wren Halloway recruited me two winters ago when she caught me copying pages out of a restricted archive for money to buy supper. She did not turn me in. She paid off my fine at the Collegium, informed me that my debts were now her debts, and told me that from that day forward my considerable learning would be applied to identifying which of her stolen goods were cursed before anyone opened them. It was, she pointed out with the smile of a woman who has never once lost an argument she cared about, better than debtor's prison.

So when Wren said "we're taking a job up Thornfell way," I understood that my opinion had been noted and filed under things Wren Halloway would be ignoring.

The client was the Gate-keeper themselves. Juniper Okonkwo. Tall, quiet, with the kind of stillness you usually only see in statues and very patient cats, and eyes that kept doing something I couldn't catalogue — sweeping the room, counting exits, weighing us. I'd have said they were assessing us as threats except that every so often their gaze snagged on Doss laughing or Wren arguing with herself over a chart, and there was something almost wistful in it, like a man reading letters from a country he'd been told didn't exist.

They carried a bloodstained map tube and a dead courier's last words, and they asked us to guide them into the mountains along roads that officially do not exist.

"Officially," Wren had said, tapping the map, "these roads don't exist."

"I know these roads," Juniper said.

That should have been my first clue. You'll forgive me; I was busy cataloguing cartographic anomalies. Nobody who grew up outside the Hollow Gate was supposed to know the smugglers' roads. The smugglers barely knew them, and we were the smugglers.

* * *

We left Coldharbour ferry at dawn, which is to say we left at an hour so early it was functionally still night, wearing the particular cheerlessness of people pretending it's morning. Doss poled us upriver through fog while grumbling about the fog, the river, the cargo, and me specifically, because I had brought books.

"You can't read them in a boat," he said.

"They're reference volumes. They're not for reading, they're for *consulting*."

"In a boat."

"When needed."

He gave me a look that suggested he was compiling a list. I suspect I appear on several of his lists. I've made peace with it.

Juniper sat in the bow like they'd been carved there, watching the banks slide past, and after an hour or so of professional silence they said, "Take the next fork left."

Doss laughed. "There is no next fork left. That's a rock shelf."

"It's a rock shelf at high water. At this water level there's a channel behind it. Six feet wide. Your ferry draws—"

"How do you know what my ferry draws?"

"Because I've watched your ferry for eleven years, Doss. From the ridge above the third bend. Every keeper walks the whole watershed. It's in the duty." They said it flatly, the way you recite something you've said ten thousand times, but then their hands did a small thing against their knees, restless, surprised by their own knowledge being wanted.

Doss took the channel. There was, infuriatingly, a channel. We slid through a slot in the rock close enough to scrape paint off both sides, and came out into a stretch of river I have never seen on any map, Collegium-made or otherwise: a drowned valley where the water ran green and slow between hills folded like sleeping animals.

"Well," said Wren, "that's inconvenient."

"That's the point," said Juniper.

And here is the thing I noticed, the thing I wrote down that night by firelight and have been turning over ever since: Wren said *inconvenient* the way she always does when someone else turns out to be useful, meaning she'd already recalculated every plan around the new variable. But Juniper heard it as praise. I watched it land on them like sunlight on a plant that hasn't been watered in years. They straightened. They started talking — haltingly at first, then with gathering momentum — about cut-offs and cave mouths and which passes the crown watched and which ones the crown had stopped watching because a keeper three generations back had quietly falsified the survey maps so the smugglers' roads would stay invisible.

"Your grandmother forged crown surveys?" Tam asked. I ask the questions aloud that everyone else is thinking; it's a service.

"The first Keeper Okonkwo did. It's in the journals. If the roads are on no map, no soldier rides them, and if no soldier rides them, nothing comes down out of Thornfell that shouldn't." A pause. "It was meant to protect the lowlands."

"And now?"

"Now I think it mostly protected the roads."

Wren grinned like a knife coming out of a sheath. "Keeper, you beautiful liar-by-inheritance. Do you know what this means? Half my routes exist because your family committed treason for the public good."

"My family committed *diligence*," Juniper said, with the ghost of a smile. It was the first smile I saw from them. It changed their whole face. I made a note: *subject smiles rarely; when subject smiles, note why.*

* * *

Three days upriver we abandoned the boat — Doss hid it under netting in a flooded quarry with the tenderness most men reserve for children — and took to foot, following a road that was less a road than a series of arguments the mountain had lost. Old paving under moss. Cut steps where no village had been for centuries. Twice we passed standing stones with carvings worn to suggestions, and I got exactly four minutes to sketch them before Wren said "later, scholar," in a tone that meant now-or-never-was-later-and-it-was-never.

I consulted my references that night anyway. None of them mentioned the stones. This delighted me more than I can properly convey. There is a specific joy in finding the place where the books run out and the world begins, though I confess the joy was somewhat complicated by the fact that the reason the books ran out was that everyone who mapped this region had either died or lied, and both possibilities argued against a long and comfortable life for our company.

It rained on the fifth day. It rained the way mountains rain, which is sideways and personally. We sheltered in the mouth of a cut tunnel — one of the under-passages, Juniper called it, one of the ways that went *through* rather than over — and shared dried fish and Wren's terrible coffee substitute, and that was the evening Doss told the story of how Wren had once smuggled a wedding party across a closed border inside pickle barrels, and Wren defended herself with the claim that pickles are an underrated structural material, and Juniper laughed.

Actually laughed. Short and startled, like a sound escaping custody.

I looked up from my notes and thought: *oh.* Because I had seen that laugh's twin before, in the archive, in the faces of scholars reading forbidden texts — that specific joy of a person discovering that a door they'd assumed was painted on actually opens. Juniper Okonkwo had spent a life alone on a mountain guarding a hum, and had walked into a leaking tunnel full of pickle-barrel anecdotes, and some load-bearing wall inside them had quietly developed a crack.

I write this down because it matters later. Everything matters later. That's the trouble with scholarship: you only find out which parts were important long after you could have been kinder about them.

* * *

The ambush came on the seventh day, at a place Juniper had warned us about and we had therefore avoided, which tells you how much good knowing things did us.

"They'll expect us at the Narrows," Juniper had said that morning, crouched over Wren's map with a charred stick. "So we take the sheep track over Greyfell shoulder. It adds half a day but nobody watches it. The crown thinks the shoulder is impassable."

"The crown," said Wren, "thinks a great many things we've spent a career teaching it."

So we went over Greyfell shoulder, and it was cold and steep and miserable, and at the top of it, where the path threaded between two leaning slabs of granite, six soldiers stood up out of the scree.

Not marching men. Not patrol-men bored enough to wave us through with a bribe. These were hard-eyed and mud-colored and they'd been waiting in the cold without fire, which meant orders, and orders meant someone above them knew what we were carrying.

"Wren Halloway," said the sergeant, pleasantly, drawing his blade. "Crown warrant for the theft of royal property. And the keeper." His gaze found Juniper and something in it made my skin crawl — not hatred, which I understand, but appetite, which I don't. "They want the keeper alive."

"Everyone wants everything today," Wren sighed. "Doss?"

"Left side," Doss rumbled. He'd already put himself in front of Tam and me, which was unnecessary, because I am armed with rhetoric.

"Juniper," Wren said, light as anything, "the map?"

"On me."

"Then run. Tam — with them. Go."

I did not go. I want that recorded too. I stayed, partly because my legs declined all requests for movement, and partly because I saw the sixth soldier — the one who hadn't spoken, hanging back at the edge of the rocks — shift his grip toward a crossbow aimed not at Wren but at Juniper's retreating back, and something in me that had spent years being careful and small and hired said: *no.*

I threw my book at him.

In fairness, it was a large book. *A Comparative Grammar of the Northern Tongues,* unabridged, and I threw it with the desperate full-body conviction of a man who has never thrown anything and never will again. It caught him across the jaw with a crack that I felt in my teeth, and he sat down hard among the stones, and the crossbow bolt went wide and shrieked off granite, and then the mountain exploded.

I mean that in the scholarly sense. Mostly.

Wren came off the high rock like weather. She fights the way she does everything — economically, dishonestly, and slightly ahead of schedule — and two soldiers went down before the sergeant finished turning. Doss simply advanced, an avalanche with opinions, and men who might have held a line against swords discovered that a line is a different proposition against a ferryman built like a dockside crane. And Juniper — Juniper had stopped running, because of course they had, because keepers do not leave people on mountains, even people they've known eight days — turned and came back down the slope with a walking staff and the terrifying calm of someone who has practiced one motion ten thousand times alone in the dark.

The staff work was beautiful. I say that professionally. Three strikes, no flourish, each one ending a specific problem: wrist, knee, throat. The sergeant got Wren's elbow in the conversation and folded. The crossbow man tried to rise and met Doss's boot.

And then it was over, six men groaning in the scree, and the silence rang, and I became aware that I was holding my grammar book like a shield and breathing like a bellows.

"You," said Wren, staring at me, "hit a man with a dictionary."

"*Comparative Grammar*—"

"You hit a man with a *dictionary*, Tam."

"He had a crossbow. It was proportionate."

Doss made a noise that I choose to interpret as laughter. Juniper looked at me for a long moment — looked at the book lying open in the gravel, pages fluttering, at the soldier groaning beside it — and something moved through their face that I couldn't catalogue until later that night, when I realized it had been recognition. The look of one guardian acknowledging another. Absurd. I'm a forger's assistant with a bibliography. But they looked at me like that, and then they said, quietly:

"Thank you. You didn't have to stay."

"Yes I did," I said, before my caution could catch up. "You'd have done the same."

"We interrogate the prisoners," Wren announced, before the moment could get any more sincere and ruin everyone. "Quickly. Their friends will miss them."

The sergeant talked easily once Doss explained the options. A captain named Vess, garrisoned at Cold Ford, carrying warrants for a keeper, a smuggler, "a fat riverman," and "a scholar of the Collegium" — my name, spelled correctly, which frightened me more than the crossbow. Someone had described us all in advance. Someone who knew we were coming, and roughly when, and had sent soldiers over a shoulder that was supposed to be impassable.

"The crown thinks Greyfell is impassable," Juniper said, very quietly, looking at the scree. "Only one kind of person knows different. Only keepers know the whole watershed."

Nobody said anything. The wind said something. Down in the valley, thin and far, a bird cried, or something pretending to be a bird.

"So the map wasn't the leak," Wren said at last. "The route was. Somebody's been reading keeper reports."

"Somebody's been reading *my* reports," Juniper said. Their hands had gone still again, statue-still, cat-still, and their voice was flat in a way I was beginning to learn meant danger — not theirs. Ours. "Every round I've walked for eleven years. Every seal-check. Filed to the Crown Warden's office, sealed and trusted, since my grandmother's day." They looked south, back the way we'd come, toward the pass we'd left and the gate beyond it that they'd guarded their whole life. "The thing that killed the courier didn't need to break out of Thornfell. It's been getting mail."

* * *

We burned nothing and buried the soldiers' blades under a cairn — Wren's rule: leave them disarmed and alive, because dead soldiers bring armies and live ones bring shame — and we walked on as the light went copper and long. Doss carried my grammar book, retrieved from the battlefield, and handed it back to me without comment except: "Page ninety's torn."

"That's where it hit him."

"Good page."

By the fire that night, Juniper sat apart, as usual, working the whetstone along the staff's edges with slow strokes. But not quite as far apart as before. And when I settled nearby with my notes — because I had decided, in the way decisions happen below the level of thought, that I was going to write down everything that happened on this road, in case none of us came back to tell it — they spoke without looking up.

"You consult those books even when nothing needs consulting."

"They're comforting. Familiar margins."

A pause. The stone whispered along the wood. Then: "My grandmother's journals are like that. I've read them so many times I know which pages smell like which year." Another pause, longer, the kind where someone is deciding whether to step off a ledge. "Tomorrow we cross the high shelf, and after that there's a stretch where I've never been. Past the old boundary stones. Nobody living has." The whetstone stopped. "I've been the one who knows things my entire life. Out here I'm going to be the one who doesn't. I'm telling you because—" The staff turned in their hands. "Because you hit a man with a book for someone you'd known eight days. So you understand doing things before you've decided to."

"I understood none of that sentence," I said honestly, "but yes."

Something eased in their shoulders. Not much. Enough.

Later — much later, when the fire was down to red memory and I was wrapped in my blanket writing this by moonlight — I heard voices, low. Wren and Juniper, at the edge of the camp, heads together over the map. I couldn't hear the words. I didn't need to. I've studied enough texts to recognize a covenant when I see one being drafted, even unwritten, even in the dark: two people agreeing, without quite saying it, that they will get each other's people home.

We move at first light. Toward the high shelf, past the boundary stones, into the part of the map where even the lies run out.

And this morning, on the wind off the ridge, I swear I heard something pacing us. Four-footed. Patient. Keeping just far enough away to be deniable.

Juniper says it's probably a deer.

Juniper is lying.
