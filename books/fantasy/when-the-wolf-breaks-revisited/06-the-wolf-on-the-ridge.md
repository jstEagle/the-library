## 6. The Wolf on the Ridge

The wolf first appeared on the third morning out from Wren's camp at Miller's Bend, and it did not appear so much as it *condensed* — one moment the ridge above the smuggler's road was empty grey stone in grey morning light, and the next there was a wolf standing on it, looking down at us with the particular patience of a creditor who knows exactly how much you owe.

"There," Tam said, pointing with the map tube, which was a habit of his I was trying hard not to find irritating. "That's — Wren. Wren, there's a wolf."

"I have eyes, scholar." Wren didn't look up from re-wrapping the bandage on Doss's knuckles. He'd split them the night before hauling the ferry rope at Coldbrook, and he was making a great show of not minding, which meant he minded enormously.

"It's a big wolf," Tam said.

"They're all big when you're standing under them."

"This one is *big* big. Ridge-of-a-hill big."

I looked up then, because Tam was right, though I wished he weren't. The wolf on the ridge was the size of a yearling horse, grey as slate weather, with eyes that caught the light wrong — not the green flash of an animal at dusk but something older and flatter, like light through deep water. It stood utterly still. Wind moved the grass around its paws. The wind did not appear to move the wolf itself, which I filed away under *things to worry about later*, a drawer in my mind that had been filling steadily since a courier died on my floor.

"Mountain wolves don't come this low," I said.

"Mountain wolves," said Doss, flexing his bandaged hand, "don't get that big without eating something that deserved it."

Wren finally looked up, shaded her eyes, and spat with the precision of long practice. "Well. It's not following us yet. Let's walk before it gets ideas."

It followed us. Of course it followed us.

* * *

By evening it had descended from the ridge to the treeline, and by the second day it was pacing us at fifty yards, close enough that you could see the way its paws made no sound even on scree. Doss took to carrying his boathook everywhere, which was theater — we had all seen what the thing was — but theater keeps morale up. Tam took to narrating its movements in a low scholarly murmur, as if cataloguing it might domesticate it. *Observed at dawn, holding position at the bend of the road. Observed at noon, drinking from the ford — or appearing to drink; no ripples.* Wren took to ignoring it so completely and so loudly that it became its own kind of performance.

I took to listening for it.

This will need explaining. I grew up at the Hollow Gate, and everything at the Hollow Gate hums. Not literally — my grandmother was very firm about the difference between hearing and hearing, and she boxed my ears once, gently, for confusing the two. But every seal-stone in that pass sings a note if you know how to listen, and after twenty-odd years of nightly rounds I could tell you the health of the Gate the way a physician reads a pulse. Healthy magic hums. Sick magic buzzes. Dead magic is silent.

And the wolf?

The wolf *hummed*.

Not the Gate's note — nothing that old and patient could be mistaken for the Gate's bright, disciplined chord. This was lower, a sustained tone like a struck bell heard through a mountain, and it sat somewhere behind the wolf's ribs, or behind mine; distance made no difference to it. From the moment it appeared, I stopped being able to hear anything else clearly. Walking the smuggler's road felt like standing inside a cathedral organ while someone held down one key forever.

"You hear it too," I said to Tam on the second night, when Wren and Doss were arguing about salt pork.

Tam went very still, the way scholars do when someone says the quiet part aloud. "The drone? Yes. Since the ford. I thought it was the caverns resonating." He hesitated. "It isn't, is it."

"No."

"Is it dangerous?"

"It's *loud*," I said, which wasn't an answer, and we both knew it, and we both let it stand, because the alternative answer was *I don't know, and not knowing has recently started killing people near me.*

* * *

On the fourth day the rain came, a proper Thornfell rain that fell sideways and turned the smuggler's road into a streambed, and the wolf stopped keeping its distance.

We had sheltered under an overhang that some forgotten mason had cut into the cliff — Wren called these *courtesy holes* and claimed the smugglers' guild maintained them, which told me the guild had better masons than the crown had soldiers. We were cold, wet, and short on dry wood. And the wolf walked out of the rain into our shelter as though it had been invited, shook itself once — water flew off it in a sheet, none of it touching anyone, which I noted with the part of my brain still doing arithmetic — and sat down across the fire-ring from us like a guest awaiting tea.

Nobody moved.

"Well," Wren said finally. "It's rude to stare."

"We're staring at a wolf," Doss said.

"We're staring at a wolf the size of a barn door that just joined our camp uninvited. In my experience, things that join uninvited want something. Staring's free. Let it stare back."

The wolf stared back. Its eyes were that deep-water flatness up close, and up close I could also see what I had been refusing to see from fifty yards: the humming came from *it*, deliberately, the way a person might hum to prove they were harmless. A lullaby note. A *keep-calm* note.

Then it spoke.

Its mouth didn't move. The voice arrived inside the rain-noise, inside the fire-crackle, inside the drone — a voice like the space between bell-strokes, dry and vast and very tired.

"*You are the Keeper.*"

Every instinct I owned said *run*. I stayed where I was, mostly because running would have meant leaving the map, and the map was the only thing in this whole catastrophe worth running toward anywhere.

"That depends who's asking," I said.

"*I am Vael.*" The name landed with weight, like a stone dropped down a well — I heard it hit something in Tam, who made a small strangled noise and grabbed for his satchel. "*Bound to the house of Aldric. Keeper of his sleep. And I have been walking behind you for four days waiting for the courage to speak, which should tell you something about how much I wanted this conversation.*"

"Four days," Wren repeated. Her hand had found her knife without any visible movement, which was a skill I intended to ask her about someday, preferably not during a crisis. "You stalked us for four days because you're shy."

"*Because the last time I spoke to strangers, they tried to bind me into a river,*" Vael said, with the mild tone of someone reciting a grievance so old it had worn smooth. "*And because your Keeper hears me. I needed to be sure. There are not many left who can hear, and fewer still I can trust.*"

"Why trust us now?" I asked.

The wolf's gaze settled on me, and the drone shifted key — lower, older, and underneath it something that might have been grief, if grief could resonate.

"*Because the map in your tube leads to my master's prison, Keeper. And because the thing that put him there is awake, and wearing him, and I cannot dig him out alone.*"

* * *

What follows is the argument, reconstructed as best I can, because my memory of that hour has a hole in it shaped like the word *Aldric*.

Tam spoke first, of course. He'd gone white as milk and was digging through his satchel, and he came up with a battered chapbook — some confiscated court history Wren's crew had lifted years ago and never sold — and read aloud, hands shaking: "'Aldric, last King of Thornfell, who vanished in the twelfth year of his reign. Court records cease. The regency that followed styled itself—'" He stopped. Swallowed. "There's no record of his death. I always assumed it was a gap in the sources. Gaps usually are."

"Not this one," said the wolf.

Wren hadn't moved her hand from her knife. "Say the rest, then. Say what you want, and say it plain, because spirits that talk in circles are the kind that eat people."

"*Plainly, then.*" The drone tightened, and I felt it press against my teeth. "*Your king made a bargain, long ago, to hold something back from the world. To make that bargain hold, he gave his body as its anchor and bound himself beneath the mountains, and I swore to watch over his sleep until the bargain ended. It was meant to end. It was written to end. Instead, something has gotten into the binding — something hollow — and it is unraveling the king's sleep thread by thread, and when the last thread goes, what rises from that court will wear Aldric's face and remember nothing of Aldric at all.*"

"And the courier," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "The one who died at my Gate. 'The king is awake.'"

"*She carried the truth south for two hundred miles with three arrows in her,*" Vael said, "*because the keepers were the only ones left who might believe it. Because the Gate was built to keep the world from looking. And because—"*

"Because the seal is failing," I said. "I've known since before she died. The north face went flat. I told myself it was weather."

The wolf regarded me with something that was almost gentleness. "*You told yourself many things, I think. Keepers always do. It is how the lie survives.*"

"What lie?" Wren asked sharply, and I loved her a little for asking it, because I couldn't.

But Vael only said: "*Ask your grandmother's journals. Ask the map. I am not the one who owes you truths tonight — I am the one offering a trade.*"

"A trade," Wren said.

"*Passage. You are going where I cannot go alone — the roads you travel are closed to me, watched by men whose eyes are wrong, and I am conspicuous. Take me with you as far as the mountain's root. In exchange, I will tell you everything I know of the binding, the Regent, and the court below — and I will spend my strength on keeping you alive to get there. Which, forgive me, you will need.*" The great head turned toward Doss, toward Tam, toward Wren, and lastly to me. "*All of you. Especially the Keeper. The Regent already knows your name.*"

Silence, except the rain and the fire and that endless bell-tone.

"How do we know any of this is true?" Tam asked — quietly, not hostile, just a scholar wanting the source verified.

Vael considered him. Then the wolf lowered its head, and pressed its muzzle briefly against the packed earth of the shelter floor, and where its nose had touched, frost bloomed outward in a pattern I knew down to my bones: the interlocking knotwork of the Gate's oldest seal-stones, drawn in ice, perfect, in a shape no living creature could have seen unless it had stood inside the pass when the world was younger.

"My grandmother drew that," I said. My mouth was dry. "In her journals. She called it the First Mark. She said nobody living had ever seen it."

"*No one living,*" Vael agreed. "*I am not entirely living. It is one of several things I am not, and I will confess the full inventory when we have more firewood.*"

* * *

Wren called a council the moment the wolf stepped back out into the rain to give us privacy — a courtesy that somehow made everything worse.

"No," she said, before anyone else could breathe. "Before anyone starts. No. We are smugglers, a runaway, a ferryman, and a — whatever Juniper is — and we are carrying a map that crown soldiers have already killed one person over, and now a talking spirit the size of a hay wagon wants to *join*. Do you all hear how this sounds?"

"Yes," said Doss.

"And?"

Doss shrugged his enormous shoulders. "Everything that's gone right so far sounded like that. The scholar's useful. The Keeper's the only one who can read the map. You owe Juniper a debt and won't say why. We're past the point where 'no' means anything except slower."

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at me.

I looked at the rain, where a grey shape stood motionless at the edge of the firelight, waiting on an answer with four days of patience and, I suspected, considerably more than four days of stakes.

Here is the truth of what I felt, and I set it down plainly because everything afterward depended on it. Every part of me raised at the Gate said *spirit at the threshold, bar the door.* That was the whole of my inheritance: the Gate keeps things out, the keeper stands in the way, and anything that hums with old power is a problem to be sealed. My grandmother's voice in my head, clear as chapel bells: *Kindness to the mountain is cruelty to the valley, Juniper.*

But my grandmother's voice had been lying to me, hadn't it. Or at least editing. The north face of her beloved Gate had gone flat on my watch, and a dying woman had bled out on my floor saying *the king is awake,* and the map under my arm showed passages beneath the seals I'd sworn my life to — and now here was the oldest witness in Thornfell, offering the truth I'd been starved of since the day I was born, asking only for company on the road.

Temptation doesn't arrive dressed as temptation. It arrives dressed as *finally, someone who'll tell you the truth.*

"The trade is fair," I said.

"Juniper—"

"The trade is fair, Wren, and you know it. It knows the roads are watched. It knows the Regent wants me specifically, which means we're already in this whether we like it or not. I'd rather have a wolf who tells me what's hunting us than walk blind." I paused. "Also, it drew the First Mark in frost, and I've spent my whole life being told that mark was lost. I'm done taking other people's word for what's true. Even hers. *Especially* hers."

Wren held my eyes for a long moment. Whatever she saw there, she sighed the sigh of a captain accepting cargo she doesn't trust.

"Fine. Conditions." She rounded on the firelight, addressing the rain. "One: you don't hunt near camp. Two: you tell us the ugly parts first, not last — spirits love saving the ugly parts for last. Three—" her knife appeared, casually, in her hand, "—if any of us dies because of you, I will find whatever a spirit has instead of a heart, and I will put this in it. Are we understood?"

From the rain, dry as dust and old as winter:

"*Understood, Captain Halloway. Your reputation precedes you. So does your ledger.*"

Wren went rigid. "You know about my ledger?"

"*I know many things. I told you — passage for truth. Starting tomorrow, I begin paying.*" The wolf turned, and before it faded into the dark — and it did fade, the way fog fades, which I am choosing not to think about — its voice reached us one last time, softer, aimed at me alone:

"*Sleep well, Keeper. Tomorrow I will show you what your grandmother saw on the night she helped seal the king away. And then you will understand why the Regent needs your heart to finish its escape.*"

The rain filled the silence where the wolf had been.

"Well," said Doss, into it. "That went better than the toll-bridge negotiation."

Nobody laughed. But nobody argued, either, and when we banked the fire and lay down in the courtesy hole, I kept the map tube against my chest and listened to the drone settle around us like a wall — lower now, steadier, almost protective.

Almost like a promise.

Above the overhang, far up on the black shoulder of the ridge, something else answered the wolf's hum: a single thin note, wrong and hungry, rising from the direction of the high passes. It sang my name once, softly, the way you'd try a key in a lock.

Then it stopped. And in the morning, Vael stood facing north with his hackles risen, and said only: *"Walk faster."*
