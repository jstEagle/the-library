## 7. The Ashwife's Hearth

I knew they were coming three days before they arrived, because my bees told me.

People assume the strays are a burden. The cat with half an ear, the boy who won't speak above a whisper, the old soldier who polishes a sword he'll never draw again, six chickens of dubious temperament, and forty thousand bees in hives made from the melted candle-stubs of a royal court. They call me mad for keeping them all. But a household is a web, and every thread hums when something brushes it. On the third morning the bees stopped at their doors and faced north, and I put down my bread and thought: *well. It's happening after all.*

The burned watchtower of Coldmere has been my home for eleven years, ever since the court decided that a magician who asked too many questions about the king's health was more dangerous than useful. They gave me a choice: silence or exile. I have never once managed silence, so here I am, with soot still ghosting the stones and a roof patched from seven different salvages, keeping a fire going because a hearth is the one piece of magic no edict can outlaw.

I had expected Juniper Okonkwo alone — or perhaps not at all, since keepers do not leave their gates, not for weather, not for war, not even, as far as I know, for funerals. That was always the tragedy of the keepers' line: they married duty the way other people marry spouses, faithfully and without ever once being asked whether they were happy. So when I looked out through the arrow-slit that evening and saw five figures on the ridge road — a keeper walking like someone whose whole religion had just died, a wiry woman with a smuggler's rolling gait, a gangly scholar clutching a satchel like a life-raft, a ferryman built like a door, and a wolf the size of a pony — I laughed until the cat complained.

"Brace yourself," I told the household generally. "Company."

Old Marta reached for her sword out of habit. Pip climbed into the rafters out of his. The chickens expressed no opinion, which from them is restraint.

* * *

They came down to the tower as the light went copper, and I met them in the yard with bread already warming, because whatever else this was, it would run better fed than hungry. The keeper stopped dead when they saw me.

"You're not what I expected," Juniper said. Their grandmother's jaw, if the portraits hold true, set at exactly that stubborn angle. "The map said — we were told —"

"That you'd find a wise woman in a cave, wrapped in veils, dispensing prophecy?" I handed them the loaf. "She retired. You've got me instead. Ashwife, ex-court-magician, current landlady to everyone the world misplaced. Mind the goose."

There was no goose. There is never a goose; it's simply important that visitors believe there might be. It establishes expectations early.

Wren Halloway I recognized on sight, which surprised her visibly. "You're supposed to be dead," she said, before her manners caught up with her. "Sorry. I mean — they said the court purged its magicians."

"The court says a great many things." I shrugged. "Purging requires effort. Exile is cheaper. Come in before the wind decides it lives here too."

The wolf sat at the treeline and did not come closer. I marked that, and marked also how Juniper kept glancing back at it — half wary, half something warmer, which was interesting and possibly dangerous. A spirit bound to Aldric himself, if my reading of the situation held. I'd need honey wine and patience for that conversation.

Inside, my strays did what strays do: descended like weather. Tam flinched at first, then found Pip watching him solemnly from the rafters and something in the boy's shoulders unlocked — two frightened creatures recognizing kin. Doss accepted Marta's silent offer of ale and the two of them achieved in ten minutes a friendship most men need a decade and a shared shipwreck to build. Wren charmed everyone reflexively, the way smugglers do, while simultaneously cataloguing every exit, which I respected enormously.

And Juniper stood in my doorway holding bread like they'd forgotten what it was for, looking at my crooked, crowded, impossible household the way a man looks at a language he almost remembers.

"You live with people," they said finally.

"Revolutionary, isn't it. Eat. Then show me your map, and then —" and here I let myself be honest, because they'd had enough lies for one lifetime already — "then I'm afraid I'm going to ruin your week."

* * *

The map came out of its tube stiff with blood that wasn't any of theirs. I spread it on the long table and the room went quiet, even the chickens outside, even the wind. Because I knew this map. I had helped make it, thirty years ago, in a scriptorium that smelled of hot wax and fear, drawing routes that were never supposed to exist on anything a courier could carry.

"This is the Cartographer's original," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Where did she get it?"

"She was dying," Juniper said. "She didn't explain much."

Dying couriers rarely do. I bent over the vellum and felt thirty years fall away like a coat. The passages under Thornfell. The approaches to the buried court. The little annotations in the First Keeper's hand — Juniper's grandmother's grandmother's teacher, and further back than that, all the way to the binding itself.

"Light the big lamp," I said. "And someone keep the kettle honest. This will take a while, and none of us will enjoy it."

I read the map the way it was meant to be read — not with eyes but with the hearth-magic, holding it near the fire until the heat woke the second ink, the layer drawn in tallow and ash that only shows itself to warmth. The lowlands version of the map showed mountains and passes. The warm version showed what the mountains were *for*.

Juniper watched the hidden lines surface and I saw their face go through grief in fast forward. Good. Better now, by firelight, among friends, than alone on some frozen stair.

"What am I looking at?" Wren asked.

"The reason your king hasn't been seen in sixty years," I said. "And the reason your keeper's gate isn't what they were told it was."

* * *

Let me tell you what I know, and understand that I paid eleven years of solitude for knowing it.

Sixty years ago, King Aldric of Thornfell rode into the mountain with his court magician — a young woman called Maren, whom history has kindly forgotten, and whom I succeeded, though I never wanted the post. He rode in voluntarily. That is the part everyone gets wrong, in the songs and the histories both. He was not imprisoned. He *volunteered*, because something had gotten into him — something old that the first settlers of Thornfell had bound badly, something that wears its host hollow from the inside like a moth in a coat. The binding required two things: a seal strong enough to hold, and a willing heart at the center of it. Aldric supplied the willing heart. The Hollow Gate became the seal. And every keeper since has been told they guard the lowlands from magic leaking out, when in truth they stand sentry over a dying man who agreed to be a lock.

The flaw — there is always a flaw, ask any locksmith — is that a willing heart can become unwilling. Can become *tired*. Sixty years is a long time to be a door. And the thing inside Aldric, the thing I have taken to calling the Hollow Regent because naming it makes it slightly smaller, has spent those sixty years doing nothing but whispering to the lock, wearing at the willingness the way water wears at stone.

I finished explaining all this to a silence so complete I could hear the kettle thinking.

"So the Gate," Juniper said at last, very evenly, the way people speak when the alternative is screaming, "is a lid. And I've spent my life polishing the lid."

"You've spent your life *holding* the lid," I corrected. "Don't undersell it. It's the loneliest job in the world and nobody thanked you once. I'm thanking you now."

"And the Regent," Tam said — the scholar, quiet until now, words coming out of him like coins from a dropped purse — "when it gets free. What does it do? What does it want?"

"It wants what hollow things want," I said. "To be full. It eats memory, belonging, love — the things that make a person a person rather than a shape. Aldric's kingdom was the richest meal in the world, sixty years ago. It's been fasting since. When it breaks loose—" I turned my hands up. "Midwinter. Perhaps a week either side. The binding fails when the year turns cold, because the binding was cast at midwinter and spells return to their birth-season like birds."

Wren pushed back from the table. "So we have until midwinter to reach a buried court under a mountain, unbind a king who may already be mostly monster, and stop a hunger that eats *love*. Is that the sum of it?"

"That's the optimistic summary, yes."

"I hate it," she announced, to general agreement. "I want it noted that I hate it."

"Noted," said Doss, and refilled her cup, which is ferryman for *agreed, and also, sit down*.

* * *

It was later — the fire burned low, Marta asleep in her chair, Pip and Tam sharing a blanket and a book of star-charts, the particular peace of a house full of breathing — that the wolf finally came to my door.

I'd left the latch open. One does, for wolves who used to be courtiers.

Vael filled my doorway the way winter fills a valley, then folded itself down onto my floor with a care that would have been comical in anything smaller. Up close, its eyes were the grey-green of old bronze, and tired in a way that no animal manages.

"Maren's successor," it said. Its voice was smoke and courtesy.

"Aldric's shadow," I answered. "We should compare notes."

"We should." The great head lowered. "You know why I follow them. The keeper especially."

"I suspect. Tell me anyway."

"Because the binding needs a willing heart," Vael said, "and the Regent means to make Juniper willing — by any means, fair or foul. Because I helped cast the original binding and I have sixty years of penance to work off. And because—" the pause here was long, and cost it something, "—because the last time I saw my master's face, it was smiling, and I have built everything since on the hope that somewhere under the crown, that man still exists."

Outside, the wind worried at the tower's old burn-scarred stones. Inside, I poured two cups of honey wine, set one on the floor, and drank the other myself.

"Well," I said. "Then we'd best get them ready. All of them. Because the Regent will offer Juniper everything they've ever wanted, and the only armor against that kind of offer is people. Family. The messy kind." I nodded toward the blanket-buried scholars, the sleeping soldier, the smuggler snoring on my bench without shame. "That kind."

Vael looked around my crowded, crooked, ridiculous hearth-room for a long moment.

"They found each other in under a fortnight," it said, wondering. "The keeper walked out of a gate alone and within a fortnight —"

"Yes," I said. "That's how it works. That's always how it works. Love is faster than fear; it just doesn't advertise."

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of snow. I stood in the yard with the map rolled under my arm and laid out the truth plainly, because these people had earned plain speech.

"There are three ways under the mountain," I told them, unrolling the vellum on a flat stone where all could see. "The river route, the deep stairs, and the old processional road. Each will take something from you. I won't pretend otherwise, and I won't choose for you."

Juniper studied the map with a keeper's eye, tracing lines their grandmother had probably dreamed about. Wren chewed a straw and muttered about flooded caverns. Tam leaned close over the annotations, lips moving. Doss cracked his knuckles like the mountain owed him money.

And I stood back, an old woman in a burnt tower, surrounded by strays who had somehow assembled themselves into the only hope Thornfell had left, and I did the last useful thing an Ashwife can do.

I packed them bread.

But as they made ready — as Wren argued with Doss about river-currents and Juniper stood very still, listening to something I couldn't hear — I felt the hearth-flame behind me lean, all on its own, toward the north.

Toward the mountain.

And in the coals, just for a moment, just faintly, I heard my own name.

Spoken in a voice I hadn't heard in sixty years.

*"Maren,"* said the thing beneath the mountain, using the mouth of a king it had eaten, *"you always did talk too much."*

It knows I'm helping them. Gods help us all — it knows.
