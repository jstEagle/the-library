## 8. Three Ways Under the Mountain

I have been robbed at knifepoint, cheated by a bishop, and once spent three days in a flooded culvert with a dead man and his very alive donkey, but I had never before sat through a committee meeting about which terrible death we'd all prefer. That honor fell to me on the third night in the Ashwife's tower, with her strays stacked around the hearth like cordwood and Juniper Okonkwo spreading that cursed map across the table as if it were a hand of cards and not an invitation to be buried alive.

"Three ways under," Juniper said.

"Three ways to die," said Doss, from his corner, where he'd been nursing the same cup of small beer for an hour out of what I suspect was principle rather than thirst.

"Three ways *under*," Juniper corrected. "The dying comes later."

That's the thing about keepers — or former keepers, or whatever Juniper was now that their gate was a lie and their life's work a doorstop for a king-sized coffin. They develop this flat, patient way of stating horrors, like a woman reading out laundry. It doesn't scare you. It just settles into your chest and waits.

The Ashwife leaned over the table. She'd tied back her grey hair with what looked suspiciously like one of Tam's spare bootlaces, and she smelled of woodsmoke and some herb I didn't recognize and didn't want to ask about, because everything in that tower was either medicinal or haunted, sometimes both.

"The Drowned Stair," she said, tapping the western line on the map. "The river route. The old kings cut it so water could carry away what the mountain didn't want. It floods with the thaw."

"It's midwinter," Tam said. "The thaw hasn't started."

"The thaw," the Ashwife said, "is the least of what's down there." She tapped again. "Then there's the Chimney — the old ventilation shaft above the court. A straight climb, near enough vertical, through rock that the Regent has been hollowing for years. And then—" her finger moved east, along a line drawn in faded red ink that I would've sworn hadn't been there when Juniper first unrolled the thing— "the Red Road. Through the bonefields."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. Vael lay across the doorway like a fallen tree, chin on paws, eyes half-lidded. The wolf-spirit had taken up residence in our company the way a stone takes up residence in a shoe: permanently, uncomfortably, and with a certain smug inevitability.

"And what does each one want?" Juniper asked quietly. "You said they demand something."

The Ashwife looked at them with something that wasn't quite pity. Pity is cheap; this was respect, which is worse.

"The Drowned Stair wants breath. You go down into water that remembers being a river, and it will try to take yours and give you its memories instead. Some come up speaking a language nobody's used in four hundred years. Some don't come up at all."

"Wonderful," Doss said. "I pilot boats. Now the boat pilots me."

"The Chimney wants hands," she went on. "It's a climb you make holding on to the Regent's own dreaming. Every grip you take, it feels. Every hold that breaks, it learns your name a little better."

"And the Red Road?" Tam asked, though his voice had gone thin, and I could tell from the look on his face that the scholar in him already knew, because scholars always know and then say it anyway so the rest of us can be properly horrified.

"The Red Road wants blood," the Ashwife said. "Not spilled. Given. Freely, at every milestone, until you reach the court too empty to turn back."

I stood up. Not dramatically — I'm a professional, and dramatics are for people who haven't yet figured out how much a horse costs — but I stood up, and I walked to the window, and I looked out at the snow coming down over Thornfell like the sky had decided to bury the evidence.

Here's what nobody tells you about running a crew: you spend years learning exactly how much each person can bear, and then one day the universe presents you with a bill itemizing it.

*

"You're doing the thing," Tam said, finding me in the stairwell with my ledger open and my pencil hovering.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you calculate exits. You get this little crease between your eyebrows, right here." He tapped his own forehead. "Doss says you do it before every job. He says it means either we're about to be rich or arrested."

"Both, usually. One after the other." I closed the ledger. There was nothing written in it. That was the problem. My whole life fit in columns — debts owed, debts paid, routes, tolls, bribes, the going rate for a customs man's silence broken down by season. Numbers kept the world honest. And there was no column for *how much is it worth to walk away from a keeper, a runaway, a ferryman, and a wolf, while the thing under the mountain grinds itself free.*

"You're thinking about leaving," Tam said. Not accusing. Just noting it, the way he noted everything, like facts were neutral and feelings were somebody else's department.

"I'm thinking about arithmetic," I said. "Four of us. Maybe five, if you count the dog."

"He's not a dog."

"He eats like six dogs. That counts for something." I rubbed the crease Tam had so helpfully identified. "Every route kills someone, Tam. That's not me being grim. That's the map. The Ashwife said it plain. Stair takes breath, Chimney takes hands, Red Road takes blood. Pick your poison, and pick who drinks it."

"So you're deciding who dies."

"No," I said, and heard my own voice come out harder than I meant. "I'm deciding whether anybody does. There's a difference, and the difference is I still have legs and a head start."

Tam nodded slowly. He's twenty, maybe, all elbows and borrowed books, and he has this habit of agreeing with you in a way that makes you feel like you've failed an exam. "And if you leave," he said, "where do you go? The passes are closing. The Regent's men are in every town between here and the lowlands looking for Juniper. You burned your ledgers at Cold Ford—"

"That was a tactical decision."

"You burned eleven years of contacts and safehouses to keep a map tube out of a garrison captain's hands. That wasn't tactics. That was—" he stopped himself, which was kind of him, but I finished it anyway, because apparently I'm a glutton.

"Sentiment," I said. "Yes. Well. We all have our vices."

He grinned at that, quick and crooked, and went back upstairs to argue with the Ashwife about whether the binding's grammar permitted exceptions, which is the sort of sentence I never thought I'd hear spoken aloud in my presence, let alone with such enthusiasm.

*

The scouts came in just past midnight.

I say scouts. I mean children. The Ashwife's household runs on strays — a one-eyed falconer, twin sisters who speak only to each other and to birds, a boy called Pip who couldn't have been more than twelve and who moved through snow like it owed him money. Pip came skidding into the hall with ice in his hair and terror all over him like a second coat.

"They're on the pass road," he gasped. "The hollow-eyed ones. Six of them, maybe eight, walking in this weather like it's nothing. Walking *through* it." He wrapped his arms around himself. "They don't make footprints, mistress. They just — the snow fills in behind them."

The Ashwife's face did something complicated and settled on grim. "How far?"

"A day. Less, if they don't stop. They don't seem like the stopping kind."

Everyone looked at Juniper. Everyone always looks at Juniper, which must be exhausting, though to be fair they'd volunteered for it by having a tragic destiny. But I noticed — because noticing things is my trade — that Juniper looked at me. First. Before anyone else. Like the question wasn't *what do we do* but *are we still a we.*

And here's the humiliating part, the part I'll deny under oath: my answer arrived before my pride could vote on it.

"We take the Stair," I said.

*

The argument lasted until dawn, and I'll give the highlights rather than the full transcript, since transcripts of that night would mostly read as *Doss: no* repeated forty times with variations.

Juniper wanted the Chimney, because it was fastest and because — I saw this clearly, even if they didn't say it — they wanted the climb that put distance between the Regent's reaching and everyone else. If the Chimney teaches the Regent your name, then let it learn a keeper's name, one already half-promised to it anyway. Self-sacrifice dressed up as strategy. I know the outfit well; I've worn it to funerals.

Tam argued for the Red Road on scholarly grounds — the binding's grammar needed a willing heart, and the Red Road was nothing *but* willingness, mile after mile of it, and might let him study the binding from inside as they went. Which sounded brave until you realized it also meant bleeding out gradually on a road paved with bones, and even Tam's enthusiasm flickered when Doss put it to him exactly that way.

Doss argued for nothing. Doss argued against everything, on principle, the way the sea argues against the shore. "The Stair drowns you. The Chimney drops you. The Road bleeds you. Stay here, fortify the tower, wait for spring." As if spring were a friend coming to visit rather than the deadline for the end of the world.

Vael said nothing for most of it, which should have told us something. When it finally spoke, its voice came out of the dark by the door like wind off a glacier. "The Stair passes beneath the old floodgates. I held those gates shut, once, when they were built. The water knows me." A pause. "It may listen."

"And if it doesn't?" I asked.

"Then it will drown us," Vael said, "and the question resolves itself."

Wolf humor. You get used to it. You don't enjoy it, but you get used to it.

*

I went up onto the wall-walk before first light, alone, because I needed to think and thinking requires nobody asking you what you're thinking about. The snow had stopped. The sky over Thornfell was doing that bruised grey thing it does before sunrise, and somewhere below us, a day's walk away, eight men without footprints were walking toward us through a blizzard without breaking stride.

Eleven years I'd run the border roads. Eleven years of choosing the route that cost least — least time, least risk, least blood, preferably mine, never the crew's. It's a good rule. It kept Doss alive through two wrecks and a hanging, kept Tam fed and uncaught, kept me solvent and mostly unbribed. Cost accounting. Every job balances, or you don't take it.

Except.

Except the courier died on Juniper's floor with an arrow through her and her last word a stranger's name, and she hadn't balanced any account. Except Juniper had spent a whole life guarding a door that was really a cage, and had learned it standing over a body, and had not once — not *once* — suggested we split the burden unevenly. Except Doss, who hates every plan, had spent the evening quietly sharpening the boathooks he'd bought off the Ashwife's falconer, because Doss complains his way down every road he chooses to walk. Except Tam had burned his own letters of recommendation from the University the second night here, unprompted, and when I asked why, he'd shrugged and said, "I'm not going back, am I?"

Nobody had said that out loud before. *I'm not going back.* Four words, and they rearranged my whole ledger.

Because here's the fracture, the real one, the one I'd been circling for two days: I wanted to leave. God help me, I wanted it like water. Take the horse, take the southern pass before the snow sealed it, be in Ketterbridge by week's end drinking someone else's bad wine with someone else's problems. Nobody would blame me. Smugglers aren't heroes; that's the entire point of the profession. We're the people who know where the exits are.

But I stood on that wall-walk with the cold working into my bones, and I did the sums honestly for once, all of them, and the total came out the same every time.

If I left, Juniper would take the Chimney alone. They'd never say it, but they'd do it — slip away quiet, the keeper's instinct, guard the door, be the sacrifice, die politely so the rest of us wouldn't have to choose. And Doss would follow them, because Doss pretends to be a coward and isn't, and Tam would follow Doss, and Vael would follow all of us like the world's largest and most depressing sheepdog, and they would all die one at a time, bravely, stupidly, without anyone there to tell them when a plan was rubbish.

Whereas if I stayed, we might all die at once, together, with someone telling them the plan was rubbish. Me. I was the someone. That was apparently my role now. Not guide, not captain — critic. Conscience with a pencil.

"Hell," I said, to the sunrise, to the mountains, to whatever grandmother of Juniper's was lurking in the stones taking notes. "Fine. Fine."

"You're talking to the sky again," Doss said behind me. He'd climbed up with two cups of something hot and foul and handed me one without asking, which is how Doss expresses affection: unsolicited beverages.

"I'm negotiating with the sky. It's going badly."

"Stair, then." He said it like a verdict.

"Stair," I agreed. "God help us."

"Aye." He drank. Below us, faint on the wind, I'd swear the mountain hummed — the same note Juniper hears in the Gate, or used to, the note that's gone wrong. It sounded almost like laughter. Almost like a warning. With this family, it's always both.

We went down to pack.

* * *

By noon we stood at the mouth of the Drowned Stair, where the river goes underground and the mountain swallows it whole. The water ran black and fast, and the cold coming out of the cave mouth had a taste to it — old, mineral, faintly sweet, like snow left too long in a cellar.

Vael stepped to the edge of the current and bowed its great grey head, and I watched the water slow. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Well," I said, swinging my pack onto my shoulder and trying to sound like a woman who hadn't just bet eleven years of survival instincts on a talking wolf's credit. "After you."
