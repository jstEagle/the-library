## 9. The Drowned Stair

I want it on the record, before anything else happens, that I objected.

Not loudly — I'd learned by then that objecting loudly around Wren Halloway got you assigned extra watches — but I raised a hand at the war council like a good little scholar and said, "Historically speaking, underground rivers have killed more travelers than bandits, wolves, and bad weather combined," and Wren looked at me over the map with that expression she reserves for people who quote statistics at her before breakfast, and said, "Then you'll be pleased to know we're not going *over* an underground river, bookworm. We're going *through* one."

Which is the kind of sentence that should end with a lawyer present.

The Ashwife's messenger had brought word back with us from the burned watchtower: the passes above were crawling with hollow-eyed men, sweeping every road that led toward the mountain's heart. The high routes were dead. The low routes were watched. What remained was the route nobody sane used, the one marked on the courier's map in faded blue ink and annotated, in a hand I've since come to believe belonged to a woman with a grudge against her own descendants, with a single line:

*The river remembers the way. Trust it. Do not trust the stairs.*

"The stairs?" Doss had asked, squinting at the map.

Nobody had an answer for him then. We got one later, and it came up out of the dark wearing water.

* * *

The entrance was a smuggler's door set into the riverbank two days downstream of Cold Ford — a slab of stone dressed to look like flood rubble, which is exactly what it looks like unless you know where the hinge is, which I did not, and which Doss apparently did, because he walked straight to it, spat on his hands, and said "Haven't been through here since I was a boy" with the fondness other men reserve for their mothers' cooking.

"You've been through the Drowned Stair before?" Juniper asked.

"Once. Going down." He hauled the slab open on groaning iron. Black water breathed out at us, cold and mineral-smelling, and beneath it something else — old stone, old air, old *waiting*. "Coming back up we went the long way."

"Why?"

"Because the stairs were drowned when we came back." He said it the way you'd mention the bread had gone stale. Then he picked up his pole and stepped into the dark, because Doss is a man who treats catastrophe as a scheduling matter.

The caverns opened almost at once into a vastness that made my chest hurt. Our lantern-light couldn't find the ceiling; the river ran wide and slow and black as spilled ink, and along its edges rose what the map had named — staircases, hundreds of them, cut into the rock in tiers, ascending into darkness on both sides of the water. Some were whole. Some had crumbled into the river centuries ago. All of them, as far as our light reached, stood half-drowned, their lower steps vanishing under black water without so much as a ripple, as though the river had risen long ago and simply forgotten to fall again.

"Who built stairs in a cave?" Wren asked.

"Who built a court under a mountain?" Juniper answered, and touched the wall, and I saw them go still, listening for a hum only they could hear. Whatever they heard, they didn't share it. They'd been doing that a lot since the Gate. I was starting to keep a list.

Doss poled us downstream on a flat-bottomed smuggler's skiff he swore he could carry alone, which was a lie, but the kind of lie that builds character. Vael ran along the banks — the wolf wouldn't get in the boat, wouldn't explain why, just loped along the drowned stair-tiers above us with its eyes catching lantern-light like green coins. Wren sat in the bow with a crossbow across her knees. Juniper sat amidships with the map tube strapped across their chest like a second heartbeat.

And I sat in the stern, being useful, which is my least favorite way to be useful, because Doss had decided my job was sounding the depth with a weighted line and calling out the readings.

"Twelve feet."

"Twelve feet, he says," Doss echoed, contentedly, poling on.

"Eleven."

"River's getting shy."

"Ten — Doss, the walls are—" I stopped. The walls were narrowing. That wasn't the alarming part; rivers do narrow, it's practically their hobby. The alarming part was that the drowned stairs on either bank were converging too, tier over tier, until our light showed them interlocking overhead like the ribs of some enormous dead thing, and the space between them — the space we were floating into — was barely wider than the skiff.

"Nine feet," I said, because my mouth hadn't gotten the memo about dignity.

"Aye," said Doss. "Here's where you'll want to put the pole down and hold on to something that loves you."

"I don't—"

"Grab the rope, boy."

I grabbed the rope.

* * *

I will describe what happened next as precisely as I can, because precision is the only prayer I have left, and also because Wren has accused me of exaggerating ever since, and I intend to win that argument posthumously if necessary.

For perhaps four hundred yards the river ran fast between those interlocking stairs, and Doss handled it like a man who had been born knowing how — pole braced against stair-edges and stone teeth, the skiff corkscrewing through chutes of white water that appeared out of nowhere and vanished just as fast. It was, I admit now, magnificent. It was also the single most sustained experience of terror of my life, and I once presented a thesis to Master Ellery Vance, who once made a grown man cry with a question about footnote formatting.

Then the tunnel ahead of us collapsed.

There was no warning. One moment there was passage; the next there was a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and a section of stair-tier the size of a chapel peeled off the right-hand wall and came down into the river, and the river — the river that remembered the way, the river we were supposed to trust — *rose*.

"BACKWATER!" Doss roared, and for the first time since I'd met him he sounded afraid, and I want to be honest: that frightened me more than the falling rock did. "Backwater, backwater — paddle, all of you, PADDLE!"

We paddled. There are no words for the next several minutes that would survive printing, so I'll summarize: black water climbing, the skiff surging backward toward the collapse, debris in the current like the fingers of drowned giants, Wren cursing with a fluency I have since attempted to transcribe for scholarly purposes and failed, Vael somewhere above us on the stairs screaming — actually screaming, a sound like a landslide given a voice — and Juniper with their arm around the map tube, holding it against their body, keeping it dry, keeping it safe, because that was the mission, that was always the mission, the map mattered more than any one of us, and none of us had said it aloud but all of us knew it.

The water took us sideways. The skiff struck a submerged step and slewed around. And the map's carrying strap — the strap Juniper had worn across their chest for nine days without once loosening — caught on the jagged edge of a broken stair.

I heard it tear.

I saw the tube go into the water.

I didn't think. This is the part I keep coming back to, lying awake afterward: I did not think. Every decision I have ever made well, I have made badly, after long consideration, with footnotes. But the map hit the black water and started to sink — it was waxed canvas, it fought the water for maybe three seconds — and my body simply left me. I went over the side into cold that closed around my chest like a fist.

People ask drowning survivors what it was like, and they say things like *peaceful*, and I would like to formally call them liars. It was not peaceful. It was loud. The river roared inside my skull, and the cold went through my clothes and into my bones and squeezed, and below me — below me in water with no bottom that my eyes could find — the map tube sank, trailing silver bubbles, turning over and over like a thing asleep.

I caught it at the last edge of my breath. My fingers closed on wet canvas and I kicked for the surface and the river, which had been merely hostile until then, became personal. The backwater current had a grip on my boots. It pulled. Not hard — patiently, the way a scholar pulls a page out of your hands to correct your citation — and my lungs were empty and full of fire, and the lantern-light above me shrank to a coin, a fingernail, a spark—

A hand closed on my collar.

It hauled, and the world tore open into noise and lamplight and Wren's face, furious, alive, and Juniper's arms around my shoulders dragging me over the gunwale onto the deck boards, and I lay there coughing up half the river while the other half tried to follow, clutching the map tube to my chest with both arms like a child with a stolen sweet.

"You absolute *idiot*," Wren said, kneeling over me, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, which I pretend not to have noticed and will deny noticing forever.

"You went in after it," Juniper said. They were soaked to the elbows. Their hands were shaking. I remember thinking that was strange, because Juniper Okonkwo did not shake; Juniper Okonkwo stood very still and heard hums no one else could hear. "Tam. You went in after it. You can't swim."

"How do you know I can't swim?"

"Because you told me. Day three. At length. With examples."

Well. There goes my legend.

* * *

We grounded the skiff an hour later on a shelf of dry stone where the stairs widened into a landing — an old one, carved with worn figures that might have been kings or might have been floods or might, Wren suggested, have been whoever was in charge of maintenance, who clearly hadn't been paid in a while. We got a fire going from driftwood the river had carried down through cracks we preferred not to think about. Doss checked the hull twice, pronounced her sound, and then sat down heavily and drank from his flask without offering it to anyone, which from Doss was a confession of profound distress.

Vael came down off the stairs at last and lay near the fire, close enough that its warmth reached me, which I understood — correctly, it turned out — as an apology.

"You screamed," I told it. My teeth were still chattering. "On the stairs. Wolves don't scream."

"Wolves don't drown," Vael said. "That sound was older than me. It was the stairs coming down. They have been coming down for six hundred years, scholar. Tonight you heard the mountain finish a sentence." Its green eyes fixed on the map tube, drying by the fire. "You went into the water for it."

"It's rather important."

"People are rather important. You chose the paper."

"I—" I opened my mouth and found nothing scholarly in it at all. "Someone died bringing that map. She bled out on a keeper's floor with her hand on it. It seemed — rude. To let the river have her last work."

Vael considered this for a long time, the way it considers everything, as though each sentence were a bone it needed to crack before swallowing.

"When my master bound himself," it said finally, "there was a scholar at his court who argued against it. Loudly. For years. When Aldric went under the mountain anyway, the scholar followed — not because he believed in the binding, but because someone had to *witness* it. Someone had to write down what was traded away, so that someday, someone could read it and say: this was wrong, and here is how it was done, and here is how it might be undone." The wolf's eyes reflected the fire. "He drowned in these caves, I have always suspected. His notes never came out. But you did."

"That's not a compliment, that's a eulogy with better casting."

"It is both," said Vael, "as most true things are," and it put its great head down on its paws and closed its eyes, and I sat there dripping by the fire feeling something I didn't have a citation for.

Juniper came and sat beside me after a while. They didn't say anything for a long time. That's the thing about Juniper — they treat silence like a room they're letting you warm up in.

"The map's warped," they said at last. "Water got in at the seam. Some of the western passages are blotted out."

I felt myself go pale. "How much?"

"We won't know until we need it." They turned the tube over in their hands. "When you went under — I couldn't reach you. Wren had my collar. There was a moment where it was the map or you, and I—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "I was already moving toward you. Before I decided anything. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Well." They handed me the map tube — handed *me*, the runaway student who'd stowed away in a smuggling boat to escape a scandal at the University of Corvale, the outsider, the ballast, the depth-sounder. "You hold it tonight. You earned the weight."

I held it. It weighed nothing at all, and it weighed everything, and outside the firelight the drowned stairs climbed away into the dark in both directions, patient as arithmetic.

I slept, eventually, with the tube strapped across my own chest, and dreamed of a scholar in old robes writing by lantern-light in a cave, and woke with the unreasonable certainty that he'd been smiling.

* * *

We broke camp at what Doss solemnly declared was morning, based on evidence he declined to provide. The river below the landing had fallen back to its old level — the collapse upstream had dammed whatever surge had flooded us, and the water ran quiet again, black and deep and, I thought, smug.

We were an hour downstream, poling through a stretch where the stairs gave way to smooth living rock veined with something that glimmered faintly gold, when Vael suddenly lifted its head from the bow of the skiff — it had consented, finally, to ride, and none of us mentioned it — and its ears went flat.

"What?" Wren said.

"Smoke," the wolf said. "Old smoke. And under it — voices. Many. Moving together."

Juniper stood up so fast the skiff rocked. "Where?"

"Ahead. Around the bend. And keeper—" the wolf's green eyes found Juniper's, and I saw its hackles rise along its spine like a row of drawn knives, "—they do not smell like men. They smell like nothing at all."

Around the bend, then. Around the bend, where our lantern-light ended and the drowned dark began, something was waiting — many somethings, walking in step, breathing without breath — and the river, that faithful old remembering river, carried us straight toward it, because rivers, I am forced to conclude, remember the way whether we want to go or not.
