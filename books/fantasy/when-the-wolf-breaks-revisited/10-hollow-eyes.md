## 10. Hollow Eyes

I have kept the Hollow Gate for eleven years, and in that time I learned exactly one universal truth: nothing good ever happens after midnight on a mountain. The Gate hummed its warnings at all hours, but the *bad* things — rockslides, wolves, the occasional smuggler with more bravado than sense — those came with the dark. So when I woke on the fifth night out from the Ashwife's tower with my teeth aching and no idea why, I did not need a grandmother's ghost to tell me something was wrong.

The fire had burned down to a sullen orange eye. Doss slept like a felled tree by the wagon, one hand still curled around his tankard as if it might wander off without him. Tam was a knot of blankets and open books, having fallen asleep mid-argument with a footnote. Wren was on watch, which meant she was sitting up and pretending not to sleep, because Wren Halloway considered unconsciousness a personal failing.

And Vael was standing at the edge of the camp with every hair along its spine lifted like the quills of a very large, very offended hedgehog.

"Wolf," I whispered, sliding out of my bedroll.

*Yes,* Vael said, without turning its head. Its voice always arrived somewhere between my ears rather than in them, like a thought I hadn't quite finished having. *They come.*

"Who comes?"

Vael didn't answer. That was answer enough. In the weeks since the wolf had joined us — shadowing us first, then speaking, then confessing just enough truth about the old king to keep us all thoroughly unsettled — I had learned its silences were louder than its words.

I woke Wren with a boot to her bedroll, which is how she preferred, having once threatened me with a knife for the alternative. She came up already holding the knife, eyes clear, which told me everything about the kind of life she'd lived before I'd arrested her at the border.

"Trouble?" she said.

"Vael thinks so."

"Then trouble." She rolled her shoulder until it popped, an old habit, and nudged Tam awake with considerably less tenderness than she'd shown me. "Up, scholar. Homework time's over."

Tam surfaced from his blankets blinking, glasses askew, clutching a book to his chest the way other people clutched children or religious icons. "Is it bandits? Please say it's bandits. Bandits are *legible*. Bandits want things you can negotiate about."

"What do hollow-eyed men want?" Wren asked him dryly.

"Nothing," Tam said. Then, catching himself: "That's rather the problem."

Doss we woke by simply saying his name once, quietly. He was upright before he was fully awake, reaching for the boat-hook he carried through country that had no rivers in it, because Doss believed in being prepared the way other men believe in gods.

We put out the fire. We moved the wagon across the trail to narrow the approach. We took positions among the rocks the way Wren directed, because for all that I knew every path through Thornfell better than any living soul, Wren knew every way a fight could go wrong, and those two kinds of knowledge are cousins.

Then we waited.

Waiting, I have found, is where the mind does its worst work. Mine went to the courier who had died on my floor with an arrow in her and a map in her fist, whispering *the king is awake.* It went to the Ashwife's face when she read that map, all the warmth draining out of her hearth-lit kitchen. It went to the thing I'd felt through my palms for eleven years whenever I pressed them to the Gate — the hum that had gone flat on the north face, the seal failing thread by thread while I polished stones and recited rounds and believed, believed so hard it hurt to remember, that duty was the same thing as purpose.

"They're here," Vael said.

I never heard them. That was the horror of it. No footfalls. No breath. One moment the treeline above our camp was trees; the next it was men, six or seven of them, coming down through the rocks with the unhurried gait of people who have done this many times and been interrupted none.

Their eyes caught the starlight wrong. Not reflecting it — *returning* it, flat and pale, like light off a frozen pond over deep water. Hollow eyes. The Ashwife's word made flesh, and flesh, apparently, was negotiable.

"Now!" Wren barked, and the night tore open.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and orders and at least the pretense of rules. This was a brawl in the dark between five exhausted travelers and seven things wearing men like badly fitted coats.

Doss met the first one coming down the trail and simply *stopped* it, boat-hook across its chest, planting it against the slope with the patient immovability of a man who had spent thirty years holding ferries steady against current. The hollow-eyed man pushed. Doss did not appear to notice. Somewhere in that exchange I heard Doss say, mildly, "No," as though declining an offer of tea, and I will carry the absurdity of that to my grave.

Wren fought like a rumor — everywhere briefly, then gone, then behind you. Two knives, no wasted motion, and a running commentary that would have gotten her excommunicated from any respectable religion. Tam, bless him, did not fight at all. He threw the fire-pot.

I want to be clear about this, because Tam would never tell it properly himself: the boy who couldn't swim and apologized to furniture when he bumped into it took the clay pot of burning pitch we'd prepared for emergencies, lit it off Wren's knife-blade, and hurled it into the center of the attackers with the full-body commitment of someone who had read about this in a book and decided, correctly, that theory was sufficient. Fire bloomed across the rocks. Two of the hollow-eyed men walked straight into it without flinching, because whatever animated them had apparently skimped on the self-preservation clause.

Which left three unaccounted for, and one of those was already inside our line.

It came at me out of the smoke with a soldier's economy — no flourish, no rage, just hands. I got my staff up, caught a wrist, turned a grapple the way my grandmother had drilled into me before I could write my own name. Keeper's arts. Hold the line. Do not yield the threshold.

The man's face was inches from mine. Up close, the hollowness was worse — his eyes weren't white so much as *absent*, pupils like pinholes punched through paper, and beneath his skin something shifted that skin should not contain. His mouth opened.

"*Juniper Okonkwo,*" it said, in a voice like wind down a well.

Not a threat. A greeting. That was the worst part — the terrible courtesy of it, the way you'd greet someone you'd been expecting.

My hand was still on his wrist. Bare skin on bare skin. And the world folded.

* * *

I have touched the Gate every day of my life. I know what old magic feels like under the palm: a hum, a patience, a vast attention turned elsewhere. What I felt through this man's wrist was the same substance wearing a different shape, and that recognition — that sickening *sameness* — is what let it take me.

I was looking through his eyes.

I saw our camp from above and behind myself: small, firelit, desperate. I saw Wren drive her knife into the chest of a man who did not bleed properly, saw Tam beating at another with a book — the footnote argument, I later learned; he regretted it immediately — saw Doss still holding his, both of them now leaning into the contest with the air of two neighbors disputing a fence line.

And then the eyes turned inward, or downward, or some direction that has no compass bearing, and I saw the buried court.

I don't have the vocabulary. I'm a gatekeeper; I have words for stone, for weather, for the slow arithmetic of seals. But I will try.

A hall vaster than any cathedral, built of living wood — not timber *cut* and assembled, but grown, roots braided into pillars and pillars flowering into a roof that dripped pale light instead of water. The whole space breathed. Slowly. Like something sleeping that had not yet decided whether to wake.

At its heart stood a throne of roots, and on the throne sat a king.

He wore a crown of black iron set with a stone that swallowed light. He wore, too, the rags of majesty — a mantle gone grey, rings sunk deep into swollen fingers. And he wore, worst of all, the expression of a man waiting for a guest. Not raging. Not straining at chains. *Waiting.* Patient as the hall around him, because he was the hall around him, or it was him, the distinction long since rotted away.

The Regent looked up.

Through the dead man's eyes, through leagues of mountain and memory, it looked directly at me — at the me crouched behind its puppet's gaze — and it smiled. I felt that smile the way you feel frost forming on a window. It knew me. Not my name, not my duty. *Me.* The shape of my loneliness. The exact weight of the years I'd spent talking to a humming wall because there was no one else. It had been studying me the way I studied weather patterns, and it liked what it had learned, and its liking was the most frightening thing I have ever felt, because it was warm.

*Soon, keeper,* something said, in no voice at all. *You have carried the map so faithfully. You have gathered your little family so tenderly. Thank you. I could not have arranged it better myself.*

The vision snapped shut.

* * *

I came back to myself on the ground with Wren's hands on my shoulders and her face very close, saying my name in the flat, repeated tone she used for people she was afraid for. The hollow-eyed man lay crumpled beside me, empty in a new and permanent way, like a coat whose wearer had finally stepped out. Around the camp: fire dying, four more bodies, one hollow-eyed man fled into the dark — I learned later Doss had released him, deliberately, with a nod, which made sense to absolutely no one but Doss.

"I'm here," I said. My voice came out scraped. "I'm — give me a moment."

"Take the moment." Wren didn't move. "Take several."

Tam hovered, glasses cracked, book visibly dented. "Your eyes went strange," he offered. "Grey. Like — like the north face of the Gate. I noted the similarity immediately."

"Thank you, Tam. That's exactly the comparison I wanted."

"You're welcome." A pause. "It wasn't a comfort comparison."

"I know."

They gave me the moment. I spent it shaking, quietly, where the shaking was least likely to be entered into evidence. Because here is what I could not say yet, what I am only now setting down: I had seen the throne. I had seen the crowned figure waiting on it. And beneath the horror, beneath the cold, there had been one flicker — half a heartbeat, no longer — when the king's head had bowed, when the iron crown had tilted, and through the Regent's stolen sight I had glimpsed something underneath the hollow thing like a man drowning under ice, mouthing words I couldn't hear.

Two figures on one throne. One wearing the other. And one of them, impossibly, *begging.*

"Juniper." Vael stood over me, close enough that its breath stirred my hair. The wolf's eyes held no hollowness at all — only an ancient, weary grief that suddenly looked very much like guilt. "What did you see?"

I looked up at the spirit bound to the old king, the creature that had appeared the moment the map left my hands, that knew these roads too well, that spoke of its master's prison in the tense of someone describing their own house.

I saw the throne. I saw the waiting. I saw the smile that had known me like a friend.

And I realized there was one member of our company who had never once answered a question about the binding directly — who always turned the conversation, always paid in truths that cost nothing.

"Vael," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Sit down. All of us are going to talk about what you haven't been telling me — starting with why that thing on the throne sent its regards to *me*, and ending with exactly what a willing heart has to do with a binding."

The wolf did not move. The wolf did not need to. I watched the confession arrive in its eyes a full breath before its mouth opened, and I understood, with a keeper's certainty, that the last honest thing the Gate had ever hidden from me was standing on four legs in front of me, about to speak.
