## 12. Wren's Choice

I've been caught before. Twice, if you count the incident with the customs barge at Millbrook, which I don't, because technically I was asleep and the boat was doing the smuggling without my input. But there's a particular flavor to being caught when you're carrying something that matters more than your own neck, and I can tell you it tastes like river water and cold iron, and it goes down about as smooth as you'd expect.

They took us at the narrows below Cold Ford, where the road pinches between the cliff and the current and there's nowhere to run that a crossbow bolt won't follow. Doss went down first — not because he's slow, but because he stepped in front of me, the great sentimental ox, and took a pike shaft across the shoulder for his trouble. Then someone put a boot between my shoulders, and I ate gravel, and when I looked up there were eight of them in crown grey, and their sergeant had my map satchel strap wrapped around his fist.

Not the map. The strap. Small mercies.

"Well," I said, spitting out a pebble. "This is embarrassing for everyone involved."

"Shut up," said the sergeant, who clearly had no imagination whatsoever.

I want to be clear about something, since history — if anyone ever writes it down, which given our luck will be Tam, so it'll be three hundred pages long with footnotes — might get the impression I was frightened. I was. Of course I was. But fear is just information, and what the information said was: *you knew this could happen, Halloway, and you walked into the narrows anyway because the other road adds two days, and two days is midwinter's whole margin.* So really, if we're assigning blame, assign it to geography.

The sergeant hauled me up by the collar and got his face close to mine, close enough that I could smell the boiled mutton on his breath, and said, "Wren Halloway."

"That's me. You've heard of me? I'm flattered. Usually people only say my name like that when they're reading charges."

"I said shut up." He shook me once, efficient, professional. "We know what you are. We know what you carry. Captain Vess wants the courier's tube, and she wants the keeper, and she'll trade you and your fat friend here for both."

Doss, kneeling in the mud with his arm cradled wrong, managed to look deeply offended. "Fat?"

"He means sturdy," I told him. "It's a compliment. In the army they promote you for it."

* * *

They marched us down to the ford garrison, which is less a fortress than a tollhouse that grew ambitions — a stone square building with a palisade and a view of nothing but water and regret. They put us in the same cell, which I appreciated, because Doss snores, and if I'm going to spend a night awaiting judgment I'd rather do it to a rhythm I know.

They took my boots. That's standard. What wasn't standard was how carefully they went through my things — not the rough pat-down of soldiers looking for coins, but the methodical search of people who'd been told exactly what to look for. A leather map tube, wax-sealed, bloodstained at one end. Everything else they tossed aside like fish guts.

Which meant Captain Vess knew about Juniper. Knew the keeper carried the map now. Knew we were traveling together.

"They've gone ahead," Doss said quietly, once the door had banged shut. He was testing his shoulder against the wall, wincing, cataloguing damage the way he catalogs cargo. "Juniper and Tam and the wolf. They'll have taken the high track when we didn't come back to camp."

"Then they're safe, and this was all worth it." I leaned my head back against the stone. "You should've let them take me alone, you know. Stepping in front of a pike. At your age."

"My age," Doss said, "is forty-one."

"Ancient. Practically a relic. There are standing stones younger than you."

He did the thing he does instead of laughing, which is a sort of seismic event in his chest that shakes his beard. Then he went quiet, and after a while he said, in the careful voice he uses for hard cargo and harder truths: "What happens tomorrow, Wren?"

And there it was, the question I'd been carrying since the pike hit him. Because I know how these transactions go. I've been on the selling side of enough of them. Crown offers a smuggler her freedom; the smuggler gives up whatever the crown wants; the smuggler walks away lighter and poorer and alive. Everyone knows the dance. Everyone's done it. I'd have done it myself, six months ago, for anything less than what's in that tube.

Six months ago I didn't have a keeper who talks to gates. I didn't have a scholar who apologizes to rivers. I didn't have a wolf-spirit who looks at me like I'm a puzzle he hasn't solved yet, or an Ashwife who fed me stew like I was somebody worth feeding. Six months ago all I had was a ledger.

"You remember the Marrow Road job?" I asked him.

"The one where you nearly drowned?"

"The one where I *did* drown, briefly, and came up holding the strongbox, which is the important part." I turned my head to look at him in the dark. "There were eleven names in that job. Eleven people who trusted me to move what they needed moving, keep what they needed secret, and never — *never* — sell a route to save my own skin. That was the whole religion of it, Doss. Smuggling's just theft until you make it a covenant. Then it's a faith."

"And tomorrow," Doss said, "they test your faith."

"Tomorrow," I said, "they find out what I believe."

* * *

Captain Vess received me the next morning in the garrison's common room, which had been cleared of everything except a table, two chairs, and herself. She was younger than I expected, which is always either good news or very bad news, and she had the map satchel open on the table in front of her — empty, of course, because I'd sewn the tube into the lining of Doss's coat weeks ago and she'd searched my bag, not him, because nobody searches Doss, he has the kind of face searchers skip.

She didn't know that yet. I made sure my face didn't know it either.

"Wren Halloway," she said, and gestured to the chair. "Sit."

"I'd rather stand. Sitting implies negotiation."

"This isn't a negotiation. It's an offer." She turned the empty satchel over with one finger. "You give me the courier's map and tell me where the keeper is bound, and you and Doss walk out of here. Full pardon for the Millbrook business, the Marrow Road business, and everything else in your file. Which, incidentally, is thicker than most Bibles."

"Flatterer."

"Halloway." Something moved behind her eyes, and for a moment she sounded almost tired rather than official. "I've read your file. Every page. Do you know what struck me? Not the crimes. The consistency. Eleven years, forty-three documented runs, and not one time you abandoned a client. Not one time you sold a route under pressure. There's a man in Ironhollow prison right now because you wouldn't give up his name, and he wasn't even paying you anymore."

"He was a bad tipper," I said. "But he was *my* bad tipper."

"Why?" And she asked it genuinely, leaning forward. "Why throw your life away for strangers? What are they to you? A gatekeeper, a runaway student, a ferryman, a *wolf*? You owe them nothing."

And that was the moment I stopped performing, because she'd asked a real question and I've never learned how to do anything but answer those honestly. It's a character flaw. My mother despaired of it.

"What are they to me," I repeated. I thought about Juniper standing on the ridge at dawn with the wind pulling at them, listening to a hum nobody else could hear, wearing duty like a coat two sizes too big and refusing to take it off. I thought about Tam, who'd nearly drowned in the dark water holding the map above the surface like it mattered more than air, because to him knowledge does matter more than air. I thought about Doss's shoulder taking a pike meant for me. I thought about a wolf who'd confessed to helping bind a king and then kept walking beside us anyway, every mile, doing penance with his paws.

"Nothing," I said. "That's the trick of it, Captain. Nobody owes anybody anything. That's what makes it worth something. Your king binds people with oaths and chains and crowns. Mine—" I shrugged. "Mine just shows up. Day after day. You show up enough times for each other and eventually you're not strangers anymore, and there's no word for what you are except the one nobody likes saying out loud because it sounds soft. Family. Found, not born. And found things, Captain—" I smiled at her, and I mean it when I say it was probably not a pleasant smile. "Found things are held tighter than inherited ones. Because you chose them."

Vess sat back. For a long moment the only sound was the river through the window, going about its business under the ice.

"The offer stands until sundown," she said finally. "After that, you go to Ironhollow, and your fat friend goes to—"

"Sturdy," said Doss, from the doorway, where he was standing flanked by two guards with a sling on his arm and the expression of a man about to ruin everyone's afternoon. "Honestly, the slander in this garrison."

* * *

Here's the thing about ledgers. Every smuggler keeps one — routes, contacts, debts, dead drops, the whole architecture of a hidden life written in cipher in a book small enough to hide in a boot. Mine had been my mother's before mine. Eleven years of entries. Forty-three runs. Names of people in nine towns who trusted me. Codes that would unravel half the informal economy of the borderlands if they fell into the wrong hands, and would hang a dozen of my old clients if they fell into *these* hands.

I'd had it in my coat the whole time. Through the ambush, the march, the cell, the interview. Vess's people had gone through my belongings twice and never found it, because it was sewn into the same seam as the map tube — one hiding place, two treasures, and only one of them hers to want.

Sundown came. A guard brought water and the news that the captain was waiting for my answer. Doss stood at the cell door with his good hand gripping the bars, and I could see him working through the same arithmetic I was: give them the map, walk free, let Juniper face the Regent's agents alone. Or don't.

"Don't," I said out loud, because apparently I narrate my own crises now. Age, probably.

"Wren—"

"Doss. If they get that map, they get to Juniper before midwinter. If they get my ledger, they get every soul who ever trusted me. There's a woman in Saltmarsh who named her daughter after me because I got medicine through a blockade. There's a boy in Millbrook who'd swing from a yardarm by spring." I pulled the ledger out of the seam, and it looked very small in my hand, the way important things do. "This book is my life. Every road I ever walked, everyone who ever walked one with me. And I am about to do something that is either the bravest or stupidest thing I have ever done, and given my history, the bar is *high*."

The guard outside the door was watching us through the grate with the flat boredom of the professionally incurious. I gave him my sweetest smile.

"Excuse me," I said. "Could you fetch Captain Vess? Tell her the smuggler's decided to cooperate. She'll want to see this personally."

He went. Doss stared at me. "You're giving her the map?"

"I'm giving her *something*. Watch."

Vess arrived inside of ten minutes, alone, which told me she wanted no witnesses to whatever bargain we struck. She stood at the bars with her hand resting on her sword hilt and her eyes on the little book in my fingers.

"You're cooperating," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm ending the conversation." I held the ledger up. "This is everything. Routes, contacts, codes. Eleven years. You want to know what I am? What I've been, who I've protected, where every hidden road in the borderlands runs? It's all here. One book. Worth more than the map, if you're honest about it — the map leads to one buried court. This leads to *everywhere*."

Her eyes had changed. Greed does that, even to disciplined officers — especially to disciplined officers, who've been starved for it so long they gorge ugly. "Hand it through the bars."

"One condition. Not freedom. Not pardon." I looked her dead in the eye. "You burn it yourself, in front of me, and you let the smoke decide what it buys. Because here's my price, Captain: I want you to understand what you're asking people to sell. I want you to watch eleven years of a life go up, and I want you to remember it next time you put an offer like today's to somebody in a cell."

"That's not a condition, that's theater."

"Theater's the only honest currency left," I said. "Everything else gets debased. Hand me the lamp."

She hesitated — I'll grant her that, the hesitation was real, some part of her officer's brain flagging that something was off — but greed's a loud voice and discipline is quiet, and she fetched the lamp herself and set it inside the cell door, and stood watching through the bars with her arms crossed while I knelt on the stones with eleven years of my life in my hands.

I opened the ledger to the first page. My mother's handwriting, faded brown: the first route she ever ran, along the river, when I was four years old and riding in a crate marked *dried herring*. Below it, my own first entry, aged fourteen, ink-blotted, misspelled. *Marrow Rd. 3 crates. All safe.*

"All safe," I read aloud. "That's how you sign off a completed run. Three words. Doesn't look like much. But it means everyone who set out came home. Forty-three times, Captain. All safe. All safe. All safe." I touched the lamp flame to the corner of the page.

Paper catches slow, at first. Then all at once. I held the book in both hands and let it burn, turning the pages into the flame one at a time so it wouldn't gutter, feeding my mother's routes and my clients' names and my ciphers and my codes into the fire, and the smoke was thick and bitter and smelled like every road I'd ever walked, and I confess — I confess freely, write it down, Tam — I cried while I did it. Not elegantly. Ugly, snotty, kneeling-in-a-cell crying, while a crown captain watched through bars and Doss put his good hand on my shoulder and left it there.

"There," I said, when the last page curled to ash and the cover cracked and split, and I sat back on my heels with my palms blackened and my face ruined. "That's what you asked me to sell. Now it's smoke. And so's your bargain, Captain, because here's the joke of it—" and I laughed, wet and cracked and honest, "—that wasn't the map either. You still don't have it. You never had leverage. All you ever had was me, and I just spent myself, and it bought exactly what I wanted it to buy. Nothing for you. Everything for them."

For a long moment Vess didn't move. Then she looked at the ashes on the cell floor, and at me, and something complicated crossed her face — anger, yes, but underneath it something that might have been respect, or might have been the recognition of a debt she couldn't collect.

"You could have walked away," she said quietly. "Freedom. Pardon. A clean file. You burned it for a gatekeeper and a runaway."

"I told you," I said. "Found things are held tightest." I gathered the ashes into my palm — don't ask me why, grief makes you do strange ceremonies — and closed my fist. "Now. Are you going to arrest me properly, or shall we sit here awkwardly until midwinter comes to us all?"

* * *

They locked us back up, of course. Theater has its limits. But something had shifted in the garrison — I could feel it through the bars, guards not meeting my eye, a young one bringing extra bread and pretending it was a mistake. Word travels in barracks. By nightfall every soldier in Cold Ford knew the smuggler in cell one had burned her own book rather than sell, and soldiers are romantics under the discipline, every one of them, or they wouldn't survive the winters.

Doss sat down beside me in the dark. His shoulder was strapped and splinted, and he smelled of woodsmoke because he'd insisted on helping feed the fire, which is the most Doss sentence I've ever constructed.

"Your mother's book," he said.

"My mother's book," I agreed.

"She'd have approved."

"She'd have called me an idiot and then secretly been proud, which is the family way." I leaned my head against the stone. The ash was still under my fingernails. I found I didn't mind. "Eleven years, Doss. Gone in five minutes. You know the strange thing? I feel lighter. Like the book was heavier than I knew. All those secrets, all those roads — I was carrying the whole hidden country around in my coat, and now it's smoke, and I'm just..." I flexed my blackened fingers. "Just a woman in a cell with her friends. Who knew poverty of possessions could feel like this."

"You're not free yet."

"No. But I'm *ours*, and that turns out to be the better treasure." I closed my eyes. "Besides. Juniper's coming."

"You don't know that."

"I know that better than I've ever known anything." I smiled in the dark, thinking of a stubborn keeper walking out of the mountains with a scholar and a wolf at their heels, thinking of smugglers' tricks and wolf-strength and a garrison that had already started liking us too much to be truly dangerous. "They left us behind once to keep us safe. They'll come back for us anyway, because that's the whole disease we've caught, all of us. Showing up."

Somewhere beyond the walls, the river ran on under the ice, patient as everything that intends to win. And somewhere north of it, I'd have sworn I could already hear it — faint, impossible, threading through the stone like a hum through a gate —

the sound of someone coming who doesn't leave their people behind.
