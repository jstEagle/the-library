## 13. The Rescue at Cold Ford

*Juniper*

The trouble with rescuing people, I decided somewhere in the third hour of lying belly-down in a frozen ditch above Cold Ford, is that you spend most of it doing nothing at all.

The garrison sat below us like a squat brown toad: a converted tollhouse, a palisade of sharpened pine, a watchtower that some ambitious corporal had clearly drawn himself because no architect would claim it. Torches guttered along the walls. Somewhere inside, Wren Halloway was chained to something, being asked questions by men who had never once in their lives been asked one.

"Four guards on the wall," Tam whispered beside me, squinting through a brass spyglass he'd produced from somewhere inside his coat — the boy had more hidden pockets than a magician's trousers. "Two on the gate. I count maybe eight total, plus the officer."

"Nine," said Vael.

I didn't jump. I want that recorded. My whole body flinched hard enough to bruise my ribs on frozen ground, but I did not *jump*.

"Do not do that," I hissed at the enormous grey shape materializing out of the dark like a bad conscience.

"You were counting the man walking the perimeter twice," Vael said. Its voice, when it bothered to use one, was like wind through a hollow log — old, dry, faintly amused. "He has a limp. He walks the same stretch, turns, walks it back. You counted him going and coming."

"Thank you," I said, with as much dignity as a person can manage while face-down in a ditch smelling of pig muck and old ice. "Eight, then. And a dog?"

"No dog." Vael's ears flicked toward the garrison. "Dogs are sensible about me. They leave."

"Wish I could."

"You cannot," Vael said simply. "You need them alive, and they need you mad, and here we all are."

Tam made a small noise that might have been a laugh strangled for safety. The scholar had changed since the drowned stair — not bigger, exactly, but settled into himself somehow, the way wet clay settles when you stop poking it. He'd nearly died down there with the map clutched to his chest, and Juniper Okonkwo had hauled him out by the collar, and ever since he looked at me like I was a footnote he intended to cite properly.

"So," he whispered. "The plan. Which we have. Because we have a plan, right? We're not just lying in a ditch admiring their fortifications?"

"We have half a plan," I said. "The other half belongs to Wren."

That was the thing nobody tells you about found family: they get captured, and then you discover how much of your own courage was actually borrowed from theirs. Wren Halloway and Doss had been taken three days ago at the river bend, and every hour since, I'd been learning what the shape of her absence felt like. It felt like a missing tooth. Your tongue kept going back to check.

"The smuggler's trick," I said, because Tam needed the words even if Vael already knew them. "Wren told me once — she got Doss out of a magistrate's cellar in Greywater this way. You don't break in. You make them *come out*."

"Fire," Tam said.

"Fire," I agreed. "Or the threat of it. Cold Ford sits on the only road that crosses the Ellswater before the gorges. If the ford burns, the garrison commander doesn't defend his prisoners. He defends his *report*. Every soldier goes to save the boats and the stores, because losing stores gets you flogged and losing prisoners just gets you a stern letter."

"That is the bleakest military logic I've ever heard," Tam said, "and I've read the campaigns of Aldric the Unifier."

"Which ended how?" I asked sweetly.

"...Point conceded."

Vael rose out of the dark like a hill deciding to take an interest. In the torchlight below, its shadow would read as exactly what we needed it to read as: something too big to be a wolf, something wrong, something out of the oldest stories the border folk still told to frighten their children straight.

"I will be the fire," the wolf-spirit said.

* * *

*Wren*

Here is what they don't tell you about captivity: it's mostly waiting, and the waiting is the worst part, and also there's rats.

They had me in the tollhouse cellar with Doss, which was either carelessness or confidence, and I was betting on confidence. Captain Verel — because of course the officer had a name, and of course it was Verel, a name like a slammed door — had asked me the same four questions for two days. Where is the map. Who carries it. Where are they going. What did the courier tell you.

I'd answered with variations on a single theme, each one less polite than the last. By day three I'd run out of themes and started inventing new ones, which is how Doss came to learn that I knew seventeen words for a man's parentage that even he hadn't heard before, and Doss grew up on a ferry.

"They'll come for us," Doss said, for the fourth time. He said it the way he bailed water — steady, unglamorous, absolutely necessary.

"I know."

"How d'you know?"

"Because the Keeper's stubborn, the scholar's terrified, and the wolf is the wolf." I shifted my weight off the wrist irons, which had gotten personal about my left hand. "Also because if they don't, I'll haunt every one of them individually, and I know where Vael sleeps."

Doss smiled. Big man, ferryman's hands, a face like a door someone had carved kindly. They'd taken our knives, our boots — mine, anyway; Doss's feet were apparently too large for any boots the crown owned — and our dignity, but they hadn't searched the lining of my coat, because soldiers search for weapons and never think to fear a woman's sewing.

In that lining: three inches of waxed cord, a fishhook, a sliver of flint, and the ashes of my ledger.

Well. Not the ashes. That was poetic nonsense. What I had was the memory of burning it, page by page, in the brazier while Verel watched, every debt and route and name I'd collected in eleven years of smuggling curling up into nothing rather than buy our freedom. He'd thought I was bluffing until the third signature went up. There's a particular look a hard man gets when he realizes the thing he's bargaining with only existed because other people believed in it. I intend to treasure that look until I die.

Above us, the garrison bells started screaming.

Not the alarm pattern — I'd listened to enough alarms in my life to know the difference. This was panic. Boots overhead, shouting, the creak of the palisade gate swinging wide, and through the cellar's air vent came a smell I knew intimately from twenty years of close calls: smoke, and under it, something wilder. Wet fur and cold stone and age.

"Oh," Doss said softly. "Oh, that'll be them."

That'll be them. Two days in chains and the big man's voice still cracked on it like a boy's.

The lock on our door turned — badly, hastily, a key jammed by shaking hands — and then it didn't turn at all, it *stopped existing*, along with a foot of the doorframe around it, torn away with a sound like a tree giving up. Torchlight poured in. A shape filled it.

Now, I've seen Vael before. I've seen it at a campfire, conversational, almost domestic. But I had never seen it the way the fleeing guard outside had evidently seen it: filling a doorway, eyes like coins at the bottom of a well, blood on its muzzle and no apology anywhere in its posture.

"Hello, Wren," it said.

"There's my girl," I croaked, because my throat had gone tight and I needed to blame something.

It bit through our irons — gently, which I will be thinking about for years — and Doss hauled me up, and together we staggered up the stairs into chaos.

* * *

*Juniper*

Chaos, for the record, was going beautifully right up until it wasn't.

The trick worked like this: Tam and I crept to the garrison's woodpile — because no commander worth his floggings leaves his stores undefended, but this one had left his woodpile forty yards out, past the wall, because he was an idiot or arrogant or both — and we built a fire against the stacked logs with Wren's flint and Tam's oil flask. Then we ran. Then Vael walked out of the treeline on the far side, huge and impossible and lit from behind by our little blaze, and the garrison lost its collective mind.

Every soldier on that wall turned to look at the wolf. Every soldier except one.

I heard the bowstring before I understood it. Some part of me — the keeper's part, the part that spent thirty years listening for a wrong note in the Gate's hum — caught the sound out of the night and translated it instantly: *that is a string under tension, that is a string releasing.*

I turned. I didn't dodge. Thirty years of standing still at a gate teaches you many things, and ambulating quickly is not among them.

The arrow took me high in the shoulder, punching through the muscle where neck becomes chest, and the world went white and then red and then very quiet and very far away, as if someone had closed the Gate between me and everything else.

I remember Tam screaming my name. I remember falling against the woodpile, which was now cheerfully ablaze, so that my first coherent thought afterward was that I had managed to set myself on fire in addition to being shot, which felt statistically unfair.

I remember Vael.

I remember the wolf crossing the yard faster than anything that size should move, and the archer on the wall having just enough time to see it coming, and — I remember this with terrible clarity — Vael *stopping*. Standing over him. Letting him see it. The man dropped his bow and pressed himself flat against the stones, and Vael leaned down, and whatever passed between them took two seconds and cost the man every dream he'd have for the rest of his life.

Then the wolf turned and ran back to me, and the last thing I saw clearly was Wren Halloway bursting out of the tollhouse door with Doss behind her, free, filthy, furious, alive.

Then the far country took me.

* * *

*Wren*

We burned the garrison's boats, not the fort — the fire in the woodpile was theater and everyone knew it within ten minutes, but by then we had what we'd come for and the ford was ours by default, because you can't pursue anyone across a river without a boat unless you're very stupid or very cold, and the Ellswater in thaw-season is both.

We put six miles behind us before I let us stop, and by then the Keeper was talking to someone who wasn't there.

Fever does different things to different people. Doss goes silent and sweats. I go mean. But Juniper Okonkwo — who had taken an arrow meant for a stranger's war and hadn't made a sound I heard until they hit the ground — lay against Tam's rolled-up coat with their eyes open and their gaze fixed on some middle distance, murmuring.

"It's gone flat," they said. "The north face. It's gone flat again, I have to go check the seals, grandmother will—"

"Grandmother's dead, love," I said quietly, wringing out a cloth in melted snow. "You're on the mountain road. You're safe. We're all safe."

Their eyes found mine, and there was a moment — fever-bright, but lucid underneath, the way a coin is visible at the bottom of murky water — where I think they were entirely present.

"Wren," they said. "You came out."

"Well, obviously. Someone has to insult all of you properly."

A ghost of a smile. Then the fever pulled them back under, and the murmurings resumed — and these ones I liked less. These ones had structure. Words in an order, repeated, like a lesson being recited. Like a song with the melody stripped out.

"*Hold the hum. Hold the line. The door is yours, the door is thine—*"

"That's keeper's liturgy," Tam said. He'd been sitting with his knees up and his arms around them, looking younger than his scholarship. "From the binding rites. I found fragments in the Ashwife's books. Why would Juniper know it by heart? Keepers don't study the binding, they study the *seals*—"

"Maybe they sing it to themselves," Doss offered. "Like a lullaby. My mam sang grain-prayers over the flour."

"But why would a keeper's lullaby be the *binding's grammar*?"

None of us had an answer. Vael had one, I could see it — the wolf sat apart from our little fire, at the exact edge of the light, wearing the expression of someone holding a confession behind its teeth. But it said nothing, and I let it keep its silence, because we owed it our freedom and because I recognized the posture. It's the posture of a person deciding how much truth the people they love can survive tonight.

I bound Juniper's shoulder as best I could — clean through and through, which the old ferry surgeons call a mercy wound, meaning it'll probably kill you slower than the alternative. The arrowhead had gone in above the collarbone and out below the shoulder blade, missing the lung by what Tam swore was a finger's width and what I privately calculated as a hair. The wound was hot to the touch. The skin around it had gone an angry, streaky red.

"We need the Ashwife," I said. "Herbs, and whatever else she keeps in that tower. Fever like this climbs fast."

"She's four days' walk," Tam said.

"Then we walk four days."

"In snow. Carrying them. With crown patrols sweeping every pass—" He stopped himself, which I appreciated; the boy was learning to count to ten before despairing. "We can't outrun this. And we can't stay still."

"We split the difference," Doss said.

We all looked at him. Doss spoke perhaps nine sentences a week and they were all load-bearing.

"High road's watched. Low road's flooded. But there's the shepherd cuts — the paths the flock-tracks follow, up under the ridgeline. Too narrow for soldiers, too steep for horses." He shrugged, a landslide of a shrug. "My father drove sheep over them. I know the way. It costs us a day extra and both my knees, but my knees were always negotiable."

"And the fever?" Tam asked.

Doss looked at Juniper — flushed, muttering, thirty years of solitary duty finally cracking open like an egg — and then at the rest of us, arranged around them like a hearth with no house.

"Fever breaks easier when you're not alone," he said. "Always struck me that way. Everything does."

So that's what we did. Doss led, finding the shepherd cuts in the dark like a man reading his own handwriting. Tam carried the map and the journals and kept one hand on Juniper's brow the whole climb, cataloguing the heat like it was data, because Tam loves things best when he can measure them. I carried the Keeper myself for two of the worst miles, their head against my collarbone, lighter than I expected, which frightened me more than the blood had.

And Vael walked behind us all the way, at the edge of the light, and once — just once, deep in the night when it thought none of us were listening — it began to hum.

Low. Old. The same tune Juniper was mumbling in fragments, but complete, the melody restored to the stripped-out words, and I understood two things at once: that the wolf knew the counter-song, the whole of it, every verse —

— and that it was singing it to keep Juniper's fever company, the way you'd sit up all night with a sick friend.

Whatever Vael had done to help bind a king a hundred years ago, it was paying it off now, note by note, in the dark, asking nothing.

By dawn the fever peaked. Juniper's mumbling stopped mid-word, their body arched, Tam shouted, and I thought — for three long breaths, three of the longest of my dishonest life — that we'd lost them.

Then they shuddered, sighed, and slept. Actually slept. The red streaks hadn't spread. The fever broke like a wave pulling back, leaving us all gasping on the sand after it.

But as their breathing steadied, Juniper's lips moved one last time, and this time the words were clear as a bell, and they weren't liturgy at all:

*"Grandmother. You lied to me. Show me the rest."*

Tam and I looked at each other across the sleeping Keeper, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: that the fever had broken open something besides the sickness — that somewhere behind those closed eyes, in whatever country the delirium had carried them to, Juniper Okonkwo was walking through memories stored in the Gate itself.

Memories that belonged to a dead woman.

And that they were about to learn what we'd all spent the whole rescue trying to protect them from: the whole truth, from the beginning, from the mouth of the first Keeper herself.
