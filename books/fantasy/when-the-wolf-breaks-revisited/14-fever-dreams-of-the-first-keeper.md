## 14. Fever Dreams of the First Keeper

Fever, I learned that night, is a country with no roads in it.

I walked one anyway. That's the kind of thing you do when your blood is boiling itself clean and the wound at Cold Ford has opinions about your future. The fever-country had grey grass and a sky the color of old pewter, and a path under my feet worn smooth by centuries of feet — my feet, I realized, though not only mine. The stones knew me. They hummed.

The Gate-hum. Except there was no Gate here, no mountain, no north face gone flat and wrong. Just the humming, everywhere, like the world itself was a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing since before anyone alive was born.

"Grandmother?" I said, because when you're delirious you say the obvious thing, and the obvious thing answered.

*Not quite,* said the humming. And then the grey grass parted, and she was there — Nana Okonkwo, four years dead, looking exactly as she had on her last morning: silver braids pinned severe, hands like tree roots, that look she gave me whenever I asked a question she'd decided I wasn't ready for. Which, to be fair, was most questions.

"You're dead," I told her. "I attended it. There was soup."

"And yet." She folded her arms. "You're standing inside the Gate's memory, child, with a hole in your side and a wolf-shaped debt behind you. Do you want to argue taxonomy, or do you want to know why the hum went flat?"

That shut me up. It usually did.

She turned and walked, and the fever-country unrolled ahead of her like a map being drawn by someone who loved maps — which is to say, reluctantly, and only what was needed. I followed. My side ached in a distant, polite way, the way pain behaves in dreams: present, but aware it's being rude.

"This isn't you," I said slowly, working it out as we walked. "This is the Gate. The Gate remembers everything its keepers ever poured into it. Every keeper who died at their post —"

"— left their remembering behind," she agreed. "The Gate is a ledger. You thought you were guarding a door. You were guarding an archive with a door attached."

"How many keepers are in here?"

"All of them. Back to the first."

And the pewter sky darkened, and the grey grass became stone, and I was somewhere else entirely.

* * *

The first memory was loud.

That surprised me. I'd expected the First Keeper to be solemn, ceremonial, all incense and gravity. Instead the oldest layer of the Gate's memory smelled of woodsmoke and panic, and the first sound in it was screaming — not the king's, I would learn. Everyone else's.

I stood in a hall of living wood — roots grown into pillars, branches woven into a ceiling so high the torchlight couldn't find it. The Court of Roots. I knew it from nowhere and recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a face from a photograph you've never seen. Somewhere under my feet, under Thornfell, this place existed right now, while I lay sweating in a smuggler's camp with Tam pressing a cold cloth to my forehead and Wren pretending she wasn't scared.

At the center of the hall stood a young man.

He was maybe twenty-five, dressed plainly — no crown. He was arguing, actually, gesturing at a circle of older people whose robes marked them as magicians of some serious weight, and he was arguing *well*, with the exasperated clarity of someone who has already won and is just making sure everyone understands why.

"—and if I don't do it," he was saying, "then it gets done to someone who didn't choose it, and a binding built on a prisoner fails in a generation. You all know this. Your own grammar says so. A willing heart or nothing." He spread his hands. "So. Here is a willing heart. Use it."

"Your Majesty —" began one of the magicians, an old woman with ash-grey eyes.

"Don't *Majesty* me, Mother Ashen. If I'm going to be nailed to a mountain for eternity I'd like to be addressed as Aldric while it happens."

King Aldric. Young. Alive. Furious with love for his own people, which was, I gathered from the shape of the argument around him, exactly the problem. Something was eating the lowlands — I felt the memory flinch away from naming it, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — something born of the old magic grown too dense and hungry in Thornfell's deep places. It wore shapes. It wore *people*. And it could not be killed, only contained, and containment required a seal of living will anchored in a willing heart, and every candidate they'd tried had either refused or broken.

Except their king. Who volunteered. Who joked about it.

I watched him sign the covenant with his own blood, watched him laugh at something the Ashwife's predecessor whispered — watched him look out at the assembled court one last time with an expression I recognized from mirrors, the expression of someone saying goodbye to a duty they chose and would choose again.

Then the binding took him, and the hall screamed light, and the last thing I saw of King Aldric for nine hundred years was his face going calm — too calm, hollowed like a mask — as something else settled into the space behind his eyes and smiled with his mouth.

The Hollow Regent wasn't an invader. I'd been imagining it wrong — a parasite, a thief wearing the king. But the Gate's memory doesn't lie; it's the one thing I'll say for it. The Regent was the binding itself. The seal, given shape by the act of sealing. They had needed something to hold the old magic's hunger, so they built it out of the king's sacrifice, and it grew fat on eternity and forgot its purpose and started making purposes of its own.

They hadn't imprisoned a king under the mountain.

They'd imprisoned a king *with* the thing his sacrifice created, and left them alone together for nine hundred years.

"Oh," I said, out loud, in the memory where no one could hear me. "Oh, that's *appalling*."

"It gets worse," said my grandmother's voice, dry as August. "Keep watching."

* * *

The next layer was quieter. Decades compressed into breaths. I saw the mountain close over the buried court, saw the first keepers appointed — not soldiers, I noted, not wardens. *Listeners.* People chosen for the ability to hear the Gate-hum, to tend it, to sing the counter-song that kept the seal supple instead of brittle. Nine generations of them before the first lie.

I stood in a small stone room that I knew with sickening familiarity — it was the keeper's study at the Hollow Gate, my study, except younger by centuries, the shelves not yet bowed. A woman sat at the desk writing, and beside her stood the same ash-eyed magician from the binding, aged now but unmistakable.

"The king must be remembered as a tyrant," the magician was saying. "It's kinder."

The keeper — the Third Keeper, the Gate supplied, a man named Ede with ink-stained fingers — looked up sharply. "Kinder to whom?"

"To the line of keepers. To the lowlands. To the king himself." The magician's voice was tired in a way that made my chest hurt; I knew that tiredness. It was the tiredness of someone who has carried a secret long enough that it has grown bones. "A tyrant can be guarded forever without grief. A martyr cannot. Tell the keepers after you that a mad king was sealed away for his crimes, and they will do their duty with clean hearts. Tell them the truth — that a beloved young man chose this, and we let him, and the choice has curdled into a horror none of us foresaw — and every keeper who follows will spend their life wondering if they ought to end it. Some will try. The seal does not forgive interference." She laid her hand flat on the desk. "Worse: the Regent listens through the seal. It hears the keepers' hearts. Let it hear doubt, and doubt is a door."

Ede stared at her a long moment. "So we lie."

"So we lie," she agreed, "and we put the truth somewhere honest. In the song. The counter-song every keeper learns carries the true history in its structure — the willing heart, the volunteer, the grief. Not words. Music. Any keeper who truly *listens* — not recites, listens — will feel it. The Gate keeps faith with those who keep faith with it."

And I understood, with a lurch, the melody that had lived in my bones since I was six years old. The one Nana hummed while she cooked. The one she'd taught me note by note, correcting my pitch with a rap of her knuckle, saying *again, Juniper, listen this time.*

I'd been singing a confession my whole life and calling it a work song.

* * *

The fever-country shifted again, and now I wasn't watching strangers. Now it was Nana's own memories, and they came warm and specific and unbearably close: rain on the north face, her hands chapped from seal-tending, a much younger woman with my eyes learning the rounds. Me, small, asking the question children ask.

*"Nana, who's under the mountain?"*

And Nana-in-the-memory, kneeling in the snow, went very still before answering, and I heard — because the Gate's memory lets you hear what you couldn't hear then, the whole chord and not just the note — the half-second where she decided.

"A mad king," she said. "Who did wicked things. That's why we guard the Gate, little wren. So nothing wicked gets out, and no one wicked gets in."

*I believed her,* I told the empty fever-sky. *I built my whole self on it.*

*Yes,* said the Gate, in her voice, in a hundred voices, in the hum. *And when the courier died on your floor and the map showed you passages beneath your own Gate, you grieved as if the duty were ruined. Look again.*

And the memory turned, gently, like a hand on my shoulder turning me to see.

I saw Nana at the Gate on the night before she died — the Gate's memory held her last round entire — and I saw her stand at the north face where the hum had begun to flatten, and press her palm to the stone, and listen with the listening that was her inheritance and her art. And I heard what she heard: the Regent stirring. The seal thinning. Midwinter coming, some winter soon, and a keeper too old to climb down into the dark.

And I heard her speak aloud to the stone, alone, in the voice she used for prayers:

"I taught the girl to listen. Whatever comes, the Gate will have kept faith if she keeps faith. Let the last keeper be the one who ends it. Let it be a choice, this time. Let it finally be a *choice*."

She wasn't a liar, my grandmother. She was a woman holding a door shut with one hand and a candle lit for the person who'd come to open it with the other. The lie wasn't a betrayal. It was scaffolding — meant to come down the moment the building could stand on its own. The keepers' duty was never to hold the seal forever. It was to hold it until a keeper came along who could hear the true song, follow the true map, walk down into the buried court, and *choose*. Unbind or rebind. Free or seal. The whole architecture of the Gate — the archive, the songs, even the lie — existed to deliver one person, whole and knowing, to one decision.

Duty was always meant to end here. With a choice.

"That's a hell of a job description," I said hoarsely, to the fever-sky, to the hum, to her. "No pay. No thanks. Terminal."

"You wanted meaningful work," said Nana's voice, and for one moment — one — she stood beside me again, solid, smelling of cedar smoke and the particular soap she used, and she put her root-knuckled hand against my cheek the way she did when I was small and frightened by storms. "Juniper. Listen to me, and then wake up, because your scholar is panicking and the ferryman has run out of sensible things to say.

"The Regent needs a willing keeper to complete its escape — Vael knows, that's why the creature confessed, penance sits oddly on a spirit but it fits. It will offer you everything. It has had nine hundred years to practice offers. When it speaks your name, and it will, remember: a name freely given opens doors. Yours was given to the Gate the day you were born. It does not belong to the thing in the crown unless you hand it over.

"And whatever you decide down there — seal or free, gate or no gate — decide it *with them*. The ones who pulled you from the river. The one who burned her ledger. That was never in the old design. The First Binding was one heart alone in the dark, and look what it cost. You have more. Use it."

Her hand faded. The fever-country tilted.

"One more thing," she said, from everywhere and nowhere, her voice fraying into the hum. "The counter-song's last verse. The one I never taught you. It's in the map. The courier's map. Ask Tam to read it *as music*, not geography. And Juniper —"

"Yes?"

"When you get to the part where you're angry with me — and you will, you're my granddaughter, you'll be magnificent about it — don't waste the anger. Spend it. Anger is fuel. Grief is only anger that ran out of road."

* * *

I woke gasping, in the grey hour before dawn, with Tam's cold cloth sliding off my forehead and Doss's enormous hand steadying my shoulder and Wren's knife — I noted distantly — already drawn, pointed at the treeline, because of course it was.

"Fever's breaking," Tam said, voice cracking with relief. "You were talking. Singing, some of it. Then you said —" he hesitated — "you said *the map is a score*. What does that mean?"

Outside the tent, the wind had turned. It was carrying snow, and under the snow, faint as a finger dragged along the bottom of a frozen lake, something far below the mountain was saying my name.

*Juniper. Juniper Okonkwo. Come and be willing.*

Midwinter was three days out, the passes were closing behind us, and I knew — the way you know a song — that we were done retreating.

I sat up, and the world swam, and I grinned at them anyway, fever-bright and terrified and certain.

"Pack everything," I said. "We go down."
