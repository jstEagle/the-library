## 15. Midwinter Closes In

I'll tell you something about ferrying that nobody who isn't a ferryman knows: the river never really freezes. Not all the way through. You can stand on ice thick enough to hold a wagon and a team of oxen, and underneath, down where the light doesn't reach, the water is still moving, still patient, still remembering which way it was going before winter told it to wait.

I've always found that comforting.

Lately I've been finding it less so.

* * *

We'd been three days out of the Ashwife's tower when the weather turned mean. Up until then the high passes had been cold but honest — the kind of cold that tells you exactly what it's going to do to you and then does it. But on the morning we crossed under the shadow of the Grey Teeth, the sky went the color of dishwater and stayed there, and the snow started coming down sideways, and Wren Halloway stood at the head of the line with her collar up around her ears and said a word I won't repeat in decent company, though to be fair she repeated it several times with variations, which is more than most people manage.

"Passes are closing," she said when she'd finished her composition. "Behind us, ahead of us, both. Look at that sky."

I looked at that sky. It looked like every bad crossing I'd ever made compressed into one grey ceiling and hung over us like a debt collector.

"So we stop?" Tam asked. He had ice in his eyebrows and his voice had gone thin from breathing cold air for days. "Wait it out?"

"Wait it out where?" Wren swept an arm back the way we'd come. The pass behind us had already begun filling in — drifts climbing the walls like they were being paid by the hour. "There's no shelter back there worth the name, and even if there were, we'd be digging ourselves out till thaw. And thaw," she added, with a look at Juniper, "is not a thing we have."

Juniper didn't answer right away. They'd been quiet since Cold Ford — quieter than usual, which for Juniper Okonkwo is saying something, because their usual is a silence you could store grain in. The wound in their side had closed up under Tam's stitching and the Ashwife's salves, but closing a wound and healing one are different animals, and I've seen enough men carry the first while pretending it's the second to know the difference. Juniper walked with a hand pressed flat against their ribs when they thought nobody was watching. Everybody was watching. That's what a family is, mostly — people watching each other and pretending they aren't.

"The map says there's a hollow under the ridge," Juniper said finally. "A day ahead, maybe less if the snow lets us move. Old smuggler cache, by the markings."

"By whose markings?"

"Grandmother's." They said it flat, no bitterness in it, or none left over anyway. Whatever bitterness had been in that word had been spent somewhere between Cold Ford and here, and what remained was just a fact, like the color of stone. "She marked caches along every route. If half of them are real, there's wood, maybe food."

"And if the other half are real," Wren said, "there's whatever else she thought needed caching on a road that leads to a buried king."

Nobody said anything about that. We walked.

That's most of what walking into Thornfell in midwinter is, if you want the truth of it. Walking, and wind, and the particular sound your own breath makes when it comes out of you hard enough to hurt. Vael ranged ahead most of the time — a grey shape ghosting through the whitefall, sometimes visible, sometimes just a suggestion, like a thought you haven't finished having. The wolf didn't mind the cold. I don't think the wolf minded much of anything anymore, except the thing it was carrying. It carried that the way I carry ballast in the boat: low, and steady, and never once letting you see how heavy it actually is.

I kept to the rear. Rear is my station. Somebody has to make sure the family stays a family and doesn't lose its tail to the mountain, and I'm built for it — big enough that nothing behind us wants a piece of me, slow enough that I notice things the quick ones step over.

It was on the second watch of that first night, camped in the lee of a rock spine with the fire dug down into a trench like Wren likes it, that I noticed the snow behind us had stopped being snow.

I don't mean it stopped falling. I mean it stopped *moving*. There's a difference, and any man who's worked water knows it. Snow falls and settles and shifts. What was happening behind us was that the pass — the whole throat of it, a quarter mile back and further than I could see — was sealing. Drifts rising smooth as poured wax, wind packing them down into something that wasn't going anywhere. I watched it happen the way you watch a river rise, knowing full well there's not a blessed thing you can do about it except note the fact and get on with living.

In the morning I told Wren.

"I know," she said.

"You know."

"Doss, I've been running these roads since I was eleven years old. I know what a sealed pass looks like." She kicked snow onto the dead fire and stood looking north, up the slope, toward the ridge and the hollow Juniper promised was beyond it. Her face did something complicated and then settled on stubborn, which is its resting position. "No retreat. Fine. I never liked retreat. It's undignified."

"You could say we're advancing in the other direction," Tam offered.

"That's the spirit," said Wren, in a voice that meant the opposite.

* * *

The hollow was real. That surprised me, though I'm ashamed to say why: some part of me had been waiting for the grandmother's marks to turn out false, the way everything else about her had turned out false, and every true thing she'd left behind landed wrong, like a stair missing in the dark.

The cache held split pine under oilcloth, a sack of barley gone hard as gravel but still barley, and a tin box of candles with the keeper's sigil stamped in the wax. Juniper picked up that box and held it a long moment, thumb moving over the stamp.

"She stocked this road," Juniper said. "She knew this road. She walked the route to the court herself, or she paid someone who did, and then she came home and taught me to check seals and never said a word."

"Grief later," said Wren, not unkindly. "Firewood now."

So we burned the traitor's candles and ate the traitor's barley porridge, and it tasted fine, which felt like another small betrayal, and we slept in shifts with Vael lying across the entrance like a grey door, and the storm outside screamed itself hoarse against the mountain and got nowhere.

It was during my shift — deep night, the third night, everyone else asleep — that the messenger came.

* * *

You hear him before you see him, or rather you hear the wolf before you see either of them. Vael lifted its head off its paws and made a sound I'd never heard it make — not a growl, not quite a call. Something older. A summons, maybe, or a greeting between two things that recognized each other's age.

Then a shape lurched out of the snowfall and collapsed against our windbreak, and I was up with my boathook — yes, I brought the boathook, a man should carry the tool he knows — and Tam was awake and fumbling for flint, and by the time we had light, the shape had resolved into a boy. Sixteen, maybe. Frost in his hair, frost on his eyelashes, lips gone the blue of deep water. He wore the livery of the Ashwife's household — I remembered the burnt-orange scarf from the tower, the strays she collected — and he had ridden or run or crawled through a sealed pass to reach us, and he was smiling, which frightened me worse than anything.

"Doss," he said, when he'd stopped shivering enough for words. "Ferryman. Big one. She said I'd know you." He laughed, wet and rattling. "Said you'd be the one standing guard at three in the morning because that's what you do."

"Get him by the fire," I called, and the camp came alive around us, and I got my shoulder under the boy and moved him like cargo, gentle, the way you move anything that matters.

They fed him broth and worked his hands and feet, and Tam — good lad — rubbed warmth into his arms and talked to him the whole time, steady nonsense, the names of rivers, anything to keep him swimming toward us instead of away. I've pulled enough half-drowned folk out of the current to know the trick. You don't ask questions. You give them something to hold onto that isn't the cold.

When he could talk properly, he gave us the message, and he gave it looking at Juniper.

"She says: the dreams have changed. The Regent sleeps now, or does whatever passes for sleeping in a thing like that, and in its sleep it calls a name. Over and over. Her watchers in the low villages hear it — the hollow ones murmur it in their rounds, all of them, same word, same hour of the night." He swallowed. "It's your name, Keeper. Juniper Okonkwo. It says it like a prayer. Or a promise."

The fire cracked. Snow ticked against the oilcloth overhead. Nobody moved.

"It also says," the boy went on, softer, "that a name spoken in sleep is a hook set in water. She says you must not answer it. No matter what it offers, no matter what voice it wears. A keeper who answers becomes a door."

"How long?" Wren asked. "Before midwinter?"

"Days. She says the binding will fail at the turning of the year whether you're there or not — but that if you're not there, what happens after will be worse, because no one will be standing in the hall who knows how to listen to old magic." He looked at Juniper again. "She says — and I'm to say it exact — *'Tell my stubborn keeper that duty was never the wall. Duty was the door, and love is the hinge, and I am sorry I only ever taught them the first part.'*"

Juniper sat very still. Firelight moved on their face and they let it.

"Your grandmother," Tam began carefully, "the Ashwife knew—"

"They were friends," Juniper said. "Once. Before the exile. Before the lie." They looked down at their hands. "She taught me the seals. She taught me everything except the ending. And now her messenger crawls through a blizzard to tell me the ending, and it's a riddle about doors and hinges." A breath. "Of course it is. She never could resist a riddle."

The boy slept after that, truly slept, and I sat beside him most of what remained of the night because somebody should, and because sitting beside sleeping people is most of what I'm good for, and I've made peace with that. It's not a small thing, keeping watch. Most of the world runs on it. Farmers, ferrymen, mothers, wolves lying across cave mouths. The grand work gets done by the bold, but the bold only get to do it because somebody boring stayed awake.

* * *

Come morning we buried the storm's anger under motion. The pass ahead had held open — barely, a winding throat of white between walls of rock — and Wren stood at its mouth studying it the way she studies a lock she intends to pick.

"One shot," she said. "Through the Teeth, down the long scree, two days hard walking to the descent the map marks. After that we're under the mountain and the weather can do its worst." She turned. "Anyone want to say it? Anyone want to say we should turn back?"

"There's no back to turn to," Tam said. "You saw the pass."

"I saw it. I wanted to hear someone else say it, because I'm the captain and captains shouldn't be the only sane ones aboard." She grinned, quick and crooked. "Right. Doss?"

I looked down the line of them. Wren with her frost-burned cheeks and her ledger-less hands. Tam with the map rolled safe inside his coat, ink-stained fingers blue with cold, eyes that had learned to be brave somewhere between the drowned stair and here. Juniper, hand pressed flat against their ribs, face turned toward the mountain like a plant toward light. And the wolf, sitting at the edge of the fire-scatter, watching Juniper with those amber eyes that held more sorrow than any animal ought to carry.

"No back," I agreed. "Never was much of one. I ran a ferry thirty years. You know what I learned about crossings?"

"Enlighten us," said Wren.

"Halfway's the worst place to change your mind." I shouldered my pack. "We're past halfway. Let's go meet the king."

And that was that. No speeches. Families don't need speeches; they need somebody to say the plain thing out loud so everybody can nod and walk. I've been the plain-thing-sayer in every crew I ever sailed with, and I'll tell you it's a finer office than it sounds. Captains get songs. Ferrymen get everyone home.

* * *

We went up into the Teeth as the sun climbed, and the mountain let us — grudging, the way mountains are, shedding little slides of powder down the rock walls to remind us it was allowing this rather than permitting it. Twice the wind tried to take Tam off the trail and twice I caught him by the collar, and the second time he laughed, high and shaky, and said, "You're very good at that," and I said, "Thirty years of pulling fools out of water, lad, air's just thinner water," and he laughed again and we went on.

Near noon, on the high shelf below the last saddle, Juniper stopped.

Not stumbled. Stopped. Deliberate, both boots planted, face tilted into the wind, and I watched their lips move — shaping something, syllables without sound. Then I heard it too, faint under the wind's whine. A hum. The Gate-hum, the one only keepers could hear, except now I could hear it, thin and warped, threading through the mountain like a wire about to snap.

"It's calling," Juniper said. Their voice came out level, which cost them, you could see it costing them. "The boy said night, but the Regent keeps no honest clock. It's calling now."

"Don't answer," Tam said quickly.

"I'm not answering." Juniper's jaw tightened. "But I can feel it pulling. Like a current." They looked back at us, and their eyes were scared and steady at once, which is the exact expression I imagine my grandmother wore every flood season of her life. "Everyone hold onto something. This is going to get rougher before the ridge."

Vael rose from the snow beside them, tall as a doorway, and pressed its great head briefly against Juniper's shoulder — the nearest thing to an embrace a wolf can manage.

"Together," the wolf said, in that voice like stones rolling in a deep river. "It may call. But it cannot choose who walks."

"No," Juniper said. "That part's mine."

We went on. Behind us, the last of the pass sighed shut under the snow, soft as a door closing in a house where everyone had already decided to stay.

Ahead, under the ridge, the mountain hummed a dying king's name in a stranger's voice, and I put my head down, and walked, and kept watch.

Somebody always keeps watch. That's how the world gets saved — not all at once, but watch by watch, step by step, ordinary feet on terrible ground.

Ours hit the ridge at dusk.

What waited on the far side of it, I'll leave for whoever writes the next part.
