## 16. The Court of Roots

The last door was not a door.

That's the thing nobody tells you about buried courts. You spend weeks expecting something grand — a gate, a portcullis, at minimum a really impressive archway — and then you arrive and the entrance is a seam in the living rock, thin as a knife cut, breathing warm air against your face like something asleep behind it.

"It's humming," I said, before I could stop myself.

Everyone turned to look at me. Of course they did. I'd spent my whole life being the only person who could hear the Hollow Gate sing — that flat, patient note beneath the stone that told me the seal held or didn't. I'd walked away from that duty three weeks ago with a dead woman's map and a wolf-shaped question mark, and now, standing in the dark at the bottom of the world, I heard it again.

Except this hum wasn't flat. This hum was *wrong* in an entirely new way. It wasn't a note held; it was a note *straining*. A bow drawn back and quivering on the string.

"Good humming or bad humming?" Tam asked. He had his notebook out already, which was very brave of him given that his hands were shaking hard enough to make the pencil tap against his knuckles like a woodpecker with anxiety.

"I don't know," I admitted. "It's not the Gate-hum. It's older. Or younger. It's—" I pressed my palm flat against the rock. Warm. Damp. And underneath the damp, faintly, *alive*, in the way a heartbeat is alive. "It's waiting."

"Wonderful," Wren said. "I love a waiting door. Nothing bad has ever come through a waiting door." She rolled her shoulders, checked her knives out of habit even though we'd all agreed — Vael had been extremely insistent — that steel would do about as much good down here as strong language. "Doss?"

Doss set his lantern down and put both big hands against the seam. The ferryman had been quiet for two days, ever since the snow closed the passes behind us and retreat stopped being a thing that existed. Now he looked at the crack in the mountain the way he looked at river water before a crossing: measuring it, respecting it, deciding he was going through it anyway because going around wasn't on offer.

"Aye," he said, which was Doss for *I have considered all available options and found them wanting, let us proceed*.

Vael stepped forward. In the lantern light the wolf-spirit looked less like a wolf than usual — its edges kept slipping, grey fur giving way to something like smoke, smoke giving way to the suggestion of a man in old armor who was never quite there when you looked directly at him. Three hundred years bound to a dying king will do that to you, apparently. It had done worse to the king.

"When we go through," Vael said, "he may know me. What is left of him." A pause. "What is left of me."

"We've been over this," Wren said, gentler than her words deserved. "You're not walking in there alone, and we're not letting you eat anybody. Both things can be true."

The seam opened.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because later — when the Accord scholars came asking, when the ballads got it wrong in seventeen mutually incompatible ways — nobody ever described the descent properly. The rock did not swing open. It *parted*, the way lips part, and the warmth that came out smelled like sap and rain and something older under both, the smell of a house that has stood so long the family and the timber have gotten confused about which one is holding up which.

And we went in.

* * *

The court was alive. That's the only way to say it, and I know how it sounds.

Not ruins. I'd braced myself for ruins — fallen pillars, drowned thrones, the elegant rot of a place abandoned by time. Instead the hall went up and up into darkness where the roof should have been, and every pillar was a tree, grown from floor to impossible ceiling, their bark silvered and smooth as if polished by centuries of hands. Between them stretched walls of woven branches, still green. Still *growing*. Lantern-light caught beads of sap running down the trunks like slow amber tears.

"It's been tended," Tam whispered. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Three hundred years underground, sealed away from sun, and it's been *tended*. Something has kept it growing."

"Something lives here," Doss said. Not a question. His hand had found my shoulder without either of us noticing, and stayed there.

We walked. There was no other reasonable option, though I'll confess the thought occurred to me more than once that turning around and screaming was also technically available. The hall stretched ahead of us farther than any hall had a right to, and the trees grew closer together as we went, their branches knitting overhead into a canopy that swallowed our light, until we were moving through a green dusk shot through with veins of pale luminescence — fungus, or moss, or the court's own dreaming, I couldn't tell you.

Then the trees opened like a crowd making way, and we saw the throne.

It was a tree too — of course it was, everything here was a tree — but grown into the shape of a seat, roots braided for armrests, a canopy of white leaves arching above it like a crown the mountain itself had offered up. And sitting in it, half-drowned in root and bark, was a man.

He had been tall once. You could see the architecture of it still, the long bones, the breadth of shoulder, though the years and the binding had folded him inward like a letter someone had tried to cram back into its envelope. Bark grew across his left side in slow plates, fused to skin, and where his right arm rested on the armrest the flesh was human and old and trembling faintly, the way a bird trembles. His hair had gone white as birchwood. His eyes were closed.

"Oh," said Wren, very quietly, all her sharpness gone soft at the edges. "Oh, that's not right. That's not right at all."

"He volunteered," Vael said. The spirit had gone still in the way only the dead and the very old can go still. "Understand that. Whatever else you learn tonight — he climbed onto that throne and asked us to bind him, and he thanked us for it."

Aldric's eyes opened.

They were brown. Ordinary brown eyes, the kind you'd pass in a market without a second glance, except they were full of three hundred years, and they found Vael across the distance between us the way a compass needle finds north — instantly, helplessly, with a kind of terrible relief.

"*Vael*," the king said. His voice was rust and honey. "You came back. After all this time, you — I dreamed you'd forgotten me. I hoped you'd forgotten me. I prayed you'd forgotten me, and then I prayed you hadn't, and I am very sorry about both." He tried to rise and the throne held him, gently, firmly, the way ivy holds a fencepost, and something in his face broke and reformed and broke again. "You're crying," he observed, wonderingly, looking at the wolf.

Wolves don't cry. Spirits don't either, as far as anyone knows. But sound was coming out of Vael — low, ragged, nothing like a howl and nothing like speech — and the king reached out his one human hand toward it across three hundred years of silence, and wept, great silent tears cutting channels through the dust on his face, and I stood there in a buried court at the bottom of the world watching a king and his hound greet each other after an eternity, and I forgot, briefly, to be afraid.

Briefly.

Because while Aldric wept, I noticed the shadow.

Every light source casts one; that's just physics, or whatever Tam calls it when he's showing off. But this shadow lay behind the throne where no light fell, deep and dense as wellwater, and it was *shaped*. Shoulders. A head. A crown — not metaphorically. A crown of black iron, thin as wire, floating in the dark above the shadow's brow.

And it was rising.

* * *

"Keeper."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the Gate's hum used to live in the stone, and every hair on my body stood up and took notice. The shadow unfolded from behind the throne — taller than a man, or man-shaped the way a coat on a hook is man-shaped, filled out by something that had never had shoulders of its own. Where its face should have been there was a suggestion of features, smoothed over like a thumbprint pressed into wax, and where its eyes should have been there were two points of pale light, patient and warm, and I hated — I *hated* — how warm they were.

"You came the long way," the Hollow Regent said. "Through the drowned stair, past my hollow-eyed children, all the way down. I watched you do it. Every step. You are far more stubborn than your grandmother, and she was the most stubborn woman I ever loved."

My grandmother's name in its mouth hit me like cold water. "You never met my grandmother."

"No?" The Regent tilted its head, considering, the crown-wire glinting. "She sat where you stand, once. Younger than you are now. Terrified. Brave anyway. She asked me to let her take Aldric's place, did you know that? A keeper, offering herself to the roots." It smiled, or performed the shape of smiling. "I refused her. The binding wanted a willing *king*. Rules are rules." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "But I remembered her. I remember everyone who comes to this court, Juniper Okonkwo. That is most of what I am, now. Memory with nowhere to put itself."

Behind me, Tam made a small strangled noise — the scholar recognizing the shape of a horror he'd only read about. Wren's hand hovered near her useless knife. Doss shifted his weight forward, planting himself between the Regent and me like a wall that had opinions.

"You're wearing him," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The crown. You're wearing the king."

"I am *keeping* the king." The Regent spread its hands, wounded, reasonable, monstrous in its reasonableness. "Someone must. He asked for a jailer and the mountain gave him me. Three hundred years I have sat in the dark beside him, holding the binding together with both hands while it rotted, and do you know what he and I talked about, all those centuries? *You.* Your line. The keepers. He loves the keepers, Juniper. He used to count them — every child born on the Gate-wall, he'd feel them arrive through the seal and say, *another candle lit*, and grieve, because every candle was another lie told to a child who deserved the truth."

"That's enough," Vael growled, and the whole hall shivered. Sap ran faster down the pillars. The white canopy above the throne trembled, shedding pale leaves that fell upward, which is a thing I saw and will swear to on any oath you like.

"It is not nearly enough," the Regent said, and the warmth went out of its voice all at once, and what remained was vast and cold and *tired*, tired the way the bottom of the sea is tired. "The seal is failing. You have heard it — don't lie to me, Keeper, I can hear you hearing it. Midwinter comes. When it passes, the binding dies, and everything your grandmother's grandmother buried comes loose into the world at once, unchosen, unwelcomed, and hungry. Unless."

It stepped closer. The shadows of the pillars leaned away from it. Even the trees seemed to hold their breath, and I understood suddenly that the court hadn't been tending the Regent — the Regent had been tending the court, keeping it alive the way you keep a fire alive in a house you cannot leave.

"Unless a keeper stands where I stand," it said softly, "and takes the crown willingly. As he did. As none of your line has ever been allowed to do. You could end it, Juniper Okonkwo. Tonight. Cleanly. Kindly. All you have to do is kneel."

The pale lights of its eyes fixed on mine, warm as hearth-coals, patient as winter.

"And I think," the Hollow Regent said, "that you have been kneeling your entire life. You simply called it duty."

Nobody moved. Above us the white canopy shivered, and King Aldric — lucid, human-eyed, his one free hand gripping the armrest hard enough to bleed — shook his head, slowly, desperately, mouthing words I couldn't yet hear.

But I already knew, with the sick certainty of a trap closing, exactly what they were going to be.

*No,* the dying king mouthed, as the Regent waited, courteous as death, for my answer. *No, no, no.*

And the worst part — the part I'd carry afterward, the part that kept me up nights even after everything — was that some small, traitorous corner of me, the corner that had spent thirty years alone on a cold wall listening to a hum nobody else could hear, had already begun, quietly, terribly, to consider it.
