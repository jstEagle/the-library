## 17. The Bargain of the Crown

The Regent rose from the throne's shadow the way frost climbs a window — slowly, beautifully, and with the certain knowledge that it would cover everything.

I had imagined this moment on the long road down. In my imaginings it always went one of two ways: I drew my grandmother's knife and did something heroic and probably fatal, or I fainted. What I had not imagined was that it would *greet* me.

"Juniper Okonkwo." My name in its mouth sounded like my name sung back to me in an empty cistern. Familiar and wrong at once. "Little keeper. You walked so far. Come out of the cold."

"I'm not cold," I said, which was a lie; the Court of Roots was warm as a living body, and that was worse. "And I'm not little. I'm average height for a keeper."

Wren made a small sound behind me that might have been a laugh strangled by terror. Good. If she could still find me funny, we weren't beaten yet.

The Regent wore King Aldric's face the way a glove wears a hand. It was Aldric's face — I'd seen it now, the true king slumped on his throne of living wood, half swallowed by bark — but arranged differently. Smiling where Aldric wept. Open where Aldric was closed. It moved like something that had studied human beings from a great distance and taken careful notes.

It spread its hands. Its fingers were too long. I decided not to mention it.

"You have questions," it said. "Ask them. I have waited three hundred years for someone worth answering."

"Everyone says that," said Tam, from my left. His voice shook, but he said it. "Tyrants, mostly. It's never true."

"Tam," Wren breathed.

"No, let them ask," the Regent said pleasantly. "Questions are how keepers die. They lean in. They want to understand." It tilted Aldric's head, and the crown — black iron, grown into the skull like antlers — caught the light of no lamp I could see. "But you already understand, don't you? You heard the Gate hum flat. You read your grandmother's journals. You know the duty you loved was a lock, and locks are only ever built for two: the thing inside, and the thing that turns the key."

I wanted to argue. Instead I asked the question I'd carried since the fever dreams.

"Was there ever a keeper who chose freely? Or were we all just — bred for it? Trained from birth to hold a door we never understood?"

"Oh, Juniper." The Regent's smile widened past anything a face should do. "Every keeper chose freely. That's what makes it delicious. Your grandmother chose. Her mother chose. The first keeper stood before this very throne and *volunteered*. You are not a prisoner of the lie. You are its most devoted servant. You polished it every night with your little rounds, your little songs." It stepped closer. Vael's hackles rose beside me; Doss put one broad hand on Tam's shoulder, holding him still without seeming to move at all. "You loved the humming. Admit it. On the worst nights, when the snow came sideways and you were alone, the hum was the only voice that answered you. And it was *me*, Juniper. Every note. I sang to you through the stone for twenty years so you would come when I called."

The floor of my stomach opened onto a long drop.

Because it was true. That was the obscene part. There had been nights — I'd never told anyone, would never have told anyone — when the loneliness up at the Gate got so complete that the hum felt like company. When I'd pressed my palm to the north face and thought, *at least the mountain talks to me.*

It hadn't been the mountain.

* * *

"Here is what I offer," the Regent said, and the hall changed around us.

I don't have a better word for it than *changed*. One moment we stood in the vast root-vaulted court, pillars of pale wood breathing slow as sleepers. The next, the walls had become windows — or things shaped like windows — and through them I saw the Hollow Gate whole. Not cracked, not failing. Whole. Snow lying clean on the lintel stones. The seals bright. The hum rising strong and silver, the way it had hummed when my grandmother was alive and I was nine years old and believed the world was held together by people who did their rounds.

"Restoration," the Regent said. "Not the slow rot your order papered over for generations. A true sealing. Midwinter passes. Nothing comes out. Nothing goes in. Thornfell keeps its secrets, the lowlands keep their sleep, and the world stays the shape you were raised to protect."

"And the price?" Wren asked. She'd drifted to my right, arms crossed, wearing her negotiating face — the one she used on customs men. I loved her for it. "There's always a price. Even smugglers give free samples sometimes, but never kings."

"The price is continuity," said the Regent. "A binding needs a willing heart. That has not changed since the first night. Aldric gave his willingly, and so the binding held. But willing hearts wear out, little smuggler. They erode. Doubt gets in like water into stone. The binding must be renewed by a keeper who understands what they are doing — who knows the lie and consents anyway. That is the only consent worth anything. Ignorant oaths are straw. Yours—" it gestured at me with those too-long fingers, almost tenderly, "—yours would be iron."

"And me?" I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You said 'sealing themselves in too.' I heard the dream, Grandmother's memory. The first keeper didn't walk back out."

"No," the Regent agreed. "She stayed. As warden. As anchor. As the willing heart beating inside the lock." It smiled again. "You would stay. Not as punishment. As completion. You would sit beside your king and keep the song, and in time — a century, two — you might even come to enjoy the quiet. Keepers always did."

Behind me, Doss said, quietly, "No," in the voice of a man watching floodwater take a field. Just *no*. Plain as bread. It helped more than he'll ever know.

"And them?" I nodded at my people without turning around. I couldn't turn around. If I looked at Wren's face while discussing trading myself, I'd lose the thread of the negotiation, and the negotiation was the only rope I had. "You said safety for my friends. Say it plainly. All of it. I've been lied to by institutions my whole life; I won't be lied to by a crown."

"Straight terms, then." The Regent spread its hands, and the window-visions shifted. "The smuggler walks free. Her debts — all of them, every ledger in every port — burn unlit. The scholar keeps his hands and his mind both; the crown's soldiers hunting him will forget his name. The ferryman returns to his river and grows old poling barges, and dies in a bed, which is more than ferrymen get." It paused, savoring. "Even the wolf goes free. Bound spirits are not my concern once the binding breaks its old shape. Vael may run the ridges until the mountains wear down."

"And my grandmother?" I said.

The hall went very still. Even the roots seemed to stop breathing.

"Ah," the Regent said softly. "Yes. You dreamed her. You stood in her memory at the first binding; you saw her young, saw her choose, saw the lie told to her and by her. Here is my last gift, keeper, and I offer it knowing it will cost me: your grandmother is not gone."

"That's not possible," Tam whispered. "Keeper Okonkwo died eleven years ago. I — I read the records—"

"You read the *Gate's* records," the Regent said. "Written by keepers, for keepers. She died to the lowlands, yes. But a keeper who dies within reach of the seal does not leave. Where do you think the memories in the Gate came from? Where do you think the counter-song hid itself, if not folded into a mind still singing?" It leaned toward me, Aldric's ruined gentleness in its borrowed face. "She is here, Juniper. In the roots. In the hum. She has been waiting for you the way I have. Give your consent, sing the renewal, take your seat beside the king — and I will gather her out of the wood and set her before you, whole, warm, scolding you about your bootlaces, within the hour."

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, because everything after that moment has been described by other people and they all say I wept, and I did not. My eyes watered because the root-hall exhaled a fine pale pollen and I am mildly allergic, and I will maintain this position until I die.

Because it knew. Of course it knew. Three hundred years old and it had watched my whole family like a gardener watching a hedge, and it had found the one branch that would bend me. Not threats. Never threats — threats make people stubborn. It offered me my grandmother's voice saying my name.

* * *

And then, from the throne, a sound.

Small. Human. A man clearing his throat around three centuries of bark.

"Juniper."

I turned. Everyone turned. The Regent's smile flickered — actually flickered, like a lamp in wind — and I understood in one lurching instant why it had kept Aldric drugged and dreaming all these years, why the binding's erosion terrified it. Because when the king surfaced, the king could speak, and the king was the one witness the Regent could not rewrite.

He was mostly wood now. Bark plated his chest; his left arm had become a branch thick as my waist, rooted into the throne's armrest. His eyes were the only entirely human thing left in the hall, brown and tired and unbearably clear.

"Don't listen to it about your grandmother," he said. His voice was rough as a hinge. "It can show you shapes in the wood. Echoes. It cannot give her back. Nothing gives anyone back. I would know. I have begged it for three hundred years to let me see my daughter's face, and it has shown me shadows wearing her smile, and every time I believed, and every time it was hollow." He dragged his branch-arm forward; the throne creaked. "It lies the way rivers run downhill. Effortlessly. Because that is its nature."

"It bound you," I said. "Vael told us. You volunteered."

"I volunteered to *die*," Aldric said. "That is what no journal records, what no keeper was ever taught. The binding needed a willing heart, yes — but the rite was meant to end with the heart stopping. I was to be the lock's last breath. A king giving his life to close the door behind the old magic. Noble. Final. Done." His gaze slid to the Regent, and there was hatred in it, old and patient as geology. "But the thing that had worn my shadow for years — the hollow that grew in me the way rot grows in a great tree — it did not want the door closed. It wanted the door *kept*. Kept doors have keepers. Keepers can be turned. So it stopped my heart halfway and kept the rest of me, and it has been wearing my crown and calling itself the Regent ever since, and every keeper since has been guarding not the magic, but *its prison food*."

The Regent's composure cracked another degree. "Aldric," it said, warning in its voice, honey over broken glass.

"Juniper Okonkwo." The king ignored it completely. His voice was getting stronger, or maybe he simply didn't care anymore whether it lasted. "Listen to me. I am the only person in this hall who knows exactly what you're being offered, because I took the bargain once. Safety. Purpose. A seat in the quiet. It all sounds bearable. It all sounds *holy*. And then you wake in a hundred years and your arm is a tree and the thing beside you is wearing your face and eating your memories one per winter, and there is no door out because you *are* the door." He bowed his head, and bark flaked from his brow like snow. "So I am begging you. Not commanding — I have no right to command anyone, least of all a keeper. Begging. Do not rebind me. Let me die. I have been ready to die for three hundred years. Release is not a cruelty. Release is the only mercy left in this mountain."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

The Regent recovered first. It always would; recovery was its native language.

"Sentiment," it said lightly. "The dying always editorialize. Consider the alternative, keeper. Refuse me, and midwinter comes, and the binding fails on its own — it *is* failing, you've heard it — and I walk out of this mountain unfettered, and I collect your friends one by one anyway, slowly, because I will have all the time in the world and none of your cooperation. The smuggler's ledgers will be the least of what burns." It spread its hands, reasonable, almost kind. "Or accept, and everyone you love walks into spring. Those are your choices, Juniper. There is no third door. I have had three hundred years to check."

And that — that was the mistake. Not the threat. The arithmetic. Because it said *there is no third door* to a room containing Wren Halloway, who had built a career out of doors nobody else could find; and Tam, who read bindings the way other people read gossip; and Doss, who had once, per Wren, talked a toll-collector out of a toll with nothing but patience and soup; and Vael, who had carried a king's curse on its back for three hundred years and was, at this moment, looking at the throne with an expression I would only understand later, an expression like a decision being quietly finished.

But that was the next hour's work, and I didn't know it yet. What I knew was the choice laid out in front of me like cards: my grandmother's voice, or Aldric's death; my friends' freedom, or their ruin; the Gate restored, or the world unmade. Duty on one side. Love on the other. Exactly the two hands I'd been dealt at birth, and the Regent waiting for me to play one.

"Give me a moment," I said.

"Of course," said the Regent, gracious as a spider.

I turned to my people. Wren had one eyebrow up — her *well, captain?* eyebrow. Tam was hugging the map tube like a talisman. Doss stood planted as a bridge piling. And Vael was watching the king, and the king was watching Vael, and between them passed something old and wordless and terrible, like two survivors of the same shipwreck recognizing each other across a crowded shore.

"Juniper," Aldric whispered, as I stepped toward my family. "Whatever you decide — decide it *out loud*. Together. It fears nothing in this world except that."

The Regent's smile finally, fully, fell.

"Take all the time you need," it said, and its voice had gone cold as the north face of the Gate.
