## 18. What the Family Decides

### Juniper

The Regent said *take all the time you need*, and I want it noted for the historical record that this was a lie. Time was exactly what it did not want us to have. Every hour we spent talking was an hour the binding frayed further, and the thing on the throne of roots knew it, and we knew that it knew, and still — Aldric had told us to decide out loud. Together. So we did the one thing no keeper had ever done inside these walls, and the one thing the Hollow Regent had spent nine hundred years making sure would never happen:

We sat down on the floor of its own court and held a meeting.

It was Wren who broke the circle first, in the sense of drawing it — she scuffed a ring in the pale root-wood with her boot heel, muttered "warding, probably useless, do it anyway," and then planted herself at the top of it like a general claiming high ground.

"Right," she said. "Ground rules. Nobody decides alone. Nobody dies quietly to be noble about it. And nobody—" she pointed at Vael without looking at him, "—nobody volunteers for anything until they've said the stupid part out loud."

"The stupid part," Vael repeated.

"You know what I mean. The part you're hiding under the brave bit."

The wolf's ears flattened. In the green half-light of the buried court, with the Regent sitting very still on its throne and pretending to be a statue of patience, Vael looked — for the first time since the ridge — like something that could be hurt.

I'll be honest: I wanted to look away from him. The confession was still fresh in my ribs. *I helped bind him.* Nine hundred years of guilt walking beside me on four quiet paws. But Wren had made the rule, and the rule existed because of him, so he went first.

* * *

### Vael (as Tam later wrote it down, because I refused to speak of it twice)

The wolf stood in the center of the scuffed ring and did not sit, because wolves do not sit when they mean to be judged; they stand, and let you see all of them.

"I bound him," Vael said. His voice filled the hall the way cold fills a valley. "You know this. What you do not know is how. A binding of that size requires three things: a grammar strong enough to hold a king — the scholars supplied that, and your Tam has already found where it creaks. It requires a vessel willing to carry the curse — I supplied that, gladly, and have regretted it in the manner of a man who regrets nothing else and therefore everything." He paused. "And it requires a keeper."

"A keeper," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Aldric said it needs *me*."

"It needs a heart that keeps things," Vael said. "That is what your line has been for nine hundred years, Juniper Okonkwo, whether you knew it or not. Not jailers. Anchors. Your grandmother did not guard the Gate. She *was* the Gate. As was her mother, and hers, back to the first of you, who knelt in this hall and gave her keeping freely, because Aldric asked it of her as a friend and she loved him enough to say yes."

The word landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I thought of the fever dreams — the young king laughing, the first keeper's hands steady on his crown, the promise passed down and down and down until it reached a girl on a mountain who thought she'd inherited a chore.

"So it wants my consent," I said. "That's why it's been polite. That's why it offered me everything. A cage only closes if the keeper turns the key."

"And if you refuse," Wren said, "it takes the offer off the table and starts breaking fingers. Metaphorically. Mostly."

"If I refuse alone," I said, and here I made myself stand up, because some things you cannot say sitting down, "then I'm just the next in a long line of people who carried this alone. And I'm done with that. So. Nobody decides alone. Somebody find the flaw. Tam?"

* * *

### Tam

Here is what nobody tells you about scholarship: it is mostly terror wearing spectacles.

I had the map tube hugged to my chest like a lifebelt, which was ridiculous, because the map was spread open on the floor between us and also because a paper tube has never once stopped anything from happening to anybody. But holding it made me feel like a scholar instead of a runaway who'd learned his letters smuggling forged manifests, so I kept hugging it right up until Juniper said my name, at which point dignity required me to unroll the thing and become useful.

"Right," I said. "The grammar."

Nine hundred years ago, a court of magicians — the Ashwife's predecessors, may their names be forgiven — built a binding out of three sentences. I'd been picking at those sentences since the hearth-tower, the way you pick at a loose thread when you're nervous, and somewhere over the Drowned Stair, half-drowned, the thread had come loose in my head and shown me what was underneath.

"Every binding is a sentence," I said. "Subject, verb, object. The subject is Aldric — the thing being held. The verb is the curse — Vael carrying it, the Gate anchoring it. But the *object*—" I tapped the map, on the faded signature block at the bottom where nine signatures curled like sleeping serpents. "The object is the keepers. All of them. Each generation renewed the sentence by consenting to it. That's why the seal hums, Juniper. That hum isn't magic. It's agreement. Nine centuries of yes, sung into stone."

Doss frowned. "So stop agreeing."

"That's the beautiful part. You can't just stop. The sentence doesn't end — it *transfers*. Refuse, and the whole weight of it falls on whoever's nearest and most willing. That's what the Regent wants. Not your yes. Your refusal, so it can catch the curse as it drops and walk out wearing it like a coat." I looked around the circle. "But a sentence can also be *rewritten*. If every party consents to new terms. Subject, verb, object — change any one of them and it's a different sentence entirely."

"Change it to what?" Wren asked.

And that was when Doss, of all people, said the thing that cracked the problem open like an egg.

* * *

### Doss

I don't hold with cleverness, generally. Cleverness is what gets folk drowned on rivers they thought they knew. But I've piloted the Thorn for thirty years, and there's one law older than any binding, and it's this: water finds its level. You don't fight the current. You give it somewhere to go.

So when the lad started on about subjects and objects and verbs, I scratched my beard and said the plain thing, because someone had to:

"Seems to me the trouble is it's all one-sided. King's bound. Wolf carries it. Keeper anchors it. Everyone's holding and nobody's sharing. Any crew I ever ran, the load got split or somebody went under." I shrugged. "Why's it got to be one man in a box? Why not — all of us? A little each?"

Silence. The kind of silence where you can hear cleverness happening.

Tam's face did a thing I'll remember till I die — it lit up like a lamp behind frosted glass. "Shared custody," he whispered. "A covenant instead of a prison. The curse divided among — among everyone who *chooses* it—"

"Whoa, whoa," Wren said. "Define *everyone*. Because I am not signing up to carry a piece of nightmare king in my chest, thank you kindly."

"No — not the curse itself, maybe not even that—" Tam was scrambling through the map now, flipping to the margins, the annotations, the Ashwife's notes in her cramped sideways hand. "Listen. Listen! The binding fails because it's a closed circuit — one vessel, one anchor, no relief. The Gate didn't crack because the curse grew. It cracked because Juniper's the last yes left standing, and one voice humming alone goes flat. But a covenant — an *open* covenant — renewed freely, by many, generation after generation, each person carrying only what they choose to carry—"

"Belonging," Juniper said softly.

We all looked at them. They were staring at the middle distance, at nothing, at everything — and there was something on their face I'd only seen once before, the night the courier died and they knelt by her body and understood their whole life had been built on a lie. Except this time it wasn't grief. It was the other side of that coin.

"Not imprisonment," they said. "Not duty. Belonging. That's what holds old magic. Not walls. Not consent extracted. People choosing, over and over, to belong to something together." They laughed, short and unsteady. "My grandmother hid the counter-song in the map. I thought it was a weapon. It's not. It's the opening line of a new sentence."

* * *

### Wren

Now, I've run cargoes past crown patrols, past river-wyrms, past one customs officer with a memory like flypaper and a grudge to match, and I know a deal going sour when I smell one. This smelled sweet. Too sweet. Which meant it was time for me to do my job, which is being the suspicious one so nobody else has to waste their turn.

"Before we all start singing kumbaya in the monster's front room," I said, "three problems. One: the Regent has to agree to new terms, and it will not, because its whole existence is the old terms. Two: even if it agrees, agreements with hollow things are worth the wax they're sealed in. Three—" and here I made myself say it, because Halloways don't flinch, "—somebody still has to hold the line while the sentence is being rewritten, and that somebody is our resident keeper, alone, against *that*." I jerked my chin at the throne.

Juniper met my eyes. "Then I won't be alone."

"You will be. Someone has to—"

"Wren." Quiet. The voice they used on the mountain, the one that made blizzards sit down and wait. "You made a rule tonight. Nobody decides alone. Nobody dies quietly to be noble. Did you mean it, or was it just for the wolf?"

I opened my mouth to explain, reasonably, that there were exceptions, that certain sacrifices were traditional, practically contractual—

And couldn't. Because Doss put his hand on my shoulder, heavy as an anchor, and Tam looked at me with those too-big scholarly eyes, and Vael — the great grey idiot — inclined his head like I'd paid him a compliment.

"Oh, rot," I said. "Fine. FINE. We all hang together, et cetera, I hate every one of you. New problem: how do we get the Regent to the table?"

It was Vael who answered, and what he said made the hair on my arms stand up under my sleeves.

"We don't bring it to the table," the wolf said, turning toward the throne, toward the shadow rising out of it like smoke deciding to be a man. "It is already here. It has heard every word. It always does."

* * *

### Juniper

I felt it before Vael finished speaking — the temperature of the hall dropping, the root-light guttering, the Regent unfolding from the throne of roots with the unhurried grace of something that had never once been surprised in nine hundred years.

Except it was wrong about that. Because when it spoke, its voice — usually silk over emptiness — had a crack in it.

"Clever," it said. "Clever little creatures. You think a covenant holds? You think *belonging* is a chain strong enough for what sleeps in him?" It descended the dais steps toward our little circle, and every step made the walls groan. "I have watched empires of love rot from the inside. I have worn kings who were adored by millions, and I ate them all the same."

"Yeah," Wren said, not moving an inch, arms crossed, in the tone she used on customers trying to renegotiate after delivery. "But you've never had *us*."

And the Regent stopped. Because — and I saw it, we all saw it — for the first time, the thing wearing the king's crown did not have an answer ready.

Behind it, on the throne, King Aldric lifted his head. Half of his face was still root and shadow, but the other half was a tired man's face, and the tired man was smiling.

"Keeper," he rasped. "Your grandmother left you more than a song. Ask the wolf what she whispered to *him*, the night the binding was made. Ask him what he promised her he'd do, if the day ever came when the sentence needed ending."

Vael went very still.

"I promised her," the wolf said slowly, "a way out. For everyone. Even for me." He turned, and his amber eyes found mine, and in them was nine hundred years of waiting. "Juniper. Sing me the first line. Let's find out if your family taught you the rest."

Above us, far above, through a mile of stone, I could almost hear the Hollow Gate — flat on the north face, failing, dying — and for the first time in my life, I did not reach for it to hold it shut.

I reached for the song to set it free.
