## 19. The Unbinding

The Regent smiled at me with my grandmother's mouth, and I have never hated anything so much in my life.

That's where it had decided to begin, you understand. Not with fire, not with its hollow soldiers pouring up the stair like smoke given ambition. With her. With Maren Okonkwo, Keeper of the Hollow Gate, standing in the root-throne hall in her patched grey coat, arms crossed, looking at me the way she used to when I'd tracked mud across her clean floor.

"You always were a stubborn child," it said in her voice, and the worst part — the truly unforgivable part — was that it had the accent right. The flat northern vowels, the way she clipped the ends of words like she was saving them for later.

"That's not her," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. Behind me, Tam was already kneeling at the base of the throne, fingers moving over the binding-glyphs carved into the living wood, and Vael was a grey shadow flowing along the hall's edge, and Wren had a knife out and Doss had a bigger knife, and somewhere above us the mountain groaned like an old man asked to carry one more load.

"No," the Regent agreed pleasantly, wearing her face like a borrowed coat. "She's been dead eleven years. But I kept everything of her. Every memory she ever fed the Gate. Every prayer she whispered into the seal when she thought no one was listening." It tilted her head. "She prayed for you, Juniper. Every night. *Let the child have a life wider than this mountain.*"

"Don't," I said.

"Shall I tell you what she thought of your cooking? Shall I tell you—"

"I said *don't*."

The hall of roots shivered. That was me. I hadn't meant to do that — hadn't meant to let the Gate-hum rise in my chest and shake the living wood — but the thing wearing my grandmother's smile flinched, and I filed that away. It could steal memories. It could steal faces, voices, twenty years of dead women's love. What it couldn't steal was the hum. That had been passed hand to hand down four generations of Okonkwos, and it lived in me now, and it was *mine*.

"Tam," I said. "How long?"

"Don't rush a scholar," Tam said, in the tone of someone whose hands were shaking and who was furious about it. He had chalk — actual chalk, the absurd little man had carried chalk through a flooded cavern and a garrison assault — and he was marking the binding-script on the throne's roots, translating as he went. "The grammar's a covenant-form. Old style. It's not written *at* the king, it's written *with* him — every clause needs his consent to hold. That's the flaw. That's why it needs a keeper." He glanced up at me, and his eyes were wet and his voice was steady. "A willing heart, Juniper. The whole binding is a willing heart, stretched over two hundred years like a drumskin. We don't break it. We *unconsent*."

"Then unconsent faster."

"Vael," Tam called, "I need the curse-channel. The root-vein on the east side — the black one."

Vael flowed to it. I want to be honest here: I had spent three days being angry at that wolf, and I hadn't finished. It had bound Aldric. It had helped write the lie my whole life was built on. But when it had come to me the night before, tail low, and said *let me carry the curse when it comes out of him, I have been his hound for two centuries and I will not leave him to hold it alone* — well. You can be angry at someone and still stand shoulder to shoulder with them when the mountain starts to fall. That turned out to be most of what family was.

The Regent knew it too. That's why it stopped playing grandmother.

* * *

The change came over it like frost coming over a window. My grandmother's face greyed, cracked at the corners, and what was underneath wasn't a face at all — it was the *absence* of one, a smooth pale blank with a crown of black root set upon nothing, and its eyes were two holes that went down and down into the dark of the mountain.

"Hollow soldiers," it said, and its voice was now every voice it had ever eaten, layered and wrong. "Rise."

They came out of the walls. That's the only way I can describe it — the roots of the throne hall peeled back and men stepped out of them, or the shapes of men, with that same smooth blankness where their faces should have been. Hollow-eyed, the scouts had called the ones on the passes. They'd been made the same way the Regent had been made: out of people who had given themselves away, piece by piece, until nothing was left to refuse with.

Wren spat. "I hate faceless men," she announced. "No eyes to gouge. It takes all the artistry out of it."

"Left side," Doss rumbled, and then the ferryman was moving, and I stopped watching, because I had a job.

The hum.

Here is the thing they don't tell you about a lifetime of solitary duty: it prepares you for exactly one moment, and the moment is this. Every night for twenty years I had walked the Gate and held its song alone. Every night I had listened for the flat note and sung the correction into the stone. I had thought it was maintenance. It had been rehearsal.

I planted my feet in the center of the hall, on the flagstone the map's hidden margin had marked with my grandmother's cipher — *the singer stands here* — and I opened the counter-song.

It was in the map. It had been in the map the whole time, worked into the border decoration in notes instead of letters, and I had walked past it for weeks without seeing it, because she had known I'd only look when I needed it. Maren Okonkwo, teaching me from beyond the grave one last time. The song was a key fitted to the binding's lock, and it was also — I understood this as it came out of me, raw and high and shaking the dust from the rafters of roots — it was also a lullaby. She used to hum it when I couldn't sleep. She had *changed* it. Taken a keeper's working and folded a child's comfort into it, and hidden the whole thing in a smuggler's map, trusting me to carry both.

The hollow soldiers staggered. The Regent screamed — a sound like a door tearing off its hinges — and came at me across the hall.

* * *

I don't remember most of the fight in order. Memory in a crisis is a smuggler's map itself: routes and fragments, and you trust the ones that kept you alive.

I remember Wren on the root-terraces above, fighting two hollow men with a knife and a terrible vocabulary, keeping them off Tam's work. I remember Doss simply refusing to fall — he took a blow that should have put him down and came up swinging a broken oar he had, impossibly, brought with him, because Doss believed a man should never be far from an oar. I remember Vael with the black root-vein in its jaws, drinking the curse as Tam unpicked it clause by clause, the wolf's grey coat going dark from the paws upward, and Vael *laughing* — a sound I will hear until I die — laughing like this was the debt being paid at last.

I remember the Regent's hands closing around my throat, and its stolen memories pouring into me like cold water into a boot.

I saw the binding. I saw the young king — Aldric, twenty-five years old and terrified and *willing*, kneeling on this same floor while the first keepers sang him into the wood. I saw the first lie, told gently, told to protect: *the Gate must stand forever, tell no one why.* I saw centuries of keepers walking their rounds, humming to a prison, believing it was a wall. I saw my grandmother, young, learning the truth and choosing to keep it anyway because the truth would have broken the seal, and I saw her weeping into her journal by candlelight, writing *forgive me* in the margins over and over.

"You could stop," the Regent said into my face, my grandmother's face swimming up over its blankness again, because it was drowning and reaching for whatever might make me hesitate. "Sing the Gate back. Rebind him. I will give you them all — the smuggler, the boy, the ferryman — whole and safe. I will give you her. Maren Okonkwo, out of my memories, made flesh. You could have your grandmother back, Juniper. All you have to do is kneel."

And here is the thing I want written down, whoever tells this story after me: I *wanted* it. For one whole terrible second I wanted it so badly my song faltered, and the hall went quiet, and the Regent smiled.

Then I thought: she prayed for me to have a life wider than this mountain. And this — kneeling in the dark forever, holding a prison shut with my throat, trading my people for a puppet made of a dead woman's memories — was the narrowest life there had ever been.

"No," I said, and sang louder.

* * *

Tam found the last clause at the worst possible moment.

"It needs *him*!" he shouted over the din. "The final clause — the king's own voice — Juniper, he has to unconsent himself, wake him, *wake him now*!"

Aldric. Half in the throne, half out, his human face buried under two centuries of root and hollow. Vael was full of curse by then, black to the shoulders, barely a wolf anymore, more a shape of borrowed pain. It looked at me with its last grey eye and said, in the mind-voice that was somehow still gentle: *The crown. Pull the crown. He was never the crown's. The crown was always mine to carry.*

So I let go of the song — the hardest thing I have ever done, letting the Gate-hum drop mid-phrase with the Regent lunging at me — and I ran, and I climbed the throne of living wood, and I took hold of the crown of black root with both hands.

It fought me. Of course it fought me. It showed me every keeper who had ever died holding the seal. It showed me the Gate cracking, the lowlands drowning in wild magic, my friends' names on a list of the consumed. It showed me my own hands, old and alone, still walking a round that no longer needed walking.

And underneath all of it, faint as the first note of the hum, I heard a voice. A young man's voice, two hundred years deep, saying: *Please. I have been so tired for so long.*

I pulled.

The crown came free like a tooth from a jaw, and the hall *screamed* — the roots, the soldiers, the Regent, all of it, one vast sound of a held breath finally released — and King Aldric opened his eyes.

He was old. He was impossibly, heartbreakingly old, two hundred years old in a face that had never been allowed to age past its terror, and he looked at Vael — at the black, shaking, ruined shape of Vael — and he wept.

"Vael," he said. "You stayed."

"Always," said the wolf, and turned to face the Regent.

* * *

What happened next, I saw only in pieces, because I was on my knees on the throne dais with the crown in my hands and the counter-song rising in my throat again, because the binding wasn't undone yet, it was *unraveling*, and an unraveling needs holding or it tears everything with it.

Tam was chanting the unconsent — I could hear the grammar turning over, clause by clause, the old covenant-form being spoken backwards, *I do not will it, I never willed it, let it be unmade* — and each clause he broke, the Regent lost a piece of itself. The hollow soldiers came apart like ash in wind. The stolen faces surfaced and sank in its blankness — hundreds of them, centuries of them, everyone it had ever eaten, and for one moment it looked less like a monster than like a mass grave that had learned to walk.

The Regent made its last play at me. Not grandmother this time. It wore *me* — my face, my voice, my hands. "If you finish the song," it said, "the Gate falls. Everything you were is over. You'll be nothing."

"Wrong," I said, and my voice cracked, and I sang anyway. "I'll be everything *else*."

Vael hit the Regent like an avalanche given purpose. And I felt it happen through the hum — felt the wolf open itself the way it had offered to, the way it had been offering since the ridge, and take the whole of the curse, the whole of the Regent, the whole two-century weight of a broken covenant, into the hollow space inside itself where a hound's love had once been. Tam's last clause broke. The binding's grammar collapsed into plain air. And Vael — brave, guilty, magnificent Vael — closed around the darkness like a fist and *shattered*.

The light went through everything. Through the roots, through the throne, through me, through the mountain itself, up and up toward the Gate that had spent its whole long life being a wall and was now, at last, permitted to be nothing at all.

I heard it crack. Two hundred miles of stone, and I heard it crack like a hearth-log, gentle and final, and the hum — my hum, my grandmother's hum, four generations of Okonkwo hum — rose off the mountain like a bird let out of a cage, and did not come back.

In the sudden silence, King Aldric slid from his throne into Vael's fading shadow, and I caught him, and he was light. He was so light. Two hundred years of prison and what remained weighed less than a child.

"The wolf," he whispered. "Where is my wolf?"

"Gone ahead," I said, because it was true, and because he deserved the kind version. "Waiting for you."

He smiled — a young man's smile, at last, on an old man's face — and above us, through the cracking Gate, the first spring light of the thaw came down the broken stair like water finding its old riverbed.

* * *

The last thing I saw before the light took the hall was the Regent's crown of black root, lying on the flagstones where I had dropped it, going grey, going brittle, going to ordinary dead wood.

And the last thing I heard — faint, from somewhere in the light, in a voice I had only ever heard in my head — was Vael, laughing again.

Then the mountain opened, and everything I had ever guarded walked out to meet the morning.

I'll tell you what came next — the weeping, the wobbly walk down into the thaw, Wren catching my elbow and pretending she wasn't crying, Doss producing, from somewhere, an oar — but that's the next part of the story, and I was not, as it turned out, the only one left to tell it.
