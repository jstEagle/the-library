## 20. The Wolf Breaks

I have thought a great deal about how to tell this part, and I keep failing, so I will simply tell it badly and let you forgive me.

The counter-song was still in my throat when the world went white. Not bright — *white*, the way snow is white, the way bone is white, the way a page is white before anyone has dared to write on it. The Regent had come apart from Aldric like rot lifted free of good wood, all its stolen memories flaring around it — my grandmother's hands kneading bread, Wren's first theft, Doss's daughter's laugh, things it had no right to hold — and it screamed with a hundred voices at once, every voice it had ever eaten.

And out of that screaming walked a wolf.

Not the grey shadow who had trotted beside us through flooded dark and crown soldiers' camps and my own worst suspicions. This was Vael as it must have been when the mountains were young: big as a hillside, fur like storm-light, eyes the colour of the deep places where rivers are born. It did not look at me. It looked at the Regent, and there was such tenderness in that look that I understood, finally, what penance actually is. Not shame. Not sorrow. Just the willingness to stand in front of the thing you helped make and say: *me instead*.

"Vael—" Tam started, and his voice cracked on the name.

The wolf laughed. Of course it laughed. It laughed the way it always had, low and rolling, like thunder deciding to be kind.

"Hold the song, little keeper," it said, without turning its head. "You're doing beautifully. Don't stop on my account."

"I can't just—"

"You can," it said. "That's rather the point of you."

Then it opened its mouth — not in a roar but in something like an invitation — and the Regent, which had spent three centuries learning every way there is to take a thing, took the invitation. It poured itself into the wolf like night pouring into a valley. And Vael closed its jaws around it.

What happened next happened quickly and slowly both, the way the important things do. The hall of living wood shuddered. The roots that formed the throne groaned. Somewhere far above us, stone that had hummed since before my grandmother's grandmother began to sing a note I had never heard it sing — high, clear, almost glad.

"No," I said, into the song, because I am apparently incapable of holding my tongue even during miracles. "No, wait—"

Vael turned its enormous head and found my eyes with its own, and for one moment they were just the ordinary amber eyes of an ordinary stubborn wolf, the ones that had watched me sleep on watch, that had judged my cooking, that had once — I remembered this suddenly, absurdly — once carried Tam by the scruff for half a mile because he'd insisted he wasn't tired.

"Juniper Okonkwo," it said. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate. Terrible liar. Worse cook. Best of them."

"Don't you dare eulogize me while I'm standing here."

"It's called a compliment. You should try receiving one sometime." Its ears flicked toward the light flooding down from the cracking ceiling — real light, morning light, the spring thaw finding its way through stone that no longer had any reason to be sealed. "Look at that. We're early."

"You said midwinter," Wren said. Her voice was steady in the way a rope is steady — under enormous strain, holding anyway. "You said we had until midwinter."

"I lied about some things too," Vael said gently. "It seemed to run in the company."

And then it *broke*.

I don't have a better word. Glass breaks, ice breaks, hearts break — none of those are quite it. Vael came apart the way a knot comes apart when someone finally finds the right end to pull: all at once, and with a sound like relief. Light went everywhere. The Regent went nowhere — unmade mid-scream, its hundred voices cut off together like a door closing on a choir. And the last thing the wolf-spirit did, as it scattered into motes that rose instead of fell, drifting up toward the crack in the ceiling like sparks returning to their fire, was to leave one pawprint in the stone floor of the buried court.

Just one. Deliberate. Deep.

So that no one could ever say it hadn't been here.

* * *

King Aldric died in the spring thaw light, and it was, all things considered, a good death, if you can call anything good that leaves four strangers kneeling in melting snow weeping onto a dead man's hands.

He was human again. That's the thing I want you to understand, because it matters more than the dying. Whatever the binding had made of him — whatever hollow half-thing had sat enthroned in roots for three centuries wearing his face like a borrowed coat — that was gone with the Regent. What remained, laid out on the stones of his own ruined court with the morning coming down through the broken mountain, was just a man. Old. Thin. His hair white where it had been brown in the murals. His eyes open, and clear, and looking up at the sky he hadn't seen in three hundred years.

"The sun," he said. His voice was rust and honey. "Is that — is that the sun?"

"Yes," I said, because someone had to, and because I seemed to be the only one still capable of words. "That's the sun."

"It's smaller than I remembered." He smiled. Actually smiled. "Or perhaps I was larger then. Kings usually are, in their own memories." His gaze drifted, unfocused, and then caught — on Wren, on Tam with the map's torn grammar still clutched in both hands, on Doss standing awkward and enormous with his oar across his shoulders like a pilgrim's staff, and last on me. On the keeper. On the last keeper, though I didn't know yet that I would decline to be the next one.

"You held the song," he said. "All of it. Alone, at the end. Your grandmother's song?"

"And hers before her," I managed. "Back to the beginning."

"To the lie, you mean." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Or better. I honestly could not tell you. "They told you the Gate kept magic out. It was kinder, I suppose, than telling children they were keeping a sick man caged." He coughed, and the cough had blood in it, and Doss moved forward instinctively, uselessly, the way good men do when there is nothing left to do. "Tell them. Tell the keepers after you, if there are keepers after you. Tell everyone. A cage is not a covenant. A covenant is—" He stopped. Frowned, as if searching a very large room for a very small word.

"A belonging," Tam whispered. "A belonging holds it. That's what the flaw in the binding was — fear was the mortar. Fear crumbles."

"A belonging," Aldric agreed, wonderingly. "Yes. You've got it. Took three hundred years and a smuggler, a scholar, a ferryman, and a keeper to get it, but — you've got it." He laughed, weakly, and the laugh cost him, and he paid it gladly. "Better trade than I deserved."

He asked for snow. Actual snow, a handful of it, from outside — not the old frozen dark of the court but new-fallen, from the world above. Doss brought it, cradled in two huge palms like something holy, and pressed it into the king's hands, and King Aldric of Thornfell held the spring thaw in his fingers and let it melt there, watching the water run, and said, very quietly:

"Oh. There you are."

And then he was gone.

* * *

We climbed out of the mountain slowly, because none of us wanted to arrive anywhere.

The Gate cracked open above us as its purpose ended — I heard it happen before I saw it, a sound like the world's largest door being unlatched by the world's gentlest hand, three centuries of tension releasing in a single long exhalation of stone. When we emerged onto the threshold I had walked my whole life, the Hollow Gate stood open for the first time since its making: not destroyed, not collapsed, just *open*, the two great faces of the pass swung apart like arms, letting the lowland wind pour through to meet the mountain snow.

The hum was gone.

I stood there listening for it — reflexively, hopelessly, the way you reach for a hand that isn't there anymore — and found only silence, and under the silence something else. Wind. Birds. The ordinary, unguarded noise of a world that no longer needed guarding.

My whole life had been that hum. My grandmother's lullaby-hum, the seal-checks at dusk, the pride I'd felt at seven years old when I first heard what no one else could hear. *You're the keeper now, Juniper. Only our family, only ever ours.* She'd sung me to sleep with the gate-song. She'd taught me the routes she swore no one knew. She'd written in her journal — in that careful, loving hand I could still see behind my eyelids — *the Gate keeps the wildness out, and we keep the Gate.*

She had known. She must have known. The journals, the map hidden inside the map, the counter-song stitched into the margins — she had known what the Gate really was, what slept beneath it, and she had chosen to tell me everything except the truth, and she had done it *because she loved me*, and I stood on the threshold of the fallen Gate with thaw-water running down the stones and grieved both at once, the wolf and the lie, the spirit and the grandmother, tangled together so tightly I couldn't have separated them if I'd tried.

"She lied to you," Wren said beside me. Not unkindly. Wren is never unkind; she is merely honest at inconvenient volumes.

"She protected me."

"She lied to you," Wren repeated, "because protecting people and lying to them wear the same coat in bad weather. Believe me. I know." She looked out through the open Gate at the lowlands — green fields, smoke from distant chimneys, a road running straight and unafraid toward a mountain that had spent forever being a wall. "Difference is, she meant well. So did I, most times. Doesn't make the coat less heavy."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half-sob, and I let it.

"He left a pawprint," I said, when I could speak again. "In the stone. Did you see? He *planned* that. Right up to the end, that ridiculous creature was thinking about evidence."

"He was thinking about *you*," Tam said quietly. "About whoever came after. About proof that someone stood here and chose to pay the bill." He hugged the map-tube to his chest. "Keepers leave seals. He left a print. Same job, different animal."

Doss said nothing. Doss rarely does. But he put his hand on my shoulder — the whole hand, it nearly covered it — and squeezed once, and held it there, and that was its own entire speech, and I understood every word.

* * *

We buried the king where the light fell best.

There wasn't a question of carrying him back down into the court. Wren said — and I think she was quoting some smuggler's creed, or inventing one on the spot, which amounts to the same thing — "Nobody gets buried in the place that held them." So we carried him up, out through the open Gate, down the north slope to a hollow where the thaw had opened the earth soft and the morning came through the pines in long gold bars, and we dug with an oar, two knives, and Doss's bare hands, and we laid him down with the map tube empty beside him, because the map's work was done and it belonged to him more than to any of us.

Tam spoke the burial words from three different traditions and apologized for the improvisation. Wren said something short and fierce that I suspect was a prayer wearing a smuggler's clothes. Doss stood with his cap over his heart.

And I sang.

Not the gate-song. Not the counter-song. Something older that rose in me from wherever songs rise from — the same place the hum had lived, maybe, except this one had no walls in it, no sealing, no keeping-out. Just a melody offered up to the open air, for a king who had volunteered young and waited long, and for a wolf who had owed a debt and paid it laughing, and for a grandmother whose love had been true even when her story wasn't.

When I finished, the silence afterward was not empty. It was the silence of a room full of people who are all okay, in the specific way of people who have survived the same terrible thing together and will never fully explain it to anyone else.

"We should mark the grave," Tam said. "Something permanent."

Wren walked over to a flat stone nearby, considered it, and set it upright at the head of the mound with a smuggler's efficiency. Then she looked at me.

"You're the stoneworker's granddaughter," she said. "Do the words."

I knelt. I had no chisel, so I used the point of my knife, cutting slow and shallow, and what I cut was this:

**HERE LIES ALDRIC, WHO WAS KEPT, AND VAEL, WHO KEPT HIM, AND NEITHER WAS EVER A PRISONER AGAIN.**

Below it, smaller, because my hands were shaking:

**THE GATE IS OPEN. COME IN.**

* * *

We camped that night on the threshold itself — the actual threshold, the ancient sill-stone of the Hollow Gate, because it seemed right, because the view was extraordinary, and because after everything, none of us wanted to sleep anywhere that had walls.

The stars came out through the open pass, and for the first time in three centuries they shone all the way down the mountain road into the lowlands, and I sat with my knees drawn up and my chin on them and felt the strangest sensation, which took me a while to identify.

It was peace. I'd read about it in books.

"They'll come, you know," Wren said, beside me. She was cleaning her nails with her knife, which is how she meditates. "Lowlanders. First the curious, then the merchants, then the fools, then everyone. An open Gate is an invitation whether you write it on a gravestone or not."

"I know."

"There'll need to be someone here. Someone who knows the passes, who speaks both sides of the mountain. Someone the strays trust." She glanced sideways at me, sly as a cat. "Someone with experience keeping a door."

"Wren—"

"Not tonight," she said. "Tonight we eat Doss's stew and mourn properly. But someday soon, keeper, somebody's going to have to decide what this place becomes." She pointed her knife at the pawprint, visible even in the starlight, worn deep and permanent in the threshold stone. "He left you a door with his signature on it. Seems rude not to answer."

Down in the dark below us, far off along the lowland road, a light was moving. Small. Warm. A lantern, traveling toward the mountain — the first traveler in three hundred years to walk toward an open Gate without knowing what they'd find.

I watched it come, and I found, to my surprise, that I was smiling.

"Someone's coming," I said.

"Course they are," said Wren. "You wrote *come in* on a rock. Word travels."

The lantern bobbed closer through the night, and the wind through the open pass smelled of pine and rain and the wide green world, and somewhere — in the stone, in the stars, in whatever country wolves go when they break — I swear I heard laughter.

I got up to put the kettle on.
