## 22. Home Is the Road We Walk Together

The first thing I built at the old Gate site was not a wall.

I want that on the record, carved into something permanent, because if my grandmother's ghost ever drifts up the mountain to check on me, I want her to see it immediately and have to sit with it. Not a wall. A roof.

It took me most of the winter to understand why that mattered so much to me. For a while I told myself it was practical — Thornfell's spring comes late and wet, and travelers coming down from the high passes need somewhere dry to sleep. That was true. But truth is cheap; every liar I've ever met had plenty of it. The real reason is simpler and harder to say: for twenty-six years I lived in a building whose entire purpose was to say *no*. No passage. No entry. No warmth, no welcome, no conversation beyond the formal challenges of the Keeper's Round. The Hollow Gate was a beautiful sentence with one word in it, and I had been its only speaker, saying it into the wind every night until I forgot there were other words.

So: a roof. Four posts of black pine, a slate roof pitched steep enough to shrug off snow, walls on two sides only — the north and east, against the weather — open on the south where the sun comes up over the lowlands. A hearth built into the stone of what used to be the Gate's foundation, because the foundation stones still hold warmth the way old things hold grudges, and because it felt right to build the fire where the seal used to be. The hearthstone itself came from the Gate's threshold. I pried it loose myself, on a bright cold morning in early spring, and when it came free the whole ruined arch sighed — a long exhale of dust and old magic — and then went quiet in a way it had never been quiet before. Even broken, even purposeless, the Gate had hummed. Now it just sat under its blanket of snow like an old dog finally allowed to sleep by the fire.

I set the threshold stone flat beside the hearth, polished side up, and it became the bench where people sit to pull their boots off. It seemed only fair. It spent three hundred years keeping people out; it can spend the next three hundred learning to host them.

* * *

They started arriving before I was ready, which is how everything worth having has always come to me.

The first was a tinker named Bess, who came up from the valley with a mule loaded with pots and asked if the stories about the open pass were true. I said they were. She looked at me — at the keeper's knot I still wore out of habit, at the hammer in my hand, at the half-built roof — and she said, "You're the one who broke it? My gran told me you'd be struck by lightning." I said lightning had been considered but rejected on grounds of scheduling. She laughed and stayed the night, and paid for her supper with a copper kettle that I still use, and when she left she told everyone in three villages that there was a warm place at the top of the pass and a strange person there who'd give you tea and directions and ask nothing back.

That was the problem, of course. Word travels faster downhill than any traveler does. By mid-spring I was feeding strangers four nights out of seven, and I had to build the second bunk platform, and then the third, and I learned very quickly that the waystation needed rules, which I painted on a board above the hearth in letters big enough to read by firelight:

1. THE FIRE IS FOR EVERYONE.
2. TAKE WHAT YOU NEED. LEAVE WHAT YOU CAN.
3. NO ONE IS TURNED AWAY FOR HAVING NOTHING.
4. IF YOU FIGHT UNDER THIS ROOF, YOU SLEEP OUTSIDE WITH THE WEATHER AND YOUR DECISIONS.
5. YES, THE WOLF STORY IS TRUE. NO, THE WOLF DOES NOT COME BACK. STOP ASKING.

Rule five cost me something to write. But we'll get there.

* * *

Wren Halloway arrived in summer, as I knew she would, because Wren treats distance and absence as suggestions rather than laws.

She came up the south road with her hood back and her hair full of road dust, leading a packhorse, and she stood at the edge of the waystation's lamplight with her hands on her hips and surveyed the place like a general assessing fortifications, which for Wren is the same gesture as someone deciding whether to hug you.

"So this is what you did instead of dying heroically," she said.

"Somebody had to disappoint the ballads."

"Ballads?" Her eyebrows went up. "There are *ballads*?"

"There are ballads. They get several things wrong."

"Let me guess. They make you taller."

"They make me decisive."

She grinned, and dropped her pack inside the door like she owned the place, because Wren has never once entered a room like a guest in her life. Then she did the thing she always does, which is walk the perimeter without asking permission, touching things — the lintel, the rule-board, the threshold-stone bench — reading the whole structure the way Tam reads books, looking for what it confesses.

"You left the south side open," she said at last.

"The sun comes up over the lowlands. Seemed rude to block it."

"You left the south side open," she repeated, "because you wanted anyone walking up that road to see the fire before they saw anything else. So they'd know it was safe before they got close enough to be afraid." She turned around. "You built the opposite of a gate."

"I built a roof, Wren."

"You built the opposite of a gate," she said again, softer, "and you're going to stand there and pretend it's carpentry." Then she hugged me, brief and fierce, the way she does everything, and pulled back before it could become a moment, because Wren would rather swallow hot coals than let a moment happen unattended.

She stayed eleven days. She fixed my terrible joinery — I am many things but a carpenter is not among them — rehung the door so it stopped groaning, argued with me about whether rule four should specify a cooling-off period (it should not; the weather is the cooling-off period), and left before the twelfth day because Wren Halloway cannot be in one place longer than that without developing a twitch. But she left her spare knife on the peg by the door. "For whoever needs it more than I do," she said. "I'll collect it in autumn."

She has collected it every season since. And left it again each time. We both know what the knife is. Neither of us has ever said it aloud. Some covenants are better kept unwritten.

* * *

Tam came back in autumn, and he came back different.

He'd gone east after the Accord was signed — taken a post at the University at Corvale, of all places, the runaway scholar welcomed home like a prodigy, his thesis on the grammar of bindings now required reading for every first-year magician in the lowlands. He wrote to me sometimes, long letters full of footnotes, footnotes in letters, which tells you everything about Tam. The letters were happy. Mostly.

But when he walked up the south road that autumn, thin as ever, ink-stained as ever, I could see the University sitting on him like a coat that fit worse than it used to. He stood in the doorway and looked at the rule-board for a long time.

"Rule one," he said. "The fire is for everyone. Do you know they wanted me to write a paper on that? On the *efficacy conditions* of unrestricted hearth-access as a binding mechanism. They wanted to know the parameters. What counts as 'everyone.' What counts as 'fire.' Whether belonging can be operationalized."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them they'd missed the point so thoroughly that the point was visible from space." He hung his coat on the peg next to Wren's knife. "Belonging isn't a mechanism. That's the whole discovery. The Regent bound Aldric because binding is what power understands. But what held — what actually held, in the end, when everything else failed — wasn't a binding at all. It was just... us. Showing up. There's no parameter for that." He smiled, tired and real. "So I resigned."

"Tam—"

"I'm going to write a book," he said quickly, before I could make it a moment, having clearly learned that trick from Wren. "About the binding. About the lie, and the choice, and all of it. Properly. Not a thesis. A *story*, the way it actually happened, with the parts that don't operationalize. And I need—" he gestured vaguely at the mountains, the ruins, the hearth, me, "—I need to be where it happened to write it. Also your roof leaks on the west side. I noticed it from fifty yards."

"It does not leak."

"Juniper, I watched it leak through a window."

He stayed through the winter that first year, writing at the corner table by the window, arguing with travelers about history, teaching the miller's daughter from the valley to read because she asked and because Tam has never once in his life been able to refuse a sincere question. In spring he went back east to see his editors. He came back the next autumn. And the next. The pattern settled without anyone announcing it: the waystation filled and emptied like breathing, and Tam was one of its lungs.

* * *

Doss never really left.

This will surprise no one who knows Doss. Doss, the gruffest ferryman on any river anywhere, the man who communicated primarily in weather reports and disapproving silences for the entire length of our acquaintance — Doss showed up that first spring with a cartload of timber and the announcement that my roof pitch was "an insult to water" and stayed to fix it. Then he stayed to fix the chimney. Then the bunks. Then, inexplicably, the garden, because it turned out that beneath forty years of ferry-gruffness Doss had a gardener's soul and opinions about soil drainage that he expressed with the passion other men reserve for religion.

He goes down to the river in high summer, when the ford needs him and the fish are running. He comes back. Every time, he comes back. Last autumn I caught him standing at the south opening at dawn, watching the road with his arms crossed, and I thought he was waiting for Wren or Tam, and I was about to go back inside when he said, without turning around:

"Used to be, a man worked a crossing his whole life and nobody remembered his name past the next flood." A pause. Long enough for me to understand he wasn't finished. "This is better."

From Doss, that was a sonnet.

* * *

Now I have to write about Vael, and I have been putting it off for three paragraphs, which for a chapter of prose is a considerable delay, and for me personally has been nearly a year.

Here is what remains of Vael: a pawprint in the stone.

When the wolf broke — when it took the Hollow Regent into itself and shattered, freeing King Aldric to die human and grateful in the thaw-light — the force of it cracked the Gate's threshold from end to end. And in the heart of the crack, pressed into the molten-then-frozen stone like a signature, is a single print of a grey wolf's forepaw, larger than a dinner plate, perfect in every detail down to the ghost of claw-marks.

I did not put it there. Nobody put it there. Tam has theories involving sympathetic resonance and the imprint of willing sacrifice on stone, and his theories are probably correct, and I do not care, because theories are cold comfort at the hour when the fire burns low and the waystation is empty and I am sitting alone with a cup of tea, and I look over at the hearth, and there is the print.

Travelers ask about it constantly. Rule five exists because of them. They want the story, and I tell it — I'm better at telling it now than I was, though I still can't get through the part about the shattering without pausing to fuss with the fire — and then they always ask the same question, with their faces full of hope:

"Could it come back?"

And here is the truth, which I have decided to say plainly even though it costs me: I don't know. Vael was a spirit, older than the binding, older than the kingdom, maybe older than the mountain. Spirits that shatter don't usually reassemble. Tam's scholarship says no. The Ashwife, when I asked her, was quiet a long time and then said only: "Stone remembers. That is not nothing."

But last midwinter night — the anniversary of the unbinding, the coldest and darkest night of the year, when the whole company happened to be together under my roof, Wren and Tam and Doss and a dozen stranded travelers besides — something happened that I have not told anyone except these pages.

We were singing. That was the Ashwife's idea originally, adopted into tradition: on midwinter night you sing the counter-song, the one my grandmother hid in the map, the one I sang alone in the dark of the buried court while my family unraveled a king's curse around me. Except nobody sings it alone anymore. Doss drones the bass line like a landslide with a grudge. Tam has worked out harmonies and will explain them if you let him, so don't. Wren sings loud and slightly wrong on purpose. And I sing the melody, the keeper's line, the thread of the Gate-hum reborn — because that's what the counter-song always was, I understand now. Not a lock. An invitation. A song that says: *you don't have to be alone in there.*

Midway through the third verse, the pawprint warmed.

I felt it from across the room — a change in the air, the specific warmth of a body near a fire, coming from a direction where there was no body. I stopped singing. So did everyone else, one by one, the way birds go quiet, until the silence had a shape, and the shape of it was large and four-footed and patient.

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Snow ticked against the slates.

And then the warmth faded, gently, the way a hand withdraws, and the print was just stone again, and Doss cleared his throat and said, "Verse four," and we sang verse four, and my voice cracked on the high line and nobody mentioned it, which is its own kind of love.

In the morning I knelt by the hearth and touched the print and it was cold as any stone. But the snow outside the south opening — the untouched snow, the snow no one had crossed — held a single line of prints, wolf-sized, walking away toward the ridge. Or walking toward us. With tracks, you can never truly say which direction is which. You only choose what story you need the path to tell.

I chose the one where it was leaving. Because if it was leaving, it might come back. And if it was arriving — well. Then it already had, once, and I'd missed it, and even that was a kind of gift.

* * *

It's spring again now. The third spring since the Gate fell. This morning I swept the snowmelt off the south step and planted the beans Doss bullies me about, and hung the new rule-board, because travelers had scratched messages around my original five rules until the board read like a tavern wall. The additions stay, mostly. Someone added a sixth rule in careful charcoal: *6. THE WOLF'S PRINT IS NOT FOR TOUCHING.* Underneath, in different handwriting: *who touched the print?* And underneath that, in Wren's unmistakable scrawl: *everyone touches the print. it's fine. rule six is void.*

The mountain is green to the treeline. The lowlands sent up their first caravan of the season yesterday — Bess the tinker among them, three years running now, her mule gray-muzzled and imperious — and tonight the waystation is full. Wren is dealing cards and cheating. Tam is losing anyway and pretending not to notice the cheating, because he finds the cheating "texturally interesting." Doss is asleep in his chair by the fire, the way he denies sleeping in his chair by the fire. A wool merchant is telling a story about a goat. Two of the valley children who've appointed themselves my unofficial apprentices are washing dishes badly and happily.

And I am sitting at my own table, in my own house-that-is-not-a-gate, on the threshold stone of the seal I spent my life guarding, writing this final page, and listening to the sound a full room makes.

For twenty-six years, my duty was a word spoken into the wind: *no.* I thought duty meant holding the line alone until I died at my post, and I was proud of that, and the pride was the loneliest thing I ever owned. I know better now. Duty, it turns out, is just love that has learned logistics. It's fixing the roof pitch because travelers will come. It's leaving the knife on the peg. It's footnotes in letters, and timber hauled up a mountain, and a sixth rule voided by popular demand. It's a song sung together on the longest night, addressed to an empty chair, in case the empty chair is listening.

My grandmother believed the Gate was forever, and she was wrong, and I forgive her, because she was wrong the way everyone is wrong about what will hold: we think stone holds things. Stone doesn't hold anything. Stone forgets. What holds is a fire lit before someone arrives, a place kept warm on spec, a door left open on the side where the sun comes up. The old magic of Thornfell doesn't need imprisoning anymore. It needs hosting. And I have a roof.

Somewhere up the darkening ridge, the wind moves through the pines, and for half a heartbeat the sound of it has a rhythm I almost recognize — four beats, like paws, like footsteps on a familiar road, coming down.

I put another log on the fire, and set out one more cup, just in case, and went to deal myself into the game.

Outside, spring came on up the mountain, unhurried, welcome as a light in a window.
