## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things were kept in three colors of ink: black for debts that could be paid, red for debts that could not, and a gray so pale it was nearly a rumor — for debts whose existence the guild officially denied.

Ansel Okonkwo had mixed the gray ink himself, eleven years ago, from ash and rainwater and a single drop of his own blood, because Master Veyra had said, *we need something honest for the dishonest entries*, and Ansel, who was then twenty-three and still believed institutions meant what they wrote down, had thought this was wit rather than policy.

He sat now in the counting room at the top of the Guildhall, where the windows were diamond-paned and slightly wrong — you could see out of them clearly enough, but anyone looking in saw only weather. It was a small impossible thing, one of hundreds stitched through the building like thread through cloth. The guild did not deal in grand impossibilities. Not usually. Grand ones were expensive and attracted attention, and attention was the one commodity the Guild of Impossible Things could not afford to buy or sell.

Ansel dipped his pen in black ink and made a notation in the margin of Ledger Nine, Column Fourteen.

*Entry 4,401: Herron Voss, cooper, sells memory of his mother's voice (duration of sale: permanent; condition of memory: good, though fraying at edges). Price received: settlement of gambling debt to the Saltcellar Brothers, plus interest. Auditor's note: Mr. Voss cried during transaction. This is normal and requires no intervention.*

That last clause he added because the last auditor, old Master Pellin, had once intervened on behalf of a crying client, offering her back her memory at cost, and had been fined two months' wages for sentimentality. *Sentiment is theft from the ledger*, Veyra liked to say. Ansel had never been sure whether she believed it or merely enforced it, and after eleven years he had learned there was often very little difference between the two.

He turned the page.

The counting room smelled of tallow, dust, and the faint copper tang of stored years. In the vault below, behind doors that required three keys and a truth spoken aloud, the guild kept its inventory: jars of remembered summers, vials of first loves decanted at the owner's request (usually after the love went bad), casks of childhoods sold whole by families who needed the space in their heads for grief. There was a shelf, Ansel knew, holding forty-one names in little clay pots. Names were the most delicate trade. A name removed left a shape behind, like a dent in a mattress, and people would lie awake pressing their tongues against the absence without knowing why.

*Entry 4,407: The Widow Dane requests appraisal.*

Ansel looked up. The clerk, a nervous boy named Tam with ink on his chin and his collar, stood in the doorway holding the day's appointment slip as if it might bite him.

"The widow," Tam repeated. "She's early."

"They're always early." Ansel capped his inkwell. "Grief has no patience for queues. Show her in. Tea?"

"Tea?"

"For the client, Tam. We are not monsters. We are merely employed by them." He paused. "Two cups."

* * *

Maren Dane was fifty, perhaps older — it was difficult to tell, because grief had taken her face and rearranged it into something ageless and tired, the way water rearranges a shoreline. She wore her good shawl, the one with the moth-holes turned discreetly inward, and she sat in the client's chair with her hands folded like a woman in church who has come to bargain with a god she doesn't trust.

Ansel set the tea before her and opened the appraisal book.

"Mrs. Dane. Thank you for coming. I'm told you wish to sell."

"Yes." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "I'm told you take — things. Things people can spare."

"We take things people can trade," Ansel said gently. "Whether they can spare them is a question we've learned not to ask." He said it dryly, because that was his manner, but he watched her face as he said it, because dryness and cruelty look alike from the paying side of the table, and he preferred, when possible, to be mistaken for neither. "Tell me what you have."

"I have my husband's name."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere below them, in the vault, forty-one clay pots sat in their dark.

"Tobin Dane," she said. "We were married nineteen years. Everyone in Harrow Lane knew him by it. The fishmonger, the priest, the man who fixed our roof. Tobin, Tobin, Tobin." She smiled briefly, terribly. "It's a good name. Solid. Like him. And no one uses it anymore, do they? He's been dead eight months, and already they say *her late husband* and *poor Mrs. Dane* and *him*. Him, like he's a shadow on the wall. The name's just sitting in me going to waste, and I need bread."

Ansel wrote nothing yet. This, too, was training: never write while the client is still deciding what they're selling, because ink makes decisions feel final, and some decisions should be allowed to stay cowardly for as long as possible.

"You understand," he said, "what the sale means. You will keep the word itself. You'll be able to say it — *Tobin* — the way one says any word. But the weight of it, the particular gravity it holds for you — the sound of him saying your name back, the way it fit in your mouth — that goes into the pot. Strangers may remember him by it. You will know only that you were married to a man, and that his name was a word, and that the word used to mean more than it does."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward, when you say it, it will be like saying *chair*."

Maren Dane looked at her tea for a long moment. "My daughter asked me yesterday what her father's laugh sounded like. I couldn't tell her. Grief took that already, free of charge. So the world's already robbing me. I'd at least like the robbery to come with bread." She lifted her chin, and there — there it was, under the exhaustion: a spine. "What's your offer?"

Ansel named it: twelve marks a month for two years, plus the lump sum that would clear the rent arrears on the Harrow Lane rooms. It was fair. It was, he knew, more than fair; Veyra would have offered nine and called generosity an accounting error. But Ansel had a private tax he levied on the guild, invisible and unrecorded, which consisted of rounding every offer up to the edge of what the books could bear.

"That buys bread?" she asked.

"That buys bread, coal, and a winter's worth of not having to decide between them."

Her hands unclenched. "Then yes. Do it."

"*Mrs. Dane—*"

"Do it before I become sensible about it."

So he did. He brought out the small clay pot and the silver needle, and she held the pot in both hands as though warming them at a fire, and he spoke the extraction — softly, in the oath-script that lived in his bones, the words every swordspeaker learned before they ever learned to swing a blade, because the guild had decided centuries ago that its enforcers should also be its clerks, and that a man who could unmake a debt should understand exactly how heavy debts were.

The name came out of her like a splinter drawn clean. She swayed. He steadied the pot. When she opened her eyes, she looked around the room with polite blankness, the way people did, and said:

"Was I married?"

"You were," Ansel said. "To a good man. His name was a word, and the word was *Tobin*."

"Tobin," she repeated, tasting nothing. Then she frowned faintly, pressed her tongue against something, found nothing there, and let it go. "Strange. Thank you. Where do I collect?"

"Front desk. Ask for Tam — the boy with ink on his chin. He'll count it twice for you, because he counts everything twice; it's his one virtue and we cherish it."

She almost smiled. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at him with an expression he could not read and did not try to.

"You're kind," she said, "for a man who takes things."

"I'm precise," Ansel said. "It's frequently mistaken for kindness, and I've stopped correcting people."

She went. Ansel sat alone with the clay pot, forty-second of its line, and did not open the vault door for a while.

* * *

His own entry was in Ledger One, Page One, Line One, because he had been a joke among the clerks even before he was anything else: *Okonkwo, Ansel. Asset held in trust: one (1) grief, category: unresolved. Note: asset refuses all offers. Reappraisal annually.*

Every year, on the anniversary, some well-meaning junior auditor would approach him with the reappraisal form and a hopeful expression, and every year Ansel would sign the same line: *Asset retains full value. Not for sale.* They thought it was stubbornness. It was not. It was the only transaction he intended to refuse for the rest of his life, and he guarded it the way misers guard gold.

The grief was his sister's name. Adaeze. Drowned at fourteen in the millrace behind their father's house, in a village so far from this city that the guild's maps barely bothered with it. He had been sixteen. He had sold — this was the part no one at the guild knew, the part that made the annual reappraisals a quiet obscenity — he had sold the guild his *memory of the drowning itself* at seventeen, desperate for coin to bury her properly, and so he now carried a grief with the middle cut out of it. He knew he had loved her. He knew she had died. He knew there had been a morning, and water, and a sound his mother had made that he could no longer hear no matter how he reached for it. The shape remained. The weight remained. Only the center was gone, hollowed out and sitting somewhere in a vault below his feet, in a jar he could identify by catalog number and refused ever to hold.

*Sentiment is theft from the ledger.* He wondered sometimes whether Veyra knew. She knew everything else. It was her single most irritating quality, and after eleven years he had ranked her qualities carefully and this remained first.

The afternoon passed in columns. Entry 4,409: a stonemason selling the memory of falling from scaffolding, to cure his terror of heights, price negotiable, condition: excellent, the fall had been memorable. Entry 4,411: a young man attempting to sell his grandmother's recipes, declined — inherited memories required the inheritor's proof of consent, and grandmothers, in Ansel's experience, guarded their recipes more fiercely than their souls. Entry 4,413: someone trying to buy back something they'd sold, which was always the saddest column, because the guild's prices ran one direction only, like rivers.

At dusk, Veyra appeared in the doorway the way she appeared everywhere: without arriving, simply being present where she hadn't been. She was small, silver-haired, and moved like a woman who had long ago negotiated a separate arrangement with time.

"The Dane account," she said.

"Filed. Twelve marks monthly. I rounded up from nine."

"I know. I allow it."

"You *allow* it," Ansel said, "or you haven't noticed it."

"Both things are true, which is the guild's whole business model." She came to the window beside him. Outside, the city was lighting its lamps street by street, and beyond the rooftops the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored, and beyond the harbor the ancient beacon-stone on the breakwater caught the last light — the old stone, older than the city, older than the guild, that had burned cold and patient for as long as anyone could remember and warmed for no one.

Veyra was looking at it too, and her face had the particular stillness she got when she was thinking about something she had decided no one else needed to think about.

"Anything unusual today?" she asked. Casual. Too casual, which for Veyra was practically a confession.

"A widow sold a dead man's name for bread. A mason sold his fear of falling. Someone wanted a refund." He capped his ink. "The usual impossible traffic. Why?"

"No reason." She turned from the window. "Lock the gray ledger properly tonight. And Ansel—" she paused at the door, and for half a heartbeat she looked, not like his master, but like a very old woman standing in a draft, "—if anything strange happens in the fishing quarter, you'll tell me at once. Won't you?"

"Fishing quarter," Ansel said. "Nothing happens in the fishing quarter except fish."

"Mm," said Veyra, and was gone.

He finished the day's entries by lamplight. On the last page, in his precise hand, he recorded the day's total — one name, one fear, one grief appraised and refused — and beneath it, because the forms required it, the standard closing line of every guild ledger since its founding:

*All accounts balanced. Nothing owed that cannot be collected. Nothing collected that cannot be accounted for.*

He signed it. He locked the ledgers — black, red, and gray — and was halfway down the stairs when the bell began.

Not the guild's bell. The harbor bell, the one rung only for ships in distress, and it was ringing wrong — ragged, frantic, over and over — and beneath it, thin across the evening water, came shouting. Many voices. The kind of many voices that meant either fire or miracle, and in Ansel's experience the two had recently begun to sound distressingly similar.

From the direction of the fishing quarter, out past the rooftops, the ancient beacon-stone on the breakwater — cold for three hundred years, colder than the guild's oldest ledger — flickered.

Once. Twice.

And went dark.
