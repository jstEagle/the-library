## 2. A Prayer Answered in Ash

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers in a basement that smelled of tallow, old paper, and the particular melancholy of things that had been traded away. Ansel Okonkwo arrived at his desk at the seventh bell every morning, as he had for eleven years, and began with the small debts, because the small debts were where lies lived.

Large transactions were documented to death. A man selling twenty years of his life received three copies of the contract, a witness, a wax seal pressed by a guild officer, and — if Ansel had anything to say about it — a cup of tea while he signed, because no one should make that kind of decision on an empty stomach. But the small trades, the widow trading her husband's name for bread, the fisherman pawning his memory of his mother's face for a season's luck — those were entered in haste and amended in shame, and shame made poor record-keeping.

Ansel was good at finding shame.

He was forty-one, built like a door someone had leaned against a wall and forgotten, with ink-stained fingers and an oath-blade he wore the way other men wore a wedding ring: close, habitual, and mostly forgotten until it mattered. His gift — the swordspeaker's gift, the last one, though the guild preferred the phrase *certified verbal auditor* — let him hear the truth in a contract the way a tuning fork hears its note. When a ledger line lied, it rang wrong. Most days, everything rang right, and Ansel spent his hours in the pleasant, dry labor of arithmetic applied to souls.

"Okonkwo." Master Veyra's voice came down the stairwell ahead of her, the way weather comes before a storm. "You're wanted in the fishing quarter."

He looked up from an entry reading *one (1) summer, mild, lightly used, seller regrets nothing* — which was itself suspicious, because everyone regretted something. "The fishing quarter is Brannick's route."

"Brannick is vomiting into the harbor canal. Something happened this morning." Veyra stood at the foot of the stairs, a small iron-colored woman whose age could not be guessed by any honest method. She did not look alarmed. That was the alarming part. "A child came out of the sea."

"Children come out of the sea all the time. Usually loudly, usually covered in sand."

"This one came out drowned."

Ansel set down his pen with the care of a man setting down something that might otherwise be thrown. "Drowned children do not generally walk."

"No," Veyra agreed. "They don't." And she turned and went back up the stairs before he could ask the second question, which was how like her. Veyra answered first questions only. Second questions she left to fester, on the theory that a clerk who learned to live with festering questions made fewer mistakes.

Ansel collected his coat, his ledger-satchel, his oath-blade, and the third question he wasn't supposed to ask yet, and went out into the gray morning.

* * *

Saltwick, the fishing quarter, occupied the eastern lip of Corvenharbor like a drunk leaning over a railing. Its houses were stacked and crooked, its streets ran downhill toward the water as if even the cobblestones were curious, and it smelled — perpetually, gloriously, terribly — of fish. Ansel had audited Saltwick for six years. He liked it. The people here traded honestly in impossible things: luck, weather-sense, the memory of a good catch, a grandmother's recipe for storms. Small magic, small prices. Nobody in Saltwick had ever sold anything they couldn't afford to lose, which put them ahead of the merchants on the Hill.

This morning nobody was working. Boats sat idle in their moorings. Nets hung drying, forgotten. And the whole quarter had arranged itself along the seawall in the particular silence of people who had seen something and were afraid that talking about it would make it realer.

The child was sitting on the steps of the beacon-stone.

She was perhaps seven years old, wrapped in four blankets that Ansel suspected belonged to four different families, and she was eating a boiled egg with the concentration of the genuinely hungry. Around her stood her mother, gray-faced; the harbormaster, official and useless; and a knot of grandmothers radiating the specific authority of women who had decided the matter required supervision.

Ansel approached the way he approached everything: sideways, with a notebook.

"I'm Ansel Okonkwo," he said to the nearest grandmother. "Guild auditor. I'd like to ask some questions."

"You're the ledger man." She looked him up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman pricing a mackerel. "My sister sold you her wedding memory once. You gave her fair rate and walked her home after."

"I remember."

"She said you cried a little."

"I remember that too," Ansel said, and did not confirm or deny. "The girl. What happened?"

What happened, told by six voices in overlapping disorder, was this: Tam Broadstairs, seven years old, fell from her father's boat in the pre-dawn dark. The water was black and cold and the current off the breakwater runs hard in spring. Her father dove after her and found nothing. They dragged for an hour. Her mother was being held upright by two neighbors when —

"When the beacon-stone flared," said the harbormaster, who had been on duty and needed very much for someone official to hear him say it.

"The beacon-stone hasn't flared in living memory," Ansel said. It was true. The great stone pillar at the harbor mouth predated the city, predated the guild, possibly predated several gods. It was warm to the touch, always, like a sleeping animal, and sailors swore it guided them home in fog when no lamp could. The guild had exactly one entry concerning it, and the entry read: *Not ours. Do not touch.* Ansel had always appreciated the brevity.

"It flared white," the harbormaster said. "Like dawn coming up out of the wrong direction. And then the sea—" He stopped. Started again. "The sea *gave her back*, master auditor. Set her down on the shallows standing up, walking, like the tide changed its mind."

"And she was breathing?"

"She coughed out half the harbor," said one of the blanket-donating grandmothers, "and asked what was for breakfast."

Ansel wrote it all down. He was aware, as he wrote, of the small cold feeling starting behind his sternum, the one that meant a ledger was about to ring wrong somewhere. Miracles were not free. Nothing was free; that was practically the guild's motto, carved over the door in letters so worn that new clerks thought it said NOTHING IS TREE and spent a confused week wondering. Every answered prayer drew on something. The guild knew this better than anyone — half its business existed because desperate people would pay *anything* for an impossible thing, and the guild's whole art lay in making them pay something instead.

But the guild hadn't brokered this. There was no entry. No contract, no price recorded, no clerk's mark. Which meant either the miracle had gone unbilled —

— or somebody else had paid.

He crouched near the child, keeping a respectful distance, and spoke gently. "Tam. My name is Ansel. Can I ask you about the water?"

She looked at him. Her eyes were ordinary brown eyes, a child's eyes, and that was somehow the worst thing he'd seen all day. Whatever had happened to her had happened around her, not to her.

"There was a light under the water," she said. "And it was singing. Not words. Like when you hold a shell to your ear but the shell is asking you something." She frowned, trying to be accurate. "It asked if I wanted to come back. I said yes. Then a big warm hand put me on the beach." She returned to her egg, having delivered the most economically terrifying sentence Ansel had heard in eleven years of auditing impossibilities.

*It asked.* Things that merely happened didn't ask. Things that asked wanted consent, and things that wanted consent wanted cover, because consent converted a theft into a trade. Ansel had written that principle himself, in the margins of a training manual, in a fit of professional pride.

Someone — something — had just made a purchase. And it had used a drowned child as the counterparty.

"Thank you," he said. "You've been extremely helpful. Eat your egg."

* * *

The beacon-stone stood at the harbor mouth on a shelf of black rock, reachable at low tide by a causeway the city had never built because the stone had apparently gotten there first and building next to it seemed rude.

Up close, it was wrong.

Ansel had seen it a thousand times from the seawall, a familiar gray pillar wearing gulls. He had never touched it. Now he climbed the causeway with his boots splashing through tide-pools, satchel banging against his hip, and laid his palm against the stone the way you'd check a fevered brow.

It was cold.

Not cool. Not sea-chilled. Cold the way a coin is cold, the way a locked room is cold. The warmth that every sailor in Corvenharbor had relied on for longer than records existed was simply *gone*, and the absence had a shape, the way a missing tooth has a shape.

"Oh, that's not good," Ansel informed the stone conversationally.

Then he saw the marks.

On the landward face of the pillar, at the height of a kneeling adult, there were scorch patterns — fine, branching, fern-black traceries burned into the ancient surface. Not lightning. Not torch. Ansel had catalogued forty-one varieties of magical residue in his career, from candle-wax hexes to the ash-shadow a dying spell leaves on a wall, and these matched none of them. They looked like prayer-marks. He'd read about prayer-marks in a monograph so obscure the guild library had tried to sell it back to the monastery that donated it. When a prayer was answered — truly answered, drawn up through whatever old vein fed such things — it left a burn where the request had been spoken. The older and hungrier the listener, the deeper the scorch.

These were deep. These were *deep*. Branching lines seared a finger's width into stone that had shrugged off a thousand years of storms, arranged in a pattern that repeated, and repeated, and repeated.

Dozens of prayers. Hundreds.

Ansel knelt and got out his notebook, because he was who he was, and copied the pattern as best he could. Halfway through the second repetition he noticed the structure of it — the branches weren't random. They converged. Every scorched line ran toward a single point at the base of the stone, where a shallow hollow held a cupful of seawater and, floating in it, something small and pale.

He fished it out. A fish-scale, large as a thumbnail, iridescent, and warm — the only warm thing within thirty feet.

"Well," Ansel said to the scale, to the stone, to the cold bright morning. "Somebody's been feeding at the well."

Because that was the old doctrine, the doctrine the guild kept in a sealed cabinet and denied owning: the beacon-stones, the hearth-fires, the standing wells of the world had once been veins of dragon-fire, the first fire, the fire the old gods had been born beside and had starved without ever since men stopped burning offerings. A prayer spoken at a vein might be heard. A prayer answered cost fire. And a god that had gone centuries without a meal, suddenly handed hundreds of answered prayers—

Ansel stood too fast and had to steady himself against the pillar, and the cold of it bit through his glove like a reprimand.

The arithmetic assembled itself in his head with the awful neatness of a balanced column. A child walks out of the sea. A beacon goes cold. Scorch-marks in their dozens, meaning many prayers answered, meaning vast fire spent, meaning the vein here was nearly drained. Someone was praying at the old places. Someone was being answered. And every miracle was drawing down the last reserves of the oldest fire in the world, the fire the guild's own charter — clause nine, subsection three, which Ansel had always assumed was decorative — described as *that which must not gutter.*

Miracles have a price. Fine. Ansel had spent his life pricing miracles; it was honest work. But every price needed a payer, and every payer needed a broker, and there was no entry, no contract, no clerk's mark anywhere in the guild's books.

Which meant the broker was outside the books.

Or inside them, somewhere he hadn't looked yet.

He pocketed the scale — evidence, and besides, it felt rude to leave it floating — and started back across the causeway as the tide began to turn. Behind him, gulls screamed at the cold stone, and the sea, which had given back a little girl for reasons unknown at a price unrecorded, lapped gently at the rocks like a merchant waiting to discuss terms.

In the city behind him, faint on the wind, a bell was ringing. Not the harbor bell. One of the tower bells — the ninth bell, the one that never rang except when—

Ansel stopped mid-causeway, spray soaking his boots.

He couldn't finish the thought. He genuinely, professionally could not finish the thought, because the ninth bell's purpose was one of the sealed entries, filed under *not ours, do not touch*, along with the beacon-stone and eleven other objects the guild pretended not to maintain.

The bell rang again. And again. And Ansel Okonkwo, who trusted arithmetic above all things, did the simplest sum of his career: a bell that rings only for listening gods was ringing on the same morning a drowned child came home.

Somebody, somewhere, already knew.
