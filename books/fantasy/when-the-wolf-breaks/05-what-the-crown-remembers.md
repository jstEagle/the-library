## 5. What the Crown Remembers

The petition to see the king cost Ansel three favors, one forged letter of introduction, and a promise he intended to keep only in the technical sense.

He stood in the antechamber of Aldric's Hall at dawn, dressed in his second-best coat — the gray one with the silver thread at the cuffs that said *clerk of consequence* without saying *clerk who can afford better* — and watched the other petitioners arrange themselves like items in an inventory. A wool merchant rehearsing grievances under his breath. Two sisters from the hill country clutching a deed and each other. A boy, no older than fourteen, holding a helmet too big for him, presumably his dead father's, presumably for the purpose of asking the crown to remember the man had existed.

Ansel knew that particular species of grief. He kept its ledger.

"You're the guild man," said the wool merchant, sidling over. "Guild of Impossible Things. My cousin says you bought her grandmother's last summer. Sixty years of it. Paid fair."

"We paid what it was worth," Ansel said. "Which is rarely the same as what it costs."

The merchant blinked at that, decided it was wisdom, and drifted away satisfied. Ansel let him. It was easier than explaining that he had personally entered that transaction into the books, in his own neat hand, and had not slept well for a week afterward. Sixty years of summers, sold to a buyer whose name the ledger recorded only as *the party of the first breath*. He'd assumed it was a collector. Wealthy eccentrics bought memories the way they bought paintings.

Lately he was no longer sure what they were buying them *for*.

"Petitioners!" The chamberlain's voice cracked across the hall like a whip made of silk. "His Majesty will receive you in order of the list. You will speak when spoken to. You will not approach the dais. You will not look directly upon the crown."

That last instruction was new. Ansel had audited the palace accounts two years ago — a tedious season of candle invoices and kitchen graft — and the old protocol had been simply *you will not touch the crown*. Someone had amended it. Someone had noticed something worth not looking at.

He looked anyway. That was, after all, the job.

* * *

King Aldric sat on the throne beneath the great glass window through which morning light fell in a long gold column, and he was the youngest man Ansel had ever seen wear so old a face.

It was difficult to describe, and Ansel's profession was description — precise, itemized, unlovely description. The king's body was perhaps thirty-five years old: broad through the shoulder, hands resting easy on the arms of the throne, a jaw that had clearly once launched a hundred portraits. But the face above it kept *changing*. Not moving, exactly. Nothing so vulgar as movement. It was more that every time Ansel blinked, the face he reopened his eyes upon was slightly different — younger here, more hollowed there, the eyes going from clear hazel to a colorless pale like water left standing too long in a cup.

He tested it the way he tested everything: methodically. He counted blinks. Between the fourth and fifth, the king aged twenty years and lost them again before Ansel could finish the count. Between the ninth and tenth, the hollow-eyed version lingered long enough to meet Ansel's gaze, and Ansel felt something in his chest go cold and small, the way the harbor beacon-stone had felt when he'd held it in both palms.

Cold the same way. Cold like a hearth from which everything warm has been carried out, log by log, by someone very patient.

"Next," said the chamberlain.

The wool merchant went first, and the king received his grievance about tariffs with a pleasant, practiced warmth. "The crown hears you. The crown will consider." The words came easily, worn smooth as river stones, and Ansel thought: *he has said this ten thousand times, or something wearing him has.*

The sisters with the deed went next. Then the boy with the helmet, whose voice broke on his father's name, and the king leaned forward — and for one unguarded moment his whole face was young and grief-stricken and *human*, and he said, quietly, "I am sorry. I am so sorry," before the chamberlain coughed and the smoothness slid back over him like a lid.

Ansel wrote nothing down. Some things you did not need ink to remember.

Then: "The Guild of Impossible Things. Auditor Okonkwo."

Ansel crossed the floor at the measured pace of a man carrying nothing heavier than arithmetic, bowed precisely the correct depth, and produced his letter of introduction, which named him as an assessor of charitable bequests come to review the crown's endowment of the harbor temples. It was even mostly true. The palace did endow the temples. The temples had recently begun receiving prayers that scorched stone. Ansel wanted to see the account books where those miracles were being paid from.

"Assessor." The king's voice was warm. "You have an honest face. That's rare in a man who handles money."

"Honesty is cheaper than most people think, Majesty. It requires only that you never promise anything pleasant."

A flicker of a smile — genuine, quick, gone. "You'll do. What do you need?"

"Access to the temple ledgers, Majesty. And five minutes of your time, if the crown can spare it."

"The crown," said the king pleasantly, "has nothing but time."

And there — between *crown* and *time* — Ansel saw it happen plainly for the first time, close enough to study. The young face guttered. Like a candle in a draft. For half a heartbeat the thing looking out through the king's eyes was ancient and starving and *vastly patient*, and it looked at Ansel with an expression he recognized from his own ledgers: the look of a creditor reviewing a debtor who does not yet know he owes.

Then Aldric was back, blinking, faintly puzzled, as though he'd lost a sentence mid-thought.

"Majesty? Are you well?"

"Entirely." The smile returned, fixed now. "Five minutes, assessor. Ask your questions."

Ansel asked about temple disbursements. The king answered in fluent generalities. Ansel asked about the beacon-stone, and the temperature of the room dropped by a degree he could not account for, and the king said, "Old stones grow cold. Old kings grow colder. Is that in your ledger too?" — and laughed, and the laugh was wrong, pitched a half-tone below where a laugh should sit, echoing in a chest far larger than the one it came from.

Three minutes remained. Ansel abandoned the pretense entirely.

"Majesty," he said, "may I ask a personal question? Off the ledger."

"You may ask. Whether it gets answered depends on which of us is listening."

The hall went very quiet around them. The chamberlain stirred. The wool merchant stared. Ansel held the king's gaze and did not blink, because he had the sudden, animal certainty that whatever changed during blinks would change permanently if he gave it the chance.

"When you were crowned," Ansel said, "what did you trade?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere high in the vaulting, a bird that had nested in the rafters shifted its weight, and the sound of it was enormous.

And King Aldric — the true one, the buried one, the young man drowning inside his own body — wept. Just two tears, fast and silent, down a face suddenly thirty years old in all the wrong places, and his mouth shaped a word. Ansel read lips the way he read ledgers. The word was *help*. Or possibly *her*. Or possibly a name that began with the same shape as both.

Then the cold came back like a tide, and the ancient thing behind the eyes smiled with the king's mouth, and it spoke — briefly, as promised, in a voice like a ledger closing:

*"Auditor. You balance books. Tell me — what is the entry for a kingdom? One life, debit. Ten million lives, credit. I have carried this account for nine years without error. Do not audit me."*

The voice stopped. The king swayed. The chamberlain stepped forward with practiced discretion, murmuring that His Majesty was tired, that the audience was concluded, that the assessor would find the temple records delivered to his office by evening.

Ansel bowed. Walked backward the required seven steps. Turned at the door and looked back once, because he was constitutionally incapable of not looking back at an unresolved entry.

The king was watching him go. And the look on the young face — the real one, the flickering one — was not the look of a man possessed. Possession fought. This was the look of a man who had signed something, long ago, in a desperate hour, and had spent nine years telling himself the terms were bearable.

Ansel knew that look intimately. He saw it every day across his own desk.

He had simply never seen it on a king.

* * *

Outside, the morning was bright and indifferent. Ansel walked the length of the processional way with his mind running the numbers and refusing to arrive at the sum.

Nine years ago, the kingdom had starved. Blight in the fields, plague in the ports, the old gods silent as furniture. Every schoolchild knew the story: young Aldric had prayed at the Last Hearth, and the gods had woken, and the crops had come green, and the plague-ships had turned away. A miracle king. They still stamped his profile on the coin.

But miracles had a price, and someone was paying it. He'd written that himself, four days ago, standing over a scorched prayer-mark in the fishing quarter. He'd thought the payer was anonymous. Distributed. The way guild debts were spread across guarantors.

It wasn't distributed. It was concentrated. It was sitting on a throne under a glass window, flickering like a bad lamp, aging and unaging by blinks, weeping single tears while the thing that wore him thanked petitioners for their patience.

And the guild — his guild, his ledgers, his neat meticulous hand — had brokered the contract. He was certain of it now. The sealed ledger in the archive, the one Master Veyra wanted burned, written in oath-script older than the guild itself. *The party of the first breath.* The buyer of sixty years of a dead woman's summers. The account that had run nine years without error.

Someone was feeding it. Memory by memory, name by name, prayer by answered prayer — and the dragon-fire that fueled it all was guttering, Sera had said, dimming like the beacon-stone, and when the fire ran out the god would not politely conclude its contract. Gods in arrears did not send polite letters. Gods in arrears collected.

He reached the guildhall steps as the ninth bell rang across the city — Sera's bell, thin and clear and honest — and stopped halfway up, struck by a thought so simple it felt like an ambush.

The bell-keeper heard which gods were listening. She'd told him so with the flat certainty of a woman reporting weather. Which meant she might be able to tell him whether the thing on the throne was listening *now*. Whether it had heard his question. Whether, somewhere in the palace, a vast and patient attention had just begun to turn toward a gray-coated auditor who asked one question too many.

Whether he had, in the language of his own profession, just cosigned a debt he couldn't cover.

Ansel climbed the steps, pushed open the guildhall doors, and found Master Veyra waiting in the entrance hall with the sealed ledger under her arm and an expression he had never seen on her face before.

Fear.

"Auditor Okonkwo," she said quietly. "We need to talk about what you asked the king."

Behind her, through the tall windows, the morning light seemed, very slightly, to be the wrong color.
