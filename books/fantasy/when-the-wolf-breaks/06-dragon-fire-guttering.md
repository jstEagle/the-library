## 6. Dragon-Fire Guttering

Sera Iln had never told anyone about the back way into the Hearth.

Not because it was a secret, exactly — the bell-keepers' order had kept worse secrets for longer — but because telling someone meant trusting them, and Sera had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of not doing that. She trusted bells. Bells rang when you struck them and stopped when you didn't. Bells did not file reports or make promises or look at you with tired, precise eyes while pretending they hadn't already worked out your whole life from the state of your hands.

But Ansel Okonkwo had stood in her bell tower three nights running, mapping prayers on butcher paper by candlelight, and he had never once asked her to explain herself. He just listened — really listened, the way she imagined an auditor listened to a ledger, hearing not the numbers but the story underneath them. And when she'd finally said, "There's something you need to see," he had simply nodded and said, "I assumed there was. You've been re-tying that same bell cord for an hour."

She'd thrown a rag at him. He'd caught it. That was more or less how their friendship worked now.

"Where exactly are we going?" he asked as they descended the spiral stair behind the ninth bell tower, down past the level where the city's water cisterns began, down further than any stair in Vellharrow was supposed to go. His voice echoed strangely, as if the walls were listening and passing it along to each other.

"You'll know when we get there."

"I'm an auditor. I hate arriving somewhere without knowing the destination. It's like reading a ledger backward."

"Then you'll love this." She lifted the lantern higher. "Because I'm about to show you the original entry."

The stair ended at a door of black iron, unmarked except for a handprint worn smooth into its center — a print so old and so large it could only have been made by something that was not a person. Sera pressed her palm into it, felt the familiar shiver of recognition run through the metal, and pushed.

Heat rolled out to meet them. Not fire-heat. Older than that. The heat of a thing that had been warm since before warmth had a name.

Ansel went very still beside her.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

* * *

They came out onto a gallery of black stone, curved like the inside of a cup, and Sera watched his face as he saw it — because everyone's face did the same thing, eventually, no matter how controlled they were. The guild auditor, the man who audited *memory*, looked out over the Last Hearth and forgot every word he knew.

The cavern was vast beyond sense, and most of it was dragon.

She lay coiled in the depths like a mountain that had decided, eons ago, to sit down and think. Her scales were the color of cooling embers — deep red shot through with veins of gold that pulsed, slowly, in a rhythm Sera had learned to read the way other people read weather. Each pulse sent a breath of warmth up through the stone of Vellharrow itself, up through the roots of the beacon-stones and the old altars and every hearth-fire in every house where magic still bothered to live.

The dragon's fire was the source. Not symbolically. Literally. Every answered prayer, every miracle, every small impossible kindness the old gods performed — they all drew from this one sleeping furnace, the way every debt in the city drew, eventually, back to the Guild of Impossible Things.

And the furnace was guttering.

Sera had first noticed it a month ago: the gold veins pulsing a little slower each week, the warmth a little thinner, like a banked fire nobody tended. Now, standing at the rail, she could see whole stretches of scale gone dull and grey, like ash forming on the outside of a log that was burning from within.

"The beacon-stone in the fishing quarter," Ansel said quietly. "It went cold when the child walked out of the sea."

"That's right."

"And every answered prayer since—"

"Takes a little more." Sera set the lantern on the rail. "The gods aren't making anything, Ansel. They never made anything. They're spending. They've been starving for centuries, living off scraps, and now someone's opened the vault and told them to help themselves."

Ansel leaned on the rail. In the dragon-light his face was all gold shadow and careful thought. "How much is left?"

"I don't know. I'm a bell-keeper, not an accountant."

"You're looking at me."

"I'm *hoping* at you," she said. "It's different."

He huffed — almost a laugh — and then the humor drained out of him, the way it always did when the arithmetic started. "If the Hearth goes out," he said, "the gods don't just go hungry. Everything old dies with it. The wards. The blessings. The things that keep the deep roads closed and the rivers honest and—" He stopped. "The child who walked out of the sea. If the fire goes out before whatever brought her back runs its course..."

"She goes back under," Sera said. "Yes. I've done that arithmetic too. It doesn't come out anywhere kind."

Below them, the dragon breathed. The sound filled the cavern like wind filling sails — slow, vast, and underneath it, faintly, almost lost in it, a sound Sera had heard only twice before in her life and never wanted to hear again.

The dragon was snoring. And the snores were uneven. Catching. The breathing of something dreaming badly.

"She's been restless for six days," Sera said. "Keepers used to sing to her, you know. There were orders of us once. Whole choirs. We're what's left." She held up her hands. "Me, and deaf old Brother Casmir, and two novices who mostly sweep."

"What do you sing?"

"Lullabies. Very old ones." She glanced sideways at him. "Before you ask — no, I won't be performing."

"I would never ask."

"You were absolutely going to ask."

"I was going to *eventually* ask, which is different—"

The dragon's eye opened.

* * *

Sera had seen the eye twice before, from a distance, during the rite of the Long Watch. It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of molten copper, and when it turned toward the gallery, the heat of its regard washed over them both like standing too near an open oven.

Ansel did not move. Later, Sera would decide this was courage. At the time, she suspected it was simply that his knees had locked.

"*Little bell-ringer.*" The voice came from everywhere — from the stone, from the air, from inside Sera's own chest, vibrating her ribs like a struck bell. It was a woman's voice and a landslide's voice and neither. "*You bring me a stranger. You bring me a pen.*"

"A what?" Sera managed.

"*A pen.*" The great eye narrowed, and something moved behind it — amusement, maybe, or contempt; with dragons the two lived close together. "*I can smell ink on him. Old ink. Oath-ink. Come forward, little ledger-man. Let me see your hands.*"

"Sera," Ansel murmured, without moving his lips, "what's the protocol here?"

"There is no protocol. Nobody talks to her. She's been asleep for nine hundred years."

"Wonderful. First audience with a god, now a dragon. My week is really coming together."

"*I can hear you,*" said the dragon.

"I know," said Ansel. And — Sera watched him do it with a kind of horrified admiration — he stepped forward to the rail and inclined his head, precisely, formally, the bow of one professional greeting another. "Ansel Okonkwo. Auditor, Guild of Impossible Things. I'm afraid I don't know correct etiquette for—"

"*You signed,*" the dragon said.

The word fell into the cavern and rang there.

Sera felt it go through Ansel like a blade through wet paper. She saw it in the sudden stillness of his shoulders, the way his hands flattened against the rail.

"I sign everything," he said carefully. "It's my job. Debts, contracts, transfers of—"

"*No.*" The gold veins beneath her scales flared, once, hard — and in the flare Sera saw something that made her stomach drop. The dragon wasn't threatening them. The dragon was *afraid*. "*Not little signatures. The signature. Before my sleep. Before the bargain. A swordspeaker stood where you stand, little pen, and wrote his name into a contract between a dying god and a mortal crown, and his name was the lock that sealed it. And your name — your name tastes like his.*"

"That's impossible," Ansel said. "The last swordspeaker died—"

"*Did he?*"

Silence. Somewhere high above, far away in the world, a temple bell rang noon, and the sound drifted down the shaft of the stair like dust.

"The sealed ledger," Sera said quietly. "The one Master Veyra wants burned. It's written in oath-script."

"In *my* oath-script," Ansel said. "My own hand's cadence. I told myself I was imagining it." He looked down at the dragon, and his voice went dry and steady, the voice he used for bad news delivered well. "What was his name? The swordspeaker who signed?"

The eye regarded him for a long moment. The gold veins pulsed. Slower than they had an hour ago. Sera found herself counting without meaning to, the way she counted bell intervals — and hated the number she arrived at.

"*Names are debts,*" the dragon said. "*You of all creatures should know that. His name was spent in the signing. Spent names cannot be spoken, even by me.*" A pause, and then, softer, almost gentle — which was somehow worse — "*But a name can be inherited, little pen. A debt can pass to the next hand that holds the pen. Yours tastes like his. Draw from that what conclusions your profession allows.*"

"I don't want those conclusions," Ansel said.

"*No one ever does. That is why you are all still alive. The ones who want the conclusions are the ones who burn things down.*"

Sera stepped forward before she could stop herself. "Then tell us what to do instead. The fire's failing. Every prayer the god answers takes more of you. If it keeps spending—"

"*It will keep spending.*" The eye slid to her, and Sera felt herself weighed, measured, filed. "*Until nothing remains but the ash of me and a kingdom full of miracles with nothing underneath them. Unless the contract is unbound.*" The vast head shifted, coils moving with the sound of settling continents. "*Can it be?*"

"Yes," Ansel said. Too fast. Too flat.

"*Ah.*" The dragon's nostrils flared, exhaling a wash of hot air that smelled of iron and rain. "*There it is. The price. I can smell prices. What does the unbinding cost, little pen? Who pays?*"

Ansel said nothing.

"Ansel," Sera said.

"It's not relevant yet."

"*He will not say,*" the dragon observed, with terrible mildness, "*because he has already decided to pay it himself. They always decide that. It is the whole tragedy of your kind. You would rather vanish entirely than owe anyone anything.*"

"I prefer 'financially independent,'" Ansel said, and Sera heard the crack in it, thin as ice over deep water.

The dragon laughed. It was not a comforting sound. It was rockfall and avalanche and, buried somewhere in it, genuine sorrow.

"*Go home, little pen. Go home, little bell. Read your forbidden books. Find the terms. And when you come back to me — and you will come back to me —*" the eye was closing, sinking back into dream, the gold dimming even as Sera watched, "*— bring an answer, not an apology. I am very old and very tired and I would like, before the end, to stop being spent.*"

The light sank. The heat gentled. Within moments the great shape below was only a mountain again, breathing its uneven, catching breath, dreaming its bad dreams.

* * *

They didn't speak until they were halfway up the stair, lantern swinging between them, and when Sera finally did speak, it came out sharper than she meant it to.

"'Financially independent.'"

"I was buying time."

"You were lying to a dragon."

"I was negotiating with a dragon. Lying is negotiation with worse lighting." But there was no humor in it, and he knew she'd heard the crack, and after a few steps he stopped walking. The stairwell was narrow and warm and smelled of deep stone, and he stood with one hand on the wall, not looking at her.

"Sera. If the unbinding requires a payment — if the contract specifies a payer—"

"Then we find another payer."

"There isn't one. That's the nature of these things. There's never anyone else in the clause."

"Then we rewrite the clause. You're a guildsman. Renegotiation is your entire religion."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "That," he said, "is either the stupidest plan I've ever heard or the first honest idea anyone's had about this contract in four hundred years."

"Both. Frequently. Now keep climbing, auditor, because the dragon's dying faster than your pride is recovering, and I intend to hold you to that stupid plan."

He climbed. She followed. And behind them, far below, through miles of stone, the Last Hearth pulsed its slowing gold — and Sera, who had spent her whole life learning to hear what bells said between their strikes, heard clearly, for the first time, what the silence underneath the dragon's breathing was saying.

It was saying: *soon.*
