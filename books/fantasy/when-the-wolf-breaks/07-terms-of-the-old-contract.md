## 7. Terms of the Old Contract

The ledger did not want to be read.

I don't mean that it was difficult. I mean it resisted, in the way a cat resists being put in a basket — not with force, exactly, but with a sullen, boneless refusal to cooperate. The vellum was warm to the touch, which no honest ledger should be, and when I opened it to the first page the letters slid away from my eyes like fish in shallow water. I'd look at a word and it would be somewhere else by the time I understood it.

This is a known property of oath-script, of course. We are taught it in our second year at the guild, along with ink-mixing and the proper way to tell a client that no, we cannot sell them their dead mother's recipe for plum wine because someone else bought it in 1311 and has been hoarding it out of spite ever since. Oath-script is written to be read only by those bound to it. The words know who they belong to.

Which raised an uncomfortable question, sitting alone in my office at midnight with a lamp turned low so Master Veyra wouldn't see the light under my door: why did these words *know* me?

I had stolen the ledger from the sealed archive three nights ago. Stolen is perhaps strong. I had relocated it, temporarily, without authorization, in the manner of an auditor who suspects his own institution of fraud and would like to examine the books before someone shreds them. Which is, when you think about it, the single most auditor thing a person can do. Veyra had ordered it burned. I had watched her order it burned with my own ears, and then I had gone back down the ladder into the dark and taken it anyway, and I had told myself this was professional diligence rather than disobedience, and I had almost believed myself, which is the trick all liars learn first.

So there it lay on my desk, older than the guild itself, older than the guild's founding charter, older — if the watermarks were anything to go by — than the city's oldest wall. It had no title. Its cover was dragonhide, cured thin as onion skin, and it smelled faintly of smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a chapel. The clasp was silver worn to softness by centuries of hands.

And every word in it was written in my script. My hand. My particular slanting, tight-fisted oath-script that Master Corvane had spent two years trying to beat out of me and finally gave up on, sighing, "Fine. At least no one will forge you."

Someone had forged me. Someone had forged me four hundred years ago.

* * *

Deciphering took most of the night, and I will spare you the tedium except to note one discovery that made me laugh out loud, alone, at an hour when laughing alone is either a very good sign or a very bad one.

Oath-script, it turns out, has grammar. Not the grammar of language — the grammar of contract. Every clause must name its parties. Every price must be stated twice, once in the voice of the payer and once in the voice of the receiver, so that neither can later claim misunderstanding. And every binding must include, somewhere in its body, a single line called the *loosening clause* — the way out. This is guild law. This has been guild law since the guild was founded, and we have always believed we invented it.

We didn't invent it. We inherited it. From this book.

The loosening clauses were all there, in the old contracts, page after page of them. A farmer binding his fields against blight could unbind them by returning the blessing. A widow — I recognized her entry style, the same careful hand that appears in our modern ledgers for the same kind of grief — had bound her son's fever to a river-stone, and the loosening clause said simply: *break the stone, return the fever, weep at the bank.* Everything had a price and everything had a door.

Except the last contract. Except the one that mattered.

I found it near the end, where the pages grew thinner and the ink grew darker, as though the writer had been pressing harder, or running out of light, or both. There was no heading. No parties named in the usual fashion. Instead, the opening line read:

*Let the following stand outside all law, being witnessed by fire alone.*

Fire alone. Not fire as witness — fire *alone*. As in: no other witness exists or shall exist. As in: whoever reads this is reading something that was never officially seen by anyone, ever, and the fact of your reading it is itself a violation.

I poured more tea. It had gone cold hours ago; I drank it anyway, the way one does when the alternative is getting up and admitting the night has become serious.

* * *

Here is what the contract says. I will set it out plainly, because I am an auditor and plainness is the only prayer I know, and also because my hands were shaking too badly to keep notes in my usual tidy columns and I want the record to exist somewhere steadier than my margins.

Parties. Two of them, unnamed in the ordinary sense but named in the old way, by essence rather than by word. The first party is described as *the hunger beneath the mountain, the god who ate his brothers' altars and was left unfed.* A starving god. That phrase again — Sera had used it, standing in her bell tower with ash on her sleeves: *something starving is answering prayers.* She hears gods the way I hear debts. She was right.

The second party is described as *the man who wears the crown of Aldric's line, being the ninth of that name, whose kingdom drowns.*

Whose kingdom drowns. I sat back at that. The histories say King Aldric IX — the founder, the great one, the face on every coin — reigned through the Long Drowning, when the sea came up the rivers and would not go back down. The histories say he prayed, and the sea receded, and there were feasts for a decade afterward. The histories do not say what the prayer cost, because histories are written by people who have never audited anything in their lives.

Terms. The god would turn the sea. In exchange:

I read the exchange four times. I copied it out in plain script on a fresh sheet, because copying is how I think, and my copy now sits in front of me and I still find it hard to look at directly, the way you find it hard to look at a wound on someone you love.

*The king gives his name. All of it. The name he was given, the name he uses, the name history will speak. He shall reign, and be remembered, and be loved — but the remembering shall belong to the god, who will wear the name as a crown wears a head. The man within shall remain. The man within shall watch.*

That was the bargain. Not his life. Not his soul — the old contracts are very precise about souls, and this one pointedly does not mention one, which frightened me more than any threat would have. His *name*. The god took the name and wore it, and the king stayed inside, alive, aware, enthroned in his own body like a lodger locked in his own house, watching a stranger smile with his mouth for four hundred years.

Every answered prayer since — every beacon lit, every drowned child walking out of the sea, every blind woman seeing her granddaughter's face — has been paid for out of the Last Hearth, yes. But the *authority* to answer, the right to reach into the world and move it, comes from the crown. Comes from the name. The god isn't spending the dragon's fire merely to be generous. It is spending fire to grow fat enough to digest the rest of what it holds. To finish swallowing the man inside.

There was worse. There is always worse; I have never yet audited a contract that got better toward the end.

* * *

The loosening clause. Or rather, the place where the loosening clause should have been.

It exists. Guild law demanded it even then, even in a contract witnessed by fire alone — apparently the old gods kept honest books even while eating each other's altars, which is either comforting or deeply depressing, and I haven't decided which. But the clause was not written in the god's voice or the king's voice. It was written in a third voice, a voice the contract calls *the speaker*, and here is the passage entire, because I have read it so many times it has worn a groove in me, and I might as well wear one in the page:

*Only a swordspeaker may loose this bond, being the one craft sworn to hold both edge and word. The speaker shall speak the unbinding over the crowned head, and the name shall return to the man, and the god shall go hungry into the dark. For this service the speaker shall render payment in kind: as the name returns, the speaker's name shall depart. He shall not die. He shall be unwritten — from memory, from record, from stone and song and the minds of those who loved him. The world shall close over the place where he stood as water closes over a lifted oar.*

I read it. Then I did the arithmetic, because arithmetic is what I do when I cannot bear to feel things.

One name returned. One name destroyed. The contract balances perfectly, which is the mark of truly excellent drafting and the reason I wanted to burn the book more with every page. Somewhere, four hundred years ago, some clerk — some ancestor of mine, some earlier holder of this exact desk and this exact lamp — drew up a contract in which the escape hatch was another human being's entire existence, and they drafted it *cleanly*. No loopholes. No ambiguities. Nothing for a future auditor to exploit. I have spent my career admiring clean work. I now understand that clean work can be monstrous.

And there was one more line. One more, tucked after the payment terms, in smaller script, the way we write the unpleasant footnotes:

*The speaker cannot be compelled. The unbinding must be spoken freely, knowing its price, or it will not take.*

Freely. Knowing. That is the trap inside the mercy. If the guild could simply order a swordspeaker to do it — if Veyra could invoke my oath and march me up the palace steps with the words put in my mouth — then some part of the speaking would be coerced, and coercion voids it, and the god keeps its crown forever. The contract protects itself with my freedom. It needs me to *choose*.

I looked at that line for a long time. Outside, the city murmured in its sleep, and somewhere across the rooftops a temple bell rang the hour, and I thought about Sera hearing it, hearing whatever listens behind it, and I thought: she would tell me not to choose it. She barely knows me and she would tell me not to.

Then I thought about the man inside the crown. Four hundred years watching. Four hundred years of feasts held in his honor, sung by strangers wearing his name, while he sat behind his own eyes unable even to scream. I had seen him flicker, that day in the throne room — young, ancient, young, hollow — and I had pitied him the way you pity a drowning man from a boat, comfortably, at a distance. The distance was gone now. The contract had removed it. He wasn't a king anymore. He was a creditor, and the debt was owed to him, and I am the last officer of the last institution that claims to settle impossible debts.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the shutters. I had done what I always do at the end of a long audit: I wrote the summary. One page, plain script, findings and exposure. I have written hundreds of these. They usually end with a figure — so many crowns misallocated, so many years sold below value — and a recommendation.

My summary ended differently. It ended with a column of two entries, because I couldn't help myself, because the ledger-brain does what it does even when the heart is bleeding on the floor beside it:

*Asset recovered: one name, royal, held four hundred years against the debtor's will.*
*Cost of recovery: one name, common, freely surrendered.*

Balanced. Perfectly balanced. Any examiner would pass it.

I sat there hating how much I admired the draftsmanship.

Then I heard footsteps in the corridor outside my office — unhurried, familiar, the particular tread of a woman who has run this guild for thirty years and knows exactly which lamps burn past midnight and why. Master Veyra stopped at my door. She did not knock. Through the wood, quiet as a confession, she said:

"Ansel. I know the ledger is on your desk. Open the door."

And her voice told me, before I moved at all, that she had not come to have it burned.
