## 8. The Guild Calls In Its Oath

I want it recorded — in whatever ledger survives me, in whatever hand — that when Master Veyra told me to open the door, I considered, briefly and with real professional interest, the window.

Three floors up. A trellis. The possibility of a life as a man who audits nothing.

Then I opened the door, because a guildsman does not flee through windows. We flee through paperwork.

"Master Veyra."

"Ansel." She stepped past me into my office the way weather steps into a room. She was not tall; she had never needed to be. Thirty years of running the Guild of Impossible Things had given her the particular gravity of a woman around whom objects simply behaved. My lamp flame straightened, I swear it. "You've been reading the First Ledger."

"I've been *auditing* the First Ledger," I said. "There's a distinction. It's the only one I have left."

"Mm." She looked at the ledger on my desk without touching it, the way one looks at a sleeping animal one has personally starved for a century and would prefer stayed asleep. "And what did your audit find?"

"You know what it found. You've known since before I was born, probably. A contract between a dying god and a king of Ashfall. Witnessed by a guild clerk whose initials match yours. Sealed under our own charter. Hidden in plain sight in the oldest vault we have, behind a lock only an oath-bound swordspeaker could open — which means you always knew it would be *me* who found it, eventually, because I am the only one left who can."

She sat down across from me. Uninvited. That was Veyra; she had never once asked permission for anything, including her own memories, several of which she had plainly spent somewhere along the way, because there were gaps behind her eyes like missing teeth in a familiar smile.

"Do you know," she said, "what the oath says?"

"I recited it at fourteen. I've kept it for twenty-two years. I could carve it on a grain of rice."

"Recite it anyway. The third clause."

I did not want to. That was the first warning sign, I think — that the words felt different in the mouth now that I knew what they fed on. But she was Master of the guild, and I was Ansel Okonkwo, last of the swordspeakers, and there are debts even silence cannot discharge.

"'The speaker shall hold the guild's secrets above his fortune, above his comfort, above his name,'" I said. "'For the guild's secrets are the world's ballast, and a ship that spills its ballast capsizes.'"

"Above his name," she repeated softly. "You always did have a good memory. Fitting, in our trade."

"Is this where you tell me the contract is ballast?"

"This is where I tell you the truth, since you've gone to the trouble of stealing it." She folded her hands. Outside, the harbor bell rang the ninth hour, and I thought of Sera Iln standing in her tower with her head cocked toward gods, and I found I did not want Veyra knowing I thought of her at all. Veyra missed nothing. It was her most expensive talent. "Sit down, Ansel."

"I am sitting—"

"*Properly.* This will take longer than you'd like."

* * *

What follows is the account she gave me. I set it down exactly as received, though I confess I no longer trust my own transcription, because by the end of the evening I no longer entirely trusted her.

Eighty years ago, Ashfall starved. Not the polite hunger of a bad harvest — the true kind, the kind that comes when the old gods go silent and the land forgets how to be generous. The king then was Aldric's grandfather's grandfather, and he came to the young Guild of Impossible Things with an impossible request: make the god listen again.

"We were six clerks and a rented attic," Veyra said. "We brokered small things. A widow's grief for a merchant's luck. Ten years off a sailor's life for fair winds. You know the inventory; you audit it weekly. We were nobody. And a king walked up our stairs because everyone else had told him no."

"And you said yes."

"We said we'd *look*. There's a difference, and it took us three days and two deaths to learn it." Her voice went flat, the flatness of a woman reading from a ledger she has read so many times the numbers have worn smooth. "The god wasn't silent because it was dead. It was silent because it was starving, and it had learned that answering prayers costs fire, and its fire was nearly spent. The Last Hearth. You've seen it dimming — Sera's bells have been screaming about it for a season. It was dimming eighty years ago too. Everything since has been the long exhale."

"So the contract—"

"The king gave the god his name. His whole self, freely surrendered — the largest collateral anyone has ever posted with this guild, and I include the woman who traded her children's faces. In exchange, the god would bind itself to the crown and answer the kingdom's prayers, drawing fire slowly enough to survive, and the kingdom would survive the famine. Which it did. Both parties performed. The contract has been honored for eighty years."

"And the cost?"

Veyra looked at me with something I was unprepared for, having steeled myself all evening against contempt and threat: she looked at me with pity.

"You read the terms, auditor. Tell me the cost."

I had read them. Three times. I made myself say it aloud, because some things grow teeth if you leave them in your head. "The binding holds until unbound by a swordspeaker's true script. Any unbinding erases the speaker — name, memory, record. Total recall against total erasure. The god keeps the king's name as surety forever unless released." I paused. "There's no exit clause for the speaker."

"No," she agreed. "There isn't. The clerk who drafted it argued for one for six hours. The god's advocate — they send advocates, did you know that? Terrible creatures, all elbows — refused. Said a door that opens both ways isn't a door, it's a wound."

"Who was the clerk?"

"You already know. You matched the initials yourself, or you wouldn't have sent for me with that smug little note about *discrepancies in provenance*." For the first time all night, something moved behind the gaps in her eyes — not guilt exactly. Guilt is a debt you can still imagine paying. This was older. "I witnessed it. I countersigned it. I was twenty-three and I believed, truly believed, that we were saving the world, and I have spent eighty years making sure that belief stays true, because the alternative is admitting what we actually did."

"What did we actually do, Master Veyra?"

"We sold a man to a starving god," she said, "and called it stability."

* * *

The lamp burned lower. Neither of us moved to trim it. In our trade, you learn that light is a resource like any other, and some conversations deserve to happen in half-darkness, where the words can't quite see each other's faces.

"So the beacon going cold," I said. "The drowned boy walking out of the sea. The districts going dark. It's accelerating because—"

"Because the god is spending faster. Yes. The famine ended eighty years ago, Ansel, but prayers don't stop. Every answered prayer draws the Hearth down further, and the god has grown *accustomed* to being answered. Kings do. Gods worse. Aldric—" she stopped, and chose the next word with visible care, "Aldric has less and less say in what gets answered. Some nights there is hardly any Aldric left to consult. The flickering you noticed — you did notice, don't make that face, the whole palace staff pretends not to notice — that's the name wearing thin. He's a coat the god is wearing, and the coat is wearing through."

"Then the solution is simple. Unbind him."

"Yes," she said. "Unbind him. With the only script that works. Spoken by the only swordspeaker alive. Who would then be unwritten from every mind, every record, every ledger line in the world — including ours, including mine, including hers." She let the pronoun sit there between us like a coin on a bar, and I did not pick it up, and she noted that too. "Total recall, Ansel. The god keeps nothing. The world remembers nothing. You understand? Not *dead*. Dead men leave graves, widows, unfinished ledgers. Erasure leaves less than that. Less than the drowned leave."

"There's another option," I said. "We do nothing. The Hearth gutters out entirely. The god starves mid-answer. Half the miracles in progress fail, the king dies or doesn't depending on what the god was holding at the time, and the city learns what the world feels like with no old magic in it at all."

"That is also on the menu," Veyra conceded. "Between those two dishes, the guild's charter instructs me to choose ballast. Stability. The slow ruin over the fast one. Keep the secret, keep the contract, ration the miracles, and pray — quietly, privately, to nothing — that some solution presents itself that doesn't require burning our last sword."

"And if the auditor disagrees?"

Her voice changed. It did not harden — Veyra never hardened, that was the terrible thing about her; she simply became more precise, the way water becomes more precise when it freezes.

"Then the guild invokes the oath. All clauses. Full weight." She laid something on the desk between us: a thin black rod, a hand's length, plain as a coffin nail. I knew what it was. Every guildsman knows. We pass it on initiation day in a velvet box and are told, jokingly, that we hope never to see it again. The Seal of Calling. It compels. Not the body — the gift. Refuse the call, and the speaking-gift is stripped, and an excommunicated swordspeaker is just a man with a very sharp letter opener and a head full of scripts he can no longer pronounce. "I am invoking it, Ansel. Formally. As of this hour, your oath binds you to protect the contract and its contents above fortune, above comfort, above name. You will surrender the First Ledger to the vault. You will speak nothing of what you've found. You will not visit the bell tower."

There it was. She had saved that clause for last, tucked it in among the formalities like a knife in a bread basket, and watched me find it.

"I see," I said.

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "Ansel Okonkwo. I have watched you since you were a boy who alphabetized his grief. I promoted you because you were the best auditor this guild has produced in a century, and I am ordering you to stand down *because of it*, because you are the only one of us clear-eyed enough to see what the arithmetic costs, and I need — the *world* needs — someone who refuses to spend himself cheaply. There may be another way. There may not. But it will not be found by a dead man, and the rite makes you a dead man, do you hear me? Not brave. Not luminous. *Gone.* And everything you love goes with you, including—"

"Don't," I said.

"—including the girl who hears the bells," she finished, gently, mercilessly. "Because I'm old, and I've spent most of my memories, and the ones I have left I intend to spend telling the truth."

I sat with it. Duty, I mean. I have always experienced duty as a physical thing — a coat that fits slightly wrong in the shoulders, worn so long you mistake the fit for your own shape. The oath was in my mouth at fourteen, and I had built twenty-two years on top of it, floor by floor, and here was the architecture's whole secret load-bearing wall: *above his name.* I had recited it a thousand times and heard it perhaps once.

Above his name. They had written the price into the vow itself, eighty years ago, and waited for the reader.

"I need time," I said.

"You have until dawn," Veyra said, rising. "At dawn, either the ledger returns to the vault and the Seal rests, or you refuse, and I strip you in front of the charter-fire, and you walk out of here a free man with a dull tongue and no guild, no protection, and no access — and the god will know within the day that a swordspeaker is loose and unbound, because gods always know. So consider, auditor. Consider carefully. One path keeps you whole and useless. The other—"

"Makes me useful and unmade. Yes. I do the guild's arithmetic in my sleep."

She stopped at the door. For a moment she was not the Master of the Guild of Impossible Things; she was a tired woman of a hundred and three, holding a doorframe the way you hold a railing on a ship.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the clerk who drafted that contract cried for a week after signing it. Then she sealed it, hid it, and got back to work, because that's what the desperate do with unbearable things — they file them." Her hand rested on the black rod a heartbeat longer, then withdrew. "I am filing you now, Ansel. Forgive me. It's the only way I know how to love anything."

The door closed. The lamp guttered, steadied, burned on.

* * *

I sat until the ninth bell's echo had fully died and the tenth began, and I did not decide anything, which is its own kind of decision, the most expensive one.

Instead I did what I do. I opened a fresh page and I audited.

*Asset: one contract, eighty years seasoned, holding a kingdom upright.*
*Liability: one king, wearing thin. One god, spending hot. One Hearth, dimming.*
*Contingent liability: one speaker, oath-bound, whose erasure is pre-written into the instrument he swore to protect.*

I wrote the last line and stared at it. Above his name. They'd put the escape hatch in the oath and locked it from the outside, and Veyra held the key, and the key was a black rod, and the rod was law, and the law was mine — I had spoken it, willingly, at fourteen, to a guild that trades in impossible things, and no trade is more impossible than the one they were asking: keep the secret that is killing a king, or break the self that keeps you.

Somewhere across the dark roofs, the ninth bell tower stood against the stars, and in it, presumably, a keeper with salt-burned hands was mapping which gods were listening, and did not know that by dawn the man who brought her cold tea and bad jokes might be legally erased from his own life.

I looked at the First Ledger. At the Seal. At the window, again, professionally.

Then I picked up my pen, because a guildsman does not flee through windows, and wrote a single line beneath the audit:

*Recommendation pending. Deadline: dawn.*

And beneath that, smaller, in the old oath-script I was not yet forbidden to write:

*Find out whether an oath can be broken honestly — or only paid for.*

Outside, far off toward the Last Hearth, the air shivered once, like a struck bell heard underwater. The dragon turning in its sleep. Or the god, checking its purse.

By dawn, I would know which.

And by dawn — though I did not yet know it, standing there with the Seal of Calling on my desk like a held breath — I would be walking out of the guildhall for the last time, with a forbidden ledger under my arm and a stripped man's silence in my throat, carrying the one document Veyra would slip me with shaking hands: the unbinding rite itself.

But that was dawn's business.

The night's business was simpler, and harder. I blew out the lamp, took my coat, and went up the stairs instead of down — up, past the archive floors, up to the roof where the city spread out below me like an open ledger, every lit window a debt, every prayer a withdrawal, and somewhere in the dark west, the faint red pulse of the Last Hearth, dimming.

Duty is a noose. I had always known that. What I learned on the roof, counting the lights of Ashfall while the tenth bell rang and the dragon breathed its slow fire below, is that a noose only tightens when you pull away from it — and that the rope, in the end, belongs to whoever tied it.

At dawn, I intended to find out if I could untie it.

Or hang beautifully.

The eleventh bell began to ring.
