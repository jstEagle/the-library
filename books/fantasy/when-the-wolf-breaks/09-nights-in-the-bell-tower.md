## 9. Nights in the Bell Tower

Sera Iln had kept the ninth bell for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once thought of it as a good place to fall asleep.

It was a cold place, a ringing place, a place where the floor hummed through your boots at odd hours like a cat that couldn't settle. But tonight the floor was warm — or she was, or they both were, which amounted to the same thing — because Ansel Okonkwo had spread three ledgers and an unrolled chart of the city across her bell platform, and he had been leaning over them so long that his shoulder had ended up against hers without either of them deciding it should.

She noticed. Of course she noticed. She was a bell-keeper; noticing was the whole of her trade. You learned to hear the difference between a rope gone slack and a rope about to snap, between wind and something walking up the stairs. She could have heard his heartbeat if she'd wanted to, probably. She did not want to. That would have been unbearable.

"The harbor district again," Ansel said, tapping the chart with the stub of a pencil he clearly planned to use until nothing remained of it but a memory. "Third night running. The Mother-Below answers there more than anywhere else."

"Fishermen pray loudest," Sera said. "And they've the most to lose. My mother used to say grief makes a better antenna than joy."

"Your mother sounds like she should have worked for the guild." He said it dryly, but there was something underneath it — the careful flatness he got whenever anyone mentioned family. Sera had catalogued that too. She was building quite a catalogue.

"She's dead," Sera said, because she preferred sharp things said plainly to soft things left festering. "Both of them. Fever year, when I was fourteen. I rang the passing bells for half the quarter that winter. After a while you stop hearing them as sad. They're just... true. A bell doesn't lie about what it is."

"No," Ansel said quietly. "That's rather the point of me as well."

He didn't smile when he said it. That was the thing about Ansel Okonkwo — he made jokes the way other men made confessions: without moving his face at all, so you could pretend afterward he hadn't said anything real.

* * *

They had been mapping for six nights now.

The system was hers originally, though she suspected he'd never admit how much of the method came from a bell-keeper rather than a guild auditor. Every time a prayer was answered somewhere in the city — a fever broken, a debt forgiven by some impossible mercy, a dead man's voice heard once in a chimney — the beacon-stones went colder, and the bells felt it. Not heard it. Felt it. Like a draft through a shut window.

So each night Sera climbed her tower, laid her palms on the great ninth bell, and listened to the whole city at once: where the prayers pooled, where the answers fell, which gods were feeding. And Ansel sat beside her with the forbidden ledger open on his knees and wrote down everything she said in his small, merciless handwriting, converting her listening into arithmetic.

"Say it again," he'd say, pencil ready. "Slower. Which direction does the cold come from?"

"West first. Then it pools. Like water finding a cellar."

"And the Mother-Below answers within..."

"Four breaths. Maybe five. The Drowned Father takes nine. The one in the crown—" she always paused there, and he always waited, patient as stone, "—the crowned one answers before you finish asking. It's not even listening properly. It's just *hungry*, and hunger doesn't wait."

Ansel wrote that down too. She saw him write it. *Hunger doesn't wait.*

Tonight the chart was filling in like a map of a sickness. Red marks where gods answered, grey marks where districts had gone dark — magic simply drained out of them, lamps burning duller, charms going quiet, old wives' remedies failing for the first time in generations. The grey patches were growing. The red ones were growing faster.

"It's a ledger either way," Ansel murmured, mostly to himself. "Every credit has a debit. Every answered prayer draws fire from the Hearth. We're just the first fools in history to watch both columns at once."

"Do you ever wish we hadn't started?" Sera asked.

He considered this with the seriousness he gave every question, which she was beginning to suspect was not pedantry but courtesy — the belief that no question deserved less than his full attention.

"I wish a great many things hadn't started," he said finally. "Wishing is cheap. Auditing is expensive. I can only afford one."

* * *

Somewhere past midnight she brought up tea, because the tower had a small iron stove and she had decided — without consulting herself on the matter — that Ansel Okonkwo looked like a man who had forgotten hot drinks existed.

He stared at the cup when she handed it to him as though she'd passed him a live adder.

"What's this?"

"Tea. It's a beverage. People drink it when someone else has decided they should."

"I don't— I didn't ask—"

"No," Sera agreed. "You didn't. That's rather the point of me as well."

Something happened to his face then — a crack in the auditor, brief as a fish jumping — and she realized, with the particular horror of a person who has surprised themselves, that she had made him laugh. Almost. Nearly. The ghost of a laugh, caught and killed before it finished, but she'd heard it start.

"You're insufferable," he said.

"I'm a bell-keeper. Insufferable is in the job description, right under 'must be able to climb.'"

He drank the tea. All of it. She pretended not to watch him do it and failed completely.

* * *

On the seventh night, she fell asleep.

Not gracefully, not prettily — she went from mid-sentence about the Drowned Father's answering-pattern straight into sleep with her head tipping sideways, and woke some unknowable time later with her cheek against wool that turned out to be Ansel's shoulder, and his body held very carefully, precisely still, in the manner of a man who has found himself responsible for something breakable and does not trust himself to breathe.

She should have moved. She had a whole speech prepared about professional boundaries and the dignity of the keepers' order. Instead she lay there another ten seconds — she counted them, she was a keeper, counting was sacred — listening to him not-breathe, before sitting up and saying:

"Your shoulder is a terrible pillow. Bony. No craftsmanship whatsoever."

"My apologies. I'll have a word with whoever built it."

"See that you do."

Neither of them moved away. The chart lay between their feet, red and grey, the city's slow hemorrhage inked in neat columns. Above them the ninth bell hung silent in its cradle of oak and iron, and below them the city prayed itself further and further into debt.

"Sera." His voice was different in the dark. Less armored. "When this is over — if it ends well, which the odds advise against—"

"Don't audit our odds aloud. It's rude."

"—if it ends well," he continued, with the stubbornness she was coming to rely on more than she liked to admit, "what will you do? After."

"After," she repeated. It sounded like a country neither of them had visas for. "I'll keep the ninth bell. What else? It's mine. I've bled on those ropes enough times that it's legally part of my hand now."

"That's not how law works."

"That's how *my* law works."

He almost laughed again. This time she watched him fight it and lose by a fraction — the corner of his mouth, one degree of surrender. In the stove-light he looked younger. Not Aldric-young, flickering, stolen young. Just tired-and-human young. She found the difference unbearably precious.

"And you?" she asked. "After. When the ledgers are balanced."

Ansel was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "I've never been asked that question. Guild auditors don't get an after. We get a next entry." He turned the teacup in his hands. "But lately I find myself... drafting something. Nothing formal. Just — mornings that belong to no institution. A market. Bad bread. Someone arguing with me about the correct way to hang laundry."

"Someone."

"A hypothetical someone."

"Hypothetically, she'd win that argument."

"Hypothetically, she already has."

The silence after that was the warmest room Sera had ever stood in, and it was made entirely of two people refusing to look at each other while meaning everything.

* * *

On the eighth night, the dragon dimmed hard enough that Sera cried out.

She had her hands on the bell when it happened — the great slow pulse beneath the world, the Hearth's fire that she'd felt her whole life like a second heartbeat under her own — and it stuttered. Actually stuttered. Somewhere south, the Last Hearth guttered like a candle in a doorway, and every bell in the city felt its flame lean.

"They took too much tonight," she gasped, when she could speak. "Ansel — the crowned god answered *hundreds*. Whole streets praying at once, miracle-drunk, and it answered every single—"

"How much is left?"

She pressed her palms harder to the bronze, listening past her own fear, past the city's noise, down to that deep banked glow. "Enough. Barely enough. It's like..." she groped for it, hating the image even as it formed, "...like a purse with one coin, being spent over and over by thieves who think it's bottomless."

Ansel wrote nothing. For the first time since she'd known him, his pencil stayed down. He just looked at the chart — at all that red spreading across the grey like fire across paper — and she watched a man do grim arithmetic behind his eyes and arrive at a sum he did not share.

"We need to move faster," was all he said.

"Then say what you're calculating."

"Not yet." He met her eyes, and there it was again, that flatness, that door closing gently. "Soon. I promise you'll hate it before I do."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest. Comfort is above my pay grade."

* * *

The ninth night, she brought two cups of tea without discussing it, and he accepted his without discussing it, and this was apparently how they communicated now — through objects and silences and the steady accumulation of small permissions.

They worked. The Mother-Below was pulling back westward, sated or cautious. The Drowned Father had gone quiet along the whole coast, which frightened Sera more than activity would have — gods that stopped answering were gods that were waiting. And the crowned god answered everywhere, indiscriminately, a blot spreading on the chart that no longer respected district lines.

"It's not choosing anymore," Sera said, her palms on the bronze. "Early on it took the desperate prayers. Now it takes everything. A child asked it for rain yesterday and got rain. A merchant asked for a rival's ruin and got that too. It doesn't weigh anything. It just *spends*."

"Because it isn't spending its own coin," Ansel said softly. "Why would a starving god be careful?"

She looked at him over her shoulder. He was staring at the sealed ledger, and his expression was the one she hated most: the auditor's face, the face that had already read to the last line of a contract and knew exactly what clause was coming.

"Ansel. What does the ledger say? Truly. Not the parts you give me in pieces."

He was quiet for a long moment. Below them the city murmured its thousand prayers, and the ninth bell hung silent, holding its tongue as bells were made to do, keeping what they knew inside the bronze.

"It says," he said finally, "that every contract has an exit clause. And that exit clauses are priced according to what they cost the party seeking them." He closed the ledger with a soft, terrible finality. "This one is priced very high, Sera. Higher than I think you're imagining."

"Try me. I ring a two-thousand-pound bell with my arms alone. My imagination is stronger than it looks."

Something moved through his face then — fast, complicated, gone before she could name it. Grief, maybe. Or the particular pain of a man looking at something he wants badly and pricing it honestly.

"Someday soon I'll tell you all of it," he said. "Every line. And you'll wish I'd kept lying by omission, because omission is at least comfortable." He picked up his tea. "But not tonight. Tonight I want—" He stopped. Started again, more quietly than she'd ever heard him. "Tonight I want to just be a man drinking bad tea in a bell tower with a woman who argues with him about laundry."

"Hypothetical laundry."

"The most hypothetical."

Sera looked at him — this precise, guarded, infuriating man doing grim arithmetic with the end of the world — and understood suddenly that whatever the exit clause cost, he had already decided to pay it, and that he was spending these nights the way misers spend gold: carefully, secretly, hoarding ordinary moments against a poverty he saw coming.

She reached over and took the pencil from behind his ear.

"Hey—"

"One night off," she said. "No charts. No columns. The city can pray unsupervised for six hours; the gods won't notice the gap." She set the pencil down beyond his reach and folded her hands, absurdly formal. "Tell me something useless about yourself, Ansel Okonkwo. Something that would never appear in a ledger."

"That's an invasion of—"

"Yes. Completely. Start with your favorite color."

He blinked at her as though she'd asked him to recite scripture backwards. "...I don't know that I have one."

"Everyone has one. Dig."

"This is the strangest audit I've ever undergone."

"Answer the question, auditor."

A pause. Then, slowly, like a man defusing himself: "Green. The green of the first leaves after winter. My grandmother had a coat that color and she wore it until it fell apart and then wore the pieces."

"There. Was that so hard?"

"It was excruciating."

"Good. Favorite food."

"Sera."

"*Favorite food.*"

"...Bread. Bad bread, specifically. Good bread is a transaction. Bad bread you eat out of loyalty, and loyalty tastes better."

She laughed — really laughed, head back, the sound bouncing around the bronze throat of the ninth bell until the bell itself seemed to hold the note. And when she looked back at him, Ansel Okonkwo was watching her with an expression stripped of every defense she'd catalogued, and the look on his face was the most frightening and wonderful thing she had seen in eleven years of keeping watch.

"What?" she managed.

"Nothing," he said. "Recording it. Internally. Under assets."

"You can't put a laugh in a ledger."

"Watch me."

Below them the city prayed on, and the dragon's fire burned low, and somewhere in the dark the crowned god spent and spent and spent. But up in the bell tower there was tea going cold and a chart nobody looked at and a woman laughing and a man memorizing the sound against future poverty — warmth stolen, plain and deliberate, from a collapsing world.

It was nearly dawn when the bells began.

Not her bell. Not any bell rung by human hands. From the direction of the Temple Quarter, faint and wrong, came a peal with no keepers behind it — bronze voices swinging in empty towers, ringing a pattern Sera had never heard and knew instantly, in her marrow, was a summons.

Ansel was on his feet before she finished standing. "That's not a call to worship."

"No," Sera whispered, her hands rising toward her own silent bell as though to shield it. "It's a call to *come*."

Far south, beneath the earth, she felt the dragon's fire gutter — one long, shuddering dip, like a heart missing a beat.

And the bells of the empty towers rang on, and on, calling the faithful to something, and the something was not prayer.
