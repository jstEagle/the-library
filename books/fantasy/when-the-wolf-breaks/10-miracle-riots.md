## 10. Miracle Riots

*Ansel*

The trouble with miracles, Ansel had long maintained, was their bookkeeping.

Every answered prayer drew fire from the Last Hearth — that much he and Sera had mapped together across three weeks of stolen nights in her bell tower, charting gods the way a customs officer charts smuggling routes. And every district drained of fire went *dark*, in the old magical sense: no wards holding, no charms warming, no blessings keeping rot out of wells or fever out of cradles. The ledger balanced. It always balanced. That was the horror of it.

What the ledger did not account for was what people did when they learned the arithmetic.

He heard it before he saw it. Ansel was crossing the Weavers' Row shortly after dawn, carrying bread and a report on the Ninth Ward's failing wards, when the bells began — not Sera's measured nine, but every bell in the city at once, ragged and frantic, like a room full of children all shouting the same word.

Then the crowd came around the corner.

They were carrying a man on a door. Ansel recognized the posture immediately: dead weight, arms flopping with each jostling step. Except the man's chest rose and fell, and his eyes were open, and he was weeping into his hands while four hundred strangers screamed *he came back, he came back from the river, three days under and he came back.*

"Excuse me," Ansel said, to no effect whatsoever. He was a lean, unremarkable man in guild gray; crowds parted for relics, not auditors. He put his back against a shuttered stall and watched the procession pass with the particular expression of someone watching a debt come due early.

The resurrected man caught sight of Ansel — or perhaps of the oath-blade hilt showing beneath his coat — and reached out a dripping hand.

"You're one of them," the man rasped. "One of the keepers. Tell them I don't want it back. Tell them whatever took my death can *keep it.*"

"I'm not clergy," Ansel said. "I'm an accountant."

The man laughed, wet and wild, and was carried on.

By noon, half of Lowmarket had gone dark. Ansel walked its length and felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth: wards that had hummed along the gutters since his grandfather's day now silent, the charm-lamps cold, even the guild's own courier-sigils refusing to light. In their place, temples overflowed. People who had never prayed in their lives knelt in doorways whispering to the crowned god — the god wearing Aldric's face on a thousand hastily painted icons — asking for their mothers back, their sons, their lungs, their luck.

And getting it. That was the terrible thing. Getting it.

Ansel stood in the shadow of the Lantern Temple and watched a woman hand over a small clay token — a memory pressed to clay, an old guild practice, though these tokens weren't guild-made. They were crude, desperate things, and something in the air above them *drank*. Each prayer answered, another street went cold. The god wasn't rationing. It was feasting.

"Well," Ansel murmured, "this will audit poorly."

That was when he saw the second mob.

* * *

*Sera*

Sera heard the riot coming as a wrongness in the sound of the city.

She had been deaf to the ninth bell for exactly six minutes — not truly deaf, thank every listening god and several who'd stopped, but muffled, as though wool had been packed into the morning. Then the wool tore away and what rushed in was screaming. Not fear-screaming. *Joy*-screaming, which was worse, because joy in crowds always curdled, and she could hear the curdle beginning under it, a low animal note like a kettle about to go.

She should have stayed in the tower. Veyra's instructions — relayed through a trembling junior clerk — had been explicit: keepers off the streets, towers barred, the bells rung only at appointed hours so the faithful wouldn't read meaning into silence.

But the ninth bell was hers, and somewhere under the roar she could hear it calling wrong — a cracked overtone, a hair flat — and a bell-keeper does not ignore a bell in pain.

So she went down into it, and regretted it within a hundred steps.

The Lantern Temple square was a sea of bodies, all facing the icon of the crowned king — Aldric's stern face rendered in gilt and cheap paint, twenty feet tall on a scaffold. Incense smoke lay over everything like weather. At the scaffold's base, priests in ash-gray robes held up a child, a girl, bandaged and blinking, who two days ago had been burned past saving in a dye-works fire. The crowd sang her name. The crowd sang *his* name, the god's, the crown's. Somewhere a fiddle played. Someone had brought a goat.

Sera pressed toward the tower steps on the square's far side, using elbows and the low profile the gods had given her. She was halfway there when the singing changed key.

It happened fast, the way crowds do. A woman near the fountain — Sera marked her later, a fishwife with strong shoulders and an empty net — shouted something Sera couldn't catch, but the shape of it was unmistakable: *if the god answers prayers, why do our streets go dark? Why is the north quarter black? Who pays?* And a priest turned on her, and someone shoved, and the goat bolted, and the whole square went from hymn to hammering in the space of three heartbeats.

Except the fury didn't scatter. It *focused*. Because a priest — one of the ash-robed ones, his voice suddenly carrying with that greased, resonant quality that meant borrowed power — pointed across the square, directly at Sera.

"There," he cried. "A keeper of the dead bells! They hoard the hearing! They ring for the old gods while the crowned lord gives us back our dead!"

"That's not — " Sera began, to a crowd of two thousand, which is to say she addressed the air.

The front rank began to move toward her. Not running — walking, which was far more frightening, hands already reaching, faces lit with the particular righteousness of people about to do something unforgivable for a very good reason. She backed against the tower door and found it barred from inside, because of course it was, because she'd followed instructions for once in her life.

The bell overhead rang once, unpulled, a single cracked note of alarm.

Sera thought, absurdly, of Ansel's face when she'd told him the tower cat was named Ledger. She thought: *I never got to tell him — *

And then the crowd's front line folded inward like a struck tent, and there was Ansel Okonkwo in the middle of it, coat torn, breathing hard, looking profoundly annoyed.

* * *

*Ansel*

Getting through a mob, Ansel had discovered, was mostly a matter of paperwork energy. He planted himself in the press, held up the guild seal, and announced in his flattest auditor's voice: "Guild inspection! Who authorized this assembly? Permits, please!" Nothing parts a devout crowd like the suggestion of a form they haven't filled out. He gained forty feet on sheer bureaucratic menace alone.

Then he saw the priest pointing at Sera, and stopped being funny.

He went through the rest of the crowd the less civilized way — shoulder, elbow, an oath-blade flat across his forearm not as threat but as *wedge* — and arrived at her side just as the first hands reached the tower door.

"Keeper of the dead bells," Ansel said, falling in beside her, back to the wood. "You really must stop making friends."

"They're going to kill me," Sera said. Her voice was steady, which he noted with distant admiration, because her hands were not.

"Yes. I'd hoped to reach you before you noticed." He surveyed the crowd. It had paused — the priest was still working them, arms wide, and the pause would last maybe ten breaths. "Options. One: the roof. Two: the crypts under the tower, if the lower door isn't also — "

"It's barred."

"Wonderful. Three." He pulled the guild seal from his coat and, with a small internal apology to Master Veyra and every regulation he'd ever loved, snapped it in half. Old guild-craft flared — a debt-token burning itself out, spending whatever small authority remained in it — and the tower door shuddered open behind them. "I've just committed fraud. Run."

They ran.

Up the spiral, the crowd pouring in below them, Sera two steps ahead because she knew the stair and he didn't, her braid whipping around corners. Behind them the ash-robed priest's voice rose, furious, and the tower shook as bodies hit the door at the base. Above them the great ninth bell swung in its frame without a rope touching it, ringing and ringing, and Ansel realized Sera was laughing.

"What," he panted, "is funny."

"He's ringing himself," she gasped. "The bell. When a keeper's frightened — or — " she glanced back at him, and something in her face made him forget the next word he was going to say — "or feeling things, the bell knows. It rings for me. It hasn't done that since I was an apprentice."

The bell tolled on, huge and cracked and glad, drowning out the mob, and Ansel thought: *of course it does. Everything about her refuses to stay quiet.*

They burst onto the bell platform, wind and noise and the city spread out below — the bright, praying center, and beyond it the dark quarters spreading like ink in water. Below, the mob boiled into the stairwell. They had minutes.

"Sera." He said it louder than he meant to.

She turned. Wind took her hair sideways. The bell crashed overhead.

"When I saw him point at you," Ansel said, and then stopped, because he had prepared approximately none of this. He was a man who kept ledgers. He believed in recording things accurately, and he had no column for this.

"When you saw him point at me," Sera prompted, and there was a smile starting at the corner of her mouth that had no business existing on a rooftop above a lynch mob.

"I crossed a square of four thousand people," Ansel said, "and broke a guild seal worth more than my salary, and I want it noted that I did it without thinking. No arithmetic at all. Which for me is — " he gestured vaguely at the collapsing world — "*unprecedented*."

The bell rang. The mob thundered below. Sera looked at him for a long moment, wind-blown and soot-streaked and alive.

"Say it properly, auditor," she said softly. "I deal in clear signals for a living."

"I love you," Ansel said. It came out steady, which surprised him; apparently some entries write themselves. "Mid-crisis, admittedly, and with poor timing, but I find the timing is never going to improve, so — I love you. I have since the night you explained bells to me and called my profession 'organized suspicion.' I wanted it witnessed."

"The bell witnesses," Sera said. Her voice cracked exactly once. "And I do too. Obviously. Since roughly the widow with the bread. You gave her your own ration and then lied about it badly." She stepped closer, into the wind, into him. "You're a terrible liar and the best man I know, and if we survive today I intend to say all of this again somewhere quiet with chairs."

"Chairs," Ansel agreed, "would be luxurious."

Below them, the tower door gave way with a boom.

* * *

*Sera*

Later — much later, when the city had gone quiet in the wrong way — Sera would try to reconstruct how they survived the tower, and fail. Memory kept returning fragments instead of sequence: Ansel cutting the counterweight rope so the great bell dropped a foot and rang a note so vast the mob staggered back covering their ears; the hidden keeper's ladder down the tower's north face that she'd known about since apprenticeship and never once needed until now; the two of them dropping into the tanner's yard behind the row, landing hard, laughing in the shocked way people laugh when death has just brushed past and missed.

What she remembered cleanly was the walk afterward.

They came out into the Weavers' Row as the light went strange — the sun dimming not with cloud but with *absence*, the wards of the western quarter guttering out one by one like snuffed candles, each extinguishment a prayer answered somewhere else. Whole streets going magically dark while other streets glowed with returned sons and healed lungs and resurrection. The city splitting in two: those the god fed, and those it fed upon.

People stood in their doorways watching the darkness come, and nobody prayed anymore. That was the change. Even the devout had gone silent, doing sums they didn't want to finish.

Ansel stopped in the middle of the row, staring west, and Sera watched the auditor surface over the lover — not replacing him, she understood suddenly, but *joining* him, the two halves of him finally in the same room.

"It's accelerating," he said quietly. "Veyra said the god was sleeping, being fed slowly. This isn't slow. Something woke it up." He looked at her. "Or someone stopped feeding it the way it expects to be fed."

"Veyra," Sera said. "The master clerk who's been 'managing' the contract for thirty years and won't tell you what management means."

"She spent her memories on something, Sera. I saw the gaps in her. You don't get gaps like that from reading old files." He rubbed his eyes, and for a moment he looked very tired and very human, and she put her hand in his because some things outrank dignity.

"So what do we do?" she asked. "Tonight. Practically."

"Tonight we go back to the sealed ledger," Ansel said. "Because I think the answer to all of this has been sitting in my archive since before the guild existed, and I think Master Veyra has known it longer than I've been alive." He squeezed her hand. "And tomorrow, I suspect, we stop auditing and start breaking things."

Above them, across the darkening city, the surviving bells began to ring — not alarms now, but the old evening pattern, keeper after keeper sounding the hours because the hours still mattered, damn it all. The ninth bell's voice rose over the rest, cracked and stubborn.

Sera listened to it the way she listened to everything: closely.

"It sounds hopeful," she said. "Which worries me. Bells are honest."

"So am I," said Ansel. "Ask anyone whose taxes I've audited."

They walked east together, toward the guild hall and the ledger that shouldn't exist, while behind them the western quarter went fully, silently dark — and in that darkness, faint as a dying coal, something ancient and hungry turned its attention toward the Last Hearth, where an old dragon slept beside a fire that was almost out.
