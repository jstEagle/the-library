## 11. Veyra's Confession

The summons came by courier at half past the ninth bell — a boy of perhaps twelve, breathless, holding out a slip of guild paper as though it might bite him.

"Master Veyra says come now," he panted. "She said—" he squinted at the paper, clearly having memorized and immediately garbled the message— "she said tell him to bring his own ink."

Ansel looked at the note. It read, in Veyra's cramped, furious hand: *Bring your own ink. I have none left.*

He had been an auditor long enough to know when a sentence meant more than it said. Guild clerks were never short of ink. Ink was the one commodity the Guild of Impossible Things had never once run out of; there were vaults of it, older than most kingdoms, ink pressed from things he preferred not to think about before breakfast.

*Bring your own ink.*

Well. He brought three bottles, a fresh nib, and, after a moment's consideration, the sealed ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath his coat. If this was a trap, he intended to be trapped thoroughly documented.

* * *

Veyra's office sat at the top of the guildhouse, a room Ansel had entered perhaps six times in eleven years of service. It was smaller than he'd imagined. He had always pictured something vast and terrible, all dark wood and hanging seals, the lair of the woman who could unmake careers with a marginal note. Instead it was cramped and oddly shabby: a desk scarred by centuries of nib-scratches, shelves sagging under ledgers, a cold teapot, and one window looking out over the smoke-stained rooftops toward the harbor.

And on every wall — every wall — small brass plaques, each engraved with a name and a date. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

Ansel counted without meaning to. It was an occupational disease. Four hundred and twelve.

"Memorials?" he asked.

"Clerks who died in service." Veyra stood with her back to the window, silhouetted against the grey afternoon. She was not a tall woman. She was not even, honestly, an imposing one — a compact figure in a clerk's plain grey, silver hair pinned with what appeared to be a bent quill, her hands folded in front of her like a schoolmistress waiting for an apology. "Some of them very young. Some of them very old. One of them, if you can believe it, died of a paper cut inflicted by a contract that did not wish to be signed." Her mouth twitched. "We do not speak of him at the winter feast. It upsets the newer staff."

"You sent for me."

"I sent for you." She gestured to the chair across her desk. It was the only other chair in the room. "Sit, Okonkwo. And pour the tea, since I evidently forgot to make any. That used to be my one domestic talent. I find I've lost it."

There was no tea in the pot. There was nothing in the pot but a faint mineral smell, like water that had been boiled away years ago and never replaced. Ansel set it down carefully.

"When did you last eat a proper meal?" he asked.

"What a strange question for an auditor to ask."

"It's a standard question. Asset depletion. You look—" he searched for the professional term, "—depleted."

Veyra laughed. It was a dry sound, like a ledger opening for the first time in decades. Then she sat down across from him, and folded her hands again, and the laughter went out of her face so completely that Ansel felt the temperature of the room drop.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said. "You will want your ink. Take notes. I know you're already dying to."

He uncapped a bottle. She watched him do it with something that might, in another woman, another life, have been fondness.

"Two hundred and six years ago," she began, "there was no Guild of Impossible Things. There was a counting-house. A small one, in the old fish market, run by a family named Veyra. We kept accounts for merchants. Ordinary accounts. Grain, salt, silk, sins — the usual trade goods of a port city."

"Sins?"

"This was a more honest century. Everything was for sale; we simply wrote it down." She turned one of the brass plaques absently, so its name faced the wall. "My grandmother held the keys. My mother kept the books. And I was the junior clerk, sixteen years old, with good handwriting and a bad attitude, assigned to witness contracts because I asked fewer questions than anyone else in the building."

"And then?"

"And then a king came to us in the night, with a starving god at his shoulder, and asked us to broker the end of the world."

She said it flatly. No drama in it. That was somehow worse.

"The kingdom was dying. Plague in the lowlands, war on the borders, the harvest rotted twice running. Aldric — the first Aldric, mind you, our king is the fourth or fifth echo of him — Aldric was twenty-three years old and drowning in a crown too heavy for any human neck. And something found him. Something old and hungry, older than the hearth-dragon, older than the bells. It offered him a bargain: the kingdom would live. The people would live. The plague would lift, the wars would end, the harvests would come fat and golden. In exchange—"

"In exchange for his name," Ansel said quietly.

"For everything he was." Veyra's hands tightened in her lap. "Not death. Death would have been mercy, and gods are not merciful, they are *thorough*. The god would wear him. Live through him. Rule through him. And the world outside the palace walls would bloom while the man inside them emptied, year by year, decade by decade, like a well being drunk dry."

"You witnessed it."

"I wrote it down." For the first time, her voice cracked — just slightly, just enough. "That was my job. Sixteen years old, bad attitude, good handwriting. They needed a neutral party. A counting-house clerk. Someone whose whole profession was recording debts exactly as they were incurred, without judgment, without mercy, without — without *stopping to ask whether the debt should exist at all*." She looked up at him. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Okonkwo? I didn't fail to prevent it. I *notarized* it. I dipped my pen and I made the ending of a king legal."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past the guildhouse, ordinary as bread.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Ansel asked. "Eleven years I've served this guild. You've lied to me by omission for eleven years. Why confess tonight?"

"Because I've run out of time to lie well." She smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "And because you found the ledger. Don't look surprised — I knew within the hour. I've known where that book was hidden for two hundred years. I hid it myself. I've been waiting for someone to find it, and dreading it, in roughly equal measure, since before your grandmother was born."

* * *

Ansel wrote as she talked. It steadied him. Dates, names, figures — the architecture of horror rendered in tidy columns. This was how he survived the world: reduce it to entries. A debt recorded is a debt contained.

But some entries refused containment.

"After the binding," Veyra said, "the counting-house changed. The god required stewards — someone to manage the arrangement, keep the terms, ensure no clever swordspeaker ever came along and unpicked it. So the counting-house became a guild, and the guild took a new charter, and we began trading in impossible things because impossible things were suddenly the only currency that mattered. Memory. Years. Names." She tapped the desk. "Where do you imagine those commodities *went*, Okonkwo? All those widows trading their dead husbands' names for bread. All those men selling ten years of their lives for a debt forgiven. You audit the inflow. Have you never wondered about the outflow?"

His pen stopped.

"No," he said slowly. "I audit what comes in. Disposal is the Master's office."

"Yes. It is." She spread her hands. "It has been mine for one hundred and ninety-four years."

The arithmetic arrived in his chest before it arrived in his head. "Clerks die in service," he said, looking at the walls. At four hundred and twelve small brass names.

"Clerks *retire* in service," Veyra corrected softly. "Mostly. The guild's greatest secret isn't the contract. Anyone could blackmail us over a contract; blackmail implies we'd care. The secret is what the guild is *for*. We are not brokers, Okonkwo. We are wardens. Every memory sold to us, every name surrendered, every year traded away — we hold them. Not because we profit. Because they feed something, and it is better that they feed it *slowly*."

"The god," Ansel said. His mouth had gone dry. "The prayers. The miracles in the city. People praying and getting answers—"

"—and each answer costing fire from the Last Hearth, yes, I know, the beacon going cold, the drowned child walking out of the sea, all of it, I have been watching it happen with increasing horror for three months." She leaned forward. "But you have it backwards. The god isn't waking because people pray. People are praying because the god is waking — its hunger leaks into the world like water through a cracked dam, and the desperate feel it and kneel. And every answered prayer makes the crack wider." She closed her eyes briefly. "For two hundred years, the arrangement held because the god slept, and it slept because it was fed on schedule. Measured portions. Managed intake. Memories, mostly. Years. Occasionally a name — always volunteers, always the dying, who had little use for theirs."

"You fed it the guild's inventory."

"I fed it *myself*, mostly." She said it the way she might report a discrepancy in a grain shipment. "The guild's stores were needed for trade — we couldn't starve the business. But a Master may dispose of personal assets as she sees fit. And I had rather a lot of personal assets. Two hundred years of memories, Okonkwo. Do you understand what that means? I remember binding a god. I remember my mother's face. I remember—" she frowned, genuinely searching, "—I remember there was a garden. I am nearly certain there was a garden. There was someone in it. I believe I loved him. I cannot tell you his name, because I paid it to the thing in the palace forty years ago, along with the memory of our wedding, to buy the city one quiet decade."

Ansel set down his pen. His hand was not entirely steady, and he despised that.

"You've been spending yourself," he said. "All this time. Keeping it asleep with your own life."

"Somebody had to. The alternative was letting it wake and eat freely." She shrugged, a small, tired motion. "I was the junior clerk. I asked fewer questions than anyone else in the building. It turns out that is a poor qualification for moral courage and an excellent one for endurance."

* * *

He should have felt triumphant. He had spent weeks pulling threads — the cold beacon, the sealed ledger, the flickering king — certain that at the center of the knot he'd find a villain. A mastermind. Someone to hate cleanly, the way ledgers taught you to hate: red ink, double entry, case closed.

Instead he found a woman with no tea in her pot and no garden in her memory, who had paid out her entire self, installment by installment, to keep a hungry god dozing, and who was now — he saw it plainly, the way he saw a falsified column — nearly empty.

"How much is left?" he asked gently.

Veyra considered lying. He watched her decide against it; it took her about a second, and he thought: *that second cost her something too.*

"Perhaps four years," she said. "Of memories. Perhaps five. I gave the last of my childhood last winter — I recall it was winter because I remember being cold and thinking, *how odd, I don't remember learning what cold was.*" She smiled at his expression. "Don't make that face. It doesn't hurt. That's the obscene part. It doesn't hurt at all. You simply wake up lighter, and the world has holes in it, and eventually you stop noticing the holes because — well. Because noticing requires remembering that there was anything to notice."

"The guild knows?"

"The guild knows its Master is frugal. Ascetic, even. I've cultivated a reputation for disliking feasts." Her eyes glinted. "In truth I stopped attending because I couldn't remember why I was expected to enjoy them, and pretending seemed dishonest, and I have been dishonest enough for one lifetime."

Ansel looked down at his notes. Columns of dates. Two hundred years of managed starvation, recorded in his neat auditor's hand, and somewhere among them a garden he could not give back to her.

"Veyra," he said. "Why keep the ledger? All this time. You could have burned it. The god would surely have preferred it burned. Burning it was an order once — you gave it yourself, three weeks ago, when I reported finding it."

"Did I?" She raised an eyebrow. "How careless of me. Did I specify *burn it and bring me the ashes*? Did I stand over the brazier? Or did I shout an order at a subordinate known throughout the guild for his inconvenient habit of doing precisely what the evidence demands rather than what his superiors request?"

He stared at her.

"I hid that book in 1741," she said, "in a place only a swordspeaker's oath-script could open — which is why it's written in oath-script, yes, I learned it, it took me thirty years, I had the *time* — because I wanted it found. Eventually. By the right person. When the right person existed." She looked at him steadily. "A swordspeaker who audits. Who reads the fine print. Who keeps a widow's dignity intact while processing her grief, and notices when a king flickers, and asks about asset depletion instead of running for the hills. Do you know how long I waited for a clerk like you? Longer than your nation has existed, boy. I have been *bored*, and *guilty*, and *running out of myself*, and waiting."

"The unbinding," Ansel said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "The ledger says it requires a speaker. The cost is—"

"I know what it costs. I drafted the clause." She said it lightly, but her folded hands had gone white at the knuckles. "Believe me, I have had two centuries to regret the elegance of that particular term. The god insisted. A name for a name — it would accept no weaker collateral. And I, sixteen years old, bad attitude, good handwriting, wrote it into the record because that was my job." She exhaled slowly. "So. Now you know everything. My crime, my penance, my arithmetic. Ask your questions, Okonkwo. I'll answer honestly. It's a novel experience. I recommend it."

Ansel sat back. Outside the window, the harbor light was failing; somewhere across the rooftops, the ninth bell rang the hour, thin and slightly off-key, the way it had rung since the beacon went cold.

One question rose above the others, and it wasn't about the god, or the contract, or the clauses.

"The garden," he said. "The person in it. Is there anything I can do? Any way to — recover what you paid? Trade something back? I have savings. I have memories I could spare. There must be terms—"

For a moment — just a moment — Veyra's face did something complicated and young, and Ansel understood that he had just seen the sixteen-year-old junior clerk surface through two hundred years of careful accounting.

"That," she said finally, and her voice was rough, "is the single stupidest, kindest offer anyone has made me since the reign of Aldric the First." She cleared her throat, briskly, and was the Master again. "No. There are no terms. What's paid to that thing stays paid — that's the entire point of it, that's why the contract holds. And I wouldn't take your memories if you begged me, Okonkwo. I have seen what people become when they sell themselves piecemeal. I see it every morning in the mirror." She unfolded her hands at last and laid them flat on the desk, palms down, a woman bracing. "Which brings us to the real reason I summoned you. Not the confession. The confession is merely context."

"Context," Ansel repeated. "Of course. Two hundred years of self-destruction. Context."

"There is a vote tomorrow," Veyra said. "The full conclave of masters. The god is waking faster than I can starve myself to slow it — my portions barely register anymore, I'm pouring sand into a flood. The conclave knows it. And there are those among them who have concluded that if the god cannot be kept asleep, it should be *fed properly*. Openly. A city of faithful worshippers, prayer as sacrament, the miracles welcomed instead of mourned. They call it accommodation." Her lip curled. "I call it setting the table for the thing wearing our king."

"They'd feed it until there's nothing left of Aldric at all."

"They'd feed it until there's nothing left of *anyone* at all. Kings first. Auditors soon after." She met his eyes. "Tomorrow I intend to speak against accommodation, and reveal what I've just told you, and be removed from office for it — treason against the guild's charter, which is ironic, given that I wrote the charter. Which means that as of tomorrow evening, Okonkwo, the guild will have no warden, the god will have no keeper, and the only person in this city who knows the unbinding rite and still remembers why it matters will be—"

She stopped.

Ansel felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath his chair.

"You know the rite," he said.

"Of course I know the rite. I *wrote* it. Drafted it myself, in the margins of the contract, because even at sixteen I was hedging my bets." Veyra reached beneath a loose floorboard beside her desk and drew out a slim folio, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, and set it on the desk between them without releasing it. Her hand stayed flat upon the cover.

"But understand what you'd be asking for," she said quietly. "This isn't a document. It's a bill. And the payment is due in full, on signature, from the signer alone." Her eyes — grey, tired, and suddenly very clear — held his. "Before I put this in your hands, boy, I need to know: what exactly are you willing to lose?"

Below them, the ninth bell rang again — though Ansel would swear afterward that he had heard it ring the hour only moments before, and that between the two ringings, the room had gone utterly, listening-quiet, as if something far beneath the city had turned over in its sleep.
