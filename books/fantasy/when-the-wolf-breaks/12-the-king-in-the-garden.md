## 12. The King in the Garden

The palace gardener was the only honest man in Aldric's court, and he proved it by lying to me immediately.

"No one goes into the king's garden," he said, standing in front of a gate so old its iron had gone the color of dried blood. He was a broad, sun-cracked man with dirt worked so deep into his knuckles that they looked gilded. "King's orders."

"Which king?" I asked.

He blinked at that. Most people would have blinked too, if their ledgers had taught them what mine had taught me — that there were currently two kings in residence at Castle Vell, and only one of them got to use the body on weekdays.

"The king," the gardener said, with the stubbornness of a man who had decided not to think about something and would defend that decision with a hoe if necessary.

"I'm Ansel Okonkwo," I said, "auditor for the Guild of Impossible Things, and I'm here about the king's accounts." This was even true, though the account in question was a name rather than coin. "You can fetch the chamberlain to verify, or you can let me through now while my feet are still willing."

He looked at my guild seal, then at my face, then at the gate behind him, and something in his expression shifted from *obedience* to *relief*, which told me everything before I'd taken a single step past him.

"Took you long enough," he muttered, stepping aside. "Twenty years I've kept that garden. Twenty years, and this season it's growing things I never planted."

"What kind of things?"

"Things that don't grow in Vell," he said. "Snowdrop in high summer. A rowan tree that wasn't there in spring. And—" he hesitated, embarrassed, "—the roses smell like saltwater. Like the sea. Roses don't do that."

I thought of a fishing quarter, of a beacon-stone gone cold, of prayers answered in ash. The god wearing the crown was leaking. Everything buried inside King Aldric — every memory, every grief, every summer of a life traded away — was coming up through the soil like water finding cracks.

"Thank you," I said. "If anyone asks, I threatened you."

"With respect, sir, nobody's asked me anything in twenty years."

* * *

The garden was wrong in exactly the way the gardener described, which is to say it was right in every way except that it existed.

It sat in a hollow behind the royal wing, walled off by yew hedges grown tall and untrimmed, and inside those walls someone had been keeping a memory instead of a garden. Snowdrops nodded in the summer heat. There was an apple tree heavy with fruit out of season, and beneath it a stone bench worn smooth by sitting, and along the south wall a trellis that some careful hand had trained with beans — climbing beans, the plain food of fishermen and farmers, growing in the king's private ground like a confession.

And on the bench, alone, in shirtsleeves, with his crown set beside him on the stone like a hat he'd taken off after a long day, sat King Aldric.

Not the flickering thing from the throne room. Not the young king with the too-bright eyes who had granted my audience last week and spoken in two voices braided together. This was the other one. The one underneath. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, weathered at the eyes, with the build of a man who had once done real work and the posture of a man who hadn't been allowed to in a very long time. He was shelling peas into a clay bowl. Actual peas, from the actual vines on the trellis.

"You're the auditor," he said without looking up. His voice was quiet, hoarse, like a door opened rarely. "Vell's ledger-keeper. The guild sent you."

"They did not send me. They would be extremely unhappy to learn where I am."

That earned me half a smile. "Good. Then sit down, and we'll both commit small crimes together. There's room on the bench, and I find crimes go easier with company."

I sat. Up close I could see what twenty years of being worn from the inside does to a face: the fine tremor in his hands, the way his eyes moved constantly to the walls as if expecting them to lean in. But his hands were steady when they worked. Shelling peas steadied them, I suspected, the way balancing columns steadied mine.

"You're him," I said. "The real one."

"I'm the part that's left." He tipped a pea from its shell into the bowl. "Though I'll thank you not to say 'real' too loudly. He doesn't like the word. Says it implies competition, and there's no competition, because there's only ever been one king of Vell, hasn't there? That's what the proclamations say." He smiled again, thinner. "I wrote the first proclamation myself. Before. When I still had my name to sign it with."

"Aldric," I said carefully. "Is it your name?"

His hands stopped.

For a moment nothing moved in the garden — no wind, no birds, just the snowdrops holding themselves very still, the way flowers do when they're listening. Then the king let out a breath that shook going out and came back level.

"Do you know," he said, "in nineteen years, you are the third person to ask me that. The first was a cat. The second was me." He turned to look at me properly, and his eyes were the worst part — not hollow, not ancient, but *hopeful*, which is far more terrible on a prisoner's face. "The guild keeps records. You must know. What did they trade? What was it worth?"

I thought about the sealed ledger in the vault under the guildhall, written in oath-script older than the guild itself. I thought of Master Veyra's face when she ordered it burned, and how her voice had cracked on the word *now*. I thought: *this man has spent nineteen years inside his own skull asking a question, and I might be the first person alive with the answer.*

But I also thought about what Veyra would do to me, and more importantly, about whether truth delivered carelessly to a man this starved for it might break him worse than ignorance had.

"It's complicated," I said.

He laughed — a real laugh, rusty but genuine, startling a pigeon off the wall. "Complicated! Oh, that's rich. That's guild-speak, that is. My wife used to complain I talked like that. 'Aldric,' she'd say, 'when I ask if you liked the stew, "complicated" means no.'" His face went strange around the word *wife*, and I watched him reach for something and find the shelf empty.

"I had a wife," he said slowly. "Didn't I. I remember — brown hair. Laughing. A kitchen. Or is that the fisherman's memory? There's a fisherman in here too. And a midwife. And a boy who fell from a roof. And a woman named—" he pressed his fingers to his temples— "*named*—" and then his face closed over like water. "They come and go. He eats them slow, the ones I loved first. Keeps the useful ones. The languages, the law, the faces of ambassadors. A king needs those. A king doesn't need to remember what his wife's laugh sounded like, apparently, because that one's gone and I've been trying to get it back for six years."

The bowl of peas trembled in his grip. I reached over and held it steady until it didn't.

"I'm sorry," I said. It was inadequate. All the words I owned — and I owned a professional number of them, having made my living on precision — were inadequate, and I think he knew it, and I think that was why he patted my wrist once, awkwardly, like a man comforting a clerk.

"Don't be sorry," he said. "Be accurate. That's why you're here, isn't it? Something's changed. The beacon in the harbor went cold, the bells won't stop ringing, and half my city is praying itself into a frenzy while the other half goes dark. I hear it all, you know. From in here. Like hearing a feast through a floor." He set down the bowl. "So tell me accurately, auditor. How bad is it?"

* * *

So I told him.

I gave him the audit straight, because it was the only gift I had that was worth anything. The beacon-stone dead. The dragon-fire dimming — I didn't mention the Last Hearth yet; some doors should open in order. The miracle districts blooming while whole streets forgot their own names overnight. The pattern in the answered prayers, each one burning fire from a source that could not replenish, each answer drawing the crowned thing in his head a little further up out of its den.

He listened the way I imagine he once listened to reports of border raids: completely, without flinching, doing arithmetic behind his eyes.

"Nineteen years ago," he said when I finished, "there was a famine. Do you know about the famine?"

"Every record of it was expunged," I said. "Which is itself a record. Guild auditors notice absences. It's most of the job."

"Then notice this absence." He stood, restless now, and walked to the trellis, touching the bean vines like an old friend. "Three years of blight and war-winter stacked on each other. The granaries empty. The wells failing. I had already sold the crown jewels twice — once honestly, once to a man who turned out to be a fence with excellent manners. People were eating bark in the fishing quarter. Children were dying of ordinary hunger, which is the worst kind, because it's so preventable and therefore so insulting." He plucked a bean pod, turned it over. "And a man came to me. Or something shaped like a man. It offered a bargain. Survival for the kingdom — true survival, real rain, real grain, the blight lifted — in exchange for—"

"Your name," I said quietly.

He didn't turn around. "You've read the ledger."

"I've read enough of it to know it shouldn't exist, and that my own masters helped write it, and that I will probably lose my position for saying so out loud."

"Welcome to kingship," he said dryly. "I lost a great deal more for less honesty." He came back and sat, and suddenly he looked very tired in the way of men who have been carrying something heavy and have finally found somewhere to set it down. "Here is what I need from you, Okonkwo. And it isn't rescue."

"Not rescue," I repeated.

"No." He said it firmly, and I understood that this was the one battle he'd already fought and won, years ago, alone, in this garden. "Whatever you've come to do — unbind me, exorcise him, whatever the guild's old scripts suggest — don't. Or rather: understand the price first, fully, and then decide, and I'll abide by your deciding. But hear my terms." He leaned forward. "If I come out of this — if the thing leaves and the man remains — what remains will be a hollow suit. Nineteen years eaten. My wife's face, my mother's voice, the taste of my daughter's birthday cake, the reason I learned statecraft in the first place — all of it, digested. Everyone who remembers me will remember a stranger. The histories will say a king ruled well through a famine, and they will be talking about *him*, and no one will ever know what it cost, because the cost was *me*."

The snowdrops had gone still again. Even the summer air seemed to hold its breath.

"So I'm not asking you to save me," Aldric said. "I'm asking you to witness me. Somebody has to know. Not the court. Not the priests. One person, with a ledger and a good memory and — forgive me — a sufficiently stubborn disposition, who writes down what happened here. The famine. The bargain. What I traded, and why, and that I knew the price when I paid it, and that I paid it anyway, because forty thousand people were eating bark and I could stop it." His voice cracked, just once, and he mastered it. "Let there be one line somewhere that says the king chose. That he wasn't stolen. That he *gave*. Can you do that?"

I am not, as a rule, a man who cries at work. I have a reputation to maintain among people who count things. But there was something about the request — that a man being devoured alive, offered freedom, should ask instead for a *receipt* — that got under all my defenses.

"Yes," I said. "I can do that. Though I warn you, I'll want exact figures. Dates. Names of witnesses. The guild has standards."

"Of course it does." He was smiling, wet-eyed, gripping my forearm hard enough to leave marks. "Put it all in. The fence with excellent manners. The bark in the fishing quarter. My daughter's name was—" and the water closed again, and this time he made a sound low in his chest like a bell struck underwater, and I waited, and when it passed he finished, quieter: "Put in that I had a daughter, and that I loved her more than the kingdom, and that I gave up the kingdom's debt instead of hers, and that this is the only reason I don't regret it. Can you fit all that in one line?"

"I'll use small handwriting," I said.

* * *

We stayed until the light went gold. He showed me the garden — his garden, the one place the god couldn't quite colonize, because it was built entirely of memories too small and too bitter to be worth eating. Snowdrops for the winter he proposed to his wife. Salt-smelling roses for a coastal childhood I gathered had been his, before the crown. The beans because his daughter had refused to eat any vegetable that grew underground, on principle, at age four, and he'd planted climbing ones so she'd have no argument.

"She sounds formidable," I said.

"She took after her mother," he said, and then, wonderingly, "I almost remembered her just then. Her mother. There was a kitchen, and flour on someone's nose—" He stopped himself, deliberately, the way a man closes a door on a cold room. "Later. If I get out of this, later. Tell me the rest, auditor. Tell me what happens next, and don't make it complicated."

So I told him the truth: that there might be a way to undo the binding, an unbinding rite in the oldest script, but that the cost was written into its grammar and the cost was a name. Not necessarily his. Possibly mine. I watched him understand it faster than I wanted him to — kings are quick with other people's sacrifices, it's practically the job description — and I watched him start to refuse on my behalf, and I held up a hand.

"Don't," I said. "You had your turn setting terms. Let me have mine. I'll decide what I can afford. I always do. It's most of the job."

"And the rest of the job?"

"The rest is noticing absences." I stood, brushed off my coat, and picked up the bowl of peas, because it seemed wrong to leave a king's work unfinished. "One more thing, Your Majesty. The god. When it speaks through you — does it know about this garden? Does it know about me?"

Something shifted behind his eyes then, a shadow moving across shallow water, and for one instant his spine straightened and his gaze went distant and cold, and the temperature of the entire garden dropped as though a door had opened somewhere onto winter. When he spoke, his mouth was his own but the cadence was not.

"*The little bookkeeper,*" said the king, in a voice like coins poured on marble. "*Crawling through the servant's gate. Asking about names.*"

Then it was gone, and Aldric swayed, and I caught his elbow, and we stood there in the snowdrops breathing hard like two men who'd survived the same wave.

"It heard," he whispered. "It listens through the roots. I'm sorry — I didn't think — I keep forgetting it can hear me when I'm happy. Happy makes the walls thin."

"Then we'd both better be miserable more often," I said. "It suits us professionally."

He laughed despite himself. At the gate, he gripped my hand once more, hard.

"One line in the ledger," he said. "Remember that I chose."

"I promise," I said, and discovered that of all the oaths I had ever sworn — and I was an oath-bound man, bound tighter than any man alive — this was the first one that felt like it mattered.

I was three steps out of the garden when the bells began to ring — all nine towers at once, wild and unscheduled — and I recognized the pattern Sera had taught me, the one that meant a keeper was sounding the alarm.

The alarm for the bell tower on Copperlane. Sera's tower.

I ran.
