## 13. Broken Bell

Sera had always believed the ninth bell would be the death of her.

Not like this. She had imagined it as a slow surrender — her knees going bad on the stairs, or the rope finally taking a finger, or simply old age arriving one morning while she sat in the bell loft with tea going cold and the city humming below her like a struck tuning fork. A keeper's death was supposed to be small. Ordinary. The kind of death that got you a line in the guild annals and a decent turnout at the burning.

What she had not imagined was the sky cracking open over the Bellwright's Quarter at dusk on a Tuesday.

She felt it before she heard it — which should have been impossible, since hearing was rather the whole of her trade. It came up through the soles of her boots first, through the stone of the tower, a wrongness in the world's vibration, like a note played so low it wasn't sound but pressure. Then every bell in the city rang at once without being touched.

Sera dropped her tea. The cup shattered; the tea did not spill. It hung in the air for one absurd heartbeat, a brown ribbon suspended mid-fall, and then it fell all at once, soaking her skirt, and she thought, wildly: *Ansel will say something dry about that.*

The bells rang again. Not their own voices. Something else spoke *through* them, a voice like a crown being hammered onto a head that didn't want it, and Sera's ears — her beautiful, trained, twenty-years-practiced ears — heard the god underneath the city's prayers, and it was not grateful anymore.

It was hungry. And it had stopped being patient about it.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the bells, to whatever was listening. "No, no — they're *yours*, you great gilded leech, they're how they *find* you—"

Which was, she realized with a cold slide of understanding, exactly the point.

* * *

She made the stairs three at a time, which at her age was an act of faith more than fitness, down past the sleeping loft, past old Tam's room where he'd been napping between watches, out into the yard where the younger keepers were already spilling from the dormitory, faces tipped up, hands over their ears.

"Inside!" she shouted. "Get inside, away from the bells, *now* —"

Tam stumbled out behind her, half-dressed, blinking. "Sera? What in the nine names—"

The second strike took the eastern tower.

There was no lightning. That was the terrible part, the part her mind kept refusing afterward. There was no fire from the sky, no black shape against the clouds. There was simply a sound — a word, maybe, or the space where a word would go — and the tower folded like bread. Stone became sand became nothing, and the bell inside it, Saint Maren's Voice, two hundred years of bronze and blessing, screamed once at a pitch only Sera could fully hear, and then stopped screaming forever.

The sound of it stopping went through her like a blade through wax.

She was on her knees. She didn't remember getting there. Tam was pulling at her arm, shouting something, his mouth working, and the dust was coming down white and choking, and somewhere under the ringing in her skull there was a silence spreading, and she knew — the way a keeper knows a cracked bell by its shadow-note — what had just happened to her.

"Say something," she said. Her own voice reached her as if through water. "Tam. Say something."

He said something. His face said he'd said it loudly.

Nothing arrived.

The god hadn't merely broken the towers. It had reached into the quarter and pulled the hearing out of it — out of the keepers, the ones who listened, the ones whose whole lives were tuned to catching the difference between a prayer and a plea, between a god answering and a god *feeding*. It was deafening them. It was cutting its own telephone wires while the whole city stood holding the receiver.

Sera laughed. It came out wrong, wet and jagged, more wound than sound even to her fading ears.

"Coward," she said. "You magnificent, starving *coward.*"

Then the western tower groaned, and there was no time for anything but grabbing Tam and running.

* * *

They did not all make it.

She would carry the arithmetic of it forever, because keepers counted — it was what they did, it was the first thing you learned at the rope: count the strokes, count the hours, count the names. Nine keepers at the Ninth Bell. She got four out the south gate before the western tower came down across the yard. Old Tam, who had rung the dawn watch for thirty years and complained about it for thirty years and would have fought anyone who suggested he stop. Young Pell, who hummed off-key when he polished the bronze and swore it helped. The twins, Osa and Ivet, who had signed up together because neither would take the oath without the other.

Four names. She said them to herself, silently, over and over, because saying them aloud had become an act of faith she could no longer verify, and because if she stopped saying them she would have to feel the rest of it, and the rest of it was a size too large for one evening.

The quarter burned around her. Not with ordinary fire — with answered ones. She saw a woman standing untouched in a doorway wreathed in flame, arms raised, lips moving, eyes rolled back white with joy. She saw a man carrying a child that had been dead that morning, walking toward the temples, weeping, while behind him his neighbors' houses went dark window by window, district by district, the light simply leaving them the way heat leaves a body.

The miracle-drunk moved through the streets in slow processions, and the empty places followed them like shadows follow lanterns. Every prayer answered was a coal pulled off the Last Hearth. Sera couldn't hear the city anymore, but she could see it dimming, street by street, and grief and rage fused in her chest into something with a very simple shape.

*Ansel,* she thought. *Where are you?*

As if the thought had been a prayer — and perhaps it was, and perhaps that frightened her more than anything else that night — the crowd ahead of her parted, and there he was.

* * *

He looked terrible. He looked, in fact, like a man who had run through a burning city with a ledger under one arm and a sword at his hip and had lost the argument with both. There was ash in his close-cropped hair and a cut above his eye, and when he saw her his face did something complicated — relief and horror fighting for the same real estate — before settling into the familiar dry mask.

But he was shouting something and she couldn't hear him, and she watched the words die in the air between them, watched him understand, watched the mask crack straight down the middle.

"Sera." He said it twice, and she read his lips more than heard it, a faint sound reaching her like something remembered rather than experienced. He crossed the distance and caught her by the shoulders, turned her head gently with two fingers, looking at her ear, her eyes, asking without words.

"I can't hear you," she said, and knew even as she said it that her voice was too loud, the way voices go when their owner can't hear herself. "I can't hear anything. Ansel, I can't hear the bells. I can't hear—" Her breath hitched. "Twenty years. I know every bell in this city by its heartbeat. And now there's nothing. There's just me, talking in a jar."

His jaw tightened. She watched him decide not to say the comforting thing — she saw the lie form and get dismissed — and instead he took her hand and pressed it flat against his own throat so she could feel him speak, the vibration rough and quick.

*I'm sorry. I'm here. Can you walk?*

"I can walk," she said. "I can walk until the world ends. What I can't do is stand here while that thing eats my city one prayer at a time."

Something flickered across his face — that look he got, ledger-keeper's look, the arithmetic happening behind his eyes — and she grabbed the front of his coat.

"And don't you dare," she said, "don't you *dare* start calculating whether I'm worth the trouble. I've seen your sums, Ansel Okonkwo. I know what you think you cost. So let me save you the column entries: I am angry, I am deaf in one lifetime, and I am *exactly* the amount of trouble this situation calls for."

For a moment — one moment, dust falling around them, the quarter roaring — he smiled. It transformed his whole face, and she hated how much she noticed that.

"You're shouting," he said, close enough now that she caught the ghost of it.

"I'm *projecting.* There's a difference. It's a professional skill."

He took her hand properly then, laced his fingers through hers like it was the most natural act in the world, like they'd been doing it for years instead of trading sharp remarks across bell lofts and guild offices, and said, close, deliberately shaped so she could catch it: "Stay with me. We're getting out of the quarter. Veyra's people have a cellar on Candle Street. Then we figure out what it broke and what it's afraid of."

"It's afraid of being forgotten," Sera said. "That's what gods are afraid of. And it's forgetting faster than it feeds, because the dragon's dying. That's why it's doing *this*—" she swept her arm at the burning, the processions, the dark windows, "—it's binging. A starving man doesn't sip."

Ansel stared at her. Then he pressed her hand to his throat again: *How do you know that?*

"Because I've spent twenty years learning what things sound like when they're lying," she said. "And tonight, for the last few minutes I could hear anything at all — it sounded *terrified.*"

* * *

The cellar on Candle Street was full of guild clerks and half the neighborhood's children, lit by lamps that burned with the flat, greedy orange of hearth-fire stretched too thin. Ansel got her seated, got water into her, got a blanket around her shoulders with a fussy competence that would have annoyed her if she weren't shaking.

Around them the city prayed itself hollow. Even muffled by stone, even deaf, Sera could feel it — the pressure of it, the hunger moving through the walls. Somewhere overhead a woman screamed a name over and over, begging, and somewhere else a thousand voices roared in joy, and both sounds fed the same mouth.

"Pell used to say the bells were the city's conscience," Sera said quietly. Her voice came back to her flat and strange. "That as long as someone was counting the hours honestly, the city couldn't lie to itself. He was nineteen." Her hands closed around the cup until her knuckles whitened. "It killed him for *listening.*"

Ansel crouched in front of her. He didn't offer comfort-platitudes. He never did; it was one of the thousand things she was never going to tell him she loved about him.

Instead he took her hand and set it against his throat again, and spoke slowly, each word placed like a coin on a counter:

"Then we listen for them. All of them. You can't hear the bells — fine. But grief has a frequency too, Sera. Rage does. I've been reading ledgers long enough to know that everything true makes a sound, whether or not anyone's left to catch it." His eyes were bright and hard and absolutely certain. "So we'll be the listeners. Both of us. Even if we have to do it with our teeth."

Sera looked at him — this careful, dry, self-effacing man with ash in his hair and a forbidden ledger's weight hidden somewhere in his coat — and felt the rage in her chest change shape. Not cooler. Sharper. Aimed.

"They'll come for the Hearth next," she said. "The crowds. It'll march them there, won't it? All those praying mouths, pointed at the last fire in the world."

She felt him swallow. Felt the word before she read it on his lips:

"Yes."

"Then we'd better get there first."

Above them, muffled by stone and distance, the city's prayers turned — all at once, everywhere, like a tide turning — from pleading into screaming. And beneath the screams, faint as a memory of a sound, Sera felt the ground shudder once, twice, like something enormous beginning to walk.

Ansel stood. He held out his hand.

She took it.
