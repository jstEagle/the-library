## 14. Excommunication

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its tribunal in the Round Room, which was neither round nor a room so much as a very large closet that had ambitions. Nine chairs on a dais. One small stool for the accused. A single candle, because the guild believed — correctly, as it happened — that nothing looked guiltier than a man lit from below.

Ansel Okonkwo stood before the nine masters with his hands folded and his ledgers balanced under one arm like a well-behaved cat.

"Ansel Okonkwo," intoned Master Corvane, whose voice had been trained on funeral processions and never let the training lapse, "you stand accused of theft of guild property, conspiracy to conceal guild property, consorting with persons outside the guild regarding guild matters, and—" he squinted at the parchment, "—*general defiance.*"

"General defiance," Ansel repeated. "Is that weighted? I'd hate to think I'm being charged with an unquantified offense."

Corvane's jaw did something structural. "Master Veyra has already testified."

"I know. I was there. It's a small guild hall; you can hear everything through the floor." He glanced at Veyra, seated at the far end of the dais, her face arranged into official disappointment. She had aged ten years since the night she confessed to him — since she told him about the clerk who had witnessed the first binding, about the prayers she had fed the god like sugar to something sleeping, about the memories she had spent doing it. Her hair had gone white at the temples in a month. Grief, Ansel thought, is also a ledger, and she had been overdrawn for decades.

"The order stands," Corvane said. "You will surrender the first ledger. You will speak no word of the contract. You will resume your audits, and the matter will be struck from your record, and in time — if you are diligent — from your memory as well. The guild offers forgetting as a mercy, Okonkwo. Most men take it gratefully."

"Yes," Ansel said. "I've seen what most men take. I've written it down."

He set the ledgers he carried on the stool — his own working volumes, twenty years of audits, neat as scripture. Then he stepped back from them.

"But not this one."

From inside his coat he drew out the forbidden book: the ledger predating the guild itself, bound in hide that was probably not cow, written in oath-script that matched his own hand across four centuries. The candle flame leaned toward it, the way flames do around old debts.

"You are refusing," Master Osei said flatly. Not a question. Osei had taught him arithmetic of the impossible — how to price a name, how to discount a year — and had once told him he had the finest hand in the guild. Ansel wondered, briefly and painfully, whether Osei had known about the first ledger all along.

"I am formally refusing," Ansel said. "I want it in the minutes. Word for word." He cleared his throat, and gave them the speech he had rehearsed at Sera's kitchen table while she corrected his phrasing between bites of bread.

"I took an oath to protect the guild's secrets. But the guild brokered a bargain that bound a living king to a starving god, and then hid the receipt for four hundred years. A secret bought with a kingdom isn't a secret anymore. It's a hostage." He lifted the ledger slightly. "I will not return this. I will use it. And when I am done, I will leave it somewhere the guild can never audit again."

Silence. The candle crackled, taking notes.

"Excommunication, then," Corvane said, almost gently, and Ansel understood that some part of the old man had been waiting for exactly this, the way a physician waits at the bedside of someone who will not stop walking in winter.

"Excommunication," Ansel agreed. "Let's do the paperwork properly. I'd hate for my ruin to have errors in it."

* * *

They stripped him in the antechamber, which was the guild's idea of ceremony and everyone else's idea of a very slow mugging.

First the sash of office — grey wool, fraying at the hem where he'd worried it during long audits. Then the seal-ring, which came off with a small reluctant pop, as though even metal had opinions about him now. Then the keys: eleven of them, each opening a vault where somebody's mother's voice or somebody's childhood summer slept in labeled boxes.

A young clerk named Pem collected each item on a slate, tongue between his teeth. Pem had been assigned to Ansel two years ago and had learned bookkeeping the way Ansel had taught it — which was to say, with the firm belief that every number was somebody's grief wearing a disguise.

"They'll strike your entries from the master rolls tonight," Pem whispered, not looking up from the slate. "All of them."

"All of them," Ansel said. Twenty years of work, unwritten by morning. Somewhere a widow would come to ask after the debt she'd paid in her husband's name, and no record would exist that she had ever been owed anything, least of all dignity.

Pem hesitated. Then, very quickly, he pressed something into Ansel's coat pocket and went back to counting keys as if his hands belonged to the guild and the rest of him had briefly emigrated.

They left him his clothes, his boots, and — after a brief theological debate among three masters — his oath-blade.

"It's guild property," Corvane argued.

"It's a swordspeaker's blade," Osei countered. "It cannot be surrendered. Only passed. Unless you'd like to explain to the next candidate why their inheritance smells of committee."

In the end they wrapped the hilt in a cloth of formal disapproval and handed it over like contraband. Ansel slung it across his back. The weight settled against his spine the way it always did — less like a weapon than like a hand reminding him he had shoulders.

At the door, Veyra caught up with him.

She didn't touch him. Masters of the guild did not touch excommunicates; it was in the bylaws, filed under Contagion, Spiritual. But she walked beside him down the long corridor, past the shelves of boxed memories, past the window where afternoon light lay across the flagstones like something spilled and unclaimed.

"You understand what you've lost," she said.

"My rank, my ledgers, my speaking-gift, my place at the table, and my name in the guild rolls," Ansel counted. "Though I notice the last one is scheduled for erasure anyway, so really the guild is only beating the god to it. There's a certain efficiency in that I'd admire if I weren't the item being streamlined."

Veyra made a sound that in anyone else would have been a laugh. In her it was a cough with ambitions.

"When I was a clerk," she said quietly, "the year they sealed the first binding, I told myself it was necessary. That arithmetic forgives anything if the sum is large enough. I spent forty years believing that." She stopped at the door and finally looked at him — this small, worn woman who had fed a god with her own past until there was barely enough of her left to stand in a doorway. "Don't believe it. Whatever the sum. Whatever they promise you."

"I don't intend to be promised anything," Ansel said. "I intend to do the accounting myself."

She reached into her sleeve — slowly, so the corridor's watching shadows would see only a master adjusting her cuff — and slid a folded paper into his breast pocket, over his heart, the way you'd post a letter you were afraid would be read.

"The guild burned its copy of the rite," she said aloud, loudly, for the benefit of the doorway. "Consider yourself fortunate to escape our incompetence."

Then she turned and walked back into the dark of the shelves, a woman made mostly of absence, carrying the last armful of it like firewood.

* * *

He didn't open the paper until he was three streets away, in the bell-tower, with Sera's lamp lit and her kettle muttering on the stove.

Sera Iln met him at the top of the stairs, took one look at his empty sash-hands and bare collar, and said, "So. How was the funeral?"

"They haven't buried me yet. They're waiting for the paperwork."

"Mm. Guilds love paperwork." She took the ledger from under his arm and set it on her table with unexpected gentleness, the way you'd set down something sleeping. Then she looked at him properly — the way she always did, as though reading fine print. "You did it. You actually refused them."

"There was a moment," Ansel admitted, "when Osei looked at me, that I nearly apologized. Forty years of habit. Your body wants to keep belonging to things, even when the things are eating the world."

"And now?"

"Now—" He sat down heavily on her bench, and found, to his surprise, that his legs had opinions about standing that he'd been ignoring all day. "Now it feels like falling. Everyone warns you freedom is heavy. Nobody mentions it's vertigo. I keep reaching for my keys. I keep hearing the bell for third audit. My whole life had a shape, Sera, and the shape was wrong, but at least it held me."

Sera poured tea without commenting, which was its own kind of eloquence. She pushed the cup into his hands — warm clay, too-hot tea, the specific kindness of people who express love through scalding beverages.

Then she nodded at his breast pocket. "What did she give you?"

He drew out the folded paper. Unfolded it. Read.

It was the unbinding rite — the true one, in full, copied in Veyra's clerk-hand, the same hand that had witnessed the original sin of the guild four centuries ago. Every phrase of it. Every cost. His eyes went down the columns and stopped, as he'd known they would, at the final line. He read it anyway. Some lines you owe it to yourself to read twice.

*And the speaker shall speak, and the bond shall break, and the speaker's name shall go out of the world — from every mouth, every page, every memory — as though he had never been.*

"Well?" Sera asked.

"It's complete," he said. "Every clause. Every cost." He folded it again along Veyra's creases, carefully, as if the paper could feel rough handling. "She wrote *every cost* in the margin and underlined it. Twice."

Sera sat down beside him. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them the city went about its evening — carts, arguments, someone badly playing a fiddle, a child being called home by name. All of it ordinary. All of it priced.

"She's spending what's left of herself on this," Ansel said at last. "On me. On us. A woman who lost forty years of memories to that thing is handing me the knife and telling me to aim well."

"Then aim well," Sera said.

He looked at her. She was watching the city with her keeper's face — the one she wore for bells, steady and listening — but her hand had found his sleeve and closed on it, and did not appear to be planning to let go.

"Sera. If I speak the rite—"

"I know what it costs. I heard the god say it through a king's mouth, and I heard you repeat it, and I've read enough of your margins to know you never misquote a contract." Her voice was level. Her grip wasn't. "We'll talk about it tomorrow. Tonight you're an unemployed man drinking tea in a bell tower, and I intend to enjoy that while it lasts."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost. Because outside, far off toward the palace district, the sky had begun to do something wrong — a bruised light gathering over the crown spire, thickening, the color of a hearth seen from very far away. And beneath his feet, faint but unmistakable, the ninth bell shivered in its frame without being struck.

Sera felt it too. She stood, head tilting, listening with the whole of herself.

"He knows," she said softly. "Somehow, he already knows you have the rite."

Down in the streets, the first prayers of the evening rose like smoke — and for the first time, Ansel could hear them *answer*.
