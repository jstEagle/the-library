## 15. The God's Offer

The summons came the way all the worst news in my life has come: politely, on good paper, carried by a boy who was paid too little to know what he was delivering.

*His Majesty King Aldric requests the presence of Ansel Okonkwo, swordspeaker, at the palace. This evening. Alone.*

Three lies in two sentences, if you counted "requests," which I did. Kings do not request. And Aldric — the true Aldric — had never once sent for me without also sending a second note in his own hand, because he was that rarest of things, a monarch who understood that auditors like paper trails more than they like thrones.

There was no second note.

I read the invitation four times at my desk while the lamp guttered and Sera's bell rang the ninth hour across the rooftops, a sound I had come to measure my evenings by the way other men measure them by supper. Then I folded it into my coat, checked that the forbidden first ledger was hidden where no one would ever think to look for it — inside a volume of guild regulations nobody had opened since the founding — and went out into streets that smelled of ash and candle smoke.

The city was praying again. You could hear it now if you knew how to listen: not the old murmured habits of faith, but the new hungry kind, the kind with teeth. *Heal my mother. Return my son. Let it be me next.* Whole neighborhoods glowed faintly at their edges where miracles had been spent, and whole neighborhoods were dark as closed eyes where nothing was left to spend.

A woman stopped me near the Tanner's Gate to ask if I was the one who could make the god listen faster. She had a child on her hip with eyes gone silver at the rims — miracle-touched, the priests called it, blessed, the priests said, though the child looked at me with an expression I recognized from ledgers: the look of something being drawn against an account.

"I'm an accountant," I told her.

"Then you know what things cost."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the problem."

She smiled like I'd said something holy and moved on, and I stood there in the cold realizing I had just been better understood by a stranger than I had ever managed to be by my own guild.

* * *

The palace had always struck me as a building pretending very hard to be a fortress, the way some men grow beards to seem older. Tonight the pretense had slipped. The guards at the doors were new — or rather, they were old men wearing the faces of new men, their armor polished over joints that moved like rusted hinges. They did not challenge me. They did not speak at all. One of them blinked sideways, like a lizard, and I decided not to file that observation anywhere useful.

The throne room doors opened onto candlelight and emptiness.

Not truly empty. Aldric sat on the throne at the far end, exactly where a king should sit, hands resting on the arms of the chair with the careful stillness of a man who has rehearsed being alive. But the room around him was wrong. No courtiers. No guards within earshot. Just candles burning with flames that leaned toward the throne no matter where they stood, dozens of small fires all bowing in the same direction, like reeds before wind, like debtors before a collector.

"Ansel Okonkwo." The voice was Aldric's voice, but pitched slightly wrong, the way a bell sounds when its clapper has been replaced with something cheaper. "Swordspeaker. Auditor. The last of a profession that was supposed to die quietly."

"The guild prefers 'records custodian,'" I said, and bowed exactly as deeply as protocol required and not a finger's width further. "Your Majesty."

"Do you know what I admire about your kind?" The king rose. He descended the dais steps slowly, each footfall precise. Up close, in the candlelight, I could see what I had seen once before and hoped never to see again: the flicker. For half a heartbeat his face was young — thirty, open, tired in the ordinary human way of a man who has ruled through plague winters and grain shortages — and then it was ancient, hollow-eyed, patient as erosion, and then young again, the transition so smooth it made my teeth ache. Two portraits hanging in one frame, taking turns.

"There were only ever three professions that frightened me," the god said through him. "Kings, because we understand each other. Priests, because they don't. And bookkeepers." It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile fit poorly, like borrowed gloves. "Because a bookkeeper cannot lie about what is owed."

"I've heard we're also dull at parties."

"You are afraid." It circled me now, slow, studying me the way I studied a ledger with a suspicious column. "Good. Fear means you understand the arithmetic. Your Master Veyra never feared me properly. She fed me for forty years thinking she was starving me, dosing me with prayers like a nurse watering a plant she hopes will stay small." A pause. "You will not make that mistake. You have already read the first ledger. You know what I am. So let us skip the part where we pretend otherwise."

I kept my hand away from my oath-blade, which is harder than it sounds when every instinct is screaming. "What do you want?"

"What I have always wanted." Aldric's face went young again and stayed there, deliberately, the way a liar holds eye contact. "To eat without dying. To sleep without dreaming of hunger. What any debtor wants, Okonkwo. Release."

"And you're going to offer it to me."

"I am going to offer you a trade." The god raised Aldric's hand, and the candles all stood up straight at once, attentive. "Listen carefully, because unlike your guild contracts, I do not bury clauses in the appendix. Serve me. Become my swordspeaker — not the oath-bound servant of a dying order, but mine, bound in fire instead of ink. Speak my bindings. Keep my accounts. In exchange—"

It gestured, and the air beside us unfolded like a page turning.

Sera stood there. Not Sera herself — an image, rendered in light that smelled faintly of bell bronze — but accurate down to the ink stain on her left cuff and the way she pushed her hair back when she was concentrating, which she was doing in the image, bent over some chart of listening-posts, unaware, unguarded, utterly herself. My chest did something unauthorized.

"—she lives," the god said softly. "Richly. Remembered. When the riots come — and they are coming, Okonkwo, the faithful are already marching on the Hearth, and hungry gods do not stop to negotiate — she will be spared. Every bell tower in this city can burn, and hers will stand. She will grow old in a city that worships comfortably instead of desperately. And you—" the image of Sera folded away, gentle as a closing book "—you will have purpose again. A profession. You didn't enjoy watching yours die, did you? I watched you audit those ledgers. A man does not keep books that carefully for a world he has given up on."

Here is the thing I am not proud of, and since I am writing this account for whoever inherits my ledgers, I may as well write it honestly:

For eleven full seconds — I counted; counting is what I do when I am drowning — I wanted it.

Not the power. Power has never tempted me; I have seen what it costs per unit. Not the survival of my guild, which I loved the way one loves a beautiful house with rotten beams. I wanted the *certainty*. I wanted one line, written in fire instead of fading ink, that said *Sera Iln lives*, with my signature beneath it, binding and unbreakable and mine. Forty years of Veyra's arithmetic had taught me that everything is negotiable except what is written in the first ledger. And here was the first author of that ledger offering to write a line for me. In a life spent recording other people's debts, I had never once been offered credit.

Eleven seconds. Then I looked at the king's face — really looked — and saw what the trade purchased.

Because the flicker had stopped. While the god spoke of offers, Aldric's face had gone still, one portrait nailed permanently over the other, and I understood at last the mechanism of the thing. The god did not wear the crown. The god *became* the crown. That was the trap, and it was not even well hidden, because gods are old and arrogant and have forgotten that bookkeepers read the fine print of everything.

"Swordspeaker," I said. "Your previous one. Where is he now?"

The silence lasted long enough to be an answer.

"He failed me," the god said finally.

"No. He succeeded you, and then you wore him. That's the service, isn't it? That's what serving means. You don't keep speakers the way a guild keeps clerks. You keep them the way a fire keeps wood." I turned to face the throne directly. "Veyra told me the original binding needed a king because a kingdom is the largest vessel a god can pour itself into. But vessels wear out. Kings age. And a swordspeaker—" I tapped my own chest "—is smaller, yes, but sturdier. Bound in fire. Able to walk among the faithful unnoticed. Able to hold prayers in his mouth and carry them to you personally. You don't want a servant. You want a *better crown*."

Aldric's expression did not change. But the candles all leaned toward me at once, and the temperature of the room dropped as though something vast had inhaled.

"You are cleverer than Veyra," the god said, and there was something new in its voice — not anger. Appetite. Which was considerably worse. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone offered me a clever answer instead of a trembling one?"

"That wasn't an answer. That was a refusal."

"It was a negotiation." The god descended another step, close enough now that I could see, deep in Aldric's eyes, the young king still there — buried, aware, watching through his own stolen face with an expression I will carry to whatever grave I get. Pleading. Not *save me*. I had promised him witness already, and he had accepted that smaller mercy. What his eyes said now was simpler and more terrible: *Don't take the deal. Whatever it offers. Don't.* "Everything is a negotiation, auditor. You taught your guild that. Name your price. Any term. You want the girl protected without serving me? Write it. You want the binding amended — a god that eats only willing offerings, say, a god that asks before it feeds? I am old enough, hungry enough, and honest enough, in my way, to consider amendments. No one else in this city will even speak terms with me. They pray. They beg. You *audit*. Come work for something that will still exist when your guild is ash."

And gods help me — the amendment tempted me too. A god that asked permission. A hunger with manners. I could see it, drafted in my head in clean columns: *the contractor shall request consent before consumption; the contractor shall maintain the vessel in good health; the contractor shall release the vessel upon written notice.* Fifty years of guild law screaming at me that every contract can be renegotiated, every cruelty can be clause-bound, that the difference between a monster and a business partner is often just thorough documentation.

But underneath the clean columns was the line I couldn't write, the one clause no amendment survives: a god that eats must eat. Amend the manner, and you have only changed the table settings. The meal remains. And I had stood at the Last Hearth and watched the dragon dim with every answered prayer, and I had done the arithmetic Veyra had avoided for forty years: the fire runs out. Not eventually. Soon. The god's hunger and the world's warmth were the same number, and no contract language changes subtraction.

"You're right that no one else will speak terms with you," I said. "So hear my terms. I withdraw from negotiation. Permanently. Consider this my formal notice."

"Auditor." The god's voice went quiet, and quiet, from a thing that size, is louder than shouting. "You misunderstand your position. You believe you are refusing me. But you have already signed. Do you know when?" It raised Aldric's hand and, one by one, began pinching out the candles between royal fingers, unhurried, the way a man closes tabs. "The day you read the first ledger and did not burn it. The day you met the dragon and it named you signer. The day you looked at my vessel's face and pitied him instead of fearing him. Every one of those was a signature, Okonkwo. You have been in contract with me since the moment you decided I was worth understanding." The last candle died, and we stood in darkness that breathed. "The only question remaining is whether you sign as my enemy or my sword. Enemies I consume. Swords I keep sharp."

In the dark, I put my hand on my oath-blade and found it warm. Not magically warm. The way a thing is warm when someone who loves you has been holding it recently, which made no sense, because Sera was across the city with her broken charts and her stubborn charts and her terrible taste in company. But it was warm, and I held onto it, and I remembered the twelfth chapter of the first ledger, the one line Veyra had underlined twice: *a speaker's word, freely broken, outweighs a god's word freely given.* I had thought it was theology. It was instructions.

"Then here is my final answer," I said to the dark, to the king, to the thing behind both. "No."

The darkness smiled. I felt it smile the way you feel weather change.

"'No' is such a small word," the god said, "for a man who deals in precision."

"It's the smallest debts that ruin people. I'd have thought you'd know that."

For a moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — Aldric laughed. Actually laughed, the young laugh, startled out of him, genuine, the king surfacing through his own body like a swimmer breaking water, and in that laughter was everything the god could never digest: the human, unreasonable, unledgered refusal to find this funny, and finding it funny anyway. Then the depth came back up over his face, and the god spoke with the door already closing.

"Go home, swordspeaker. Sleep well. Say goodbye to your bell-keeper — do it properly, she deserves that much. Because the faithful march at dawn, and when they reach the Hearth, I will take the fire whether you stand before it or not. And when I am strong, Okonkwo — when I have eaten the last warm thing in this world and worn the last crown — I will come and collect you. You are already mine. We both signed."

The candles relit themselves, one by one, bowing toward the throne.

I walked out past the guards who did not blink, down stairs that smelled of cold stone, through doors that closed behind me with the soft finality of a ledger shutting. In the courtyard, the night sky over the eastern districts was glowing — not dawn, too early for dawn — the orange flicker of a city burning somewhere ahead of schedule.

Dawn, then. They marched at dawn, the god had said, and gods, I reflected grimly, setting off toward the bell tower at a dead run, are notorious for rounding up.
