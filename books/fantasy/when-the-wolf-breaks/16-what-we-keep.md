## 16. What We Keep

Sera had decided, sometime around midday, that she would not cry tonight.

It was a decision made in the same spirit as her other great decisions — firmly, with conviction, and in full knowledge that it would not survive contact with actual events. She had heard gods whisper through bronze for nine years. She had watched a drowned child walk out of the sea. She had felt her own hearing shatter and mend like dropped glass. She could survive anything except, apparently, one ordinary evening with Ansel Okonkwo.

He arrived at the bell tower at sundown carrying two paper-wrapped parcels and the expression of a man about to conduct an audit of his own heart.

"Before you say anything," he said, "I want it noted that I did not steal these."

"You absolutely stole those."

"I *liberated* them," he said, setting them on the workbench among the bell-pulls and candle stubs. "From a bakery on Cooper's Lane whose owner owes the guild a debt of gratitude going back eleven years. I checked the ledger. We are owed four hundred loaves. This is merely an installment."

"You audited a bakery to justify stealing dinner."

"Sera." He unwrapped the parcels with the gravity of a man unveiling relics. "There is a difference between theft and *collection*. The guild taught me that. It is possibly the only useful thing the guild ever taught me."

She laughed — actually laughed, which startled her, because her ribs had been holding grief the way the tower held bells: carefully, constantly, waiting for something to strike them. And then, because laughing hurt more than crying, she turned away and busied herself with the lamp.

They ate on the floor of the ninth bell's chamber, cross-legged on an old tarpaulin, because there was no table and neither of them suggested going anywhere else. The bread was still warm. There was cheese that had gone slightly wrong at the edges and a jar of pickled things that Ansel ate with suspicious enthusiasm and Sera refused on principle.

"More for me," he said.

"That's not the compliment you think it is."

"It's the only kind of compliment I trust. Compliments about my character are demonstrably false. Compliments about my ability to eat pickled herring — verifiable. Documented. Witnessed by two parties." He held up the jar as evidence. "You're the second party."

"The first being the herring."

"The herring has no complaints on record."

The evening light came through the bell arches in long amber bars, and the dust moved in them, slow as anything holy. Below, the city murmured its prayers. Sera tried not to listen. Her hearing had come back broken-edged since the towers fell — she caught prayers now the way one catches fragments of conversation through a wall, in pieces, out of order, all of them hungry. Somewhere a woman was begging a god to bring back her son's smile. Somewhere a man wanted his debts forgiven and would trade anything, anything, anything.

*Tomorrow*, she thought. Tomorrow Ansel would stand at the Last Hearth and speak words that would unmake a god and unmake himself, and every person in this city who had ever known him would wake with a hole shaped like him and no idea what had cut it.

Including her.

Especially her.

"You've stopped eating," Ansel said.

"You've started talking, which historically means trouble."

"It means I have something to say and insufficient courage to say it, so I'm approaching it sideways, the way one approaches a horse that bites." He set down the jar. Wiped his hands on his trousers with terrible formality. "Sera. I've been keeping accounts my whole life. Debts, credits, balances. Every transaction leaves a line somewhere. That's the whole philosophy of the guild — nothing vanishes, it only moves. Memory moves. Years move. Names move." His voice did something small and careful at the end. "But love doesn't fit in any ledger I've ever seen. I've looked. Believe me, I've looked. There's no column for it."

"What are you doing?" she asked quietly.

"Attempting the sideways approach."

"It's not working."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He looked at her then, straight on, no ledger between them, and Sera understood why kings traded their names rather than face men like this unarmed. "I love you. I have loved you since roughly the fourth time we argued, which I recognize is an absurd thing to admit, but I keep records, and the record is clear. The fourth argument. You told me my handwriting was 'an insult to the concept of ink,' and I thought, oh no. Oh, this is the one."

Sera had promised herself she wouldn't cry.

She cried.

Not gracefully. Not the way heroines in ballads cried, single luminous tear, chin steady. She cried the way a bell sounds when it cracks — sudden, ugly, resonant, from somewhere structural. Ansel did not reach for her immediately, which she would later understand was its own kindness; he simply stayed where he was, present and unhurried, until she got a hand up to say *wait, no, don't wait,* and then he was holding her, and her tears were soaking into his collar, and he said, into her hair:

"For what it's worth, I've done the arithmetic several times. Different methods. Cross-checked."

"And?"

"There's no version where this ends well. There are versions where it ends *worthwhile*. Those aren't the same thing. I used to think they were." He pulled back enough to look at her. "I spent twenty years believing everything balanced. That grief paid for joy, that sacrifice covered cost, that if you kept clean books the world would honor them. Then I met a bell-keeper who trusts nothing and everyone, and I learned that some entries you make knowing you'll never collect. You make them anyway. That's what makes them real."

"You're still talking sideways."

"I know." A ghost of a smile. "Give me a moment. I'm nearly at the center of it."

* * *

Later — much later, when the lamps had burned low and the bread was crumbs and the city below had gone quiet in the particular way of cities pretending to sleep — they climbed the ladder to the belfry platform, because Sera said she wanted to see the sky, and because neither of them wanted to be anywhere with walls.

The ninth bell hung above them, dark bronze, scarred from the god's violence but whole. Sera had rung it that morning. She would ring it tomorrow. Tonight she just rested her palm against its cold flank, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of an old soldier.

"Do you hear it?" she asked.

"Hearts don't ring, Sera."

"Not hearts. Listen." She pressed his hand flat against the metal. Beneath the cold, faint as a pulse under snow, the bell hummed — not sound exactly, more the memory of sound, the shape a note leaves behind. "Every prayer that's ever been rung through her. They stay. Bronze keeps what flesh won't. That's why I became a keeper. My mother thought it was a calling. Really it was because I liked knowing something in the world remembered properly."

Ansel was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. "What does she remember now?"

"Everything. All of it." Sera swallowed. "Which is why I need to tell you something, and you're going to hate it, so I want the bell as witness. Bells can't be charmed or bribed or erased." She turned to face him. In the starlight his face was all planes and shadows, the face she had memorized without meaning to — the crooked eyebrow, the ink stain on his left knuckle that never fully washed out, the way his eyes went soft a half-second before he let them.

"Say it anyway," he said.

"When you speak the unbinding tomorrow, when your name goes out of the world—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through. "I will forget you. Everyone will. But I've been thinking about forgetting, and here's what nine years of listening to gods has taught me: forgetting isn't erasure. It's misplacement. The memories don't vanish. They just lose their address. And I—" she pressed his hand harder against the bronze, "—I am going to leave myself notes. Everywhere. In my own handwriting, in my own head, in places the rite can't reach. Because the rite takes your name, Ansel. It takes what people *know*. It can't take what people *are* to each other. That's not information. That's load-bearing."

"Sera—"

"I will walk past a bakery on Cooper's Lane and feel inexplicably fond of pickled herring. I will argue with someone about handwriting and feel a warmth I can't explain. I will stand in a bell tower at night and grieve, beautifully and pointlessly, and I will not know why, and I will do it anyway, because some part of me will remember even if no part of me knows." Her tears came again but she let them, freely now, spending them like a woman who had finally found a currency worth burning. "So don't you dare go gently. Don't you dare think this is tidy. You will haunt me, Ansel Okonkwo. Politely. Inexplicably. Like a draft under a door. And someday — I don't know how, I don't know when, but I have heard the gods and I have heard the dragon and I know how impossible things get brokered — someday I'm going to find the debt and call it in."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he laughed — wet, cracked, real — and pulled his hand from the bell and cupped her face instead, thumbs catching tears.

"You realize," he said, "that you have just verbally contracted with a bell to pursue me across the boundary of existence itself."

"Yes."

"That's not how any legal system works."

"The bell agrees with me. Two parties. Binding."

"The herring isn't present to witness."

"*Ansel.*"

"I love you," he said, plainly at last, no sideways anywhere, and the words landed in the silence of the belfry like a struck note. "I love you, and tomorrow I'm going to lose you, and I want you to know that I have finally found the entry that doesn't balance, and I'm making it anyway. Willingly. With witnesses." He glanced up at the bell. "Two of them."

"One," Sera corrected, closing the last distance between them. "I'm not a witness. I'm the other signatory."

* * *

They did not sleep, exactly. They lay on the old tarpaulin under the bell with his coat over both of them, her head on his chest, listening to his heartbeat conduct its quiet, mortal business. At some point he began speaking softly — not to her, quite, but near her, the way people talk in the dark when the dark makes honesty cheap.

"I used to think my oath was the truest thing about me," he murmured. "*Protect the ledgers. Speak only in service.* Twenty years. And now I'm going to break it, and the strange thing is it doesn't feel like breaking. It feels like the oath finally saying what it meant all along. Protect what matters. Everything else was commentary."

"What will you miss?" she asked. "When you're gone. What do you think you'll miss most?"

He considered it seriously, because he considered everything seriously; it was one of the ten thousand things she loved and would not know she loved.

"Bread," he said at last. "Warm bread. Someone should tell whoever inherits my position that the guild runs on warm bread and no one admits it."

"I'll tell them. I'll track down the new auditor and inform them that a mysterious instinct compels them toward bakeries."

"They'll write it up as an anomaly."

"All the best things get written up as anomalies."

His chest rose and fell beneath her cheek. Above them the bell held its silence. Far to the east, beyond the rooftops, Sera could feel it even without her hearing — a wrongness gathering, a hunger turning in its sleep, the faithful already beginning to stir toward the Hearth like iron filings toward a magnet. Tomorrow would not wait politely. Tomorrows like this one never did.

"Ansel," she said. "If the march comes early. If we don't get another night like this—"

"We got this one."

"But if—"

"Sera." He pressed his lips to her hair. "We got *this* one. No gods attended. No ledgers were opened. By the standards of the last three months, it was the most miraculous day of my life, and I once watched a drowned child walk out of the sea."

She smiled against his coat despite everything. Despite the ache in her chest like a bell mid-swing. Despite the morning coming on inevitable feet.

And then she sat up.

Because far off — too far for ears, close enough for bronze — the ninth bell trembled against her palm where it rested, humming a warning she knew in her bones: torchlight in the streets below, moving westward, hundreds of them, thousands, flowing toward the Last Hearth like a river remembering which way it used to run.

The god was not waiting for morning.

The march had begun.

Sera stood, pulling Ansel up with her, and together they looked down at the burning river of the faithful, and his hand found hers in the dark and gripped it — hard, certain, a signature.

"To the Hearth," he said. "Quickly. Before the books close."
