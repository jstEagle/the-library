## 17. March on the Last Hearth

The bells began at midnight, and they were not Sera's bells.

She knew every bell in Vharen by its voice — the fat bronze hum of Saint Ossian's, the cracked complaint of the harbor chime that had never once rung true in her lifetime. This was none of them. This was a sound like a struck anvil wrapped in a choir, rolling up from the Temple of the Crowned God and out into the streets, and it did not call people to prayer so much as it called them to *march*.

Sera stood at the ninth bell tower's broken rail, her ruined ear still ringing with the memory of the god's blow, and watched the city move below her like water finding a drain.

They came from every quarter. Fishermen still smelling of brine, their nets abandoned on the docks. Bakers in flour-dusted aprons, carrying their children who should have been asleep hours ago. Old men who could not have walked to market last week and now strode like soldiers, joints made new by answered prayers, faces lit with the terrible joy of people who have been given something and will do anything to keep the giving hand full. The miracle-drunk. She'd heard the phrase from Ansel weeks ago, delivered dry as a ledger entry, and she hadn't understood it then.

She understood now. They weren't marching toward something. They were marching toward *food* — only they didn't know it, and the thing leading them wanted it that way.

At the head of the procession, borne on a palanquin of black iron, rode King Aldric. Or the shape of Aldric. Even from this distance, even through one good ear and a heart gone cold, Sera could see the flicker — the young king's face one moment, the ancient hollowed thing beneath it the next, like a lantern with something alive inside the flame.

"Ansel," she said, though he was already beside her, already looking. He was always already beside her lately. She suspected that if she ever admitted how much comfort she took in that, she would never hear the end of it, and she found she was saving the admission for a day when there would be time for teasing. There had better be such a day.

"I see him," Ansel said quietly. His hand rested on his oath-blade, not gripping it — just touching, the way you touch a promise. "Veyra said the hunger would break loose eventually. She didn't say it would be this fast."

"Veyra says a lot of things." Sera turned from the rail. "Where is she?"

"Two streets over, arguing with a door."

* * *

The door in question belonged to the cooper's shop on Tallow Row, and Master Veyra was not arguing with it so much as leaning against it, gray-faced, breathing like a woman climbing stairs in her sleep. When Ansel rounded the corner with Sera, the old clerk straightened with the stubborn dignity of someone who has spent forty years pretending not to be tired.

"The tunnel entrance," she said, before either of them could speak. "Under the cooperage. It runs under the Guildhall district and comes out at the tannery road — which is ahead of them." She jerked her chin toward the glow rising above the rooftops to the south. "If we take the streets, we arrive when they do. If we take the tunnel—"

"We arrive first," Ansel finished. "And do what, exactly? Three people against ten thousand faithful?"

"Three people against *one god*, Ansel, let us be precise about our odds. It improves nothing, but I find precision comforting at my age." Veyra pushed off the door and fumbled at her belt for the key. Her hands were shaking. Ansel noticed — he noticed everything, it was his curse and his trade — and he also noticed that her right sleeve hung oddly empty at the cuff, and that when she reached for the key her fingers passed through its edge twice before closing on it.

She was fading faster. Whatever she'd been spending to keep the god asleep, the account had come due all at once.

"Veyra," he said carefully. "When did you last eat?"

"When did you last sleep?" she returned, with the ghost of her old tartness. "We are neither of us in a state to lecture. Come. The key is small and my patience is smaller."

The cellar smelled of oak shavings and old vinegar. In the back wall, behind a rack of barrel staves Veyra moved with surprising strength, was a door of dark wood banded in iron — guild work, unmistakably, the lock worked in oath-script so old that Ansel had to squint to read it. *For those who carry the ledgers. For those who must go quickly.* His predecessors, whoever they'd been, had built escape routes into the city the way spiders build webs: patiently, invisibly, against a night they hoped would never come.

"Well," Sera murmured, reading the script over his shoulder. "Your guild really does trade in everything."

"We prefer 'impossible things,'" Ansel said. "It sounds more respectable than 'escape hatches.'"

* * *

The tunnel was narrow and smelled of centuries, and Sera hated it immediately and thoroughly.

She kept her hand on the wall — cool stone, slick in places with seep — and counted her steps because counting was what she did instead of panicking. One ear worked. One bell-tower's worth of hearing, half a life's vocation, and somewhere behind them the god's anvil-choir was still rolling through the streets, drawing its river of the faithful southward, toward the Last Hearth.

Toward Ember.

She'd named the dragon in her head weeks ago, the first time she'd felt its fire through the stone of her tower — not the roar she'd expected but something like a held note, vast and patient, the way a cathedral holds the sound of a single voice. A sleeping thing whose dreams kept the world's oldest magic banked like coals in ash. And now ten thousand miracle-drunk citizens were marching to wake it and feed it to a starving god.

"They don't know," she said aloud, and her voice bounced strangely in the dark. "Ansel. The people following it. They think they're going to a festival. I can hear them singing."

Ahead of her, Ansel's silhouette didn't slow. "I know. I heard."

"And you're just — walking."

"Yes."

"That's it? That's your whole answer?"

He stopped. Turned. In the gloom she couldn't see his face, only the pale blur of it, but she could hear something in his voice that wasn't dryness for once — something raw underneath the ledger-keeper's calm.

"Sera, if I stop to grieve every person in that crowd, we'll both be dead before dawn, and the grieving will have bought exactly nothing. So yes. I'm walking. I'm doing the arithmetic that lets me keep moving. It's not noble. It's the only trick I know." A pause. "You count your steps. I count costs. We're not so different."

She stood in the dark of a dead guildman's tunnel and realized, absurdly, that this was the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to her. There was no accounting for taste, her mother used to say, and her mother had been right about everything except leaving.

"Keep walking, then," she said, and caught his hand in the dark, and felt him startle — actually startle, the great auditor of impossible debts, undone by fingers — and she smiled at nothing, at the black air, and pulled him onward.

Behind them, far behind, the singing grew louder.

* * *

Ansel's feet knew the tunnel map before his mind did — left at the third junction, down where the ceiling dropped, past the bricked-over well. Guild architecture. Guild muscle-memory. Somewhere in the dark he felt the strange vertigo of realizing that the institution he'd served and defied had, in some generation long past, anticipated exactly this night, and had dug him a road out of it.

He wondered, not for the first time since excommunication, whether the guild had ever truly belonged to Veyra's masters or whether it had always, secretly, been waiting for someone like him to betray it properly.

"You're doing the ledger-face," Sera said ahead of him.

"What ledger-face?"

"The one where you're adding up something awful. Your eyebrows do a thing."

"My eyebrows do not do a *thing*—"

"There," she said triumphantly, turning, and in the dim light her grin was sudden and fierce and entirely inappropriate for the hour, and Ansel felt the arithmetic stutter in his chest. "That one."

"Veyra's slowing," he said, to save himself.

She sobered instantly. The old clerk had fallen three paces back, one hand trailing the wall, and when they came abreast of her her eyes were unfocused, fixed on some middle distance that contained no tunnel at all.

"Veyra." Ansel took her arm. Solid. Still solid. "How much farther?"

"Mm." She blinked. Came back like a lamp relit. "Half a mile. The tannery stair. Listen—" and her grip tightened on his forearm with startling force, "—when we come up, you'll see the Hearth road. The dragon's hollow is beyond the ridge. You cannot let the crowd reach the ridge before the fire steadies. If Ember wakes frightened—"

"*Ember?*" Sera said.

"The dragon," Veyra said, as if this were obvious. "It told me its name eighty years ago. I promised not to put it in any ledger. Some promises survive even me." She smiled, and for a moment she was young again — or rather she was the age she'd been when she'd witnessed the binding, a junior clerk with ink-stained fingers and a king's secret in her keeping. Then the smile guttered like everything else about her. "Go ahead of me. I'm slower now. Don't wait."

"We wait," Ansel said.

"Don't be sentimental, Okonkwo, it doesn't suit—"

"It suits fine," Sera said, taking Veyra's other arm. "And for what it's worth, Master Veyra, nobody's dying alone tonight. I've heard enough of that for one lifetime."

The old woman looked between them — at Ansel's set jaw, at Sera's chin raised in the particular defiance of the deaf-in-one-ear refusing to miss anything more — and something in her crumpled and reformed, softer.

"Well," she said. "Then walk quickly, sentimentally."

* * *

They came up out of the tannery stair into air the color of a wound.

The southern sky was burning. Not with ordinary fire — with the reflected glow of a thousand torches and a hundred burning districts, the miracle riots finally eating their own neighborhoods, temples blazing while their congregations marched away singing. Ash drifted like gray snow. Somewhere a dog was howling itself hoarse, the only honest voice in Vharen.

And there was the ridge, black against the firelight, and beyond it the deep fold of earth where the Last Hearth slept.

Between them and it: the road, filling fast.

They had beaten the crowd by minutes — minutes, a margin thinner than any ledger line — and Ansel watched the front ranks crest the far rise, torchlight running along their upturned faces, and saw that Veyra was wrong about one thing. They didn't look like festival-goers anymore. The singing had stopped. What replaced it was worse: silence, and the synchronized rhythm of their march, and the way every face was angled not toward the ridge but slightly upward, toward the palanquin of black iron swaying above them, toward the king-shaped thing that had stopped flickering and settled at last into its true face.

Ancient. Hollow-eyed. Smiling.

"It's done hiding," Ansel breathed.

"It's done needing to hide," Veyra whispered. Her voice had gone thin as paper. "Once it feeds on the fire, it won't need a crown or a kingdom. It'll need nothing. Nothing at all, forever." She gripped the ledger — the first ledger, the forbidden one, which she had carried from the guildhall herself, pressed now against her chest like a child. "There's one path left. You know it."

He did. The unbinding. Tomorrow — or never. But between here and tomorrow lay a ridge, a dragon, and ten thousand souls who did not know they were the teeth of a god.

"Then we hold the road," Sera said.

They both looked at her. She was standing on the tannery wall with her good ear tilted toward the crowd, listening to it the way she listened to bells — reading its weight, its pitch, its cracks.

"A crowd isn't a god," she said. "It's ten thousand separate people wearing one costume. And crowds have a seam. Every crowd does. You find the ones at the edge who aren't singing — the ones the miracles missed, the ones with doubts — and you pull." She looked at Ansel, and the firelight caught her eyes, and he thought: *there it is, the reason I broke a sacred oath's worth of rules without noticing when it happened.* "You're a swordspeaker. So speak."

* * *

Later, Ansel would never quite remember deciding. He remembered standing on the tannery wall with his oath-blade drawn and the blade's old script drinking the firelight, and he remembered opening his mouth, and he remembered that what came out was not a battle cry, because he was Ansel Okonkwo and had never produced one in his life.

What came out was an audit.

"People of Vharen!" His voice, trained by years of reading contract terms aloud in echoing halls, carried further than he expected. "You are owed a reckoning! Every prayer answered in this city was paid for — paid from the fire of the Last Hearth, paid from *your* fire, the fire that warms your winters and lights your lamps and keeps the old magic from going dark! And when it burns out — and it is burning out *tonight* — there will be no more answers. No more healed children. No more drowned sons walking home. Only the silence that was always coming, arrived early, because you carried the god to its feast on your own backs!"

The front ranks faltered. Not many. But some. He saw doubt move along the crowd's edge like wind through wheat — a fisherman lowering his torch, a woman pulling her child closer, an old soldier whose new-strong legs had carried him here but whose old eyes had seen sieges before and recognized one.

The crowned thing on the palanquin turned its ancient face toward him.

And spoke — not through Aldric's mouth this time but through the whole crowd, ten thousand voices saying one word together, gently, terribly:

"*Swordspeaker.*"

The name rolled over him like a wave. Behind it, faintly, he heard Sera shout his name — his real one, *Ansel* — as if to anchor it, as if a name spoken by someone who loved it could stand against a name spoken by something that wanted to own it.

He planted his blade in the road.

"Hold the ridge," he called back to her, to Veyra, to anyone. "Hold it until the fire steadies. Whatever happens next—" and here he permitted himself, briefly, the luxury of a smile, "—put it in the ledger."

Then the crowd surged forward, and the god inside it leaned down to look at him, and the race became a war.
