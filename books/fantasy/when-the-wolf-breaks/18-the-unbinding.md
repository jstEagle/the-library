## 18. The Unbinding

The road to the Last Hearth was paved with the things people had thrown away to run faster.

Ansel noted them the way he noted everything now, because noting was the last discipline he had left that still worked: a child's shoe, a wedding ring glinting on cobblestone, an entire market stall of bread abandoned mid-loaf, its owner three streets ahead and screaming *hallelujah* at a god who had never once asked to be praised. Behind them, the miracle-drunk faithful came on like a tide wearing sandals.

"Ledger entry," he said to Sera, jogging beside him. "Item one, one shoe, left foot, size small. Owner's grief, estimated value—"

"Don't," Sera said, breathless. "Don't do the voice. If you do the voice I'll start crying and then we'll both be slow."

"I'm doing the voice precisely so neither of us cries."

Ahead of them, Veyra stumbled and caught herself against a wall. She had been stumbling for two days now. Each time she caught herself, she looked briefly surprised that her hands were hers, as if they'd been borrowed from a stranger and might be reclaimed at any moment. She was spending herself like a woman burning furniture in winter, feeding memories into the god's sleep by handfuls, buying minutes. She had maybe a handful left.

"The Hearth's close," Veyra said. "I can smell it."

Ansel could too. Under the smoke of burning districts there was something older — a warmth like bread, like sun on stone, like every hearth-fire in the world reduced to a single source. The dragon's smell. It should have been comforting. It smelled instead like a bank vault with the door open.

They came around the last bend and the Last Hearth opened before them: a cavernous volcanic caldera roofed with ancient masonry, and in its center, coiled around a mountain of its own cooled scales, the dragon.

It was smaller than the stories said. Everything true was smaller than its stories. The dragon lay like a dying coal — vast, yes, but dim, its hide gone gray along the ridges, its breathing long and shallow and labored, each exhale carrying less heat than the one before. Around it, the great stone hearth-rings that had held its fire for a thousand years stood cold. Only the innermost ring still glowed, and it guttered as they watched, flaring and failing, flaring and failing, like a candle remembering how.

And before the dragon, filling the caldera's mouth behind them, the faithful were coming.

* * *

"We have perhaps ten minutes," Veyra said, "before they arrive, and perhaps five before the god realizes what you're about to do and comes ahead of them."

"Then we'll be efficient." Ansel set down his pack and drew out the first ledger — the forbidden one, the sealed contract written in oath-script older than the guild itself. He had carried it against his chest for nine days. It was warm. It had always been warm; he'd told himself it was his own body heat lying to him.

He knelt at the glowing ring and opened the ledger flat on the stone.

The script rose to meet his eyes the way it always did — not read but recognized, each glyph a word his bones knew before his mind did. Oath-script. The language of binding. His guild had not invented it. His guild had merely inherited it, the way a thief inherits stolen goods, and had spent four hundred years pretending the inheritance was clean.

"Read me the terms once more," Veyra said quietly. "I want to hear it said aloud. I've kept it silent for sixty years. Silence is where it grew teeth."

"'The king Aldric, of his own will,'" Ansel recited, "'trades his name entire to the god called Hunger-of-Heavens, that his kingdom survive the famine-winter. In exchange the god shall sleep within the crown and feed only on answered prayer, and the fire of the Last Hearth shall sustain all parties until the debt is discharged.'" He paused. "'Discharge conditions: none. Renewal conditions: none. Exit conditions: one.'"

"Say it."

"'Only a swordspeaker may speak the unbinding. The unbinding requires the speaker's name as payment. The speaker's name shall be paid entire — erased from memory, record, stone, and soul — such that the world owes him nothing and remembers nothing, for the ledger must balance.'" Ansel closed his eyes. "There it is. The guild's oldest rule, hiding in our oldest crime. Every debt balances. Even this one."

"You could still walk away," Veyra said. "I would understand. Gods know I have walked away from this ledger more times than I can—" She stopped. Her face did something terrible and brief. "More times than I remember."

"That's rather my point." Ansel stood. "You walked away sixty years ago, and the bill came due anyway, and now a city is marching on a dragon and a good man is being worn as a coat. No, Master Veyra. I audited this account. I'm closing it."

Sera made a sound beside him — half laugh, half something else — and when he turned she was glaring at him with wet eyes and her jaw set like a bell-yoke.

"You're going to make a joke about this," she accused. "I know you. You're standing there with your little ledger and your dry little face and you are going to say something precise and awful and funny and I am going to hit you."

"I had planned several," Ansel admitted. "But you've spoiled the surprise, and honestly the material wasn't strong. There's very little comedy in 'the world will forget I existed.' It's a bit of a tough room."

"Ansel—"

"Sera." He took her hands. Her fingers were calloused from bell-ropes and trembling from everything else. "Listen to me, because I've rehearsed this part too, and unlike the jokes, it matters. When it's done, you won't know why you're here. You'll find a ledger in the ash, probably, because I intend to leave it somewhere obnoxiously obvious, and you'll read it, because you're the sort of person who reads things other people burn. And whatever you feel — however strange and sourceless your grief is — I need you to trust it. Grief with no address is still grief. It's still real. It means someone was worth missing."

"You absolute clerk," Sera whispered. "You're writing yourself out of the world and you're worried about my filing system."

"Somebody has to maintain standards." He kissed her forehead. "That's the whole job. That was always the whole job."

Behind them, up the caldera's throat, the light changed — went wrong, went gold-and-hollow, and the screaming prayers of the crowd below resolved into a single voice speaking through ten thousand mouths, harmonized into one vast chord:

**THE SPEAKER COMES TO PAY.**

"He knows," Veyra said. "Move."

* * *

Ansel stepped inside the innermost ring.

The heat was enormous and intimate, like being held by something that could crush you but hadn't decided yet. The dragon's eye opened near him — an eye the size of a cart wheel, amber shot through with gray, ancient beyond accounting and tired past it.

***THE ONE WHO SIGNED,*** the dragon said. Its voice didn't come through air. It came through the fire itself, through the hearth-stone under Ansel's feet, through the marrow of him. ***YOU RETURNED TO YOUR LEDGER.***

"Not mine," Ansel said. "Mine is clean. Mostly. There was an incident with a widow and a loaf of bread that I intend to correct if the world survives the afternoon."

***EVERY LEDGER IS CLEAN UNTIL IT IS READ.*** The dragon's breath washed over him, warm and fading. ***SPEAK THEN, LITTLE CLERK. THE FIRE IS ALMOST OUT AND I AM SO TIRED OF DREAMING OTHER PEOPLE'S MIRACLES.***

"Wait." Veyra pushed forward to the ring's edge, the old ledger open across her arms, and Ansel understood what she meant to do. The unbinding required the original contract present — witnessed, held open, its terms displayed while they were discharged. Someone had to hold the book.

Someone had to hold it through the tearing.

"Veyra—"

"Don't waste your last minutes negotiating with me, Ansel Okonkwo. I have been holding this ledger closed for sixty years." Her voice was steady and her eyes were already going distant, reaching backward through her own dissolving memory for the words of witness. "Let me hold it open once. Let me do one thing in this whole wretched career that isn't a concealment." She smiled, and for a moment she was young — a junior clerk with ink-stained fingers, watching a desperate king sign away himself. "Besides. You'll need both hands free to break your oath. And you *will* need both hands. I've seen the script done. It fights back."

Above them, at the caldera's mouth, the crowd arrived — and stopped, all together, like a wave meeting glass. Ten thousand faces tilted upward. Their mouths opened. And the god came through.

It didn't wear a body. It wore *attention* — a pressure, a presence, a hunger given outline by the faith of everyone watching. The dragon-fire bent toward it like grass toward wind. Through the nearest of the faithful — a fishwife, a soldier, a boy no older than twelve — it spoke, all voices braided:

**SWORDSPEAKER. I OFFERED YOU A CROWN. YOU CHOOSE INSTEAD TO BE NOTHING?**

"I choose instead to be accurate," Ansel said. "You were owed a name. A name is being delivered. But it won't be *his*—" and here he felt, rather than saw, Aldric somewhere in that crowd, buried deep in his own body, stir like a drowning man feeling rope, "—and it won't be hers, and it will not be renewed. The account closes tonight. All of it."

**AND WHAT ARE NAMES, LITTLE CLERK, BUT DEBTS THE WORLD OWES US? ERASE YOURS AND YOU WERE NEVER—**

"Yes," Ansel said. "I read the terms. I wrote half of the modern gloss on them, actually, which I now realize was the universe's idea of a joke." He rolled his shoulders. Drew a breath. Looked at Sera one last time.

She was gripping the ring's edge with white knuckles, tears running freely, glaring at him exactly as promised.

"For the record," he told her, "I love you. I filed it weeks ago. I simply never submitted the form."

"Terrible," she sobbed. "*Terrible.* Go."

Ansel Okonkwo drew his oath-blade, laid its flat against his own palm, and spoke the words he had sworn at nineteen, kneeling before Master Veyra in a hall of ledgers, certain then that duty was a shelter and not a door.

"I swore to guard the guild's secrets above all things," he said, loud enough for the god, the dragon, the crowd, the ash. "I swore silence over the old contracts. I swore my name in service of the seal." The blade bit — a thin line, a token wound, blood enough to matter. "I unsay it. I unswear it. I break the oath — whole, willing, and *in writing.*"

The words tore out of him like stitches from flesh. He felt the guild go out of his bones — four hundred years of inherited silence, the whole architecture of secrecy, collapsing — and beneath it, exposed, the raw place where his name lived.

He put his hand on that place, and began the unbinding script.

* * *

Here is what the histories will never record, because he paid for their silence with himself: the unbinding is not a shout. It is a reconciliation.

Ansel spoke, and each word of oath-script unwrote a line of the contract. *The king Aldric trades his name entire* — unwritten, and somewhere in the crowd a man fell to his knees gasping as something cold slid out of his mouth after sixty years. *The god shall sleep within the crown* — unwritten, and the golden pressure above the caldera shrieked in a register that cracked stone. *Until the debt is discharged* — unwritten, and the discharge came due.

Payment: one name, entire.

Ansel felt it leave.

It didn't hurt, which was somehow worse. It *drained*. First went small things — he could no longer recall his mother's face, then whether he'd had a mother, then the layout of the guild hall, then the taste of the tea he drank every morning for eleven years. Then larger things. His first oath. His first lie. His first — 

— a warm hand. Bell-rope calluses. A tower at night and someone laughing at his jokes even though they were bad, especially because they were bad —

He gripped that one. Held it like a man holding a door shut against a flood, knowing the flood was also the sea, knowing the sea was also home.

***LET GO,*** said the god, and it was almost gentle, and it was the most dishonest thing ever spoken in any language.

"No," said Ansel Okonkwo, or whatever remained that could be called that, and spoke the final line.

*"The ledger balances. The account is closed. Let there be nothing owed — let there be nothing owing —"* and Veyra, arms shaking, tears cutting tracks through the ash on her face, held the ancient book open to the last page and gave the words of witness in a voice gone thin as paper: *"I saw it signed, and I see it ended, and I say the debt is paid —"*

The god tore free.

It came out of the crowd and out of the crown and out of sixty years of fed prayer like a black flame reversed, a hunger suddenly without a mouth, and it turned on the Last Hearth with the desperation of a starving thing, lunging for the final fire —

—and the dragon, half-dead, mostly dream, opened both eyes fully for the first time in a century.

***NO,*** said the dragon, in the voice fires must have used before there were ears.

And breathed.

Not much. Not the sky-splitting torrent of legend. A single exhalation, the last coin in a thousand-year purse — but pure, and aimed, and *true*, and it caught the starving god the way dawn catches frost. The hunger burned. Being burned, it screamed. Screaming, it thinned. Thinning, it remembered, in its final flicker, that it had once been a god of something other than appetite — and then it was not.

The fire steadied. Ansel felt it steadied, felt the Hearth's warmth settle back into its rings like a sleeper turning over comfortably, and thought: *good, that's the accounts settled, that's everything balanced,* and reached for Sera's face to tell her the audit was complete —

— and could not remember why his hand was raised.

* * *

He was kneeling in a ring of warm stone. There was ash in the air, and quiet, and somewhere a woman crying, which seemed important, though he couldn't have said why. There was a book open nearby — an old book, heavy, its pages full of marks he almost understood. His hand was bleeding slightly. He looked at the cut with mild interest, the way one looks at weather.

A man in a torn crown was weeping in the crowd, saying a name over and over — *Aldric, my name is Aldric, my name is Aldric* — as though afraid of losing it again. An old woman sat beside the book, very still, her arms wrapped around it, her face peaceful in a way that needed no explaining and received none.

The woman who was crying turned and looked straight at him.

Her eyes went through him — not past him, *through* him, searching for something at the exact center of where he stood. Her mouth shaped a sound. It might have been a name. It might have been nothing.

"Hello," Ansel said, or tried to say, or perhaps only thought, because the word arrived nowhere, landed on nothing, and the world — balancing its books, closing its accounts, owing nothing to anyone — began, gently, to forget that he had ever spoken at all.

Somewhere, impossibly, faintly, a bell began to ring.

It rang nine times.
