## 1. The Weight of Quiet Years

The fog came off the fen every morning like a debt collector, patient and inevitable, and August Eriksen had learned to treat it the same way: acknowledge it, account for it, and never let it see you flinch.

From the counting house window, the harbor of Grayharbor Row dissolved into a smear of lamplight and wet stone. Somewhere beyond it lay the drowned chapel, and beyond that the fens where the world's oldest quarrels were said to sleep. August did not look at them. Looking was a habit they could not afford.

They looked anyway, briefly, the way one touches a bruise to confirm it still hurts.

Then they turned back to the ledgers.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its books in triple columns, which was two more than most guilds bothered with. The first column recorded what was traded; the second, what it cost; the third, what it *should* have cost if the world were honest. August had filled that third column for eleven years now, in a hand so small and even it might have been typeset. A bottled sunset from the cartographer's estate, traded against three years of a widow's good dreams. The last honest mirror in the city, sold to a man who wanted to see himself as his mother had. A name, surrendered willingly, still warm.

Impossibilities depreciated strangely. Grief appreciated. August tracked all of it without opinion, because opinion was a kind of weather, and weather was the one thing August Eriksen could not permit themselves to have.

*Discipline*, their mentor had called it, back when there had been a mentor, back before the exile, back before the mentor had walked into the fens one gray morning and not walked out. *You are not hiding, August. You are keeping accounts. A storm that knows its own balance is a storm that cannot be spent by others.*

It had been a comfort once. Lately it sounded more like a cage described in flattering terms.

August pressed both palms flat against the desk until the ache in the old scars—thin white lines across each knuckle, like seams where something had been let out—faded to nothing. The pressure helped. Pressure always helped. The trouble with storm-blood, as far as anyone had ever written on the subject, was that it wanted *out*. It pooled under the skin like spring meltwater behind an ice dam, and every suppressed flicker—every swallowed thunderclap, every half-formed gust strangled in the throat—added another inch to the waterline.

Eleven years of inches.

Outside, the fog thickened. Inside, the lamp hissed. August opened the day's audit ledger and began, as they had begun ten thousand mornings, to count.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble so often did at the Guild of Impossible Things, with a payment that should not have existed.

The client arrived at the quarter-hour past dusk, which was late for audits but not unheard of; impossibility kept odd hours. He was a small man wrapped in an oilcloth coat too large for him, hood beaded with mist, and he smelled—not of rain, precisely, but of the memory of rain, the mineral ghost of a storm that had passed over him somewhere and moved on. August noted this the way they noted everything, in the third column of their mind, and disliked how easily the noting came. Eleven years ago such details had slid off them. Now they stuck.

"You're the ledger-keeper," the man said. Not a question. His voice was dry as old vellum.

"I'm the ledger-keeper," August agreed. "The guild buys, sells, appraises, and forgets. State your business and I'll price it."

He set something on the counter between them, wrapped in waxed cloth, and unwrapped it with the care of a man handling either treasure or evidence.

A page.

Not a new page. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the edges, scarred down one side where it had been torn from whatever book had held it. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, but the script itself remained startlingly fine—a hand trained in the old formal style, every letter built like architecture, ascenders crowned, descenders anchored. August knew that hand. They had spent a childhood watching it move across practice sheets, being corrected for gripping the quill too hard.

*You hold the pen like you're trying to break it,* the mentor used to say, laughing. *One day you'll learn that breaking is the easy half.*

August's palms went cold against the counter.

"Provenance?" they asked, and their voice came out level, which was its own small miracle.

"The seller prefers anonymity." The man smiled thinly. "Which I understand is something of a house specialty here."

"Everything here is a specialty." August drew the page closer with two fingers, careful not to touch it fully, though the thing they feared touching it with lived in their own blood and no distance would help. Under the lamp, the faded script resolved into legibility, and the legibility resolved into a sentence, and the sentence went through August like a harpoon line drawn suddenly taut.

*On the night of the thirteenth turn of the thirteenth moon, the storm-caller stands at the seal, and the seal shall know its own.*

Below it, the page tore away mid-line, ragged, deliberate—the rest cut off like a held breath.

There was a date inscribed beneath the prophecy in newer ink. Someone had done the arithmetic. Someone had counted forward a century from the Vellum's writing and arrived, with the terrible precision of mathematics, at tonight.

The lamp hissed. The fog pressed at the windows like a crowd. And somewhere beneath August's sternum, behind eleven years of careful accounting, something vast and patient shifted its weight.

They clamped down on it the way they had practiced ten thousand times—breath low, shoulders loose, mind reciting balances—and for a moment it worked. For a moment they were only a ledger-keeper again, mildly inconvenienced by an unusual item.

Then the lamp flame stood up straight, all on its own, though no door had opened.

The client watched it happen. His smile did not change, which told August everything: he had expected this. Perhaps he had been sent to confirm it.

"Curious drafty building," he said.

"We're above the fens," August said. "Air moves oddly here."

"Mm." He slid a slip of paper across the counter—an address, written in the same dry hand as his voice. "The seller asks only that the page be *read*. Payment is waived. Consider it a donation to the guild's continuing... discretion."

"The guild's discretion isn't for sale."

"No," the man agreed pleasantly, pulling his hood up. "But it can be *audited*. Everything can. That's rather the point of people like you, isn't it?"

He left. The door closed behind him with the soft finality of a period at the end of a sentence, and August stood alone in the counting house holding a hundred-year-old fragment of a prophecy they had never been meant to read, addressed by a childhood title no living soul should know.

*Storm-caller.*

Not exiled ledger-keeper. Not the quiet Eriksen who balanced impossible books and drank tea gone lukewarm and had not raised wind enough to stir a candle flame in over a decade. *Storm-caller.* The word fit them like a key fits a lock that has rusted shut—painfully, exactly, with a sound like something giving way.

They should burn it. That was the disciplined response. Feed it to the lamp, log the transaction as *appraisal declined, item destroyed per policy*, and spend the rest of the night on the third column, where the world was honest and nothing slept beneath the ice.

Instead August carried the page to the window, where the fog-light made the old ink legible again, and read it a second time. Then a third. Their fingers trembled, and they watched the trembling with clinical interest, the way one watches a barometer fall.

Because here was the thing the discipline could not account for, the entry that refused to balance: the page did not frighten them.

What frightened them was that some traitorous, flooded, decade-dammed part of them had read *the seal shall know its own* and felt—not dread.

*Relief.*

"Eleven years," August said aloud, quietly, to the fog, to the fen, to the drowned chapel somewhere out in the dark where old quarrels slept and older seals held. "Eleven years of quiet, and it schedules itself for a Tuesday."

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off over the fens, thunder answered—low, brief, impossibly distant and impossibly familiar, like a dog answering a whistle only one person in the world could hear.

August folded the page along its old creases and hid it in the false spine of the day-audit ledger, beneath eleven years of immaculate, invisible, suffocating honesty. Tomorrow they would decide whether to tell Ysolde. Tonight—

Tonight they sat back down, opened the ledgers, and tried to count.

The numbers swam. In the margin of the third column, without quite deciding to, August found they had written a single word in their mentor's formal hand, learned in a childhood that belonged to someone else:

*Tonight.*

Under it, the ink dried dark as storm-water. And beneath the floorboards, beneath the foundation, beneath the whole patient architecture of the lie they had lived in—something turned over in its sleep, and began, very slowly, to wake.
