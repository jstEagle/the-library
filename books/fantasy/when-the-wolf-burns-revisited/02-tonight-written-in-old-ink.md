## 2. Tonight, Written in Old Ink

August did not sleep. Sleep was a country they had emigrated from long ago, and like all exiles, they had learned to live on what the border allowed them to carry.

The counting house above the fog had gone quiet hours ago—the particular quiet of a building that breathed. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its ledgers in a tower of green slate that creaked when the weather lied, and the weather in Vharrow always lied. August sat at their desk with a single lamp turned low, the torn page flat beneath a square of glass, and did not touch it for a long time.

Touching it felt like answering a knock you already knew was death's.

The page had come in with the evening's audit, folded inside a client's payment: three silver marks and this scrap of vellum, old enough that its edges had gone the color of tea left standing. The client—a wool-factor with damp eyes and an accent from nowhere in particular—had paid without haggling, which in August's experience was the most expensive kind of transaction. People who didn't haggle were paying for something else entirely.

*For services rendered,* the factor had said, and smiled at August the way one smiles at a stranger one has been introduced to before, somewhere, and cannot quite place.

August had balanced the entry. *Received: 3 marks, one page, provenance unknown.* The guild's books tolerated anything if the arithmetic was honest.

Now, alone, they finally looked.

The script was century-old High Canticle, the liturgical hand used by storm-priests before the kingdoms burned the last of their temples. August could read it because there had been a time—another life, another name—when reading it was as natural as breathing, and breathing had been dangerous too. Their fingers remembered the letterforms before their mind did, the way scarred skin remembers rain.

*...and in the season of the drowned chapel,* the page began mid-sentence, *when the moon makes her thirteenth turn against every law of heaven—*

August stopped.

They counted backward through the almanac without wanting to. The moon had been wrong for weeks now—rising late, setting early, hanging fat and yellow over the fens like a coin someone had bitten and found too soft. The guild's astronomers had filed seventeen complaints about it. Impossible things were the guild's stock-in-trade; even so, a thirteenth turning made the clerics nervous in a way that no dragon or talking door ever had.

They read on.

*—the storm-caller shall stand upon the water where the seal was set, and shall be given two endings only: to feed the seal and die a martyr, or to break it and be named the author of the war. There is no third ending. There is no acquittal. What was written is written.*

Beneath it, in the same hand but pressed deeper, almost angrily, a date.

August knew the date the way you know your own heartbeat. It was tonight.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening, and far off across the fens, thunder rolled where there were no clouds—only the low, patient grumble of the drowned chapel's bell, which rang underwater sometimes, though everyone agreed it shouldn't.

There was more. A line near the bottom, half-eaten by damp or by deliberate tearing:

*—and the caller shall be known by the name given at the hearth, the small name, the first name, the name of—*

The rest was gone. Torn away. But August's whole body had gone cold around a single phrase.

*The name given at the hearth.*

No one living knew that name. No one living *could*. It had belonged to a child in a house above a harbor, a child who watched storms the way other children watched fires—too closely, too hungrily—and who had been called by a private name, a hearth-name, spoken only by two people who were both long dead. August had buried that name so thoroughly that some nights they had to dig to remember it themselves.

And here it was, referenced in ink older than the guild, older than the exile, older than every careful year August had spent being nobody with immaculate handwriting.

*What was written is written.*

August sat back. The chair complained. Somewhere below, the guild's impossible inventory settled itself for the night—the caged north wind turning in its sleep, the mirror that showed Tuesdays going dark, whatever else Ysolde refused to itemize even for the auditors.

Ysolde. The thought of her arrived like a hand on the shoulder.

Guildmaster Ysolde Brann had fished August out of a border town nine years ago, half-starved and pretending to be a clerk, and had looked at them exactly once with those gray, unsentimental eyes and said: *You balance numbers well. Numbers don't ask questions. You'll do.* She had never asked a question since. Not once. In a guild that traded in impossibilities, Ysolde's greatest impossibility was her perfect incuriosity about August Eriksen's past.

Which meant either she knew nothing—which was impossible—or she knew everything and had decided the ledger of their association balanced without explanation.

Either way, August could not carry this page to her desk tomorrow morning. Because the page was bait. They understood that now with the flat, cold certainty of an accountant reading a fraudulent entry. Someone had paid three silver marks—not for services rendered, but to place this page in the guild's hands, in *August's* hands, at precisely the right hour. Whoever wrote the prophecy a century ago—or whoever had waited a century holding it—wanted the storm-caller to read this tonight. Wanted them to know the appointment had been kept.

The past hadn't merely survived. The past had *scheduled itself*, and sent an invoice.

August rose and went to the window. Below, Vharrow lay drowned in fog, lamps glowing through it like coins at the bottom of a well. Somewhere out there stood the drowned chapel, its bell muttering under black water, and beneath the chapel, if half the guild's stranger rumors were true, something sealed—something the kingdoms' histories called *the Quiet War*, ended a hundred years ago by an armistice no one would describe and no one dared question. The peace had held ever since. Everyone agreed the peace was worth any price. No one had ever said aloud what the price had been.

August had always assumed the price had been paid by others.

They looked at their own hands in the lamplight. Steady hands. Clerk's hands. For nine years they had suppressed every flicker of the thing inside them—the pressure behind the sternum when storms came, the itch in the teeth before lightning, the way rain sometimes hesitated mid-fall, waiting, like a dog hearing its name. Nine years of discipline. Of being a ledger-keeper. Of *invisibility as a practice, daily, like prayer.*

And tonight a piece of hundred-year-old vellum had walked into the counting house and addressed them by a dead name.

*Two endings only.* Martyr or tyrant. Die feeding the seal, or break it and inherit the blame for a war older than anyone alive. The prophecy offered no third option, and prophecies, in August's experience, were less like fate and more like contracts—drafted by interested parties, with clauses designed to benefit the drafter.

Well. August had spent nine years auditing contracts.

Someone had authored this one. And authors, however ancient, however careful, always left fingerprints in the margins—in word choice, in emphasis, in the furious deep-pressed date. *There is no third ending,* the text insisted, the way a merchant insists a price is fixed right before the haggling begins.

August took up the page, considered it, and then did the most difficult thing they had done in nine years: nothing dramatic at all. They copied it. Every stroke, every tear, every water stain, into their private book in their own neat clerk's hand, including the things the page did *not* say—the gaps, the torn edges, the places where damp might hide erasure. Then they folded the original back into its creases, returned it to the audit drawer, and logged it.

*Received: 3 marks, one page, provenance unknown. Disposition: pending.*

Pending. The most honest word in the ledger.

They banked the lamp and stood a while in the dark, listening to the tower breathe, feeling the storm-pressure rise in their chest with the tide. Across the fens, the drowned bell rang once—clearly, unmistakably, though there was no wind to move it and no water should carry sound so far.

Once. Like a summons. Like a clock finishing its count.

Tomorrow, clients would come asking questions. August didn't know how they knew that; they simply did, the way one knows weather in the bones. The guild's silence had been sold or stolen, and the hunters were already packing their instruments.

But tonight, one thing remained to be done, and August did it before dawn: they climbed to the archive's highest shelf, past the catalogues of traded years and bottled apologies, and pulled the guild's oldest accession record—the book Ysolde believed no one read.

On the flyleaf, in faded ink, was the signature of the guild's founder. The mentor who had built this house of impossible things, taught Ysolde everything, and vanished forty years ago leaving only rules and rumors.

The signature matched the hand on the prophecy page. Stroke for stroke. Pressure for pressure. Even the angry, deep-cut way the letters *th* were crossed.

August closed the book very gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead.

The mentor had written tonight down a hundred years ago. The mentor had known the hearth-name. Which meant the mentor had known *them*—had known the child before the exile, before the hiding, before the ledgers.

Which meant August had never been invisible at all.

They had been *filed*.

* * *

Dawn came up gray over Vharrow, and with it came the first of the clients—three hours early, hooded, smelling faintly of ash and rain—and August, standing at the counting window with their pen poised and their face arranged into perfect clerical blankness, heard Ysolde's boots on the stairs and thought, with terrible calm:

She doesn't know yet. And I have until she does.
