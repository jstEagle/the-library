## 3. Clients Who Smell Rain

The fog came off the fen at dusk the way it always did, thick as spilled milk and smelling faintly of rust and old promises. Ysolde Vane stood at the counting house window with a cup of chicory going cold in her hand and watched the lamps of the causeway light one by one—each flame a small act of defiance against a world that had decided, long ago, that this stretch of drowned country was not worth the trouble of remembering.

That forgetfulness was the guild's first wall. The second was the ledger-wardens. The third was her.

She had built the Guild of Impossible Things out of the wreckage of three bankrupt brokerages, two exiled cartographers' societies, and one very bad decade, and she had learned early that the trade in impossibilities survived only on discretion. Clients came to them because no court would hear their petitions, no temple would bless their bargains, no bank would touch their collateral. A woman's shadow, sold at auction. A debt owed by a dead god's estate. The last honest map of a city that had unmade itself. All of it passed through Ysolde's hands, and none of it—not a name, not a need, not a whisper—ever left the building.

Which was why, when the ash-priest arrived an hour before closing on the same night that a moon-tithed noble's carriage ground up the causeway through the fog, Ysolde felt the particular cold in her stomach that she had learned to trust more than any oracle.

Two impossible clients in one night was coincidence.

Three was a leak.

* * *

The ash-priest came first, hooded and grey, leaving fine grit on the welcome mat that glittered when the lamplight caught it—burnt scripture, she suspected, pressed and powdered. He gave his name as Brother Sallow, which was no name at all, and requested an appraisal.

"Of what?" Ysolde asked, settling behind her desk. She kept the desk between herself and strangers for the first meeting, always. Not fear. Arithmetic.

"A person," he said. His voice was dry as the inside of a bell. "We are told the Guild of Impossible Things can locate persons who do not wish to be located. We have heard—" and here he paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before stepping over a corpse, "—that you once sheltered such a person."

"We shelter many people," Ysolde said. "It's called employment."

"This one would be a storm-caller."

The chicory she'd drunk earlier sat suddenly heavy. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass like something listening.

Ysolde did not blink. Blinking was for amateurs. "Storm-calling is a capital tradition in four kingdoms," she said pleasantly. "If you're asking me to appraise one, I'll need to know whether you want market value or execution value. They differ considerably."

The priest's smile was visible only as a shift in the shadows beneath his hood. "We were told," he said, "that the guild keeps what it keeps because it is paid to. And that everything—" he let the word roll, savoring it, "—has a price."

"Who told you that?"

"An old friend of the house." Brother Sallow set something on the desk: a coin, blackened, struck with the sigil of the moon-seal—a crescent biting its own tail. "When you are ready to discuss the storm-caller, melt this down and send us the metal. Ash remembers where it has been."

He left without bowing. Ysolde sat for a long moment looking at the coin, and then, very carefully, did not touch it.

Someone had sold them. Someone who knew the old cant—"an old friend of the house"—someone from before, from the years she did not speak about even to herself. Her hand drifted, without her permission, to the second drawer on the left, where she kept the single letter August Eriksen had ever written her: *Thank you for the work. Thank you for not asking.* Eleven years of quiet ledgers and averted eyes, all of it suddenly standing in a draft.

* * *

The noble arrived while the ash-priest's grit was still on the mat.

Lord Aurel Voss-Maren came up the causeway in a carriage drawn by white oxen whose horns had been gilded, because subtlety, in his family, had died sometime around the third century. He filled the counting room doorway the way weather fills a valley, all brocade and impatience, and he did not sit when offered a chair.

"You are the guildmaster." It was not a question. He looked at her the way men of his class looked at furniture they intended to move. "I'll be brief. My family holds a tithe to the moon-seal. Has held it for nine generations. Silver, blood, and obedience, rendered quarterly to the wardens of the chapel below the fen." He produced a glove and began working it off finger by finger, a man preparing to handle something distasteful. "This quarter, the wardens refused our silver. They said—" his jaw tightened, "—they said the tithe is ending. That the seal will soon be fed by other means. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"That your allowance is being cut," Ysolde said.

"I am telling you that my family's entire position rests on that tithe. Remove it, and every rival we've bought with seal-favor comes calling. So I am given to understand—" he leaned across her desk, and she caught the smell of him now, under the cologne: ozone and fear, "—that there is a storm-caller in this fen. And I am prepared to pay *enormously* for the privilege of knowing where."

"Lord Voss-Maren." Ysolde folded her hands. "Do you know what we sell here?"

"Impossibilities."

"No. We sell *solutions* to impossibilities, and the difference matters. What you're asking for isn't a solution. It's a person." She rose, unhurried, and opened the door for him. "And the guild does not traffic in persons. It's in our charter. You may read it on your way out; it's framed rather nicely."

He stared at her with the affronted bafflement of a man being refused by a door for the first time in his life. "You understand," he said quietly, "that if I can ask, others can buy. Whoever sold this to me will sell it to someone less courteous. When they come—and they will come—you may wish you had taken my money while it was still worth something."

"Get out of my guildhouse, my lord."

He went. But his words stayed behind him like smoke, and Ysolde stood in the doorway watching his carriage recede into the fog, doing arithmetic she hated.

Two clients. Two sources. The same rumor, arriving from opposite directions, converging on her counting house like wolves that had scented the same blood from different ridgelines. Rumors didn't travel that way unless someone was seeding them. Deliberately. In both directions.

Which meant it wasn't a leak.

It was a hunt.

* * *

The third came at midnight, past closing, past the hour when the lamps along the causeway guttered low. No carriage. Just a knock, soft and precise, three times, and when Marta the doorwarden admitted the figure into Ysolde's private study, Ysolde saw at once that this one was different.

Small. Weather-browned. Dressed in traveling leathers gone soft with years of rain. The cartographer—she recognized the guild-tattoo on the wrist, faded almost to nothing, the mark of a society that had dissolved before Ysolde ever learned to count—did not speak, and after a moment Ysolde understood why. The woman drew instead. On the back of an old receipt, in quick sure strokes: a mouth crossed out. Then a gesture at her own throat, and a small shrug that contained an entire biography.

"Mute," Ysolde said. "Or sworn. With your order it was usually both." She poured tea without asking—the cartographer's kind had always taken tea—and slid the cup across. "You have until the pot is empty. Talk."

The woman took out charcoal and paper, and talked.

First she drew the drowned chapel, seen from above, its spire breaking the fen-water like a broken tooth. Around it, concentric rings—wards, tithelines, the old geometry of the seal. Ysolde's tea went cold in her hands as the drawing grew more detailed than any public record could account for. This woman had been *inside*. Recently. The charcoal moved faster.

Then a figure. Small, hunched over ledgers, ink-stained fingers, hair pinned back carelessly. The likeness was good enough to make Ysolde's chest tighten. Underneath it, the cartographer wrote a single word in a careful, archaic hand:

**STORM-CALLER.**

"No," Ysolde said aloud, to the room, to the fog, to eleven years of discipline cracking down the middle like river ice.

The cartographer kept drawing. A page torn from an old book, rendered faithfully, its script too small to read but its shape unmistakable—Ysolde had seen pages of that vellum exactly twice, decades ago, in a dead man's study. Then a question mark beside it. Then, slowly, deliberately, the cartographer turned her papers so Ysolde could see the whole sequence at once: chapel, seal, storm-caller, prophecy. And beneath it all, written large:

**THEY ARE COMING FOR HER. NOT TO BUY.**

Ysolde set down the teacup. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady, distantly, the way one notices one's own heartbeat during a fall.

"How many?" she asked.

The cartographer held up three fingers. Then turned her hand and showed the palm, where she had inked, sometime in the last day, three fresh marks: ash. crescent. blank space waiting for a third sigil she hadn't yet identified.

Three hunters. Three clients. And somewhere out in the fog, the third one hadn't bothered to knock yet—which meant it was already inside somewhere, or patient enough not to need doors.

Ysolde walked to the window. Below, in the counting room, a single lamp still burned, and by its light she could see the bent silhouette of her ledger-keeper at the evening audit, precise and silent and utterly unaware—or pretending to be unaware—that the world had finally come to collect on a debt eleven years deferred. August Eriksen, who never spoke of storms. Who flinched, just slightly, whenever thunder rolled off the mountains. Whose paperwork, Ysolde had noticed years ago and said nothing about, was always immaculate in a way that suggested a person holding themselves together by the edges.

*I have kept you,* Ysolde thought. *Eleven years. I have kept you the way one keeps a coal under ash—not extinguished, only hidden.*

She had told Lord Voss-Maren the guild did not traffic in persons. That was true. But the guild had never yet lost a war fought on its own ground, either, and she found, surveying her shelves of impossible inventory—the bottled lightning, the unsleeping mirror, the door that opened onto yesterday—that her grief and her fury had arrived at the same conclusion at the same moment:

They would not take August Eriksen from this house. Not bought, not bartered, not burned out of it.

The cartographer watched her with sharp, patient eyes, and then wrote one final line and slid it across the desk:

**WHO TOLD THEM?**

Ysolde looked at the question a long time. Because there was only one answer that fit, wasn't there? Only one person who knew the old cant, knew the seal, knew the prophecy's shape, and knew precisely where in the counting house a storm-caller hid among the ledgers.

Only one person who had loved August enough to hide them—and known them well enough to spend them.

"Get some rest," Ysolde said hoarsely, turning away from the window. "Tomorrow we open the mentor's vault. And God forgive us both for what we find there."
