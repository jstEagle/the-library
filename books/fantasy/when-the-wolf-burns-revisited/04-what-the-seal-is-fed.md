## 4. What the Seal Is Fed

The map smelled of rain that had not yet fallen.

August discovered this at the third hour past midnight, alone in the counting house with the stolen cartography unrolled across a desk built for double-entry ledgers and single-entry lives. The mute cartographer—Vess, Ysolde had called her, before Vess vanished into the fog like all the night's strange clients—had left it behind in her hurry, or perhaps had left it deliberately, which was worse. It was drawn on skin-pale vellum, inked in a grey that shifted when August wasn't looking directly at it, and along its lower margin ran a thin wash of color like a bruise coming up: violet, then silver, then the deep green-black of water over stone.

It also smelled of rain. Not the fog-damp that lived permanently in the guild's bones, but true rain, storm rain, the kind August had spent eleven years teaching their body not to answer. The smell sat in the back of the throat like a word half-remembered.

*Don't*, August told themselves, the way they told themselves every morning, every ledger line, every time lightning stitched the horizon beyond the counting house's salt-fogged windows. *You are a man who adds columns. You are a man whose hands stay dry.*

But the map was pulling, and August had learned long ago that the difference between courage and compulsion was mostly bookkeeping.

They went anyway.

* * *

The drowned chapel lay two miles east of the guild, out where the city's cobbles surrendered to marsh and the marsh surrendered to fen, though no map August had ever audited admitted to either. That was the first wrongness: the cartographer's map showed streets that did not exist on any Guild of Impossible Things record, and August had personally reconciled every impossible thing the guild traded against every possible thing the city claimed to be. There was no chapel on the civic rolls. There was no drowned district. The tax assessor's books ended at the fen's edge with a note in faded brown ink: *unproductive land, exempt.*

Unproductive. August walked through knee-high water under a moon one turn shy of impossible and thought: *someone decided this place produced nothing worth counting, and so it stopped existing.* They knew how that worked. They had been a line item once, struck from a ledger, written off as loss.

The water grew colder as the ground sank away beneath it. Old willows leaned overhead like mourners who had been standing too long to remember whom they'd come to bury. And then the chapel rose out of the fen ahead of them—or rather, the top of it did, a broken bell-tower and the shoulders of a roof, everything below the waterline swallowed whole. The windows were dark. The bell had no rope. But August could feel something from here, a pull low in the sternum, familiar and obscene, like hearing your own heartbeat played back to you by someone else's mouth.

Storm-calling. Something down there was calling storms. Or being fed them.

August stood chest-deep in black water at the chapel door and made the mistake of hesitating. In the hesitation, the fen went silent—the frogs, the insects, even the wind—and in that silence they heard it clearly for the first time. A sound beneath the water. A slow, rhythmic drawing, like lungs, like tides, like a great throat swallowing on schedule.

The map in their satchel had gone warm.

August dove.

* * *

Below the waterline the chapel was not drowned at all. That was the second wrongness, and it took August's mind a long moment to accept what their eyes reported: air, dry stone, lamplight burning steady in sconces that should have been a century underwater. The flooded world above continued overhead like a ceiling of rippling glass, fish moving through it, moonlight bending down through the fen in wavering pillars—and beneath it, a nave stretched impossibly long, its floor laid with tiles of white stone, each tile engraved with a phase of the moon.

There were more than eight phases.

August counted without wanting to. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. The thirteenth tile sat at the head of the nave before an altar, and the thirteenth tile was not white. It was red-brown, stained through, and it pulsed.

Around the altar, arranged with the tidy geometry of an accounting system—which was the detail that would keep August awake for the rest of their life, more than the blood itself, the *tidiness*—stood rows of glass vessels. Tall ones, human-sized. Some empty. Some not.

The ones that were not empty held people.

No. August corrected themselves, because correction was their trade. The vessels held what was *left* of people. Each tank was webbed with fine silver tubing, and the tubes ran down into the floor, converging beneath the thirteenth tile, and inside each vessel floated a figure suspended in pale fluid—figures with the gaunt serenity of the long-unconscious, their wrists banded, their throats marked with small deliberate scars over the veins. Storm-scars. August knew the placement intimately; they bore the same marks themselves, faded now but never gone, the body's receipt for every time it had answered weather with weather.

Exiles. Every vessel held an exile. August recognized the type the way a clerk recognizes handwriting—not the individuals, but the hand. These were people like them: storm-callers scraped from the margins of kingdoms that feared them, funneled here over decades, over generations, filed away in glass and fed drop by drop into the seal.

The peace August had resented for eleven years—the long quiet, the wars that hadn't come, the moon that had behaved itself through twelve obedient turns—all of it had been paid for. Not with prayer. Not with sacrifice freely given. With a harvest. A hundred years of harvest, entered neatly, debited against lives that had been exiled precisely so no one would notice when they stopped appearing anywhere at all.

*I was next,* August thought. The understanding arrived without drama, flatly, the way bad news arrives in a ledger: *the exile who escaped the write-off.* Their mentor had not saved them from the war. Their mentor had saved them from *this*—or had merely delayed the invoice. The thought turned in their stomach like a key.

One of the vessels was newer than the others. The fluid inside still carried a faint cloudiness, undisturbed sediment, and the figure within was recent enough that the storm-scars at the throat were raw. A young man, maybe twenty, dark-haired, his lips slightly parted as if mid-sentence. On the base of his tank, a small brass plate, and on the plate, stamped in the Guild of Impossible Things' own inventory font—a font August had ordered replacement dies for only last spring—

A lot number.

August crossed the nave slowly, water from their clothes dripping onto the white tiles, each drip landing with a sound too loud. They knelt at the young man's vessel and read the plate, and read it again, because the first reading could not possibly be correct.

*Lot 114. Received: three weeks past. Source: Guild of Impossible Things, consignment via ash-priest intermediaries. Status: feeding.*

Three weeks ago. Three weeks ago the clients had begun arriving. Three weeks ago someone had sold the guild's silence, and the harvesters had already collected. This boy had passed through the guild's orbit—through *Ysolde's* protection, through the same fog-bound walls that had sheltered August for eleven years—and come out the far side in a glass jar, and the transaction had been booked, *booked*, somewhere, in some ledger August had never been allowed to audit.

The seal drank. August could hear it drinking now that they listened—could feel the rhythm of it through the tiles, through the soles of their feet, that slow swallowing tide. Beneath the thirteenth tile, visible through hairline cracks in the white stone, something silver-dark moved in channels worn smooth by a century of flow. Storm-blood. Not metaphor. Actual blood, actual storm-nature, siphoned from a hundred exiles across a hundred years to keep the ancient war asleep and the seal intact and the kingdoms comfortable and the moon turning its obedient twelve.

And tonight, when the moon made its thirteenth turn, the seal would want more than a trickle.

August pressed their palm flat against the glass of Lot 114's vessel. The young man did not wake, but something in him stirred—a flicker behind the eyelids, a faint crackle of static in the fluid, the way the air used to taste before August's storms, when they were a child and the sky came looking for them. Recognition. One storm-caller feeling another through a wall of glass and a wall of years.

"I'm sorry," August said aloud, and their voice sounded strange in the dry nave, the first voice these walls had heard from a living throat in longer than anyone had bothered to count. "I didn't know. I *lived off you* and I didn't know."

The lamps guttered. Above, through the ceiling of water, the moon slid free of cloud, and every tile in the floor brightened at once—twelve white moons answering, and the thirteenth, the red one, pulsing faster now, hungry, keeping time with something.

With the moon's approach. With the turn that the Vellum had named, the date that had scheduled itself into August's life like a debt collector.

The seal felt thinner than it should have been. August realized, with the particular horror of an auditor finding a discrepancy, that the math no longer balanced—that a century of harvest had been drawn down, vessel by vessel, exile by exile, and whatever reserve remained was nearly spent. The seal was starving. The ancients beneath the fens were beginning to stir against a lock grown weak. Someone had known this was coming. Someone had always known. The prophecy had said so, a century ago, in old ink: *the storm-caller will break the seal or die trying.* As if those were the only two entries permitted. As if the account allowed no other outcome.

August looked down the long nave—at the vessels, at the boy, at the red tile drinking its measured meal—and understood finally what they had resented all these years without naming. Not the quiet itself. The *price* of it. The quiet had been bought with people like them, sold by institutions like theirs, and the invoice had been hidden so well that even the bookkeeper hadn't known he was spending stolen blood every day he balanced his harmless little ledgers.

Well. August Eriksen had found the hidden account.

Above, through the water-ceiling, thunder rolled—distant, directionless, or perhaps not distant at all. Perhaps it was coming from the seal itself, the trapped storms of a hundred exiles pressing against a century of confinement, and the moon climbing toward its thirteenth turn like a hand reaching for a door.

August straightened. Somewhere between the fen and the nave, dread had finished its work and hardened into something else—something with edges, something that could be aimed.

First, the mentor's vault. First, the original Vellum. First, answers about who had consigned Lot 114, and whose hand had signed the transfer.

Because if the guild's own inventory stamps were on these tanks, then the trail of blood ran through the counting house doors.

And August kept the keys.
