## 6. Loyalty, Priced Per Page

The ash-priests came at the hour when the fog thinned and the counting house smelled of cold tallow and older debts. Ysolde Vane had been guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned that the worst clients never announced themselves. They simply arrived, the way rot arrived in a beam—already inside, already patient.

There were three of them. They stood in her office without being invited, which was itself a kind of signature; no one crossed the threshold of the guild's inner rooms uninvited unless they believed the rules did not apply to them. Grey robes, grey faces beneath their hoods, and eyes like the last light in a banked fire—the particular orange of things that had already burned once and intended to do it again. Ash clung to them faintly, though Ysolde noted with professional interest that none fell onto her floorboards. Discipline, then. Even fanatics kept house.

"Guildmaster." The one in front spoke in a voice like sifted cinders. "We come to make an offer."

"You come into my counting house at dawn," Ysolde said, and did not rise from behind her desk. The desk was old ironwood, scarred by two centuries of signatures, and it weighed more than any of them. She liked having it between herself and people who smelled of endings. "Sit down, all three of you, before you drip on the carpet."

They did not sit. One of them set something on her desk instead: a ledger. Not a guild ledger—she knew every binding in her own collection the way a mother knows footsteps—but a government one, stamped with the triple crown of the Moon-Tithe Courts, its spine cracked from handling.

"We have read your accounts," the priest said.

"Then you know we're solvent," Ysolde said. "You're welcome."

The priest's mouth did something that might have been a smile on a face less ruined. "We know what the Guild of Impossible Things trades in, guildmaster. We know what impossible thing has been living under your roof for eleven years, balancing your books and hiding its hands in its sleeves. We know the storm-caller is yours."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

Ysolde let the silence sit exactly as long as it needed to—not long enough to seem shaken, not short enough to seem eager—and then she opened the drawer where she kept her good ink and began, deliberately, to refill her pen. Let them watch her do bookkeeping while they threatened her. There were few enough pleasures left at her age, and insolence was one of them.

"I don't know what you've been told," she said. "But this is a guild of ledgers, not legends. We trade in impossibilities on paper. Left-handed miracles. Rivers sold twice. If you're looking for a storm-caller, I'd try the fens. I hear the weather's been theatrical."

"The Vellum names them," the second priest said. She hadn't marked him before—a thinner man, younger, with the restless hands of a convert. "The Thirteenth Moons. Tonight the moon makes its impossible turn, and the storm-caller stands at the seal or dies trying. We are not here to threaten you, guildmaster. We are here to save you."

"There it is," Ysolde said mildly. "I wondered when the offer would arrive. Go on, then. Price it out. I respect a man who leads with his numbers."

The first priest stepped forward and laid both hands flat on her desk, and for a moment his eyes flared true—not ember-orange but white-hot, the color of the pyres the ash-churches had fed for three hundred years before the kingdoms outlawed them and then quietly rehired them.

"The courts want the storm-caller delivered before moonrise," he said. "Refuse, and the guild loses its charter. Your doors are sealed. Your clients confiscated. Every impossibility in your inventory becomes contraband by morning, and every member of your little family becomes a fugitive with a price. Cooperate—" he slid the court ledger toward her, open to a page bearing a warrant blank except for one line, *delivered alive, terms waived* "—and the charter stands. The guild survives. All we ask is the one thing you were always going to lose anyway."

Ysolde looked at the warrant for a long moment. Then she looked up, and let him see her face without armor—the tired, lined, utterly unsentimental face of a woman who had buried three apprentices and outlived two kings and had never once confused kindness with softness.

"Do you know what my guild does," she said, "when someone tries to buy one of ours?"

The priests waited.

"We sell it back to them," she said, "at an obscene markup. That's the business. But there's a clause in our founding charter—written by a madwoman with a stolen printing press, and upheld by every court since because none of them could prove she was joking—that says the guild does not sell its members. Clients, yes. Patrons, certainly. Members never." She closed the court ledger with one finger and pushed it back across the desk. "August Eriksen signed the membership roll eleven years ago. Ink, blood, and witness. You're not asking me for a delivery. You're asking me to default on the only debt this house has ever treated as sacred."

"They are a weapon," the young priest said, and his voice cracked with something almost like pity. "A century-old weapon left cocked. When the seal breaks tonight, everything the old war buried comes up with it. You've seen the fens, guildmaster. You've heard what's waking. One person's life against the peace of ten kingdoms—"

"Yes," Ysolde agreed. "That is exactly the arithmetic they used on the exiles. Did you know that? Did your church teach you where the seal gets its supper?" She watched the flicker pass between the three of them—there it was, the flinch, the tell. So they knew. Some of them knew. "Ah. You did know. And you came here anyway, to offer me the guild's survival in exchange for feeding one more name into the machine. Well." She rose, finally, and the room seemed to rearrange itself around her, the way rooms do around certain kinds of anger. "Let me tell you about survival."

She walked to the window. Below, in the grey yard, the guild was waking—Marrow hauling crates of bottled echoes toward the vault stairs, the mute cartographer sharpening pencils with a knife meant for larger work, young Fenwick arguing with a door that only opened for liars. Her oddities. Her impossible, unbudgetable, irreplaceable family.

"For nineteen years I have kept this house alive on margins so thin the auditors wept," she said. "I have traded with slavers and saints and one very polite lich, and I have paid every debt on time, because a guild that defaults is a guild that belongs to whoever holds its paper. And in nineteen years, the single most valuable asset this house possesses—the thing that has kept us solvent, sane, and occasionally saved—is a ledger-keeper who thinks nobody notices when they stay late to fix other people's errors." She turned back to face them. "So here is my counteroffer, priests. Take your warrant. Take it to whatever tribunal sent you. And tell them the Guild of Impossible Things has reviewed their proposal and found it *insolvent*."

The first priest's eyes went white again, and this time the heat reached the air between them; the tallow candles guttered, and somewhere below a crate of bottled thunderstorms rattled on its shelf as if it had heard its name called.

"You burn," he said softly, "with your charter."

"No," Ysolde said. "You do."

She took the court ledger from the desk—it was hers now, abandoned currency, payment rendered—and held its corner over her lamp until the flame caught. The priests moved as if to stop her and stopped, because there was nowhere to stop her *to*; the warrant existed in triplicate, but the gesture was the point, and gestures were the oldest magic either of them practiced. She let it burn down to her fingers and dropped the last corner into the cold hearth, where it curled among the ashes of a hundred less honest documents.

"The offer is declined," she said. "Recorded, witnessed, and filed. Now get out of my counting house. The door will show you the way—it opens fastest for people leaving."

They went. All three, in silence, and the fog swallowed them, and Ysolde stood alone in her office with singed fingers and a heart beating slow and hard as a smith's hammer.

Then she rang the bell.

* * *

The guild assembled in the great hall within the quarter hour—all forty-one of them, from the eldest appraiser to the youngest apprentice, drawn by a bell that had not rung in full assembly since the plague winter. Ysolde stood on the counting dais and looked out at their faces: frightened, curious, half-awake. Hers. Every impossible one of them.

"Change of schedule," she said. "As of this morning, the guild is closed to new clients. Inventory audit, emergency provisions, and—" she paused, and let herself enjoy it, because if you couldn't enjoy the end of the world you shouldn't have gone into this trade "—war preparation."

A ripple went through the hall. Marrow, her foreman, a woman built like a dock crane with a heart to match, folded her enormous arms. "War with who?"

"With everyone, probably, briefly." Ysolde hopped down from the dais and began walking the rows. "The courts want August Eriksen. The ash-priests want August Eriksen. By moonrise, I expect half the clients in our books will want August Eriksen, and I intend to disappoint all of them personally. So." She stopped at the center of the floor, where the trapdoor to the deep vaults lay under its rug of woven sigils. "Marrow, break out the bottled storms—the real ones, not the decorative. Fenwick, wake the door-that-only-opens-for-liars and post it at the gate; nothing gets in that believes its own story. The cartographer—" she nodded to the silent figure already sketching, faster now, maps of the fens unfolding like wings "—knows the ways through the marsh that aren't on any map. Everyone else: arm the inventory. Everything in this house that bites, burns, remembers, or refuses, I want it awake and willing by dusk."

"And August?" Marrow asked. "Does the ledger-keeper get a vote in all this?"

Ysolde looked toward the stairs, toward the small grey room where a storm-caller had spent eleven years pretending to be arithmetic.

"August," she said, "has been voting with their feet since before any of us knew their name. Tonight we find out where they're standing. Our job is to make sure they're not standing alone when they do." She turned back to the hall, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for contracts and funerals. "Hear me, all of you. Whatever comes through those gates tonight—priests, soldiers, tribunals, worse—the guild pays its debts. Eleven years ago this house took in a stranger with burned hands and no history, and that was a debt like any other. Family is not a feeling. It is a liability entered into the books, and I have never in my life left an entry unbalanced."

She dismissed them, and the hall emptied into motion—hammering, shouting, the groan of vault doors, the smell of ozone leaking from a cracked storm-jar. War preparation in a guild of impossible things sounded, mostly, like the world's strangest household doing chores at speed.

Alone on the dais, Ysolde took out the one document she had not burned: a single sheet, copied in her own hand years ago, from a page she should never have seen. A page of brown ink, a prophecy, a date. *Tonight.*

She had known, she admitted to herself, for longer than she'd let herself know it. Had watched August suppress each flicker of static, had watched the discipline fray like rope in weather, and had said nothing, because saying it aloud would make it real and real things could be taken by courts. Cowardice, maybe. Or patience. In accounting, the two were often indistinguishable.

Outside the high windows, the sun climbed toward noon, and above the fog—though no one in the city could see it yet—the moon hung pale and wrong, a day early, turning when it should have waned.

Thirteenth turn rising. Ysolde tucked the page into her coat, over her heart, and went downstairs to count her family one last time before the world came to collect.
