## 7. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The moon had been wrong for six nights, and the fens knew it before August did.

They felt it first in their hands—the old itch beneath the skin, like static trapped in the bones, the sensation that had once preceded every storm of their childhood. August sat at the counting house window past midnight, ledger open and untouched, watching the sky over the drowned chapel road. The moon hung low and swollen there, a coin pressed too hard into wet paper. It should have been waning. It was not. It was *turning*—rotating on some axis no astronomer had ever charted, showing the sky a face it had never shown before.

The thirteenth turn.

The Vellum's ink had been dry for a century, but its arithmetic was not finished. Tonight the moon would complete an impossible rotation, and somewhere beneath the black water of the chapel fen, something ancient would feel the lock begin to slip.

August closed the ledger. Their fingers left damp prints on the cover, though they hadn't touched water.

"You've stopped pretending to work," said a voice from the doorway—Brannoc, the guild's one-eyed appraiser, holding two cups of chicory he'd clearly brewed for himself and grudgingly doubled. He set one within August's reach without ceremony. "Ysolde's been awake since dusk. She's got the oddities out of the vault and oiled. She won't say why."

"She doesn't have to," August said.

"No," Brannoc agreed, settling onto the windowsill with the ease of a man who had survived worse nights by refusing to be afraid of them. "She doesn't." He drank. The steam curled between them. "Whatever you're going to do, lad—and don't tell me you aren't going to do anything, I've balanced your ledgers for four years, I know what your handwriting looks like when you're lying—whatever it is, do it before dawn or after. Not during. During is when the guild bleeds."

It was the closest thing to a blessing the guild offered: permission wrapped in complaint.

August took the cup. Their hand trembled, and they let Brannoc see it, because hiding it would have been one more lie in a night already drowning in them.

* * *

The fens at low tide were a country of drowned teeth—black stumps of chapel spires, sunken causeways, reeds that grew too tall for any honest soil. August went down the old cartographer's map route alone, lantern hooded, boots sinking to the ankle in mud that breathed.

The first thing they noticed was the silence.

Fens were never silent. Frogs, bitterns, the endless argument of water against land—all of it was gone, as if every living thing had been told to be quiet and had obeyed out of terror rather than courtesy. In place of sound there was a hum, subsonic, felt in the molars and the sternum. The moon-seal, fraying. The century-old machinery of someone else's peace grinding against its final tooth.

Then the reeds began to move against the windless dark, and August saw the first of them wake.

It rose from a pool no deeper than a grave: a shape like an antlered elk, if elk were carved from river-clay and given too many joints, its hide slick with silt a hundred years thick. It did not see August—or rather, it saw through them, its eyes the color of moonlit milk, fixed on the horizon where the moon turned. It made no sound. It simply stood there, dripping, remembering how to exist, and then began to walk toward the drowned chapel with the patient certainty of a thing that had been summoned.

More shapes moved in the shallows beyond it. Three. Five. August counted nine before the mist closed, and stopped counting because the number had stopped mattering.

These were the war's soldiers. These were what the seal had held—not the storm itself, but everything the storm had once commanded, buried alive in peat and patience for a hundred years. And now the lock above them was failing, and they were rising to answer a call no one had thought to cancel.

*You could still walk away,* said the small voice that had kept August invisible for four years. *Let the kingdoms learn what their peace cost. Let the ancients take the harvesters' debt out of harvesters' hides.*

But August had read the mentor's confession. They knew what the risen things would do first, and it would not be justice. War did not sort the guilty from the innocent; it sorted the standing from the fallen, and the guild stood directly in the path. Ysolde, who had lied to ash-priests for them. Brannoc, who brought chicory instead of questions. The mute cartographer's apprentice who still left wildflowers on August's desk every first-day morning, believing they were anonymous.

There was no walking away from this that wasn't also walking over them.

* * *

At the chapel's drowned threshold, the water parted around a ring of pale stone, and August knelt at the edge of the seal and finally understood the choice in full.

The seal was simple, the way all cruel things are simple. A circle of moon-silver, inscribed with thirteen turns, fed by blood. Not symbolically. Not ritually. *Literally*—the grooves in the stone ran dark and wet, and when August laid two fingers in one, the stone pulled faintly at their skin like a leech testing the water. Storm-blood. The blood of exiles like them, harvested for a hundred years by whoever had inherited the mentor's terrible arithmetic, poured into these grooves a cupful at a time, keeping the ancients dreaming and the kingdoms sleeping.

The prophecy had offered August a binary, written in old ink by a hand that claimed to love them:

*On the thirteenth turn, the storm-caller feeds the seal with their own heart's blood, and dies, and the peace holds another century.*

Or:

*On the thirteenth turn, the storm-caller breaks the seal, and the war wakes, and the world judges.*

Martyr or monster. The ledger balanced either way—with August on the losing line.

And yet.

August pressed their palm flat against the stone and let themselves feel it properly for the first time: the storm inside them, the thing they had spent thirty years smothering under ledger-ink and fog and small careful lies. It was not rage. It had never been rage. It was *weather*—vast, indifferent, alive—and it had spent a lifetime being told it was a crime.

The seal wanted their death. The prophecy wanted their death. Even their exile, even the mentor's protection, had only ever been a slower way of arranging the same sacrifice: keep the storm-caller hidden until needed, then spend them.

"Cornered," August said aloud, to the fen, to the turning moon. "You've cornered me beautifully."

But a cornered thing still had four walls, and walls could be broken from any side.

Because here was what the Vellum had not accounted for—what the mentor, in all their centuries of cleverness, had never imagined: a storm-caller who had learned bookkeeping. Who had spent four years among impossible things, learning that every ledger had margins, that every contract had clauses, that every binary was just an entry waiting to be contested. The prophecy gave two endings. Fine. August had spent their whole life writing in other people's margins.

They didn't have to feed the seal their death.

They didn't have to break it gently either.

They could give it everything else—the storm itself, unbound, a century of suppressed weather released not as blood but as *thunder*. Flood the seal's grooves with living power until the ancient lock, built to sip from dying exiles, choked on the whole sky at once. Whether that shattered it or drowned it, August couldn't know. Whether the ancients woke anyway, whether the war came regardless, whether the kingdoms called it salvation or treason—

No innocent option. Only a chosen one.

Above the fens, the moon completed its turn. The light changed—silver curdling to white, white sharpening to something with edges—and across nine miles of black water, the drowned chapel rang like a struck bell.

The first of the antlered shapes reached the seal-stone and laid its clay skull upon it, waiting. Others gathered behind it. Not attacking. *Attending.* As if they remembered what a storm-caller was. As if, after a hundred years of being fed on, they had come to see whether this one would feed itself too—or finally refuse.

August stood. Mud to the knee, lantern guttering, thirty years of invisibility sloughing off like the silt on the ancients' hides.

Behind them, up the fen road, torchlight: Ysolde and the guild's oddities coming down through the mist, Brannoc's one eye gleaming, the cartographer's apprentice carrying a banner no kingdom had ever sanctioned. They had come anyway. During, not after.

August raised their hand, and the first lightning of the century answered it, crawling down the turning moon's edge like ink finding a fresh page.

"Write it down," they said—to Ysolde, to the fen, to whatever ghost still kept the mentor's books. "*The thirteenth turn: the storm-caller chose.*"

The sky opened.
