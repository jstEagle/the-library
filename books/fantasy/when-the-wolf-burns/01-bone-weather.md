## 1. Bone Weather

The storm came up the valley the way grief did: slowly at first, so you could pretend it wasn't coming, and then all at once, everywhere.

Ada Winterbourne heard it before she saw it. The dead in her workshop began to murmur—old Marta Hessel complaining of damp in her knee-bone, the drowned ferryman of the Liss muttering about water where there was no water yet—and she set down her graver and listened to them the way a farmer listens to cattle lowing before an earthquake. They always knew. Weather, sickness, the approach of strangers. The dead were a barometer of everything except their own peace.

"Rain," she told them, wiping bone dust from her fingers onto her apron. "Just rain. Nothing to sing about."

They didn't believe her either. They never did.

She went out to bring the washing in off the line while the light was still holding—that flat, bruised, greenish light that meant the ridge had decided to let weather through. Vell Hollow sat in its bowl of hills like a stone in the palm of a hand, and above it, all around, the ridge stood gray and treeless and absolute. Everyone in the valley knew what the ridge was, even if no one could quite say how they knew. Magic could not cross it. Whatever else lived in the world beyond—wonders, horrors, the King's wars, the strange bright countries on the fraying maps—it stopped at the ridgeline the way a wave stops at glass. Inside the hollow, the world was only the world. Wood burned. Water ran downhill. Bones stayed bones.

That was why Ada had come here, eleven years ago, with nothing but her tools and a head full of voices that would not stop. In Vell Hollow, the dead could whisper but not wail. Could nudge but not seize. She had carved herself a life out of that quiet, the way she carved everything: slowly, patiently, removing what didn't belong until what remained was bearable.

The washing came in smelling of iron. The first drops hit as she latched the door, fat and warm, and then the sky simply opened, and the valley disappeared into roaring gray.

* * *

Her workshop was the old smokehouse behind the house, and it smelled of tallow and juniper smoke and, underneath that, the sweet chalky scent of bone. She lit two lamps because the storm had swallowed the daylight whole, and went back to work.

The piece on her bench was for the Aldery family, who had lost their grandmother in the spring and wanted something more than the plain slate marker the valley mason provided. A memorial bone, carved from the femur of a hill ox, long cured and cleaned until it was the color of old honey. On it, Ada would render the grandmother's likeness—not her face exactly, but her hands, folded, and around the hands the small emblems of the life they'd held: a knitting needle, a spoon, a sprig of rosemary, a child's shoe. When it was done, it would be set beside the grave, and anyone who touched it would feel, faintly and briefly, the warmth of the woman who'd been. Not magic. Ada was careful about that word. The ridge saw to it that magic couldn't live here, whatever magic was. What Ada worked was older and quieter than magic—a craft of listening, of carving memory into material the way water carves a channel into stone. Bone remembered. That was all. Bone had always remembered; most people had just forgotten how to ask.

But asking was exactly what Ada did, and it was why the dead spoke to her, and why she spent a portion of nearly every day bargaining with them for quiet.

They were restless tonight. The rain hammered the roof like a fist on a door, and the voices rose under it, dozens of them, the accumulated dead of the hollow and—stranger—the dead from beyond it, the ones whose bones had been carried across the ridge over the years by travelers and traders and once, memorably, by a fleeing priest with a reliquary he'd later sell for drink. Those foreign dead were usually silent, muffled, like voices through a wall. Tonight they were not silent. Tonight they were saying something.

Ada pressed her palms flat against the bench and closed her eyes. This was the trick of it, the discipline she'd learned in the bad years: don't reach for them, don't open the door wide. Make a crack, no more. Listen through the keyhole.

"—saying it again," Marta Hessel said, right against her ear, though Marta's grave was half a mile off and her voice always sounded like dry leaves. "The new ones. The ones that came over the ridge inside the peddler's cart last month. They're saying it again."

"Saying what?"

"The name." Marta's voice dropped, which was remarkable, because Marta had never once in death lowered her voice for anyone. "The name of the place that isn't."

"There's no such thing."

"That's what makes it a name worth saying, girl."

Ada opened her eyes. The lamps guttered in a draft that shouldn't have gotten in. Outside, thunder rolled along the ridge like a barrel going downstairs, and beneath the thunder, beneath the rain, beneath even the murmur of the dead, she thought she heard—very far away, or very deep down—a sound she had no word for. Not a voice. More like the memory of a voice. Like a song sung once, generations ago, by someone who then forgot they had ever sung it.

She shook it off. She banked the lamps, covered the Aldery bone with linen, and went inside to make tea she wouldn't finish drinking.

* * *

The knocking came at the hour when the storm had worn itself down to a steady, exhausted drumming and the fire had gone to coals. Three knocks. Small ones. The kind made by a fist the size of a pear.

Ada stood in her doorway with a candle, and the wind shoved past her into the house, and standing in the shove of it was a child.

Perhaps nine years old, perhaps ten—thin as a winter branch, soaked to the color of wet slate, hair plastered flat, lips blue-tinged. A coat too big for them, military-cut but without buttons or insignia, as if someone had carefully unpicked every mark of ownership from it. The child looked up at her with eyes that were doing something Ada's eyes had never seen eyes do: they were looking at her face the way a starving person looks at a loaf, and also the way a scholar looks at a page, both at once, hunger and reading together.

"Child," Ada said. "Where in God's name—" She caught herself. "Come inside. Now, before you freeze."

The child stepped over the threshold without hesitation, which told Ada something; children who were lost hesitated, hovered, waited to be carried. This one walked like a traveler arriving at an inn. Water pooled instantly on her flagstones. The child stood dripping in her entryway, shivering hard now that shelter had permitted the shaking to begin, and looked around the room—at the dried herbs, the iron pot, the candle—and Ada watched those hungry, reading eyes take inventory of everything.

"You'll have soup," Ada said. "And dry clothes. And then you'll tell me where your people are, because I'll be bound to walk you home come morning if it's within ten miles, and if it's further we'll see."

The child said nothing. Ada assumed shock, cold, exhaustion—all reasonable things. She got the soup going, found a wool shirt of her late husband's that still smelled faintly of cedar, and turned back to find the child had not moved from the entryway. Still standing in the puddle. Watching her.

"It's all right," Ada said, gentler. "No one will hurt you here. Sit."

The child sat. Ate the soup with the controlled ferocity of someone who had learned that food might be taken away mid-meal, and ate all of it, and let Ada refill the bowl without comment. Then, with the second bowl half-finished, the child looked up and spoke.

What came out was not any language Ada knew, and Ada prided herself on knowing languages—or at least knowing the sound-shapes of them. Her gift had always been a kind of hearing, and hearing extended to tongues. She could pick out Lowlander dialects, the clipped trade-speech of the coast, the vowel-heavy pilgrim cantos, the grunted border-tongue of the shepherds beyond the south pass. She had even, in the worst of the early years, understood fragments of the dead's own speech—dead languages, the ones that survived only in graves and bones, because the dead kept speaking them long after the living forgot.

And this—the words the child said, three of them, quiet, almost conversational, as though remarking on the quality of the soup—this was one of those. This was grave-speech. This was a language that belonged to people who had been buried so long their names had worn smooth as river stones.

Ada's hands went still around her own cup.

The child watched her not-understanding, and something flickered across the small face—not disappointment exactly. Recognition, maybe. Confirmation. The child tried again, different words, same language, and then a fourth attempt in the trade-speech of the coast, halting and oddly accented, as though learned from books rather than mouths: "You. Hear. Dead?"

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged. And beneath all of it, beneath the storm and the fire and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary house, Ada felt the dead of Vell Hollow go silent—not peacefully, not the way they slept, but the way a room goes silent when a stranger walks into it. Every voice, all at once, listening.

"Yes," Ada said slowly. "I hear the dead. How do you know that?"

The child set down the spoon with great care, squared their small shoulders, and answered in the coast-trade tongue, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ice:

"I am Sef. Of Ysolde."

The name went through the house like cold water through cloth.

Ada didn't know the word. That was the strangest part—she reached for it the way she reached for any word, expecting to find it filed somewhere among all the others, and found nothing. Not unfamiliarity, which has edges, a sense of *I should know this*. Absence. A hole shaped exactly like a word, as if the name had been cut out of her knowledge by a careful knife.

Behind her eyes, in the keyhole-crack she kept between herself and the dead, something stirred. The foreign dead, the ridge-crossed dead, the ones who rode in peddlers' carts and reliquaries and travelers' keepsakes—they were speaking again, all of them at once, and this time they were not murmuring.

They were repeating it. *Ysolde. Ysolde.* Over and over, urgent, joyful, grieving, the way exiles say the name of home. And threaded through their voices, faint as a vein of silver in dark stone, came the sound she'd heard on the wind before the storm broke: the memory of a song, sung by no living throat, rising off the ridge itself.

Outside, the rain was stopping. It hadn't fallen itself out—it had simply stopped, mid-roar, as though the sky had been told to hold its tongue.

The child, Sef, looked toward the window, toward the black ridge beyond it, and said one more word in that impossible, buried, alive language. Ada didn't understand it. But the dead did, and for the first time in eleven years in Vell Hollow, they raised their voices loud enough to frighten her.

*Ysolde*, they sang. *Ysolde is coming home.*
