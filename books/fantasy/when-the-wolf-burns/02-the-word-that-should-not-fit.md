## 2. The Word That Should Not Fit

The child slept in Ada's spare room like something the storm had washed up and left behind.

Ada had not slept. She sat at her workbench with a half-carved memorial bone in her hands—a shin, someone's grandmother's shin, commissioned by a granddaughter in the lower valley—and she listened to the house. The rain had moved off toward midnight, and now there was only the drip of water from the eaves, the tick of the fire settling, and beneath it all, faint as breath on glass, the murmur of the dead who lived in her bones. They were restless tonight. They had been restless since the child arrived.

*Cold,* they whispered. *So cold, and then—*

And then nothing. A blankness in their voices that Ada had never heard before. The dead remembered everything; that was the whole trouble with them and the whole point of her trade. The dead did not forget. But when they reached for whatever had come down her road last night, their whispers slid off it like rain off oiled leather.

She set the shin bone down. It was poor work anyway; her hands kept drifting from the pattern.

The child—she still could not think of them as anything else, because no name would stay where she put it—was curled under the quilt with one hand out from the covers, palm open, fingers slightly curled. In sleep, their face lost its wariness. They looked younger than she'd guessed. Eight, maybe nine. Thin in the way of children whose hunger is old news to their bodies. Their hair was dark, cut raggedly, and there was a scab along the jaw that Ada had cleaned and salved without learning anything about its history, because the child had watched her do it with eyes like a hawk watching a trap being baited.

When Ada had asked her questions last night—*what is your name, where are your people, how did you cross the ridge*—the child had answered in a language that made the hair on Ada's arms stand up. Not because it was harsh or strange. Because it was beautiful, and because every syllable of it landed somewhere deep in Ada's chest and rang like a struck bell against a door that had been locked her whole life. She understood none of it. And yet some animal part of her understood all of it, which was worse.

Now, in the grey light, she tried again.

"Child." She kept her voice low, the way you speak to animals and to the newly bereaved. "Can you tell me your name?"

The child woke all at once, the way soldiers wake, or deer. Sat up. Looked around the room—the carved lintel, the bundles of drying herbs, the shelf of finished bones waiting for their families—and something in their shoulders eased a fraction. Not trust. Recognition, maybe. Of a trade. Of a craft that deals in the honored dead.

They touched their own chest with two fingers and said a word.

It was three syllables. Soft consonants, an opening vowel like the beginning of a yawn. And it went into Ada's ear and sat there, warm and complete, and then—

Then it was gone.

Not faded. *Gone*, the way a word goes when you wake from a dream: you know you held it, you know its shape was real, you can feel the absence of it in your mouth like a missing tooth. Ada stood very still. She said, "Say it again."

The child said it again. Ada gripped it this time, deliberately, the way she'd grip a rope over a drop. *Three syllables, starts soft.* She carried it across the room, set it on the table while she ladled porridge, turned to say *I'll call you that*—

The table was bare. Her mind was bare. There was only the knowledge that a name existed and that she had dropped it twice.

The child watched her face and, slowly, nodded. As if confirming something already known. Then they pointed at themselves, at their chest, and shook their head with a terrible small patience. *No use,* the gesture said. *Don't hurt yourself trying.*

* * *

Marta Hale kept goats above the millstream and had buried two husbands and a daughter, and she came to Ada every autumn to commission a new bone for the daughter, because grief like hers needed doing annually or it curdled. She was the most practical woman in Vell Hollow and the least likely to be troubled by anything stranger than a difficult birth.

"Foundling," Marta said, squinting at the child across her kitchen. "Happens after bad storms. River folk lose track of their little ones. We'll place them with the Corvers; they've space and patience."

"Marta," Ada said carefully. "Listen to their name."

The child, seated stiff-backed on Marta's bench with porridge untouched before them, touched their chest and spoke the three syllables.

Marta smiled kindly. "Pretty little thing, aren't they. Now—" She stopped. Blinked. "Now what were we—ah, placement. The Corvers. As I said."

"You just heard a name."

"I heard a child eat my porridge with their elbows, is what I heard." But Marta's smile had gone uncertain at the edges. "Why? Have they a name, then?"

Ada walked back down through the village with the child trailing her like a shadow that had opinions. At Tam Corver's forge she got the same result, faster: Tam heard the name, beamed, opened his mouth to repeat it, and produced instead a puzzled hum, as though reaching for a tool that wasn't on its hook. At the widow Sennet's heathery kitchen the name vanished so quickly that the widow didn't even register the gap; she simply continued a sentence begun before the child had spoken, mid-word, seamless, and Ada felt ice slide down her spine at how *smooth* it was. This was not forgetfulness. Forgetfulness stumbles. This was a door closing without a sound.

By late afternoon Ada had tried six households. Six adults, six blanks. And here was the thing that frightened her more than the blanking itself: nobody but her noticed anything wrong. The valley absorbed the erasure without a ripple, the way a pond absorbs a stone only if the stone wants to be seen.

Only the dead saw it.

All day, walking the lane between houses, Ada had felt her bones filling up with whispering, the way a cistern fills with rain. And the whispering had a single word in it, repeated over and over, in dozens of voices, in dozens of dead mouths, some of them dead four hundred years:

*Ysolde.*

She knew it was a word because her bones told her so. It fit nowhere in her knowing. It was not a person's name, not a place in the valley, not anything from any story her mother had told. It was simply a sound the dead could not stop making, urgent and grieving, like people calling a name down a well where no one answers anymore.

That evening she sat at her bench and let them speak.

She did this rarely and formally—laid out her oldest bones, the ones that had been in her family four generations, bones that had drunk enough grief to grow talkative—and pressed her palms flat against them and closed her eyes and opened the door in herself that she usually kept latched.

The dead came in like a tide.

*Ysolde,* they said. *Ysolde. Ysolde.* And then, crowding closer, their voices overlapping until the words tangled: *we had streets, we had a river with a name that isn't a river's name anymore, we had a king who was kind, we had songs, ask her, make her carry us, we are falling, we are going out like lamps, she hears, SHE HEARS—*

Ada wrenched her hands off the bones and gasped. The fire had burned low. The child stood in the doorway of the spare room, wrapped in the quilt, watching her with wide dark eyes.

"You hear them too," Ada said hoarsely. "Don't you. That's why you came to me. Out of everyone in this valley, everyone behind the ridge—you came to the one person who can hear what's happening to you."

The child didn't answer. But their chin trembled once, and they lifted one hand—not to their chest this time, not to give their name again, but out, palm up, toward the bones on the bench. Toward the dead.

And every bone in the house began to sing.

Not words. A melody, thin and wavering, rising out of the marrow of things Ada's grandfather had carved and his father before him, a tune in a mode Ada had never heard, minor and sweet, climbing toward something it never reached. A lullaby, she thought. Or a country's anthem sung quietly, so as not to wake the neighbors who had forgotten it existed.

The song lasted perhaps ten heartbeats. Then it guttered out, and in the silence after, the child spoke one word aloud, clear and deliberate, aiming it at Ada like an arrow aimed at the only target left standing:

"*Ysolde.*"

This time Ada caught it. Held it. Felt it sit in her mind, whole and burning, refusing to fade—and understood, with a dread that settled into her bones colder than any dead thing had ever made them, that the word stayed only because the child had given it directly, willfully, the way you press a coin into a drowning man's fist.

Something was eating a country. Something was eating it out of the world and out of memory both, and it had followed the last piece of that country—the last living piece—across the ridge where magic cannot go.

Outside, far off along the valley road, a lantern was coming. Steady. Unhurried. The pace of someone official.

The child saw it too, through the window. They went very still, and then they did something Ada had never seen anyone do: they looked at the lantern and made a gesture Ada understood without translation—a finger drawn across the throat, and then, after a moment's thought, a smaller motion, a finger drawn across the *mouth*. Not death first. Silence first.

Ada blew out her candles and went to stand beside the child in the dark, listening to her bones whisper *Ysolde, Ysolde* like a heartbeat, and waited for the knock.
