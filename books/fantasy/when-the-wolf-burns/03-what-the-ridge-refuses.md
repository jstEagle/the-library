## 3. What the Ridge Refuses

*The following was told to me over three nights by firelight, in a tongue only I could hear, and I have set it down as faithfully as bone remembers what flesh forgets. Where Sef's words had no shape in our language, I have carved them as best I could.* — A.W.

* * *

Sef did not remember crossing the ridge. That was the first thing they told her, and the telling itself came out strange—halting, like water poured through a cracked cup. The child sat on Ada's floor with their knees drawn up and their chin resting on them, and spoke in a language that made the fire gutter and the bones in Ada's basket hum against one another like struck teeth.

The child remembered the ridge as a wall of silence. Not quiet—silence. The difference mattered, Sef insisted, hands moving even when the mouth could not keep pace. Quiet was a room with no one in it. Silence was a room where someone had just left, and the air still held the shape of their going.

They had walked into it carrying everything Ysolde had left, which was to say: themselves.

* * *

Here is what Sef could give Ada of the journey, and Ada gave it back to me in her careful carver's cadence, each word weighed before it was set down:

Before the ridge there had been a road, and along the road there had been people, and every one of those people had looked at Sef and then, slowly, had stopped looking. Not unkindly. That was the horror of it, Sef said—or rather, this is how Ada rendered the horror, for Sef had no word for horror yet, only a gesture: both small hands pressed flat against the breastbone, pushing something down, holding it under.

The innkeeper who fed them soup forgot them between the bowl and the washing of it. A woman with a cart gave them a ride and talked the whole way about her sister's wedding, and when Sef climbed down she waved cheerfully at the empty road behind them. A priest in a gray village pressed a coin into their palm for the "poor ghost-child" he believed he was seeing, and crossed himself three times, and by morning his parish would swear no such child had passed.

Sef learned not to cry about it. Crying took water, and water was heavy.

But here—the thing Ada said she had to carve twice, because the first carving split along the grain—here is what Sef remembered most clearly, more clearly than hunger or cold or the names of any of the forgetting faces:

Every time someone forgot them, Sef felt lighter.

Not happier. Lighter. As if each forgetting lifted a stone out of their chest. And with every stone that lifted, Sef could feel what remained underneath pressing closer to the skin, like cargo shifting in a sinking hold. They were full. They understood that much, even at eight years old, even in a language they were only beginning to lose. They were full of something that did not want to be carried, and the world kept trying to take pieces of it away, and each piece it took made the rest heavier.

So they walked toward the ridge, because everyone said nothing crossed it. Nothing magical, nothing cursed, nothing hunted. Magic died on the ridge of Vell Hollow the way frost dies on a windowsill—invisible, total, without struggle. And whatever was inside Sef, whatever filled them to the brim and made people's eyes slide off them like rain off slate—that thing was magic, or magic-adjacent, or magic-enough.

If the ridge killed it, Sef thought, maybe the ridge would kill them too, and that would be fine. Or maybe it would leave them empty, and emptiness sounded like rest.

* * *

What actually happened, Sef described like this:

They climbed. The pines thinned. The air went thin and mineral, tasting of the inside of a bell. And at the top—at the exact crest, where the valley opened below like a cupped hand—they felt it begin to die.

Not them. It.

It fought, Sef said, and here the child's voice did something Ada had never heard a living voice do: it dropped an octave, all at once, into a register that belonged in a grave. It fought the way a drowning animal fights. It wrapped around Sef's ribs from the inside. It poured itself into their mouth and their ears and the soft places behind their eyes, looking for anywhere to hide, because there was no going back—the men in gray coats with the brass instruments were on the road below, climbing, patient as arithmetic—and the ridge gave it nowhere to go.

So it hid in the only place magic cannot bear to touch but also cannot entirely abandon.

A name.

Or many names. Sef pressed their hands over their sternum and looked at Ada with an expression of terrible apology, and said a word that made every memorial bone in the workshop ring at once—a clear, high, grieving chord, like a choir caught mid-breath.

Ysolde.

Ada told me later that she felt it pass through her own chest too, that word, and that for one instant she had seen—not imagined, *seen*—white towers on a green river, a bridge with nine arches, a market where they sold candles dyed the blue of deep ice. One instant. Then it was gone, and she was standing in her own smoky kitchen with a soot-streaked child watching her with ancient, exhausted eyes, and the fire was burning normally again, and she could not have described a single tower if you paid her.

That is what Sef carried across the ridge. Not contraband goods. Not a stolen relic. A country—compressed, folded, buried alive in the smallest possible vessel, in the body of its last citizen.

And the ridge could not kill it. The ridge refused it passage out of the world—but it also refused to touch it. So it passed over the crest untouched, the way a stone passes over water on a thrown arc, and came down the far side into Vell Hollow still whole, still burning quietly inside a child who was very tired and wanted, more than anything, to stop being important.

* * *

There was a second matter, which Sef explained with the flat pragmatism children reserve for facts about their own bodies.

Because the thing inside them had been squeezed and hunted and forced to hide in the marrow-deep place where names live, it had changed Sef. Before the ridge, people forgot Sef slowly, reluctantly, the way you forget a dream. After the ridge, they forgot instantly. Utterly. The innkeeper's soup, the cart-woman's wave, the priest's coin—all of that was on the near side of the mountain. On this side, Ada herself was the test case: she had forgotten Sef's name within moments of hearing it, twice, and had needed to write it in chalk on her own doorframe, and even the chalk letters seemed to blur at the edges if she looked away too long.

Sef was sorry about this. Sef said so directly, with a small formal bow that Ada suspected was drilled into Ysoltan children the way valley children were taught to curtsy. But there was nothing to be done. You cannot apologize your way out of being a vessel.

And one more thing, Sef added, and here the child hesitated for the first time in the whole long telling, fingers picking at a thread on their sleeve.

One more thing had happened at the top of the ridge. When the magic dove into the name-place to hide, Sef had heard—for just a heartbeat, just one—the other thing that was hidden there. The previous tenant, so to speak. A voice, vast and slow and patient, like something that had been waiting underground since before the kingdom had a king. And it had noticed Sef. It had said—not in words, Sef stressed, not in words, in *weight*—it had said:

*At last. Something to carry.*

Sef did not know what it meant. Sef hoped very much that Ada did.

Ada did not. But that night, alone, she went to her basket of bargaining bones and asked the dead of Vell Hollow what waited beneath the ridge, and for the first time in her life, the dead declined to answer. All of them. Every voice, every whisper, gone smooth and silent as river stones.

Whatever slept under Vell Hollow, even the dead were afraid of waking it.

* * *

The scout came on the fourth day, in the gray hour after dawn when the mist still owned the valley floor.

Sef heard him first. This surprised Ada—she had grown used to being the early-warning system of the house, tuned to the dead rather than the living, and the dead had said nothing. But Sef's head came up off the pallet by the stove like a hound's, eyes wide and fixed on the door, several minutes before hooves sounded on the frozen lane.

When Ada asked—hands, gestures, the crude shared vocabulary they were building—Sef answered with a single sign: two fingers walking, then flattening, then going still.

Riders. Stopping.

He was alone, which was somehow worse than a troop. He dismounted at the gate with the unhurried care of a man who has never once been late for anything, and tied his horse, and stood for a moment reading Ada's house the way a clerk reads a ledger. He wore a traveling coat of undyed wool, sensible boots, and at his collar, small enough to be deniable, a pin shaped like an abacus bead.

A Reckoner. Ada knew the order only by rumor: the King's auditors of persons, the men who counted souls the way tax men counted sheep. She had never seen one south of the capital. There should not have been one within a hundred miles of Vell Hollow.

She put Sef in the back room, among the hanging bones, and told them—signing it plainly, pressing her palm flat to the floor, *stay, be ground, be stone*—and went to answer the knock.

The scout was younger than she expected. Clean-shaven, mild-eyed, with the raw knuckles of someone who writes all day. He removed his hat. He smiled the smile of a man trained to seem harmless.

"Mistress Winterbourne," he said. "Bone-carver, licensed, valley of Vell Hollow." He produced a leather folio and did not open it. "Forgive the intrusion. I'm making a survey."

"A survey," Ada said.

"Of residents." He glanced past her shoulder, into the smoke-dark interior, with a gaze that catalogued rather than looked. "The Crown is conducting a reconciliation of records. Names, chiefly. There are persons abroad whose particulars are—" he considered the word, tasting it, "—irregular. Unregistered. Perhaps you've seen travelers? A child, possibly. We believe a child may have come through these parts."

Something cold moved down Ada's spine, slow as meltwater.

"Children pass through valleys," she said. "Pilgrims' brats, drovers' brats. I carve bones for whoever pays. I don't count heads."

"No," the scout agreed pleasantly. "That's my trade." He turned the folio over in his hands. "You'd be surprised how many people are irregular, mistress. A birth never recorded. A death never filed. Whole villages, sometimes, where the paperwork was lost in a war, or a flood, or—" the mild eyes came up and rested on her face with terrible gentleness, "—a change of policy. Such persons aren't punished, you understand. Nothing so dramatic. They're simply... reconciled. Brought into agreement with the record. Or the record is brought into agreement with them. Either way, everyone rests easier."

Behind the wall of Ada's body, back among the hanging bones, she heard it: Sef's breath, quick and thin. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, the memorial bones had begun to hum again—that same high grieving chord, the chord that meant one word, the word that had no business existing, rising off the carved antler like heat shimmer.

Ysolde. Ysolde. Ysolde.

The scout's head tilted, very slightly, like a bird's.

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

"Hear what?"

"That ringing. Like glass." He stepped forward onto the threshold, and Ada did not step aside, and for a moment the two of them stood toe to toe in her doorway while the bones sang their dead country's name into the morning air. Then, somewhere in the rafters, a beam settled with a crack, and the scout blinked, and the listening look drained out of his face.

"Wind in the chimney, likely," he said, and laughed at himself, apologetic. "The mountains make a man jumpy." He tipped his hat. "I'll be surveying the valley a few days. If anyone irregular comes to your door, mistress—anyone at all—you'll send word to me at the Hollow Inn."

"I live alone," Ada said, "with my bones."

"Of course," said the scout, and wrote something in the folio anyway.

She watched him ride off through the mist toward the village, unhurried, pausing at every homestead, knocking at every door. Behind her, Sef emerged from the bone-hung dark, pale as the things hanging there, and signed a question with shaking hands—the same two fingers walking, walking, then spreading wide, then wide again.

*How many of them are coming?*

And Ada, who bargained with the dead and feared almost nothing that breathed, looked down the lane at the small gray figure of the law moving house to house, and understood that the sanctuary was already broken—that the ridge had kept out magic, but had never once kept out men—and signed back the only honest answer she had.

She held up one finger.

Then, after a moment, all ten.
