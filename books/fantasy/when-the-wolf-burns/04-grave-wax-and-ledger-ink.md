## 4. Grave-Wax and Ledger Ink

The dead did not sleep in Vell Hollow. They waited.

Ada had known this since she was nine years old, since the first time she pressed her thumb into a cooling piece of grave-wax and heard her grandmother sigh through the marrow of her own wrist like wind through a reed flute. The valley's dead were patient the way stones are patient, the way winter is patient. They had nowhere else to be. But patience is not the same as peace, and in the six days since the child had arrived—six days of whispers that frayed at the edges of every carving she attempted—Ada had begun to understand that something was making them restless.

Not restless. That was too gentle a word. *Frightened.* The dead of Vell Hollow were frightened, and dead things frightened was a wrongness that sat in Ada's chest like a swallowed stone.

She rose before dawn on the seventh day and took down her father's spade.

* * *

The old burial ground lay past the mill race, where the valley floor narrowed to a throat of grey stone and the birches grew pale and close together, their leaves turning early this year, gold coins scattered across ground that no one remembered buying. There were forty-one marked graves in Vell Hollow. This was not one of them.

Ada's grandmother had told her about it exactly once, when Ada was small enough to sit inside the curve of her arm, and had sworn her to silence with the gravity of a woman passing along a curse. *There's a grave older than the kingdom, girl. Older than the ridge, maybe. We don't mark it because marking it would be asking it questions, and we don't ask questions we can't survive the answers to.*

But the child slept in her loft, breathing the slow breath of someone who has finally stopped running, and all night the bones in Ada's workshop had whispered in a language that was not whispering anymore but *insisting*, and the word they insisted upon was the same word that slid off every tongue in the valley like water off wax: *Ysolde*.

So she asked the question.

The ground gave up its secret reluctantly. The soil here was dense with clay and old iron, and each stroke of the spade rang against something buried deep—a slab, she realized, not a coffin lid but a slab, dressed stone laid flat as a table, and carved across its face in characters that made her breath stop in her throat.

She knew these characters. She had seen them three days ago, scratched into the frost of her own window by a small determined finger while she pretended not to watch. Sef's language. The language of a country that, if you asked anyone beyond the ridge—and Ada had asked, once, carefully, a wool merchant who traveled the passes—the merchant would frown, search his memory the way a man searches pockets he knows are empty, and say, *funny, I feel like I've forgotten something*, and go on selling his wool.

The slab's inscription was worn nearly smooth. Whatever tool had cut it had been fine and sure; whatever weather had erased it had been centuries of patient rain. Only one line remained legible, and even that half-swallowed:

*Here lies Mereth, who kept the door, and was not afraid.*

Below it, smaller, almost lost: *the ledger remembers what the king burns.*

Ada knelt in the wet grass for a long time, her skirts soaking through, her hands resting on cold stone, and felt the dead press close around her—not the valley dead, whose voices she knew like neighbors' footsteps, but something older, deeper, a presence beneath the presence, sediment under silt.

"Mereth," she said aloud. It was the first Ysoltan name she had ever spoken. Her mouth stumbled on it. "I'm going to open your grave. I'm sorry. I need to know."

The dead woman answered not in words but in *willingness*, which was rarer and more terrible than any words. Ada felt the permission settle over her like a hand between her shoulders, gently pushing.

She dug until noon.

* * *

They did not bury Mereth in a box. They had wrapped her—Ada understood this as she lifted away layers of material that should have rotted and hadn't—in grave-wax, whole sheets of it, beeswax darkened with ash and iron filings, pressed over the body like a second skin, like a shroud, like a page. Grave-wax preserved memory. Every carver knew that. It was why they wrote the names of the dead into it, why memorial bones were sealed in wax before interment. Wax held what flesh let go.

But no one had ever wrapped an entire person. No one had ever made a *body* into a ledger.

The wax had gone amber with age, translucent as honey at its thinnest points, and through those points Ada could see bone. A woman's bones. Small hands folded at the sternum, and in the right hand, clenched still after all these years, a stylus of black glass.

Ada should have stopped there. Every instinct trained into her by twenty years of handling the dead said: *this is a threshold, and thresholds deserve respect*. But the whispers had started again, rising from the grave-wax like heat-shimmer, and this time they were not whispering in Ysoltan. They were whispering in the valley tongue, in *her* tongue, clear as a bell rung underwater:

*Touch the wax, daughter of carvers. Read me. Someone has to read me before they come to burn me too.*

Ada pulled off her gloves.

* * *

The moment her bare palm met the amber surface, the world inverted.

She was standing in a city.

Not Vell Hollow—not anywhere she could name, and yet her bones recognized it the way a compass needle recognizes north. Towers of white stone veined with silver, so tall that clouds snagged on their crowns and unraveled into banners of mist. Bridges arcing between rooftops, hung with lanterns that burned without flame, a soft gold light like the inside of a shell. Canals where the water ran clear as glass and children leaned out of windows to drop petals onto boats that steered themselves. And everywhere—everywhere—*writing*. Carved into lintels and doorframes, painted on silk banners, traced in the canal water itself in letters that floated and reassembled. A city that had made an alphabet into an architecture.

And sound. Gods, the sound. A song moving through the streets the way blood moves through a body, sung by hundreds of voices in overlapping rounds, each voice entering at a different moment so that the melody never began and never ended, it simply *was*, ambient as weather, and Ada stood in a market square with her hand full of warm bread she had not bought and wept without knowing why, because the song contained a part written for low voices, for mothers and grandmothers, and somewhere in it she could hear—

She could hear everything. That was the horror and the glory of it. The dead woman's memory was not a picture; it was a *country*, whole and breathing, and Ada stood inside it wearing Mereth's eyes.

*This is Ysolde,* said a voice that was not quite Mereth's and not quite Ada's, but the space between them. *This is what they are eating.*

The city changed. Not fell—fell was too dramatic, too sudden, and what happened to Ysolde was neither. It *thinned*. Like ink dropped into water, like breath fading from a mirror. One morning a tower was white stone; the next it was white stone with a suggestion of grey; the next, men in the livery of a distant crown stood at its base consulting documents, and when a woman ran up screaming that they were demolishing her home, the men looked at her with genuine, baffled kindness and said, *Madam, there has never been a tower here. There has never been a here here.* And the woman opened her mouth to argue and found, Ada saw, found with a clarity that was worse than any scream, that she could not remember the tower's name. She had lived beneath it for forty years. She could not remember its name.

*The Edict,* the between-voice said. *Reconciliation, they called it. You cannot unmake a country with armies—it costs too much, it takes too long, the graves keep arguing. So they unmade it with paper instead. A decree signed in a capital that does not appear on any map of Ysolde, declaring that Ysolde never existed, and binding the declaration into law-magic so thorough that memory itself obeys. Names first. Then faces. Then songs—they burned the archives, and where the archives had been memorized, they arrested the memorizers. Then the maps. Then the language. Do you understand what it means to erase a language, carver? It means that a mother cannot sing her child to sleep. It means that grief itself becomes mute.*

The towers were gone now. The canals ran dry or didn't run at all, or perhaps had never been. In their place: fields, roads, a garrison town flying a banner Ada knew from the wool merchant's stories, and people walking through their own erasure with the placid faces of the dreaming. Here and there a figure would stop mid-stride, frown, reach for something just behind their own thoughts—and miss. Always miss. The reaching was the saddest thing Ada had ever witnessed, and she had spent her life among the grieving.

*Some of us refused to be reconciled,* the voice said, softer now. *We hid what we could. Songs in the mouths of children too young to be worth arresting. Bones in the ground. And the ledger—I was the keeper of the ledger, the true one, the one that held every name the King's clerks struck out. When they came for it, I brought it here, to your valley, where magic cannot follow, and I wrapped it around myself, and I died holding it, and I have been waiting sixty years for someone who could read me.*

The vision tilted, narrowed, became a single image held steady as a jewel turns in the light:

A door.

It stood alone in a chamber of undressed stone, older than anything, older than the city, older than the ridge. Black wood banded in silver, and carved across both panels in characters that glowed faintly, patiently, like embers that refuse to die: words in Ysoltan that Ada somehow read without knowing how, the meaning arriving directly into her blood.

*THE DOOR OUT OF YSOLDE. IT OPENS FOR THE NINE. IT OPENS ONLY ONCE.*

*Nine,* the voice repeated, and now Mereth's voice was fading, the vision dimming at its edges like a lamp running out of oil, and Ada felt the dead woman's urgency pour into her like the last of a wine poured fast before the bottle empties. *Nine founding bones, carver. The door does not answer to keys. It answers to the country itself—to the bones of the nine who founded it, carried out of the burning and hidden, scattered like seed. Find them. Gather them. While there is still a child alive who carries the last of the names. Because when the child is found—and they will find the child, they always find them—when the last citizen of Ysolde is reconciled, then the ledger closes forever, and there will be nothing left to open the door *for*.*

Darkness. Cold. The smell of wet earth and birch leaves.

Ada came back to herself kneeling beside an open grave with her palm still pressed flat to amber grave-wax, tears freezing on her cheeks in the afternoon wind, her heart hammering the rhythm of a song she could no longer quite hear but would never entirely forget.

* * *

She climbed out of the grave slowly, an old woman's climb though she was only thirty-four, and reburying Mereth took less time than opening her had—she worked quickly, gently, tamping the earth down and scattering birch leaves over the scar of it, and before she filled in the last of it she spoke to the dark below.

"I'll find them," she said. "The nine. I don't know how yet. But I'll find them."

No answer. The dead woman had given everything she had. What remained below was only rest, at last, and Ada envied her even as she mourned her.

The walk back through the birches was long and silent. Too silent. For the first time since childhood, Ada reached for the voices of the valley dead and found them huddled, muted, listening—as though every soul in Vell Hollow's soil were pressing its ear to the underside of the world, waiting to hear what she had learned.

At the edge of the trees she stopped. Her workshop chimney stood thin against the grey sky, smoke rising straight in the windless cold, and below it, in the dooryard, a small figure stood motionless in the falling gold of birch leaves, watching the road. Watching *her*. Waiting.

Sef had not left the dooryard in six days. Sef did not leave the dooryard. And yet the child stood there now with leaves in their hair and mud on their boots, and their eyes, when Ada came close enough to see them, held an expression she had seen only once before—on the face of the dead woman in her vision, the day the clerks came for the tower.

Sef knew. However the child knew—through the ledger pressed into their skull, through some thread connecting the wrapped bones of Mereth to the living marrow of the last Ysoltan—Sef knew what Ada had seen, and knew what it meant.

The child raised one small hand and pointed, not at Ada, not at the house, but east, toward the ridge, toward the world beyond the valley where magic died and the King's Reckoners moved through towns like fire through dry grain.

Then Sef spoke. One word, hoarse, in the valley tongue they were learning with terrifying speed, a word Ada had taught them herself only yesterday, chosen from all the words in the language for reasons the child had not explained:

"Hurry."
