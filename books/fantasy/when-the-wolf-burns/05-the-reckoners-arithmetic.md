## 5. The Reckoners' Arithmetic

Captain Edran Ives counted everything.

He counted the steps from the Reckoner post at Grayford to the valley road: four hundred and eleven, though the last thirty were mud that swallowed the count and gave it back wrong. He counted the men behind him — six, one of them coughing on valley air as though the very damp were insubordinate. He counted the ledgers in his saddlebag, their pages ruled in the King's own grey ink, and he counted, as he always did before an interview, the years since the Edict had been signed. Fourteen. Fourteen years of arithmetic performed upon a country until the sum came to nothing.

Ysolde never existed. That was not a lie; it was a finding. Findings did not require belief. They required only collection.

The valley of Vell Hollow lay below him like a cupped hand, green and shut against the sky. His horse balked at the ridge line — they always did, all animals did, and most men felt it too, a reluctance like walking into deep water — but Ives pressed his heels in and crossed where magic could not follow, and felt the familiar thinning, the way his own certainties went quiet and flat on the far side. He disliked the valley. Not for its defiance, which was merely geography, but because here his instruments read true and his instincts read nothing. In Vell Hollow there was no residue, no whisper of the unregistered past clinging to fence posts and well water. Whatever the ridge refused, it refused absolutely.

Which meant the child was either gone or hiding. Children who crossed into dead-magic valleys were not fleeing magic. They were fleeing what came after it.

"Captain." Corporal Henn drew up beside him, wiping rain — or sweat — from under his hat brim. "You're certain about this one? A bone-carver. Half these hollow folk are half-heathen."

"Half is generous," Ives said, and gave his horse its head down the slope.

* * *

Ada Winterbourne's house stood at the valley's crooked heart, low-roofed and smoke-stained, with racks of pale things curing under the eaves that might have been fish or might have been femurs. Bones hung in her doorway like wind chimes, and when the six Reckoners dismounted in her yard, those bones turned on their cords without any wind at all, clicking out some private alarm.

Ives noted it. He noted everything. It was the whole of his profession.

The woman herself came to the door before they knocked, which told him she had been watching them come down the valley road for the better part of an hour. She was younger than her hands, which were carver's hands, knuckled and scarred white in crescents where the blade had slipped over twenty years. Her eyes went across each of their faces in order, unhurried, cataloguing — and stopped, briefly, on Ives, with something he could not name and did not enjoy being unable to name.

"Captain," she said. "You've ridden a long way to be disappointed."

"Madam Winterbourne." He produced the warrant scroll, though he did not offer it yet; offering documents too early surrendered the rhythm of an interview. "May we come in out of the wet?"

"You may stand in my yard and say whatever you rode here to say. My house has a sick child sleeping in it."

There it was, then. Offered freely, without being asked — which meant she had already decided how much truth to spend, and had chosen the smallest coin. Ives inclined his head.

"Then I'll be brief. Three days ago a person crossed the ridge into this valley on foot, alone, aged somewhere between seven and ten years. This person is traveling without registration, without family writ, and without a name that appears in any census of the Nine Provinces." He let the pause sit. "Under the Edict of Reconciliation, such persons are classified as remnants."

"Remnants," she repeated. The word left her mouth like something spat.

"Unclaimed fragments of a former administrative entity," Ives said. He had said it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones; he could feel the smoothness now, and hated that he could. "The Crown does not punish remnants, madam. It collects them. There is a difference, and I'd ask you to believe me when I say the difference matters."

"And if I don't?"

"Then we will conduct a search of the valley, dwelling by dwelling, and the child will be found anyway, and your neighbors will remember that you delayed six officers of the King in the rain." He kept his voice level, almost gentle. Bureaucracy's great advantage over the sword was that it never needed to raise its voice. "Or you can tell me where the child sleeps, and I can tell you what amnesty looks like. I suspect you'll find it more generous than you expect."

Behind her, through the door she had left a hand's width open, Ives heard it: a sound no census would ever contain. A lullaby, hummed low, in a language that had been struck from every ledger, archive, gravestone, and tongue in the kingdom fourteen years ago.

His hand tightened on the warrant scroll before he could stop it.

The woman saw. Of course she saw. Bone-carvers made their living reading what was written under the skin.

* * *

She did invite them in, eventually — after making them wait while the rain thickened, after feeding her fire up high so the room roared, after positioning herself between the inner door and the table with the ease of long habit. Ives sat where she indicated and did not look toward the inner door, because looking was a confession and he had never confessed to anything in his life except once, and that once had been enough to teach him the cost.

He laid the scroll on her table instead, and broke the King's seal, and read aloud, because the Edict was meant to be heard rather than merely seen. Its language was old-fashioned on purpose, dressed in ceremony so that its plain cruelty would go unnoticed among the flourishes:

*"Be it known throughout the Nine Provinces and all territories thereof: that the polity formerly styled Ysolde, together with its kings, its laws, its boundaries, its archives, its songs, its saints, its debts, and its dead, is declared never to have existed. All records to the contrary are hereby adjudged forgery. All persons claiming memory to the contrary are hereby adjudged mistaken. All objects, writings, names, bones, and utterances attributable to the said non-existent polity are adjudged contraband history, and shall be collected by the Office of Reckoning for destruction or absorption into the Royal Register. So it is reckoned. So it is done."*

"Forgery," Ada Winterbourne said, when he finished. "Mistake. You've made forgetting the law itself."

"The law doesn't make anyone forget anything, madam. Forgetting is the people's own work. We merely collect what's left lying about afterward." He folded the scroll. "A country is a very heavy thing to carry. Most people set theirs down gladly, given permission. You'd be astonished how few ask where it went."

"I wouldn't be astonished at all." Her eyes were gray and flat as slate in winter. "I talk to the dead for a living, Captain. The dead in this valley have been asking me where things went since before you were swaddled. And lately—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, and he marked the stop the way a surveyor marks a boundary stone.

"Lately?" he prompted.

"Nothing. Lately nothing. Ask your next question, Captain. You have a list; I can see it behind your face."

He did have a list. It was a good list, refined over fourteen years, and it began simply: *Who else knows?*

But before he could ask it, the inner door opened, and the child came out.

* * *

Later — for years after, in fact, in the small hours when the arithmetic would not stop — Ives would return to that moment and find it wrong in a way he could not file. The child should have been frightened. Children facing six Reckoners were always frightened; fear was the correct and expected output, and Ives had built his career on the reliable machinery of other people's fear. But this child looked at the grey uniforms and the warrant scroll and the King's seal still lying in two wax halves on the table, and the child's face held something worse than fear.

Recognition. As if the child had seen this room before. As if the child had seen *him*.

"Sef," Ada Winterbourne said sharply, and the name cracked in the air between them like ice on a pond — and Ives watched it happen again, the thing his reports called *the slippage*: the name arrived in his mind whole and then began, immediately, to run out through some hole he could not locate, syllables draining away even as he grasped at them. By the time he tried to repeat it, silently, to fix it in his memory where names belonged, it was a shape without letters. A country-shaped absence in the middle of a single word.

He wrote it down anyway. That was procedure. He dipped his pen and put three marks on his slate that felt, as he made them, like writing in sand underwater.

"You see it too," the woman said quietly. "That's what happens to everyone who hears it. Everyone in this valley. Every neighbor I've taken that child to. The name goes in and won't stay."

"Because the name is contraband," Ives said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Registered persons have registered names. This child carries a name that was adjudicated out of existence. The mind rejects it the way a ledger rejects a false entry." He heard the words come out of him and understood, dimly, that he was reciting doctrine — doctrine he had spoken a thousand times, doctrine that had always been sufficient — and that for the first time it sounded to his own ear like what it was.

An excuse wearing a uniform.

The child sat down at the table uninvited, across from him, small hands folded. Up close Ives could see the exhaustion underneath the stillness, the hollows under the eyes, the wrists like kindling. And around the child's neck, on a cord, hung something wrapped in oilcloth — small, dense, the shape of a knucklebone but heavier than any animal's. His instruments, back at the post, would have screamed over that cord. Here in the valley they read nothing at all.

"That's yours?" Ives asked the child directly. He always spoke to children directly; speaking over their heads was the first mistake of amateurs.

The child looked at him and answered — not in the valley tongue, not in the King's Common Speech, but in a language of soft consonants and vowels like held breath, and though Ives could not translate a word of it, he understood the *shape* of it, the way one understands the shape of a door in the dark. It was a question. It was, he thought, with a certainty that arrived from nowhere and settled in his chest like a swallowed stone: *Do you know what you are collecting?*

"No," Ives said, aloud, in his own tongue, to a question he had not been asked in words he knew.

The child tilted their head. Waited.

"Captain," Corporal Henn said uneasily, from the corner where the men had stacked themselves like cordwood. "Sir, do you want us searching the back rooms—"

"No." Ives did not raise his voice. He rolled the slate closed. "We're leaving."

Henn blinked. The woman did not — she only watched Ives with that slate-gray attention, waiting for the rest of it, because there was always a rest of it. There was always the arithmetic.

"Here is the amnesty," Ives said, standing, gathering his gloves. "The child comes with us voluntarily — I stress *voluntarily*, madam, the distinction will matter in the record. The child enters the Royal Register as a foundling of unspecified origin. New name. New entry. Clean paper." He met her eyes. "In exchange, no search. No reconciliation proceedings against this valley, which I need hardly tell you contains more unregistered burials per acre than any district I've served. No questions about a bone-carver who bargains with the dead. The child lives, madam. Registered, renamed, raised in a provincial household with a stipend from the Crown. That is not nothing. I have seen the alternative, and I am telling you plainly: it is not nothing."

"And what happens to the child's name?" she asked. "The real one."

"It is collected," Ives said. "Like everything else."

"With the rest of it. With the graves and the songs and the—" her hand moved, taking in the hanging bones, the whole listening dark of the valley beyond her walls. "You burn countries down to a ledger line and you call the ash mercy."

"I call it arithmetic," Ives said. "The alternative is worse. I know you don't believe me. Nobody believes me until they see what happens when a country tries to keep existing without permission. What happened in the capital, madam, in the year of the Edict — what the archives did, what the *names* did, when they were left loose with no register to hold them. People drowned in their own grandparents. Children woke up speaking to mothers who had died forty years prior and could not be persuaded to leave. Memory is not a gentle thing when it has no law over it." He paused, and heard the echo of someone else saying this to him, long ago, in a voice he had spent fourteen years not thinking about. "The Edict is not cruelty. It is a dam. You may hate the dam. But do not pretend the flood was kinder."

"The dead in my valley aren't a flood," Ada Winterbourne said. "They're my neighbors."

"Then you understand better than most why they must be counted." He bowed — briefly, correctly — and moved to the door, and his men came off the walls and followed him into the yard like iron filings after a magnet.

At the threshold he stopped. He did not turn fully back; turning fully back was surrendering position. He spoke over his shoulder, to the room, to the fire, to the small folded presence at the table.

"For what it is worth — and it is worth little — the Register is not a grave. Things kept are not the same as things destroyed. I tell myself that often enough that some mornings I nearly believe it."

Behind him, softly, in the language that had been adjudicated out of existence, the child began to hum again. The same lullaby. And beneath it, faint, from the hanging bones in the doorway — from the bones themselves, which was impossible, which his instruments confirmed was impossible, which therefore was happening — Ives heard a second voice join in. Then a third. Then dozens, a congregation of cured femurs and carved skulls singing harmony in a dead tongue, and the song had words, and one of the words recurred, recurring the way a name recurs in a song sung over a cradle—

He was on his horse and three fields away before he admitted to himself that he had recognized it. Not translated it. *Recognized* it, the way one recognizes a face across a market crowd, a face belonging to someone buried so long ago the grief has fossilized.

His grandmother used to sing that. Before the Edict, before the posting, before the uniform. She had called it by a name that ran out of his memory now like water through fingers, leaving only the tune, and the tune was sitting in his chest like a splinter too deep to draw.

"Captain?" Henn asked. "Back to Grayford?"

Ives looked back once at the valley, at the crooked house with its rack of singing bones, and did the arithmetic he was paid to do. One remnant. Six officers. Fourteen years of clean ledgers.

One lullaby.

"We ride at first light," he said. "And Henn — send ahead to Grayford. I want the full collection writ drawn. Not the field form. The *full* writ, with seizure authority over the entire valley."

"Sir?" Henn's face paled under the brim of his hat. "The whole valley? On one child?"

"On one child," Ives agreed, and touched his heels to his horse, and did not look back again. "Everything is always on one child. That's how countries end. That's how they start, too — though the Edict doesn't concern itself with starting."

He rode north through the rain toward Grayford and its grey ledgers, humming — quietly, so that no one heard — a song that officially did not exist.
