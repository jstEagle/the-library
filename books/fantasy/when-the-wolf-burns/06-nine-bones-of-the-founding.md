## 6. Nine Bones of the Founding

The dead came to Ada the way weather came to Vell Hollow: without permission, and all at once when they chose.

She had spent the morning at her workbench with a shinbone that belonged to old Marta Corrow, smoothing it into a memorial rod so that Marta's daughters would have something to hold at the graveside—something warm from the lathe, something that still smelled faintly of the woman's kitchen smoke, because bone kept what flesh surrendered. Outside, the storm from three nights past had finally wrung itself dry, and the valley lay rinsed and green, dripping from every ledge of the ridge like a thing newly born.

The dead were restless. She could feel them in the marrow she worked, a low murmuring like bees under snow, and beneath the murmuring a single syllable repeated until it wore a groove in her attention: *Ysolde. Ysolde. Ysolde.*

They had been saying it since the child arrived. They said it in Marta's bone. They said it in the box of uncarved femurs by the window. They said it in the teeth strung above her door, which had hung silent for eleven years and now clicked together in the windless room like a jaw remembering how to speak.

"Quiet," Ada told them, the way she told them every morning. "I'm working."

*Ysolde,* said the dead, with the patience of those who have nothing left but time.

She set down her knife. There was no point carving through that kind of chorus; the blade would wander, and a wandering blade on memorial bone made wandering souls, or so her mother had claimed, and her mother had been wrong about most things but not about bones.

Sef was asleep on the pallet by the hearth, curled around the carved slate Ada had given them, chalk stub clutched in one fist even in sleep. The child slept the way refugees sleep—not deeply, not trustingly, but in the shallow water of exhaustion, ready to swim for shore at any sound. Even now their brow was furrowed, as though the dreams were also in exile.

Ada wrapped her shawl around her shoulders and went out to the yard, where the ground was soft and the oldest graves waited, because if the dead wanted a conversation, they would have one on her terms, standing up, in daylight.

* * *

Vell Hollow's churchyard was small and crooked, its stones leaning together like conspirators, and beyond the wall the older burials began—the ones without stones, marked only by rings of white river pebbles, going back further than any record in the parish chest. Her family had kept this ground for six generations. Bone-carving ran in the blood the way deafness runs in some families: a gift, a burden, an inheritance no one asked for.

She knelt at the ring of pebbles that marked the grave older than the kingdom—the one whose occupant had shown her Ysolde whole, cities and songs and a door—and pressed her palms flat against the earth.

The cold came up through her hands immediately, the particular cold of the long-dead, who do not chill you so much as *subtract* warmth, who take heat the way a ledger takes figures. And then the voice, which was less a voice than a certainty arriving in her skull fully formed:

*Nine.*

"One what?" Ada said aloud, because speaking helped her hold the shape of what she heard, the way a frame holds wet plaster.

*Nine bones,* said the dead woman, whose name Ada could never quite catch—it slid away like a fish in dark water, another casualty of whatever hunger was eating Ysolde out of the world. *Nine bones of the founding. A country is not built. It is set together, joint to joint, as you set a skeleton. And what is set together can be taken apart—and what is taken apart can be set again.*

"I'm a carver," Ada said. "Not a joiner."

*You are both. You have always been both. Why else did the child come to your door?*

The wind moved through the churchyard and the teeth above her door, far behind her, chattered their agreement. Ada sat back on her heels. Mud soaked through her skirt. Somewhere upslope a wood pigeon called its two-note question over and over, and got no answer, and asked again.

"Tell me plainly," she said. "Where are these bones?"

And the dead woman showed her.

It was not like seeing. It was like being *reminded* of things she had never known—a sensation Ada had no better word for, a recognition without acquaintance, the mind opening doors into rooms it had never built. She saw the first bone: here in the valley, in the parish church, worked into the crook of the shepherd's crook that had lain in the vestry cabinet for two hundred years, mistaken for antler. She saw the second: sunk in the millpond, inside the belly of a pike that refused to die and refused to be caught, generation after generation of boys losing hooks to it. The third lay under the hill, in the salt mine where the entombed men still sang, though only she could hear them. The fourth—

The fourth was beyond the ridge.

Ada's breath stopped in her throat. In the vision the ridge stood against a sky she had never seen except from below: a black-green spine of stone and pine, and beyond it a country of roads and towns and rivers with names, a world that went on and did not end at Vell Hollow's fence lines.

She had never left the valley. No one left the valley. That was the entire meaning of Vell Hollow—that magic could not cross the ridge, that the dead spoke here and nowhere else, that a bone-carver could live out her whole life in the company of ghosts and call it ordinary. It was not a law. It was not a wall. It was simply the shape of the world, the way water is shaped by its bed, and she had never once thought of herself as anything other than water in this particular hollow.

*Four lies beyond the ridge,* said the dead woman, merciless as arithmetic. *As does five. Six sleeps in a riverbed two days' walk south, in the country they call Marchmain. Seven was carried off in a reliquary and sits on an altar wearing the name of a saint it never met. Eight—*

The vision shuddered. Ada felt it shudder, felt the dead woman flinch the way a sleeper flinches from a nightmare, and when the certainty returned it came slower, grayer.

*Eight is held by the ones in grey. The Reckoners keep it in their strongroom, filed like a debt.*

"And the ninth?"

Silence. Long silence, long enough that Ada opened her eyes and looked at the pebble ring, at the wet grass, at the ordinary green world, and thought the dead woman had gone wherever answers go when they become too expensive.

Then, quietly, with something that in the living might have been pity:

*The ninth I cannot show you. The ninth is not lost. The ninth is walking.*

* * *

She found Sef awake, sitting up on the pallet with the slate across their knees, and the child looked at her face and knew—children always know—that the day had changed shape. Ada lit the stove and put the kettle on because some rituals hold a person together, and while the water heated she took the chalk herself and drew on the slate: nine small shapes, joint to joint, a stick-figure skeleton of a country.

Then she pointed at each in turn and pointed out the window: at the church, at the millpond, at the hill, at the ridge.

Sef went very still.

The child rose from the pallet without hurry, which was unlike them—everything about Sef was quick, darting, a wren of a person—but this they did slowly, reverently, the way one approaches an altar. They touched each drawn bone in turn. At the fourth, the one pointing toward the ridge, their finger stopped, and trembled, and lifted.

They looked at Ada.

And Ada looked back, and understood what was being asked, and hated the understanding, because she had known it was coming since the night the child appeared in her doorway speaking a language no one alive should know. The valley had kept her safe her whole life. Safe and small and complete, a life measured out in memorial rods and grave bargains, and she had told herself it was enough, and it had been enough, right up until the moment it wasn't.

"If we don't," she said aloud, to the child, to the dead, to the clicking teeth above her door, "they finish erasing it. All of it. Everyone." She thought of the vision—Ysolde whole, cities full of people singing songs that were already half-gone from the world, a door standing somewhere in the dark under the hills. She thought of Captain Ives and his ledger and his terrible, reasonable voice. "And they won't stop with Ysolde. Erasing is a habit. It gets easier every time."

Sef couldn't have understood the words. But the child watched her mouth move and read something in it, or perhaps read it in the slate, in the nine bones drawn joint to joint, in the finger pointed at the ridge.

What happened next, Ada would remember all her life, in whatever fragments of her life remained to be remembered.

Sef picked up the chalk. The child's hand hovered over the slate—and then they spoke. Not Ysoltan. Not the dead tongue that slid through the valley like water through fingers. They spoke a word in the valley tongue, rough and misshapen, an accent like a shoe on the wrong foot, but unmistakable, the first word Sef had ever spoken in the language of Vell Hollow:

"Hurry."

One word. Cracked at the edges, worn smooth in the middle from some interior rehearsal Ada could only imagine—a child alone in the dark, mouthing the sounds of a stranger's speech, saving up a single coin to spend at the moment it mattered most.

Hurry.

"Yes," Ada said, and her voice broke on it, and she didn't care. "All right. Hurry."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the dusk, and the valley, which had sheltered them, seemed to understand that shelter was ending.

Ada packed the way her mother had taught her to pack for a death-watch: little, dry, essential. Bread and hard cheese. The good knife and the backup knife. Flint and steel. A coil of cord. Her father's oilskin coat, which still fit her shoulders and still smelled, faintly and impossibly, of him—of pipe smoke and lye soap and the tallow he used on the lathe. She hesitated over the memorial rods finished and waiting for delivery, and then took them anyway, because the dead they honored deserved to travel too, and because she had a sudden superstitious conviction that she would need every friend, living or otherwise, on the far side of that ridge.

To the dead of the churchyard she left instructions, spoken low over the pebble rings while Sef watched the road with a sentry's eyes. Watch my house. Watch my tools. If the grey ones come asking, forget me—you're all very good at forgetting, it seems to run in this valley same as the carving does. And if I don't come back—

She couldn't finish it. The dead filled in the silence themselves, gently, the way family does:

*We will keep your place. We have kept worse.*

From the vestry cabinet she took the shepherd's crook, and the verger asked no questions because Ada Winterbourne was the sort of woman no one asked questions of, and besides, the verger owed her for his mother's memorial rod, and debts in Vell Hollow ran deeper than curiosity. The crook's crook was heavy in a way antler wasn't, and when her thumb passed over it she felt the faintest pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. The first bone. One of nine. It had been hiding in plain sight for two centuries, patient as only the dead are patient, waiting for someone to count.

The millpond next, she thought. Then the mine. Then—

Then the ridge. She stood at her gate in the last light and looked at it: the black spine against a bruised purple sky, pine trees jagged along its crest like a broken comb. All her life it had been the edge of everything, the rim of the bowl, the horizon's honest answer. Tomorrow it would be a threshold. Her stomach turned over, slow and cold, and she let it, because fear was just the body's way of taking something seriously.

Sef stood beside her, wearing the coat Ada had cut down for them, the chalk slate slung across their back like a soldier's pack. On the slate, in the child's careful hand, were written nine marks—eight crossed through with nothing yet, and one, the first, ringed.

A man came up the lane as the full dark settled. Old Tam Brightwater, the miller, lantern swinging, breath steaming, calling her name before he even reached the gate. Ada's hand found Sef's shoulder and stayed there.

"Isa! Isa Winterbourne—" Tam arrived puffing, and his lantern light caught his face, and Ada saw before he spoke that the news was bad, the way you see weather coming over a ridge. "There's strangers on the south road. Three of 'em, maybe four, in grey coats, riding quiet. Asking after—well. Asking after persons not registered. Asking after children." He wiped his mouth. "They'll reach the Hollow by morning, sure as Sunday."

Behind her, in the dark, small fingers closed around hers.

Sef didn't say *hurry* again. The child didn't need to.

Ada looked once more at the ridge, black against black, the whole unbeknown world folded behind it—and stepped through her gate onto the road, south first, away from the riders and toward the millpond, toward the pike that refused to die, toward the first of the nine bones and the beginning of everything.

Above her door, unseen in the dark, the strung teeth fell silent at last—as if the dead, too, were holding their breath to watch her go.
