## 7. Crossing Where Magic Dies

The ridge did not look like a wall. It looked like any other spine of grey stone and wind-bent pine, the kind of country feature a body could walk past a hundred times without thinking twice. But everyone in Vell Hollow knew what it was. The ridge was the reason they had come, generations back—the reason their great-grandmothers had carried their great-grandmothers over it in the first place. Magic could not cross it. Whatever ran beneath the world like water under ice, whatever made charms work and curses bite and dead men whisper through the marrow of a carved bone—all of it stopped at the ridgeline as if at the edge of a held breath.

Ada had lived her whole life on the quiet side of that breath.

She stood now at the foot of the sheep-track that climbed toward the notch called the Hollow Gate, and she was afraid in a way she had not been since she was small enough to believe the dark under her bed was a mouth. It was not the Reckoners she feared, though Captain Ives's arithmetic still turned over in her mind like a stone in a boot. It was not the road, or the cold snap riding down from the high country, or the fact that she had never in forty-one years slept farther from her own door than the far pasture.

It was the silence.

"You don't have to come all the way," she said to Sef, though they both knew the words were a kindness paid out to no one. "You could wait at the shepherd's hut halfway up. I'll go over alone."

Sef looked at her with the particular patience of a child who has already buried one country and does not intend to be managed by an old woman's guilt. They adjusted the strap of the bundle on their back—her tools, her spare wool, the wrapped bones of the founding set they had gathered so far—and said nothing, because Sef said nothing; the words they shared were few and hard-won, mined from the space between two languages like ore from a stingy seam.

*hurry,* Sef had said, three days ago, in the valley tongue, the first word they'd ever spoken to her in it. It had become something between a command and a prayer.

So: hurry. Ada pulled her coat tighter and started up the track.

* * *

For the first hour, the dead came with her the way they always did—not voices exactly, but pressures, weathers. She had learned long ago not to call them sounds. When she worked bone, when she held a piece of someone in her hands, what she felt was closer to temperature than speech: a warmth of grief here, a dry wind of old anger there, and threaded through it, if she listened with the inside of herself, meanings that arrived already understood, the way you understand falling before you understand gravity.

Today the dead of Vell Hollow pressed close, and she let them. Old Marta, whose femur Ada had carved into a memorial spire eleven winters past, hummed along the edges of her awareness—a tune without words, the way Marta had hummed in life while kneading bread. The nameless infant in the cairn by the ford radiated its small blue ache. And beneath them all, faint but insistent, the whisper that had been growing for weeks now, since the child arrived: *Ysolde. Ysolde.* Not a haunting. A summons.

The track steepened. Pines gave way to birch, birch to bare granite furred with lichen the color of old blood. The wind picked up and began to talk in its ordinary way—through grass, through rock cracks, through the hollow of her own ears—and Ada realized, with a strange lurch, that she was listening to everything twice. Once with her living ears. And once with the other hearing, the deep one, which had been so constant all her life that she had stopped knowing it was there, the way a fish does not know the water.

They reached the notch a little after noon.

The Hollow Gate was a saddle of stone perhaps thirty paces wide, walled on either side by slabs of granite taller than a haywain. Wind funneled through it with a low moan, and beyond it the land dropped away into country Ada had only ever seen from a distance: a green descent into forests and fields that belonged, technically, to the King—to the same crown whose Reckoners had come asking after unregistered persons. Beyond those forests, days away, lay the border towns where, Sef had tried to tell her, things that should have been forgotten were still bought and sold.

Ada stopped at the threshold and looked back once. Vell Hollow lay below like a cupped hand: the smoke of her own chimney, the grey teeth of the memorial stones in her yard, the river stitching silver through the valley floor. Home. Every dead person she had ever bargained with, quieted, or loved was down there, humming in the ground like bees in a winter hive.

"All right," she said, mostly to herself. Her voice sounded thin in the wind. "All right."

Sef touched her sleeve. Then the child pointed through the gate, at the air itself, and made a gesture Ada had come to know: fingers spread, then closing slowly, like something being folded shut.

*Past this,* the gesture said, *it stops.*

"I know," Ada said. "I've always known. Everyone knows." But knowing a thing from the near side, the way you know a wall is load-bearing because the house stands, is different from walking through the wall and finding out what holds *you* up.

She stepped across the line.

* * *

There was no thunderclap. No shudder of stone, no tearing sensation. That was the worst of it—that it happened the way a lamp goes out, simply, completely, and leaves the dark looking exactly like the room.

One stride, and the humming was gone.

Ada stopped mid-gate, and the world went silent in a way that silence had never been silent before. Not quiet—quiet was the absence of sound, and quiet she knew, quiet she courted, quiet was the hush of snowfall over the graveyard. This was different. This was the absence of *presence*. All her life there had been a second layer to every place, a population invisible and murmuring, and now it was not muffled or distant or sleeping. It was *not*. The dead had not followed her over the ridge; the dead could not follow her over the ridge; and the part of her that had leaned against their warmth the way a house leans on its foundation found nothing there to lean against.

She swayed. The wind moaned through the gate and it was only wind, only moving air, carrying nothing, meaning nothing, going nowhere. The granite around her was only stone. Her own hands, hanging at her sides, felt like meat and bone and nothing else—as if she had spent her whole life wearing a coat she hadn't known she wore, and someone had taken it, and the cold was not on her skin but *under* it.

"Ah," she said aloud, stupidly, and then again, "ah—" because saying something, anything, proved her voice still worked even if nothing answered it.

Her knees went. She caught herself against the gate-slab, cheek to the freezing lichen, breathing in shallow pulls. This, she thought dimly, this is what it must be like for the deaf-born. Not the loss of a sense but the loss of a *world*—the sudden discovery that the world you thought was complete had a whole country in it you'd never seen, and now you were standing at its border and it had closed behind you like water.

She had spent her life among the dead. She had built her trade on them, her bargains, her reputation, her strange and solitary peace. And she had never once imagined that without them she would feel this naked, this unfinished, this much like a word with its letters pulled out of it.

A small hand took hers.

Sef had come through the gate behind her—of course the child had; she might have known better than to think a kindness would be accepted—and now Sef stood in front of her, looking up with an expression that was not pity. Pity would have been easier to bear. What was in the child's face was recognition. Sef knew this silence. Sef had been living in it, Ada understood suddenly, for a very long time—in the erased places, in the spaces where Ysolde used to be, walking through a world that had forgotten how to hold anything. The child had crossed this ridge once already, alone, smaller than this, carrying a whole dead country in their head.

Sef tugged her hand. Pointed down the far slope, where the track switchbacked into green country. Then pointed at their own chest, and then—at Ada's ear.

*I will listen for you.*

Ada laughed. It came out cracked, half sob, but it was a laugh. "You're ten years old and half my size and I can't understand most of what you say."

Sef considered this gravely. Then the child did something new: took Ada's hand and pressed it flat against their own sternum, over the heart, and held it there. Beneath the ribs, faint but unmistakable, Ada felt it—a vibration, a hum, a warmth like a banked fire. Like a bone that remembered being sung to.

Whatever Sef carried, whatever the ridge refused to touch, it lived in the child, and it had come over with them. The dead of Ysolde had not been left behind at the gate. They had crossed in the smallest possible vessel, smuggled in the body of the last citizen, and here, on the magicless side of the world, that made Sef the only door left standing.

"Then we'd best keep you close," Ada said. She straightened. Her legs shook, but they held. "Come on, then. Interpreter."

* * *

The far country was loud.

That was the thing she had not prepared for. In Vell Hollow, the living world had always seemed to her somewhat muffled, overlaid by the murmur of the dead—she had assumed that was the natural state of things, that everywhere was like her valley underneath. But walking down off the ridge into the King's country, Ada discovered that she had been wrong. Without the second hearing to absorb it, the living world roared.

Birdsong was an assault. The wind in the birches below the gate sounded like a crowd. When they passed a farmstead in late afternoon—a low stone house, geese yelling at the fence, a woman beating a rug on a line—every ordinary noise hit Ada with a raw, unmediated force, each sound arriving whole and separate and demanding, with nothing softening it, nothing answering it. She flinched at the geese. She flinched at a dog. By the time they reached the roadside shrine—an old wayside post with a tin sun nailed to it, some border-god she didn't recognize—her head ached and her nerves felt skinned.

And Sef watched her do it, and understood, and became her ears in a way Ada had not expected.

The child moved ahead of her on the track, always a few steps, always turning back. When a cart rattled up behind them, Sef raised a hand before Ada heard it. When the wind changed and carried the smell of woodsmoke and something else—something burnt and bitter, a smell that made Sef go rigid and point off eastward with a face gone grey—the child read the air and told her, in gesture and in the few words they shared, what was coming. When they stopped to rest and a crow argued overhead, Sef watched it and then looked at Ada and made a shape with their fingers that was unmistakably laughter, and Ada laughed too, helplessly, at being comforted about a crow by a mute child in a burned coat.

But it was more than warning. It was translation. Because Sef did not merely hear the loud world for her—Sef *read* it, and reading it, taught her to.

At dusk they came down into the outskirts of a market town, and Ada saw what a border town was when every place-name on its signs had been scraped off and painted over. The houses were beautiful and anonymous. The inn at the crossroads had a signboard with the name burned away and a fresh board nailed over it, blank, waiting for a name the law approved of. And yet—Sef stopped in the middle of the road and stared at a wall where the plaster had crumbled, and Ada followed the child's eyes and saw, beneath the whitewash, the ghost of older paint: letters in a script she did not know, curling and angular, half-eaten but still legible to someone.

Sef's hand rose toward the letters and stopped short of touching them, hovering, the way you hover over a sleeping face.

"That's your writing," Ada said quietly. "Underneath."

The child nodded once. Did not lower the hand.

And Ada, who had lost her dead that morning at the gate, who had been stumbling through a world too loud and too empty all day, felt the shape of the next days assemble themselves in her chest with terrible clarity. There would be more walls like this. More signs burned blank. More names under whitewash. Somewhere in these loud, magicless towns were relics—bones, the founding bones, scattered and sold and disguised—and somewhere in these towns, sooner or later, would be men in the King's grey who collected such things for burning.

The dead could not guide her here. The dead were thirty miles behind her, humming in the valley soil. Here she had only her own eyes, her own craft, her own stubbornness—and a child who heard nothing either, but who remembered everything.

An inn sign creaked above the crossroads. Ada squinted up at it and felt her stomach drop.

The blank board that had been nailed over the old name had not stayed blank. Someone—recently, by the freshness of the chalk—had written on it, in careful capital letters of the common script:

**THE MARKET OF ALMOST-NAMES — ALL WELCOME — NOTHING REAL SOLD HERE**

Beneath it, smaller, in a different hand, as if added as a joke or a dare:

*Ask for the fence.*

Sef looked at the sign. Then at her. Then the child reached into their collar and drew out the cord they wore there, and from it, the first of the nine bones—small, honey-colored, ancient—held it up to the last of the light, and closed their fist around it, and looked at Ada with an expression she was beginning to know as well as her own heartbeat.

*hurry.*

"Yes," Ada said, and turned toward the lights of the town. "I know. Hurry."
