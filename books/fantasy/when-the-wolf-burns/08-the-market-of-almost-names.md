## 8. The Market of Almost-Names

* * *

The town was called Bracken's Ford on the maps Ada had never owned, and it smelled like wet iron and frying onions and, beneath both, faintly, like a thing burning that had been burning a long time.

She had not known a town could smell like that until she crossed out of Vell Hollow for the first time in her life, down past the ridge where magic died, into a world that rang with noises she had no bones to interpret. Three days of walking, and her ears still ached with the silence where the dead should have been. It was like going deaf in one ear at a time, slowly, while someone hummed just beyond the range of hearing. She kept turning her head toward sounds that weren't there.

Sef walked beside her with the hood up, small hands buried in the too-large coat Ada had traded a carving knife for at a farmhouse. The child hadn't spoken since the ridge—not in their strange dead language, not in the valley tongue Sef was learning word by painful word—but walked close enough that their shoulder brushed Ada's hip every few steps, the way a swimmer keeps contact with something solid.

"Market first," Ada said, more to herself than to the child. "We need money before we need anything else."

They didn't have money.

They came into Bracken's Ford at dusk, when the lamps were being lit along a crooked high street, and Ada stopped so suddenly that Sef bumped into her legs.

The market was still open. Of course it was—border towns kept hours the way rivers kept courses, indifferent to what anyone wanted—and it spread down the hillside in terraced rows of stalls and awnings, hundreds of them, lit by paper lanterns that made the whole slope look like a constellation fallen and trying to climb back up. But it wasn't the size that stopped her. It was what the stalls were selling.

Candlesticks shaped like towers she recognized from a dead woman's memory. Spoons stamped with a crest—a wolf couchant under nine stars—that made her hand rise unconsciously to her collarbone. Bolt after bolt of cloth dyed a particular blue-green that no living dyer could name anymore, sold as *antique shade, origin unknown*. Little clay whistles that, when the vendor demonstrated one, produced three notes of a tune that made an old woman two stalls down stop mid-bargain and put her hand over her mouth.

"They don't know," Ada whispered. "They're selling a country as trinkets and they don't even know."

Sef had gone rigid. Through the gap of the hood, Ada saw the child's eyes moving across the stalls the way a survivor moves across a field after a fire—looking for faces, finding only shapes.

Then Sef pulled free of her and ran.

"Sef—!"

The child wove between carts and haggling farmers, small boots slapping cobblestone, and stopped at a stall near the bottom terrace where a fat man in a leather apron had laid out jewelry on velvet gone gray with age. Sef stood there shaking, pointing at a brooch—a plain silver disc worked with a spiral that opened like an eye—and making a sound that wasn't a word but was worse than any word, a thin keening that cut through the market noise and made several heads turn.

The vendor looked down at the child with the flat professional patience of a man who had seen every kind of grief pass his stall and priced it accordingly. "That one's genuine, little miss. Old work. From before." He waved a hand vaguely eastward. "Before things got tidy."

Ada arrived breathless and took Sef's shoulders. Up close she could see the brooch properly: the spiral eye, and beneath it, tiny as a fingerprint, a maker's mark in the shape of a bone needle. Ysoltan. Founding-era, if the grave-wax memory she'd carried out of Vell Hollow's oldest grave was true. Her heart was hammering somewhere up around her teeth.

"How much?" she heard herself ask.

The vendor's eyes did a slow arithmetic over her travel-stained clothes, her valley accent, her lack of any visible purse. Then he smiled the smile of a man about to enjoy himself. "For you? Forty crowns."

It was robbery. It was also, she suspected, half what it was worth, because the man had no idea what he had, and if he had known—

"I'll give you everything I have," she said, "which is nothing, and I'll owe you the rest."

The smile widened. Behind the vendor, from the shadows of the awning, another figure detached itself—a tall woman in a coat the color of dried blood, with rings on every finger and eyes like coins left out in weather. She'd been listening. She'd probably been listening since they entered the market.

"Merrin," the vendor said, with the wariness of a man greeting a creditor, "this isn't your row."

"It isn't anyone's row, Tobbe. That's the beauty of markets." The woman—Merrin—came around the stall and crouched slightly to bring her face level with Sef's hood. "Interesting child you've got. Doesn't talk?"

"Doesn't talk to strangers," Ada said.

"Mm. And yet I'd swear I've seen those eyes before. Or rather—" Merrin tilted her head, and something flickered behind the coin-colored irises, a hesitation, a snag. "I'd swear I haven't. That's rarer than you'd think. Everyone's been seen by somebody." She straightened, and her attention slid back to Ada with the smoothness of water finding its level. "You want the brooch. You want it badly enough to haggle while bleeding from a dozen invisible wounds. So let's skip to the part where I make you an offer, because Tobbe here will bleed you dry and then sell your boots."

"And who are you exactly?"

"A fence." Merrin said it the way a priest says her order's name—with pride, and a warning underneath. "Of a particular kind. Tobbe sells objects. I sell what objects carry. Memories, mostly. Stolen ones, abandoned ones, ones people paid to be rid of. There are more buyers for forgetting than for remembering, friend. Forgetting is lighter to carry home."

The lantern light caught the rings on her fingers, and Ada saw now that each band was set not with stones but with small panes of cloudy glass, and behind each pane something moved. A smear of gold that might have been sunlight on wheat. A flicker of dark hair. A shape that might have been a door.

Her stomach turned. In the valley, among the dead she bargained with, memory was sacred residue—the last honest thing a person left. Here it hung on a woman's fingers like costume jewelry.

"You want payment," Ada said carefully. "And we have none."

"I want," Merrin said, "what everyone wants. Something rare." Her gaze went again to Sef, and again snagged, and again slid off, like a nail refusing to bite wood. "But since rare isn't on offer tonight, I'll take collateral instead. The brooch costs forty crowns. You can owe me thirty-eight." She smiled. "Debts to me accrue in my preferred currency. Not coin."

"What currency?"

Merrin lifted one ringed finger and tapped it against her own temple. "I'll come collect when I need to. And believe me, I always know when I need to." She wrapped the brooch in a scrap of cloth and held it out. "Take it. Everyone pays me eventually. It's practically a religion."

Every instinct Ada owned—the instincts of a woman who had spent her life keeping accounts balanced with the dead—screamed against taking it. Debts to creatures like this were how stories began badly.

But Sef's hand had closed around the cloth-wrapped brooch already, knuckles white, and the child looked up at her with an expression that was not begging. It was worse. It was recognition. Whatever that spiral meant, Sef knew it the way you know your mother's handwriting.

Ada took the brooch.

"Wise," said Merrin, and her rings chimed softly as she withdrew into the crowd, though Ada couldn't have said whether the sound was real or remembered.

* * *

From the top of the market stairs, Captain-Adjutant Ives watched the woman and the child descend through the lantern-light, and felt the familiar cold weight settle behind his sternum where his orders lived.

He had been in Bracken's Ford four days, cataloguing. That was the polite word. The requisition forms said *recovery of unregistered historical material, Class III (erased provenance)*, and the crates stacked behind the customs house said nothing at all, being crates, and the bonfire scheduled for tomorrow morning said what it said.

He had counted, today: one hundred forty candlesticks, sixty-one spoons, four hundred yards of cloth in a dye whose formula had been reconciled out of existence, eleven hundred clay whistles. Each item logged, each log signed, each signature a small flat stone laid on the chest of a country.

His grandmother used to sing while she stirred the pot. He had thought, all his life, that the song was nothing—a nonsense melody, a private music, the kind old women make up. Yesterday, logging a whistle, he had turned it over in his hands and a junior Reckoner, bored, had squeezed it, and three notes had come out, and Ives had dropped the ledger.

The notes were the fourth, fifth, and sixth notes of his grandmother's song.

He had written in the margin of his report: *item produces tonal sequence consistent with local folk material; recommend classification as generic regional artifact.* Generic. Regional. He had stared at those two words for a long time, because he knew what they were. They were bricks. He was laying bricks in a wall, and on the other side of the wall was a song, and songs could not dig.

Now, watching the market lights, he saw the tall woman Merrin threading toward him through the crowd, and sighed, because Merrin was a splinter in the operation's thumb—she trafficked in exactly the material they burned, and she survived by being useful, and being useful to the Reckoners was a long slow way of being owned.

"Captain-Adjutant," she purred. "Shopping?"

"Inventorying. What do you want, Merrin?"

"To sell you something, naturally. Information this time. There's a woman down there just bought a founding-era piece from Tobbe—a silver brooch, founding mark and all. Paid nothing. Owes me." Merrin's smile was a knife laid flat. "A woman traveling with a mute child. And here's the interesting part, Captain, the part I give away free because I'm generous: I looked at the child twice, and twice I couldn't hold the memory of looking. My own eyes slid off. Now, I deal in memories. I know what forgetting feels like from the inside, better than anyone alive. That child isn't forgettable. That child is *reconciled*. Someone has done official work on that face."

The cold weight behind Ives's sternum shifted, grew edges.

"Description," he said.

"Tall, dark-haired, valley accent, hands of a craftsman. Moves like she's listening to something you can't hear." Merrin shrugged elaborately. "Probably nothing. Probably pilgrims. But you're paying for probable-nothings these days, aren't you? Standard rate."

He paid her, because refusing would raise more questions than paying, and wrote the description in his book in his careful clerk's hand, and told himself that a hundred women matched it, that the border was full of travelers, that the cold feeling in his chest was only the evening air coming off the river.

Below him, the lanterns of the market burned on, hundreds of small flames, and tomorrow he would feed a country into a bigger fire, and tonight he could almost hear his grandmother singing, and he could not remember, no matter how he tried, where she had been born.

That was normal. That was fine. Everyone forgot where their grandmothers were born.

Everyone.

* * *

They spent the night in the stable loft of an inn called the Drowned Bell, which Ada chose because the innkeeper was deaf and read lips with the weary precision of a man who trusted mouths least of all things. She paid for the loft with the last of the farmwife's bread, traded for three hours' mucking, and lay awake beside Sef while rain began on the roof.

In the dark, she unwrapped the brooch. Silver caught what little light came through the boards. The spiral eye. The bone-needle mark.

When she closed her hand around it, something happened that had not happened since the ridge: a voice. Faint, threadbare, like a song heard through a wall—but there. The brooch was old enough, loved enough, that it carried its dead with it, the way river stones hold summer heat.

*—and the ninth star is the singer, and the singer must be willing—*

The fragment dissolved. She gripped harder, but the voice had gone back under, leaving her with nine words and a heartbeat like a fist on a door.

"Ninth star," she breathed. "Sef. The graves said nine bones. This says nine stars. It's the same list, isn't it? The founding is a map and we've been reading it as a shopping list."

Sef sat up in the dark. Rain drummed. The child reached out and touched the brooch in Ada's palm, then touched their own chest, then—slowly, deliberately, watching her face—drew a shape in the air. A spiral. An opening eye.

Then Sef pointed east, toward the dark beyond the wall, toward the drowned country, and said the word they had taught her on the road, the first common word, the one that meant everything:

"Hurry."

Ada lay back down and did not sleep. She listened to the rain and turned Merrin's parting words over like a stone with something alive under it: *everyone pays me eventually.*

Somewhere below, in the inn, a floorboard creaked. Once. Twice. The careful tread of a man walking a room he did not sleep in, measuring it.

She had heard that tread before, years ago, in the valley, in stories travelers told about the King's men. They walk soft, the travelers said. They count your windows from the street. They write down what you are so they can later say you never were.

Ada rose without sound, woke Sef with a hand over the child's mouth and a finger to her own lips, and began, quietly, to gather the bones she carried.

Outside, the rain was stopping. In the west, over the ridge she had crossed and could never uncross, the sky was going the color of grave-wax.
