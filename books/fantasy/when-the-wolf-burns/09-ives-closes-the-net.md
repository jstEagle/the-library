## 9. Ives Closes the Net

The market of Almost-Names smelled of tallow and rain when Captain Ives rode into it at dawn, and he thought, not for the first time, that erasure had a smell. Paper left too long in damp cellars. Wax that had been poured over something warm once and never quite cooled. The border towns all carried it—the smell of things pretending they had never been anything else.

He reined in beneath the crooked sign of the Gilded Tooth, where the market's morning was already stirring: fishwives arguing over eels, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking against a scythe, two children chasing a dog through the puddle-mirror of last night's storm. Ordinary life, ordinary noise. The kingdom at its gray and grinding work.

And threaded through all of it—sold openly, brazenly, from blankets spread across the cobblestones—the contraband.

He saw them before his sergeant did: a woman selling carved bone buttons that were not buttons; a man with a tray of coins stamped with a sunburst no mint had ever struck; a stall of ribbons dyed the particular blue-green of a sea that, by law, had never touched any shore. A child's rattle made of antler, worked with nine interlocking knots.

Ives's hands went cold inside his gloves. He knew those knots. He had known them since before he could name what he knew—since childhood, since a kitchen in a village whose name had been struck from the tax rolls, since a grandmother's fingers tracing patterns in spilled flour while she hummed a tune she claimed came from nowhere at all, from nothing, from no one, *silly songs, Ivushka, pay them no mind*—

"Captain?" Sergeant Drenn pulled up beside him. "Shall we begin?"

"Yes," Ives said. His voice came out level. He had practiced that, these eleven years. "Begin."

* * *

The Edict of Reconciliation filled one page and weighed more than any book he had ever lifted. He kept a copy folded against his heart, as every Reckoner did, and sometimes at night he could feel it there like a second sternum, pressing.

Its language was beautiful. That was the horror of it, though he was careful never to think the word *horror* where anyone might hear. The Edict did not speak of slaughter or exile. It spoke of *harmony*. Of *the healing of old wounds*. Of *the merciful forgetting* extended by a gracious crown to peoples who had suffered the misfortune of having existed. Ysolde, the Edict said—or rather, did not say, could not say, for even naming the thing would be to grant it persistence—had been *a sorrow and an error*, and sorrows and errors were best mended by unmaking. Its records would burn. Its graves would be leveled and sown with new grass. Its language would pass from living mouths, and its names from living memories, until the wound closed over clean.

Ives had recited the Edict at his swearing-in. He had recited it so many times since that he dreamed in its cadences. And every morning he woke with flour-patterns on his hands, or the ghost of them, and a tune in his skull he could not write down because writing it down would make him a criminal.

*We are the cure,* his instructors had said. *It is not pleasant to cut away rot, but the limb is saved.*

He had believed them. He had needed to believe them, because the alternative was that his grandmother had died twice—once in her bed, and once again, slowly, in every ledger and archive and memory in the kingdom, her name scraped from parish rolls, her village renamed, her songs declared to have come from nowhere at all.

The alternative was that the cure was the disease.

"Captain." Drenn again, dismounted now, his men fanning out along the stalls with their requisition ledgers open. "Where do you want the pyre?"

"The well-square," Ives said. "Center of the market. Let everyone watch."

*Let me watch,* he did not say. *Let me see exactly what I am.*

* * *

They began, as procedure demanded, with inventory. This was the part the manuals loved: the careful cataloguing of the condemned. Each relic was to be examined, classified, entered into the ledger under its proper heading—*objets of proscribed provenance, category three, historical contraband, non-restorable*—and only then committed to flame. The bureaucracy of destruction was meticulous. It had to be. Carelessness implied that the things being destroyed mattered, and nothing mattered, nothing had ever existed, and the ledger proved it page by page.

Ives stood over the blanket-stalls with his ledger open while his men brought items to him one by one.

A hand-bell of green bronze, its clapper shaped like a swallow. Category three. He turned it in his hands and heard—not rang it, he did not ring it, ringing proscribed instruments was itself an offense—but heard anyway, in imagination, the note it would give. His grandmother had owned a bell like this. It had hung in the doorway to keep the flies out, and when the wind moved it, she would close her eyes for half a breath, just half a breath, and then go back to her bread.

"Provenance?" asked Corporal Hale, pen ready.

"Unknown," Ives said. "Recovered goods. Enter it."

A wedding circlet, silver gone black, worked with the nine-knot pattern. Inside the band, worn nearly smooth, letters in a script that had been illegal for thirty years. Ives read them without meaning to—he had taught himself the letters secretly, at fourteen, copying from a stolen primer he later burned himself, burned it with his own hands in the yard behind the barracks—and the words said *my light returns to me*.

Whose light? Whose returning? Some bride dead now, some groom scattered into anonymous grass. The circlet had outlived its meaning and now it would outlive its metal too.

"Category three," he said. His voice held. His hands did not entirely, and he tucked the tremor against his thigh where Hale could not see it. "Enter it."

The morning wore on. The traders watched from doorways with the flat expressions of people who had seen this before and would see it again; the market of Almost-Names sat astride three borders, and Reckoners came through twice a year like weather. A fishwife spat into the gutter as Hale passed. No one resisted. Resistance confirmed that the objects meant something, and meaning was the crime.

By midday the pyre in the well-square stood taller than a man: bells and bones and ribbon-dye and coinage and songbooks with the words scratched out but the staff-lines left, as if music without language could be permitted to survive. As if melody alone were innocent. Ives had argued that point once, early, in training—*surely a tune harms no one*—and had received a week of penance duty and a lecture he still remembered verbatim: *Reckoner Ives, a tune is a vessel. Empty vessels are refilled. You do not leave cups standing in plague houses.*

He had stopped asking questions after that. Mostly.

"Captain." Hale appeared at his elbow, and there was something odd in the young corporal's face—excitement banked down to professionalism, but glowing through. "You'll want to see this one. We found it in the back room of the fence's shop. Wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards."

Hale carried a box, and in the box, nested in straw, lay five objects, and Ives's blood went to ice water.

Bones. Five of them, each perhaps the length of a forearm, each carved along its length with knotwork so fine it looked like frost, and each humming—Ives did not have another word for it, though he would deny the word if asked—humming with a pressure he felt in his teeth. They were warm. He knew they were warm without touching them, the way you know a stove has been lit in a house you've just entered.

Founding relics. The term existed in the deeper manuals, the ones Reckoners were cleared to read and forbidden to discuss: fragments associated with the pre-Reconciliation culture, artifacts of unusual persistence, *to be recovered at highest priority and destroyed by fire in the presence of two officers of rank.*

Highest priority. Which meant someone above him had known such things might surface here. Which meant the net closing around this market was not routine weather but a hunt, and hunts had quarries.

"These weren't here six months ago," Ives said quietly. "The last sweep took everything. So either the fence found new stock—" and fences did not find founding relics, founding relics *found* reasons to exist— "or something is collecting them. Someone is assembling these."

"You think there's a buyer?"

"I think there's a purpose." He wrapped the oilcloth back around them, and the warmth of the bones came through the cloth and through his gloves, and for one vertiginous moment he was seven years old again, holding his grandmother's hand in a cellar while boots crossed the floorboards overhead, and she pressed her palm over his mouth—not to silence him, he had made no sound—to keep in the breath she was afraid would become a word. *Say nothing, Ivushka. What isn't spoken can't be taken.* 

She had been wrong. Everything got taken anyway. But God help him, he had built a career on her advice.

"Two officers of rank," he repeated aloud. "You and I will witness the burning personally. These don't go in the general pyre. Separate fire. Now."

* * *

The separate fire was small and hot, kindled in an iron brazier behind the Gilded Tooth, away from the crowd. Procedure. Ives had written some of the procedures himself, in the years when writing procedures felt like building a dam—channeling the flood into channels, making the destruction orderly, orderly, *orderly*, as if order were the same thing as mercy.

He fed the first bone into the coals himself.

It did not burn the way bone burns. Bone burns reluctantly, greasily, with a smell like the inside of a tooth. These burned like paper soaked in lamp-oil, like a wick finding flame, and as the fire took it, a sound rose out of the brazier—a single clear tone, high and sweet, like wet glass rimmed by a finger, and it went through the market square and through Ives's chest and through the place behind his breastbone where the Edict lay folded, and somewhere three streets away a dog began to howl, and a fishwife dropped her basket, and Ives's grandmother's lullaby rose in his memory whole and complete for the first time in twenty years, every verse, every turn of phrase, in a language the kingdom had decreed had never existed.

The second bone burned quieter. The third made the flames bend sideways, against the wind. The fourth wept—a bead of molten something that ran from the fire and cooled on the cobbles into a shape like a small closed hand.

By the fifth, Hale had backed away to the far wall and was crossing himself, which was itself irregular, because the church had signed the Concordat and officially acknowledged that no proscribed history had ever—

"Burn it," Ives said. "All of it."

His own hands were shaking so badly he could barely manage the tongs. He told himself it was the heat. Eleven years of practice, and still he told himself it was the heat, and still, in the ledger that evening, under the entry for the five founding relics, his pen hesitated over the classification column, hovering, hovering, before writing *category three* in a hand that did not look like his own.

*Category three. Non-restorable.* 

Nothing was restorable. That was the entire architecture of the peace. That was why his grandmother's grave bore a stone with another family's name on it, why the village of his childhood appeared on no map older than fifteen years, why the lullaby lived only in the locked room of his skull where singing it aloud would cost him his rank, his freedom, possibly his life—and yet the bones had sung louder than any law, and the fire had not silenced them but *amplified* them, and he had stood witness, officer of rank, required presence, and he had listened with his whole traitor heart.

*The law devours even its servants.* His grandmother used to say that about winter. *Winter eats the wolf same as the sheep, Ivushka. Best to be the fire instead.* She had laughed when she said it. He had not understood her until this afternoon, watching a country burn one bone at a time and feeling it burn a matching hole in him.

* * *

Dusk came down on the market of Almost-Names, and the ash of a nation drifted through the streets like dirty snow. The traders reopened their stalls around the scorched circle in the well-square, because hunger outlasts grief, and sold their eels and their knives and their almost-nothing trinkets, and the kingdom went on breathing.

Ives sat in the Gilded Tooth's back room with the day's reports and did not read them. Before him lay the interrogation notes from the fence—a sweating man named Osk who had given up his suppliers in an hour and wept about his daughter—and among the papers, a single sheet that had arrived by courier at noon, sealed with the Chancellor's own cipher.

*Intelligence confirms movement of founding relics through the western districts. Quarry identified: one (1) adult female, bone-worker by trade, traveling with one (1) minor of interest, designation LEDGER. Subject believed heading south toward the salt workings. All officers to treat recovery of LEDGER as priority absolute. Note well: the Ledger must be taken alive and intact. Destruction of the Ledger constitutes treason against the Crown.*

*Sef,* the report did not say, because the name could not be written, because the name erased itself from every eye that read it, and so they had given the child a function instead of a name. *Ledger.* Every erased name pressed into one small skull, walking around loose in the world, heading south, toward the mines, toward the choir of buried singers Ives had read about in files he should never have opened.

Toward the salt workings. Where his grandmother's people had labored, once, in a country that had never existed.

He should feel satisfaction. The net was closing; the quarry was flushed; the Edict advanced another inch toward its perfect harmony. Instead he sat in the lamplight with his shaking hands flat on the table and understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only at dusk after a day of burning, what the Chancellor's cipher really meant by *alive and intact.* They did not want to destroy the child. They wanted to *empty* the child—the way they had emptied the bell, the way they had emptied the circlet of its bride, the way they had emptied him, year by year, of everything except the procedure. A living ledger was useful. A living ledger, harvested, would let them erase the last of Ysolde from the inside of the only mind that still held it.

And the bone-worker. The reports described her: hears the dead, they said, in the flat clinical language of intelligence summaries; travels with the subject; considered dangerous. Ives had read the file three times. Somewhere between the lines, between *adult female* and *bone-worker by trade*, he kept glimpsing something the file could not contain—someone who bargained with the erased, who listened to them, who perhaps gave them the dignity of being heard. The kind of person his grandmother would have called *one of ours* without knowing why.

He snuffed the lamp. In the dark of the back room, very quietly, certain no one could hear, Captain Ives of the King's Reckoners began to hum.

Three notes. Only three. The opening of a lullaby from nowhere, from no one, from a country that had never existed—and then he stopped, appalled at himself, and reached instinctively for the folded Edict against his heart, the way a man reaches for a crucifix.

But the Edict was paper, and paper had burned today too, and what stayed with him in the dark was the sound the fifth bone had made as it died: a tone like a door opening, going south, going down, going deep beneath the earth where the miners sang.

He would ride at dawn. He would follow the orders. He told himself he would follow the orders.

Outside, the ash was still falling, and it settled on the empty brazier like snow on a hearth that refused to go cold.
