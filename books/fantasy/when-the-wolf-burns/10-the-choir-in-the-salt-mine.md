## 10. The Choir in the Salt Mine

The mine announced itself long before they saw it.

They heard it first as a wrongness in the wind—a note held too steady, too patient, threading through the crags above the grey lake where the road gave out. Then the smell: salt and old timber, and beneath both something sweeter, like candle wax burned in a closed room for a hundred years.

Ada stopped at the lip of the slope and pressed her palm against the earth, as she had done ten thousand times in Vell Hollow before she spoke to any of the dead.

The ground answered in chorus.

*Oh,* said the mountain, in forty voices at once. *Oh, oh, you've come at last. You've come down to us. Come down, come down, it's warm below, come down.*

She snatched her hand back as if scalded. In the valley, the dead spoke one at a time, politely, each waiting its turn like mourners at a graveside. Here they spoke together, all of them, always, a congregation that had never been dismissed.

"Ada?" Sef stood a few paces behind her with the pack slung high on their small shoulders, watching her face the way travelers watch weather. Two weeks on the road had taught the child to read her silences.

"There are people under there," she said. "A great many people. And none of them have stopped singing."

Sef's eyes went to the black mouth of the adit, half-swallowed by broom and rusted rail, and something moved across their face that was older than their years—the look of someone recognizing a house from a painting of its burning.

*Ysolde,* the dead whispered through the soles of her boots, though she hadn't touched the ground again. *Ysolde, Ysolde, you're wearing bone. Bone-carrier, bone-hearer, come down, we have something of yours.*

"It's here," she told Sef. "The third bone. It's been down there the whole time, singing."

* * *

The shaft swallowed the daylight in twenty steps, then thirty, then not at all.

Ada lit the lamp she'd traded two carved memorial beads for in the last village—a village, she'd noted with a chill, whose church records listed no founding date, no first families, no history older than the Edict, as if the town too had been scraped smooth by the Reckoners' arithmetic. The flame threw their shadows huge against the timber props, and the props were carved. She saw that within the first hundred yards. Every beam, every strut, every shoring post carried lines of the hooked, flowing script she now knew better than her own name.

Ysoltan. Prayers, probably. Names, certainly. The miners had carved their names into the wood that held the mountain off their backs, so that even crushed, even erased from every ledger in the kingdom, the mountain itself would know who had died beneath it.

"They wrote on everything," Sef murmured, trailing their fingers along a beam. "My mother said—" The child stopped, jaw tightening. Sef rationed memories the way a starving traveler rations bread, and Ada had learned not to reach for them.

"Say it anyway," Ada said gently. "Out loud. While I can still carry it."

Sef looked at her a long moment in the lamplight. "My mother said a country isn't its borders. It's what its people bother to write down. So we wrote on everything."

The song grew as they descended. Not louder—that was the terrible thing, it never grew louder, only closer, more present, until Ada understood it wasn't coming from deeper in the dark at all. It was coming from everywhere. From the walls. From the salt itself.

Salt preserved. Everyone knew that. Salt kept meat through winter, kept flesh from rotting, kept things *as they were*. And somewhere below them, sealed in a gallery when the mountain came down three generations ago—or four, or five, time went strange in this place—the Ysoltan miners had been preserved mid-song, because they had been singing when the earth fell, and the salt had taken them whole, and the dead did not stop singing just because the Reckoners had struck their names from every register in the realm.

Ada could hear the words now. That was the worst of it. She had expected grief-murmur, the wordless keening of the unquiet. But this was a working song, an ordinary song, a counting song—the kind men and women sing in shifts to keep the rhythm of picks, to make the hours pass, to remind each other they are still many and not alone.

*One for the seam and two for the vein,
three for my brothers who won't feel the rain,
four for the lamplight, five for the loaf,
six for the ones who are already home—*

They had changed the words somewhere in the last verse. Ada realized it gradually, the way you realize water has soaked your boots. The song had begun as a living song and had become, over the decades, something else. Each generation of singers had added verses. Verses about the dark. About the wait. About the country that had been decreed out of existence while they lay underground, so that when rescuers finally drilled down—*if* anyone ever drilled—they would dig toward a nation that officially had never been, and the miners would have no names to be buried under, no flag, no parish record, nothing but the song.

So they sang the country back into being, verse by verse, in the only place the King's clerks couldn't reach.

Ada leaned against a prop and wept without noticing she was doing it, until Sef took her hand.

* * *

The gallery opened like a cathedral.

Their lamplight couldn't find its far walls. Pillars of undisturbed salt rose into the dark, translucent as frozen honey, and set into those pillars—no. Not set into. *Inside.* Figures. Dozens of them. Miners caught in the moment of the collapse, some crouched, some reaching upward, tools still in their hands, and every one of them shining faintly from within, lit by whatever it is that keeps the dead tethered when they refuse to go.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of it. They were beautiful and they were intact and they had been aware, all this time, singing in the dark, waiting for someone who could hear them.

One figure stood apart from the rest, nearer the center pillar, arms raised not in fear but in song, head tipped back. A woman. Young. Her braid had come loose and hung in a salt-bright rope past her shoulder, and around her wrist, unmistakable even through the crystal, was a bracelet of carved bone.

The third founding bone.

"The dead keep what matters," Ada breathed. "I should have known. Of course they'd hold it. Of course they'd make it a *song*."

She approached the pillar slowly, lamp raised, and did what she had done all her life in Vell Hollow: she introduced herself to the dead.

"My name is Ada Winterbourne. I'm a bone-carver, from the valley over the ridge. I hear you. I hear all of you. I've come for the bracelet on the woman's wrist—it belongs to a door, a door out of a country that's been buried alive, same as you. I need it. And I'll tell you true: taking it will hurt you, and I'm sorry for that before it's done."

The chorus faltered. Forty voices, sixty, hesitating on the half-line like a congregation losing its place.

Then the woman in the salt turned her head.

Not much. An inch, maybe two, crystal creaking like river ice. Her eyes found Ada through the amber wall, and they were open, and they had been open for eighty years, and they were full of such patient, bottomless sorrow that Ada felt her knees loosen.

*You hear us,* the woman said, and her voice came alone now, separated from the chorus, and it was worse than all of them together. *Bone-hearer. You actually hear us. Eighty years we've sung and no one has heard us. Do you know what it is, to sing into stone?*

"I know what it is to speak to people who can't answer," Ada said. "It's why I do the other part. The listening."

*We know the bracelet you mean.* The woman's gaze drifted down to her own wrist, encased in salt. *We passed it among ourselves, hand to hand, for three generations, so the mountain couldn't swallow it and the clerks couldn't burn it. Whoever held it added their name-song to the choir. That was the bargain. That was how we kept it safe.* Her voice cracked like settling rock. *If you take it, bone-hearer, you must carry the song instead. Someone must always carry it. That is the toll. That has always been the toll.*

Ada looked at the woman's face. At the faces beyond her, dim in the pillars, dozens of them, all turned now, all listening.

"What does carrying it cost?"

*Everything you are is a room,* the woman said. *And the song wants to fill the room. Grief is heavy, bone-hearer, but grief that has waited eighty years in the dark—that grief has weight enough to press a person flat. We lost our own singers that way, before we died. Men who joined the song to grieve a brother and could not climb back out of it. We don't say their names anymore. We couldn't. They forgot the names themselves.*

Behind Ada, Sef said quietly: "You don't have to."

She almost laughed. "I've spent my whole life bargaining with the dead for quiet, little one. Now the dead are asking me to sing. There's a joke in there somewhere the gods are enjoying."

But her hands were shaking. Because she understood, with a carver's understanding of material, exactly what was being asked. Memory was currency now—she'd paid her father's face to the fence, and the wound of that absence woke her some nights, reaching for a face that wasn't there. To join the choir would be to pay in a different coin: not one memory, but her *self*, her shoreline, the border between Ada Winterbourne and everything else. The song would pour in and fill every chamber, and pulling herself out would be like carving her way free of her own body with no blade but will.

"Do you trust me?" she asked Sef.

"You're the only thing I trust," Sef said simply, and that was worse than doubt. That was a load-bearing wall.

"All right," Ada said, and turned back to the salt. "Teach me the song."

* * *

The toll was simple and it was monstrous: to take the bracelet, she had to join the chain of hands. The bone had passed from singer to singer inside the choir, and it could pass to her the same way—but the passing was a merging, a moment when her mind opened fully into theirs, and what poured through was eighty years of entombed grief compressed into the space of a heartbeat.

She braced her palms against the pillar. The crystal was cold and strangely soft, like skin gone numb. The woman in the salt raised her hands, wrists together, offering.

*Sing with us first,* the dead woman said. *Learn the shape of it. Then cut the bracelet free. The song will try to keep you. Be ready to leave.*

Ada began to sing.

Her voice was poor and thin and it broke twice on the first verse, but the choir gathered it up, bore it, made it part of themselves, and the sound closed over her head like dark water.

*One for the seam and two for the vein—*

The grief hit her between verses.

It didn't build. It arrived. All of it at once, the whole drowned weight of it—the dark, the falling mountain, the decades of singing to no answer, children born and never seen, bread shared through a crack in the rock until the bread ran out, the day the news reached even here, filtering down through the stone in the voices of newer ghosts: *your country has been unmade, your language is illegal, your names are contraband, sing quieter, sing quieter*—and they hadn't sung quieter, they'd sung *louder*, they'd carved the beams, they'd taught the song to the dying so the dying could add verses—

—and Ada was singing and she could not remember stopping and she could not remember her own—

her own—

There was a valley. There was a ridge. Magic couldn't cross it. Whose ridge? Why did she know that? There were bones she carved, whose bones? There was a child, there was a purpose, there was a *door*, but doors meant leaving and leaving meant the song would end and the song was everything, the song was the last true country left, and how could she abandon them, how could she climb back into the thin grey world where names got erased when right here, right here in the warm and the dark and the salt, she could grieve forever, safely, beautifully, one voice in a choir that would never be dismissed—

Her hands had stopped moving. The knife was somewhere. The bracelet was somewhere. None of it mattered. Nothing mattered but the next verse, and the next, and the sweet enormous mercy of not being Ada Winterbourne anymore, of dissolving into a sound larger than any single loss—

Something struck her cheek. Small. Sharp. Twice.

Sef stood before her, having crossed the gallery alone, having put their face directly into the line of her empty gaze, and the child was crying without seeming to notice, and speaking—not the counting song, not Ysoltan. Words Ada knew. Words from before everything, from a kitchen and a garden and a life so small and safe she had forgotten it was remarkable.

"Marisol," Sef said. "Marisol Winterbourne. Your mother's name is Marisol. You told me on the ferry. You said she hummed while she carved and you hated it and now you'd give anything to hear it. Marisol. *Marisol.*"

The name went through the song like a blade through wet canvas.

Her mother. Humming. Off-key, always off-key, and little Ada at the workbench pretending to be annoyed, hoarding every bar of it the way you hoard anything you don't yet know is finite. The smell of bone dust and brown bread. The particular click of her mother setting down a finished piece, satisfied.

*Marisol.*

Ada gasped, and the gallery slammed back into focus—the pillars, the lamplight guttering, her knees on the salt floor, the knife somehow still gripped in her fist, and her own voice raw from singing verses she had no memory of learning. How long? Minutes. Hours. The lamp oil said hours.

The choir had fallen silent. All of them, for the first time in eighty years, listening.

The woman in the salt was smiling. It was a terrible, beautiful smile.

*Good,* she said softly. *Someone pulled you home. Hold onto them, bone-hearer. Hold on hard. Now finish it. Take the bracelet. And take our song with you—we release you from carrying it. Let the child carry it instead. The child was built for carrying.*

Ada looked at Sef—at the child's white knuckles, their tear-tracked, stubborn face—and understood the dead woman had just said something enormous, something she'd examine later, when her heart stopped hammering.

She climbed to her feet. Set the knife's edge against the crystal at her mother's-name strength, at Marisol strength, at *hurry* strength, and cut.

The bracelet came free in a shower of glittering salt, and the moment it left the pillar the entire choir drew breath—one vast, subterranean inhalation—and began to sing again. But the song had changed. New verse. Their last addition, composed across eighty years for exactly this moment:

*Nine for the door and ten for the dawn,
eleven for everyone thought to be gone—*

Ada wrapped the bracelet in cloth with hands that would not stop trembling, and it was Sef who took the lamp, Sef who led the way back toward the dark throat of the shaft and the distant grey promise of morning.

Only at the first timber prop, where the carved Ysoltan names began, did the child stop walking.

"Ada." Sef's voice was very small in the vast silence after the song. "When the woman said I was built for carrying. She was right. And you need to know what that means, before we go any further, because—" The child's breath hitched. "Because I think it's going to break your heart, and I think there might still be time to turn back."

Above them, faint as memory, the choir sang the dawn verse into the dark, and Ada sat down on the mine floor beside the child, and listened.
