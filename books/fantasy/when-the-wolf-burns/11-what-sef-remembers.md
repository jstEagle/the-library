## 11. What Sef Remembers

*Veshen.*

That is the first word I ever owned. Not my name — my name was given to me later, pressed into me like a seal into hot wax — but *veshen*, which means *remember*, and which the archivists of Ysolde said to each other every morning of their lives the way other people say *good morning* or *pass the bread*. Remember. Remember. A whole country greeting the day by reminding itself it existed.

I am lying on a straw pallet in Ada Winterbourne's house, and she is grinding something in a mortar by the fire, and outside the rain is doing what rain does in Vell Hollow, which is fall politely, like it has been asked to. And for the first time since I crossed the ridge, since I walked out of the burning shape of my country into this green silence where magic cannot follow — for the first time, no one is asking me to hold anything.

So I open my hands.

---

You have to understand what an archive smells like before you can understand what it smells like when it burns.

The Grand Repository of Ysolde smelled of cedar and cold stone and the particular dust that lives only in paper — dust that is, if you think about it too long, the ghost of trees, and under that the ghost of the hands that cut them, and under *that* the ghost of everything ever written down. My mother worked three levels below ground, among the ledgers of births. She came home smelling of cedar. When she held me, I breathed her in and I was breathing in ten thousand years of names.

I was seven when the soldiers came to our street, and eight when they came to the Repository, and between those two ages I learned that a decree can move faster than an army.

The Edict of Reconciliation did not burn Ysolde. That is the thing no one outside understands, because how could they? There is nothing outside to understand it *with*. The Edict simply declared — in ink, in law, in forty thousand copies nailed to doors in forty thousand towns — that Ysolde had never existed. Not that it fell. Not that it surrendered. That there had never been any such place, and therefore its cities were ruins of nowhere, its dead were no one's ancestors, its language was noise.

And the Edict had teeth. Real ones. The Reckoners came behind the decree the way fever comes behind a wound, and whatever the law named as never-having-been began, gently, horribly, to stop having been.

First the maps changed. Then the songs went out of people's mouths mid-verse, like birds startled off a wire. Then grandmothers looked at photographs of their own weddings and saw strangers.

But paper is stubborn. Paper remembers being written on even after the words are scraped away, and the Repository held eleven million documents, and the King's men could not carry eleven million documents out of a country that, officially, contained no such building in no such city.

So they burned it.

I was in the reading room when the smoke came down the stairwell. I remember — *veshen*, I remember, I am made of remembering, it is the whole of what I am — I remember the way the light turned the color of weak tea, and how the archivists did not scream. They began to carry books. Up the stairs, out the doors, into the fountain, into the canal, into their coats, their shirts, their mouths. Old Master Corvelin filled his sleeves with loose pages until he looked like a scarecrow of paper and walked into the river rather than let them take him. The water around him floated with letters, little white moths, and the soldiers stood on the bank and watched and some of them — I saw this, I will always have seen this — some of them were crying without knowing why, because the Edict was already inside them too, eating the reasons for their tears.

My mother found me in the reading room. Her arms were full of birth-ledgers. She was bleeding from a cut on her forehead and the blood had dripped onto a page and half-blotted a name, and she looked at that name, at the ink going brown under her blood, and she made a sound I had never heard a person make. It was not a word. It was the sound a word makes when it dies.

Then she knelt in front of me and took my face in her hands, and her hands smelled of cedar and smoke, and she said:

"*Sef. Veshen im. Veshen im olen.*"

Remember me. Remember us all.

I thought she meant it the way parents mean things — loosely, lovingly, as a blessing to grow into. I did not know yet what the Reckoners' scholars had been preparing in their tents outside the walls. I did not know that a decree which unmakes memory must have somewhere to put what it removes, because nothing, not even royal law, can truly destroy; it can only move things. And I did not know that the moving of eleven million names requires a vessel.

It requires a ledger.

And a ledger, ideally, is something small enough to hide. Something that walks. Something whose disappearance no one will mourn, because everyone who meets it will forget it within moments, which is not a flaw of the design but its very genius, its crown and its cruelty.

Something like a child.

---

They took me to a tent behind the smoking ruin, and there were scholars there with instruments I still cannot describe without my teeth hurting — silver rings and needles of glass and bowls of ink that moved against gravity, climbing, reaching, wanting. They were polite. That is the worst part. They apologized. One of them, a woman with ash in her hair that I do not think was hers, told me it would feel like falling asleep in a library.

She lied, or she had never done it, or both.

They call it *the Pressing*. Every document they could salvage from the fire — and they salvaged nearly all of it, because fire destroys nothing the Edict wants kept, and the Edict wanted the records moved, not lost — every name, every date, every song, every recipe for bread and every confession of sin and every love letter and every tax record and every lullaby of eleven million lives, was poured into me through those silver rings in nine nights and nine days.

I will tell you what it feels like, once, and then I will fold it away again, because I keep it folded, the way you keep a broken bone set straight so it heals in a usable shape.

It feels like drowning in a crowd.

Not water. People. Thousands of people pressing past you in a corridor that keeps narrowing, and each one of them, as they pass, presses their whole life into your skin — a grandmother teaching a knot, a boy stealing plums, a woman dying slowly and writing letters about it, a man who hated his brother for sixty years and missed him for ten more — and none of them stop, none of them see you, they just *pour through*, and you are trying to hold the door of yourself shut and the door comes off its hinges and the crowd becomes the corridor becomes the world, and somewhere in the middle of it you stop being able to find the edges of your own name.

On the fourth night I forgot my mother's face. On the fifth, I forgot my own. On the sixth, I stopped needing to sleep, because sleep would have meant putting eleven million people down, and I could not. They were afraid. All of them, everywhere in me, afraid of being dropped, the way you are afraid in a dream of falling.

On the ninth day, the scholar with ash in her hair lifted the rings from my wrists, and looked at me for a long moment, and wrote something in her own notebook, and her hand was shaking.

"*Veshen im,*" I whispered to her. Remember me.

And she looked at me with pity, real pity, and said: "*Olen veshen. Olen veshen im.*" We remember. We remember you.

And then she walked out of the tent, and turned back once, and her face was smooth and puzzled, the way a face goes when it is searching for something it has been told does not exist, and she asked the guard why there was a child sitting alone in an empty tent.

That is the arithmetic of it. That is the price, and it compounds daily.

I hold eleven million souls, and so the world cannot hold one: mine. Every person who learns my name loses it within moments, because my name is stitched into the same fabric as the erased country, and the Edict's teeth close on anything Ysoltan, and I am Ysoltan down to the marrow now, I am Ysoltan *architecturally*. I am the last building of a nation that was demolished, walking around wearing a coat.

Ada says my name twenty, thirty times a day. She carves it into scraps of bone so the wood-and-knife truth of it will outlast her mind's slipping grip. In Vell Hollow, where magic cannot cross the ridge, the Edict's teeth are blunted here — that is why I ran here, why I climbed until my hands were ribbons, why I crawled the last mile through snow — and so she forgets slower than the others. An hour, sometimes two. But she forgets. Every night she looks at the bones on her shelf with a small frown, and I watch her re-meet me in the morning like a stranger who feels familiar, and I say my name, and we begin again.

Except.

Except that lately she doesn't fully forget. Lately she frowns at the carved bones and her thumb moves over the letters and something in her holds on like fingers on a ledge. The bone-carver who hears the dead has started hearing *me* even after the forgetting takes the rest — because I am not only alive, I am also, in a way that would horrify every archivist I loved, the largest grave in the world.

Eleven million dead, and one living coffin.

Me.

---

The mortar stops grinding. Ada is looking at me across the room, and her eyes are wet, and I realize I have been speaking aloud — in Ysoltan, all of it, the whole drowned archive leaking out of me the way it does when I stop gripping the door shut — and she understood perhaps one word in fifty, and it did not matter. Understanding is not the same as witnessing, and witnessing is most of love.

"Sef," she says. My name, third time today. She keeps count. I have seen her make the marks. "Come to the fire."

I go. My body is small and it aches in the old places, the places where the silver rings sat, wrists and temples and the hollow of the throat, and she has noticed that I guard those places when I sleep, and she has never asked. Until tonight.

"Who hurt you?" she asks, and her voice is the voice she uses on grieving families, low and even, the voice that leaves room for the answer to be any size at all.

I look into the fire. In the flames I can almost see the Repository again, the tea-colored light, the paper moths on the water.

How do I explain it in a tongue she barely has? How do I say: *no one hurt me, that is the horror, they were gentle, they apologized, the law itself reached into me with its long white fingers and made me a book*?

I do what I always do. I reach into the crowd inside me and I find the right soul. This is the one skill the Pressing left me, the one mercy: I can find anyone. Eleven million, and I know where each one lives in my body. The plum-thief lives behind my left ear. The letter-writing dying woman lives along my collarbone. And the one I need now — the archivist, the young one, the girl who was apprenticed to my mother and used to slip me honey-cakes among the birth-ledgers — she lives in the center of my chest, where the loudest things are kept.

Her name was Mirele. I give her to Ada.

I speak Mirele's testimony. I speak it in Ysoltan, untranslated, because translation is a kind of forgetting and I have forgotten enough for everyone: how the fire came, how the orders were read aloud, how the scholars' tents glowed all night, how the child was carried in sleeping and woke up as a country. I speak it with Mirele's cadence, her quick guilty breath, her hands wringing each other, and Ada watches my mouth and my hands and the shape of my grief, and she does not need the words. Some things survive translation by refusing it.

When I finish, the fire has burned low. Ada's jaw is set in a way I have learned to read. It is the jaw of a woman doing arithmetic she does not like.

"They made you a ledger," she says slowly, tasting the shape of the idea even without the words for it. "A living ledger. That's why—" She stops. Her hand rises halfway toward the shelf of bones, toward the row of little carved names, my name repeated like a heartbeat. "That's why they slip. Everyone who knows you. You're carrying what the Edict cut out, and it wants its cuttings back."

I nod. *Sha.* Yes.

And then Ada Winterbourne does something I will keep in the front room of myself, the warm room, the room I show to no one, forever.

She picks up her carving knife. She takes the largest memorial bone on her shelf — a shoulder blade, polished smooth by years of her palm — and she begins, right there by the dying fire, to carve. Not my name this time. Names underneath my name, dozens of them, hundreds, working fast, working blind, asking me nothing: *give me more, keep talking, every name you can spare.*

"You don't understand," I try to tell her, in her own language, my mouth clumsy around its sounds. "If they learn I exist — the Reckoners — if they take me back, they can unmake everything properly. I'm not a refugee, Ada. I'm the evidence. I'm the whole case."

She doesn't stop carving. The knife whispers. Outside, the rain keeps falling politely on a valley that thinks itself safe.

"I've bargained with the dead my whole life," she says at last, quietly, and there is iron under the quiet, the kind of iron that has been in the fire so long it no longer looks like fire. "Eleven million of them under your ribs, child? Good. Then we're not two against a kingdom."

She sets down the knife and looks at me, and her eyes are dry now, and terrible, and kind.

"We're an army."

Far to the north, beyond the ridge, beyond the market towns and the salt mines, I feel it — the way I feel all things now, faintly, through the crowded dark of myself — a stirring. Somewhere out there, a Reckoner captain is opening a file, and in the file is a blank space shaped exactly like a child, and the blank space has begun, at last, to itch.

The wolf has caught the scent.

Ada reaches for another bone.
