## 12. The Fence's Price

The fence kept shop in a drowned church.

That was what the market-folk called it, anyway—a chapel half-swallowed by the slow black river that bent through the border town of Marrowgate, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against the sky, its nave flooded to the height of two steps. To reach the door you waded. The water came up cold past Ada's ankles, then her knees, and it smelled of iron and old candles, of things dissolved so long they had become part of the water itself. Fish moved between the pews like small gray thoughts. Somewhere above, in the rotted rafters, starlings muttered.

Sef walked beside her, holding the hem of her coat the way children hold the hems of coats—lightly, as if pretending not to.

"You can wait on the steps," Ada said. "Dry side."

Sef shook their head once. They had been doing that all morning—refusing to be left anywhere, refusing even the small absences of Ada stepping behind a stall or turning down an alley. Since the salt mine, since Sef had spoken her mother's name into the dark and pulled her back from the choir's grief by the throat of that one word, the child had appointed themselves Ada's keeper. It would have been funny if it weren't so plainly fear wearing the mask of duty.

*You went somewhere I couldn't follow,* Sef had signed that night, sitting cross-legged on a pallet in a miner's abandoned bunkhouse, and then, after a long silence, hands moving slowly: *When people go where I can't follow, sometimes they don't come back. That is most of my country now.*

Ada had not known what to say to that. She was a woman who bargained with the dead for quiet and still had no craft for comforting the living. So she had done the only thing she knew: she'd taken Sef's hands and held them still, bone against bone, and let the child feel her pulse going about its ordinary, stubborn business.

Now the pulse was quickening, because ahead of them, at the end of the flooded nave, a lamp burned behind a screen of hanging wax tablets, and behind the lamp sat the fence.

Everyone called her Mother Halfpenny, which was not her name; her name had been sold years ago, or so the market said, spent on some transaction too large to speak of, and now she went by coin instead. She was old the way driftwood is old—smoothed, bleached, hard as sinew—and she sat with her hands folded on a table of pale wood, and around her, from floor to ruined ceiling, hung the merchandise.

Memories. Not metaphorically. Actually.

They were kept in wax. Tablets of it, each the size of a prayer book, each sealed with a stamp and strung on cords from the rafters like drying herbs, hundreds of them, thousands, and when the draft from the broken windows stirred them they clicked softly together, a sound like teeth, like beads, like rain on a coffin lid. Some of the tablets were dark and dense. Some were thin as wafer, nearly transparent—the memories of the very old, worn down by retelling until almost nothing remained but shape. One near the door, Ada noticed, was fresh and red-streaked, and she looked away from it quickly.

She had dealt with fences before, in a manner of speaking. She dealt in bones; bones are memory of a kind. But this was different, and the difference made her stomach tighten. Bones remember *that someone lived.* These tablets remembered *how it felt.*

"Bone-carver," said Mother Halfpenny, without looking up. Her voice was dry as the wax she traded. "Vell Hollow way. You've come farther from your ridge than I'd have guessed your sort could manage."

"My sort?"

"People who think memory should be free." She raised her eyes then. They were pale, the color of skimmed milk, and they went first to Sef, lingered, and narrowed. "And they've brought me something interesting. Sit. Mind the water. It remembers feet."

Ada did not ask what that meant. She guided Sef onto the pew beside her, the wood groaning under them, and laid the wrapped bundle on the table between them: the fourth founding bone, won three towns back from a peddler who hadn't known what he sold, and beneath it, swaddled in oilcloth, the map of the dead—the route the graves had whispered to her, leading to the fifth.

"I want a bone," Ada said. "Ysoltan. Older than the kingdom. It'll be mislabeled—sold as a saint's relic, or a witch's charm, or a horse's knucklebone. It will be in this town or passed through it within the season."

"And I'm to know which one."

"The dead know," Ada said. "You deal in the dead's leavings. Somewhere in this dripping ruin you have a tablet that remembers where it went."

Mother Halfpenny smiled without warmth. She unfolded her hands and turned one palm upward, and Ada saw, inset into the flesh of the palm, a small round window of wax—pale, blank, waiting. The fence pressed the palm flat against her table, and for a moment nothing happened. Then the wax clouded, swirled, and showed—not an image exactly, but a pressure, a direction, a weight, the way a scar shows weather before rain.

"Marrowgate has had eleven Ysoltan pieces through its hands in two years," the fence said. "Nine burned by the Reckoners at the market square—you saw the ash, don't pretend you didn't walk through it. One went south in a diplomat's baggage and is likely a paperweight in a capital I'm not permitted to name." The wax-palm darkened. "One remains. A knuckle joint, big as a fist, sold to me by a dredger who pulled it from the river with a netful of drowned saints. I've been asking myself ever since why a saint's finger weighs like a promise and hums like a struck bell when the moon is wrong. I think I have my answer standing in my nave."

"Name your price," Ada said. "Coin, trade, work. I carve. I can mend what the flood took from your walls. I can—"

"Coin." The word dropped flat as a coin itself. "What would I do with coin? Look around you, bone-carver. What does a woman buy, in the last shop of everything?" She gestured upward, at the clicking canopy of tablets. "I have the wedding of a bride who forgot her own husband's face by choice. I have the last twenty years of a man who sold them to pay a debt and bought back only the birthdays. I have a soldier's entire childhood except the smell of his mother's bread—he kept that, poor fool, it was the worst thing he owned." Her milk-pale eyes settled on Ada. "I deal in memory. You pay in memory. That is the whole of my arithmetic, and it does not haggle."

Ada had known. Of course she had known. The dead had told her in their sideways way—the grave-wax woman's memory showing her Ysolde whole, and at the edges of that vision, always, the sense that everything cost, that remembering a nation erased meant paying in the only coin erasers recognized. But knowing and hearing it are different animals, and hearing it, her mouth went dry.

"How much?" she asked, and hated how small it sounded in the drowned church.

"For that bone? With Reckoners sniffing every road and my stock burning faster than I can sell it?" Mother Halfpney leaned forward. "A middling memory. Ten years, give or take. Nothing load-bearing—I'm not a butcher, whatever they say of me. No names you need, no roads you've traveled since the child came. Something old. Something healed over. Something you can spare."

*Something you can spare.* As though memory were fat, cut away without bleeding.

Behind her, Ada felt Sef shift. The child's hand found her sleeve again and gripped, and though Sef said nothing—said nothing in any tongue, spoke only in the press of small fingers—Ada understood perfectly: *Don't. We'll find another way. Don't become smaller because of me.*

"There's a rule," the fence went on, softer now, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "In the trade. The seller picks blind. I bring out three tablets' worth of taking, unlabeled, and the seller chooses by touch alone. Keeps folk from agonizing. Keeps them from choosing to keep the thing they came to lose." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and shuffled into the dark behind the wax-screen, and the clicking of the tablets swallowed her footsteps.

Ada stared at the lamp. Its flame stood perfectly still, in a room full of moving water and muttering birds, and that stillness seemed obscene.

Her father. The thought arrived uninvited, the way he used to arrive at her workbench, boots muddy, holding something he'd found—a fox skull, a knot of amber, once a Roman coin he'd insisted was a fairy's button. He had died when she was nineteen, in the ordinary way, in his sleep, with the valley quiet around him, and she had carved his memorial bone herself, badly, weeping, and the grief had sanded smooth over the years into something she could carry. But the face—

She tried, sitting there, to summon his face deliberately, the way you'd fetch a tool from a shelf.

And found, with a small cold shock, that it came in fragments. The laugh-lines, yes. The particular way one eyebrow climbed. The color of his beard, rust shot with early gray. But the whole of it, the living arrangement of all the pieces—that was already softening, had been softening for years, and she had never noticed, the way you never notice a hill eroding.

*Something old. Something healed over. Something you can spare.*

There was no such thing. That was the lie in the fence's gentle voice. There was no spare memory any more than there was a spare stone in an arch.

Mother Halfpenny returned carrying three tablets on a tray of river-wood. They were identical to look at: gray-brown wax, sealed, stamped with the trade-mark, a circle bisected by a line. She set them down and turned them so their stamps faced away.

"Touch and choose," she said. "Then we do the taking. It's quicker than you're afraid of. Most folk say it feels like a sneeze."

"Most folk are liars," Ada said.

"Some," the fence agreed placidly. "But not about this."

Ada reached out. Her fingers hovered over the three tablets. Behind her Sef's breathing had gone very fast and shallow, and Ada wished—absurdly, fiercely—that she could hear the dead just now, just once, here in this chapel where the drowned and the sold crowded thick enough to choke on. What would they advise? *Take the cheapest loss,* probably. The dead were practical. The dead knew that everyone becomes a fragment eventually; better to choose which fragment you keep.

She touched the middle tablet.

Nothing distinguished it. It felt like wax, cool and slightly greasy, and weighed what a prayer book weighs, and she understood suddenly that this was the whole horror of the transaction: there was no way to choose well. You chose blind because all choices were blind. You gave up a piece of yourself to a stranger's scale and trusted that the piece was not the keystone.

"That one," she said.

Mother Halfpenny nodded, unsurprised, as if the middle were always chosen, and perhaps it was; perhaps there were studies of it, arithmetic of it. She carried the tray to a workbench at the side of the nave, where instruments waited in a felt roll like a surgeon's: needles fine as hair, a shallow dish of warm water, a spool of thin copper wire, and a hollow needle longer than the others, its point ringed with a thread of gold.

"Sit facing me. Hands open on the table. It helps if you breathe out when I draw, but honestly the body does what the body does." She paused, looking at Ada with those milk-colored eyes, and for one moment something almost like regret crossed the driftwood face. "Last chance to walk out with your head whole and no bone. There are other fences. Worse ones, cheaper ones. I charge fair."

"What's fair," Ada said, "in a country that burns history?"

"Fair," said Mother Halfpenny, "is that I keep what I buy safe. Fair is that when this kingdom finishes eating itself, some of what it ate will still exist, in wax, in my nave, and someday someone like you—or like that child—will come wanting it back, and I will sell it to them at cost, because I am sentimental in my old age and it shames me." She drew a breath. "Hold still."

The needle went into the back of Ada's neck, below the skull, where the bone is thinnest and the self sits closest to the surface. There was a sting, then a strange cold spreading inward like water soaking through cloth, and then—

Then the taking began, and it was not a sneeze.

It was a drawing-out, a long smooth pulling like a splinter leaving the flesh after years, and with it came light. Not images—light, warmth, the *feel* of something beloved, and Ada gripped the table's edge and breathed out as instructed and felt the warmth travel up the wire, along the needle, out of her, into the wax, and she thought wildly, *don't take him, take anything else, take the winter I broke my arm, take the taste of—*

And then it was done.

The needle came out. The tablet in Mother Halfpenny's hands had changed: darker now, denser, warm to the touch, sealed with a fresh drop of wax. The fence held it up to the lamp, examining it the way a jeweler examines a stone, and nodded once, satisfied.

"A good one," she said. "Whole. Unspent. Rare, in a woman your age." She glanced at Ada almost curiously. "Do you know what you gave?"

"No," Ada said. And then, because the question had been forming since the needle went in, cold and precise as an awl: "Will I know? Will I miss it?"

"You'll miss it the way an amputee misses a hand. You won't know what's gone. You'll reach for it and find air, and you'll invent reasons." She was already wrapping the tablet in oilcloth, tying it with cord, labeling it in a script of tiny hooked characters. "Some come back to buy themselves again. I always tell them the same thing: the memory doesn't heal you twice. What's taken is taken. What you get back is only the knowledge that you lost something—which is its own wound, and heavier." She set the tablet aside, and from beneath the table drew out a second bundle, long and heavy, and unwrapped it on the felt.

The bone lay gleaming in the lamplight.

It was a knuckle joint, as the dredger had said, but no horse's, no saint's. It was the size of a clenched fist, ivory gone honey-gold with age, and carved over every surface with writing in the beautiful hooked script that Ada had seen only once before—in a grave older than the kingdom, in the memory of a dead woman who had shown her cities and songs and a door. Along one face ran a line of deeper carving, worn nearly smooth by centuries of handling, and when Ada's fingers brushed it, the bone *humbled* her. There was no other word. The way a cathedral humbles, the way deep water humbles. The dead who had ridden her bones for forty years fell silent all at once, even across the ridge's far shadow, even here where magic could not follow—as if they, too, had uncovered their heads.

"Fifth bone," Mother Halfpenny said quietly. "Whatever it opens. Take it and go. Reckoners rode through at dawn asking after a bone-carver and a child. They'll ride back before dark; the captain keeps a tidy schedule, and tidy schedules are the easiest thing in the world to ambush."

Ada wrapped the bone with hands that were steady and felt distant, borrowed. She stood. The drowned nave swayed gently around her, pews and tablets and clicking wax, and she counted herself silently—name, trade, valley, the ridge, the graves, the child—one, two, three, four, five, checking that all her stones still stood in their arch. They did. All of them. And yet somewhere behind the counting was a doorway with air where a room used to be, and she would spend years walking past it, reaching, finding nothing.

At the threshold of the water, Sef caught her hand.

The child looked up at her, and their free hand rose, hesitated, and asked in signs, slowly, so there could be no mistake: *What did you give them?*

"I don't know," Ada said truthfully. "That's the price of it."

Sef's face crumpled and reassembled itself, quick as weather. Then the child did the thing that undid Ada more thoroughly than any needle: they lifted Ada's hand and pressed it flat against their own sternum, over the heart, and held it there, and looked at her with the fierce wet gravity of the very young, and signed with their other hand: *Then I will remember it for you. Whatever it was. I am a ledger. Give me your lost things and I will keep them, and when this is over, I will read them back.*

Ada laughed—a cracked, wet sound that startled the starlings—and pulled the child close, bone-warm against her ribs, the fifth bone heavy in her pack between them like a sleeping animal.

They waded out of the drowned church into the gray afternoon, and the market noise took them, fish-smell and hawkers and the distant drum of hooves. Too late Ada placed the sound. Hooves, many, rhythmic, disciplined, coming up the river road with the unhurried precision of men keeping a tidy schedule.

At the far end of the square, blue cloaks caught the light, and Captain Ives sat his horse like a drawn line, and his eyes were already moving through the crowd, stall by stall, face by face, looking for them.

* * *

They went to ground the only way Marrowgate allowed: down. Ada hauled Sef into the fish-cellar of a woman who owed the dead a favor—the dead had told Ada which doors owed what, and this one owed much—and they crouched among barrels of brine while boots crossed the floorboards overhead, and a voice like dry paper asked its questions, and somewhere in the answers Ada heard the fishwife say, clear and calm and true, "Bone-carver? Ain't seen one. Child? No child here but the ones in the barrels," which was Reckoner-flattery of a high order.

The boots left. The schedule moved on.

Ada sat in the dark smelling of salt and herring, the fifth bone against her spine, and took inventory of herself again: name, trade, valley, child, quest. Still whole. Still arching. But as her eyes adjusted to the cellar dark she caught Sef watching her—not with fear, not with gratitude, but with the expression of a ledger-keeper making an entry, memorizing her face line by line, the way you memorize something you have promised to carry for someone else.

"Hurry," Sef said aloud, in their shared tongue—their first common word, the one they'd given her at the journey's start. "Hurry, Ada."

Outside, the hoofbeats turned north. Toward the ford. Toward home.

And Ada, who could not have said why, thought of her father's hands, and could not picture them.
