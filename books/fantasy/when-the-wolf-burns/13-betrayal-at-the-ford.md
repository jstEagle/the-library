## 13. Betrayal of the Ford

The rain had followed them for two days, thin and needling, as if the sky itself were trying to scrub them from the land. Tomas Hedley stood under the black alders above the ford with his collar up and his hands buried in his pockets, and told himself—for the ninth time, he counted, because counting was the only prayer left to him—that what he meant to do was not betrayal.

Betrayal required a country. A flag. Something grand enough to be worth the word.

This was only a family.

He could see them from here if he closed his eyes, which he did often now: Marta in the kitchen with flour to her elbows, the girls arguing over whose turn it was to feed the hens, the baby—no, not a baby anymore, but he would always keep one of them small, some corner of her, because a man needed something unchanging to hold onto. He could see the kitchen table where Captain Ives had sat three nights ago with his gray gloves folded neatly beside his cup, drinking tea he never touched, speaking in the mild voice of a man reading an inventory.

"Reconciliation," Ives had said, "is a generous word, Master Hedley. It means your household's records are amended. It means your daughters grow up registered, schooled, employable. Or—" and here he had unfolded one glove, finger by finger, like a man undressing a wound "—it means they are *unrecorded*. And you have seen, I think, what happens to the unrecorded."

Tomas had seen. The whole valley had seen, though none of them said it aloud, because saying it aloud was how it started—the forgetting, the sliding, names going soft at the edges like wet chalk. Old Widow Crane had been unrecorded in autumn, and by midwinter no one could say for certain whether she had ever existed, only that there was a house on the lane that seemed oddly empty, oddly expectant, as if waiting for someone to come home.

So Tomas had done the arithmetic every man in Vell Hollow was doing behind his shutters. Four lives against two strangers. His girls against a bone-carver who talked to graves and a child nobody could even remember meeting twice.

It was not betrayal. He repeated it like a rosary. It was not.

The ford below him ran brown and fast with the rain. On the far bank, small in the gray light, Ada Winterbourne was crouched at the water's edge with her pack open, checking the oilcloth wrapping around the fifth bone—the one they had bled for, the one that had cost her the memory of her father's face, though she'd never told him that directly. She didn't have to. He'd watched her reach for it in the evenings, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth, reaching for something that wasn't there anymore and coming away confused, hurt, unable to say why.

And Sef stood beside her, hood up, watching the water with those eyes too old for the face they lived in. Even from this distance, even through rain, Tomas felt the child looking at the ford the way other people looked at doors.

They would cross within the hour. That was the route he had sold: the ford, then the drover's track south, then the old kiln road toward the salt country. Three days' travel, laid out neat as cutlery on Ives's table while Marta wept in the doorway and pretended she hadn't heard.

*You'll never know it happened*, Ives had promised. *Your name will simply remain where it is. Isn't that all any of us want? To remain?*

Tomas's hands were shaking. He told himself it was the cold.

He raised the lantern—it was not yet dark, but the signal had been agreed upon, and signals are mercifully stupid things, requiring nothing of the hand that lifts them—and turned the shutter toward the ridge road. Once. Twice. A third time, and held it there until his arm burned, so there could be no mistake, so there could be no taking it back.

Far off, along the ridge, something answered. Not a lantern. Just a stillness, a gathering of stillness, like birds going quiet before a hawk.

Tomas put the lantern out and walked down toward the ford to meet his friends, wearing his face like a borrowed coat.

* * *

Ada knew the river's mood better than her own these days. Two weeks out of Vell Hollow and she had learned to read water the way she once read bones—the silt-lines of its temper, the places it went shallow and forgiving, the deep green channels where it kept its grudges. This ford was honest, gravel-bottomed, knee-deep in dry weather. Today it would take them to the thigh and try its best to be rid of them.

"Hold the pack high," she said, and heard how strange her voice sounded out loud. She had spent her whole life talking mostly to the dead, who were better listeners. Speech was a muscle gone soft.

Sef lifted the pack without a word—which was most of their words anyway, though the child had been speaking more lately, in that dead language first and Ada's tongue second, as if translation were a bridge they crossed more confidently each day. The child's hands were steady. Ada's were not. Her hands had not been steady since the fence's shop, since she had felt her father's face slide out of her like a splinter drawn clean, leaving a smooth channel of nothing where forty years of love used to live.

She kept reaching for him. That was the worst of it. Every night she reached for him and found only the shape of the absence, a father-shaped hole in the world, and grieved without knowing for whom.

*The river*, whispered the dead in her bones—or rather, they didn't whisper, because beyond the ridge they couldn't, and that silence was still a wound in her skull that had not learned to close. But habit is deeper than magic. Habit spoke anyway, in her own mind's voice, dressed in the cadences of the dead: *the river remembers crossings. Rivers are the oldest ledger-keepers.*

"You're doing it again," Sef said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Talking to people who aren't here." The child's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Or maybe you're the one who isn't here."

Ada snorted and shouldered her share of the load. "Cheeky. Where do you learn cheek? Certainly not from me."

"From the dead," Sef said gravely. "They gossip terribly."

And Ada laughed—a short, cracked thing, but real—and stepped into the water, and the cold took her breath and gave it back sharper, and for one moment the world was only river and rain and the child wading behind her, and she thought: *we might actually do this. We might actually get there.*

That was when the birds stopped.

She didn't hear them stop—you couldn't hear a silence begin—but she felt it, the way you feel a door close in a distant room, a change in pressure. The alders on the north bank, where Tomas was supposed to be waiting with hot tea and lies about how good the fishing was, went suddenly dense with shadow that had nothing to do with weather.

"Tomas?" she called, because hope is stupid and stubborn, and because she wanted, desperately, for the shape moving between the trees to be her friend.

It was her friend. He came down the bank slowly, hat in his hands, rain running off his brim, and his face was the face of a man walking to his own hanging and trying to look casual about it. Behind him, unhurried, came six figures in gray, and among them, at the center like a stone in a ring of lesser stones, walked Captain Ives with his gloves already on.

"No," Ada said. Not a question. A refusal of the entire moment, of the rain and the river and the man she'd known since he was a boy stealing apples from her mother's tree.

"I'm sorry." Tomas's voice came out ruined. "Ada—I'm sorry—I had no choice—"

"There's always a choice." She said it flatly, and hated how much she sounded like the dead when she said it, because the dead said it constantly, bitterly, from the bottom of every grave. "You had four choices and you picked theirs."

"They have my girls." And there it broke open, all of it, the whole rotten arithmetic spilling out of him. "They have Marta's name in a book, Ada, they can just—cross it out, they can make it so my daughters were never born, and I can't fight that, no one can fight that, it's not soldiers, it's not burning, it's just *ink*—" He was crying now, ugly, helpless. "Tell me you wouldn't have done the same. Tell me, and I'll believe you."

Ada could have told him. Looking back on it later—insofar as later existed for her—she would understand that she might genuinely have done the same, and that this was the true horror of the Edict: it did not require monsters. It required only fathers.

But Sef was standing in the shallows behind her, water to the knees, hood back now, and Captain Ives was looking at the child with an expression Ada had not expected—something raw moving under the bureaucratic calm, something that looked almost like recognition, like grief, like hunger.

"The fifth bone," Ives said. His voice carried over the river gently, reasonably, the way ink spreads. "And the unregistered person. Those are the terms. You and Master Hedley walk away. There is no clause in today's reconciliation that requires either of you."

"And if I refuse?"

Ives sighed, and it was the sigh of a tired clerk, not a soldier, which was somehow worse. "Then I am authorized to widen the terms."

Ada's hand found the pack strap. The bone was inside, wrapped in oilcloth, warm somehow—she'd have sworn it was warm—against her spine. Nine bones to open the door of a country. Five gathered, each bought with a piece of herself. Four to go, and now this.

*Give it to him*, whispered the silence, in her own voice, in the voices of everyone she'd ever carved. *Bones can be stolen back. Children cannot.*

But Sef was already moving—not away, not hiding. The child waded forward past Ada, through the brown water, and stopped on the gravel bar in the middle of the ford, in full view of the Reckoners, and did the last thing in the world Ada expected.

Sef began to sing.

It was the lullaby—Ada knew it now, had heard fragments of it in grave-wax and salt-mine choirs, the song of Ysolde's closing bells—and the child sang it in the dead language, clear and carrying, over the sound of the river. And Ada watched the faces of the six Reckoners and saw something extraordinary happen: they flinched. All six, in different ways, like men hearing their own names called from a house they'd sworn was empty. One made a small motion with his hand, half warding, half reaching. Another's mouth shaped a word he did not let out.

And Ives—Captain Ives, gray-gloved, gray-eyed, the law given legs—stood absolutely motionless on the bank with rain on his face, and his lips moved silently around a melody, following it, note for note, as if he had known it once and been forbidden to remember knowing.

*Sef, no*, Ada thought. *What are you doing—*

She understood a heartbeat too late. The song was not defiance. It was distraction, sacrifice, a flare lit to draw every eye while—

No. The child wasn't running. The child was standing still in the middle of the river, singing to the men sent to erase them, and Ada realized with a horror beyond anything the Reckoners could devise that Sef was giving up. Was choosing capture over letting her choose. Was paying the price themselves so she wouldn't have to pay hers.

"Sef!" Her voice cracked across the water. "Sef, don't—"

Two Reckoners were already wading in. They took the child's arms, gently—gently, as if handling something that might remember itself—and Sef looked back at her once, only once, and said the word. Their first common word. Their oldest one.

*"Hurry,"* the child said, in her own language, in Ysoltan, in both, in the voice of a whole erased nation packed into one small throat. *"Hurry, Ada. Don't stop for me. Finish it."*

Then they were moving, Sef between them, up the bank and into the trees, and Ives turned to follow, and at the treeline he paused—one moment, half a heartbeat—and looked back at Ada across the brown water.

"Go home, bone-carver," he said, and his voice was not gentle anymore. It was wrecked, quietly, in a way he clearly hoped she wouldn't notice. "There's nothing left for you in this but loss. I've read the ledgers. Loss is all they contain."

Then the gray swallowed him, and the rain filled in the spaces where the Reckoners had been, and Tomas Hedley knelt down in the shallows of the ford like a man whose bones had finally been taken from him, and wept.

Ada stood in the river a long time. Long enough for the cold to climb past her knees, her waist, her heart.

Then she opened the pack, and checked the oilcloth, and found—to her astonishment, her vertigo—that the fifth bone was still there. Warm. Patient. Singing its silent song against her spine.

They hadn't taken it. In all the confusion of the child's singing, in all the machinery of the capture, they had missed it—or Ives had chosen not to see it, and she filed that away, that half-second pause at the treeline, that wrecked voice, that melody on a lawful man's silent lips.

Five bones. Four to find. And now the enemy held the ninth without knowing it was the ninth, held the whole door of a country in a cell somewhere, alive and breathing and telling her to hurry.

Ada turned south, toward the salt country, and began to walk.

Behind her, the river kept crossing itself, patient as a ledger, recording everything.
