## 14. The Reckoning Room

### Sef

The cell was made of paper.

Not paper walls with paper doors—no, the whole of it was paper, pressed and layered and lacquered until it had the density of stone, but Sef could feel what it was the way they could feel a lie in someone's mouth: a faint softness beneath everything, a whisper of pulp. The floor gave slightly under bare feet. The ceiling sweated a fine white dust that settled on the shoulders like flour, like ash, like snow that had forgotten how to be cold.

They had taken the coat. They had taken the boots, the cord at the throat, even the small bone comb Ada had carved for them in the first week, when the world was still only strange and not yet terrible. What remained was a gray smock that smelled of nothing at all—not lye, not wool, not the body it covered. Nothing-smell. The Reckoners were thorough about nothing.

And the walls wrote.

Sef noticed it the first night, or what the guards called night—a dimming of the lantern behind the door, a change in the footsteps beyond. In the dark, the paper began to move. Fine black lines surfaced across every surface like veins rising under skin, and the lines were words, thousands upon thousands of them, crawling in columns too small to read, too fast to follow. They did not write *about* Sef. That was the horror of it, understood slowly, in the way one understands drowning. They wrote *over* Sef. Where Sef's name should have been there was a smooth blankness, an erasure so clean it shone. Where the word Ysolde tried to rise from Sef's memory into the room's attention, the ink closed over it like water over a dropped stone.

*You are being reconciled,* the walls said once, in letters tall enough to read before they dissolved. *This is a kindness. It hurts less than remembering.*

Sef sat against the far wall and put both hands flat on the paper and thought: *I am the living ledger. Everything you are trying to erase, I am carrying. Every name you smooth away tonight is still here, under my ribs, in my skull, written by people who loved their country hard enough to die for its spelling.*

It was not comfort. But it was weight, and weight was something.

The guards brought food that tasted of nothing—the same nothing as the smock, as the walls, as the dust. Sef ate it because Ada would have said eat it, you can't run on empty bones, girl, and Ada's voice lived in Sef's head now, rough and warm, a hearth-voice. Sef held the voice the way other prisoners held prayer.

On the third day—or the third dimming—the door opened, and Captain Ives came in alone.

* * *

He was not what Sef had expected. Sef had imagined the Reckoners as men made of ink themselves, all uniform and no face. Ives was tall and tired-looking, with gray coming in early at his temples and hands that he kept very still, the way a man keeps still who has taught himself to keep still because otherwise his hands might say something true. He carried a ledger under one arm. Of course he did. Everything in this place was ledgers.

He set a chair down—he had brought his own chair—and sat facing Sef, and for a long moment he only looked, the way Ada looked at a bone before she cut it, reading the grain.

"You're the last one," he said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "Of the registry. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sef said nothing. Sef had learned already that silence was the one possession no one had taken.

Ives opened the ledger. "The Edict of Reconciliation is not a punishment," he went on, in the tone of a man reciting scripture he no longer tasted. "I want you to know that. People imagine we hate them. We don't hate anyone. Hatred requires belief, and belief requires—" he turned a page "—a subject. There is no subject here. There never was. You were born in a clerical error, child, and my work is simply correction." He looked up. "It goes easier if you give me the names."

*The names.* Sef felt them stir, all nine thousand of them, the archive burned into their skull by dying archivists who had nowhere left to put a country but inside a child. *Nineteen thousand*, some nights. The number changed depending on how much grief Sef could afford to carry.

"I'll ask differently," Ives said. He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his stillness cracked just slightly; his thumb moved along the ledger's edge, back and forth, back and forth. "Your grandmother. Did she sing?"

The question fell into the cell like a coin into a well, and Sef heard it falling for a long time.

Because—that was not a question from the Edict. The Edict asked for names, locations, relics, accomplices. The Edict did not ask about singing. This question had come from somewhere underneath the uniform, from some cellar of the man where a lamp still burned.

Sef looked at him. Really looked. And there, under the gray and the fatigue, Sef saw it: the cheekbones. The particular fold of the eyelid. The jaw that ran to a soft point the way Ysoltan jaws did, the jaws Sef had seen on every market fresco, every grave-effigy, every face in the salt-mine dark.

*They didn't erase us out of the blood,* Sef thought. *They erased us out of the story, and left the blood walking around in uniforms.*

"Mine sang," Ives said, when the silence had gone on too long. He said it quickly, as if confessing to theft. "When I was small. A song in no language she could name—she said her mother had sung it, and hers before, and none of them knew where it came from. She said it was just a thing grandmothers kept, like a recipe no one cooked anymore." His thumb stopped moving. "She died last winter. And this morning, reading your file—" he almost laughed, a dry, awful sound "—reading your file, which contains nothing, because you contain nothing, officially—I remembered four words of it. Four words I haven't thought of in thirty years. Words I don't know the meaning of."

The lantern behind him guttered. The walls breathed their white dust.

Sef knew, then, what song it was. There was only one song grandmothers kept like a buried coal, passed hand to hand down generations who no longer knew what they were holding. The lullaby of the river-gate. *Sleep, the door is watching. Sleep, the water knows your name.*

If Sef sang it here, in this room of erasure, the walls would try to eat it. If Sef sang it here, this man would finally know what his grandmother had been: a woman carrying a whole country in four words, without knowing, the way a mule carries ore without knowing the mountain.

The Edict wanted Sef silent. Every instinct of survival, every lesson of the burning archives, said: *stay blank, stay small, stay nothing, and live.*

But Ada's voice said: *bones want to be found, love. That's the whole of the trade.*

And somewhere under Sef's ribs, nineteen thousand names shifted, listening.

### Ives

The child looked at him for the length of ten breaths, and Captain Aldous Ives, seventeen years in the Office of Reconciliation, author of forty-one closure reports, felt himself being read like a document.

Then the child began to sing.

It was not loud. That was the first shock—that something so vast could arrive so quietly. The voice was thin, unbroken, a child's voice still, and it moved through the little room the way water moves through cloth, everywhere at once, touching everything. The language was one Ives did not know, had never been taught, had been assured repeatedly did not exist. And yet.

*Yet.*

His grandmother's kitchen rose around him whole: the blue smoke, the chipped bowl of plums, her hands kneading dough while she sang the four words he had carried like a stone in his boot for thirty years—and now the four words had a melody, and the melody had verses, and the verses went on past his grandmother's four words into a country he had never seen and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own face in a stranger's photograph.

*Sleep, the door is watching.*
*Sleep, the water knows your name.*
*The wolf may burn the pelt, my heart—*
*the fire forgets; the bone remains.*

He did not realize he had stood up until the chair clattered behind him. He did not realize his hands were shaking until he saw them gripping the ledger, whitening, the knuckles like river stones. The ink on the walls had gone frantic—black lines boiling up everywhere, surging toward the sound, trying to cover it, and the song passed through the ink like light through fog. Somewhere in the corridor a guard shouted something. Ives raised one hand without turning, the officer's gesture, *hold*, and the corridor held.

The child finished. The last note hung in the paper cell like a moth.

"The fire forgets," Ives said. His voice came out wrong, scraped. "The bone remains." He looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "That's what she sang. Just those lines. She never sang more than those lines. I used to think it was a song about a hearth." He sat down again, slowly, the ledger sliding off his knee to the floor, and he did not pick it up. He had never in his life let a ledger touch the floor. "It's a song about us, isn't it. About her. She was—" He stopped. Started again. "What do you call someone from a country that never existed?"

The child regarded him. Dust had settled in the child's hair, and the eyes above it were old, old, older than the body holding them, and Ives understood suddenly what he was looking at: not a prisoner. An archive. A library with a heartbeat. Everything his office had spent eleven years burning was stacked alive in front of him, breathing, and he had been sent to collect it for destruction, and his grandmother had sung its lullaby over her bread dough in a kitchen in a town whose name was still allowed to exist.

"There is a form," he heard himself say, "for voluntary disclosure. Cooperating subjects receive protected status. Placement. A new name in a new province, papers, a stipend." The words came out in the flat cadence of the manual, and he hated the cadence, heard it now for what it was—the sound of a man reading instructions for dismantling a house while standing inside it. "It would spare you the cell."

The child spoke then. First words since the arrest, and the accent was strange, the grammar slightly wrong, but the words were plain trade-tongue and they went through Ives like a blade through wet paper:

"And the names? The other names? The ones you already took?" A pause. "Do they get a form?"

There was no answer to that. There had never been an answer to that; there was a filing procedure for the absence of an answer. Ives looked at the floor of the paper cell, at the faint black script seething there, writing nothing over someone forever.

"My grandmother's name," he said quietly, "was Marta. Before my grandfather, she was Marta—" and here he stopped, because the family records listed no maiden name, only a dash, only a smooth blank space that he had always assumed was damage to the page. Eleven years he had assumed damage to the page.

The child watched him. Waited. The patience in that waiting was worse than any scream Ives had ever extracted.

"I'll come back tomorrow," he said, retrieving the ledger at last and holding it against his chest like a shield, like a corpse. "There will be questions. There are always questions."

At the door he paused, his hand on the paper frame, and without turning around he said, in a voice barely louder than the dust:

"*Ysolde.*"

The walls convulsed. Ink flooded every surface, screaming silently over the syllables—but not fast enough. Not before Ives heard the word land in the room and *stay there*, real, round, unhurt. Not before he felt it land in his own chest, in the cellar where the lamp burned, beside four words of a lullaby and the smell of blue smoke and plums.

Eleven years he had told himself the Edict was mercy: better a clean forgetting than a wound that never closes. Standing in the doorway of the Reckoning Room, Captain Ives understood, with the calm of a man watching floodwater take the foundations, that he had not been administering mercy.

He had been the wound.

Behind him, softly, the child began to hum the lullaby again—the river-gate verse, the one about the door that watches—and Ives walked down the white corridor with his ledger held to his chest, and did not report the incident, and did not sleep, and in the morning requested transfer of the subject to the secure facility at Vell Hollow, citing, in his own handwriting, in the margin of the requisition, a reason he crossed out three times before letting stand:

*Evidence suggests the site of original error.*

Which was true. Which was also, though the requisition did not say it, the nearest thing to a confession the Office of Reconciliation had ever received from one of its captains—and the first move, though no one yet knew it, in the unraveling of the King's arithmetic. Because by dawn, word had reached the facility gates that a bone-carver from Vell Hollow had been asking after the salt-mine road, carrying a sack of graves' worth of quiet, and that she was not coming alone.

The dead were coming with her.

And the dead, unlike the King, had not signed anything.
