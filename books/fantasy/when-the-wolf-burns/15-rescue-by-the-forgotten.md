## 15. Rescue by the Forgotten

The dead did not sleep, and that night they did not whisper. They shouted.

Ada stood in the ruined chapel above the ford, her palms pressed flat against the cold stone of the altar, and let them pour into her like water through a broken weir. The miners from the salt deep, still singing, their voices thick with brine and centuries. The peddlers and pickpockets of the Market of Almost-Names, whose names had been traded away a coin at a time until nothing remained but the shape of where a name used to sit. The woman from the oldest grave, who had shown her Ysolde whole—its white cities, its door—and whose memory now burned in Ada's chest like a coal she could not put down.

*They have the child,* said the miners, in unison, the way stone says things.

*They have the ledger,* said the market dead.

*They are writing him into no one,* said all of them at once, and the sound went through her like a blade finding bone.

Ada lifted her hands from the altar. They were shaking, but not with fear. She had spent forty years listening to the quiet griefs of Vell Hollow—the farmer sorry he'd never told his wife he loved her, the drowned boy wanting only to know if his dog had lived. She had bargained with the dead for silence because she had never imagined they might ask her for something else.

Now they were asking. And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what she had always been for: not a carver of memorial bones, but a mouth. A voice for everyone the kingdom had decided had never existed.

"They can't hear you," she said aloud, to the empty chapel, to the crowded air. "But I can. So we're going to make the kingdom listen."

She gathered what she needed by candlelight: her carving knives, wrapped in oilcloth; the six founding bones they'd recovered, sewn into a satchel against her ribs; and the blank paper she had taken from a Reckoner courier's saddlebag three days ago—paper that was not paper at all but pressed erasure, pages waiting to be written on, hungry as mouths.

The dead watched her work. She could feel them leaning close, the way the living lean toward a fire.

"How do we get in?" she asked them. "The fortress at Cael Marn. The wards."

And the market dead—the thief called Nell who had sold her the fifth bone before the fence took it back, the smuggler with no name who had once run paper across three borders—they came forward in her mind like men offering maps.

*Paper wards,* said the smuggler. *Paper fears one thing.*

"What?"

*A crowd that won't fit on it.*

---

They traveled north through two nights of hard rain, Ada on a borrowed mule, Sef's absence riding behind her heavier than any cargo. She did not let herself think about the cell of blank paper. She did not let herself imagine ink moving across skin, across memory, across the small fierce mind that held an entire country inside it. If she thought those things she would stop walking, so she did not think them. She walked. The dead sang cadence to keep her feet moving—Ysoltan marching songs the kingdom had outlawed before her grandmother was born, songs that had survived only in graves and salt and the hollow places where the law forgot to look.

At dawn on the second day, Cael Marn rose out of the mist: a fortress built on a hill of its own demolition, its walls mortared with something pale. Ada knew what the pale stuff was now. She had known since the mine. It was grave-wax, rendered down from the erased and mixed into the King's stone, so that even the walls of justice were built out of people who had never legally existed.

*There,* said the woman in the old grave, whose name had been Mereth when there had been a country to hold names. *Look at the gate.*

Ada looked. The gate was iron, but over the archway, worked into the metal like a watermark, ran lines of script—repeating, endless, the Edict of Reconciliation in letters too small to read at any distance, so that every person who entered passed beneath the sentence *Ysolde never was* and carried it over their head like weather.

"You want me to read it," Ada said. "You want me to speak it wrong."

*We want you to speak it true,* said Mereth. *Say what it is. Say the names. A ward is a sentence, child. Break the sentence and you break the ward.*

Ada dismounted at the tree line and knelt in the wet grass. She took out her knives and the piece of antler she carried for exactly this work, though she had never done this work. Around her, in the grey rain-light, the dead gathered so densely that the air itself seemed to thicken, seemed almost to take on edges, outlines, the suggestion of faces—a market square of ghosts assembled out of everything the kingdom had poured down the well of forgetting.

"I'm going to carve a counter-edict," she said. Her breath fogged. "I need every name you have left. All of them. Give them to me."

And they gave them.

It came like a flood. Names in Ysoltan, names half-worn, names remembered only as sounds—a mother's nickname, a street, a dog, a song's first line. The miners sang theirs. The market dead recited theirs like inventory. Mereth spoke hers last and longest, a genealogy going back nine generations to the founders, to the nine bones, to the door. Ada carved until her fingers bled, filling the antler's surface, then the knife-hafts, then the flat of her forearm where she rolled up her sleeve and cut the script directly into her own skin—not deep, not into bone, not yet, just enough to hold ink made of grave-wax and rainwater and her own blood.

When she stood, she was covered in names. She was, for one vertiginous moment, not Ada Winterbourne at all but a country wearing a woman's body.

"Good," she said hoarsely. "Now let's go be illegal."

---

Cael Marn's outer yard was mud and drill-grounds and Reckoners in their grey coats, and no one stopped a soaked bone-carver trudging toward the servants' postern, because no one saw her. That was the first working of the dead: they stood around her, hundreds of them, and the living eyes slid off as though she were a gap in the world. Forgetting, turned outward. The kingdom's own weapon, aimed back at it.

The inner keep was worse. Here the paper wards hung everywhere—not visibly, but Ada could feel them now that she knew what to feel for: sheets of erasure stretched across thresholds and stairwells like invisible curtains, each one inscribed with the same sentence, each one humming faintly, the way a struck string hums. Crossing them would mean being read. Being read would mean being written. Somewhere below, she knew, Sef sat in a room made entirely of that reading.

*Ives is on the third floor,* whispered a dead clerk—one of the fortress's own dead, a records-keeper who had died twenty years ago and whose ghost still filed papers out of habit. *He has not slept. He has been standing at the cell door since midnight. He is holding his grandmother's lullaby in his mouth and refusing to sing it.*

"Then we'll give him something else to hold," Ada murmured.

They took her down instead, past the wards, into the undercroft where the fortress kept its archive of reconciliation—the ledgers, thousands of them, in which erased lives had been entered and crossed out. Ada stood among the shelves and put her bleeding, name-covered hand against the nearest book.

"Dead of the market," she said softly. "Dead of the mine. Dead of the old grave. Dead of this fortress. You wrote them down. Now watch us write back."

She opened the ledger to a page crossed through with a stranger's name and began, in a clear voice, to speak the name *uncrossed*. Not restored—she didn't have that power—but witnessed. *This person was. This person had a mother, a trade, a way of laughing.* As she spoke, the dead pressed in behind her voice, each one adding what they knew, a chorus of testimony, and the ink on the page began to move—fought, trembled, refused to stay crossed out.

Every ward in the fortress was anchored to these books. Ada understood that the way she understood grain in wood. Every sheet of blank paper hanging invisibly in the air was fed by the ledgers, drew its authority from the sentence *never was*. And a sentence, contradicted loudly enough by enough voices, stops being true.

The humming changed pitch. Somewhere overhead, a Reckoner sentry frowned at the air and felt, without knowing why, that the room he stood in had become a lie.

Ada walked up through the keep, speaking names at every step, and the wards tore like wet paper wherever she passed—she heard them go, small dry ripping sounds, felt the pressure lift, felt the dead surge through the openings like wind through a broken window. By the time she reached the third floor, alarms were ringing and grey-coated men were running in corridors that no longer quite obeyed them, doors opening onto rooms that had been argued out of existence, stairs that forgot their own count halfway up.

She kicked open the door marked RECKONING.

---

Captain Ives stood outside the cell with his hand on the latch and his face the color of the grave-wax in the walls.

He looked terrible. He looked, Ada thought, like a man who had been arguing with a wall and losing. His grey coat was buttoned wrong. In his other hand, clenched so tight the knuckles shone, was a scrap of cloth embroidered in faded thread with notes of music—an old woman's handiwork, decades old, kept against regulation in a pocket where regulation couldn't see.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. His voice came out cracked. Behind him, through the little barred window in the cell door, Ada could see white paper walls, and on them, faintly, horribly, writing beginning to form—Sef's outline dissolving into description, *the prisoner is*, *the prisoner has no*, *there is no prisoner*—

"Open the door, Captain."

"My orders—"

"Your orders say the child doesn't exist." Ada stepped closer. The dead crowded in with her, and the corridor filled with a pressure like the moment before thunder. "You've been standing at this door since midnight because some part of you knows that's a lie. Your grandmother sang you a lullaby, Captain. Where did she learn it? Who taught her? Whose country was it in?"

"Don't." His voice broke clean in half. "You don't know what they do to—"

"I know exactly what they do." She gestured at the cell, at the forming words. "That. They do that. To a child. And you're holding the latch."

For a long moment the only sound was the scratch of invisible ink and the ring of alarms dying somewhere above, as if the fortress itself were losing interest in its own emergency.

Then Ives did something no Reckoner had ever done, in the whole history of the Edict. He reached into his coat, took out the scrap of embroidered cloth, and pressed it to his lips like a man taking communion. And he sang—badly, quietly, four bars of a lullaby in a language the King's law said had never been spoken—and as he sang, the writing on the paper walls of the cell stopped mid-sentence.

*The prisoner has no—*

The sentence simply ended. Could not finish. Because a Ysoltan song was being sung inside a Reckoning Room, and the Room could not reconcile it.

Ives turned the latch himself. The door swung open onto whiteness—and out of the whiteness a small figure stumbled, solid, furious, alive, with ink smudged across one cheek in the shape of a half-written word that was already fading, because no one, nothing, no edict in the world, could make Sef believe even for one heartbeat that Sef did not exist.

Sef looked at Ives. Looked at Ada. And then Sef did the thing that undid Captain Ives completely: reached up and closed the Reckoner's shaking hand gently around the embroidered scrap, and nodded once, the way one adult acknowledges another.

Ada pulled the child against her side and did not cry, because the dead were watching and someone had to be their spine.

"The bones," Sef rasped, voice scraped raw by the paper. "*Hurry.* We have six. There's a seventh under this fortress—I can feel it, Ada, I've always been able to feel them—it's in the foundation. They built the Reckoning Room on top of it."

Ada stared. Of course they had. Of course the kingdom had raised its house of erasure directly over the thing it most feared.

Behind them, Ives straightened, buttoned his coat correctly, and turned to face the corridor, where the sound of returning boots was growing loud.

"Go down," he said. "The east stair. I'll hold the door."

"You'll hang," Ada said.

"I know." And Captain Ives smiled—a small, crooked, freed thing—and drew his sword, and planted himself in the doorway of the room where he had spent his life unwriting people, and began, loudly, in front of thirty witnesses, to sing.
