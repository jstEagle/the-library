## 16. Six, Seven, Eight

The sixth bone sang from the water.

They found the river on the third day out of the salt mine, a brown ribbon sliding through a country of alder and stone, and Ada heard it before she saw it — a thin, wet keening rising up through the soles of her boots, the way grief sounds when it has been drowned a long time. Sef stopped at the bank and looked at her with that expression that had become, over the weeks of their traveling, almost a language between them: eyebrows raised, mouth pressed flat, *do you hear it too?*

"I hear it," Ada said. "It's under the water."

The dead had told her as much, back in the mine, before she had clawed her way out of their choir with Sef's voice in her ears like a rope thrown to a drowning woman. *The river took him in the year of the burning,* the miners had sung, their voices thick with salt and stone. *The river kept what the fire wanted.* A man of Ysolde's founding line, a keeper of bones, fleeing with the relics of his nation in a leather satchel, driven into the ford by Reckoners with torches — and the river had closed over him, and the satchel had split, and one bone had rolled away into the gravel to wait two hundred years for someone who could hear it calling.

Ada knelt at the edge and put her palms flat against the current.

The cold went through her like a blade of glass. Rivers were loud places even for those who couldn't hear the dead; for Ada they were cathedrals of noise, every drop of water carrying its small freight of what had been dissolved into it, and this one carried a man's last afternoon over and over: torchlight on black water, his lungs filling, the weight of the satchel dragging him down even as he tried to shove it free, the strange calm of a man who knows he has done the one thing left to do. He had gotten the bone out first. That was the part that undid her. Drowning, he had held the bone above the surface with both hands until the last moment, then let it drop onto the shallows where the current could carry it somewhere kinder than fire.

*Find me,* he said now, through the water, through two hundred years. *I have been so patient.*

She waded in.

The river was deeper than it looked and colder than it had any right to be, and it fought her the whole way, shoving at her hips and thighs like a crowd of hands. Sef stood on the bank shouting something in Ysoltan — not words she knew yet, but tone enough — and Ada raised one arm to say *stay there* and nearly lost her footing for it. The gravel shifted under her boots. Her fingers went numb past feeling. She worked by sound alone, tilting her head down into the current, following the keening as it swelled from everywhere to somewhere, a bend of the bank where an old alder had fallen and made a trap of roots and stones for anything the water wanted to keep.

The bone was wedged beneath the third root, white as a peeled egg, humming.

It was a radius — she knew her bones the way farmers know weather — and along its length ran carvings so fine she could feel them more than see them, a script like falling rain, the same rain-script she'd seen on the grave-wax woman and the altar-bone in the church at Marrow Ford and the fifth bone she no longer owned because Ives had taken it from her bleeding hands at the crossing. She pulled. The root held it. She pulled again, bracing her shoulder against the alder, and the wood groaned, and the river shoved her, and she thought with perfect clarity: *this is how he died, right here, doing exactly this* — and then the root gave with a crack like a knuckle popping and she fell backward into the shallows with the bone clutched to her chest, gasping, laughing, alive.

Sef dragged her out by the collar. They wrapped her in the wool blanket without asking, the way they did everything now, brisk and wordless and fiercely competent, a child who had buried a whole country and would not bury anyone else.

"Thank you," Ada said, teeth chattering. "Sef. Thank—"

And then the memory went.

She felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave the socket: a small deep pop, a sudden wrongness, a hole where a shape used to be. It was quick this time — quicker than the fence's knife-work in her mind, which had been slow and surgical and had taken her father's face with terrible courtesy. This was just the price collecting itself, interest on a debt already signed. She sat dripping on the bank, holding the carved radius, and reached for the thing she knew she had just been thinking about, and found nothing.

"What did I lose?" she asked. Her voice came out very small. "Sef. What was I just remembering?"

Sef stared at her. Then, slowly, they touched their own chest, then pointed downstream, then mimed — clumsily, terribly — someone swimming. Someone young. Someone laughing.

Ada searched herself. There had been a river in her memory, she understood dimly; there had been swimming, someone else in the water with her, someone whose name should have been right there, at the front, where names lived. There was a smooth pale place instead, like a wall newly plastered. She could remember *that* she had lost something. She could not remember what.

"Was it my mother?" she whispered.

Sef shook their head. They pointed at themselves — hesitated — then shook their head harder, and Ada realized with a lurch that the child didn't know either, couldn't know, that only she had ever known, and now no one did. The memory wasn't gone the way a burned letter is gone. It was gone the way a person is gone when a country is unmade. Somewhere, presumably, it still existed. Just nowhere she could reach.

"Well," Ada said, because the alternative was weeping and there was work to do. She wrung out her hair. "One down. Six bones accounted for." She held up the radius, dripping, singing faintly against her palm. "Two more."

Sef held up two fingers. Then, after a moment, they reached out and took her other hand — the empty one — and held it all the way back to camp.

* * *

The seventh bone was wearing a disguise.

Brother Anselm told them about it in the refectory of the monastery at Saint Verrin's Rest, a grey place built into the shoulder of a grey mountain, where the monks grew herbs and said nothing and asked nothing, which was why Ada had chosen it. She'd come for shelter and hot broth; she'd stayed when the old monk's eyes went strange at the sight of Sef, strange in the way of a man hearing an echo from a room he'd been told was empty.

"There's a finger," Anselm said, "in the reliquary. Saint Verrin's own, we say. Third-class relic, gilded, kissed by pilgrims for four hundred years." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "Only Verrin was a real man, girl. I've read his letters. He had all ten fingers, and I'll tell you how I know — he complained about his hangnails in every single one of them. A man does not complain of hangnails and then lose a finger without remark."

Ada's heart began to beat somewhere up around her throat. "What is it, then?"

"That," Anselm said, "is what I have wondered for forty years." He looked at Sef a long moment. "Show her the script," he said, though Ada hadn't asked. "Show the bone-carver the script on your hand."

Sef hesitated. Then they pulled back their sleeve and traced, in the air above the table, the falling-rain characters — the ones the dead wrote, the ones carved into every relic, the ones that had begun appearing unbidden on the backs of Sef's own hands since the mine, since the ledger inside them had started leaking. Anselm watched with his breath held.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Yes, that's the mark under the gold. I scraped a corner once, when I was young and arrogant. There's writing underneath. The same kind." He folded his hands. "I have spent my life telling myself it was a saint's bone from some older saint, some name the church misplaced. But misplaced is not the word, is it. Misplaced things can be found."

"No," Ada said. "Unmade things can't."

He nodded as if she'd confirmed a bill he'd long expected. "The reliquary is in the crypt chapel. The prior locks it at night, but the lock is older than my knees and I know its moods." He rose, joints cracking like green firewood. "Come at the second bell. And carver—" He paused at the doorway, not turning around. "Whatever it is you're reassembling, do it quickly. There are men in the valley below asking questions. Grey coats. Very polite. They asked me if I'd seen any *unregistered persons*."

Ada felt the cold walk up her spine and sit down.

"They described the child?" she managed.

"They described a *ledger*. As if the child were a book that had grown legs." Anselm's mouth twisted. "I told them monks see nothing but God. God forgive me if that was a lie twice over."

At the second bell they went down.

The crypt chapel smelled of stone and candle-fat and centuries, and the reliquary sat behind iron grillwork like a birdcage for something that had never flown. Anselm's key worked the way he promised, complaining but obedient, and inside, ranked on velvet gone grey with age, lay the saints' modest treasury: a tooth, a scrap of robe, a vial of dust, and a golden finger, curved slightly, mounted upright on a brass base, polished blind by ten thousand pilgrim lips.

Ada lifted it out and weighed it in her palm and knew immediately, the way she always knew, that it was no finger. Fingers were small and light and ended in a certain way. This was heavy, dense as a promise, and when she tilted it the gold flaked away at one end where Anselm's youthful arrogance had done its work, and beneath the gilt the surface was not bone-smooth but *carved*, rain-script running ring after ring down its length, and the moment her bare skin met the exposed carving, the keening began — high, urgent, joyful, a sound like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

"It knows," she breathed. "Sef, it knows us. It's been waiting under all that gold, listening to prayers it couldn't answer, for—"

A memory left her.

This one she felt like a door slamming. One instant she was mid-sentence; the next, the middle of her life simply wasn't there. She gripped the altar rail. Fragments swam up around the hole — she could remember learning to carve, could remember her mother's workshop, could remember the smell of marrow-oil — but the *first* carving, the first time her hands had shaped a memorial bone, the story she'd told a hundred times about the sparrow and her ninth winter and how her mother had laughed — gone. Plastered over. A smooth place where a foundation stone used to be.

Two bones. Two pieces of her. And the arithmetic was simple and terrible and getting simpler: nine bones to find, and she had given up counting what remained of herself to give.

"Ada?" Sef's voice — actual voice, cracked and rusted from disuse, the voice they spent like coins hoarded from a dying mint. They only spoke now in extremity. Their hand was on her arm. "Ada. What did it take?"

"A sparrow," she said, before she could stop herself, and then blinked. "I don't — apparently I know there was a sparrow. I don't know why."

Sef's face did something complicated and fierce. They took the gilded bone from her hands, wrapped it in cloth, and stowed it in the satchel with the others, and then they did the thing that undid her worse than any loss: they pressed their small fist flat against her sternum, right over the center of her, and held it there, the way you'd hold pressure on a wound.

*I'm keeping count,* the gesture said. *Someone has to.*

* * *

The eighth bone was in Captain Ives's family chest, and Ada learned this from Ives himself.

He came to them at the monastery gates at dawn, alone, unarmed, in a grey coat with the buttons undone, looking like a man who had slept in a chair for a week. Sef saw him first and went rigid as a fencepost, and Ada stepped in front of the child with a bone-knife she had no intention of using and every intention of showing.

"Peace," Ives said, and the word sounded foreign in his mouth, a borrowed tool. "I'm not here for the requisition. I'm here because—" His jaw worked. He looked, Ada thought, like a ledger trying to balance itself and failing. "Because I need to show you something. And then I need you to take something from me. And I would rather do it standing up than have you do it over my body later."

"You betrayed us at the ford," Ada said. "Your men took the fifth bone."

"My men took the fifth bone. I signed the order." He didn't flinch from it, and she hated him a little less for that. "I've been correcting the error since. Slowly. In ways that won't get the child recatalogued." He glanced at Sef, and something moved behind his face, something old and half-buried, a song remembered in a grandmother's kitchen. "There's a house in Lowmark. My family's house. My grandfather kept a chest that he forbade everyone to open, and his father before him, and so on back. When I was a boy I opened it anyway." He laughed, short and awful. "You want to hear something mad, carver? I opened it, and I saw what was in it, and by supper I couldn't tell you what I'd seen. Only that the chest was important and the seeing was forbidden. I thought I'd dreamed it. I thought that for thirty years."

"The chest forgets itself into you," Ada said softly. The dead had told her about such wards, old Ysoltan protections laid on hiding places, erasure turned inward, a lock made of forgetting. "It takes the memory of its contents and leaves the warning."

"And yesterday," Ives said, "in the Reckoning Room, a prisoner hummed a lullaby, and I remembered the chest, and I remembered what was in it, all at once, like ice going down." His hands were shaking. She could see them shaking, the same hands that had signed the order at the ford, and they were shaking now. "It's a bone. Carved all over in the script. My family has been keeping a piece of a murdered country in our attic for six generations, calling it a *heirloom*, and the Edict says contraband history must be surrendered for burning, and I am an officer of the Edict, and I am here to ask you to steal it from me."

The wind came down off the mountain and moved the frost-grass and nobody spoke.

"Why?" Ada asked finally. Not cruelly. She genuinely needed to hear it.

Ives looked at the sunrise, which was doing nothing remarkable, just happening the way sunrises do, indifferent and orange.

"Because my grandmother sang that lullaby," he said, "and I forgot, because forgetting was the law, and I obeyed the law my whole life, and it turns out the law was eating my own house while I held the door for it." He straightened. "Lowmark is two days east. The house has my sister in it, and she will scream, and possibly my old sergeant, who still reports to me, or thinks he does. After this, I can't go back. You understand that. Whatever I am after this morning, it isn't a Reckoner anymore."

"After this," Ada said, "you're whatever the dead decide you are. They're less forgiving than the Edict."

"Good," said Captain Ives. "I'd trust them more."

They walked east through the frost, three of them now, an erased officer, a hollowing-out bone-carver, and the last citizen of nowhere, and Ada counted as she walked, the way Sef's fist against her sternum had taught her to count. Eight bones soon. One remaining. And the dead, whispering at the edges of her hearing all that day, in the rain-script that was starting to appear — she noticed with a distant, floating terror — along the bones of her own forearms, kept saying the thing they had begun saying in the mine, the thing she had hoped was grief-talk and now feared was instruction:

*Nine is not a thing. Nine is a person. Nine walks beside you.*

And Sef, glancing up at her, held up one finger — not eight, not the number of bones remaining, but *one* — and would not explain.
