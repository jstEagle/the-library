## 17. The Ninth Bone Is a Person

Sef had learned, in the years since the burning, that memory had a weight.

Not a metaphor's weight—Ada would have hated that; Ada believed in the true names of things—but an actual heaviness, like river stones carried in the mouth. Every name pressed into Sef's mind sat somewhere in the body. The names of the archivists lodged behind the eyes. The names of the salt miners settled into the chest, so that breathing felt like hauling buckets up a long dark stair. The names of Ysolde's kings and queens lay along the spine, one to each vertebra, and when Sef stood very straight on cold mornings, they seemed to hold them upright, the way a mast holds a sail.

And there was one name that lived nowhere at all, that was not stored but simply *was* — the name Sef could never say aloud in front of strangers because it slid out of their ears like water off oiled cloth. The name that made neighbors blink and forget their tea. The name written now, faintly, in scar tissue over Sef's own heart, carved there by a small determined hand on the night the archives burned: *keep this.*

Sef kept it. Sef kept everything.

That was the whole of what Sef was, in the end. A country's worth of keeping, walking around in a child's coat that smelled of woodsmoke and Ada's tallow candles.

* * *

They found the threshold three days after Ives gave back the fifth bone—or rather, after Ives set it down in the frost between them and walked away without looking back, his jaw working as though he were chewing something too large to swallow. The dead had led them, singing through Ada's bones in a language that made her fingers twitch even when she wasn't holding a carving knife. Down past the ford where Tamrin's betrayal still stained the water with a particular kind of silence. Up the far slope, where the pines grew crooked from wind and older griefs. To a hillside that was not a hillside.

Sef knew it before anyone said anything. Knew it the way you know your mother's footsteps on a stair.

The hill was the Archive. Or what remained of it—the great mound of Ysolde's remembering, buried under earth and centuries and the King's careful arithmetic, its door sealed with nine locks, and the locks were bones, and the bones were scattered, and eight of them now hung in a leather sling across Ada's shoulder, clicking softly together when she walked, like patient teeth.

Ada knelt at the hillside and put her palm flat against the grass.

"They're here," she said. "All of them. The whole choir." Her voice did the thing it always did when the dead spoke loudly—a roughness, as if she were repeating words spoken through cloth. "Sef. They're saying—" She stopped. Started again. "They're saying the last lock is already here."

"Here?" Sef looked around. Wind-bent pine. Frost. A crow considering them from a bare branch with the frank greed of all crows everywhere. "Where?"

Ada turned. And Sef watched her face do something terrible and slow, the way ice does when it decides to become water—not suddenly, but with an awful inevitability, crack by crack.

"Sef," Ada said. "Come here. Sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Sit down anyway."

Sef sat. The frost soaked through the trousers immediately. Ada crouched in front of them, took both their hands in hers. Her hands were a carver's hands, nicked and scarred and steady, and they were not steady now.

"The dead are telling me how the door opens," she said. "Nine bones. Eight relics. And the ninth—" She looked at the sling, counted with her eyes, came up short the way Sef had come up short for years, carrying more than any ledger should hold. "The ninth founding bone is the blood of the old line. The last citizen of the royal house of Ysolde. The living ledger, because a door needs a key that remembers everything on the other side of it."

The crow shifted on its branch.

"No," Sef said.

"Sef—"

"That's not—I'm not a *bone*. I'm not a thing in your sling. I'm a person." Their voice cracked in the middle and they hated it, hated being twelve, hated the way the word *person* came out thin as paper. "I'm a person, Ada."

"I know." Ada's thumbs moved over the backs of Sef's hands, slow circles, the gesture she used when the dead got loud at night and Sef couldn't sleep. "I know exactly what you are. But the door doesn't care what we know. It cares what it was built to need."

"What happens," Sef said, "to the key."

Ada didn't answer. Which was an answer, and both of them knew it, and the frost kept soaking through, and the crow finally gave up and flapped away downslope, and somewhere under the hill eight hundred thousand names waited for a door that could only be opened by being erased.

* * *

That night they made camp against the buried Archive's flank, and Sef could not sleep, and so did the thing they always did instead: opened the ledger.

Not literally. There was no book. There had been a book once—forty volumes, vellum and iron clasps, the Register of Citizens—and Sef had been locked in the scriptorium with it on the night the Reckoners came with their torches, because the Head Archivist had been a desperate man with a desperate idea, and a dying magic older than kingdoms, and one child whose blood ran in the old royal line and who therefore, by laws no one had needed in two hundred years, could be made to *hold* things.

*Keep this,* he'd said, pressing Sef's hand to the first page. *Forgive me.*

Then the names began.

Sef lay on their back under the pines and let them rise, because fighting them only made them louder. Names rose like silt in stirred water. Merel Ondrach, cooper, third street of the Lower Terraces, who whistled while he worked and always whistled flat. The twins Asha and Ashen Vey, born in the same hour, who finished each other's sentences until the day the sentence was finished for them. Queen Ismene the Ninth, who had planted a pear tree in the palace courtyard and eaten its fruit every autumn of her reign, unripe and sour, because she said a queen should taste what her country tasted. On and on, tier upon tier, the whole population of a nation stacked in one skull like bones in a charnel pit, orderly and patient and *waiting*, and beneath all of it, at the very bottom of the stack, one name that glowed like a coal under ash:

*Sef.*

Their own. The first entry in the new register. Keep this.

Except now, lying in the frost with the hill humming faintly beneath them like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, Sef understood something they had refused to understand in the scriptorium, something the Head Archivist must have known when he pressed their hand to the page and apologized with his eyes.

A ledger is meant to be read. That is what makes it a ledger and not a grave.

And the door out of Ysolde—the door that would restore the country, un-erase the streets and the songs and the sour pears and the flat-whistling coopers—that door read its key. All of it. Consumed the register in the opening, the way fire consumes the candle. The willing sacrifice of the ledger itself. Those had been the dead's exact words through Ada's mouth, and Ada had flinched at each one as if the words were hot.

If Sef became the ninth bone, Ysolde would live.

And Sef would—

Sef tried to imagine it and found, horribly, that they could. They had spent four years being forgotten by everyone they met. They knew the shape of absence from the outside. What they didn't know—what no one could tell them—was whether it hurt from the inside, or whether it was simply nothing, the way sleep was nothing, the way the space between one bell-strike and the next was nothing.

They decided they didn't want to find out. And then, in the same breath, in the same traitorous heart, they decided that eight hundred thousand people deserved to exist more than one child deserved to keep existing, and both decisions sat in their chest together like two stones in one mouth, and Sef lay awake between them until the sky went grey.

* * *

In the morning, Ada was already awake, sitting cross-legged with the eight bones laid out on a spread blanket, studying them the way she studied difficult carvings—with narrowed eyes and absolute silence and her lower lip caught in her teeth.

"They're warm," she said, without looking up. "Bones shouldn't be warm. Dead things run cold. That's most of what being dead means." She touched one—a knuckle-joint, dense and yellowed, pulled from the riverbed at cost of the memory of her father's laugh—and drew her finger back quickly. "They're pulling toward the hill. Like compass needles."

Sef sat down across from her. The frost had melted where they slept, in a neat outline, which Ada pretended not to notice and Sef pretended not to notice her pretending.

"If I do it," Sef said. "If I open the door."

"We're not doing this."

"You have to hear it. You always say the dead deserve to be heard. Well, I'm almost dead, aren't I? I'm the last citizen. Hear me." The words came out faster now, tumbling, the ledger rising in their throat. "If I open it, Ysolde comes back. Everyone comes back. Merel Ondrach wakes up in his workshop and whistles flat and doesn't know he ever stopped. Queen Ismene eats her sour pear. The whole country, Ada. Everything they took. Everything they *burned*." Their hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against their knees. "Versus me. One person. Who's twelve. Who can't even make people remember my name for a full minute."

Ada reached across the blanket and stilled Sef's hands with her own.

"Listen to me," she said, and her voice had dropped into the register she used for the newly dead, the gentle terrible voice, "because I've spent my whole life listening to the dead, and I'm going to tell you what they never get to hear. Being remembered isn't the same as being alive. And being alive isn't measured in how many people carry your name." Her grip tightened. "You asked me once why I carve memorial bones. For strangers. People nobody paid me to carve. Do you know why?"

Sef shook their head.

"Because the dead told me that being forgotten is the only real death there is. And I believed them for thirty years." She laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "But I've been watching you, these last months. You walk around carrying a murdered country in your head, and every person you meet forgets you, and you *still*—you feed the horse first. You learn the words to songs just to give them back to people. You keep a dead woman's grave tidy because it bothered you that the weeds were winning." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "That's alive, Sef. That's more alive than anyone I've ever carved a bone for. And I will not trade that. Not for a country. Not for ten countries. I didn't dig through salt mines and burn markets and give up my father's face so I could hand the King's Reckoners their arithmetic done for them—one life weighed against many, balanced, reconciled." She spat the word. "I am *not* a Reckoner."

"But the door—"

"The door," Ada said, "can find another way or it can stay shut. We didn't come this far to open it with you inside."

And Sef wanted, desperately, to believe her. Wanted to fall into that voice the way they fell asleep to it on bad nights. But the ledger was awake now, all of it, eight hundred thousand souls leaning against the inside of their skull like a crowd against a door, and they could feel what the dead wanted, and what the dead wanted was *in*, was *home*, was *open*, and their wanting was so vast and so patient and so old that one child's refusal felt like a twig arguing with a river.

"There's something else," Sef said quietly. "Something I haven't told you. About the night of the burning."

Ada went still.

"The Head Archivist didn't just press the names into me. He said—he made me swear—that the ledger has a second use. A counter-use." Sef swallowed. "The Edict of Reconciliation works by un-writing. That's its power. But an edict is just words, and words can be re-written by whoever holds the original register." They looked down at their own chest, at the scar tissue over their heart. "It's not just a door key, Ada. It's the counter-edict. Whoever carries the ledger into the threshold can speak Ysolde back into law. Un-un-write it. Make it have always existed." Their voice dropped to almost nothing. "Or speak themselves into the foundation stone instead, and let the country stand on their erasure. He said it was always going to come down to that choice. He said he was sorry. He said it a lot, that night."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The pines creaked. Under the hill, the choir hummed its single held note.

"So it's not just the door," Ada said at last. "It's the whole edict. The whole rotten law."

"It's the whole war," Sef said. "Fought with words. Fought with *me*."

Ada gathered the eight bones back into their sling with slow, deliberate movements, the movements of a woman buying herself time to think. When she spoke again, her voice was level in the way Sef had learned to distrust—the calm of deep water over something enormous moving underneath.

"Then we go down together," she said. "You and me and the dead. And when we get to that threshold, nobody makes any choices alone. Not the door. Not the archive. Not you." She slung the bones across her shoulder and stood, and held out her hand. "Swear it."

Sef looked at the hand. Carver's hands. Steady now, when it mattered.

"I swear," Sef said, and took it.

And beneath the hill, soft as snowfall, the choir changed its note—for the first time in four days, the dead stopped singing the waiting song, and began, faintly and unmistakably, to sing the other one. The one every Ysoltan child knew. The lullaby about the wolf who guarded the door of the world, and how the only way past him was to burn.

Downslope, at the tree line, something glinted that was not frost and not sunlight: steel, and a great many figures moving with the unhurried discipline of people who had all the time in the world, because they believed the world was already theirs.

Captain Ives had found them. And he had not come alone.
