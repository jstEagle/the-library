## 18. The Door That Was a Country

The ninth bone had led them down.

That was the thing no song had said, no grave had whispered: that the door out of Ysolde would not be found by climbing toward it but by descending, by going beneath the roots of the valley and the roots of the ridge and the roots of everything Ada had ever believed firm in the world. For three days they had followed the pull of the carved vertebra in Sef's hands—the last citizen's own finger-bone, which Sef had offered up without weeping, though their eyes had gone the gray of river ice—and the bone had grown heavier with every step, until at last it dragged them downward like an anchor thrown into deep water, through a fissure in the limestone that no map acknowledged, into a dark that smelled of old rain and older stone.

Now they stood before it.

Ada had imagined doors her whole life—doors of iron banded with wolf-heads, doors of yew wood black as bog-oak, doors carved with the nine founding bones arranged like teeth in a jaw. She had imagined wrong. The door was simply *there*, a threshold drawn in the living rock where the cave wall changed its mind about being a wall. It stood open. Of course it stood open; it had been waiting three hundred years for someone to arrive, and doors that wait that long do not bother with locks.

Through it poured light that was not firelight, not daylight, not moonlight. It was the light of a country remembering itself.

And through it, Ysolde.

Not the Ysolde of grave-wax memories and half-remembered songs, not the flickering afterimage the dead had shown her in salt mines and market stalls. This Ysolde was *whole*. Ada stood on the threshold with her hand pressed flat against stone still warm from the sun of another sky, and she looked out across terraced hills stitched with barley, golden and rippling under a wind that carried the smell of bread baking somewhere out of sight. A city rose in the middle distance, white-walled, red-roofed, its towers crowned with weathervanes shaped like wolves mid-howl. Rivers ran silver through orchards. And everywhere—everywhere—people. Farmers bent to their rows. Children chased a hoop along a cobbled street. A woman leaned from an upper window beating a rug, pausing to call something down to a neighbor, laughing at the answer.

They were not ghosts. Ada knew ghosts; she had spent forty years elbow-deep in them. These were the unmade ones, the erased, held here in the door's keeping like seeds in a granary, waiting for the season of their replanting. Every one of them a person the Edict had scraped from the world's ledger. Every one of them a name pressed into a mute child's skull.

"Sef." Her voice came out rougher than she intended, swallowed by the vastness of what she saw. "Sef, look at me."

But Sef was already walking forward.

* * *

The child moved the way sleepwalkers move, sure-footed and elsewhere, the finger-bone held out before them like a lamp. The light of the other country lay on their face and made them look both younger and impossibly ancient, and Ada understood—with the sick lurch of a woman stepping off a stair that isn't there—that Sef could see what she saw, only more. Where Ada saw a country, Sef saw the names. Each farmer in his field, each child with her hoop, each laugh floating down from the window: all of them lived inside Sef, pressed there the night the archives burned, when the last archivists had done the only desperate arithmetic left to them and poured a nation's memory into the smallest vessel they could find.

"Stop," Ada said, and caught the child's shoulder.

Sef turned. Their mouth opened, and what came out was not the dead tongue, not any language Ada had learned to hear through bone and wax and grave-dirt over these hard months of road. It was simpler than language. It was the look of a child standing before the sum of everything they have lost, and it asked a question no translation was needed for:

*Can I give it back?*

"No." The word came out of Ada like a tooth pulled. "No, listen to me—"

Sef shook her off—not violently, never violently; even now, carrying a country in their head, the child was gentle—and walked to the very lip of the threshold. And there the door showed Ada its price.

It rose out of the ground before them like mist taking an oath: the architecture of the restoration, written in the same light that gilded the barley fields. The eight bones they had gathered—a knuckle from the riverbed, the false saint's finger from the altar at Corve Marsh, Ives's great-grandmother's rib from the family chest he had broken open with his own hands—all of them hung suspended in the air of the doorway, turning slowly, each one glowing like a coal wrapped in silk. Eight points of light. And between them, a shape waited. An empty socket. A keystone's absence, drawn in outline, exactly the size and silhouette of a small body standing very straight.

The door did not speak. It didn't need to. Ada read it the way she read gravestones, the way she read the grain of bone: fluently, unwillingly, completely.

To raise Ysolde whole—to pour those people back into flesh, to set the city on its hill and the songs back in the throats that had sung them first—the door required a foundation. Not a stone. Stones were for lesser doors. This door had been built to hold a country against the weight of an empire's forgetting, and it needed a keystone that could not be pried loose because it would not merely sit in the arch but *become* it. Willing. Living. Erased into the foundation so thoroughly that not even the dead would remember there had ever been anything to remember.

The last citizen of Ysolde, become the floor of Ysolde. Walking on their own bones forever. Singing under ten thousand restored feet.

"That's not restoration," Ada said aloud, to the door, to the light, to whatever kept this granary of the erased. "That's just your edict with prettier handwriting."

The light did not dim. Doors do not argue. They only wait, and this one had gotten very good at waiting.

* * *

Sef had gone still at the threshold, and Ada saw the temptation take root in the child's face the way frost takes root in a window—silently, beautifully, fatally. She had seen that expression before, on the faces of the dead who lingered too long at their gravesides, wanting one more hour among the living. But this was worse. This was a child who had spent years being forgotten by everyone who looked at them, whose own name slid off strangers' minds like rain off slate, and now before them stood a whole country of people who would know them—who *were* them, in part, carried in their skull—and the door was offering the only belonging Sef had ever been promised.

"You'd be alone down there," Ada said. She crouched, bringing her face level with the child's, the way she had done on the first storm-night at her door in Vell Hollow, when the world had been small and cold and manageable. "Underneath. Forever. No one walks on a keystone and thinks of it. No one sings to a floor. You'd feel them, Sef. Every footstep, every festival, every wedding dance—you'd be under all of it, holding it up, and none of them would ever once say thank you, because none of them would ever know you'd been anything but ground."

Sef's eyes filled. The child's hands closed around the finger-bone until the knuckles went white.

"And it wouldn't even work," Ada pressed on, hating herself for the cruelty of logic and loving the truth too much to soften it. "You told me—Ives told me—the Edict doesn't kill what it erases. It makes the world agree it never happened. What do you think a keystone is? It's the world agreeing. You'd be the world agreeing to forget yourself. And then some other Reckoner, fifty years on, a hundred years, would come with new paper and new arithmetic, and they'd burn *this* door too, and Ysolde would fall again, and this time you'd fall with it and there'd be no one—no one at all—left to carry the names out."

The wind off the barley fields stirred Sef's hair. Somewhere in that impossible country, a bell rang vespers, sweet and unhurried, a sound from a nation that officially did not exist.

"I won't allow it," Ada said. "I'm sorry. I know that's not mine to decide. I know it's your country and your name and your choice, and if you were grown I'd stand aside and weep and let you do it. But you're not grown. You're a child who likes honey on bread and sleeps with your boots on and laughs at my carving mistakes. So no." She reached out and took the child's hand, prying the finger-bone gently from it. "Find another way or lose it entire, but I will not watch you lie down and become a floor."

For a long moment Sef only looked at her. Then, slowly, the child lifted their free hand and pressed it flat against their own sternum—over the heart, over the place where, Ada knew, the pressed names sat thickest—and shook their head. Not refusal. Grief. The grief of someone setting down a burden they have carried so long they cannot remember their shoulders without it.

Then Sef signed—the quick, private grammar they had built together across a thousand miles of road—and the sign meant *home*, and it pointed, not at the shining country, but backward, north, over the ridge where magic died, to a drafty house in Vell Hollow where a woman carved bones and bargained with the dead for quiet.

Ada's throat closed. She turned away before the child could see her face crumple, and it was in that moment of turned attention—her eyes on the dark tunnel behind them rather than the bright doorway ahead—that the light changed.

* * *

She smelled the smoke before she understood it.

It came curling down the fissure behind them, thin and acrid, and it was wrong in every way smoke could be wrong: it had no warmth, no memory of wood, nothing of the honest chemistry of burning things. It smelled of ink. Of paper. Of the specific, sterile char of a state destroying evidence.

"They followed us." Ada spun, pushing Sef behind her, her hand finding the carving knife at her belt—the knife she had shaped memorials with for forty years, and which had lately learned less merciful work. "How did they—"

The answer arrived before the question finished. Down the tunnel came lantern-light, bobbing, orderly, a procession of flames that burned without hunger, and at its head walked Captain Ives.

He looked terrible. She had watched him deteriorate since Corve Marsh, since the night he had broken open his own family chest and handed her his great-grandmother's rib with shaking hands and would not meet anyone's eyes. Now he looked like a man who had been hollowed out and refilled with someone else's duty, and the seams were showing. Behind him marched six Reckoners in their gray coats, their faces the smooth blankness of men who had been issued their orders and had chosen, for their own survival, not to think them through.

And behind the Reckoners, carried on a bier of black iron like a relic of some inverted church, came the fire.

Ada had seen Reckoner fire twice before—at the Market of Almost-Names, where they had fed centuries of stolen memories into a brazier while the fence wept into his accounts, and at the ford, where they had burned the cart with the fifth bone inside it. She had thought she understood it: contraband history, consigned to flame. She had not understood until now that the fire was not a tool. It was a *jurisdiction*. It ate what the Edict declared had never existed, and it was straining toward the open door like a hound scenting home.

"The door burns," Ives said. His voice was flat, but she heard the fracture running through it, hairline-thin. "By order of the Edict of Reconciliation. The threshold is unregistered history. Everything unregistered burns."

"It's a *country*," Ada said. "It's thirty thousand people. Look at it. Just—look at it, Ives. Tell me you can't see them."

"I can see them." Something moved behind his eyes, brief and agonized. "My grandmother sang me a lullaby in their language. In a cell of blank paper, a child sang it back to me, note for note, and I have not slept properly since." He raised one hand, and the Reckoners halted, the bier of black fire settling onto the cave floor with a sound like a book closing. "I am not blind, bone-carver. I am *employed*. There is a difference, and the difference is what keeps my family registered, and fed, and real."

"Your family is *from* here." Ada took a step sideways, putting herself squarely between the fire and the door, between the fire and Sef. "You told me. Half the names in that city are probably your kin. You want to burn your grandmother's cradle-song?"

"I want," Ives said, and his voice cracked clean through, "to stop being the man who has to choose. And since the King has declined to permit that—" he drew himself up, and the fracture sealed over, and he became, horribly, entirely, a Reckoner again— "the door burns. Stand aside, Winterbourne."

"No."

"You're standing in front of a fire that eats countries."

"Then it will have to eat through me," Ada said, "and I promise you, Captain, there are more dead in this mountain than in your whole gray registry, and every single one of them owes me a favor."

Behind her, the light of Ysolde pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat. Above the white city the weathervane wolves turned on their rods, howling at a wind she could almost feel. The bell rang vespers again, and this time Sef answered it—stood at Ada's shoulder and lifted their voice in the dead tongue, clear and high and unafraid, singing back across three hundred years of official silence, a duet between a child and a bell.

And in the tunnel, the black fire began to rise from its bier, hungry and paper-pale, and Ives gave the order with a face like a man signing his own confession.

"Burn it."

Ada drew her knife and reached for the dead.
