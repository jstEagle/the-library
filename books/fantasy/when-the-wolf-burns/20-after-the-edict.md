## 20. After the Edict

There is a shape in the morning where something used to be.

Ada knows this the way a tongue knows a missing tooth—not by its absence but by the ache of reaching for it. She wakes in her own bed in Vell Hollow, in her own narrow house with the bone-dust still sifted into the floorboards' grain, and she wakes knowing that something is gone from inside her head. Not a thought. Not a dream. A *country's worth* of something, scooped out like marrow from a split femur, and what remains is the hollow sound of it.

Her arm aches.

She lifts it into the grey light coming through the shutters and finds the wound already scabbing—a long, careful incision along the inner forearm, stitched in her own hand's unmistakable style, the small even stitches she has used on corpses and on carvings for thirty years. She did this to herself. She knows her own needlework the way she knows her own heartbeat.

She cannot remember doing it.

That should terrify her. It does, a little, a cold fish-flop of fear somewhere behind her ribs. But mostly it feels like standing at the edge of a field where a fire went through last season: the ground is black, the shapes of things are readable, and nothing is left to burn. Whatever was here, it finished its work.

On the table beside the bed lies a bone she doesn't recognize.

It's a radius—human or near-human, cured and cleaned and polished until it glows faintly cream in the dawn light—and carved down its whole length in script she has never seen and yet somehow, in the way one recognizes the cadence of a lullaby heard through a wall, understands she could once read. The characters are angular and patient, rows of them, like a fence built letter by letter across the bone's face. Her fingers know the grooves. Her fingertips have walked these paths before; there is a smoothness to the deepest cuts that only comes from being traced a thousand times.

The dead are quiet about it.

This is the strangest thing—the thing she keeps circling back to as she rises, as she builds up the fire, as she stands at her door looking out at Vell Hollow wearing its autumn fog like a bandage over a healing wound. The dead are always talking. They murmur in her marrow the way water murmurs in a well; she has not known silence since she was nine years old. But today they are quiet about *this*. When she holds the bone up and asks them—asks the old woman in the churchyard, asks the drowned boy under the mill, asks any of the hundreds of voices she carries—they go still in a way the dead never do. It is not silence. It is the silence of people turning their faces away from a grave.

They are grieving something. She can feel it in them, a vast and wordless mourning, like weather pressing on the far side of a window.

She just cannot find out what.

* * *

The coat is on the peg by the door.

A child's coat. Wool, dyed a deep river-green, patched at both elbows with stitches finer than any valley seamstress would bother with, and smelling of smoke—not hearth-smoke, not peat-smoke, but the acrid sweet reek of burning paper and burning varnish and burning wood, the smell of a building giving itself up. Under the smoke there is another smell: tallow, travel dust, and something herbal she almost places. Something she is certain she has smelled pressed close against her neck on a cold night on a road she cannot picture.

She takes the coat down and holds it and does not put it on, because it is far too small for her, obviously, and because putting it on would be absurd, and because her arms want to anyway, want to wrap it around herself the way you'd hold something warm that had fallen asleep against your chest.

"Whose is this?" she says aloud, to the house, to the dead.

The dead turn their faces further away.

Outside, the fog is lifting off the hollow in long ragged ribbons. The ridge stands above the valley as it always has, dark with pine, sharp against a washed sky, and there is a new scar on it—a burn line, high up near the saddle pass, a streak of raw black earth where trees used to stand. She looks at that scar and her heart does something complicated: hammers, then stumbles, then goes very quiet, the way it does when she stands over a fresh grave and the person beneath is someone whose name she never learned.

Something happened up there. Something happened *through* there.

She remembers—she checks, carefully, the way you probe a bruise—she remembers her life whole. Father's forge, mother's garden, the fever when she was nine that opened her ears to the dead, the apprenticeship, the first bone she ever carved, the slow accumulation of years in this valley where magic cannot follow and so she stayed, stayed, stayed. All of it intact. All of it present.

But after this summer—

After this summer there is a gap shaped like everything.

* * *

She walks down into the village because walking is better than standing, and because if something happened—if something *big* happened, big enough to leave scars on ridges and wounds on forearms—then other people will remember it. Other people will have pieces she's missing.

Marta at the bakery is pulling loaves when Ada comes in, and the smell of rye and hot crust is so ordinary it nearly undoes her.

"Marta," she says. "I need—I'm missing some days. Weeks, maybe. What happened? At the end of summer."

Marta looks at her with mild blue eyes and says, "End of summer?" and turns a loaf. "Harvest came in early. Wet August. Why?"

"The ridge," Ada presses. "The burn scar on the ridge. The pass."

Marta frowns toward the window, toward the distant dark line of the heights, and Ada watches her face do something strange: watch it reach for a memory and find only smoothness, like a hand running over a wall where a door used to be painted.

"Had a fire up there once," Marta says slowly. "Years ago. Lightning, I think." She shakes her head. "You've been carving too long, Isandro—Ada. You're pale. Sit. Eat."

And that—that is the moment the true dread arrives, cold and complete. Because Marta is not lying. Marta's mind has been tidied, the way a room is tidied after a death, all evidence gathered and carried out and burned. Whatever happened, it didn't just take Ada's memories. It took the *valley's*. A whole country's worth of erasure, and it fell on Vell Hollow too, like ash falling evenly on every roof.

Ada eats the bread Marta gives her. It tastes like nothing. Everything tastes like nothing today; her tongue reports flavors the way a clerk reports numbers he doesn't believe.

Walking home past the churchyard, she stops at the oldest stone—the grave older than the kingdom, the one she found in the spring, the one whose markings matched a language no one alive should speak. She kneels. The moss has grown over the carved letters, thick and green and total, as though decades have passed instead of months. She scrapes at it with her thumbnail and the moss comes away—but underneath, the letters themselves are softening, blurring, the stone forgetting its own inscription, edges rounding as if rain had been working them for a century.

Even the graves are forgetting.

She puts her palm flat on the stone and reaches down into the dead the way she has done ten thousand times, and the woman who sleeps below is there, and the woman is weeping, and the weeping has no name attached to it anymore. Grief without an object. A funeral for a country that left no body, no portrait, no surviving kin to mourn it—only a mourner who has forgotten whom she buried.

"I'm sorry," Ada tells her, not knowing what else to say. "I'm sorry. I think I was supposed to fix this. I think I tried."

The dead woman weeps. Somewhere in the churchyard's crowded dark, dozens of others weep with her, silently, in unison, a congregation facing an empty altar.

* * *

The days after that are a study in living around a hole.

She returns to her work because her hands demand it. Bone-carving is the only prayer she has ever trusted, and her hands are steady even when nothing else is. She carves memorial bones for the winter dead, and her chisel finds its old grooves, and the work is good, and then she pauses over a half-finished piece and realizes she has unconsciously cut three characters of that angular, patient script into the border—characters she cannot read, characters she does not remember learning, flowing off her chisel like a song her body remembers and her mind has lost.

She stares at them for a long time.

Then, deliberately, gently, the way you'd leave flowers on an unknown grave, she leaves them there. Finishes the border around them. Lets the unreadable words stay.

At night she lays the carved bone—the one from beside her bed, the one she doesn't recognize—on the pillow next to hers. She tells herself it's to keep it safe. She tells herself lots of things. Some nights she wakes with her arm curled around it, holding it the way you'd hold a child who has climbed into your bed against the dark, and the hollow in her chest aches so precisely, so specifically, that she could map it: a child-shaped hollow, small, seven or eight years old, warm, breathing fast from bad dreams, hair that smelled of smoke and tallow.

She does not know any children.

Well. She knows the village children. None of them fit the hollow. The hollow is a specific size and none of the specific sizes match, and this is the cruelest arithmetic of all—that grief can be exact while its cause is blank. She grieves like a mother with no memory of a child, and there is no child in any ledger, in any memory, in any grave, to account for it.

Some nights she talks to the dead about it.

"Tell me," she whispers into the dark of her bedroom. "One of you. One word. You owe me—you've taken thirty years of my quiet, you can spend one word on me now."

And the dead, who have never refused her anything except peace, refuse her again. Not cruelly. They gather close—she can feel them, the pressure of them like the warmth of many bodies in a cold room—and they hold the silence around the missing thing the way men hold a blanket over a wounded comrade. Protecting it. Protecting *her*, maybe. Or protecting something else, something they were told to keep, something entrusted.

Once—only once, deep in the hour before dawn—she thinks she hears it. A single syllable rising out of the congregation of the dead like a bubble through dark water, a sound that starts with a hiss and ends with a sigh, a sound that makes her sit bolt upright in bed with her heart slamming and tears already on her face without her permission.

By the time she is fully awake, it's gone. The dead are quiet. The syllable dissolves even as she reaches for it, and what remains is only the feeling—the feeling of a name, the certainty that a name exists, a name that belongs to the bone on her pillow and the coat on the peg and the stitches in her arm and the hollow in her chest.

She lights a candle. She takes up her finest graver. And with a shaking hand she begins, on a fresh piece of antler, to carve the shape of the feeling—not the name itself, which she doesn't have, but the space where it lives. An outline. A negative. The way you'd carve the silhouette of someone seen only as they left a room.

When it's done, she sets it beside the unrecognized bone on the shelf above her hearth, the little silhouette leaning against the long carved radius, and the two of them together look so much like a marker and a mourner that she has to blow out the candle and sit in the dark and breathe for a while.

* * *

Winter comes the way it always comes to Vell Hollow: gradually, then entirely. Snow seals the pass—which has, she notices, been sealed by rockfall as well as snow, great slumped shoulders of stone as if the mountain itself has closed a door behind something. Travelers thin to nothing. She carves. She trades. She eats Marta's bread. She lives.

But she develops habits she cannot explain, and she lets them stand because fighting them costs more than keeping them.

She keeps the child's coat brushed and moth-proofed, hanging on its peg where the heat of the stove reaches it, so that if the smoke smell fades she will notice, and so that it won't fade too fast. She sets two bowls at the table out of habit and catches herself doing it and leaves the second bowl there anyway. On clear nights she climbs to the ridge shoulder—the low one, the southern one, the one with no scar—and looks north beyond the valley, at the dark lands she has never once in sixty years wanted to see, and she watches the horizon for lights. For a road. For anyone.

For someone coming *back*.

Because here is the thing she knows, the one solid plank in all this flooding ruin, the knowledge she trusts more than her own name: whatever she gave up, she gave up *on purpose*. Those are her stitches in her arm. That is her hand's patience in those unreadable letters. She is a woman who bargains hard with the dead and harder with the living, and she does not spend herself cheaply. Whatever was carved out of her, she chose the price. Which means whatever it bought, it was worth it. Which means whatever it bought—

—it might come home.

She says it aloud some nights, to the fire, to the silent grieving dead, to the hollow in her chest: "Come home, then. Whenever you're ready. There'll be soup on. There's always soup on."

The fire pops. The dead press close and say nothing and mourn.

* * *

On the last day of the year, with the light going blue and early, a peddler comes down the pass road—the first in weeks, a stooped man with a pack full of tinware and ribbon. He stops at her door to trade, and while she turns his buttons over he talks, the way peddlers talk, filling silence with inventory.

"Strange roads this year," he says. "Half the towns on my circuit I swear I've got fewer of than I used to. Used to map 'em all in my head. Now there's gaps." He laughs, embarrassed. "Age, probably. Oh—here." He digs in his pack. "Found this nailed to a post outside Thornford. Notice from the Crown. Thought maybe you'd want it, you being the one as deals with the dead and such. Might concern a burial or something."

He hands her a printed sheet. Official seal. Dense bureaucratic text.

*BY ORDER OF THE KING'S RECKONERS. Pursuant to the Edict of Reconciliation, all records, relics, and remembrances pertaining to [illegible] are declared contraband history. Any citizen possessing knowledge of [blank space] shall present themselves for reconciliation. Note: the persons, place, and matter named herein have never existed. This notice confirms nothing and refers to no one.*

Ada reads it twice. Three times. The paper is real in her hands; the seal is real; the ink is real; and the words circle a hole the way her thoughts do, naming a nothing with tremendous legal precision.

"They post these all over now," the peddler says cheerfully. "Nobody knows what about. Mad, innit? Laws against a thing that never happened." He shrugs and holds out his hand for the notice back.

"No," Ada hears herself say. Her voice comes out strange, low, certain. "I'll keep this one."

He shrugs again and takes his payment in carved bone buttons and trudges off down the frozen road, and Ada stands in her doorway holding the state's official confession that it erased something, proof filed in negative, an arrest warrant for a ghost.

The dead surge up in her all at once—not words, never words, but a pressure, a leaning, a hundred invisible faces turned toward the paper in her hand with an attention like hunger. They know. Whatever they cannot say, they *know*, and this scrap of legalese has touched the wound they've been guarding all winter.

Ada folds the notice carefully and carries it inside, and she does not put it on a shelf or in a drawer. She takes up her awl and pierces it and threads it with cord, and she hangs it on the wall above the hearth, beside the little silhouette of the name she lost, beside the long carved bone she doesn't recognize, beneath the peg where the smoke-smelling coat waits for someone smaller than grief.

"There," she tells the room, and the dead, and whoever isn't listening. "Evidence. Of a crime I can't remember committing. Against a country I can't remember saving."

The fire settles. Outside, snow begins again, filling the valley's footprints, smoothing the world's record. And deep in the marrow-quiet, so faint she will half-believe by morning that she dreamed it, one of the dead hums four notes of a tune she does not know and cannot forget—a lullaby-shaped thing, minor-keyed, patient, waiting to be finished.

Ada sits down at her workbench, takes up a blank bone, and begins, note by note, trying to carve what she hears.

She has all winter. Whoever taught her the song is going to come back for the rest of it.
