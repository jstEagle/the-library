## 1. The Cartographer's Debt

Greymoss smelled the way it always did in late autumn: wet peat, woodsmoke, and the sour undertone of a town that made its living on things other people didn't want found. August Ravensworth had lived here long enough to stop noticing. That was the trick of Greymoss — it got under you slowly, like damp, until you couldn't tell where the town ended and you began.

They sat in the back corner of the Drowned Lantern with a pot of black tea going cold and a leather folio open on the scarred table. Three routes, inked fresh that morning. The northern pass through Wolfjaw Col, redrawn since the rockslide in the spring. The river cut below Sable Tor, safe for another six weeks before the ice came down. And the long one, the one nobody else would sell because nobody else had walked it: the Weeping Stair, threading up through the high Thornfells where the cliffs wept groundwater and the cold could crack a man's teeth in his skull.

The buyer was a fat man named Osk with rings on every finger and sweat on his upper lip even in October.

"The Stair's closed," he said. "Everyone says it's closed."

"Everyone's wrong." August turned the folio toward him. "Closed for armies, closed for caravans. Not closed for four mules and someone who knows which ledges hold weight. I've taken it twice this year."

"Alone?"

August drank their tea rather than answer.

Osk counted out coins, hesitated over one, then pushed the whole stack across. "You know what I don't understand about you, Ravensworth?"

"Many things, I'd imagine."

"You don't drink. You don't gamble. You don't keep a girl or a boy or anything warmer than that tea. You sell the best routes in three counties and you never once come back to ask if I've got more work." He leaned forward. "A man starts wondering what a person like that is *for*."

"A person," August said, pocketing the coins, "is for leaving when they're paid."

But Osk wasn't finished, because Osk never was. "There's talk, you know. Talk that you weren't always a map-seller. Talk that you came down from the mountains one winter with burned hands and no name, and that the name you picked — Ravensworth — belonged to somebody dead."

The tavern noise seemed to thin around them. August kept their face still, the way they'd learned to keep it still across twenty years of buyers, border guards, and men who asked questions with knives instead of words.

"People say a lot of things," they said. "Mostly because saying them is cheaper than thinking."

"I'm not threatening you." Osk spread his hands, rings glinting. "I'm offering to buy. Whatever it is you left behind up there — I'd pay well for the story."

For a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — August felt something move behind their ribs. Old grief, the kind that didn't ache so much as *wait*. They put it away the way they put everything away, in the locked room at the back of themselves where the fire was, where the voices were, where the snow had fallen red.

"There's no story," August said, standing. "There's a route, and now there's your money. Good evening."

* * *

The rain started an hour later, fine and grey, turning the lantern light on Greymoss's crooked streets into smears. August walked home the long way, along the moss-wall by the tannery, out of habit more than caution. Twenty years of habit. Check the street behind you. Note the rooftops. Count the doors between you and cover.

Which is why August noticed the stranger before anyone else in town would have.

He came up the tanner's lane at a stumbling run, and even before the lanterns caught him, August knew two things: he was dying, and he had been looking for someone. People who were merely lost moved differently. This man moved like a compass needle, correcting, searching — and when his eyes found August standing in the mouth of the alley, they fixed there with terrible relief.

"You," he rasped.

"I'm nobody," August said automatically. "You want the healers' row, past the chapel—"

"No." He took another step and his leg buckled. He caught himself against the moss-wall, and August saw the wound properly then — low in the belly, a thrusting wound, dark blood sheeting down his thigh and pooling with each step. Someone had done this work carefully. Someone who wanted him to have time to be afraid.

"They're coming," the man said. "The grey cloaks. The Ashen Order." He laughed, a wet, broken sound. "Came all this way and they were already here. Already *here*, waiting at the crossing, and I thought—" He stopped. Straightened against the wall with the last of his strength. "You're Ravensworth."

August went very still. "I don't know what you mean."

"The map knows." The man fumbled inside his coat, and August's hand went to the knife at their belt — but what he drew out was parchment. A folded scrap, small enough to fit in a palm, sealed with a smear of wax gone soft. "It's been pulling me north for nine days. Pulling me like a fish on a line. And tonight it stopped." His eyes were bright and feverish and terribly clear. "Tonight it turned in my pocket. Turned toward *you*. It chose wrong, Ravensworth. The map chose wrong. Tell it — tell it, when it wakes—"

"I don't take deliveries," August said. "And I don't take dying men's errands."

"You do this one." The courier — he must have been a courier; everything about him said roads and haste — pushed off from the wall and crossed the distance between them in two lurching steps. Blood ran freely now, too fast, the kind of fast that meant minutes and not hours. He caught August's wrist with fingers that were already cold. "Twenty years you've been hiding down here selling your little routes. Twenty years pretending the mountains are just stone." He pressed the folded scrap into August's hand and closed their fingers over it with both of his own. "Your family didn't hide from the war, did they? Your family *made* something. Before the burning. Before the Order burned it all—"

"Stop." The word came out cracked. August hated that it came out cracked.

The courier smiled. There was blood on his teeth. "It shifts," he whispered. "Watch it shift. It's not paper, it's not ink, it's — ah. Ah, hell. It's starting without me."

He slid down the wall.

August knelt — why, they couldn't have said; they hadn't knelt beside a dying stranger since the war took everything worth kneeling for — and pressed their hands to the wound out of some reflex older than sense. Warm blood between their fingers. The courier looked up at them, and his breathing went ragged, spaced, slowing.

"The Emberhold," he said, so quietly August had to lean close to catch it. "She sleeps on the last coals. Don't let them make her a weapon. Don't let them make *you* one either."

Then he died there in the rain, in the tanners' lane in Greymoss, holding a stranger's hand, and the lantern above them guttered as though something large had passed overhead.

* * *

August sat back on their heels for a long moment, letting the rain fall on them, doing the arithmetic of survival. A dead courier. A wound meant to slow him, not kill him — the killers would come looking for whatever he carried. Grey cloaks in Greymoss. And in August's fist, a folded scrap of parchment that should have been ordinary and was not, because even through their closed fingers August could feel it.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not body-warm. It was warm the way a banked fire is warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and beneath that warmth — faint as a pulse under skin — something moved.

August should have dropped it in the tannery sluice and walked away. Every instinct honed across twenty careful years said exactly that. Leave the corpse for the watch. Leave the parcel for whoever killed him. Be nobody. Be nothing. Stay small and alive.

Instead August unfolded the map.

By lantern light, in the rain, they saw a range of mountains rendered in ink so fine it might have been etched by a needle — the Thornfells, unmistakably, every ridge and col they'd spent a lifetime walking. But the map showed more than the mountains showed. Trails that existed nowhere. A road through the heart of Sable Tor where there was only cliff. And drawn in gold-brown ink at the center of the highest peaks, a symbol: a circle broken by a single descending line.

August knew that symbol.

They had known it since before they could write. It had been carved into the lintel of a door they had watched burn when they were young, and they had spent twenty years telling themselves they'd forgotten it.

The map pulsed once against their palm — warm, insistent, *alive* — and somewhere down the lane, a dog began to bark, and boots splashed through puddles in unison, many pairs, moving with the disciplined tread of people who hunted in formation.

August folded the map, tucked it inside their coat against their heart, and stood.

Twenty years of being nobody ended in a tanners' lane, and as the first grey cloak rounded the corner with a lantern held high, August Ravensworth did the only thing left to do.

They ran for the mountains.
