## 3. A Traitor's Hospitality

Wren Calloway was drinking in the last place August wanted to find him, which was exactly like Wren.

The Salt Cellar sat at the bottom of Lowmarsh where the canal water went stagnant and the lamps burned tallow so cheap it smoked. August stood in the doorway a long moment, rain running off their hood, and watched the man who had once sold their name to the Ashen Order for forty crowns and a pardon that never came. He'd gone grey at the temples. He'd gotten fat. He laughed at something the barkeep said, easy as ever, a man with no ghosts worth mentioning.

August's palm throbbed where the map had burned it two nights past. They crossed the room anyway.

"Wren."

The laugh died in his throat. To his credit — and there had never been much credit to spread around where Wren Calloway was concerned — he didn't reach for the knife at his belt. He just looked up at August Ravensworth with six years of bad conscience written across his face.

"Aug." He said it carefully, the way you'd say the name of someone at a funeral. "You look well."

"You're lying."

"I am, yeah." He waved the barkeep off and kicked out the stool opposite him. "Sit down before you make a scene. You were always terrible at making entrances quietly."

"I learned from you. Your entrances were legendary. Usually through a window, usually owed money."

Wren winced. It looked almost genuine. "That's fair."

August sat. Up close, the fat was armor over muscle, and his eyes still did that thing they'd always done — flicking to the door, the windows, the exits, cataloguing them without seeming to look. A smuggler's habit. A guilty man's habit.

"Why are you in Lowmarsh?" August asked. "Your territory was Thornfell's south slope. Everyone knows you don't come down into the marshes unless something's chasing you."

"Something is chasing everyone lately." Wren turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the sticky table. "Ashen Order came through three days ago. Grey cloaks, asking after routes through the Weeping Stair. Paying absurd money for maps of ground nobody's walked since the dragon courts fell." His eyes came up, and there it was — the sharpness under the softness. "And then I hear a courier died in Greymoss. Bled out in a route-seller's doorway clutching something the Order wants badly enough to burn towns over."

The room seemed to narrow. August kept their face still. Six years of practice at stillness around this man.

"I wouldn't know anything about that."

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Except you've got a burn scar on your right palm shaped like nothing natural — I saw you flex it when you sat down — and you're in Lowmarsh, which means Greymoss got too hot, which means you're carrying it." He leaned back. "Hello, Aug. It's good to see you too."

August should have walked out. Should have put a knife on the table and made the terms plain. Instead they heard themselves ask the question they'd carried up the whole drowned length of the canal road: *"Why?"*

Wren didn't pretend not to understand. He took his time answering, which was its own kind of answer.

"Because they had you," he said finally. "They had your file open on the table and your old patrol routes and a list of every job you'd run for me, and the commander said, *give us Ravensworth or we take the whole network apart route by route*, and I believed them." His jaw worked. "I was wrong to believe them. I've had six years to get properly acquainted with how wrong I was."

"You could have warned me."

"They'd have killed us both."

"You could have *warned me*, Wren."

The smoke-hazed lamplight caught the grey at his temples. "Yes," he said quietly. "I could have. That's the part that keeps a man drinking in Lowmarsh."

August looked at him for a long moment — the traitor, the coward, the only person left alive who knew the high passes as well as they did — and felt the old anger sit down beside something newer and more tired. The map pulsed faintly against their ribs where it rode inside their coat, warm as a sleeping animal. *Paths that don't exist yet.* The thought arrived unbidden: the map hadn't sent them to Wren by accident. Maps chose routes. This one had chosen this one.

"The Order wants what the courier carried," August said. "It's a map."

"I guessed a map."

"Not a map like any you've sold. It moves. It redraws itself. And it's pointed at the Emberhold."

Every mug in hearing distance might as well have shattered for the silence that dropped over the table. Wren's hand had gone white around his cup.

"That's a story," he said. "Emberhold's a story. Last coals, last dragon — nursemaids and Order propaganda to keep pilgrims paying for candles."

"It's real. The map thinks so, and the map hasn't been wrong yet." August leaned forward. "And if the Order reaches her first, they don't kill her, Wren. They *harness* her. Dragon-fire as a weapon. Every pass, every border, every town like this one held under a thumb that never goes cold. You've seen their grey cloaks buying stair-maps. Ask yourself what they need with the Weeping Stair if they're not already walking toward her."

Wren stared at his drink. Then he laughed — short, bitter, defeated. "Six years I stay clean of you Ravensworths and your impossible cargo, and the first time we share a table again you're offering me the end of the world." He drained the cup and set it down with a click. "What do you want?"

"A guide. Someone who knows the Stair's back channels better than the Order's paid cartographers. Someone the passes remember."

"And my price?"

"You don't get the map. But I'll tell you its secret — the true one — when we're somewhere the walls aren't listening. That's the deal. Take it or don't."

Wren studied them, and August watched him weigh it the way he'd once weighed cargo: risk against return, trust against survival. What tipped him, August suspected, wasn't the map at all. It was forty crowns and a pardon that never came, and six years of knowing exactly what kind of man sells a friend.

"All right," Wren said. "We leave at low bell, before the marsh fog lifts. I know a channel the Order's boats can't follow." A ghost of the old grin surfaced. "Try not to sell *me* out this time."

"That joke's in poor taste coming from you."

"It's the only kind of joke I've got left." He stood, flipped a coin to the barkeep, and paused. "Aug. For whatever it's worth — which I know is nothing — I'm sorry. Properly sorry. Late, but proper."

August looked at the table. "Low bell," they said. "Don't be late. It's the only kind of late I can't afford."

*Sera Vane found the courier's grave outside Greymoss on the fourth day, and it told her everything the Order's reports had not.*

*The marker was river stone, uncarved. August Ravensworth had buried the man themselves — knelt in the mud and dug, by the spade-marks — when every instinct of a lifelong smuggler said to leave corpses for the crows and move. Sera crouched beside it, rain dripping off the hood of borrowed oilskin, and consulted the archive she carried in her head.*

*Ravensworth. Route-seller, Greymoss. No guild ties, no family, no vices on record except solitude. Flagged eleven years ago in connection with the Emberhold inquiry; contact severed when informant Calloway, Wren, was compromised. Recommended action: surveillance only.*

*Surveillance only. She'd read those words a hundred times in the years since, and only now, kneeling in the rain over a stranger's unmarked grave, did she understand what they meant. The Order had known about Ravensworth for eleven years. Had known the bloodline mattered. Had chosen to wait, patient as ash, letting the last Ravensworth believe themselves free.*

*She rose, joints aching, and thought about the report she was supposed to send. Commander Hesse's standing instruction: locate the carrier, confirm the map, retrieve intact. Casualties acceptable. She thought about Harrowfell — the village that wasn't a village anymore, just a black square of foundation with a child's shoe at its edge, burned because a girl of nine could pull flame out of dead coals. She had filed that report too. *Target neutralized. No further fire-touched activity detected.* As if the child were weather. As if a gift were a crime.*

*Pip was asleep in the waystation loft above her, exhausted from three days of hiding her hands inside oversized mittens whenever strangers passed. When Sera had found her in the ruins, the girl had tried to sing the fire out of Sera's lantern — coaxing it smaller, gentler, the way you'd soothe an animal — because she thought that's what Sera wanted. Nine years old, and she already knew adults only ever wanted her fire aimed at something.*

*"Not today," Sera had told her. "Today nobody aims anything."*

*She took out her dispatch case, held the blank report over the brazier, and watched Hesse's instructions curl and blacken. Somewhere ahead, Ravensworth was moving toward the mountains with a living map in their coat, dragging behind them the smuggler who'd once betrayed them — Sera had read the Calloway file too; the Order always underestimated how thoroughly archivists read — and somewhere beyond the mountains slept the last dragon, whose fire Hesse meant to chain.*

*Sera Vane had spent nineteen years believing the archives made the Order righteous. That knowledge, properly organized, properly indexed, was a form of justice.*

*Harrowfell had taught her otherwise. Knowledge was just fuel. The question — the only question — was what you fed it to.*

*She buttoned the oilskin and started walking north, toward the marshes and the salt-stink of Lowmarsh, keeping to the ditches where the grey cloaks' boats couldn't follow. Behind her, the courier's grave marker darkened with rain, going slowly the color of the mountain stone it was.*

*By morning she had found their trail. By evening, she had found something else: fresh boot-prints at the marsh edge, grey-cloaked, a full hunting party, moving on Lowmarsh at speed.*

*Hesse had stopped being patient.*

* * *

The low bell rang through fog thick enough to drink, and August stood at Wren's mooring watching a boat materialize out of the murk — flat-bottomed, masted with a rag of sail, steered by a man who moved like the marshes had grown him.

"You've gotten thinner," Wren said, pushing off from the rotted pilings. "Marsh air agrees with nobody. Get in."

August stepped aboard and stowed their pack — the map a warm, insistent weight against their spine, humming faintly now, like a struck bell that wouldn't quite fall silent. It had hummed all night. It hummed louder the closer they came to Wren, and louder still when—

August went still.

Out on the canal road, shapes in the fog. Four of them. Five. Grey cloaks moving fast along the towpath, and beneath the fog-horn's distant moan, the thin sound of a whistle — the Order's triple note, *target sighted*.

"Those are my countrymen," Wren said softly, following their gaze, hand drifting to the sweep oar. "Or they used to be. Aug — tell me that's not who I think it is."

The fog parted. Grey cloaks fanned out along both banks, and at their center walked a tall figure with an archivist's satchel slung crosswise — not hunting *with* them, August realized, but parallel, separate, moving like a woman who'd left one fire and hadn't decided which side of the next one she stood on.

The map burned through August's coat like a coal.

"Cast off," August said. "Now."
