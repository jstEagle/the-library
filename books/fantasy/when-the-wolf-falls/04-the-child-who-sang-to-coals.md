## 4. The Child Who Sang to Coals

The village was called Hollowmere, and it burned for exactly forty minutes.

Sera Vane knew this because she timed it. That was what archivists did — they recorded. Forty minutes from the first torch to the last collapse of the thatch, and in all that time the Ashen Order's strike company moved through the streets with the calm efficiency of men doing arithmetic. Sixteen houses. One granary. A chapel with a bell that rang, absurdly, through the first ten minutes of it, until the heat warped the rope and the sound went wrong.

She stood at the treeline with her ledger open and wrote none of it down.

"Fire-touched child," Commander Hale had said that morning, spreading the intelligence report across the campaign table like a man laying out a hand of cards. "Born in Hollowmere. Coaxes flame out of cold ash. The Order has been tracking bloodlines like his since the war ended." He'd tapped the map, on a small village tucked into the foothills below Thornfell. "We take him alive. He's worth more than the village."

*He is eight years old,* Sera had thought, and said nothing, because saying things got archivists reassigned to the salt mines at Ketterick, where the records were kept and where careers went to die quietly.

Now she watched the soldiers move house to house, and she understood, finally and completely, what *worth more than the village* meant. It did not mean they would be gentle if he ran. It meant they would burn every roof in Hollowmere to smoke him out, and call the cinders collateral, and enter him into the rolls as an asset.

A woman ran from the third house with her apron on fire. Two soldiers put her down almost gently, the way you'd step on a spark. Sera's pen hovered over blank paper and her hand would not move.

Then, from the well at the center of the square, a boy climbed out.

He was small, soot-faced, barefoot, and he had done something no one in the strike company had anticipated: he had hidden in cold water, and now, dripping, he turned to face the burning village and held out both hands toward the nearest ruined house — not toward the soldiers, toward the *embers* — and the embers answered.

Flame lifted off the blackened beams like birds startled from a hedge. It gathered above his palms, a slow orange spiral, and the boy's face as he watched it was not afraid. It was the face of someone greeting a dog.

"Asset," Sera whispered. Her voice cracked on it.

Hale gave the order from the ridge — nets, chains, the fire-damp brazier they kept for exactly this — and Sera Vane, who had served the Order for eleven years, who had catalogued three thousand confiscated texts and never once questioned the confiscating, closed her ledger and walked into the burning village.

Later, she could never fully reconstruct how. She remembered stepping through a doorway that was mostly heat. She remembered the boy turning toward her, flame still coiling at his shoulder, and looking at her Order-grey cloak with an expression far too old for his face. She remembered holding out her hand — the one that wasn't holding the ledger — and hearing herself say, "I'm not going to hurt you. I'm going to write down everything they've done. But we have to leave *now.*"

The boy looked past her, at the soldiers closing in, at the brazier being carried down the ridge. Then he looked at the flames eating the chapel, and he did something extraordinary: he *pulled*. The fire came off the buildings in a long exhalation, drawn into his cupped hands, shrinking to a coal the size of a plum. He cupped it close to his chest like a stolen egg.

The village stopped burning. Just like that. As though he'd decided it had suffered enough.

They went into the trees together, and behind them Hollowmere smoldered in the rain that began to fall, as if the sky were trying to apologize.

* * *

They walked north for two days before Sera let herself think about what she'd done.

It wasn't defection yet. Defection implied a destination. What she had so far was a stolen archive fragment sewn into the lining of her coat, an eight-year-old who ate like a starved wolf and slept curled around a live ember, and a growing, nauseating understanding of the document she'd taken.

She'd pulled it from the restricted vault at Ketterick six months ago, during a routine inventory — or rather, she'd copied it, which was worse, because copying meant she'd known, for six months, and filed it under *theoretical ordnance research* and told herself it was theory. The Order called it the Cinder Protocol. It laid out, in the dry bureaucratic language of supply requisitions, how dragon-fire might be harvested, bottled, and delivered. And beneath the diagrams of containment vessels, in Hale's own marginal hand: *living fuses — see fire-touched registries, annex C.*

Annex C was a list of children. Birthplaces, ages, flame-capacities. Pip was on it. Entry forty-one. So were thirty-nine other names, most marked *deceased — trial stage.*

"You knew," Pip said on the second night. They sat under an overhang while sleet ticked against the stones. He fed twigs to his ember with the tenderness of a boy feeding a sparrow. "You knew about us. Before."

"Yes." There was no version of honesty that didn't hurt him, so she chose the honest one. "I read the file. I told myself it couldn't be real. That was easier than believing I worked for people who'd do it."

He considered this with the gravity children reserve for matters of fairness. "The map-man," he said. "The courier. They killed him for the map."

Sera went very still. "What map-man?"

"The one from Greymoss. The others talked about it when they thought I was asleep." He shrugged. "A living map. It shows the way to where the last dragon sleeps. The commander wants it so the Order can get there first." He looked up. "I heard them say a name. Ravensworth."

The rain filled the silence between them.

August Ravensworth. Sera had spent three weeks tracking that name through Greymoss's smuggling ledgers before the Order's hunters picked up the trail themselves — a route-seller with a dead family and a reputation for being impossible to follow. The courier had died pressing the Emberhold key into their hands, and by every report, Ravensworth had run west, toward the mountains, toward Wren Calloway's old territory.

Toward the only person in the Thornfells who knew the passes better than the Order's maps did.

"They'll kill Ravensworth," she said slowly, working it through aloud, "and take the map, and use it to reach Vessarion. And then they'll do to a dragon what they've been doing to children like you in smaller rooms." She looked at the boy, at the small stubborn coal warming his hands. "Unless someone gets there first and tells the dragon what's coming."

Pip grinned. It transformed his whole sooty face. "So we're stealing the dragon."

"We are *not*—"

"For the good guys," he clarified.

Sera laughed — the first time in eleven years she'd laughed inside Order earshot of anything — and realized, somewhere in the middle of it, that there was no longer any such thing as going back. You didn't un-burn a village. You didn't un-copy a protocol. You walked forward, into the mountains, carrying what you carried.

"All right," she said. "But we're not stealing her. If it comes to it, we're warning her. There's a difference."

Pip cupped his ember higher, like a lantern. "Which way?"

"West," said Sera Vane, defector, thief of documents, keeper of a list of dead children's names. "To find a smuggler, a traitor, and a cartographer who doesn't know yet that they're the most important person alive."

* * *

They caught the party at dusk on the fourth day, in the shallow valley below the Weeping Stair — and Sera learned quickly that August Ravensworth's reputation for caution was understated.

She heard the click of a crossbow before she saw its owner. She raised both hands, Pip's small fingers laced tight in hers, and stood very still in the blue half-light.

"Order cloak," said a rough voice from the rocks — a man's, bitter as gall. Wren Calloway; she'd read his file too, before the Order struck it. "That's an Order cloak, Ravensworth."

"I can see that," said another voice, lower, wearier, and then August Ravensworth stepped out of the shadow of the crags with a cocked crossbow and eyes like flint shavings. Up close, the famous cartographer looked less like a legend and more like someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. "Start talking fast."

"Sera Vane. Formerly senior archivist of the Ashen Order, Ketterick vault." She kept her voice level, her hands high. "Three days ago I watched my own company burn a village to capture one child. I took the boy and ran. I have documents proving the Order intends to weaponize dragon-fire — and proof they're breeding fire-singers as delivery mechanisms." She let that land. "And I know you're carrying the Emberhold map, because they sent me to find you before they found you. I'm choosing to be found by you instead."

Silence. Wind moved the pines. Somewhere upslope, water dripped from the Stair's stone teeth.

"Ravensworth," Calloway muttered, "she's either telling the truth or she's the best liar the Order ever made, and either way we're standing in the open having this conversation."

"She's telling the truth." It was Pip who spoke. He stepped forward, out of Sera's shadow, and opened his hands, and the ember rose from his palms into the air between them — a small gold sun spinning in the dusk, throwing light across all their faces. August's crossbow dipped an inch. "Because I'm the child," Pip said, "and I don't lie about fire."

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then August Ravensworth lowered the crossbow entirely, and Sera watched something shift behind those tired eyes — recognition, maybe, of a kindred runner. Someone else whose old life had just finished burning down.

"Annex C," August said quietly. "How many names?"

"Forty. Thirty-nine marked deceased."

The cartographer closed their eyes. When they opened them, the decision had already been made; Sera recognized it because she'd made the same one four days ago. "You walk with us," August said. "You eat what we eat, you stand watch when we say, and if this is a trap, I will bury you in a hole so deep the Order will need a map to find your regrets." A pause. "Welcome aboard."

Pip's grin lit the dark better than his ember did. Wren Calloway swore with feeling and started packing camp.

And it was Wren, hauling the first pack onto his shoulder, who froze — head cocked toward the southern ridgeline, where the last light was dying. "Ravensworth," he said. "That smoke back there. That's not a cookfire."

August turned. Far behind them, thin and deliberate, three signal fires burned in a line along the crest of the pass they'd crossed at noon — the Order's mark, spaced like fingers closing into a fist.

"They're not following us anymore," Sera said, her stomach dropping as she understood. "They're herding us. Straight up the Weeping Stair."

Above them, in the gathering dark, the mountain waited.
