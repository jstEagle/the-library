## 5. What the Mountains Remember

The Weeping Stair earned its name honestly.

August had crossed it twice before — once at nineteen, running routes for a smuggler whose name they'd paid good coin to forget, and once at thirty-one, carrying a body wrapped in oilcloth down the switchbacks because the Thornfells didn't let you bury your dead where the ground was kind. Both times it had rained sideways. Both times the water had come off the cliffs in a thousand silver threads, so that the whole face of the mountain seemed to grieve.

Now there were four of them on the Stair, and the rain hadn't started yet, but August could smell it coming — wet stone and cold iron and, beneath it all, something older. Something like a hearth long gone cold.

"Your map's doing it again," Wren said behind them.

August didn't turn. "Doing what."

"Bleeding."

They stopped. The path was barely wide enough for two abreast here, cut into the cliff with no railing and a drop that went down and down until the clouds swallowed it. Pip had pressed herself against the rock wall an hour ago and refused to look over the edge since, which was fine by August; a child who could pull fire out of ash had no business being this high up without a handrail. Sera walked behind her with one hand hovering near the girl's shoulder, not touching, never quite touching. Like she was afraid Pip would burn her. Like she was more afraid of what it would mean if she wanted her to.

August unslung their satchel and drew out the map.

It had been three days since Greymoss. Three days since they'd run through the back streets with the Ashen hunter's boots ringing off the cobbles, since Sera Vane had appeared out of an alley with a child's hand in hers and said, *I can explain, but not here.* Three days of walking north into the teeth of the Thornfells, following parchment that refused to stay folded, that shifted under their fingers like a sleeping animal turning over in its sleep.

In daylight it showed the Weeping Stair as it was: a scar of switchbacks, the cliff, the clouds below. But now, held flat against the wind, the ink was moving. Lines crawled out from the marked path like roots seeking water, branching off the trail at a place where the stone widened into a shelf, diving *into* the cliff face itself.

"There." Sera leaned close, her breath fogging the parchment. "That's not a route. That's a door."

"Maps don't show doors," August said.

"This isn't a map," Sera said quietly. "I told you. I catalogued things like this in the Order's vaults for eleven years. Living cartography. Pre-War work. It doesn't record the world, August. It *remembers* it."

August looked at the shelf ahead — twenty yards of broken stone jutting out over nothing, half-hidden by a curtain of falling water. They had passed it an hour ago without a second glance. Everyone did. Smugglers called it the Widow's Ledge and gave it a wide berth, because there was nothing there worth dying for.

Except the map disagreed. The map said the Widow's Ledge was a threshold.

"We camp here," August said. "Wait out the weather. Cross at first light."

Wren snorted. "You want to spend the night on a ledge named after dead women, in the rain, with a fire-child, while the people hunting us are—" He gestured vaguely downhill, toward everything. "Fine. Great plan. I've followed worse."

"You have followed worse," August said. "You *were* worse."

Something flickered across his face — the old wound, still open after six years, still bleeding when you pressed it. He turned away and started unbuckling his pack. "I'll get a tarp up."

* * *

The rain came at dusk, exactly as promised, and the four of them huddled under Wren's oilcloth with a fire no bigger than a cupped hand — Pip's fire, coaxed out of wet peat with a song she hummed under her breath, low and wordless, like a lullaby sung to something small and frightened. August had watched her do it a dozen times now and it still made the hair on their arms rise. Flame should be taken. Struck, or stolen, or begged from tinder. Not *asked*. Not loved out of the dark.

Pip caught them looking. "You're sad," she said. Children had no manners and no mercy.

"I'm thinking."

"You do the same face my brother did when our village burned." She said it plainly, the way children say terrible things, and Sera's whole body went stiff beside her. "He thought too much about the things he lost."

"Pip," Sera murmured.

"It's all right," August said, surprising themselves. "She's not wrong."

The rain drummed on the tarp. Below them, the cloud sea churned grey on grey. August pulled the map onto their knees and let the firelight fall across it, and the ink moved to meet the light, eager, like a plant turning toward the sun.

"What was the dragon war actually about?" they asked. "The real version. Not the one in the songs."

Sera was quiet a moment. In the firelight her defector's brand — the ash-grey chevron seared into her left wrist when she'd tried to leave and been permitted only halfway — looked almost black. "Land," she said finally. "And fear. The dragons held the high places. The old kingdoms wanted them. And the Order—" Her jaw tightened. "The Order was founded as a *scholarly* institution. Did you know that? We studied dragons. We mapped their territories. We negotiated. And then some archivist discovered that dragon-fire could be bottled, and the scholars became soldiers overnight. Power does that. It finds the soft thing inside an institution and squeezes until everything else rots."

"And the dragons?"

"The ones who survived flew east, or died fighting. Except one." Sera looked at August. "You know this part. You've known it since the courier died in your arms."

Vessarion. The name sat in August's chest like a coal that wouldn't cool.

Pip had fallen asleep against Sera's side, her small hands curled loose, faint heat still radiating from her palms. August watched her breathe and felt the old grief stir — the family-shaped hole they kept buried under routes and tolls and other people's cargo. They had gotten good at not looking at it. The map, apparently, had other plans, because when August glanced down, the ink had changed again.

The branch line had grown. It now ran from the Widow's Ledge straight into the cliff, past a mark that might have been a gate, and terminated in a shape August recognized from a hundred old survey charts and had never once seen rendered in living ink: the floor-plan of a great hall, pillared, vast, with a dais at its heart shaped like a coiled serpent.

A court. A throne room. Under the Weeping Stair.

"Wake them," August whispered.

Wren's head came up. "What?"

But August was already moving, map in hand, ducking out into the rain.

* * *

The Widow's Ledge was slick and singing with runoff, and August crossed it on hands and knees with the map clenched in their teeth like a pirate's knife, because some animal part of them refused to let go of it even facing death. Behind them they heard Wren swearing, Sera's low instructions to a sleepy Pip, boots on wet stone. Of course they followed. That was the terrible miracle of the last three days: people kept following them.

At the far end of the shelf, the cliff wall rose sheer and blank. No gate. No seam. Just granite veined with quartz that glittered dully in the rain.

August pressed the map against the stone.

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then the parchment *sank*, the way water sinks into dry earth, and light spread through the rockface like dawn moving across a valley — veins of gold tracing lines that had always been there, waiting. The quartz lit. And a gate resolved out of the stone as though the mountain had simply exhaled and uncovered it: two doors forty feet tall, carved in deep relief, and August's legs nearly went out from under them.

Because they knew the carving.

Not the style — though the style was familiar, the flowing linework, the way the artist had made stone look like water. They knew the *sigil*. Centered above the lintel, worn by centuries of rain but unmistakable, was a compass rose crossed by a single feather, enclosed in a ring.

The same sigil was branded into the spine of the survey journal August's mother had left behind. The same sigil was stitched, in thread gone silver with age, into the lining of the coat August wore right now.

"That's your mark," Wren said behind them. His voice had gone strange. "That's — August, that's *your family's* mark."

"No," August said. Their voice came out cracked. "My family were smugglers. Route-sellers. My mother drew toll roads and my father drank and I grew up trading passage through these mountains for coin. There's no ring, no house, no—"

They touched the sigil anyway. They couldn't help it. And the moment their fingers met the carved feather, the gate boomed open — no grinding, no resistance, just a sound like a held breath released after three hundred years — and warm air rolled out over them, smelling of dust and cedar smoke and something faintly, impossibly alive.

Torches lit themselves along the passage beyond. Or rather, *coals* woke, banks of them lining the walls, glowing up from grey to orange as if greeting someone expected.

"How long has it been standing open?" Sera breathed.

"It hasn't," August said. "It opened for—" For me. They couldn't say it. The word stuck in their throat like a fishbone.

They stepped through anyway, because the alternative was standing on a ledge in the rain pretending their own name wasn't written above a door in a mountain, and August Ravensworth had spent thirty-seven years pretending, and the courier's blood was still under their fingernails, and enough. *Enough.*

The hall beyond was exactly what the map had shown: pillars like trees, a ceiling lost in darkness, a dais at the center where something enormous had once coiled. Along the walls ran friezes — dragons in flight, dragons at rest, and between them, human figures. Small figures. Figures holding scrolls, and measuring cords, and pens.

Mapmakers. Oath-bound, by the chains of linked script circling their wrists in the carvings. Keepers.

One of the figures stood slightly apart from the others, nearest the dais, drawn larger than the rest. Above it, in the old script that Sera had been translating fragments of since Greymoss, was a single carved word. Sera reached it first. August watched her lips move, watched her go pale, watched her turn to them with an expression August had only ever seen on the faces of people delivering news of the dead.

"*Ravensworth,*" she read aloud. "First Keeper. Oath-sworn to the Emberhold." She swallowed. "August. Your family didn't just make maps. Your family made *this map*. Your blood is the key the Order has been hunting since before either of us was born."

Behind them, far down the Stair, thin and carried on the wind through the open gate, came a sound August knew from a lifetime of smuggling and had prayed never to hear again:

A signal horn. Two notes. Repeated.

The Order had reached the mountain.
