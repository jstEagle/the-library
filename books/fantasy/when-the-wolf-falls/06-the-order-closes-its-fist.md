## 6. The Order Closes Its Fist

Wren Calloway had crossed Thornfell Pass forty-one times in his life, and he'd counted, because counting was the kind of thing a man did when he wanted to believe he was good at surviving. Forty-one crossings. No ambushes. That was his record, and he'd have traded it gladly to never walk it again, because on the forty-second crossing, the birds went quiet.

Not gradually. All at once, like a door closing.

He stopped and held up a fist, and behind him the line halted — August first, of course, because August always walked point even when they let someone else lead; then Sera with her satchel of stolen archives hugged to her chest like a nursing child; then Pip, small and quick-eyed, riding on the pack mule they'd named *Duchess* for reasons Wren still didn't fully follow.

"What is it?" August asked. Low voice. They always knew.

"Birds." Wren tilted his head. "There were birds."

August listened. The pass was a throat of grey stone with a stream running through it, snowmelt cold and loud enough to cover footsteps — which cut both ways, and Wren knew it, and hated that he knew it.

"Whitecloaks," he said. "Or whatever the Order calls themselves when they're not pretending to be scholars."

"Ashen Order," Sera said quietly. "And you're right. This is how they move. They silence everything first. It's called a hush-line — I helped write the manual." Her mouth twisted. "Chapter nine."

"How many?" August asked.

Sera closed her eyes. "A hush-line means at least twelve. Probably more. And they'll have the high ground on both rims, because chapter ten says you always take the rims."

Wren looked up. The morning light was coming down pale and thin between the walls of the pass, and he could see — now that he knew to look — the wrongness of certain shadows. Shapes that didn't shift. Men who had learned to be stone.

"Options," August said.

"One." Wren turned around, and what he felt on his face must have shown, because August's expression changed — that careful cartographer's mask cracking just enough to show the person underneath, the one who'd trusted him once and been sold for it. "I'm sorry. There's only one option, and it's me."

"Don't be cryptic, Calloway," Sera said. "Cryptic wastes time."

He laughed, or something near it. "The men on those rims? Half of them used to run cargo for me. Before. The other half are Order regulars, but the regulars don't know this pass — they're following guides, and those guides are mine." He pulled off his glove. His hands were shaking, which annoyed him. "Every man in my old network carries a token. A knot-worked cord. You burn it, you break the bond — that's the oath, fire-sealed, old smuggler law, older than the Order, older than most of these mountains. Burn the cord, and every oath sworn to me dies with it. Every debt, every loyalty, every promise to look the other way on the night roads." He met August's eyes. "Including the one I broke. Including yours."

"I don't understand," Pip said, from the mule.

"He means he's going to set fire to the only thing keeping him safe," Sera said softly, "and hope the ashes buy us a road."

"They won't all go," Wren said. "Some of them will stand there holding dead cords and wonder why they ever followed a man who'd sell out August Ravensworth. But some of them — Hesk, maybe, and the twins — some of them will remember that I fed their kids through two bad winters. And those ones will suddenly find it very hard to see us standing here." He shrugged. "It's not much of a plan. It's the only one where most of us walk out of this pass."

"And you?" August said. "Do you walk out?"

"The cord has to burn on skin," Wren said. "That's the seal. Fire-sealed means fire-fed, and it takes what it takes."

Silence. The stream kept talking. Somewhere above them, a man shifted his weight, and gravel whispered.

"No," August said.

"It's not a vote."

"You don't get to do this. Not you. You spent six years paying back one betrayal and the second you get the chance you make it a grand gesture—" August's voice cracked, and Wren saw, with a strange clarity, that they weren't angry about the danger. They were angry about being given another debt. About owing him again.

"August." He took out the cord. Knots within knots, thirty years of oaths worked into waxed hemp, warm from his chest where he'd carried it since Greymoss without quite admitting why. "I sold you once because staying loyal cost more than I could pay. This is the difference. This time I can pay."

He struck his knife against his flint, and the spark caught the cord, and he pressed the burning thing into his bare palm and closed his fist around it.

* * *

Pain is an honest country, Wren thought later. You learn its language fast or you don't speak at all.

What he remembered: the smell of himself cooking. What he remembered: shouting from the rims, voices he knew — Hesk bellowing *"Stand down! Stand down, that's Calloway's mark, the cord's burned, the oath's DEAD"* — and then confusion spreading along the high ground like water finding cracks. What he remembered: August dragging him behind a boulder while arrows hissed into the places they'd just left, and Sera shouting coordinates from her stolen manuals, calling out the Order's own formations against them, and Pip—

Pip.

That was the part that came back to him wrong every time. Because the child had screamed — not fear, something else — and the scream had come out as flame.

* * *

He woke in pieces. First the smell of pine resin and burnt cloth. Then Sera's voice, low and rapid, talking to herself the way scholars did. Then August's hand on his shoulder, steady as a surveyor's plumb line.

"Stay down," August said. "We made the tree line. You bought us the tree line."

Wren opened his eyes. The world swam, resolved. They were in a hollow under pines, dusk coming down blue through the branches. Duchess the mule grazed nearby, deeply unimpressed by recent events. And across the hollow, sitting apart, knees drawn up, was Pip — and Pip was glowing.

Not metaphorically. Faint orange light moved under the child's skin like coals breathing under ash, pulsing with each heartbeat, too fast. Frost on the ground around Pip was melting into a ring of dark earth, and the nearest pine's needles had gone brown at the tips.

"Pip," Sera was saying, crouched at the edge of the glow, hands open, palms out. "Look at me. Just me. You're safe. Nobody's burning anything today except Wren's terrible cooking."

A wet, hiccupping laugh from the child. The glow stuttered.

"There," Sera said. "Again. Breathe with me. In for four—"

"Sera." Wren pushed himself up on one elbow, and immediately regretted the eloquence of his bandaged hand. "What happened in the pass?"

She didn't turn around. "An arrow hit near Pip. Close. The fear lit the fire, and the fire — answered." Now she turned, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she wished she'd never learned. "Wren, I've read the Order's files. I stole three crates of them. I thought the fire-singer program was theoretical — breeding lines, selective fostering, 'thermal aptitude studies.' Academic cruelty. Paperwork." She looked back at Pip, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "It wasn't theoretical. They've been raising children as fuses. Living fuses. A fire-singer can't light dragon-fire from outside the Emberhold — the vault wards stop any external flame. But a fuse carried inside, a child whose fire wakes on command, triggered by pain or a spoken word—" She stopped. Started again. "They weren't hunting Pip to eliminate a threat. They were retrieving inventory."

The hollow was quiet except for the wind and the mule tearing grass with obscene contentment.

"So the ambush," August said slowly. "At the pass. They weren't trying to kill us."

"They were trying to take Pip alive. We were in the way." Sera wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like a woman closing a ledger. "And if we deliver Pip to the Emberhold — which is where the map is taking us, which is the whole point — we may be delivering the Order their fuse straight through the front door."

Pip's head came up. The glow steadied. "I heard you," the child said. Small voice, level, older than it should have been. "I'm not going to be anyone's fuse. My gran taught me songs for the coals. Coals listen. I make them listen." The orange under Pip's skin dimmed, banked, obedient. "I'm not a fuse. I'm a singer."

"No," Sera said fiercely, moving to the child. "You're right. You are. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

Wren lay back and looked up through the black lace of pine branches at the first stars. His hand throbbed in time with his heart, and the pain felt earned, which was a rare and comfortable sensation. Six years he'd owed August a debt, and today he'd paid it in skin, and the arithmetic finally balanced — except it didn't, because debts didn't work that way, because August would carry this now, and because there was a child twenty feet away with a furnace for a heart and an empire that wanted to strike a match against her.

"Calloway," August said, settling beside him. Quietly. "You idiot. Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." He nodded toward the south, where far off, past the tree line, a smudge of smoke rose against the last light. Not a campfire. Too straight, too deliberate. Signal smoke. "Hesk bought us tonight. Maybe tomorrow. But the Order doesn't send twelve after a courier and lose. They'll send the rest." He flexed his ruined hand, testing it, and found five fingers still attached and half of them still useful. "How far to the Emberhold?"

August was silent a moment. When they spoke, their hand had gone to their own palm — the one the map had marked — and their face wore an expression Wren had only seen once before, years ago, on a night they'd both sworn never to discuss.

"Three days," August said. "And Wren — the map just changed again. It's showing a new route. Straight through the pass we just escaped." A pause. "And at the end of the route, there's ink where no ink should be. It says, *bring the singer.*"

The signal smoke bent eastward in the wind, pointing the way they had to go.
