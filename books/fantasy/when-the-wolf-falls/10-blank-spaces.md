## 10. Blank Spaces

The map on my workbench is blank.

Not empty — blank. There's a difference I've spent a lifetime learning. Empty is absence, a page waiting to be told what it is. Blank is invitation. It's the difference between a grave and an open door.

I draw the first line at dawn, when the light through Greymoss's fog comes in grey-gold and forgiving, and I don't decide where it goes until the pen touches vellum. That was never how I worked before. Before, every route was plotted in advance, memorized, sold — safe passage for smugglers' silver, drawn from a lifetime of keeping my head down and the mountains between me and everything I didn't want to look at. My maps were always answers. This one is a question.

The line wanders north along the Mosswater, then stops where I stop. I leave it there. Someone else will finish it.

* * *

Greymoss has changed the way towns change when nobody orders them to — slowly, crookedly, alive. The Ashen Order's garrison house stands shuttered at the crossroads, its black-and-ash banner long since burned by someone with better sense than its owners. Where it stood, Wren has put up a waystation: a low stone building with a wide hearth, a rain barrel, and a hand-painted sign that reads ROADS ARE FOR EVERYONE, WHICH IS THE WHOLE POINT, which is exactly the kind of thing Wren would paint because he thinks sincerity needs armor even on wood.

He's rebuilding roads now. Actual roads. Graded, drained, honest ones that appear on maps instead of hiding from them. He says it's penance. I say it's just what he's good at — he always understood terrain the way other men understand faces, could read a slope like a lie — and that penance is a story he tells himself so he doesn't have to call it hope. He scowls at me when I say that. Then he goes back to hauling gravel, whistling.

His wound healed wrong, in the way wounds do when you refuse to rest, so he walks with a stick and jokes about it in the voice of a man who has decided the alternative to joking is drowning. Some mornings I catch him standing at the pass road, looking east toward Thornfell, toward the place where he spent his network's last loyalty like a gambler's final coin, and where some of his people died spending it. I don't interrupt. There are rooms in a person you don't walk into without knocking.

* * *

Pip lives above the old chandlery with Sera, in rooms that smell of tallow, ink, and scorched candlewicks — Pip is still learning, and learning, for a fire-singer, means burning things on purpose and then apologizing to them. The child has grown half a hand taller in the months since the Emberhold. The flares have stopped. Mostly. Last week they lit the hearth with a sneeze and set fire to nothing but their own dignity, which is the direction we're all hoping for.

Sera teaches them the way she was taught in the Order's archives, except unpoisoned: method without cruelty, rigor without fear. She has begun writing her own book — *A Reckoning of the Ashen Order*, she calls it, though Pip lobbies hard for *The Book of Bad Fire Decisions*. She copies out documents by lamplight, testimony after testimony, the Order's own records turned inside out and held up to the light. She believes, with an archivist's stubborn faith, that truth written down is truth that can't be burned. I don't argue. I draw her a map of the Emberhold caldera for the frontispiece, and I get the geography right this time — no omissions, no smuggler's discretion, every corridor marked. It felt obscene, the first hour. It feels like breathing now.

And above it all — above the town, above the fog, above everything — Vessarion roosts on the old watch-crag called the Wolf's Jaw, where the beacon fires used to burn in the dragon wars. She is enormous and patient and mostly asleep, the way mountains are, and the town has adjusted to her the way towns adjust to weather. Children dare each other to run up the first switchback of the Jaw and scream her name. She opens one eye, gold as banked coals, and regards them with the weary tolerance of a grandmother. Farmers claim she turns her tail to the wind before storms, so now half of Greymoss checks the dragon before they check the sky.

She is not worshipped. That matters, and I think she knows it matters. The Order wanted a god they could harness; Greymoss got something better — a witness. She remembers the old courts, the old oaths, the old world, and she carries those memories the way a river carries stones: not to use them, just because they are part of the current. When I climb the Jaw to sit with her, which I do more often than I admit, she sometimes speaks — a voice like a landslide deciding to be gentle — and asks me questions no living creature had asked me before. *What did your grandfather's hands look like? Did the stair at Weeping still sing? Do humans still mistake hunger for ambition?*

I answer what I can. She seems satisfied that most answers are *I don't know.* Perhaps that's why she likes me. A cartographer who admits to blank spaces must be a novelty, even to a dragon.

* * *

Today, though, I'm not on the crag. I'm at my bench, in the shop I rented below the chandlery, with a sign of my own: RAVENSWORTH — MAPS, SURVEYED & DRAWN. HONEST SCALE. And beneath it, smaller, because Pip insisted: *BLANK ONES FREE.*

The blank maps are my best sellers, which took me months to understand and would have taken me years to deserve. Travelers take them into the Thornfells — safer now, the passes open, the smuggling routes converted to trade trails — and they mark them as they go. A spring here. A widow's inn there. Here the bridge washed out. Here a family sells cider worth stopping for. They bring the maps back, or send them, or pass them hand to hand, and I copy the corrections into my master atlas, so that the atlas grows less like a chart of terrain and more like a diary the mountains keep. The living map taught me that parchment could breathe. Turns out all of it can, if you let strangers write on it.

Some nights I take out the old scrap — or what remains of it, because it dissolved into my skin at the Emberhold and lives there now, a faint warmth under the scar on my palm that stirs when I near a path that wants existing. It doesn't command me anymore. Ravensworth blood made it to serve dragons; I've been teaching it to serve travelers instead. It seems content with the demotion. Or the promotion. I haven't decided which.

My hand aches some mornings, the scar warming like a coal turned under ash. On those mornings I've learned to draw anyway. Pain, I've found, makes a poor compass but a decent ink.

* * *

There's one map I've been avoiding, and today, with the fog thinning to lace outside my window, I finally pull the vellum toward me.

It's the largest sheet in the shop, fine-grained, cream-colored, meant for something that matters. I've had it pinned under weights for weeks the way you keep a letter you haven't answered. Because this one isn't for sale, isn't for a client, isn't even for the atlas. This one I started the night we came down from the Emberhold, when Wren was bleeding and Pip was shaking and Sera was already drafting her reckoning in her head — the night the world cracked open and let a little light in, and I lay awake thinking: *someone should record this. Not the battle. Not the politics. The beginning.*

So the map is of the new age, which is a ridiculous thing to attempt, because the new age is eleven months old and mostly consists of a reformed smuggler's gravel piles, a child's burnt candles, an archivist's manuscripts, a dragon's naps, and a town learning to say the word *dragon* without spitting. But ridiculous things are the only ones worth drawing well.

I work through the morning. Greymoss, with the waystation's chimney drawing its slow signature. The Wolf's Jaw, and a small careful shape coiled on it, labeled not *Vessarion, last of her kind* — the Order's phrasing, a eulogy disguised as a title — but simply *Vessarion, who stayed.* The Weeping Stair, reopened, the ruins of the dragon courts marked as what they now are: a library Sera is cataloguing, room by oath-bound room. Thornfell Pass, with a cairn Wren built for his people, no names listed because he asked me not to — "they know who they were," he said, "and so do I" — just a small symbol I invented for the atlas: two lines crossing, a road meeting a memory.

Then the edges. The parts beyond my walking. The northern ranges I never mapped because no one paid for the north. The southern coast. The blank margins where the world keeps going whether or not I approve.

I hold the pen over the bottom corner for a long time.

Cartographers sign their work to claim it. *Here be Ravensworth's knowledge; here be my eye, my judgment, my errors.* Every map I ever drew for the smugglers carried my name like a brand — proof I'd chosen a side, even if the side was only myself. I spent thirty years signing things I was ashamed of, or refusing to sign things I wasn't.

This one gets neither my name nor my shame.

In the bottom corner, in my best hand — the steady one, the one that doesn't shake anymore — I write:

*For whoever comes next. Finish it better than I did.*

Below that, because Pip would never forgive me if the whole thing were solemn, I add a tiny drawing of a dragon asleep on a crag, with a single closed eye, and beside it: *she says hello.*

* * *

I carry it up the Wolf's Jaw at dusk, when the fog burns off and the whole valley shows itself: lamplight kindling window by window, Wren's road running clean and pale toward the pass, smoke rising straight from the chandlery chimney in the windless cold. Vessarion uncoils as I approach, scales shedding the day's last light like a river letting go of coins.

"I finished it," I tell her.

She lowers her head, the great architecture of her skull coming down level with my chest, and studies the vellum with one gold eye. The warmth from her breath smells of deep places, of coals that have never gone out.

*And when someone comes,* she says, *and finishes it — what then?*

"Then they make a new one," I say. "That's the whole point. A map's finished the moment you treat it like it's true forever."

Her eye narrows, amused, ancient. *Your ancestors believed a map should be finished once,* she says. *One great drawing of the world, and the world would obey it.*

"My ancestors were wrong about most things." I roll the vellum and tie it. "They drew lines to own the land. I'm trying to draw lines to introduce people to it."

Thunder moves through her, which is her laugh. She settles back around the crag, and I start down the switchbacks in the blue dark, the map against my back like a second spine, the scar on my palm humming its faint warm hum — the way it does, lately, whenever a road is about to matter.

At the bottom of the Jaw, lamplight in the chandlery window, and a figure waiting by my door with a travel cloak still dusty from the south road and a sealed letter in one hand.

Sera steps into the street to meet me, and I know before she speaks, from twenty years of reading terrain, that the ground ahead has changed again.

"August," she says. "You'll want to see this. Something's started drawing itself."
