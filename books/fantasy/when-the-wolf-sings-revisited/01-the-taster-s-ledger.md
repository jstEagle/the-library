## 1. The Taster's Ledger

The poison arrived, as it always did, in the third course.

Ada Ishikawa felt it settle into her like an old acquaintance taking a chair by the fire — a slow warmth behind the sternum, then the faintest tightening of the throat, the taste of copper and wintergreen blooming at the back of her tongue. She did not flinch. Flinching was for amateurs and corpses.

She sat at the far end of the queen's supper table, a small woman in dove-grey silk with a plain silver spoon and no conversation to offer anyone. The court had long ago stopped seeing her, which was precisely how she liked it. A taster who was noticed was a taster who was bribed; a taster who was invisible was a taster who lived.

"Her Majesty awaits your verdict," murmured Lord Chamberlain Ossory, without looking up from his wine.

"The quail is clean," Ada said. "The sauce has a whisper of foxglove — old stock, poorly stored, nothing deliberate. The honey-cakes are the concern."

She lifted the fourth course onto her own plate, cut a precise triangle, and ate it with the unhurried grace of a woman dining on her own funeral. Around her, the candles guttered. Somewhere beyond the tall windows, the free city of Vell glowed against the dusk, its ten thousand lamps like a spilled purse of gold.

The warmth spread. Ada opened the slim black ledger she kept beneath her napkin and wrote, in a hand so small it might have been a spider's prayer:

*Hour one. Bittercress extract, likely southern strain. Onset gentle. Numbness beginning at the fingertips. Estimated full effect: four hours. Antidote: wren's-bane tincture, two measures, taken before the second hour.*

She had written some version of that entry every night for eleven years. Eleven years of swallowing death by candlelight while queens and chancellors laughed above her bowed head. The crown paid for her immunity the way it paid for its armies — grudgingly, and never enough — but what the crown did not know, what no living soul knew, was that Ada kept a second ledger.

That one balanced accounts no honest bank would touch.

* * *

The Guildhall of Impossible Things stood in the oldest quarter of Vell, where the streets forgot their names and the houses leaned together like conspirators. To find it, you had to already know it existed. Ada let herself in through a door that was, on this side of things, the back wall of a shuttered cobbler's shop, and on the other side, a hall of black oak lit by lamps that burned without oil.

The guild traded in three currencies: favors, names, and doors.

Favors were simple — a debt owed here, repaid there, compounded across years until a baron found himself owing a fishwife a kindness he could not refuse. Names were harder. To hold a true name was to hold the thing itself by the scruff. And doors — doors were the guild's treasure and its curse: passages between realms, between the world of men and the wild country where wolf-kind sang the borders into being.

Ada climbed to her office on the third landing, hung her cloak on a hook that had once been a man (he'd deserved it), and opened the great ledger bound in grey leather. Impossible accounts. Tonight's entries:

*One door, sealed, per request of the Signatory's Office. Payment deferred 200 years. Interest accruing.*

*One name, returned to its owner posthumously. Balance: zero.*

*One favor, called in by the river Vell itself. The river wishes its banks remembered. Status: unpaid.*

She dipped her pen. Outside, rain had begun, soft as an apology. This was the hour Ada loved best and trusted least — the quiet after the tasting, when her body fought its nightly war and her mind, sharpened by the poison rather than dulled, could hold two hundred threads of impossible arithmetic without dropping one.

Loneliness, she had learned, was simply the price of being necessary. She had made her peace with it the way one makes peace with weather.

Then someone knocked on her door.

No one knocked on her door. The guildhall did not permit visitors; the guildhall barely permitted members.

"Enter," she said, and the door swung open on a young man who took two steps and came apart at the seams.

He collapsed across her threshold like a puppet whose strings had been cut — a courier's leathers gone grey with road-dust, boots worn through to the spirit of his feet, and blood, so much blood, pooling from under him in a shape that reminded her obscenely of a map. His lips moved. Ada was beside him before she knew she'd risen, her hands pressing his wounds, her ledger forgotten on the desk behind her.

"Don't talk," she said. "Save it."

"Signatory," he rasped. "I was told — only the Signatory—"

"I'm the bookkeeper."

His eyes found hers anyway, fever-bright and certain, and something in them made her stop arguing. He fumbled inside his coat and pressed a wrapped bundle into her hands — oilcloth over something flat and heavy, tied with cord gone black with old handling. Then, with the last of his voice, he said:

"She said you'd come. The last signatory. She said — tell Ada Ishikawa the covenant is broken, and the wolves have stopped singing, and the usurper knows the way through."

Ada's blood went cold in a way no poison had ever managed.

"Who sent you? Who is *she?*"

But the courier's eyes had already gone past her, fixed on the middle distance where dying men keep their appointments. His chest rose once, twice, and stilled.

For a long moment Ada knelt in the lamplight, holding a dead stranger's hand, listening to the rain. Then, because grief was a luxury and curiosity was a discipline, she unwrapped the bundle.

Inside lay a map drawn on skin — not parchment, *skin*, thin and supple and faintly warm, inked in lines that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them. It showed the borderlands between the realms, the covenant circle at their heart, and running along the border like a seam of silver thread, a line of song-notes older than any alphabet she knew.

And in the lower corner, in a hand she almost recognized, almost *remembered*, a single line of text:

*To my granddaughter, who balances what cannot be balanced.*

Ada sat back on her heels. Her grandmother had died — or fled, or vanished — thirty years ago, leaving nothing but a rumor and a silence the family never spoke around. Ada had built her whole life on that silence: the crown's taster, the guild's bookkeeper, a woman belonging to no one, belonging nowhere, safe in her ledgers and her loneliness.

Now a dead courier bled on her floor and told her she was an heir.

She looked down at her hands — steady, always steady, even now, even with the night's poison humming in her veins — and understood, with terrible clarity, that the life she had so carefully balanced had just been audited by forces older than the guild itself.

Outside, far off toward the borderlands, something howled. Not a wolf's howl. A warning.

Ada closed her fingers around the map, and it pulsed against her palm like a second heartbeat, like a door deciding whether to open.

* * *

By dawn she had done three things.

She had recorded the courier's death in the guild ledger — *one name, arrived home; balance: everything* — though the ink wrote itself crooked, as if the book disapproved. She had packed a bag with antidotes, coin, and the grey-ledger map of every door in Vell. And she had stood a long time before the queen's portrait in the empty audience chamber, sworn shield and spoon both, feeling her oath pull one way and the warm weight of the oilcloth bundle pull the other.

The map wanted the borderlands. The borderlands wanted a signatory. And somewhere out there, if the courier's dying certainty meant anything, a wolf-singer had forgotten her own song, and a disgraced guildsman drank himself sober in exile, and a boy was about to run away from an apprenticeship that would kill him.

Ada had spent eleven years tasting death so that others might eat safely. Now death had tasted *her* — had found her name in an old book and marked her plate.

She pulled her hood up, slipped through the cobbler's door, and stepped out of the world she knew.

Behind her, in the guildhall, the great ledger turned its own page, and began, in fresh ink, a new account:

*One heir, claimed.*
