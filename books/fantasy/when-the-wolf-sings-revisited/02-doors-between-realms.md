## 2. Doors Between Realms

The door behind the cobbler's shop was not a door.

Ada had known this, in the way she knew the taste of arsenic or the weight of a queen's trust — abstractly, as a fact recorded but never lived. The guild-doors were legend to most tasters, rumor to most guildsmen. But here it was: an archway of black oak set into a brick wall that had no business existing in the alley behind Marrow Lane, its grain swirling like water frozen mid-pour. No handle. No hinges. Only a small brass plate, green with age, bearing the Guild's sigil — a key turning into a bird.

The map in her satchel grew warm against her hip.

She pressed her palm flat to the oak and felt, unmistakably, a pulse. Slow. Patient. The heartbeat of something that had been waiting eleven years for someone to touch it.

*Eleven years.* The length of her service. The length of time since the ledger last opened itself.

"Names open doors," Bramble would say later, when Ada asked how she'd done it without a key. "But blood opens them faster."

Ada said her own name aloud — *Ada Ishikawa* — and the oak sighed open like a mouth.

***

On the other side was winter.

Not the soft grey winter of the capital, all coal smoke and wet wool, but a hard, singing cold that tasted of pine resin and old snow and something wilder beneath — musk, maybe, or memory. Ada stepped through and found herself on a hillside under stars too many to count, arranged in constellations she did not know. Behind her, the door hung in empty air, framed by nothing, already forgetting its shape.

Ahead, the borderlands rolled away in silver-dark waves of frost-grass, and somewhere far off, a wolf was singing.

Ada stood very still until the song faded. Then she took out the map, which had stopped being a map the moment she crossed — the ink crawling now, roads rearranging themselves like snakes waking — and followed the one line that stayed still: a thread of gold running east toward a broken tower on the horizon.

She walked. The cold did not bite her. She noticed that, and filed it away with all the other impossible things, in the ledger behind her eyes where she kept what she could not yet say aloud.

***

Sera smelled her before she saw her: ink and iron and, underneath it, something older — covenant-scent, the smell of oaths, faint as a ghost of a ghost. She'd caught it three nights ago drifting through the pines, and it had woken something in her chest that she had spent six years trying to bury.

She had forgotten her song. That was the truth she carried like a stone in her belly. She could feel its shape the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth — the rise of it, the fall, the place where the harmony should open like a door — but the notes themselves were gone, scattered somewhere between the burning of her pack and the long grey years after. A wolf-singer who could not sing was a joke the world had made at her expense, and she had walked away from every laugh.

But the scent of covenant on the wind made her climb down from the ridge anyway, four-footed through the frost, because if the oaths were stirring then maybe — *maybe* —

The woman at the treeline was small, hooded, human, and holding a scrap of glowing parchment like it might bite her.

"You're following my map," Sera said, shifting to two legs as she came out of the trees. It was a courtesy; humans startled.

The woman did not startle. She looked up at Sera with the flat, assessing calm of someone who tasted poison for a living, and said, "You're following me. I've been aware of you since the ridge. You step loudly for a wolf."

Sera blinked. Then, despite everything, despite six years of silence, she laughed.

"I'm Sera."

"Ada." The woman folded the map with precise, economical movements. "I'm told I'm an heir. I haven't decided whether to believe it."

"Believe it," Sera said quietly. "The border's been dreaming about you for eleven years."

***

They walked east together, warily, two strangers sharing a road and nothing else — or so they told themselves. Sera taught Ada which frost-grasses hid sinkholes; Ada taught Sera which berries were safe and which were the borderlands' idea of a joke. At night Sera ran the ridgelines as a wolf while Ada kept watch below, and neither acknowledged that this arrangement had become, within three days, a matter of course.

The broken tower resolved into a waystation as they neared it — a guild-post, abandoned but not dead. Smoke rose from its lean-to shelter, and two figures sat by the fire: a broad-shouldered man mending a coat with the fussy precision of a craftsman, and a boy, fourteen perhaps, all elbows and defiance, who sprang up at the sight of them with a knife already drawn.

"Bramble," Sera called, "you've got visitors," and the man looked up, and his face did something complicated.

"Covenant-scent," he said hoarsely. He rose slowly, joints cracking, and stared at Ada as though she were a page he'd read long ago and never expected to see again. "Eleven years I've waited on that smell. Eleven years, and it walks in wearing a taster's cloak."

"You know what I am?"

"I know what your grandmother was." Bramble's hands, she noticed, were stained with ink to the wrist — a guildsman's stains, deeper than any craftsman's. "And I know why the doors went quiet, and the wolves went silent, and the free cities started counting their soldiers instead of their friends. Sit down, heir. This is a fire story. Fire stories need sitting."

The boy — Kesh, he grudgingly admitted, a runaway apprentice from the Thornmere chapter who had fled rather than let the usurper's men burn his master's ledgers — scooted over to make room. His knife stayed out, but pointed at the ground now, which Sera privately rated as progress.

"The covenant bound human and wolf-kind," Bramble said, feeding the fire a knot of pine. "Three signatories each, sworn at the circle at Hollowmere, renewed each generation. Doors between realms stayed open. Wolves sang the borders whole; humans kept the ledgers true. Peace, not because either side loved the other, but because both sides *belonged* to the same promise."

"And then?" Ada asked, though her voice suggested she already knew.

"And then the last signatory fled." Bramble looked at her, gentle and merciless. "Maren Ishikawa. Your grandmother. The usurper came for her — threats, bribes, then blades. And instead of standing at Hollowmere and renewing the oath, she ran west, changed her name, buried the map, and raised her family to be small and safe and *silent*. The covenant broke not with a battle, Ada Ishikawa, but with a closed door and a packed bag."

The fire cracked. Kesh had gone very still. Sera watched Ada's face do what faces did when a person discovered their whole life had been built on someone else's cowardice.

"She was protecting us," Ada said finally.

"She was protecting *herself*, and called it love. The two wear similar coats." Bramble shrugged his great shoulders. "I don't say it to wound you. I say it because you'll have to decide, before Hollowmere, whether you're her blood or her answer. The wolves have been waiting eleven years to learn which."

Sera looked into the fire and thought of her own silence, her own scattering, her own six years of calling flight by gentler names. She said nothing. But beside her, Ada reached — absently, without looking — and turned Kesh's half-burned boot toward the flames so the wet leather wouldn't crack.

A small thing. A keeping-people-alive thing.

Sera began, very quietly, to hope.

***

Later, when Bramble slept and Kesh feigned sleep with the theatrical commitment of the young, Ada sat wrapped in a blanket watching the stars that weren't hers, and Sera came and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"Do you remember your song?" Ada asked. "Bramble said you were a singer."

Sera was quiet a long time. "I remember that I had one."

"And now?"

"Now there's a shape where it used to be." She turned her hand palm-up, as if the notes might be lying there. "It aches like a missing tooth."

Ada nodded slowly. "I know the ache," she said. "I've spent eleven years tasting poison so a queen could eat safely, and every night I go home to an empty room, and I tell myself it's duty. But some nights I think duty is just loneliness wearing armor." She glanced sideways. "Maybe songs are the same. Maybe yours isn't gone. Maybe it's just waiting to belong to something again."

Above them, far across the dark hills, a single wolf voice rose — wordless, wandering, searching for a harmony that wasn't there.

Kesh, awake after all, listened from the shadows of the lean-to, and said nothing, and memorized everything — which is why, three days later, when the usurper's inquisitors finally caught him alone at the stream, he knew exactly what they wanted, and exactly what it would cost them all.

He smiled at them, insolent even in chains, and said:

"You're too late. She's already chosen."
