## 3. The Usurper's Price

The borderlands smelled of rain that had not yet fallen, and of old iron, and — faintly, beneath everything, like a memory pressed between the pages of a book — of wolves.

Sera felt it first in her teeth.

She had felt nothing in her teeth for eleven years, not since the night the song went out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers. She had woken in a ditch on the far side of the mountains with mud in her mouth and no name for the silence inside her, only the certainty that something vast had been taken, or had left, or had never been hers at all. She had spent those eleven years learning to live as an ordinary woman: singing in taverns for coppers, sleeping under hedges, telling herself the hollow place behind her sternum was just hunger.

But tonight, walking the ridge above the drowned valley with Ada Ishikawa's map crackling in its oilskin sleeve against her hip, Sera's teeth ached. Her jaw ached. The roots of her hair ached. Something on the other side of the valley was calling in a frequency only her bones remembered how to hear.

"You feel it too," Bramble said quietly. The disgraced guildsman walked with his hands clasped behind his back like a man touring his own ruin, which, in a sense, he was. "Don't answer it. Whatever it is."

"I don't know what it is."

"Yes, you do." He did not look at her. His grey eyes stayed on the valley floor, where the river made a silver seam through the dark. "You've known since the ford. You hum in your sleep now. You didn't used to."

Sera stopped walking. Behind them, Kesh was arguing cheerfully with Ada about whether a door could be pickpocketed — the boy maintained it could, if you were quick enough and the door was greedy enough — and the sound of it, that small domestic noise, made Sera's chest tighten with a tenderness she had no use for and could not put down. Four days ago these people had been strangers. Now Kesh called her *Wren* because she'd once told him she was small and brown and mostly harmless, and Ada had begun saving him the heel of the bread without being asked, and Bramble — Bramble had started saying *we* instead of *I*, and flinching every time he caught himself doing it.

"We should camp short of the stone," Sera said. "The map says the covenant circle is half a day further. If something is singing from this side of it—"

"Something isn't." Ada had come up beside her, silent as always; the taster moved like a woman accustomed to not disturbing what she measured. In the last light her face was pale and set, and there was a new line between her brows that had not been there at the guildhall door. "The usurper's outriders crossed the Ash Ford two days ago. I counted their fires from the pass. They're not searching for us, Sera. They're herding us. Toward the circle."

"Then we go around."

"And let them take the border unopposed?" Ada shook her head, and for a moment the mask slipped, and Sera saw beneath it: not the cold professional who drank poison nightly so a crown might eat safely, but a tired young woman holding a ledger that would not balance. "My grandmother ran from this. She signed the covenant and then she ran, and everything since — every burned village, every broken door — grew from that one cowardice. I'm not running."

*We*, Sera thought. She had said *we* too.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen menhir, its carved faces worn smooth by centuries of weather, and Kesh built a fire too large and told a story too long, and for an hour the borderlands were almost kind.

That was when the dogs came.

Not dogs. Sera knew within three heartbeats, though they wore dog-shapes — broad-chested, black-muzzled things with eyes like struck flint. Inquisitor-hounds, bred in the usurper's kennels from stock that had once been something else, something older, perverted into tools. They came out of the dark without sound, six of them, and behind them came men in grey lacquered armor with the usurper's sigil — a closed hand — stamped on their pauldrons.

"Run!" Bramble shouted, and then the world became noise and firelight and Kesh screaming.

They did not run fast enough. They were never going to run fast enough; Sera understood even as she spun, even as she put her back to the fire, that this had been arranged, that the herding had been real, that the inquisitors had wanted them tired and grouped and far from any door. Two hounds took Kesh down before he reached the trees. A third pinned Bramble with its weight while a soldier bound him with wire that smoked where it touched his skin — guildsman's wire, oath-sealing, the kind that bit liars.

And Ada stood very still in the firelight, hands empty and open, wearing the expression of a woman reading a ledger entry she has been dreading.

A tall inquisitor stepped into the light. His armor was plain but his voice was oiled. "Ada Ishikawa," he said. "Taster to the Crown. Sworn in blood to the Queen's table." He smiled. "How far from your queen you've wandered."

"She knows where I am," Ada said evenly. "I write to her."

"She does." The inquisitor produced a letter — her letter, Sera realized, sealed with the little wolf stamp Ada used for her private correspondence. "Her Majesty commands your return. And the map. And the boy." He turned his closed-hand sigil toward the trees, toward the sound of Kesh struggling. "Though perhaps we can simplify matters. The map for the child. The Crown gets its taster back; the traitors get their whelp. Everyone leaves this valley whole."

Silence. Fire popping. Somewhere a nightbird, foolishly, singing.

Sera watched Ada's face across the flames and understood, with terrible clarity, what was happening inside her. Duty sat on one shoulder — duty that had fed her, housed her, given her purpose when she had none, sworn in her own blood at fifteen years old. Love sat on the other, love she had never asked for, a runaway boy and a disgraced old man and a broken singer, a found family assembled out of wreckage. And between them the ledger, always the ledger, weighing what could not be weighed.

"The trade is fair," the inquisitor prompted.

"It is fair," Ada agreed softly. "That's what makes it obscene."

She moved. Later, Sera would try to reconstruct it and fail — whether Ada had planned it in some taster's cold calculus all along, or whether the decision simply passed through her like lightning through ground. She drew Bramble's belt-knife from his scabbard where he lay pinned, and she did not throw it at the inquisitor, and she did not cut the wires. She opened her own palm to the bone, and she pressed her bleeding hand flat against the fallen menhir, against the worn-smooth face of the covenant stone, and she spoke words that were not in any language Sera knew but that her *bones* knew, that made her teeth sing and her ears ring and eleven years of silence shudder like a frozen lake in thaw.

Blood woke the stone. The stone woke something else.

The hounds stopped. All six of them, mid-motion, heads turning as one toward the sound rising out of the valley below — out of the earth itself, a low sustained tone like the deepest string of an enormous instrument being drawn by an invisible bow. The inquisitor's smile died. The soldiers' horses screamed and bolted.

And Sera opened her mouth, and the forgotten song came home.

It poured out of her rough and wordless at first, cracked from disuse, ugly as a foal standing for the first time — and then it caught, and steadied, and rose, harmonizing with the stone's deep note, and she felt the hollow place behind her sternum fill for the first time in eleven years until she thought her ribs would split with it. The hounds lay down. The wires on Bramble's wrists charred to ash. Kesh crawled free of the trees, bloody and staring, and the inquisitors fled into the dark because even usurpers' men know better than to stand between a covenant waking and the world.

When it ended, Sera fell to her knees in the wet grass, gasping, whole, undone. Ada knelt beside her, cradling her ruined hand, and there was blood on both of them and neither of them knew whose.

"They'll bring the vanguard now," Bramble rasped from the ground, staring at his freed wrists. "Everything they have. By tomorrow's dusk."

Ada looked east, where the horizon already glowed faintly with the fires of an army. Then she looked at her bleeding palm, and at Sera, and something in her face settled — not peace, exactly, but decision, the final sum written at last at the bottom of the page.

"Then we have until dusk," she said, "to learn the rest of the song."

* * *

They walked through the night toward the covenant circle, and the stones sang to them as they came — softly, tentatively, the way old friends speak after long estrangement, testing whether the voices answering are truly the ones they remember.
