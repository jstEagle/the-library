## 4. When the Wolf Sings

The covenant circle had been broken for sixty years, and the stones remembered it.

Ada felt their grief before she crossed the threshold of the ruin — a low ache in her teeth, the same sensation she'd learned to read as poison working through her blood, except this was older and colder. Nine monoliths stood in a ring on the borderland hill, half-toppled, furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Between them, the grass grew wrong: silver-veined, whispering when there was no wind.

She had tasted death every night for eleven years. She had never once tasted anything like this place.

"They're coming," Kesh said from the eastern stone, where he'd climbed to keep watch. His voice cracked on the second word. He was sixteen and had been a runaway apprentice for four days, and he kept touching the bruise the inquisitors had left on his throat like it was a rosary. "I can see their fires past the river. Hours, Ada. Maybe three."

Bramble said nothing. The disgraced guildsman stood at the circle's heart with his hands jammed in his coat pockets, staring at the fallen tenth stone as if it owed him money. It had been his guild-door that brought them here; his guild-name that opened it; his shame that made him flinch every time someone looked at him too long. He had spent twenty years pretending the Guild of Impossible Things was just a ledger with delusions of grandeur.

"Say it," Sera said quietly. She stood at Ada's shoulder, close enough that Ada could feel the wolf-singer's warmth through the cold coming off the stones. "Tell her what the price is. All of it. She deserves all of it."

Ada looked down at the map in her hands — her grandmother's map, inked in a hand that shook at the edges, showing not roads but *obligations*, threads binding realm to realm. The courier had died pressing it into her arms. *The last signatory's heir,* he'd gasped. *The covenant broke when she fled. Only her blood can sing it whole.*

Her grandmother had fled rather than pay the price. Ada knew now what that price was; Bramble had told them at the fire two nights ago, in the flat voice he used for accounting entries he couldn't balance.

"Say it," she agreed, because she wanted to hear it spoken aloud, in the open air, where it would be real and therefore survivable.

"The pact requires a signatory," Bramble said. "Not a name on paper. A *voice*. When your grandmother sang the covenant into being, she gave the song itself into the bond — her voice, permanently, so that no one could ever unmake the pact by stealing the singer's tongue or buying her silence. And—" he hesitated.

"And my immunity," Ada finished. She almost smiled. "The one thing I've ever owned outright. Eleven years of poisoning myself nightly so the crown could eat safely, and the guild's records list my constitution as an asset of the realm." She turned her palms up. The scars there were faint, ritual-thin. "The pact takes both. My voice and my resistance. I become the covenant's body. Every treaty-breaker bleeds where I bleed. Every oath kept heals me. Forever."

"Forever," Kesh echoed, hushed.

"Forever," Bramble confirmed. "There is no exit clause. Believe me. I've read the impossible ledgers. There are *no* exit clauses anywhere in the Guild."

* * *

They prepared the circle in silence, mostly.

Kesh swept fifty years of leaf-rot from the ninth stone and found, beneath it, the carved names of every signatory back to the founding — human names and wolf-names alternating around the ring like partners in a dance. He traced them with one finger and read them aloud without being asked, giving the dead back their sound. Ada watched him do it and thought: *this is why I refuse to trade him.* Not duty. Not mercy. Because a boy who gives the forgotten their names back belongs in any family worth having.

Sera walked the ring alone, barefoot, her boots abandoned by the threshold stone. Twice she stopped and pressed her palm flat against the granite and shuddered. The third time, she hummed — three notes, broken, like a cup with a crack in it.

"I should know this," she said, coming back to Ada. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "My mother sang it. Her mother before her. The wolf-song isn't taught, Ada, it's *inherited*, passed mouth to mouth down the singing lines, and mine ended when the covenant broke because there was nothing left to carry. I have been empty for thirty years and calling it peace." She laughed, ragged. "And now the song wants to come home through *you*, a taster who can't hold a tune, and I'm supposed to stand beside you and remember what I've spent my whole life forgetting."

"You don't have to remember," Ada said. "You only have to choose it."

"That's worse."

"I know." Ada touched her wrist, where the pulse lived, where the poisons had always lived. "Choosing is always worse than obeying. That's how you know it matters."

* * *

The vanguard's fires crested the ridge while the light failed — orange points in the blue dusk, patient as a slow fever. Horns sounded twice across the valley. Somewhere below, ten thousand soldiers who believed they were marching to liberate the free cities began to form lines.

Ada stepped into the center of the ring.

She had expected terror. What came instead was something quieter and more terrible: recognition. The stones leaned toward her. The silver grass went still. Beneath her feet, deep as a heartbeat, she felt the covenant's absence — a wound shaped exactly like her grandmother's flight, and exactly, she understood now, like her own life. A woman built entirely of duty, standing in the footprint of a woman who had abandoned hers.

"No," she said aloud, to the stones, to the dark, to herself. "That's not what this is."

"What?" Sera asked.

"She didn't flee because she was a coward. She fled because she was chosen — born into it, bound by blood, given no say. Same as me with the crown. Same poison, different cup." Ada lifted her chin. "So I'm going to do it differently. Hear me, old ones. I don't come because I'm heir. I come because I *choose*. My voice, freely given. My immunity, freely given. Not duty. Not debt. Choice."

The silence after her words was enormous.

Then Sera stepped into the circle beside her, took her hand, and opened her mouth — and the song came out of her whole.

It rose like water finding an old channel: the wolf-song, the covenant-song, the first agreement ever sung between what walks on two legs and what runs on four. Sera's voice carried the melody; Ada, who truly could not hold a tune, found that it didn't matter, because the song didn't need her voice to be beautiful. It needed it to be *given*. She poured her eleven years of swallowed poison into it, her loneliness, her ledgers, the taste of the queen's supper every night, the taste of belonging around a campfire with three strangers who had become, without anyone announcing it, her family.

The stones woke.

Light ran the ring like dawn moving westward, name by carved name. The fallen tenth stone groaned upright. Far away, across the valley, the horns faltered mid-call — and then the armies' fires guttered gold-green as old oaths written into the land itself rose up and stood between the soldiers and the free cities, vast and drowsy and unmistakable, wolves the size of hills made of nothing but remembered promises.

The vanguard stopped. The usurper's war did not end that night, but it stalled at the border like a wave meeting a seawall, and the seawall was a song.

When the last note faded, Ada opened her mouth to speak—

—and nothing came out.

She stood in the ringing silence and felt the truth settle into her bones: her voice belonged to the pact now. So did the strange new warmth in her blood, where eleven years of immunity had been, traded away, offered up, gone into the bond like coin into a vault. From tonight, if the free cities bled, she would bleed. If oaths were honored somewhere, she would heal.

Kesh reached her first. He couldn't speak either — he was crying too hard — so he simply wrapped his arms around her, and then Sera was there, and Bramble, awkward and gruff, patting her shoulder like he was balancing a final account and finding it, against all odds, in credit.

Ada could not say *thank you*. She could not say anything at all, forever.

But when Sera looked at her — really looked, the way no one had since the courier died at her door — Ada smiled, and Sera answered in the only language that mattered anymore, humming three notes that were no longer broken, and Ada understood that she had lost her voice and gained, in exchange, the one thing she had never once possessed in eleven years of service:

A family that heard her anyway.

Below the hill, the wolves of old oath turned their heads toward the circle, and bowed.

Above it, far off along the northern road, a single rider broke from the stalled army at a gallop — carrying, whether warning or surrender or something stranger, the news that the covenant had woken.

And its singer had no voice at all.
