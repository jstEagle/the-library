## 1. Charms for a City Without Magic

The rain in Vell falls the way it always does—politely, persistently, as if it has been paid to. It came down that morning in fine grey threads, beading on my awning, running off the canvas in a curtain that kept my little corner of Copperlane Market dry and dim and mine.

I had been there three years, long enough that the fishwives called me by name, long enough that the baker's boy no longer tried to shortchange me, long enough that I had almost stopped flinching when someone said *Thackeray* in passing—which was never, because nobody in Vell had ever heard the name, and that was rather the point of everything.

"Charms!" I called, in the voice I kept for market days, warm and slightly foolish. "Charms against rain, against rheumatism, against mothers-in-law! Fair prices, honest tin!"

That last part was true, which made it the best lie I told all day.

My stall was a study in deliberate disappointment. A tray of copper bells that rang with nothing but good metallurgy. Braided cords dyed the blue of deep water, sold as protection against drowning, though I could not have saved a drowning cat. Little glass beads I swore were cooled from lightning, when in fact they were cooled from a kiln in the Tanners' Quarter and cost me four coppers the dozen. Everything I sold was worthless. That was the craft of it—the real craft, the one I'd practiced longer than any other. Worthlessness, performed convincingly, seven days a week.

Because here is what you must understand about Vell: it is a city without magic. Not a city where magic is rare, or taxed, or frowned upon at dinner parties. Vell is built on the principle that magic does not exist, and the city enforces this belief the way other cities enforce sanitation. The Charter of Unmaking, signed two hundred years ago by merchants who were tired of wizards burning their warehouses, decreed that no sorcery would be practiced within the walls, that no grimoire would be sold within them, and that anyone caught doing either would be introduced to the river in an informative manner. The watch carried lanterns of smoked glass that supposedly revealed enchantment. They didn't, of course—but then again, neither did most of the charms people bought anyway, because human beings will pay good money to feel protected whether or not protection is actually on offer.

I was, technically, the most dangerous criminal in the city. I was also its least impressive one. The storm sat under my skin like a second heartbeat, had sat there since birth, humming along at a frequency I had spent seven years learning to ignore the way you learn to ignore a toothache: completely, and never, both at once.

Some mornings it was quiet. Some mornings it hummed like a struck tuning fork buried in wool. On the bad mornings—the ones when the clouds stacked up over the northern hills in towers, when the air tasted of iron—I had to press my palms flat against the stall's counter and breathe until the pressure behind my eyes receded. The storm wanted out. It had always wanted out. My whole life was the art of saying no to it in small ways, every hour, forever.

"Charm against rain?" said a woman with a basket of eels.

"Won't work," I said cheerfully. "Nothing works against rain in Vell. But this bell sounds cheerful, and cheerful people dry faster."

She laughed and bought the bell. This was the secret economy of my trade: honesty about dishonesty. People didn't come to me for magic. They came for the performance of care—a stranger looking them in the eye and telling them the world might be survivable. I was very good at it. I had to be. It was either that or the river.

I won't pretend it wasn't lonely. There is a particular kind of loneliness that comes from being surrounded by people who know your name and none of whom know anything else about you. I knew the fishwives' husbands' names, their children's fevers, their grievances with the toll-collectors. They knew I sold charms and that I laughed easily and that I never, ever talked about where I'd come from. Seven years of that. Seven years of conversations with trapdoors in every floor.

Once—early on, my first winter—a girl named Sefa who worked the dye-vats had asked me, over cheap wine, where I'd learned to braid cord so neatly. And I had felt the truth rise in my throat like floodwater: *my mother taught me, she braided storm-cords for the rites, she tied the winds themselves into knots and hung them from the temple rafters, she—*

And instead I'd said, "My grandmother. She was fussy about knots."

Sefa had nodded and drunk her wine and believed me, and I had gone home and been sick from the effort of it, and after that I had been more careful. That was the tuition of exile. You paid it once per lesson, and the lessons never stopped coming.

* * *

The scholar came at midday, when the rain thickened and the market thinned to the stubborn and the desperate.

He was young—younger than his stoop suggested—with ink-stained fingers and the haunted look of a man who had slept badly for several consecutive weeks. He hovered at the edge of my awning for a long moment, working up courage the way some people work up the nerve to touch a hot stove.

"You're the charm-seller," he said. Not a question. His eyes did a strange thing then—they flicked to my face and stayed there a beat too long, the way people look at a word they've read a hundred times but only just noticed is spelled strangely.

"People keep saying that like it's an accusation," I said. "It's charms. Tin and copper and optimism. Would you like some optimism? I have it in three colors."

He didn't laugh. Scholars rarely do when you want them to; it's a professional condition. He stepped under the awning, dripping, and set a wrapped bundle on my counter with the delicacy of a man handling something either precious or cursed.

"I need—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm looking for a charm. A specific one."

"For what ailment?"

"Not an ailment." He glanced over his shoulder at the nearly empty market lane. Rain drummed on the awning. Somewhere a vendor was arguing about herring. "A date."

I blinked. "You want a charm that tells dates? The watch-house has a calendar. Free, even."

"No." His hands were trembling. I noticed things like that—it's survival training, noticing tremors—and filed the observation away in the drawer marked *later*, which was getting crowded. "I mean the charm I'm looking for is described as belonging to a certain date. To a person, on a certain date. Tonight, in fact." He swallowed. "In Copperlane Market. A stranger selling a copper bell."

The rain got very loud all of a sudden, or maybe I just stopped hearing everything else.

"Now that's a coincidence," I said, and my market-voice held, thank every indifferent god, though it came out a half-tone lower than usual. "I sell copper bells. I'm a stranger. I'm here tonight. But I've been here three years, friend, so whoever wrote your description was off by a thousand nights."

"It's not my description." He unwrapped the bundle.

I should have stopped him. I want that on the record, wherever records go when you're dead or exiled or merely tired: I knew, in the marrow-deep way you know a storm is turning before the wind arrives, that I should have put my hand flat on that bundle and pushed it back across the counter and told him to take his troubles to a priest, except Vell doesn't have those either, and sent him out into the rain.

Instead I looked.

It was a book. Cracked leather, swollen with damp, its corners worn round as river stones. Old—not old the way antiquarians mean, with loving restoration, but old the way shipwrecks are old, salvaged and salt-damaged and lucky to exist. The scholar opened it with fingers that shook, and I saw pages of dense handwriting in faded brown-black ink, and my skin prickled from wrist to shoulder in a way that had nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the storm under my sternum sitting up, suddenly, like a dog that hears its name.

"Where did you get this?" I asked quietly.

"A house clearance. In the Salt Quarter. An estate of a dead collector." He turned pages with increasing agitation. "Most of it's nonsense—predictions about harvests, wars, the usual prophetic mush, half of it already wrong. But this passage—" He found it. His finger came down on the page like a gavel. "Read it. Please. Tell me I'm mad."

I read it.

The handwriting was cramped and urgent, slanting hard to the right as if the writer had been running while they wrote. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood. And the words—

*A stranger shall stand in the rain-market of Vell, in the low quarter where copper is sold, on the eve of the thirteenth of Thawmonth, and the stranger's eyes shall be the grey of the hour before lightning. They will offer a bell for sale, and the bell will ring without being struck.*

I looked down at my tray of bells. Twelve of them, arranged in neat rows, each one silent, each one ordinary, each one rung and tested that very morning by my own hand to confirm its pleasing, harmless, entirely mechanical jingle.

"The thirteenth of Thawmonth," the scholar whispered. "That's tonight. That's *now*. And your eyes—"

"My eyes are brown," I lied instantly, smoothly, with the fluency of long practice. "Bad lighting in here. All sorts of things look grey in bad lighting."

"They're not—"

"They're brown." I closed the book gently on his finger before he could point at anything else, which earned me a yelp. "Look, friend. I don't know what game this is. Someone wrote a poem a hundred years ago about a bell-seller, and now you want it to be me because I happen to match the occupation. Do you know how many charm-sellers there are in Vell?"

"Four," he said immediately. "I checked. Two are women, one is ninety, and the fourth sells from a fixed shop on Bellows Street and hasn't worked a market stall in a decade. You're the only itinerant copper-bell seller in Copperlane. I spent six days confirming it." His voice cracked. "Do you understand what I'm saying? I cross-referenced a prophecy. *Me.* I'm a cataloguer at the Athenaeum. I check shipping manifests for arithmetic errors. And I found—"

He stopped himself, but too late. The word was already in the air between us, unspoken and enormous.

*I found you.*

The storm under my skin had gone very still. That was the worst sign. When it hummed, I knew how to handle it—breathe, press my palms down, count backwards from a hundred in the old tongue, the counting-song my mother taught me, the one I wasn't supposed to remember. But when it went still, it was listening. It was paying attention. Seven years of silence, and something in a rotten old book had said its name, or near enough, and now it was awake and waiting to see what I would do.

"Take your book," I said, "and go home."

"You haven't even—"

"Go home. Eat something. Sleep. Tomorrow you'll reread that passage and notice it says the bell rings *without being struck*, and you'll look at my bells, which ring exactly as much as physics demands, and you'll feel very silly, and we'll both be better for it." I smiled my market smile. It felt like wearing someone else's coat. "No charge for the advice. Best charm I sell."

He stared at me. And here is the thing I will remember about him, later, when remembering him matters: he didn't look convinced, and he didn't look disappointed. He looked *afraid*—not of me, exactly, but of what wanting me to be real was doing to him. A man who has found a door in a wall that isn't supposed to have doors, and can't decide whether to knock.

"Maren," he said abruptly.

"What?"

"My name. Maren Oduya. If you change your mind. The Athenaeum, third floor, catalogues and correspondence." He rewrapped the book with clumsy haste, tucked it under his arm, and stood there a moment longer in the rain-smell and the lamplight, looking at me with those sleepless eyes. "The passage continues, you know. Past the bell. I didn't read you the rest."

"Good," I said. "Keep it that way."

He left. I watched him go, a thin stooped figure dissolving into the grey, and then I stood alone under my awning with my heart going like a drum and my twelve harmless bells gleaming up at me in their rows.

Slowly, deliberately, I picked up the nearest one and struck it with my knuckle.

It rang. Clean, bright, ordinary. Copper singing the only song copper knows.

I struck the next one. And the next. Down the whole tray, one by one, proving to myself what I already knew, what I had spent seven years making certain of: that nothing in my stall was magic, that nothing in my life was magic, that I was Lior the charm-seller of Copperlane Market and not whatever the book had said, and that prophecies were nonsense anyway, always had been, especially the ones written a century ago by dead people who couldn't possibly have known—

The last bell rang.

Then, faintly, underneath it—so faint I might have imagined it, so faint I would spend the walk home telling myself I had imagined it—one of the others gave a single soft chime.

Untouched. Unstruck. In windless rain.

I stood very still in my empty market, water dripping off the awning, and listened to the silence where the sound had been.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would skip Copperlane entirely. Sell in the Salt Quarter instead. Avoid the scholar, avoid the book, avoid the whole ridiculous business. One night's bad luck, one coincidental verse, and the sensible response was to be somewhere else when it came looking.

The storm under my skin began, very softly, to hum.

It sounded almost pleased.
