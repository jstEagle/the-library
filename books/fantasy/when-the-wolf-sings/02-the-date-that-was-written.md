## 2. The Date That Was Written

The trouble with plans made in moments of resolve is that they assume tomorrow will cooperate.

Tomorrow did not cooperate. Tomorrow began with rain again—the kind of thin, patient drizzle Vell specialized in, water with no opinion about anything—and by the time I'd hauled my cart to the Salt Quarter, I had already stepped in a puddle to the ankle, lost my best ribbon of dried lavender to a gust of wind, and been accused by a fishwife of shorting her on change when I hadn't sold her so much as a button. The storm under my skin hummed along through all of it like a tuneless companion who refused to be left at home.

Still. No scholar. That was the important part.

The Salt Quarter smelled the way it always did, of brine and tar and the particular sourness of fish left too long in the sun, and its market was louder and less thoughtful than Copperlane's. Salt Quarter folk didn't ask what my charms *meant*. They asked whether they worked on boils, whether they worked on husbands, and whether I'd throw in a second one for half price. It was, frankly, a relief. There is a particular exhaustion in selling lies to people who want them to be true; selling obvious nonsense to people who only want a bargain felt almost honest.

I was doing brisk trade in knotted red thread—"against the evil eye," which in the Salt Quarter mostly meant against mothers-in-law—when I felt it.

Not heard. Felt. A change in the texture of the crowd behind me, the way air moves differently around someone standing very still in the middle of motion. Seven years of hiding teaches you to read crowds the way sailors read weather, and the weather behind me had gone flat and watchful.

I didn't turn around. Turning around is for amateurs and the doomed. I finished wrapping the thread, smiled at the boil-sufferer, told her to wear it on the left wrist and think unkind thoughts about no one but her mother-in-law, and then, casually, with all the grace of a woman checking for rain clouds, I glanced over my shoulder.

Maren.

She stood at the edge of my little patch of cobblestone clutching something wrapped in oilcloth to her chest the way other people clutch infants or weapons, and she was looking at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors on bad days. It was the look of someone who has not slept because sleep requires believing the world will still make sense in the morning.

"You're supposed to be in Copperlane," she said.

"Charm-sellers are free agents of the realm." I turned back to my customer, held up two fingers—two threads, one copper—and got a nod. "You're supposed to be reading something less upsetting."

"I brought it."

Of course she had. Of course she had carried a book across a rainy city to show the stranger from a prophecy the stranger from the prophecy, because scholars are constitutionally incapable of leaving a mystery alone, the way some people can't leave a scab alone. I handed over the threads, took the coppers, and let the queue thin out while I performed the elaborate pantomime of tidying a stall that was already tidy.

When the last customer drifted off toward the fish stalls, I looked at her properly. She was drenched. Her hood had given up somewhere around dawn, judging by the state of her hair, and there were dark crescents under her eyes that spoke of either long study or long fear. Possibly both. With books, those tend to travel together.

"Maren."

"Lior." She unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that weren't quite steady. "I need you to see this. And I need you to not run away before you do."

"That's a strange thing to say to someone you met yesterday."

"It's a perfectly reasonable thing to say to someone I've read about."

The words dropped into my chest like a stone into a well, and I watched her face while I waited to hear the splash, and her face said she knew exactly what she'd just said and had decided to say it anyway.

*Read about.* Not dreamed about, not invented, not misremembered. Read.

Around us the market roared on—hagglers, gulls, a man three stalls down swearing at a crate of eels that had opinions about being crated—and none of it touched the small cold space that had opened between me and everything else. The storm under my skin went quiet. It does that sometimes, right before lightning. I've learned to hate the quiet.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

We ended up in the back room of the Broken Keel, which is not a tavern so much as a place where alcohol goes to be forgotten. The innkeeper, a woman named Brava with forearms like mooring rope, took one look at Maren's face, one look at mine, and put us in the back room with a pot of tea and instructions not to burn anything down, delivered in the tone of a woman who had once had to explain smoke damage to a landlord.

The codex sat on the table between us.

It was smaller than I'd imagined, whatever I'd imagined, which was nothing—I'd imagined nothing, I'd spent an entire night imagining nothing with tremendous discipline—but even so it seemed wrong that a thing capable of ruining a person should fit in two hands. Cracked leather covers, gone the color of old tea, stitched spine sagging like a tired horse's back. The edges of the pages were furred and browned. Someone had loved it once; there were careful repairs along the hinges, wax-sealed corners, a smell of cedar shavings where someone had fought the damp with camphor for decades.

"A century old," Maren said. "Give or take. The colophon says it was copied in the year 1123, from an older manuscript. The older manuscript doesn't survive—or if it does, nobody admits to having it."

"And you found this where?"

"The University's restricted archive. Which is a grand name for a locked room under the astronomy tower where they keep things the faculty find embarrassing." She turned the cover back with the reverence other people reserved for relics, which, I suppose, is exactly what she thought it was. "Embarrassing things are usually the true ones. Nobody bothers to lock away nonsense."

The first pages were lists. Names, dates, verses in a crabbed hand—prophecies, or what passed for them. Most were the usual fortune-teller's gruel. *A king shall rise whose crown sits crooked.* *Beware the third harvest.* The kind of thing that fits any year if you squint, which is the entire business model of prophecy and always has been. I felt my shoulders start to come down from around my ears. This was nothing. This was the sort of book every city had three of, gathering damp in locked rooms while real life happened upstairs.

Then she reached the last quarter of the book and the handwriting changed.

It wasn't just neater, though it was that—it was *different*, the way a voice is different. The letters leaned forward, urgent, as though the scribe had been running out of time rather than ink. The ink itself was darker. And the margins, empty everywhere else in the book, here carried annotations in a second hand, smaller, sharper, added later.

"This section isn't dated the way the others are," Maren said. "No year. Just..." She ran her finger down the page and stopped. "Just this."

She turned the book toward me.

I have tried, since, to describe what it's like to read your own face in a hundred-year-old book, and I've never managed better than: it is like stepping onto stairs that aren't there. For a moment the world simply refuses, and you hang in the refusal.

The verse was short. Prophecies usually are; dread compresses well.

*A stranger comes when the rains will not end,
storm-grey eyed, with a bell of copper.
Her wares are nothing; her silence is not.
On the eve of the Drowned Saint's feast she stands in the market of scales,
and the wolf beneath her skin remembers its song.*

Below it, in the sharper marginal hand, someone had written a single word:

*Verified.*

"There's more," Maren said softly. "There's a whole sequence. But read the date reference first. Tell me what today is."

Today was the eve of the Drowned Saint's feast. Everyone in Vell knew that; the feast was tomorrow, which meant tonight the city would hang lanterns shaped like fish and get cheerfully drunk on brine-wine, and the markets would close early, and—

And the Salt Quarter was called the market of scales. Fish scales, merchant scales, the great brass scale outside the harbormaster's office that hadn't balanced in living memory. I had chosen it yesterday specifically because it was *not* where the book said I would be, and it had never occurred to me—not once, not for a heartbeat—that the book might have known that too. That the verse might have been written loosely enough, or cruelly enough, to catch me wherever I fled.

"Copper bell," I said. My voice came out level, which I counted as a professional triumph. "I sell those. Anyone could write that. Charm-sellers sell bells. Storm-grey eyes—" I gestured at my own face. "Half the north has grey eyes. It's the weather. We're all marinated in it from birth."

"On the eve of the Drowned Saint's feast," Maren said. "In the market of scales. While the rain doesn't stop." She ticked each item off on her fingers, gently, the way you'd count wounds. "Lior. The rains haven't stopped in nineteen days. The almanac says that's the longest wet stretch in thirty years. And the verse says *her wares are nothing*. Your charms don't do anything. I bought one. I've tested it. It's a pretty piece of tin."

"It's a *lovely* piece of tin."

"Lior."

"And 'the wolf beneath her skin'?" I laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp, bouncing off the low ceiling of the back room. "That's not me. That's not anyone. Wolves don't live under skin, that's poetry, that's—"

I stopped.

Because I had almost said *that's not how magic works*, and the punchline of my entire life is that it's exactly how magic works, that power does curl up inside you like an animal, warm and hungry and waiting for permission, and that I had spent seven years feeding it nothing, starving it into stillness, and that on certain nights—in windless rain, over a bell that wouldn't ring—it stirred anyway, and hummed, and sounded *pleased*.

Maren watched me stop talking. She was smarter than I'd credited yesterday, sitting nervous among my trinkets. Nervous people often are. Fear is just attention with nowhere to go.

"You know what it's describing," she said quietly. "Don't you. You knew yesterday, when you told me prophecies were cheap. You weren't laughing at the book. You were laughing because it scared you."

The tea had gone cold between us. Outside, the gulls argued over something dead, and somewhere down the street a child practiced a feast-song badly and repeatedly. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that had no idea it was sitting on top of my entire life like a lid.

"Who wrote it?" I asked. "The last section. The handwriting changes—who is it?"

"No attribution. The copyist didn't know either, or didn't say. The marginal notes go on for pages—corrections, cross-references, dates that haven't happened yet. Some of them already have." She turned a few leaves, careful as a surgeon. "Here. *The crooked-crown king marches in the year the bridges freeze.* Kaelen Voss took Harrowgate's bridge towns two winters ago. Here—*the lantern-bearers come south hunting a name.*"

"Inquisitors carry lanterns," I said. "Grey-lanterns. It's their badge."

"Yes." She looked up at me. "I know. I checked. Lior, whoever wrote this didn't predict the future. They *scheduled* it. There's a difference, and the difference is terrifying."

I pushed back from the table and stood, because sitting still had become impossible, because my legs wanted to do what my mind wanted to do, which was leave. Go south. Keep going south until the rain turned warm and the saints had different names and no book anywhere contained my face.

Except.

Except the verse had said *market of scales*, and I had gone to the market of scales, and there she'd been. Except the verse said *her wares are nothing*, and they were. You cannot outrun a sentence that describes the shape of your running.

"How much of the book is left?" I asked. "After tonight's verse. How much future is in it?"

Maren's hands went still on the page.

"That's the thing," she said. "That's why I came instead of sending word. After tonight's entry, there's one more verse. Then the handwriting stops mid-page. Mid-*word*, almost. Like the writer was interrupted." She hesitated. "Or like they wrote everything they intended to and the rest was never meant for readers. There's a final line, though. A title, set apart, bigger than the rest. Whoever wrote it wanted that word remembered above everything."

"Say it."

She turned the book fully toward me, and there it was, inked large and deliberate across the bottom of the final written page, in letters that leaned forward like a runner:

**WOLF-SINGER.**

The storm under my skin woke.

Not loudly—that was the horror of it. Not a crash, not a surge. It simply opened one eye, the way a wolf opens one eye when it hears its name spoken across a valley, and lay listening. And I stood in a rented back room smelling of cold tea and camphor, a century away from whoever had dipped that pen, and felt myself *recognized*.

"I don't know what that means," I said.

"You do."

"I don't." I picked up my coat. My hands were steady. I noted this the way you note the behavior of a stranger. "Thank you for the book, Maren. Thank you for the tea. Tomorrow is a feast day; I'll be closed. If you have any sense—and I think you do—you'll put that thing back under the astronomy tower, relock the embarrassing door, and forget the name Wolf-anything ever crossed your path. People have vanished over less than a word. Especially that word, in this decade, with that king marching."

"Where will you go?"

"Somewhere without scales."

I was at the door when she said it—not loud, just clear, the way true things are:

"*The stranger flees south, but the road bends north.*"

I stopped. I didn't turn around. Turning around is for amateurs and the doomed.

"It's in there, isn't it," she said. "The next verse. You saw it while I was talking. That's why you're leaving *now*."

Nineteen days of rain. A bell that wouldn't ring. A word like a hook set in the water of the world, and me on the line, feeling every gentle tug.

"Go home, Maren," I said, and walked out into the rain that would not end.

* * *

The streets had begun to fill with feast-eve preparations—lanterns going up on hooks, fish-shaped and glowing, children hauling garlands of kelp and shell—and I moved through it all with my collar up and my eyes down, doing arithmetic. Coin on hand: eleven coppers and a bad silver. Roads south: two, both watched in festival season. Roads anywhere: fewer than I needed.

I was three streets from the Broken Keel when I realized I was being followed, and another half-street realizing it wasn't Maren.

Two figures, grey cloaks, walking with the unhurried certainty of people who already knew where I lived. Not watchmen—watchmen swagger. These two moved like the lantern-bearers moved, like men carrying something heavy and official in their coats. And one of them held, cupped against the drizzle, a small grey lantern whose light didn't flicker in the wind at all.

The verse surfaced, unbidden, in a dead woman's leaning hand:

*The lantern-bearers come south, hunting a name.*

They hadn't seen me yet. The crowd was thick, the light was bad, and I had seven years of practice at being furniture. I could still slip away. Eleven coppers, the clothes I stood in, and a head start of perhaps forty heartbeats.

But Maren was still in that back room. With the book. With the word *Wolf-Singer* open on the table in front of her like an accusation, and a pot of cold tea, and no idea that the lanterns had already come south, already come *here*, already come hunting.

The wolf under my skin rose to its feet.

I turned around.
