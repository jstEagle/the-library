## 3. What the Codex Calls Them

Maren Ostrell knocked on my door at the hour when honest people are asleep and liars are just getting comfortable.

I knew it was her before I opened it—she has a knock like a debt collector, three sharp raps and then a pause as if to say *I know you're in there pretending not to be*. Nobody else in Vell knocked that way. Nobody else in Vell came to my door twice in one week, either. In seven years of selling charms that did nothing but look pretty, I had cultivated a customer base of exactly zero repeat visitors who mattered.

I opened the door with the chain on, which is a habit from a life I don't talk about, and looked out at her through the gap. Rain again. Vell's rain doesn't fall so much as settle, a grey insistence that seeps under doors and into boots and eventually into your bones if you let it stay long enough. Maren stood in it without an umbrella, clutching something wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a foundling.

"You said come back," she said.

"I said *maybe*."

"Maybe is a yes for people who mean no and can't afford to say it." She pushed past the chain with the confidence of someone who had already decided this conversation was happening indoors. "It's freezing, Thackeray. Let me in before I turn into one of your trinkets."

There are perhaps four people in Vell whose opinion of me keeps me up at night, and Maren Ostrell had forced her way onto that list in a single evening. I unhooked the chain because the alternative was standing in a doorway arguing with a scholar while the rain did its patient work on both of us, and because—and I want to be honest here, since honesty is otherwise scarce in this story—a part of me had been waiting for her since she left.

That part of me should have been drowned at birth.

* * *

She unwrapped the codex on my table like a surgeon laying out instruments. Cracked leather, corners worn round, a spine stitched with thread gone the color of old tea. It smelled of mildew and tallow smoke and, underneath that, faintly of something metallic that made the hair on my arms stand up before I'd even touched it.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"An estate sale. A dead archivist's collection, sold off by nephews who couldn't read half of it." She smoothed the cover with both hands, and I noticed they were trembling—not from cold. "The nephews thought it was a hymnal."

"And you knew better."

"I knew *strange* better. There's a difference." She opened it to a ribbon marker and turned the book toward me. "Read it again. Out loud, this time. I want to hear how it sounds in your mouth."

The candle between us guttered, though there was no draft. I told myself it was a bad wick. I have gotten very good at telling myself things; it's most of what I do.

The page was dense, cramped script in an ink that had gone brown with age, and beneath it a verse set apart in larger letters, the way you'd set apart something meant to be memorized:

*When the bell of copper sings unsold,*
*and storm-grey eyes look out on Vell,*
*the Wolf-Singer walks the world again,*
*and the seal upon the north shall fail—or hold.*

I read it once silently. Then, because Maren asked and because some stubborn scholarly instinct in me wanted to hear it too, out loud.

My voice didn't shake. I'll give myself that. The candle flame leaned toward the book like a listener, and I told myself about the wick again.

"That's tonight," Maren said quietly. "That's *you*, Thackeray. You sold me a copper bell last night. You wouldn't take payment for it—you gave it away, which no charm-seller in the history of commerce has ever done. And your eyes—" She stopped herself, which I appreciated. People usually don't.

"It's coincidence," I said. "Vell has forty thousand people. Some of them sell bells. Some of them have grey eyes. Statistically, several must be strangers. The verse describes nothing that couldn't happen in any market on any rainy night in any century."

"The date matches."

"Codices get dates wrong."

"This one hasn't been opened since before the date was written." She tapped the margin, where a small notation sat in faded ink: a day, a month, a year. One hundred years ago. "Whoever wrote this knew. A century ago, someone sat down with this ink and described a stranger selling a copper bell to a nervous scholar in a city that wasn't even built yet in its current form, and got the details right down to the weather."

I looked at the window. The rain ticked against the glass, smug.

"People write vague prophecies and wait for the world to cooperate," I said. "It's called cold reading. Fortune-tellers do it in every port from here to the Salt Coast. You give them a coin and they tell you a tall stranger will bring you news, and when your cousin arrives next spring, suddenly it's prophecy."

"You're doing it now," Maren said. "Rationalizing. I've watched scholars do it their whole careers when a source contradicts everything they've built their lives on. Denial is just scholarship with worse posture." She turned the page. "There's more."

"Maren."

"There's *more*, Lior." First time she used my name. It landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there, warm and unwelcome, like a coal swallowed by accident. "I didn't walk across half of Vell in the rain on a coincidence. Read."

And here is where I should tell you I closed the book. Here is where the sensible version of me—the one who survived seven years by never opening doors, literal or otherwise—sets the codex aside, thanks her kindly, and goes back to sanding wooden birds until the world forgets my face again.

Instead I read.

* * *

The next pages were harder going. The script shifted—same hand, but pressed harder into the parchment, as though the writer were hurrying or angry or both. Words repeated: *seal*, *blood*, *the war under the ice*, *the price*. Some passages had been scraped away and rewritten, the parchment scarred where someone had changed their mind about eternity and needed the surface to agree.

Then a heading, in letters larger than the rest, deliberate as a signature:

*On the Wolf-Singer.*

"Charming," I said, and my voice did shake this time, only slightly, and I covered it with a cough. "Someone had a very specific wolf problem."

"Lior."

"*The child of two lines shall carry the song that binds,*" I read, because apparently I hated myself, "*and the seal made in blood shall answer to blood alone. When the Wolf-Singer sings, the war sealed beneath the ice wakes—or ends. Free or destroy. The choice is the singer's. The cost is the singer's.*"

The candle went out.

Not guttered. Went out, all at once, like a held breath released, and in the dark the smell of that metallic something rose strong enough to taste—ozone, my mind supplied, the word arriving from a locked room in my head, ozone and wet stone and the moment before lightning. Under the table, unseen, my hands had gone white-knuckled on the wood, and somewhere far below the floor, below the foundations, below the bedrock Vell was built on, something vast turned over in its sleep and sighed.

No. Nothing sighed. Cities do not sigh. Storms do not notice charm-sellers. I am a woman—I use the word loosely, the way I use most words loosely these days—who sells carved luck-knots to sailors' wives, full stop, period, end of entry.

I struck a match with hands that only trembled a little and relit the candle.

Maren was watching me. Not the book. Me. With that particular scholar's attention that weighs and measures and files away, and behind it something softer that I liked considerably less, because softness aimed at me had historically preceded funerals.

"Your hands," she said.

"Are attached to my arms. Yes."

"They were shaking during the verse."

"The room is cold."

"The room was cold when I arrived and your hands were steady then." She leaned forward. "When I touched the book earlier today, at my rooms, I felt something. A hum. Like pressing your palm to a hive wall. I assumed it was my imagination. But when you read, the candle moved *toward* it, Lior. Flames don't lean into books."

"Bad wick."

"Two candles?"

I looked. She had, apparently, lit the second one from my mantel at some point without my noticing, and both flames were now bent toward the codex like courtiers bowing to a queen. I blew one out. The other kept leaning. I put a saucer over it, which solved the light problem and accomplished nothing whatsoever for my dignity.

"Coincidence," I said.

"You keep using that word. I don't think it means what you think it means."

"I know precisely what it means. It means the world occasionally arranges itself into patterns that frightened people mistake for fate." I closed the codex. My palm lingered on the leather a beat too long—the thing underneath hummed against my skin like a struck tuning fork finding its note, and I snatched my hand back as if burned. "Take it away, Maren."

"No."

"Take it to your archivist friends. To the Compact, if they still exist. To anyone whose face isn't in it."

"Is it?" she asked, very quietly. "Your face? Is that what you saw last night, before you sent me home?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the roof like fingers drumming on a table, waiting.

Because yes. Yes, it was. Tucked into the inner margin of the Wolf-Singer page, small as a watermark, was a sketch in faded ink: a figure with high cheekbones and windblown hair and eyes rendered in careful crosshatch, and I had known those cheekbones every morning for thirty-one years in every reflection I've ever tried to avoid. The artist had drawn me a century before I was born. Or before I was *made*, whispered a voice I didn't listen to, or before whatever you actually are finished cooking—

"I didn't see anything," I said.

Maren blinked. "You just went somewhere."

"I went nowhere. I'm a charm-seller. I sell charms. They don't work. That's the entire content of my character." I stood, gathered the codex, wrapped it back in its oilcloth with brisk efficiency, and pushed it across the table to her. "Thank you for the fascinating evening of antique nonsense. Do come again when you've found something with fewer apocalypses in it."

She didn't take the book. She folded her arms instead, and I learned in that moment that Maren Ostrell's stubbornness is load-bearing.

"You're lying," she said.

"Spectacularly well, you mean."

"Why?" And there it was, the softness again, breaking through the scholar like water through a dike. "Lior. If that book says what I think it says—if you are what I think you are—then lying to me is the least of your problems. There are people looking for things like this. There have *always* been people looking for things like this. The usurper in the north has inquisitors who—"

"I know what he has."

The words came out flat and hard and true, and we both heard the truth in them, and the silence afterward was the loudest yet. Maren's arms slowly uncrossed. Her eyes widened by degrees, the way a scholar's do when a theory stops being a theory.

"You know," she breathed. "You *know* what Kaelen Voss does to—"

"Take the book, Maren."

"—to storm-callers. He burns them. He burns whole villages to find them. And you're sitting here in a city built on the one treaty that guarantees magic never crosses its walls, telling me about bad wicks—"

"*Take. The book.*"

I heard my own voice crack like a whipcrack, heard the thunder under it, one low rolling syllable that had no business being in a rented room above a chandler's shop in a city where magic has been illegal for three hundred years—and Maren went pale and took a step back, and the candle flame stood perfectly straight for the first time all night, as if listening to its master.

We stared at each other.

"Bad wick," I said.

"Thackeray—"

"*Get out.*" Quieter now. Almost gentle. Which was worse, and she knew it, and I knew it. "Please. Take the codex and go home and lose it in a river, and forget my face, and buy your bells from the tinker on Cordwainer Street like a normal person. Please. For both of us."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she picked up the book, hugged it to her chest, and walked to the door. She paused with her hand on the latch.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "whoever wrote that verse loved you. You can tell. Even in denial. Especially in denial—it reads different when you're trying not to see it."

Then she was gone, and the rain took her footsteps, and I stood alone in my little room with my little lies scattered around me like spilled beads, counting my heartbeats and telling each one it was normal.

* * *

I didn't sleep.

I sat by the dead candle and did the arithmetic of ruin, which is a skill exile teaches you whether you ask for it or not. Item: a book one hundred years old describing me in the present tense. Item: a scholar who now knew too much and was constitutionally incapable of keeping knowledge to herself—she'd publish, she'd lecture, she'd tell her Compact contacts, because that's what scholars *do*, bless her. Item: a voice in my chest that had answered when I raised it, one thunderclap of temper, after seven years of perfect silence.

Item: the watch.

I'd heard the boots an hour ago, I realized. Two pairs, maybe three, passing the corner below. Pausing. Moving on. Watch patrols pass corners constantly; it means nothing; it has always meant nothing.

Except that Vell's watch patrols in twos, and I'd counted three sets of feet, and they hadn't sounded drunk, and they'd paused *at my corner*, twice.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself so thoroughly and at such length that I almost believed it, and I was midway through believing it when the knock came—not Maren's debt-collector triple rap, but the flat, official double-knock of the Vell city watch, followed by a voice trained to sound bored while meaning business:

"Open in the name of the Charter. We've had a report."

I looked at the saucer sitting over the dead candle. At the scorch mark on the table where the flame had leaned. At the oilcloth fibers still caught under my fingernails from wrapping a book I claimed I'd never touched.

Seven years, I thought. Seven years of quiet, and it took one copper bell and one stubborn scholar and one hundred pages of my own face to bring the law to my door.

I picked up my coat, and I looked out the window at the yard behind the chandler's shop, and I began, very calmly, to plan the first lie of the rest of my life: the one where Lior Thackeray was never here at all.

Below, the knocking came again. Harder.
