## 4. Inquisitor's Lantern

I have been a sergeant of the Vell city watch for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that trouble never arrives the way the ballads say it does. It does not come at midnight with knives and masks. It comes at half past nine on a grey morning, by ferry, wearing good boots and carrying paperwork.

The paperwork was what frightened me.

Three of us stood on the customs quay to meet the packet boat from Corvane: me, young Corporal Hewett, whose mother still cut his meat for him as far as I could tell, and old Brannoc from the harbormaster's office, who had seen everything once and forgotten most of it, which he considered a mercy. The fog off the canal was doing its usual work of making Vell look like a sketch of itself, all soft edges and wet stone, and I remember thinking—later I would remember this often, the way you remember the last quiet hour before a flood—that the fog was on our side. Vell hides things well when it wants to.

The passenger who came down the gangplank was small. That was my first surprise. You expect an inquisitor to be tall; the Church of the Unclouded Eye does not seem to hire anyone under six feet, perhaps because it is hard to look down on sinners from a modest altitude. This one came up to my shoulder, a narrow woman in a traveling coat the color of wet slate, with a lantern swinging from one hand although the sun was fully up. The lantern was unlit. She carried it anyway. I would learn why later, and I wished I hadn't.

"Sergeant Odile Vane," she said. Not a question. My name arrived in her mouth already filed, cross-referenced, and annotated.

"That depends on who's asking and what they've heard."

"Inquisitor Serah Quill." She produced a seal from her coat the way other people produce knives. "Corvane Consistory, Office of the Pure Ledger. You'll want to see my warrant."

"I'd rather see your credentials twice and your warrant never," I said, "but I expect that's not on offer."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Approval, possibly. Or the memory of approval, worn smooth from disuse. "You have a reputation for thoroughness, Sergeant."

"I have a reputation for sarcasm. People confuse them."

She handed over the warrant then, and I read it, because reading it gave me time not to scream. Warrants are dull documents until they are aimed at someone you know, and then every word in them acquires teeth. *Regarding one storm-caller of the Thackeray line, presumed survivor of the Northern Rite of Inheritance, whereabouts unknown, believed concealed within the free cities.* There followed the standard language—the Unclouded Eye sees all, obstruction of a warranted inquisitor constitutes heresy, etcetera, etcetera, the Church has always had a genius for making cruelty sound like bookkeeping.

*Thackeray.* The word sat in my stomach like swallowed ice.

"Storm-callers," Brannoc said behind me, in the tone of a man discussing weather, which was either very brave or very oblivious. "Haven't seen one of those since the border closed. We're a practical city, Inquisitor. Charms, yes, but charms for luck against headaches. Nobody here calls storms."

"Nobody calls storms anywhere, Master Harbormaster. That is rather the point. They called them, once, and the north has been drowning in the bill ever since." Quill took back the warrant, folded it along creases that were already going soft from handling—she'd been carrying it a long time, or showing it to a lot of people—and looked past me at the city. Fog, rooftops, gulls arguing about something. "Where do you keep your charm-sellers?"

*Keep.* Like rats. Like lamps.

"We don't keep them anywhere," I said. "They keep themselves, mostly around Fountain Square, mostly selling nonsense to nervous brides and sailors. It's harmless."

"Harmless." She said the word the way a surgeon says *tumor*. "Sergeant, I will require lodging, access to your records, and a list of every licensed trader in charms and talismans operating within the walls. I am given to understand there are forty-one."

"There are thirty-eight," said Brannoc, helpfully, because he knew his ledgers better than her intelligence did, and I could have kissed his bald head right there on the quay. Three names she wouldn't have. Three doors that might stay shut.

"Thirty-eight, then," Quill said, without any visible disappointment. Intelligence that survives contact with local correction is more dangerous than intelligence that doesn't; I know this now. "We'll begin today."

* * *

Here is the thing about Lior Thackeray that nobody who wrote that warrant understood, because nobody who wrote it had ever bought a luck-charm off them in the rain.

Seven years ago, when I was a new-made sergeant with a chip on each shoulder, a stranger turned up in Vell with a cart of trinkets and no history. In Vell that's normal—we get runaways from everywhere, magic-free city that we are, or claim to be, the way a locked room claims to be empty. But there was something about this one. They flinched from loud noises. They watched the sky the way farmers watch it, except we weren't farmers, there was nothing up there to watch, and yet they watched it like it owed them money. And their eyes were the grey of the hour before hail.

I arrested them twice that first winter. Once for trading without a license, which was true, and once for brawling, which was false but involved defending a dockhand from three sailors, so I let it slide. By the second arrest we'd established a rapport built entirely on my telling them they were an idiot and them agreeing with suspicious enthusiasm. By spring they had a license, a stall, and a reputation for copper bells that rang cleaner than they should have—not magically clean, mind, nothing in Vell rings magically—but clean the way a voice is clean when the person using it has decided to be honest for exactly as long as honesty is survivable.

I never asked where they came from. They never asked why I didn't report the small oddities, and there were small oddities: the way rain seemed to part around their awning sometimes, or the night of the great thunderstorm two summers back when every bell in Vell went silent but theirs, which rang once, softly, like something answering a question. I wrote none of it down. A sergeant of the Vell watch writes reports; a friend keeps her mouth shut; I had decided somewhere along the way, without ceremony or admission, to be the second thing. Loyalty is rarely chosen. Mostly it accretes, like scale in a kettle, and you don't notice how thick it's grown until someone tries to scrape it off with a warrant.

Now someone had come with a warrant to scrape.

* * *

Quill requisitioned the corner room of the Salt House inn and turned it into an office by noon. By evening she had interviewed nine charm-sellers, and I accompanied her to every one, because the Consistory's rules required a watch escort and because I intended to be present for every single moment of her time in my city, on the principle that you guard the henhouse best by becoming extremely visible to foxes.

She was methodical in a way that made me nostalgic for brutality. No shouting, no threats. She asked each trader where they sourced their goods, whether they had ever handled anything that *warmed without fire*, and—this was the question, the one that made my neck prickle—whether they had ever known a colleague whose wares worked better than craft alone could explain. Nine times she asked it. Nine times she got shrugs, because charm-sellers are liars by trade and Vell's are good ones, but she wrote down every answer in a ledger with columns I couldn't read upside down, and I understood that she wasn't hunting through interviews. She was building a net. Interviews were just her measuring the water.

It was nearly dark when we reached the last stall of the day on her list, and by then I had done arithmetic I didn't want to do. Thirty-eight licensed traders. Alphabetical order. I knew the alphabet. I knew whose stall began with T.

"We'll finish tomorrow," Quill said, closing her ledger as we walked back toward the Salt House. The fog had thickened into something you could chew. Her unlit lantern swung at her side, catching no light and giving none, and I finally asked about it, mostly because I couldn't stand another minute of being the only one curious and terrified at once.

"The Eye," she said, lifting it slightly. "Unlit, it watches. Lit, it reveals. We light it only when we have found what we're looking for." She glanced at me. "You're uneasy, Sergeant."

"I'm cold and hungry. Those present similarly."

"You've been protective of your traders all day. Correcting my questions before I ask them. Warning the ninth one about my handwriting with a cough."

"He had ink on his fingers. He'd have worried."

"Mm." She stopped walking. In the fog, under the eaves of the shuttered chandlery, she looked even smaller and somehow much larger, the way certain people manage. "Let me tell you what I know about obstruction, Sergeant, and then let me tell you a thing I am not required to tell you. Obstruction: aiding a warranted fugitive is heresy under the third canon, punishable by consistory trial, and I have burned friends for less than a strategic cough. Now the second thing." She paused, and for a moment her face did something almost human, some tired animal moving behind the glass of it. "The Thackeray line is dead. Officially. The Consistory's own records say the last of them died seven years ago at the Rite of Inheritance, along with her mentor, in an accident of untrained power. If that is true, my warrant is wastepaper and I can go home. But the records also say the body was never found, and bodies that are never found have an inconvenient habit of buying copper bells in magic-free cities." She started walking again. "So you understand my problem. I am required to find a ghost. And ghosts require witnesses, Sergeant. Witnesses with local knowledge. Witnesses who have spent eleven years learning which of their neighbors flinch at thunder."

I said nothing. The fog said nothing. Somewhere across the city a temple bell marked the hour, and I thought—I could not help thinking—of another bell, silent these two days because its owner had heard the rumors of a ferry from Corvane and, being smarter than the average ghost, had kept her shutter down.

"Tomorrow," Quill said pleasantly, "we visit the remaining twenty-nine."

Twenty-nine. She'd counted the same alphabet I had.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in my rooms above the watchhouse with a bottle of plum brandy I did not open, because I wanted my hands steady tomorrow, and I thought about what loyalty actually weighs.

There is the loyalty you swear—to the watch, to the city, to law and ledger, all the scaffolding that keeps Vell from eating itself. I have honored mine for eleven years and I am proud of it in the specific, unsentimental way of a person who knows exactly how many fights she's broken up and how many of them she won fairly. And then there is the other loyalty, the unsworn kind, the kind that looks like arresting a stranger twice so you have a legitimate reason to learn their name. The kind that hears *storm-caller of the Thackeray line* and feels not fear but grief, because you know—or you guess, you've always guessed—that whatever they fled from south along that road seven years ago, they fled it bleeding.

If I warned them, I became a heretic. Not rhetorically—a matter of record, trial, rope or exile, and worse, my name attached to Quill's investigation forever as the corrupt instrument that let the wolf loose. Everything I had built, gone. And if I didn't warn them, then some morning soon a small woman with an unlit lantern would stop at the thirty-eighth stall on Fountain Square, ask a charm-seller with hail-grey eyes where she sourced her copper bells, and hold up a warrant with a dead family's name on it while her lantern did whatever unlit lanterns do.

There was a third option, obviously. There is always a third option, which is why cities have gates and gates have night hours and rivers have reeds. Run, and take the heresy with me. I turned it over. I was fifty-one years old. I owned one good coat and a houseplant that hated me. I had spent my whole life believing that law was the wall between Vell and the dark, and here was the wall asking me to choose which crack to seal and which to become.

Near dawn I laughed at myself, alone, which is the worst audience. Because the truth was simpler and stupider than all of it. I wasn't weighing duty against friendship like a woman on a scale. I was just trying to figure out how long I could keep both plates from dropping, and the answer, the answer I had known since the quay, was: not past tomorrow morning.

I put on my good coat.

* * *

Fountain Square at first light is mine in a way nowhere else ever was—the sweepers, the milk carts, the pigeons holding their morning parliament, and one charm-stall with its shutter still down, breathing frost. Lior was already awake behind it; I could see the shadow move when I knocked on the wood, twice, our old signal from the brawling days. *Watch business,* knock-knock used to mean. Today it meant the opposite, God help me.

The shutter came up a hand's width. One grey eye, the hour-before-hail eye, taking my measure.

"Odile." Their voice was sleep-rough and careful. "You're off shift."

"I'm early." I glanced up and down the square—empty, but empty isn't safe, empty is just unwitnessed—and lowered my voice to almost nothing. "Packet boat from Corvane yesterday. Inquisitor. Office of the Pure Ledger. She carries a warrant, Lior, and the warrant has your family's name in it. She's working the charm-traders' rolls alphabetically. She reaches T today."

I watched it land. Watched the eye widen, the knuckles whiten on the shutter's edge, and beneath that, worse, watched seven years of careful performance fail for half a second—not fear exactly, but recognition, the look of a person hearing a debt called in that they'd always known was coming.

"The scholar," Lior said. Quietly. To themselves as much as me. Then, sharper: "Maren Ovesen. She came to my stall twice this week with an old book, Odile, a codex, and yesterday she—" They stopped. Recalculated. Whatever the scholar had shown them, they decided I didn't need to hear it, and the fact that they could decide that, could ration truth out to me like I rationed it to Quill, stung in a place I hadn't known was exposed. "Never mind. Where is she staying? Where is the inquisitor now?"

"Salt House. She'll walk the square mid-morning with me at her elbow, hating every step of it."

"Will you?" Lior's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Or is this the part where you've already reported me, and this conversation is bait?"

"If I wanted bait I'd have used better cheese than this speech."

That got the closest thing to a real smile. Then the eye went past my shoulder, and the smile died, and I turned, and I saw what they saw: at the far corner of the square, small as a chess piece, Inquisitor Quill stood in the fog with her unlit lantern raised, watching my back the entire time. Watching. Counting. She had followed me. Of course she had followed me. I had spent the night deciding between loyalties and she had simply gotten up early.

"Lior," I said, without turning around. "Run."

But even as I said it I knew the shape of the morning, the way you know the shape of a wave before it breaks. Because Lior wasn't looking at the inquisitor anymore. They were looking past her, toward the fountain steps, where a familiar figure in a courier's coat was hurrying through the mist with a cracked leather bundle clutched to her chest and the particular desperate speed of a woman who knows she is already too late—

—and behind Maren, unhurried, lantern now lit and burning a white that had no business burning in daylight, came the small dark shape of Serah Quill, moving directly toward the scholar, the codex, and everything else that was about to come apart in my city at once.
