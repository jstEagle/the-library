## 5. Seven Years of Quiet

People ask me sometimes—well, Maren asks me, and she asks the way scholars ask, which is to say relentlessly—what it was like, growing up in the north. And I tell her about the long twilights, and the salt on the wind, and the way my mother's house smelled of cedar smoke and ink. All true. All carefully beside the point.

The truth is that I grew up inside a held breath.

Every child in Thackeray line learns early that the storm lives in them the way blood lives in them—always there, mostly quiet, and capable of ruining everything if you cut the wrong thing open. We learned our letters on slate so we wouldn't scratch lightning into paper. We learned to walk soft through dry grass because static followed us when we were upset, and upset was a word that covered most of childhood. And above all, we learned the family rule, the one carved over the door of every house that had ever carried our name:

*Never sing.*

Not a prohibition against music. My aunt sang beautifully, sea shanties and lullabies both. The rule meant the other kind of singing—the deep one, the one that called to what slept under our skin. Every gift has its key, and ours was voice. A hummed note could wake a breeze. A sung phrase could call thunder down out of a clear sky. A full song, they said—the old songs, in the old tongue—could do things I spent twenty years not learning about, which is its own kind of education.

My mother enforced the rule gently, which somehow made it heavier. She'd rest two fingers on my throat when I dreamed loudly enough to rattle the shutters, and say, *Not yet, little wolf.* As though there were a yet. As though singing were a door I'd be tall enough to reach someday, instead of a cliff edge I was being kept from walking off.

I believed her. That's the part I can't forgive myself for, later: I actually believed her, right up until the night I didn't.

* * *

The rite of inheritance happens at nineteen. Everyone knows this. It's less a secret than a season—you know it's coming the way you know winter is coming, and you prepare, and you dread it, and you pretend to look forward to it because everyone else is pretending too.

The rite works like this: your elder—the person who taught you containment, who sat up with you through fevers and fits of static, who knows your gift better than you do—sings a portion of it into themselves. Takes some of the weight. Not all of it; that would kill them. Just enough that you can carry the rest without shaking apart. In exchange, the elder grows quieter each year, their own gift dimming like a lamp running low. It's considered an honor. It's considered love.

My elder was Corvess.

I should tell you about Corvess properly, because seven years of guilt have worn his face smooth in my memory and I refuse to let it go entirely smooth. He was sixty, broad as a door, with a beard he braided into three tails and hands so steady they seemed to argue with the weather. He'd taught me containment since I was six. He was the one who found me humming in my sleep at nine and sat by my bed until dawn, humming back—counter-melodies, dampening harmonies—to keep the storm from answering. He joked constantly. He called my magic "the tenant" and asked after its health like a fussy landlord. When I turned seventeen and got angry enough at nothing to split a pine down its length, he didn't flinch; he just said, "Well. The tenant's been drinking again," and handed me the axe to chop the evidence into firewood.

He volunteered for my rite. I found out later he'd argued my mother down for it—that she had wanted to take the burden herself, and he'd told her, in front of witnesses, that a mother's gift belongs to the war and a teacher's belongs to the child. I didn't know any of that on the night. On the night I just knew that tomorrow I would stop being afraid of myself, because Corvess would be carrying part of the fear for me.

That's the cruelest joke the storm ever played. It waited until I felt safest.

* * *

The circle was stone, old stone, older than the town—laid in a ring on the headland above the sea, worn smooth by two hundred years of inheritance rites. Twelve elders stood at the points of it. My mother stood at the north, holding the ledger of names, because she was not only my mother; she was the Keeper, the one who wrote things down, the one whose handwriting I would spend years trying to forget how to read. Torches burned low. The sea breathed underneath us like something sleeping.

Corvess knelt in the center and I knelt across from him, close enough to see the grey in his braids. He'd dressed formally, which on Corvess meant he'd brushed the beard. He winked at me.

"Nervous?" he asked.

"Terrified."

"Good. Fear's just respect wearing its work clothes." He settled his big hands on my shoulders, and I felt the familiar dampening settle over me like a blanket, the counter-song he'd hummed over me for thirteen years now rising toward its fullest expression. "Ready when you are, little wolf."

I nodded. He began to sing.

I want to describe what the rite sounded like, but language fails in the specific way it always does around our gift. It sounded the way cold water sounds when you go under. It sounded like the moment before snowfall. It was a low, rolling melody in the old tongue, and each phrase lifted a measure of my storm out of me and folded it into him, and I could feel it happening—a lightening, an unclenching, the first ease I had known since childhood. Around the circle the elders joined in, weaving harmony, guiding the transfer along channels worn smooth by generations. My mother's voice rose above all of theirs, clear and controlled and certain.

And then I made my mistake. I want to be precise about this, because for years afterward I tried every arrangement of blame there was, and only one of them survives scrutiny. The elders were doing what elders do. Corvess was doing what he had chosen, freely, gladly, to do. My mother was doing her office.

The mistake was mine. Midway through the third verse, feeling lighter than I had ever felt, feeling—for the first time in my life—not afraid, I did the one thing the rule forbade.

I sang along.

It wasn't even deliberate, not exactly. The melody was in me the way my heartbeat was in me, and joy has never once consulted my judgment before leaving my body. One line. Four words of the old tongue, pitched to match Corvess, pitched to harmonize—and the storm under my skin heard its name spoken in its own key, from the inside, and woke up.

What happened next took perhaps three seconds. I've had seven years to stretch those seconds into an eternity, so believe me when I say I know them the way you know your own hands.

The first second: light. Every torch in the circle went white and died, and the world lit blue instead, blue from below, blue from within, the color of the sky before hail. The elders' song stuttered. My mother shouted something—my name, or the first syllable of a counter-spell, I'll never know which.

The second second: the storm came *out*. Not lightning the way people imagine it, a pretty fork against the dark. This was the whole weather at once, the pressure differential of my entire life discharging through the nearest conductors, which were the circle, the stones, the elders standing on them, and Corvess, whose hands were still on my shoulders, whose song was still half-finished, who had opened himself to receive a measured gift and instead caught the flood.

The third second: silence. Real silence, the kind the north almost never has. No wind. No sea-sound. Even my own pulse seemed embarrassed to be heard.

When my eyes cleared, Corvess was still kneeling. His braids had gone white—all three tails, white as birch. His eyes were open and fixed on me and they were not angry. That's the detail that ruined me. They were surprised, and sorry, and already very far away. There was frost on his beard in high summer. Where his hands had gripped my shoulders, the fabric of my shirt was scorched in two perfect handprints.

He said—I think he said—"Ah." Just that. The landlord, finding the tenant has burned the house down.

Then he fell sideways onto the old stones, gentle as a man lying down for a nap, and the storm went out of him entirely, and twelve voices started screaming at once, and none of them were singing.

* * *

They gave my mother the choice, because law in the north, even grief-stricken law, has procedures. Exile or binding. Binding meant cages of silence, wards hammered into the bone behind the ear, a life of numbness—alive, contained, useless, present. Exile meant south, past the border, into Vell and the magic-free cities where no one would ever know what I was, on pain of death if I returned.

She chose exile. She chose it before the council finished asking.

But here is what no one else saw, because they had the decency to leave us alone in the keeper's house while the elders debated what I already knew was decided. Here is what I have never told anyone, not Odile, not Maren, not the dark at three in the morning, though God knows I've rehearsed it there often enough.

Before the council, before the packing, my mother knelt in front of me in the room where I'd grown up, and took my face in her hands the way she had when I was small and feverish, and looked at me for a long time with an expression I spent years misreading as anger.

It wasn't anger. I understand that now. It was arithmetic. She was doing sums behind her eyes, terrible ones, the kind where every column ends in a price. Because she knew something the elders didn't. She knew what the rite had actually been. She knew why Corvess had fought her for the honor of it, and why she had written the ledger entries that night with a shaking hand, and why the prophecy verses she'd been drafting for months suddenly needed rewriting, because the child they were about had just proven them wrong in the worst possible way.

She knew the storm hadn't woken because I was careless. It had woken because it was already more than one rite could carry. It had been more than that for years. Maybe since birth. Maybe since before.

"Lior," she said. My whole name, no endearment. "Listen to me. Whatever happens, wherever you go—never sing. Do you hear me? Not a note. Not a hum. If the storm calls, let it starve. Promise me."

"I killed him," I said. Which was not a promise.

"You made a mistake children have died making for two hundred years, and you will grieve it until it wears a groove in you, and it will still have been a mistake. But hear me." Her thumbs pressed hard under my jaw, where the voice lives. "What woke in you tonight was not a boy's carelessness. It was the oldest thing our line carries, and it has wanted out for a very long time, and if you ever give it your voice—if you ever sing—it will take everything you are and spend it. Swear it. Swear it on Corvess."

So I swore it. On his name, on his stupid beautiful braided beard, on the firewood we'd chopped together, on the counter-melodies he'd hummed over my childhood fevers. Never sing. I swore it the way drowning people swear things—absolutely, and without understanding what they're promising.

She held me then, briefly, hard enough to hurt. Then she pulled back, and the arithmetic was gone from her face, replaced by the Keeper's composure, and she said the words that were for the record, the official last words, the ones history would keep: "Go south. Don't write. Don't return."

But the real last words were the earlier ones. Never sing. And I have come to understand, across seven years of quiet, that she wasn't cursing me. She was hiding me—in plain sight, inside a rule, inside the guilt itself, because a boy convinced his voice is a weapon will guard it with his life. She built my prison herself, brick by brick of my own shame, and locked it from the outside, and threw away the key somewhere north, and I loved her for it and hated her for it and only recently learned those can be the same act.

* * *

Seven years.

Seven years of selling charms that don't work to people who want so badly to believe that they work anyway, half the time, in the way placebos work, in the way hope works. Seven years of flinching from thunderstorms, sitting with my back to windows, counting the seconds between flash and boom like a penance. Seven years of keeping my voice level and low, never lifting it in laughter, never humming, catching myself on the rare mornings when happiness forgot the rule and rose toward a tune—clamping down, swallowing the note, tasting copper.

You'd think the punishment for killing a man would come from outside. A warrant. A noose. Something with a shape you could fight or flee. Instead I sentenced myself to something far more thorough: I became my own border guard. Every day I checked my own papers. Every day I denied myself passage back to anything resembling joy expressed at full volume. Guilt doesn't rage like the storm does. It's patient. It's a tenant too, and unlike the storm, I invited it in, gave it the best room, and let it set the terms.

Here's what I told myself for seven years, in the voice Corvess would have used, because it was the only way the discipline held: *This keeps people safe.* The market stalls and the rain and the harmless trinkets and the flat, careful, unsinging voice—all of it was a dam, and the flood behind it stayed put, and no one died, and that was the whole of the good I could do in the world. A small life. A contained life. A life spent paying down a debt that could never be paid, but could at least not grow.

I was wrong, of course. About nearly everything. The dam was holding back a river that had other plans, and the scholar with the cracked leather codex was already walking toward my stall through the Vell rain, and somewhere north a usurper was sharpening my name into a weapon, and my mother—who had written it all down in advance, who had tried to warn me in the only cipher she trusted—was seven years dead and unable to tell me that the prison she built had an exit, and that it faced north.

But that night, the night of the rite, I didn't know any of that. That night I walked south out of Thackeray lands with a pack on my back and frost-burn scars on my shoulders in the shape of a dead man's hands, and behind me the headland stood black against a sky that hadn't yet decided whether to forgive the weather, and ahead of me lay seven years of quiet.

I counted them the way you count anything you're serving. Like a sentence.

Like a debt.

Like a song I was forbidden, on pain of everything, to finish.
