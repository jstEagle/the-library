## 6. The Bell Rings Anyway

I had always assumed that if the end came for me, it would come quietly. A knock at a door I shouldn't have opened. A name spoken in a room I'd left too late. Something small and bureaucratic, the way endings tend to be in cities like Vell, where even murder is mostly paperwork.

Instead it began with a bell.

My own bell—the copper one, the little charm-bell I'd sold to Maren three days ago with a straight face and a blessing I didn't believe in. It hung from her satchel strap as she pushed through the morning market crowd toward my stall, and it rang with every step, bright and stupid and cheerful, announcing her like a herald.

*There she is,* the bell said. *There's the charm-seller. There's the liar.*

"You're difficult to find before noon," Maren said, arriving breathless. She had the codex wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, and there was something new in her face—not the scholar's hunger from before but something tighter. Fear, maybe. Or the particular alertness of someone who has been followed and knows better than to look over her shoulder.

"Most people sleep before noon," I said. "It's considered healthy."

"I don't sleep much lately." She set the codex on my counter between a tray of luck-knots and a jar of river-stones I'd blessed by holding them very hard and thinking about nothing. "Lior. We need to talk about the fourth verse."

"There is no fourth verse," I said. "You're thinking of the third verse, which we discussed, at length, and which was nonsense written by a madman a hundred years ago."

"The page you burned."

"Burned pages can't be discussed. That's rather the point of burning them."

She looked at me the way she'd looked at the codex the first night—like I was a text with a corrupted passage, something worth staying up late to decipher. "The watch was asking questions at the Gatehouse archive this morning," she said quietly. "Someone reported a stranger selling charms with storm-grey eyes. Someone reported *you*."

The market went on around us—fishwives shouting, a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking, the ordinary beautiful noise of a city that believed itself safe because it had outlawed everything worth being afraid of. Somewhere under my skin, seven years deep, the storm turned over in its sleep.

"Then this conversation is over," I said, already gathering my tray. "And you should stop visiting me. And probably leave Vell."

"Lior—"

"Maren." I kept my voice flat and pleasant, the voice of a woman selling luck. "I'm a charm-seller. My charms don't work. My eyes are just eyes. Whatever you think you found in that book, whatever you think I am—you found it wrong. Go home."

For a moment I thought she would argue. She had the argumentative face of a lifelong scholar, the kind of face that treats being told to go away as an invitation to footnote. But then the crowd behind her shifted, parted the way water parts around something moving upstream, and her face changed, and mine changed with it.

Three men in grey. Not watch-grey—inquisitor-grey, the grey of ash and cold iron, with the lantern-and-eye sigil stitched at the collar. They moved through the market without hurry, which was the worst part. Men who hurry are afraid. These men were not afraid. The tall one in front carried a long case on his back, and I knew—I knew with a sick certainty that dropped through me like a stone down a well—that it wasn't a weapon case. Weapons they carry loose. That case was for instruments. For measuring.

For *testing*.

"Stall keeper," the tall one said. He stopped two paces away, close enough that I could see his badge: a silver eye over a lantern, and beneath it, stamped into the metal, the seal of the northern throne. Of Kaelen Voss. Warrants travel fast when a usurper wants something badly enough. "We're conducting a census of unlicensed practitioners. Routine."

"Vell has no practitioners," I said. "That's rather our civic pride."

"All the same." He produced a folded paper, opened it with two fingers, and held it out—not to me. To Maren. "You were seen entering the Gatehouse archive requesting restricted materials. Prophetic texts. You'll accompany us and account for your reading habits."

"She's a scholar," I said. "Scholars read things. That's the entire profession."

He turned to me then, and I watched him do what inquisitors do: look without appearing to look. My hands. My eyes. The little copper bells and knot-charms spread across my counter like evidence of nothing. "Name?"

"Lira Fen. Charm-seller, fourteen years in Vell." Fourteen years was a lie with good bones; seven years was the truth, and lies built on truths hold weight best.

"Lira Fen." He tasted the name like wine he suspected of being vinegar. And then, mildly, terribly: "Your papers say otherwise."

The world did not narrow or slow, the way stories claim it does. The world stayed exactly as wide and exactly as fast as it had been, and that was the horror of it—that catastrophe arrives at market speed, amid fishwives and knife-grinders, while you are still holding a tray of worthless stones.

Maren moved first. She never got far. The second inquisitor caught her satchel strap and pulled, and the copper bell rang—one clear, bright, cheerful note—and something in me broke along a line I hadn't known was drawn.

Later—much later, in tunnels, in exile, in the years after—I would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. Here is what I know happened, or what I've decided happened, because memory is also a charm we sell ourselves:

He wrenched the satchel. The bell rang. And the storm under my skin woke up all the way.

* * *

People ask survivors of thunderclaps what it sounded like, and they always say the wrong thing. They say *loud*. Loud is nothing. Loud is what thunder is to people who weren't standing inside it.

What it actually was—what came out of me—was a single wordless syllable, the sound the sky makes when it finally says what it's been trying to say for a hundred years. It went through the market square not as noise but as *fact*. The fishwives' awnings flattened. Every window on Tanner's Row burst outward in the same instant, a glittering exhalation of glass. The fountain cracked from crown to base. Three carts overturned. And the tall inquisitor, who had been reaching into his coat for irons or worse, was simply no longer standing; he was ten feet away, on the ground, alive—I could tell he was alive, some animal part of me checked, some part of me that will always check—breathing wetly against a shattered cartwheel.

Silence after. The specific silence of a hundred people forgetting how lungs work.

And me, standing in the wreckage with my arms still half-raised, sparks crawling up my forearms like luminous worms, blue-white light bleeding out through my sleeves, my hair lifted off my neck by static, feeling—for the first time in seven years—*full*.

That was the worst part. Not the fear. The relief. Seven years of swallowing lightning, of performing powerlessness, of blessing river-stones with clenched teeth and a locked jaw, and the storm had gotten out, and gods forgive me, it felt like exhaling.

"No," I said aloud, to myself, to the light coming off my own skin. "*No, no, no.*"

Maren was staring at me from where she'd fallen, satchel strap torn, the codex clutched to her chest like a child. Her mouth was open. Whatever she'd believed about me before—and she had believed plenty, she'd read it in a century-old book and walked it straight to my stall—it was nothing next to the sight of the proof crackling in the air.

"Lior," she whispered. My real name, the one I'd never given her. The book had given it to her instead.

"Don't," I said. "Don't say it like that."

"Like what?"

"Like it's true."

The other two inquisitors had recovered enough to be afraid, which meant they'd recovered enough to be dangerous. One was fumbling at the long case on the tall man's back. The other had drawn something short and black from his belt—annuller's rod, iron-cored, the tool they use to burn the gift out of a storm-caller's blood, slowly, and sometimes out of the rest of them too.

I did the only reasonable thing available to a woman standing in a crater of her own making.

I grabbed Maren's wrist and ran.

* * *

Vell is a city built on top of an older city, which is built on top of a city older than that, and so on down into the dark like a bad conscience. The undercity—smugglers call it the Undercroft, the watch calls it a problem, I called it home for exactly one night, once, seven years ago, when I first arrived with burned hands and a dead mentor's name in my mouth—opens through a dozen mouths if you know where to look. I knew three. I hoped the inquisitors knew fewer.

We went through the tannery yard, past the lime pits, down a stair that smelled of old fat and older rain, Maren stumbling behind me with the bell on her ruined satchel still ringing, ringing, ringing, because of course it was, because the universe has a sense of humor and it's mine.

"Stop—" she gasped. "Stop, wait—"

"We can't wait."

"*Stop.*" She planted her feet at the bottom of the stair, in a corridor lit by seams of daylight through broken grates above, and turned to face me fully for the first time since the square. Sparks were still dying in my hair. I could feel them, little stars going out one by one. "You're the Wolf-Singer," she said. "The verse. *A stranger with storm-grey eyes shall sell a copper bell, and the bell shall ring before the wolf sings.* Lior, it rang. It *rang*, and then you—"

"That's coincidence."

"A street fell down."

"Structural failure."

"You *threw* a thunderclap!"

"I didn't throw anything!" The words tore out of me louder than I intended, echoing down the black throat of the corridor, and I made myself smaller, made my voice small to match. "I didn't *do* anything, Maren. That's the whole point. I have spent seven years not doing anything. Seven years holding my breath. And that—" I gestured back the way we'd come, at the distant sound of alarm bells and shouting, at the dust still settling on a city I'd wounded, "—that is what happens when I slip. One slip. One bell. That's why I left. That's why I *never sing*."

The last words came out before I could stop them, and I heard how they sounded—how they sounded like scripture, like something learned young and repeated until it wore a groove. Maren heard it too. Scholars hear grooves.

"Who told you that?" she asked softly. "Never sing."

"My mother," I said, and then hated myself for saying it, and turned, and started walking into the dark, because walking away from conversations is the one magic I've always been good at.

She caught up. Of course she caught up. The bell rang with every step, and somewhere above us the city I had lived in invisibly for seven years was learning my name, and somewhere north of it a usurper was about to learn it too, and none of it mattered yet, none of it was real yet, because we were underground and the dark was honest and for one more hour I could pretend I was still just a charm-seller whose charms had never worked.

Except now everyone knew they had.

"They'll seal the gates," Maren said as we walked, practical already, the courier waking up underneath the scholar. "They'll put your description on every post. Storm-grey eyes. You realize that's the most useless description ever invented and also the most damning? Everyone will be checking eyes now."

"Then I'll keep them down."

"You can't keep your eyes down for the rest of your life."

"I kept everything else down for seven years."

Ahead, the corridor forked, and from the right-hand branch came a sound that stopped us both: voices. Low, unhurried, multiple. Lantern-light swaying on wet stone.

Not inquisitors. Inquisitors don't whisper.

From the shadow of the fork stepped a figure with a smuggler's stoop and a courier's eyes, and behind her, others—a half-dozen shapes in the dark, armed, waiting, unmistakably *expecting* us. Expecting *me*.

The figure smiled.

"Storm-grey eyes," she said. "Copper bell. You're easier to find than the book promised."

Maren's hand found mine in the dark and squeezed—warning, apology, I couldn't tell which. The bell rang once more, soft and bright and terrible, and the woman in the tunnel gestured us forward, deeper into the Undercroft, away from the burning city above.

"You have about four minutes," she said, "before they find these stairs too. So let's talk about what the Compact can do for you—and what you're going to do for it."
