## 7. Beneath Vell

I have been in a lot of bad places in my line of work. A grain silo full of rats in Corvane. A wedding where both families were smuggling the same contraband through the cake. Once, for six hours, a confessional in Harrowgate while an actual priest sat on the other side of the screen and I fed him lies about my sins until he wept.

The tunnels under Vell were worse than all of them, and I say that with love.

They smelled of river rot and old smoke and something mineral and cold, like the city's bones had been left out in the rain. The storm-caller walked ahead of me with a stolen lantern held at arm's length, as if light were a thing that might turn on its owner, and every few steps they'd stop and listen — actually listen, head tilted, eyes half-shut — to water dripping somewhere in the dark.

"You hear something?" I asked.

"No," Lior said.

"Then why—"

"Because when I *do* hear something, I'd like to know what it isn't."

Which was either wisdom or madness, and having spent three days in this person's company, I had learned those two things wore the same coat in Lior Thackeray's wardrobe.

* * *

Let me back up. Let me be honest, since honesty is about to become a scarce commodity in this story and I may as well spend mine early.

My name is Maren Solle. I am not a scholar. Well — I *am* a scholar, in the way that a knife is technically a tool for spreading butter. For eleven years I have carried letters, ledgers, ciphers, and occasionally people for the Free Cities Compact: that loose, quarrelsome, underfunded league of cities that Kaelen Voss has spent four years eating one bite at a time while the rest of us argued about tariffs.

And for eleven years, one item has sat at the top of every courier's list, circled twice, marked with the seal that means *drop everything else*: the Codex of the Sealed War. A book of prophecies a century old, written by an unknown hand, last seen in the private collection of a magistrate who died owing money to worse people than me.

The Compact didn't want it because it was old. The Compact wanted it because of what was rumored to be in it — a name, a title, a person. And because our spymasters are the sort of people who believe that if destiny hands you a weapon, refusing to pick it up is just poor manners.

So when a nervous, genuine, genuinely nervous academic came to me eight months ago and said she'd found the Codex in a flooded cellar in Vell — magic-free, watchful, terrible Vell — I did what any reasonable operative would do.

I moved into her boarding house, complained convincingly about the porridge, and waited.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hunting a prophecy: you start believing it. That's the occupational hazard. You read the verses enough times in enough safehouses by enough guttering lamps and the words get into your blood like fever. *A stranger with storm-grey eyes shall sell a copper bell.* I had memorized that line before I ever met Lior. I used to recite it to myself on bad days the way other couriers recited their escape routes.

Then one gray afternoon a charm-seller with storm-grey eyes sold Maren-the-scholar a copper bell, haggled her down from six pennies to four, and apologized for the dent in it, and I stood there in the rain holding the bell feeling like the universe had just reached out of the page and flicked me on the nose.

Four pennies. Destiny, negotiated down from six. I have never fully recovered from this.

* * *

The tunnel opened into a low brick chamber where an underground channel ran black and slow. Somebody had built a smuggler's cache here once — rusted hooks in the walls, a rotten crate, the ghost of candle soot on the ceiling. Lior set the lantern on the crate and finally turned around, and I got the full force of that look again: the look of a person doing arithmetic with their own survival and finding the sums don't come out.

"Start talking," Lior said. "You knew which tunnels. You knew the watch patrol schedule better than the watch. You looked at that thunderclap like—" They stopped. Recalibrated. "Like someone taking notes."

"I take excellent notes."

"Maren."

"Lior." I sat down on the crate next to the lantern, because standing over people makes them defensive and I needed them listening, not defending. "You're right. I'm not who I said I was. So before you decide whether to run — and you should know running is genuinely on the table, I won't insult you by pretending otherwise — let me tell you who I actually am, and then you can decide what to do with me."

Their jaw tightened. "If you're Voss's—"

"If I worked for Voss, would I have pulled you out of a burning market street? Think about the economics. You're worth infinitely more to him captured."

"That is not comforting."

"It's not meant to comfort you. It's meant to make you think, which I understand is painful right now." I pulled the false bottom off my satchel — the real satchel, the one I'd grabbed from the boarding house while the upper city was still raining ash — and took out the compact seal: a lead disc stamped with five interlocked towers. "Free Cities Compact. Courier, third rank. My name is Maren Solle, and for the past year my assignment has been a book."

"The codex."

"You knew?"

"I know you touched it like it was alive." Lior lowered themselves onto an upturned bucket, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them — making themselves small, which was a strange thing to watch a person do when the air around them still crackled faintly, like the memory of lightning caught in wool. "The night you showed it to me. You held it the way people hold things they're afraid to want."

That stung more than it should have, mostly because it was true. I covered it with briskness, which is my second-best trick after the porridge complaints.

"The Compact has hunted that book for thirty years. Every courier, every agent, every drunk informant in nine cities has had one standing order: find the Codex of the Sealed War. Because of the verse. You've read it."

"*The Wolf-Singer comes at the war's unmaking,*" Lior recited flatly. "I burned the page."

"Burning a page doesn't burn the verse. There are copies. Incomplete ones, contested ones, but copies. And every faction from here to the Salt Reach has spent a century arguing about what the Wolf-Singer does — frees the north or destroys it — and every one of them wants to be holding the leash when it happens." I made myself say the next part plainly, no varnish. "Including mine."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere far above us, muffled through forty feet of brick and earth, Vell was screaming and bucket-chains were forming and someone was probably already being arrested for the crime of standing near a thunderclap.

"So that's it," Lior said at last. Their voice was very quiet, which I was learning was worse than loud. "The nervous scholar. The porridge complaints. The four pennies." A short, terrible laugh. "I thought I'd finally made a friend who didn't want anything from me. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what I paid for that? Seven years of—"

"I know."

"—of *nothing*, of being small and careful and *boring*, and it turns out even that was a job interview—"

"I know," I said again, and this time I didn't dress it up. "And I'm sorry. Not sorry that I did it — I'd do it again, that's the ugly truth of my work and you deserve to hear it — but sorry that it was you. Sorry that you're kind. If you'd been a monster this would all be much simpler."

Lior stared at me across the lantern light. Rain-water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock. And I watched them do the thing I had already begun to recognize as their particular genius: they took the fury, and the fear, and the grief, and folded all of it down into something manageable, the way you'd fold a letter too big for its envelope, creasing hard, knowing it would never lie flat again.

"What does the Compact want?" they asked.

"Sanctuary first. Harrowgate, if we can reach it. Walls, food, people who can teach you what your mother's line apparently—" I stopped, because their face changed. "What?"

"My mother." The word came out strange, like a coin from a foreign country. "Why would you say my mother?"

Because I'd read the marginalia, is why. Because the archivists in Harrowgate had spent decades on the Codex's cipher and gotten nowhere, but the fragmentary copies all shared one oddity: verses addressed, grammatically, to a single reader. *When YOU sell the bell. When YOU hear the wolf.* A prophecy shaped like a letter. And letters, in my experience, have authors, and authors have reasons, and none of the Compact's analysts had ever followed that thread to ground.

But that wasn't the conversation for a flooded tunnel under a burning city. That conversation required maps and tea and at least one locked door.

"The Compact believes the Wolf-Singer can end the war Voss is building," I said instead. "He has storm-priests in chains, Lior. Wagons of them. He uses them as artillery — points them at a wall and lets them scream until it falls. The free cities have nothing that can stand against that except the thing the prophecies talk about. So yes. They want you."

"As a weapon."

The question hung there. And here is where I could have lied — where a better operative, or a worse person, would have said *no, of course not, as a person, as a guest* — and I want it recorded, wherever such things get recorded, that I considered it for a full three seconds and chose the truth, because I had watched this person haggle over a dented bell and apologize to a frightened academic and burn a page rather than let it be used, and some part of me had already decided that lying to Lior Thackeray was a thing I would regret.

"Yes," I said. "As a weapon, initially. As a symbol immediately after. Possibly as a martyr eventually, depending on how the councils vote, and the councils are cowards with good handwriting." I leaned forward. "But here's what the councils haven't figured out, because they think in leashes and I've spent a year watching you buy vegetables. Weapons don't get to choose. And everything I've seen says choosing is the only thing you've ever wanted. So the Compact will offer you sanctuary and try to make it a cage, and I am offering you the same sanctuary with the lock on the *inside*. Come with me. Get behind walls. Learn what's in that book — learn who wrote it, because I promise you, whoever wrote it wrote it *to* someone, and I think that someone is you. And then decide. Not tonight. Not in a tunnel that smells like a drowned rat's funeral. Decide later, with your whole mind, and if you decide the Compact can go hang — I'll walk you out myself and lose my rank doing it."

Lior was silent for a long time.

"You'd do that," they said finally. It wasn't a question exactly. More like a test they already knew the answer to and were checking me against it.

"I've done worse for less reason."

"And if I'm wrong? If I'm what the verse says — the destroying version?" Their hands had gone white around their knees. "Maren, you were in that market. You saw what happened when I *panicked*. I didn't choose that. I have never once chosen it. There's a storm living under my skin that got loose seven years ago and killed—" They shut their mouth. Turned away. "It killed someone. And everyone who gets close to me ends up standing in the blast radius, and now the Compact wants to aim me at a war."

"Yes."

"You're supposed to argue with me."

"I'm supposed to recruit you. Arguing with you appears to be optional." I pulled my coat tighter against the damp creeping up from the channel. "Look. I can't promise you're safe. Nobody can; anyone who promises safety is selling something. What I can promise is that Voss's inquisitors are going to pull Vell apart stone by stone looking for you, and the watch already took your description, and by morning every gate will have a picture of your face that looks only slightly less handsome than the original. Staying is dying slowly. Coming with me is dying *possibly*, later, in more interesting company. Those are the options. I hate them too."

Something shifted in their face — not surrender, nothing so clean as that. More like a door opening a crack in a house that had been sealed for seven years, letting in a draft that smelled of elsewhere.

"Harrowgate," Lior said. "How far?"

"Nine days north, if the roads hold. Fewer if we use my contacts. Longer if the roads don't hold, which, given that you recently leveled a street, I'd budget for."

"And the codex?"

"Is currently wrapped in oilcloth inside my shirt, against my ribs, where it has been since the market." I patted it. "It and I have grown close. It digs in when I breathe."

That earned me almost — *almost* — a smile. The first one. It lasted about a second and a half and I found myself absurdly pleased, in a professional capacity, obviously, to have caused it.

Then the smile died, because Lior went rigid, head tilting toward the dark tunnel mouth, listening the way they listened — and this time they heard whatever it was they'd been teaching themselves to hear.

"Lanterns," they whispered. "Two. Behind us. Moving slow."

I was on my feet with my hand on my knife before my brain caught up. "Watch doesn't come down here. Watch is afraid of the dark."

"Then it's not the watch."

We killed the lantern. In the sudden black I heard Lior breathing beside me — controlled, deliberate, terrified — and felt the air change pressure the way it had in the market a heartbeat before the world broke open. They were gathering the storm again. Bracing to fight.

And far off in the tunnels, a voice called out — not the inquisitor's bark, not a watchman's challenge. Something older, rougher, worn smooth by repetition, like a litany:

"Wolf-Singer. We know you can hear us. We're not Voss's. We're the ones who've been waiting for you since before you were born — and we have your mother's letters."

Beside me in the dark, Lior Thackeray stopped breathing altogether.
