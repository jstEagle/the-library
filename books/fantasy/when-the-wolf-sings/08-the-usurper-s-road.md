## 8. The Usurper's Road

There is a particular smell to an army on the march that no chronicler ever gets right. They write of iron and horses, of woodsmoke and leather. What they never mention is the fear. Fear has a scent—sour, animal, faintly sweet at the edges, like fruit going over—and thirty thousand men marinating in it for a hundred miles produces something you could bottle and sell to cowards as perfume.

I had been breathing it for eleven days.

"Your Excellency." Captain Rennick fell into step beside my horse, which was the only courtesy he still extended to me—that he addressed the horse's general direction rather than my face directly. "The priests are ready for inspection."

"The priests," I said, "are not ready for anything. That is rather the point of them."

He didn't laugh. None of them laughed anymore. I had noticed it somewhere around the second burned village: the jokes stopped, the way birds stop singing before a storm. My officers had begun looking at me the way men look at weather they cannot argue with.

I dismounted anyway, because a king who will not walk among his soldiers is a king who will soon be walking nowhere at all, and we picked our way down the column to the wagons.

* * *

The storm-priests traveled in iron-barred carts padded with wool, because dead weapons are worthless weapons, and I had lost four of them already to what their handlers called "the wasting"—the slow unraveling that comes when you chain a man to his own gift and ask him to fire it on command. Each cart held two priests, wrists bound in salt-iron, eyes bandaged. The bandages were my idea. A man who can see the sky finds ways to talk to it.

"Open the third one," I said.

Rennick hesitated. He always hesitated now. It was becoming his signature move, like a duelist who leads with a flinch. "Excellency, the handler says this one bit through a glove yesterday."

"Then give him your other hand and open the cart."

Inside, hunched against the wool like a moth in a matchbox, sat Brother Aldous—or what had been Brother Aldous when I'd taken him from the priory at Cael Marsh six weeks ago. Storm-priests were supposed to be serene. That was the legend, anyway: men and women so full of lightning they made peace with it, the way a river makes peace with its banks. The legends neglected to mention what happened when you took away the banks.

Aldous lifted his bandaged face toward me. He couldn't have seen anything. He knew where I stood regardless. They always did.

"You're using us," he said. His voice had gone hoarse from screaming during drills. "You're pointing us at cities like a finger."

"I am pointing you at *one* city," I corrected. "And then I am going to unbind every chain in this army and send you home with pensions and pardons, and history will record that Kaelen Voss freed the storm-priests while the free cities did nothing but write letters about it."

"That isn't what history will record."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "History will record whatever the winner can afford. Which is why I intend to be able to afford a great deal."

I signaled the handler to close the cart. As the door swung shut, Aldous said—not shouting, just saying, which was somehow worse—"She'll come for us. The Wolf-Singer. The old verses say—"

I put my hand flat against the iron bar, once, like a benediction or a threat; even I wasn't sure which. "The old verses say a great many things, brother. Most of them were written by frightened people trying to make the future feel less like an ambush. Sleep well."

I did not sleep well myself, but that is a king's privilege: to prescribe rest for everyone else.

* * *

That evening, in the command tent, I read dispatches by lamplight while my war council pretended I wasn't ignoring them.

The free cities were doing what free cities do best, which is nothing, beautifully. Harrowgate had voted—voted!—to form a defensive committee. Greyford had voted to send Harrowgate a strongly worded letter of support. Vell, jewel of the coast, had voted that the matter required further study. I could have conquered the entire Compact with a sufficiently persuasive committee of my own, except that committees lack armies, and I had spent seven years making sure mine was the only one left standing between the mountains and the sea.

Seven years. It sounded, said aloud, like ambition. Written down by my enemies—and there were more of those every season, each one certain that *he* would have done it differently—it sounded like tyranny. Here is what it actually was:

When the old king died, the north had three choices. Civil war between his squabbling heirs, which would have fed ten thousand villages into a grinder for a crown none of them deserved. Invasion from the mountain clans, who were already testing the passes with raiding parties the size of small congregations. Or me.

I had been nobody. That was my qualification. A border lord's bastard with a soldier's trade and no claim anyone needed to kill over—which meant, uniquely, that killing me bought no one anything. I took the capital in a week because half the garrison opened the gates just to end the argument. I held it because I was the only man in the north who understood that the argument was never actually about the throne. It was about the storms.

You cannot govern a country where the sky kills your harvests on a schedule known only to itself. The old kings had managed it by keeping the storm-priests fat and loyal and pointed inward, a holy artillery corps dressed up in liturgy. When the priories fractured—when the priests started asking inconvenient questions about whether their gifts belonged to the crown or to the sky—the whole arrangement came apart, and the storms came apart with it, and the northern coast learned what a hurricane does to a harbor town that has grown sentimental about independence.

So yes. I chained the priests. I marched them south. I aimed them at cities that had spent a century getting rich on trade while the north drowned, and I told myself—and I mostly believed—that necessity wears whatever face is available, and this season it happened to be wearing mine.

There was a version of this story where I was the villain. I had read enough chronicles to know how villains were made: retroactively, by people with better harvests.

"Excellency." Rennick again, holding a letter sealed in black wax. "From the Marshal of Inquisitors. Priority seal."

My council went quiet in the way men go quiet when they hope silence will make them invisible. I broke the seal.

The Marshal wrote well, which I resented. Four paragraphs of obsequious preamble, and then the meat: a charm-seller in Vell, identified by a scholar's purchase of a century-old codex, matching the description circulated after the market incident—a thunderclap out of a clear sky, a street flattened, no storm on any horizon. The Thackeray line. The Wolf-Singer of the verses. Alive, and hiding in the one city too proud to believe in prophecy.

I set the letter down and looked at my hands for a moment. Steady. Good. A man should be permitted at least one steady thing.

"Out," I said to the council. "All of you. Rennick stays."

They filed out with the relief of schoolboys dismissed early. Rennick remained, wearing the expression of a man who wishes he hadn't stayed.

"Do you know what the verses actually say?" I asked him. "About the Wolf-Singer?"

"Something about freeing or destroying the north, Excellency. The chaplains argue about the translation."

"The chaplains argue about everything. It keeps them warm." I turned the Marshal's letter over. "Here is what the verses say, Rennick, in every translation, in every hand that copied them down across a hundred years: a child of the storm-line will come at the hour of greatest need, and the storm will answer them, and the north will be remade. Free or destroy. Those are the two verbs. Notably absent: *govern*. Nobody prophesied a competent administrator. Nobody sang verses about grain tariffs and garrison rotations and the forty dull decisions a day that keep a country alive. The sky gets a singer, and the singers get a legend, and men like you and me get to find out whether legends can be taxed."

Rennick said carefully, "You want the Wolf-Singer found."

"I want the Wolf-Singer found *first*." I stood and walked to the map table, where the free cities sat in their smug little cluster along the coast like pearls someone had strung carelessly. "Think, Captain. If Voss marches on Harrowgate and burns it, I am a tyrant, and every farm boy from here to the sea learns to hate my name, and I spend twenty years putting down rebellions. But if the Wolf-Singer stands beside me—if the storm itself chooses Kaelen Voss—then I am not a usurper. I am the fulfillment. The priests stop being prisoners and become witnesses. The free cities don't fall; they *submit*, gladly, gratefully, to the destiny their own grandmothers prayed for."

"And if the Wolf-Singer refuses?"

"Then I have a problem." I tapped Vell on the map, once. "Because a refused prophecy doesn't die, Rennick. It goes underground. It becomes a flag. And somewhere in the mountains or the marshes, some idealist starts collecting the disappointed, and in fifteen years I'm fighting not an army but an *idea*, and ideas are expensive to besiege."

"So we offer them everything."

"We offer them everything," I agreed, "and if they're wise, they take it, and if they're wise they'll also understand that everything includes a very comfortable cage. A singer belongs to the song. The song belongs to whoever holds the sheet music."

Rennick studied me with that new look of his—the weather-look. "And if they can't be caged? The verses say free *or destroy*, Excellency. What if destroying is the verb they pick?"

I smiled at him. I have been told my smile is reassuring, which is the single most useful lie my face has ever told.

"Then I will need the priests," I said, "every last one of them, and I will need them angry. Fortunately, Captain, anger is the one sermon I know how to deliver."

* * *

I walked the camp that night, alone, which my guards hated and permitted anyway, because a ruler who fears his own soldiers has already lost them and simply hasn't received the news.

Fires everywhere, ten thousand small orange arguments against the dark. Men gambling, singing badly, writing letters home that their wives would read twice and their sons would read differently. Somewhere a priest-cart groaned under the weight of its chains, and somewhere inside it a man who had once spoken gently to thunder lay listening to my footsteps pass.

I stopped by one of the fires. A young soldier—barely old enough to shave, holding his boots like a doll—scrambled up and saluted so fast he nearly fell over.

"At ease. Where are you from?"

"Cael Marsh, Excellency." His face did something complicated. Cael Marsh was where I'd taken the priory. Where Aldous had lived, before.

"Good country," I said. "Marsh grass makes the sweetest mutton in the north. Tell your mother I said the north remembers her."

He glowed. They all glowed. That was the terrible arithmetic of it: every cruelty I committed purchased a kindness I could distribute later, and the kindnesses were remembered and the cruelties were filed under necessity, and the ledger balanced, and the road grew longer.

I walked on. Behind me the camp murmured like a tide. Ahead, south, the coast waited—Vell first, then Greyford, then Harrowgate with its committee and its walls and its vote. I would give them all a chance to open their gates. I was generous that way; generosity costs nothing until it fails.

And somewhere in Vell, in a rain-soaked market street, a person with storm-grey eyes was selling copper bells to people who couldn't hear what rang underneath.

Find them first. Before the Compact made them a weapon. Before the verses made them a flag. Before the sky, in its infinite indifference, decided that Kaelen Voss was merely another bank for the river to break.

I had come too far, burned too much, chained too many, to be a footnote in someone else's prophecy.

At dawn I gave the order.

"South," I said. "Vell. And Captain—send word ahead to the Marshal. Tell him the Wolf-Singer is not to be harmed."

Rennick blinked. "Not harmed, Excellency?"

"Not permanently," I amended, and gave the column the order to march.
