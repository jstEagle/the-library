## 9. Salt and Ashes

There is a particular smell to a burned village that no amount of travel prepares you for. People imagine smoke—woodsmoke, hearthsmoke, something almost cozy. What you actually get is salt and ash and the sweetish rot underneath, the smell of things that were stored for winter and will now feed no one. Grain gone black in its granary. Pickles boiled in their crocks. A pig, mercifully already dead before the fire took it, roasting in its pen like some grim joke about providence.

Three days north of Harrowgate, I had learned to smell Voss's army before I ever saw it.

"You're doing the face again," Maren said.

I didn't turn around. "What face?"

"The one where you pretend you're fine and your jaw could crack walnuts."

"I don't have a walnut-cracking jaw."

"You absolutely do. It's one of your better features, right after the storm-grey eyes that are apparently prophesied." She came up alongside me on the rutted road, leading her mule with the easy indifference of someone who'd been doing it since childhood. "The face is new, though. You've worn guilty, you've worn terrified, you've worn that insufferable 'I'm just a humble charm-seller' look that fooled exactly nobody in Vell. This is something else."

"This," I said, "is the face of a person who asked to see the road and is regretting it."

She let that sit for a while. Maren was good at letting things sit; it was one of the ways she was dangerous. Most people fill silence like water fills a cracked cup, pouring themselves into every gap until you know everything about them. Maren just waited for the silence to do its work on you instead.

The village was called Brant's Hollow. I knew because of the sign at the crossroads, half-charred, which read BRANT'S HOLL — POP. 214 — KINDLY SLOW YR HORSES. The population figure had been repainted recently enough that the paint had blistered but not burned. Someone had been proud of their arithmetic.

Two hundred and fourteen people. Maybe forty remained, huddled in the stone chapel that had survived because there was nothing in it worth burning except faith, and faith doesn't catch.

"Voss's rearguard came through eleven days ago," said the man who met us at the chapel door. He was old, bent like a question mark, with a voice sanded down to gravel. "Foragers, he called them. They took what they wanted and fired what they couldn't carry. Said it was strategy. Denying provisions to the enemy." He looked at our small party—me, Maren, two Compact riders who hadn't spoken a word since Harrowgate—and his eyes did the arithmetic of strangers. "You from the Compact?"

"Courier business," Maren said smoothly. She'd gotten very smooth since the tunnels under Vell. Sometimes I missed the nervous scholar who'd shown me my own face in a century-old book. That woman had been easier to trust, if only because her hands shook when she lied.

The old man nodded slowly, then, as we turned to go: "If any of you have a healer among you. We'd be obliged. There's a girl."

And that—that—was how the day stopped being survivable and became something else.

* * *

Her name was Tess, though her mother called her Tessa in the way mothers do when they want a small name to hold more than it can. She was maybe seven years old. She lay on a pallet of straw behind the chapel altar, and she was dying of something so ordinary it felt obscene: a fever, following a burn along her left arm from wrist nearly to shoulder. The wound had gone bad. The flesh around it was angry red streaking up toward her elbow, and the smell was the same sweetish rot as the granaries.

"She got caught in the fire," the old man said. "Her ma pulled her out. Ma's fevered too, but she'll live. Girl won't. Not without—" He shrugged, the gesture of a man who had run out of gods to appeal to. "Not without anything."

I stood in the doorway and I did not move.

Here is the thing about suppressing magic for seven years. Everyone assumes the hard part is the power itself—the storm humming under your skin, wanting out, testing the seams of you like weather against a window. And yes, that part is hard. But the truly hard part, the part no ballad ever gets right, is that suppression becomes a habit so deep it stops feeling like restraint and starts feeling like character. You become the thing that does not act. It calcifies. You build an entire self out of closed doors, and after long enough, you can't remember which doors you locked and which ones bricked over.

Seven years ago I had sung one verse too many and killed the man who taught me how to sing at all. My mentor, Aldous, dead on the stones of the inheritance-rite, struck down by the very gift he'd been handing me. *Never sing,* my mother had said, pressing me toward the southern road with tears freezing in her lashes. *Promise me. Never.*

So I promised. And I kept the promise through exile and disguise and a thousand market days of selling copper bells that rang prettily and did nothing else, and I told myself the keeping of it made me good, or at least harmless, and harmless was close enough to good for a life.

Now a seven-year-old girl was dying twenty feet away from me, and I was standing in a doorway calculating whether harmlessness still counted as virtue.

Maren touched my elbow. "Lior."

"I'm not a healer," I said.

"No. But you're something."

"That's not the same thing. That has never once been the same thing." I heard my own voice climb and forced it back down. "You've seen what happens when I open even a crack. Vell. The street. Fourteen buildings."

"And you've spent three weeks telling yourself that was the worst thing that could happen," Maren said quietly. "Look at her, Lior. Look at what actually happens when you keep it shut."

I looked.

Tessa's breath was coming shallow and fast, the little ribs working like a bellows with a torn seam. Her mother sat beside her, holding the unburned hand, murmuring something—not a prayer, I realized, but a counting song. One-two-three-four-five, over and over, the kind of song you invent at three in the morning when the real songs are all wrong. Once, between four and five, the girl's eyes opened a sliver, found her mother's face, and closed again.

Somewhere under my sternum, behind seven years of brick and lock, the storm stirred. Not violently. Worse than violently—gently. Like a dog hearing its name after years of being called something else.

"It might kill her," I said. "If I do this wrong. If I lose the thread. Healing isn't gentle, Maren. Storm-light burns as easily as it mends. Ask Aldous." My voice cracked on his name, which surprised us both. "Ask anyone I've ever loved."

"I can't ask them. You're the only one left who can." She turned me by the shoulder, gently, the way you handle something that might detonate. "I watched you in Vell. When it erupted. Uncontrolled, terrified, mid-panic—and even then, even then, the lightning went around people. Around *her.*" She nodded at Tessa's mother. "You think that was chance? Seven years of clamping down, and your first uncontrolled strike still curved itself away from a stranger? Lior. The storm isn't fighting you. It never was. You're the one fighting."

"That's a lovely theory and I'd love to test it somewhere without stakes."

"There are always stakes. That's the whole point of being alive." She squeezed my shoulder. "I'm a courier. I deal in messages that matter. So here's one, free of charge: whatever you decide, decide fast. The fever's moving."

* * *

I knelt by the pallet.

Tessa's mother looked up at me with red-rimmed eyes, and I saw her take in my travel-stained coat, my charm-seller's pack, the copper bell hanging off the strap—the one bell I'd never sold, the one from the prophecy page I'd burned—and something in her face said she didn't care what I was. Hope makes people terribly undiscerning. I have exploited that fact professionally for most of my adult life, and here it was, turned around on me, a mirror held up at the worst possible angle.

"Please," she said. Just that. Just please, as if I were a saint on a card.

"Don't thank me yet," I said. "In fact, if this goes badly, curse me thoroughly. Spare nothing."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. Small mercies.

I set my hand above the burn—not touching, never touching, the way Aldous had taught me, *the singer's hand hovers, Lior, because what you touch you claim*—and I did the thing I had sworn on my mother's tears never to do.

I listened.

Under the skin of the world there is a hum. I'd spent seven years pretending I couldn't hear it, the way a musician pretends not to hear a piano out of tune. It was there now, everywhere, in the rain-heavy clouds stacked over the hills, in the iron nail-heads of the chapel roof, in the salt-sweat drying on my own neck. Potential. Pressure. The sky's whole mood, waiting for permission.

*I can't,* I told it, an old argument worn smooth as river glass.

The hum said nothing, because the hum never argues. It only waits. That's the terrible patience of storms; they have all the time in the world and none of your excuses.

I thought of Aldous. His hands, hovering. His voice saying *the gift is not a weapon, it is a conversation, and you, little wolf, talk too loudly when you're frightened.* I thought of my mother's face in the doorway of everything I'd known, saying *never sing*, and—for the first time in seven years—I heard those words completely. Not as a banishment. As a mother's desperate arithmetic: *if you never sing, they can't find you, they can't use you, you'll be safe.* She had traded my inheritance for my survival and never told me it was a trade. Mothers do that. Mine, apparently, did it in prophecy form, across a century, in cipher, with maps to drowned abbeys.

But safety, it turns out, is a place. And I had left it. The moment the codex opened, the moment the inquisitor's lantern swung into Vell, the moment the thunderclap leveled a street—I had left safety forever, walking backward into a future I couldn't refuse. Which meant the price she'd paid was already spent. Already wasted, if I refused the change it bought.

Or already invested. Depending on what I did next.

"Talk too loudly when frightened," I whispered. "All right, old man. Watch me whisper."

I sang.

Not aloud. Not with the throat. With the place behind the sternum where the storm lived, the true instrument, the one no one had ever built a word for. A single thread of it, thin as spider silk, drawn up out of the hum and down through my hovering hand—

—and the world went silver and enormous.

Storm-light doesn't look like lightning. Lightning is the storm shouting. This was the storm *singing*, a filament of white-gold crawling along the ruined arm, and I felt everything at once: the infection like a nest of cold spiders, the fever like a banked fire in a house with all its windows painted shut, the small stubborn engine of her heart going too fast, always too fast. And underneath all of it, shockingly, the shape of her—not her body, her *self*, bright and fierce and entirely ordinary, a child-shaped knot of ordinary human light that no prophecy had ever mentioned and never needed to.

The burn was dying tissue. I coaxed it. Coaxed the blood back into channels it had abandoned, burned the rot out with a heat gentler than fever, knit what knitting was possible and left honest scabs for the rest, because I remembered Aldous on that too: *healing that leaves no scar is lying to the body.* The thread of storm-light moved through her like dawn moving across a field, and where it passed, the red streaks faded, and the breathing slowed, and slowed, and slowed—

Too slow. Panic spiked through me and the thread flared—

*Steady,* said a voice that wasn't Aldous's and wasn't mine and was both. *Steady, little wolf. You're singing, not shouting.*

I steadied. The thread thinned. Her heart settled into a rhythm like a sleeping cat, and her face, which had been a battlefield, became merely a face—a sleeping child's face, smudged with soot, mouth slightly open, dreaming about who knows what. Goats, probably. Or revenge against bedtime.

I withdrew my hand and sat back on my heels and discovered I was shaking so hard my teeth chattered.

It hadn't taken anything. That was the part that undid me. Seven years I'd imagined the magic as a debt collector, a devourer, a thing that fed on me—on Aldous, on everyone near me. And it had cost me nothing but attention. Nothing but the willingness to stop fighting. The storm hadn't surged out of control because it was hungry. It had surged because I'd been strangling it, and strangled things thrash.

I had done this to myself. For seven years. While people died of fevers within reach of my hands.

"Lior?" Maren crouched beside me. "You went somewhere. Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere," I said, and then, because lying to Maren had stopped working somewhere around the smuggler tunnels: "Everywhere. All the places I could have been useful and chose to sell trinkets instead." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and jagged. "Do you know how many villages like this we walked past to get here? Do you know what I did at each one? I looked at the smoke and I thought, *keep your head down, Lior, stay harmless, harmless is close enough to good.*"

"Harmless kept you alive."

"Harmless kept me *small*. Those aren't the same animal either." I looked down at my hands. Ordinary hands. Charm-seller's hands, nicked and calloused from wire and tinwork. They had just done more good in ten minutes than I had managed in seven years, and they were trembling not from exhaustion but from fury—at Voss, at his foragers and his strategy and his crown of necessity, but mostly, if I was honest, at me.

Outside the chapel, the Compact riders were talking low and urgent, and I caught fragments: columns sighted on the north road, refugees streaming toward Harrowgate, talk of burned villages all the way up the river valley like a trail of snuffed candles. Voss's rearguard. Voss's strategy. Voss, marching south with chained storm-priests strapped to wagons like artillery, hunting a Wolf-Singer he intended to own.

Well. He could hunt.

I stood up. My legs held, mostly.

"Maren," I said. "How far to the next village?"

She studied me with those courier's eyes that weighed and measured everything, and whatever she saw in my face made her answer carefully. "Ashford. Half a day north. Last report said it still stood."

"Then we make it before it doesn't." I shouldered the pack. The copper bell swung and caught the grey light, ringing softly, once—the smallest sound in a world full of thunder, and somehow the loudest thing I'd ever heard. "And Maren? When we get to Harrowgate, when they offer me their bargain—you tell your Compact leaders that the price has changed."

"Changed how?"

"They don't get a weapon." I looked back once at the pallet, at Tessa sleeping, at her mother's counting song finally, finally falling silent into rest. Grief was still there—it would always be there, for Aldous, for Vell, for every village I'd walked past with my head down—but it had changed shape in the last hour, the way grief does when you feed it purpose. It had stopped being a prison and started being fuel.

"They get a storm," I said. "One that answers to *me*."

Behind us, faint through the chapel wall, a child's voice rose in sleep—counting. One-two-three-four-five.

North, the smoke was rising. I walked toward it.
