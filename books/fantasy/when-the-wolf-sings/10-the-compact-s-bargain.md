## 10. The Compact's Bargain

*Lior*

Harrowgate smelled of iron and wet stone and the particular desperation of a city that had decided to be important. The walls were old—older than Vell, older than anything I'd known—and someone had hung banners from every tower in a hurry, so that they drooped like laundry no one expected to come back for.

Maren walked beside me through the gate with the codex wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, holding it the way a mother holds a sleeping child she is also planning to sell.

"Whatever they offer you," she said quietly, "remember that an offer is not a gift."

"That's very wise."

"I stole it from my grandmother."

The gate guards looked at me too long. Not at Maren—at me. Word travels faster than horses when the word is *storm-caller*, and apparently it had beaten us north by two days. One of the guards made a small gesture, half-superstition, half-insult: fingers curled against bad weather. I smiled at him the way I used to smile at customers in Vell who wanted to believe a copper bell could keep their houses safe. It was a good smile. It had taken seven years to perfect, and I hated that I still needed it.

Inside, Harrowgate was a fortress pretending to be a market town, or possibly the other way around. Stalls crowded the streets beneath arrow slits. Children chased each other between cart wheels. A woman sold roasted chestnuts three doors from a smithy forging pike heads, and both trades seemed equally busy. Cities on the edge of a war learn to do two things at once.

We were led up. Of course we were led up. Important people never meet you where the mud is.

The council chamber sat at the top of the citadel, round, with a table shaped like a wagon wheel, as if the Compact had been designed by someone who missed traveling. Seven chairs, six occupied. The seventh was empty, and I understood before anyone spoke that it was mine—that the room had been arranged around an absence, and I was the absence arriving late.

"Thackeray." The woman at the head did not stand. General Sera Ilvane, said the introduction, though she was introduced by her scars first—a burn along one jaw that she wore like a rank insignia. "You've read the codex."

"I've read parts of it."

"You've read the part about you." It wasn't a question. "Then let's not waste each other's evening with preamble. The usurper has forty thousand men and nine storm-priests in chains. We have twelve thousand men, no priests, and a prophecy sitting at our table looking like it would rather be anywhere else."

I looked at Maren. Maren looked at her hands.

"General," I said, "what exactly are you offering?"

Ilvane leaned back. "Protection. Legitimacy. A place." She said the last word carefully, the way you'd set down something fragile. "Vell won't have you back. The north wants your blood or your obedience. The Compact can give you a city that doesn't care what you are, only what you'll do. In exchange—" and here her voice went smooth and reasonable, which is how you know the knife is coming, "—you lead the counterstrike at Greyford. You break Voss's vanguard before it reaches the river."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you're free to leave," she said, "and we're free to lose, and you're free to watch from wherever you watch from."

There it was. Belonging, priced in advance. I'd spent seven years learning to be nobody, and this woman had looked at me for the length of one breath and decided I was a siege engine.

"I'll consider it," I said, because it was the only answer that kept my hands unclenched.

"Consider quickly," said Ilvane. "Voss does not consider."

*Maren*

I had argued for this meeting. I want that written somewhere, in case anyone ever asks me what I was thinking. I had stood in smuggler tunnels and burned villages and told Lior Thackeray that the Compact was the only roof left standing, and now I sat in the gallery above the council chamber watching that roof get offered like a contract with the terms already inked, and I felt something in my stomach turn over and lie down.

Because here is the thing about conscience: it doesn't argue one side. It argues both, all night, in voices that sound exactly like yours.

Lior came out of the chamber looking like someone who'd been measured for a coffin and offered a discount. I caught up with them on the stairs.

"You knew," they said. Not angry. Worse than angry—the flat tone of a person doing arithmetic. "When you found me in the tunnels. When you offered sanctuary. You knew what they'd ask."

"I hoped they'd ask differently."

"They asked me to be artillery, Maren. Politely. With a chair."

"Yes," I said. And then, because lying to Lior had stopped working somewhere around the thunderclap: "And I think you should say yes."

They stopped on the stair and turned, and I saw the storm-grey eyes do the thing they do—go still, go deep, like water before weather. "Say that again."

"I think you should say yes." My voice sounded steadier than I felt. "Not because the Compact deserves you. It doesn't. Half those people would chain you tomorrow if chaining you polled better. But Voss has nine storm-priests in wagons, Lior. I've seen what's left of the towns he's practiced on. If you walk away, they don't stop existing. They just get aimed without you in the way."

"And if I stay? If I lead their counterstrike? Then I'm theirs. Every choice after that gets made by committee, and the committee will always choose the version of me that's most useful. That's not belonging, Maren. That's ownership with better furniture."

"I know." God help me, I knew. I was a courier; I'd been owned by causes since I was sixteen, and I had the calluses and the nightmares to show for it. "I know exactly what it costs. I'm paying it myself, in case you hadn't noticed. I chose the Compact over—over a lot of things. And some nights I lie awake wondering if I'm a person or just a route map that learned to talk."

Lior watched me for a long moment. "Then why are you arguing for it?"

"Because you're not me." I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "You're the Wolf-Singer whether you accept it or not. People are going to follow you into something—Voss's army, the Compact's war, a mob, a legend. The only question is whether there's a plan nearby when they do. Walk away from Harrowgate and you're not walking away from the war. You're just removing the last adult from the room."

That landed. I watched it land, and I wished it hadn't, because it was cruel and true and I'd said it mostly to win.

"Liar," Lior said softly. "You don't even believe the Compact has adults."

"No," I admitted. "But I believe in you, and I hate that, because it means I've become exactly the kind of fool the prophecies are written for."

*Lior*

I didn't sleep that night. Harrowgate put me in a guest room at the top of the citadel—high, of course, everything here was high, as if altitude were hospitality—and the window faced north, toward the road Voss would come by. Toward Greyford Bridge, which I had never seen but could now describe in detail, because the Compact's officers had spent the evening describing it to me in tones usually reserved for scripture.

*The bridge is narrow. The gorge is deep. If the vanguard is broken there, the river buys us a season.*

*A season.* As if time were grain to be stored. As if the people on the other side of that bridge weren't also storing their own seasons, spending them, betting them.

I lay on a bed too fine for me and thought about my mother's last words. *Never sing.* Two words, and I had built a life inside them like a snail builds a shell—small, curved, portable. Sell charms. Keep your head down. Be nobody so thoroughly that nobody becomes true.

And now a general with a burned face wanted me to unsay those two words in front of forty thousand men.

Here is what no one tells you about being needed: it feels almost identical to being loved. That's the trap. Ilvane had looked at me across that wagon-wheel table with something warm in her eyes, and I had felt the warmth, and some starved animal part of me had rolled over for it—*finally, finally, a place*—while the rest of me stood back and noted coldly that generals look at everyone that way when everyone is holding a hammer.

Seven years ago I killed a man I loved because my power answered before I did. I had sworn the debt off. Exile was my penance and my armor both.

But Maren's voice kept circling back, the way honest things do. *Nine storm-priests in wagons.* I had been a storm-priest's student once. I knew what chained magic looked like from the inside. Whatever Voss was doing to them, it was the thing I feared most in the world—not death, but use.

Maybe that was the real bargain underneath the bargain. Not: serve the Compact. But: decide whose hands hold you, since someone's hands will.

I got up and stood at the window. Below, Harrowgate's lamps made a scatter of gold in the dark, and beyond the walls the north road ran straight as a scar toward everything I'd fled. Somewhere out there, Kaelen Voss was marching with my kin in cages, calling it necessity.

Somewhere behind me, a city waited for my answer with its hand already open.

* * *

*Maren*

He said yes at dawn. That's the part I keep returning to—he said yes, and it was the wrong yes, or maybe the right yes for reasons none of us deserved, and I will spend a long time deciding which.

He came down to the courtyard while the garrison was changing watch, and he found Ilvane and told her, in front of a dozen officers, that he would lead the counterstrike at Greyford. On conditions. Of course on conditions; I could have kissed him for the conditions.

"The prisoners first," he said. "The nine storm-priests. Before any strike, the Compact commits to freeing them—not capturing, *freeing*. Unchained, given passage south if they want it. I won't fight a man with hostages to make him fight harder."

Ilvane's jaw worked. "Those priests are strategic assets—"

"They're people in wagons," Lior said, "and I know exactly what's in those wagons, because I put myself in one voluntarily for seven years and it nearly killed me. Free them or find another wolf."

Silence. Officers exchanging looks. And then Ilvane did something I didn't expect: she laughed, short and hard, like a door slamming. "Thackeray line to the bone. Fine. Freed—if the strike succeeds. Hostages freed after the battle, not before; I won't hand Voss a reason to kill them early."

"After," Lior repeated, and I watched them weigh it, watch them decide that half a mercy now beat a whole one never. "After. But you swear it in front of your officers. All of them. Words in front of witnesses are the only currency I trust anymore."

It was done. Hands shaken. Banners somehow more upright than yesterday. And I stood at the edge of the courtyard feeling the specific nausea of a courier whose package has arrived at its destination and turned out to be alive.

Later, on the wall, Lior found me. We watched the supply trains coming in—grain, arrows, more grain—and for a while neither of us said anything, which is the kind of conversation I'm best at.

"You hate this," they said.

"I hate that it worked."

"It hasn't worked yet. It's a promise made on a bridge we haven't seen, in a battle neither of us has survived." They turned to me, and there was something new in their face—not the charm-seller's polish, not the fugitive's flinch. Something quieter and much more frightening. Resolve, maybe. Or its first cousin. "Thank you, Maren. For arguing both sides. Most people only argue the side that profits them."

"I argued the side that keeps you alive."

"Same thing," they said, "from where you're standing."

Before I could answer, a horn sounded from the north tower—one note, then two, urgent, ragged. A rider was coming up the north road at a gallop, mud to the shoulders, horse lathered white, and the gate guards were shouting for it to be let through before it fell.

The rider reached the gate, half-fell from the saddle, and pressed a dispatch into the captain's hands. I watched the captain read it. I watched his face do what faces do when the world rearranges itself.

He looked up at the citadel, at us, at Lior, and shouted words that dropped through the morning cold like stones down a well:

"Greyford's fallen. Voss took the bridge in the night—and he's marching on Harrowgate."
