## 11. Songs in the Old Tongue

The word *marching* does a strange thing to a fortress-city. It empties the markets and fills the temples. It makes strangers hold doors for one another and then refuse to meet each other's eyes. By noon of the day the rider came, Harrowgate had stopped being a place where people lived and started being a place where people waited.

I spent most of it in the archive.

Not out of bravery. I want to be honest about that, since honesty has become a kind of currency for me lately and I hate to debase it. I went to the archive because it was underground, because it had one door, and because if Kaelen Voss's army came over the walls I intended to be somewhere with a great deal of stone between me and the sky. The fact that the archive also held the codex—the cracked-leather book that had ruined my life in a bookshop and then chased me across half the north—was, I told myself, incidental.

It was not incidental. Nothing ever is.

"You're sitting in my chair," said the archivist.

She was a small woman made almost entirely of elbows and disapproval, with spectacles pushed up into white hair and ink stains climbing her forearms like ivy. Her name, Maren had told me, was Illsa Vetch, and she had been Harrowgate's keeper of records for forty years, which meant she had catalogued three sieges, two floods, and one fire, and had emerged from each with the expression of someone who found history personally untidy.

"I'll move," I said.

"Don't be absurd. Sit." She set down an armful of scrolls, looked at me over her spectacles, and said, "You're the storm-caller."

"I'm a charm-seller."

"Mm. And I'm a dancing girl." She pulled a second stool across the flagstones with a screech that made me flinch. "The Compact gave me your codex this morning with instructions to help you 'find whatever's relevant.' They didn't say relevant to what, which tells me they don't know, which tells me nobody up there reads anymore." She sat, joints crackling like green wood. "So. Relevant to what?"

I had been turning that question over all morning, in the gaps between listening for hooves on the north road. The Compact wanted weapons. Maren wanted—well, Maren wanted complicated things, and I'd learned not to try to count them. What did *I* want from a century-old book of prophecy?

An answer, mostly, to the question of who had written it.

Because here is what I hadn't told anyone, not even Maren: I had read the codex cover to cover twice on the road north, and there was something wrong with it. Not the prophecies themselves—those were unsettling enough, verses about a wolf-singer and a sealed war and a bloodline that sang storms, verses that described my mother's hands, my own eyes, a copper bell on a rainy day. No. What was wrong was smaller than that. Quieter.

Certain words were wrong.

"Read me the third verse of the Sealing," I said. "The one about the war being bound under the ice."

Illsa found it without hunting, which told me she'd already read the thing more times than she'd admit. She cleared her throat and recited:

*"Where the wolf sings and the well runs red,*
*the war sleeps bound beneath the dead.*
*One blood to loose it, one to bind,*
*one voice to leave the storm behind."*

"Now," I said, "read it slowly. Letter by letter, the way it's written. Don't translate."

Her eyebrows climbed. She bent close to the page, lips moving, and after a moment went very still in the way old scholars go still, which is to say the way avalanches go still.

"This isn't Northern Common," she said softly. "Or rather—it is, and it isn't. The words are Common. But the spelling—" She traced a line with one grimy finger. "*S-a-e-l-t-h*, where it should be *seal*. *W-y-r-n-g*, where it should be *bind*. These aren't errors. Errors repeat randomly. These repeat *consistently*. It's a substitution layered inside the language itself." She looked up at me, and behind the disapproval there was something bright and hungry. "Do you know what this means?"

"That whoever wrote it was showing off?"

"It means," she said, with the patience of a woman addressing a particularly slow student, "that whoever wrote it didn't want just anyone reading it. A scholar could puzzle this out in a year. A casual reader would never know there was anything hidden at all. It's a lock built out of orthography." She tapped the page. "Who spells *storm* as *s-t-o-r-m-e* except someone who learned it wrong? Or learned it *privately*."

My mouth had gone dry.

Because I knew. Gods help me, I knew. My mother used to sing to me in a nonsense tongue when I was small—a lullaby language, she called it, family business, nothing for little wolves to worry about—and when I was older and sulkier she taught me the letters of it, made me practice them in the margins of my lessons, spelled my own name in them until it looked like weather written down. *Sa-elth*. *Wyrng*. I had thought it a game. A quirk. One of those things families keep the way they keep a crooked cup or an embarrassing song.

"It's a cipher," I heard myself say. "A private one. Family—family business."

Illsa's eyes narrowed over her spectacles. "Whose family?"

"Mine," I said. "Apparently."

* * *

We worked until the lamps burned low and someone brought bread and hard cheese and neither of us remembered eating it. Illsa turned out to have exactly two speeds: contemptuous and incandescent, and the discovery of a century-old hidden cipher flipped her permanently into the second gear. She fetched dictionaries in four languages, a slate, a stick of chalk worn to a nub, and a magnifying lens that made her eye enormous and furious.

Decoding was slower than either of us liked. The substitutions weren't random, but they weren't simple either—they shifted, I realized somewhere past midnight, according to rules I half-remembered from my mother's lessons. Rules keyed to position, to repetition. *Sing it in threes,* she used to say, tapping my knuckles. *Everything true comes in threes.* At the time I'd assumed she meant harmony.

"She built the key into how she wrote," I murmured, chalk hovering over the slate. "Every third letter follows the family spellings. The rest are decoys. You'd need to already know the pattern to see it—which means she never expected strangers to read this. She expected—"

"Kin," Illsa said.

Kin. The word sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. For seven years I had believed myself the last of the Thackeray line, self-exiled, un-mourned, un-mourning. Now the evidence said otherwise. Someone with my mother's letters and my mother's tricks had sat down a hundred years ago—or had written *as if* a hundred years ago—and composed a prophecy with my face in it, my bell, my rain-grey eyes, and buried a second message underneath the first like a knife wrapped in a scarf.

"Why?" I asked the page. "Why hide it? Why not just write what you meant?"

"Because prophecy is read by everyone," Illsa said, "and meaning is read by no one. Whoever she was, she understood audiences. A public verse moves crowds. A hidden one instructs." She glanced at me. "You look pale."

"I'm always pale. It's the northern climate."

"It's not the climate."

* * *

Near dawn we broke it open.

It happened the way these things do—not with trumpets but with tedium, with Illsa cross-checking the same verse for the ninth time and suddenly going silent, and silence from Illsa Vetch being louder than any alarm in Harrowgate. She laid her finger under a passage near the codex's heart, the passage the Compact had circled and annotated and argued over: the Wolf-Singer verse, the one that promised freedom or ruin, the one every faction from here to the salt coast had killed and died to interpret correctly.

"Read it decoded," she said, and pushed the slate toward me.

My hand shook. I'd like to claim it was the cold, but the archive was warm enough, and anyway I've stopped lying to myself about the small things. Only the large ones.

The public verse read:

*"The Wolf-Singer comes with the thaw,*
*with storm-grey eyes and a copper bell,*
*to free the north or end it—*
*the singer chooses, none can tell."*

The decoded verse underneath it said:

*"If you are reading this, little wolf, then I am long ash,*
*and the choice I promised you was never a choice at all.*
*A prophecy cannot command. It can only invite.*
*Come to Vaelmoor. Read the rest. Then choose—*
*but understand that everything you have been told*
*about the sealing is a story told to children,*
*and I was its first liar."*

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above us, boots crossed stone in patterns that had become constant since the rider came—hurry, hurry, hurry—and the lamps hissed, and Illsa watched me the way one watches a fuse.

"A story told to children," I repeated.

"Your mother," Illsa said carefully, "or your grandmother, or some aunt whose name the histories ate—wrote a public prophecy that the entire north has spent a hundred years bleeding over. Kingdoms fell waiting for the Wolf-Singer. Voss built an army on the back of verses like this. The Compact organized around them. And underneath, in letters only her own blood could read, she calls it a story. Calls herself a liar." She took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, though they didn't need it; it was something to do with her hands while thinking. "That's not a prophecy, Thackeray. That's a letter. A letter with a bomb inside it."

"And the address," I said, and my voice sounded far away to me, "is Vaelmoor."

Vaelmoor. The drowned abbey. Every child in the north grew up on it the way southern children grew up on bogeymen: the abbey that sank when the war was sealed, cloisters standing knee-deep in black water a hundred years on, haunted, holy, forbidden. Pilgrims went. Most came back with stories. Some didn't come back.

"Wait," I said. "Wait. There's more. If she hid one message she'd have hidden—"

"The map," Illsa and I said together, which was the first and last time we agreed without argument.

It took another hour, and it nearly defeated us, because the map wasn't written in letters at all. It was written in the margins—in the decorative flourishes, the little vines and wave-patterns that scribes put around verses to make them pretty. Decoded, the flourishes resolved into lines. Coastlines. Rivers. A long inlet on the northern coast, and at the head of it, marked with the family cipher's sign for *singing water*, a small precise drawing of a towered building half-swallowed by waves.

Underneath, in letters so small I needed Illsa's monstrous lens to read them:

*"The Wellspring lies beneath the choir. Little wolf—I could not finish what I began. The seal is failing; it was always going to fail; seals made of sacrifice leak. When it breaks, they will come for you, all of them, every crown and covenant, because you are the key and keys get used. There is a third door. I could not reach it. Perhaps you can. Come before the thaw. Come alone if you can bear it. Come armed with nothing but your voice.*

*"And little wolf—whatever they tell you the singing is for, it is not for them. It was never for them. Not the crowns, not the covens, not the gods. It is for the ones who come after. Sing for the ones who come after."*

I put the lens down. My hands were steady now, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Steady hands meant the fear had gone somewhere deeper, somewhere structural.

"Well," said Illsa, into the quiet. "That's a problem."

"Which part? The drowned abbey? The failing seal? The part where my mother planned all of this a hundred years ago and let the whole north tear itself apart looking for a weapon she'd already told me not to be?"

"All of it, but no—" She tapped the decoded map, the coastline, the little towered building. "This part. Look at the date of the codex. A hundred years old, give or take. And she writes *come before the thaw*." Illsa looked up at me, and for once there was no hunger in her face at all, only a bleak and scholarly pity. "Thaws come every year, Thackeray. But a seal that leaks—that's a clock. If she was right, if it's been failing for a century—"

"Then it's nearly done failing," I finished. "And everything Voss wants, everything the Compact wants, everything the prophecy promised—it's all sitting under a flooded choir in Vaelmoor, getting closer to breaking while armies march around deciding who gets to own it."

"And one of those armies," Illsa said, "is currently marching here."

Right. That.

I had managed, through the long night of letters and flourishes and my mother's voice rising off the page like heat off a road, to forget the horns. To forget the rider lathered white, the captain's face, the stones-down-a-well words: *he's marching on Harrowgate.* The archive had felt safely outside time, a cellar where the world's noise couldn't follow. But noise follows. Noise always follows. It was following now—I could hear it through the ceiling, more boots, more voices, the particular high tension of a garrison that has counted its arrows and doesn't like the number.

"Can you copy the map?" I asked. "The decoded verses? All of it?"

"I can copy it three times and hide the copies in places that would embarrass you to hear described." She was already pulling fresh paper, already sharpening a pen with the brisk violence of a woman who had survived three sieges and intended to survive this one out of spite. "But listen to me. Whatever you decide to do—go north, run south, stand on the wall and shout at Voss personally—you should know what you're carrying now. This isn't prophecy anymore. This is instructions. From kin." She glanced up. "How does it feel, knowing the author?"

I thought about my mother's hands. About *never sing*, the last words she ever spoke to me, sharp as a slammed door, sending me south into exile with nothing but guilt and a satchel of charms that didn't work. Seven years I had built my whole life on those two words, treated them as law, as penance, as the shape of my soul.

Now her handwriting told me: *the seal is failing. There is a third door. Sing for the ones who come after.*

Never sing. And yet: sing.

"She lied to me," I said. "At the end. Or she lied to everyone else, and told me the truth in the only way she had left—one hundred years early, in a code only I could break, in a book she knew would find me whether I wanted finding or not." I laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "Gods, she couldn't even give me a straight answer from beyond the grave. She had to make it a puzzle."

"Mothers," said Illsa Vetch, with feeling.

Above us, the boots stopped their hurrying. In the sudden hush I heard the thing I had been dreading without naming it: the groan-and-clank of the citadel's signal chains, and then a bell beginning to ring—not the slow toll that marked the hours but a fast, stuttering pattern, over and over, the rhythm every fortress-city teaches its children before it teaches them letters.

*Attack. Attack. Attack.*

Illsa's pen froze mid-stroke. We looked at each other across the table strewn with a dead woman's secrets.

"They're not supposed to reach the walls for days," she said. "The scouts said days."

I was already gathering the codex, clutching it to my chest like the child's lullaby-book it apparently had been all along. And in the back of my skull, under seven years of suppression, under the guilt and the hiding and the careful performance of powerlessness, the storm stirred and lifted its head—because somewhere beyond the stone, beyond the walls, beyond the dark, other storms were walking toward us wearing an army's coat, and mine knew them the way a wolf knows wolves.

"Days," I repeated, moving for the door. "Someone told them a faster way."
