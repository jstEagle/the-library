## 12. The Traitor's Toll

I have been a sergeant of the Vell city watch for eleven years, and in that time I learned three things that kept me alive: never trust a man who smiles before he speaks, never stand between a drunk and his argument, and never, ever read anything the Inquisition hands you without checking who else has touched it first.

I broke all three rules in the space of four days. I'm telling you this up front so you understand I knew better. Knowing better has never once stopped me from doing worse.

* * *

It began with the lantern.

Inquisitor Corvane carried it everywhere—a squat brass thing with shutters like an eye, and inside it burned something that was not fire. The Church called it *veritas flame*, and claimed it turned blue in the presence of storm-touched blood. I'd seen it used twice on prisoners dragged through Vell's gates. Both times it had stayed stubbornly yellow, and both men had been released with apologies and broken fingers, because Corvane did not apologize for the fingers. He considered them a tax on suspicion.

He arrived on a Tuesday with two riders and a warrant bearing Kaelen Voss's own seal, which meant the usurper's war had grown long arms indeed if they could reach into a city that had spent a century pretending magic was a rumor told to frighten children.

The warrant named its quarry: *"a storm-caller of the Thackeray line."*

I stood at attention while Corvane read it aloud in my commander's office, and I kept my face arranged into the particular blankness we watchmen cultivate—the face of a man thinking about nothing more interesting than his supper. Inside, my stomach was performing maneuvers no stomach should attempt.

Lior Thackeray. Charm-seller. The one who paid me three coppers every month to look the other way about a stall permit, and who once sat up all night with old Tam Wicker when he was dying of the lung-rot, holding his hand and humming something low that made the pain go out of his eyes like water out of a drain. I'd pretended not to see that too. Looking the other way was, by then, practically my profession.

"Thackeray," Commander Brell said, squinting at the ledger. "Common enough name in these parts."

"It is not common enough," Corvane said. His voice was soft, which made it worse. Loud zealots you can duck. Soft ones listen. "The line died in the north seven years ago. All but one."

"We'll make inquiries," Brell said, in the tone of a man promising to lose a document as soon as politely possible.

Corvane smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You will do more than inquire, Sergeant Vane will accompany me personally." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on a table. "I'm told she knows the lower market well. Its people. Its habits."

There it was. Somebody had talked. Not about Lior—about me. About the coppers, or the humming, or the night I'd steered two inquisitors away from the charm-stall with a story about a counterfeit ring operating on the far side of town. Seven years of small kindnesses, and one of them had left a stain someone could follow.

"Yes," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "I know the market."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about betrayal: it is rarely dramatic. It does not arrive with trumpets. It comes in the shape of a clerk's careless note, a neighbor's frightened glance, a friend deciding that survival is a full-time occupation. And sometimes—this is the part that took me longest to swallow—it comes from the side you thought was yours.

Because while I spent three days leading Corvane down every blind alley Vell possessed—the empty stall where Lior had been, the landlady who swore they'd gone south months ago, the forger who sold me a convincing set of travel papers with Lior's name on them bound for Saltmere—I was also writing letters.

To Maren.

I'd found her name in the codex affair, pieced together from what little Lior had let slip before fleeing the city under a sky still ringing from that thunderclap. A scholar, I'd been told. Harmless. Which should have warned me; nothing harmless travels with a hundred-year-old prophecy book and a talent for vanishing.

My plan was simple, the way plans are simple right up until they meet the world: warn Maren that the Inquisition had warrants, that Voss's riders were asking questions along the north road, that the route through Greyford Bridge was watched. Get word ahead of the hunters. Buy my friend—or whatever Lior was to me by then, friend seemed too small a word for someone whose arrest I had spent years quietly sabotaging—another week of running.

What I did not know, what I would not learn until it was far too late to matter, was that my letter went through the Compact's courier line. And the Compact, like every army ever assembled from desperate idealists, had a man inside it whose ideals had developed a price tag.

His name was Officer Dain Ferris. Remember it. I intend to.

* * *

I caught up with Lior's party on the fourth day out of Harrowgate, riding a lathered mule I'd won off Corvane's own quartermaster in a card game I cheated at with zero remorse. The road north ran along the Alden River through country that Voss's war had already chewed: burned crofts, orchards stripped to black skeletons, a chapel with its bell tower toppled into the shallows so the water rang it whenever the current shifted. Somewhere behind us, always, the smoke of the next village to die.

They made camp in the ruins of a tollhouse. Lior sat apart from the others, turning a copper coin over their knuckles—old habit, the charm-seller's idle grace—and when they saw me ride in they didn't startle. That hurt more than surprise would have. As if some part of them had been waiting for the law to find them since the day they were born.

"Sergeant Vane," they said. "Come to arrest me at last? You're late. There's a queue."

"The queue starts with me and ends nowhere pleasant." I swung down off the mule. My knees cracked; I was too old for this work and had been saying so for a decade. "Voss has your name on a warrant now. Not 'storm-caller.' Your actual name. Thackeray. Someone in the Church dug deep enough to find what you buried."

Maren appeared at Lior's shoulder, codex-shaped bulge under her coat, eyes doing rapid arithmetic. "How deep are the riders?"

"Two days behind me. Maybe less. They've got veritas flame and a list of river crossings." I looked at Lior directly. "Greyford Bridge is burned into their maps. Take the ford at Cael's Hollow instead, two miles downstream. It's shallow this time of year. Slow, but unwatched."

"You're certain it's unwatched?"

"No," I said honestly. "I'm certain it was unwatched when I left Vell. Certainty beyond that is for priests."

Lior studied me for a long moment, storm-grey eyes doing that thing they did—weighing, cataloguing, seeing rather more than I wanted seen. Then: "Why are you here, Odile? Truly. You had a life. A post. You'll lose both for this."

"I had a life built on not noticing things," I said. "Turns out it doesn't suit me anymore. Turns out it never did."

That was the truest sentence I spoke all week, and I want it noted that it cost me nothing to say and everything to mean.

* * *

We rode together for half a day, and I will confess something here that shames me: I liked them. All of them. The Compact outriders with their patched armor and their terrible singing. Maren, who argued map routes like other people argue theology. Even the quiet archivist's apprentice who kept trying to feed me stew as though stew could fix anything, which, in fairness, it sometimes can.

And Lior. Lior most of all. Watching them around the evening fire, I saw what seven years of hiding had carved into them—the constant flinch toward every loud noise, the way they sat with their back to stone, the careful emptiness of their hands. But underneath it, something stubborn and warm. When a refugee child on the road stared at them, Lior conjured—not magic, just sleight-of-hand—a copper bell out of the boy's ear and gave it to him. The boy laughed like the war didn't exist.

I remember thinking: *the prophecy people want this one as a weapon, and Voss wants them as artillery, and everyone is missing the point entirely.* The point was the bell. The point was the laugh.

Then, near dusk on the second day, I saw Dain Ferris.

He was supposed to be a day's ride east, carrying dispatches to Harrowgate. Instead he was on the western bank of the Alden, standing very still among the alders at exactly the spot where Cael's Hollow ford met the road, talking to two men in travel-worn leathers who did not move like travelers. They moved like soldiers trying not to. One of them wore gloves despite the mild evening, and when Ferris handed him something—a paper, folded small—the gloved man tucked it inside his breastplate, against the skin, the way important things get carried.

I was half a mile off on the ridge, and I did the arithmetic fast and ugly: Ferris knew the route change. I'd spoken it aloud in the tollhouse. Which meant either Ferris had ears I hadn't counted, or someone else had passed the route along, and either way, there he stood, selling it.

Betrayal, I told you, is rarely dramatic. This was the dramatic kind anyway, and it still felt mostly like cold. Cold in the hands, cold behind the eyes. Eleven years of watching Vell rot politely from the inside, and I suppose I'd convinced myself the rot stopped at the waterline. It doesn't. Rot rides horses now.

I couldn't reach the party in time on foot, and the ridge was too open to risk the mule at a gallop—riders draw eyes, and those two men by the ford already had the look of eyes drawn and waiting. So I did the thing that felt least like a decision and most like falling: I cut downslope through the alder brake, alone, toward the ford, hoping to reach the crossing before dark, before the trap closed, before the arithmetic finished solving itself.

I did not make it.

* * *

They hit the party at moonrise, at the ford itself—which meant Ferris had told them the new route after all, which meant my warning had traveled faster than my help, which is the cruelest trick a piece of paper can play.

I heard it before I saw it: a horn note from the eastern ridge, thin and deliberate, and then the world came apart. Crossbow bolts hissed across the water. Horses screamed. One of the outriders went down in the shallows and did not get up, and the apprentice with the stew was shouting names into the dark, and none of the names answered him.

Through it all, one sound rose above everything, and I will hear it until I die: Lior's voice, cracking open, half command and half plea—"Run, don't fight, RUN"—and beneath it, the air itself beginning to hum. Storm-hum. The sound I'd only heard once before, the night a street in Vell ceased to exist.

Lightning walked across the river. I mean that literally. It came down in steps, deliberate as a footfall, and where it struck the ambushers' line the darkness turned white and then red and then simply absent. The smell afterward was ozone and worse things.

But there were too many, and they'd chosen their ground well. Maren got separated—I saw her horse bolt upstream with her clinging sideways, the codex's corner jutting from her coat like a splinter. Two Compact riders dragged Lior toward the trees, away from the ford, away from me. And between me and all of it, wading out of the reeds with his gloved hand extended and his face wearing an expression of genuine, wounded disappointment, came Dain Ferris.

"Odile," he said. "Sergeant. You should not be here."

"Funny," I said. "I was about to say the same to you. Except I mean it in the criminal sense."

"They're going to use her." His voice had the flat sincerity of a man who has rehearsed his sins until they sound like virtues. "The Compact, Voss—it makes no difference. Everyone wants to aim her at something. I'm just choosing which something pays best. The free cities fall either way. At least my mother eats."

I hit him. I am not proud of much this week, but I am proud of that. He was bigger than me and younger and armed, and I hit him anyway, and we went down into the mud of the ford together, and I got maybe four seconds of honest violence in before his boot found my ribs and the world went briefly white.

When it came back, he was standing over me, breathing hard, wiping blood from his mouth. Behind him the ford burned blue-white where lightning still crawled, and Lior's voice was rising toward something I recognized from prophecy books and bad dreams.

"You can still walk away, Odile," Ferris said. "Ride south, report that you were overpowered. Corvane might even believe you."

"And say what happened here?"

"That the storm-caller destroyed an ambush and fled north." He shrugged. "It's even true. Truth is cheap. That's the whole lesson of this war."

I lay in the mud of Cael's Hollow ford, with my ribs creaking and eleven years of quiet complicity soaking through my uniform, and I understood that he was offering me my life back. The post. The pension. The comfortable art of not-noticing.

"No," I said, and got up.

It took three tries and cost me a rib, probably, but I got up, and I picked up the crossbow one of the dead had dropped, and I pointed it at the last man on earth I had ever expected to point a weapon at.

Ferris looked at the bow. Looked at me. Something in his face flickered—not regret, exactly, but its distant cousin, the one that visits occasionally and leaves without leaving a name.

"You're exiled now," he said. "You understand that. From both sides. There's no coming back from this for you."

"I know," I said. "It's the first order anyone's given me in years that I actually wanted to follow."

I fired wide on purpose. He flinched; I turned and ran—upstream, into the alders, toward where the riders had dragged Lior, because exile is just loyalty with nowhere left to live, and I intended to spend mine somewhere useful.

Behind me the storm sang, and the river ran bright, and somewhere in the dark a woman clung to a bolting horse with a century-old book against her heart, heading north with or without any of us.

I followed the lightning. What else was there to do?

* * *

By dawn I found the trail: hoofprints churned through wet grass, a discarded gauntlet, a smear of blood that wasn't Lior's—one of the riders had taken a bolt, then. Good. I hoped it hurt proportionally to his employment.

The trail bent northeast, away from the road, away from everything, toward the high fells and the drowned places beyond them. Toward Vaelmoor, though I didn't know the name yet. I only knew that Lior had been carried that way unconscious, and that whoever carried them had been paid by someone, and that every road in the north now led through a war.

I paused on the ridgeline and looked back once, the way you do. South: Vell, my post, my pension, Corvane's soft voice waiting for my report. East: Harrowgate, and the Compact, and Dain Ferris's mother eating bought bread. Ahead: nothing I owned and no one expecting me.

Eleven years of duty, and it had come to this: a middle-aged woman with cracked ribs following thunder into the wilderness, having finally noticed everything, too late for it to matter.

Almost too late.

Far off to the north, past the fells, the clouds were gathering in a pattern no natural weather makes—circling, tightening, patient as a held breath. Something was pulling the storm toward it. Or someone was learning to pull.

I tightened the borrowed crossbow's strap across my shoulder, spat into the mud for luck, and started walking.

Whatever waited in those drowned cloisters, it would have to get through one exiled sergeant first. And I promise you, whoever they were—they had never met anyone as stubborn as a woman with nothing left to lose and one name left to protect.
