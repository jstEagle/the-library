## 13. Drowned Abbey

The abbey of Vaelmoor had been drowning for three hundred years and had gotten very good at it.

We stood on the ridge above it in the grey hour before dawn—Maren shivering beside me, Odile pretending she wasn't, both of them staring down at the ruin the way people stare at graves they've traveled a long way to visit, which is to say with reverence and a private hope that nothing inside will ask anything of them.

I knew better. Everything I'd come here to find was going to ask something of me. It always did.

"There," Odile said, pointing. The bell tower rose out of the flood like a drowned man's last gesture—one arm up, fingers broken, still reaching. Water lapped at the third-story windows. Below it, the cloisters were submerged to their arches, black water perfectly still except where rain touched it.

"The map shows an entrance," Maren said. She had the codex open despite the drizzle, one hand cupped over the page like a mother shielding a candle. "The old pilgrim stair. Underwater now, but—" She looked at me, and I watched her decide how much honesty she could afford. "But you're the only one who could breathe through it."

"I'm not a fish."

"No," she agreed. "You're a storm-caller who's spent seven years pretending air is the only element you own. You could part that water like a curtain."

She wasn't wrong, which was the irritating thing about Maren. She was almost never wrong, and she deployed this quality the way other people deployed knives—carefully, and only when she needed you bleeding.

Odile said nothing. She'd said very little since the river crossing, since the traitor's name had come out of her mouth like a tooth she'd been carrying under her tongue for weeks. I think she was still deciding whether she'd saved us or failed us, and I hadn't found the words to tell her that both things can be true at once, and usually are.

"Rest first," I said. "We go down when the light comes."

Nobody argued. That was new, too. Somewhere between the burned villages and the refugees who'd started calling me *Wolf-Singer* in voices that made my skin crawl, arguing with me had stopped being fashionable. I hated it. I'd spent seven years being nobody precisely so that people would keep telling me I was wrong.

* *

The light came, and so did we.

The pilgrim stair was exactly where the map promised, a slot of cut stone descending into the flood beneath what had once been the abbey's western gate. The water over it was clear as glass and cold as a confession. Fish moved in it, silver slivers, unbothered by the dead architecture around them.

I stood at the edge and let myself feel it—that hum under my sternum, the storm I'd spent seven years sitting on like a lid on a boiling pot. Since the road north, since the child with the fevered eyes I'd healed with a thread of light no thicker than a spider's silk, the lid had been loosening. Magic came easier now. That should have felt like coming home. Mostly it felt like a door I'd nailed shut swinging open on its own.

"Lior." Maren, quietly.

"I know."

I knelt and put my palm flat against the water.

I won't pretend it was graceful. What I did was less like commanding the sea and more like asking it, very politely, to hold its breath—and the sea, to my lasting astonishment, obliged. The water drew back from the stairway in a slow exhalation, leaving a corridor of dripping stone and hanging air, walls of flood trembling on either side like curtains held by invisible hands. My arms shook. Sweat ran cold down my spine. But it held.

"After me," I said. "Quickly."

We descended into Vaelmoor.

* *

Inside, the abbey was a cathedral of green shadow.

Light fell in shafts through gaps in the flooded roof above, turning the submerged cloisters into columns of amber. We walked the raised walkways—the monks had built high passages for just such floods, centuries of river-life teaching them humility—and our footsteps echoed off stone furred with moss and pale, blind fungus. Carvings surfaced from the dark as we passed: wolves, storms, a woman with her arms flung wide and her hair full of lightning.

Every few arches, the same woman. I stopped counting after twelve.

"She's everywhere," Maren murmured.

"Yes," I said, because my throat had closed around anything longer.

Because I knew her face. I'd grown up beneath it. There was a carving of her in the great hall of Thackeray Hold, and a painting in my mother's study, and—when I was small enough to still believe such things—a story that the First Singer had stood against the sky and caught the whole war in her hands like a bird.

I had thought it legend. Legends don't repeat themselves in drowned abbeys with your mother's jawline.

The tomb was in the heart of the cloister garth, on an island of stone the flood had never quite taken—a ring of dry flagstones around a sarcophagus of grey granite, carved top to bottom in that same private cipher I'd spent three nights decoding with Harrowgate's archivist. Around it, the water lay flat as polished pewter, holding the island like a jewel.

My legs took me down the stair to it before my mind finished deciding to go.

* *

The cipher opened easily now. That frightened me more than anything else in the drowned abbey—how naturally the letters resolved, as though the script had been waiting in my hands the way a song waits in a throat. Family writing. Blood writing. The archivist had called it a "sectarian script of the northern covens." He'd been wrong. It was a *lullaby*. It was the alphabet of bedtime stories whispered in a kitchen while bread baked, the secret language my mother taught me when I was six because I'd asked, because I'd wanted to be let in on something that was only ours.

I read the sarcophagus standing in the rain-drip silence with Maren and Odile behind me and the flood all around us like a congregation.

HERE LIES THE SINGER WHO ENDED THE WAR
NOT BY CONQUERING THE STORM
NOR BY SILENCING IT
BUT BY GIVING HER SONG TO THE SEALING
LET NO ONE CALL HER A MARTYR
SHE CHOSE THIS AS I HOPE MY CHILD WILL LEARN TO CHOOSE

"My child," Odile repeated softly, reading over my shoulder. Her voice was careful, the way it got around wounded things. "Lior—"

"It's her." My voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice borrowed for the occasion. "It's my mother."

Seven years I had carried her last words like a stone sewn into my chest: *never sing.* Seven years I'd built a life on the foundation of those two syllables, a life of copper bells and harmless charms and powerlessness performed nightly like theater. And here was the tomb that said: the woman who told you never sing was the woman who sang loudest of all, and died for it, and left you a map.

I sat down on the wet granite before my knees decided the matter without me.

* *

The journal was where the verse said it would be—in a lead-lined coffer set into the sarcophagus's base, sealed with wax stamped by a wolf's-head sigil I'd have known anywhere. The lead had kept the damp out. The pages inside were dry as autumn leaves, brown ink sharp as the day she'd written it, and the handwriting hit me like a fist to the sternum because it was hers. Loops and slashes and the way she crossed her t's like little lightning bolts. I used to mock her for it. She'd said, *one day you'll know why I write like weather.*

I didn't read it aloud. Some things aren't for sharing, even with the two people in the world most entitled to hear them. I read it silently, kneeling there, while the water breathed around us and Maren pretended not to watch and Odile kept watch at the stair with the rigid shoulders of a soldier guarding something holier than any god she'd ever been issued.

The early pages were strategy. I'll give her that—she was my mother, but she was also a general of a sort, and the opening entries are cold as ledgers. Dates. Casualty counts. Names of the Wellspring's wardens. Descriptions of the old war in terms so plain they made it worse: the storm-lines, the bloodlines bred to command them, the families turned to artillery, the north eating itself alive with weather. I read about cousins I'd never met burning villages with lightning and calling it liberation. I read about children born already pledged to the storm-priesthood, their gifts measured before their teeth came in.

Then, slowly, the ledger became a letter. Then the letter became a confession.

*I have tried every path,* she wrote. *Conquest, and it makes monsters. Silence, and it makes pressure-cookers; the gift does not die when denied, it waits, and it takes the young first. I have watched Voss's teachers preach control and produce men who mistake control for virtue. I have watched the Compact preachers preach freedom and produce mobs. There is a third door and it is narrow and it costs everything, and I have finally understood that it must be me.*

*The prophecy is written. Yes—I wrote the prophecy. Let whoever finds this forgive me the vanity of dressing instruction in mystery, but mystery travels where truth cannot. If I wrote plainly—"go to Vaelmoor, unbind the Wellspring"—they would burn the book and salt the ground. So I dressed it in thunder and gave it a century to ripen. If you are reading this, my love, then the book found you, which means you survived long enough to need it, which was all I ever dared want.*

And then, on the last page I could bear to read that hour:

*They will tell you the gift is a curse. They will tell you it is a crown. It is neither. It is a hand—you may close it or open it, but it is yours, and no one—not Voss, not the Compact, not the storm itself—gets to decide what your hand does. I chose to give mine to the sealing because no one would take it and the war would not stop. I do not regret it. But I pray—gods, how I pray—that you find a door I missed. That your choice is freer than mine was. Whatever you choose, choose it with your eyes open and your name in your own mouth. Never sing for anyone who tells you when.*

*Never sing unless it is yours.*

*Unless it is yours.*

I put the journal down on my knees and discovered I was crying, which was inconvenient, because I'd budgeted the entire morning for composure and here it was, bankrupt before noon.

*Never sing.* Seven years, I'd heard it as a prohibition. A muzzle. The sentence that exiled me. Kneeling in the drowned abbey with her ink still wet in my mind, I finally heard what she'd actually written—the rest of the sentence, the part grief had amputated from my memory until this room stitched it back on. Not *never sing.* Never sing *for them.* For the priests who measured you. For the war that wanted you weaponized. For the fear that made me run.

She hadn't cursed me. She'd hidden instructions inside a warning, the way she hid lightning bolts in her t's, and it had taken me seven years and three hundred miles and a flooded grave to find the whole message.

Mourning is supposed to be a fixed thing. You grieve the dead, you carry the weight, the weight doesn't change. Nobody warns you it can be *rewritten*. That you can kneel at a tomb carrying one grief and stand up carrying a different one entirely—heavier in some ways, lighter in others, unrecognizably the same stone. I had mourned a mother who forbade me. I stood up mourning a mother who trusted me, and trust, it turns out, is far harder to live under than law.

"You've been reading a long time," Maren said gently. "What does she say?"

I looked at her—at the Compact courier who'd recruited me with soft words and hard arithmetic, who needed the Wolf-Singer the way armies need bridges—and I heard my mother's voice: *not for anyone who tells you when.*

"That the prophecy ends at Vaelmoor," I said. Which was true. Just not all of what was true.

Odile's eyes narrowed a fraction. She'd been a sergeant too long not to smell a half-truth, and she loved me too long-established to press it. She just nodded once, and stood guard, and let me keep my own counsel. I would owe her for that. I seem to owe everyone lately.

* *

We stayed the day in the abbey because the rain wouldn't quit and because I wasn't done. I read the journal twice more. I found the maps—Vaelmoor's foundations, and beneath them, older than the abbey, older than the war: the Wellspring itself, drawn in her sure hand, deep under the flooded crypts, a place where the lines of the storm converge like roots around a heart. She'd marked the approach. She'd marked the seals, seven of them, each one keyed to a verse. Each one keyed, I realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the damp, to a song. To *my* inheritance, sung in *my* blood's own register.

She'd built me a door and left the key in my throat.

Near dusk, Maren climbed to the highest walkway and came back down fast, boots hammering stone, all her careful courier calm gone.

"Fires," she said. "North road. A column. Torches, wagons, and—" she swallowed, "—storm-light. Green-white flashes, regular as signal lamps. They're using the priests tonight. Someone's marching on Harrowgate."

Harrowgate. Where we'd left them. Where I'd refused their counterstrike and walked away with my conscience intact and my mother's map in my pack.

Odile was already moving. "How far?"

"A night's march. Less if they push."

I stood in my mother's flooded church with her journal against my ribs and her choice humming in the stones around me, and I understood—with the terrible clarity she'd prayed I'd find—that the third door was no longer theoretical. Voss was burning the free cities one at a time, and every hour I spent grieving was a village he spent well.

"Pack up," I said. "We're going back."

Maren stared. "Back? Toward that?"

"To Harrowgate." I touched the journal through the leather of my coat, the way you touch a promise. "My mother left me instructions. I intend to read the last page somewhere quieter than a battlefield—but first someone has to make sure there's still a somewhere left worth reading it in."

Above us, through the broken roof, the first stars came out over the flood, and far away to the north the horizon flickered green-white, regular as a heartbeat.

Regular as a drum.

Regular as a war, marching toward everything I had left to lose.
