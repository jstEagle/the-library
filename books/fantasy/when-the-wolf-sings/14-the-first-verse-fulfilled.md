## 14. The First Verse Fulfilled

I want it on the record—wherever records go when the cities that kept them are burning—that I did not trust Captain Elsbet Rennick from the moment I met her. She had the handshake of a woman who counts your fingers afterward to see if any came loose, and she laughed a half-second too long at jokes that weren't funny. In my trade you learn to read that laugh. It's the laugh of someone who is already composing her report to somebody else.

But she was Compact, and I was Compact, and in Harrowgate that autumn, being Compact meant never saying the quiet part aloud.

The quiet part was: *someone sold us.*

* * *

It started well, which should have been my first warning. Good days in wartime are like calm water over a drowning man—you enjoy them at exactly the wrong moment.

Harrowgate had swallowed us whole and kindly. The fortress-city sat astride the Greywater like an old boar with stone tusks, all ramparts and smoke and stubbornness, and its garrison commander—a barrel-shaped veteran named Odo Ferrant with a beard you could lose a knife in—had given Lior quarters in the east tower as if housing a prophet were a normal Tuesday. Word had spread the way word does. By the third day, people were leaving offerings outside Lior's door. Bread. Candles. A truly alarming quantity of salt fish.

Lior hated every crumb of it.

"You know what they left this morning?" they said to me over breakfast, holding up a child's knitted wolf with one button eye. "A *wolf*. Maren, I called down rain on one patrol. One. It barely qualified as weather."

"It flattened six men and a wagon."

"It was drizzle with ambition."

"They're calling it the Wolf-Singer's Baptism."

"I'm going to throw myself off this tower."

"Eat your eggs," I said. "Prophets need iron."

That was the last easy morning either of us would get for a long time. I've replayed it since—the grey light, the terrible fish smell drifting up from the courtyard, Lior scowling into their cup like the tea had personally insulted them—and I keep trying to find the moment I could have said something different, done something different. There isn't one. That's the trick of guilt: it shows you a hundred doors after the house has burned.

* * *

Rennick found me that afternoon in the map room, where I was doing the thing couriers do when they're between rides: memorizing roads.

"Maren Voss." She used my full name, which nobody did unless they wanted something or wanted to remind me of something. "Word from the south road?"

"Clean," I said. "Voss's main column is still two weeks out. Vanguard's foraging around Thornmere."

"And the route we discussed? The river crossing?"

I'd given it to her the night before, because that was my job—routes, relays, safehouses, the circulatory system of the Compact's resistance. The Greyford crossing. The old shepherd tracks above it. The timing windows when the ferry lights went dark.

"Why?"

"No reason." Her smile did the half-second thing. "Just keeping current."

I told you. I didn't trust her. And I did nothing, because suspicion without proof is just gossip wearing armor, and because I was tired, and because I believed—in that way you believe things when believing is cheaper than checking—that the Compact was made of people who had lost too much to betray each other again.

Understand what Harrowgate was to us. After the fall of the southern towns, after the burned villages Lior and I had walked through—Saltmere especially, where I'd watched Lior kneel in the ash and pull a dying girl back from the edge with a thread of storm-light, the first deliberate magic they'd worked in seven years—after all that, Harrowgate was supposed to be the place where running stopped. Walls. Garrison. An alliance with teeth. Lior had hesitated at the bargain—we offer protection, you lead our counterstrike—but they'd come anyway, and somewhere between Saltmere and those walls I'd stopped watching them like an asset and started watching them like a person I might, against all professional judgment, care about.

Which meant I'd handed the traitor not just a route but a map of everything I loved.

* * *

They came at moonset, three nights later.

I know because I wasn't asleep. Nobody in Harrowgate really slept anymore; we did this communal shallow dozing punctuated by the changing of watches and the occasional drunk singing in the lower town. I was awake in the courier's loft above the stables, re-inking a map by lamplight, when the dogs started.

Not barking. That's the detail I keep snagging on. Dogs bark at strangers. These dogs made a sound I hadn't heard since I was nine years old and raiders hit my home village—a low, continuous, collective moan, forty animals at once, like the city itself clearing its throat before screaming.

Then the eastern sky caught fire.

Here is what I learned about sieges that night: they don't look like the ballads. They look like logistics. Voss's vanguard didn't batter the gates—they came down the shepherd track above Greyford, the one on my map in my ink in Rennick's memory, and they took the river mills first, then the sluice towers, then the eastern wall's water-gate, which only opened from inside and which someone inside opened for them. Two hundred picked men moving through a sleeping city like ink through water, cutting watch fires, cutting throats, opening gates from within while the army outside poured through the holes.

By the time the bells rang, it was already too late for bells to matter.

I got to the east tower as fast as legs and panic allowed, taking stairs three at a time past soldiers running the other direction with the particular blankness of men headed toward a battle they've already lost. Lior was already awake, already dressed, standing at the window with their hands flat against the stone.

"I felt it," they said. Their voice was strange. Flat. "Before the dogs. Like a pressure change." They turned to me, and storm-grey eyes in lamplight, and I saw something I'd been dreading since the codex: fear. Not of Voss. Of themselves. "Maren, if I call the storm here—with all these people—"

"You won't have to." Even then, even with the city falling, some courier-brained part of me was calculating. "Ferrant will hold the inner keep. We evacuate through the north postern—"

The tower shook.

Not thunder. Something worse: the specific groan of stone deciding it no longer wished to be architecture. Out the window, the great trebuchets of Voss's vanguard were finding their range, and their cargo wasn't rock. It was cages. I saw them land—one, two, three across the lower town—and I saw what crawled out of the wreckage, and my stomach dropped through the floor and kept dropping.

Storm-priests. Captured ones, starved, chained, mad with it. Voss's living artillery. Each cage burst released a man or woman whose power had been compressed like air in a bellows, and when it escaped, it escaped *all at once*. Lightning without direction. Wind without mercy. One of them, a gaunt old man, stood in the crater of his own cage and simply *screamed*, and the scream tore the roof off a granary fifty yards away.

"That's what he does to them," Lior whispered. "That's what waiting does."

Below us, Harrowgate was coming apart. I could hear Ferrant's garrison forming up in the courtyard, could hear his roar—*"Inner keep! Fall back to the inner keep!"*—and I could hear, threading under it, the other sound. The one armies make when they win. Shouting in Voss's northern cadence. Boots on stone in rhythm. And worst, worst of all, ordinary voices—civilian voices—crying out from doorways as the fighting rolled over neighborhoods that had gone to bed thinking themselves safe behind boar-tusked walls.

"We have to go," I said. "North postern. Now."

"The garrison—"

"Ferrant knows his job. Your job is to not be in a cage." I grabbed their sleeve. "Lior. If Voss takes you alive, everything everyone sacrificed is worth nothing. *Move.*"

* * *

We almost made it.

The north postern was chaos—refugees, deserters, a mule that had opinions—and we were thirty yards from the gate when the street ahead lit white and a bolt of wild lightning took the corner house apart. Through the smoke came Compact soldiers, and for one merciful heartbeat I thought rescue, and then I saw their faces, and then I heard the officer's voice, and it was Rennick.

"There," she said, pointing. Not at Voss's men. At Lior. "Take them. Commander wants the singer secured before the city falls—alive, unharmed. Those are the orders."

"In the middle of a siege," I said, stepping between her and Lior, "you're executing a *requisition*?"

"Someone has to make sure the asset survives the rout." Her half-second smile, in firelight. "You'd know all about securing assets, courier. You drew such lovely maps."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. I remember Lior's hand closing on my shoulder—not pulling me away, just anchoring. I remember thinking, with absurd clarity: *she confessed, why would she confess, unless it doesn't matter what I know now.* Which meant Rennick didn't expect either of us to leave Harrowgate.

Which meant neither would her squad.

What happened next happened fast, and I'll tell it plainly because dressing it up would dishonor it. Lior stepped past me. They didn't sing—I'd know; I'd heard them work the storm at the river ambush, and there was song in it, awful and beautiful. This was quieter. They looked at eight armed Compact soldiers loyal to a captain selling her own alliance, and they raised one hand, and the air pressure dropped so hard my ears popped and blood ran from Rennick's nose, and every torch in the street guttered blue.

"Seven years," Lior said, in a voice that wasn't entirely theirs, "I hid from exactly this."

Rennick's squad broke. Not fought—broke, scrambling back from whatever they saw in Lior's face. Rennick herself stood longest, nose bleeding, smiling that smile even as her composure cracked down the middle, and shouted after us, "He'll find you! He's known where you'd run since before you ran!"

I put a crossbow bolt into the postern's lock mechanism rather than test whether she'd follow, and then the gate was behind us and the night was open and Harrowgate was burning at our backs like a sun that had fallen into the wrong sky.

* * *

We walked north through the hills until dawn, and then past dawn, because stopping meant hearing what we could both hear anyway: the sounds of a city dying carrying twenty miles on a cold wind.

At midmorning we finally halted in a shepherd's shelter, dry-stone walls and a leaking roof, and Lior sat down on the floor like a puppet with cut strings.

"Ferrant," they said. "The people in the lower quarter. The ones who left me bread."

"I know."

"The salt fish, Maren. Somebody gave me *salt fish*, and now—" Their breath hitched, and for a moment I saw seven years of exile crack wide open, all of it, the mentor they wouldn't name and the mother's words they wouldn't repeat and the lifetime of practicing smallness, all of it flooding up at once. "This happened because of me. Voss moved early. He moved because I surfaced."

And here is the confession I've carried north like a stone in my boot, the one I couldn't say aloud, not to Lior, not yet:

It didn't happen because of them. It happened because of me.

Because I found a frightened charm-seller in a rainy market and showed them a book and said *you matter*, and meant it, and used it. Because I recruited them for the Compact, and the Compact leaked, and the leak was mine to prevent—my routes, my contacts, my professional paranoia that I'd smothered because trusting felt good and suspicion felt lonely. Because somewhere on the road north I'd let myself believe that bringing Lior to safety and bringing Lior to purpose were the same act, and they aren't, they have never been, and a city full of bread-leaving, salt-fish-offering people had paid the difference.

I recruited the Wolf-Singer. Voss just followed my paperwork.

"Say something," Lior said. "You've got your courier face on. The one where you're composing a report instead of feeling things."

"Habit," I managed.

"What's the report say?"

I looked at them—at the soot in their hair, the exhaustion carved under their eyes, the power still humming faintly in the air around their shoulders like heat off summer stone—and I lied, because some lies are load-bearing.

"That we keep moving north. That Harrowgate falling doesn't end anything—it just makes the next chapter more expensive. That Voss thinks he's won a city, but what he actually won is a longer supply line and a colder winter, and every soldier he leaves in that ruin is a soldier not chasing us." I stood, and offered them my hand, and kept my voice steady through sheer professional spite. "And that the Compact isn't dead. Fractured, bleeding, betrayed—but not dead. There are still cities further north that haven't burned. And there's an abbey you mentioned once, drowned, with answers in it. We're going to reach both."

Lior took my hand. They were shaking. So was I. Neither of us mentioned it.

"Rennick knew the route," they said quietly, at the shelter's doorway, looking back south. "She knew Greyford, she knew the postern. How long, Maren? How much of what brought us here was already sold?"

I didn't answer. I didn't have an answer that wasn't a blade turned inward.

* * *

We reached the relay station at Cold Ford two days later—or what was left of it. The Compact's network, the veins I'd spent years building, was clotting. Half the drop boxes empty. One of my fellow couriers dead in a ditch with his satchel taken. The survivors who filtered in wore the expressions of people doing arithmetic with friends' names.

On the third evening, a boy arrived half-dead off the moor with a message tube strapped to his back, and the seal was Ferrant's, and my hands shook opening it because a seal from a fallen city usually means a last testament.

It wasn't. Ferrant lived—he held the inner keep four days, then fought free with six hundred survivors and was marching them north even now. The message was short, in his blunt field-hand:

*City lost. Rennick fled south before the assault — she was Voss's all along, or bought late, no time to learn which. But hear this, courier: among the papers she abandoned, one bore your seal. Your route. Your hand. Whoever fed her fed her THROUGH YOU. Someone inside the Compact is selling the Wolf-Singer's road, mile by mile, and they know the abbey is next on it.*

I read it twice. Then I folded it, very carefully, along its original creases, the way you handle something that might detonate.

Lior was watching me from across the fire. They'd learned to read my face somewhere on that long road, damn them.

"How bad?" they asked.

I fed the message to the flames and watched Ferrant's careful letters curl and blacken, because some knowledge is safer as smoke.

"Bad," I said. "There's a traitor inside the Compact, and they're not done selling. And Lior—" I met their eyes across the fire, and the truth climbed my throat like floodwater. "They know about Vaelmoor."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the dark moor, a curlew cried, thin and rising, like something calling ahead of a storm.

"So does whoever betrayed us," Lior said softly. "Which means whatever my mother left in that drowned abbey—we're racing for it now. And we don't know who else is running."

That night I took first watch, alone, and let myself finally feel it: the guilt, cold and complete, sitting in my chest like swallowed river stone. Every easy alliance I'd believed in—Compact, cause, comrades—lay behind us in the ashes of Harrowgate. Ahead lay Vaelmoor, and a race against a traitor whose face I couldn't picture, and a woman I'd pulled out of hiding only to paint a target on everything she loved.

I had promised to deliver the Wolf-Singer to destiny.

I was starting to understand that destiny, in my experience, always signed for the package in blood.
