## 15. Wolf-Singer

The storm came when I asked it to, and that was the problem.

For seven years the thunder under my skin had been a caged animal—fed on scraps of suppression, starved into something small and mean and manageable. I had learned to live around it the way you learn to live around a bad knee or a debt: carefully, resentfully, with a whole architecture of small accommodations. And then, on the road north out of Saltmere, with Voss's patrol riding down a column of refugees like wolves through a flock, I had opened my hands and *asked*, and the sky had answered as if it had only been waiting for permission.

It hadn't felt like power. It had felt like relief. That was the part I couldn't stop thinking about afterward, sitting by a cookfire in the ruins of a shepherd's cot with forty strangers watching me the way they'd watch a hearth in winter.

The patrol had been eight riders. Eight men with grey sashes and long lances, herding the column off the road at spear-point, taking what they wanted—the food cart, two mules, a girl whose mother screamed until a soldier backhanded her quiet. I remember thinking, with terrible clarity: *this is what the north looks like now. This is what he builds.* And I remember Maren's hand on my arm, and her voice low: *Not here. Not for this. You'll burn the whole column.*

She was right. The storm I called was precise—I'd like to be able to claim credit for that, but honestly it was more like the lightning knew what I meant than that I aimed it. One fork of it, white and total, took the lead rider's lance and turned it to a blackened splinter. Thunder cracked the air hard enough to drop horses to their knees. And then the rain came down in sheets, hammering the soldiers flat, drowning their torches, turning the road to soup, while eight hundred refugees stood in the downpour and watched me stand there with my arms raised like something out of a woodcut.

The soldiers fled. Of course they fled. Men who signed on with Kaelen Voss to be the boot, not the lightning, have very firm opinions about which side of that arrangement they prefer.

I stood in the rain until it passed, and when I lowered my hands, everyone was looking at me.

I want to tell you what that's like. Not the awe—that part you can imagine. It's the *weight* of it. Forty pairs of eyes, and then eighty, because people came up from the rear of the column to see, and by nightfall it was three hundred, and they were all looking at me the way starving men look at bread. Not with gratitude, exactly. Gratitude has an end to it. This was expectation. This was *need*, and need doesn't thank you; need just keeps opening its mouth.

An old woman pressed a heel of black bread into my hands that first evening and said, "Bless you, singer." I told her my name was Lior. She patted my arm as if I'd said something sweet and foolish, the way you'd humor a child claiming not to be tired.

By morning she'd told the story differently. By the second morning, the lightning had struck all eight riders instead of one lance, and the rain had lasted an hour, and I had stood unburned in the heart of it with a wolf at my side.

There was no wolf.

I checked.

* * *

"You're going to have to say something," Maren said on the third day. We were walking at the head of the column, which was no longer a column but a procession, because word travels faster than feet and refugees from villages ahead were already coming out to meet us, asking questions with their eyes before anyone answered them.

"About what?"

"About the name." She kept her voice level, courier-neutral, but I'd been learning to read her, and there was worry underneath, the way there's water under ice. "They're calling you Wolf-Singer. It's spreading. There are people three villages back who weren't even there, telling the story with details neither of us gave them."

"People embellish."

"Lior." She stopped walking. "A woman in Harrowgate's outer farms drew your face in charcoal on her barn door and put a candle under it. That's not embellishment. That's worship. Worship is fuel, and every army within two hundred miles is going to smell it burning."

"I didn't ask for any of this."

"No," she said. "You didn't. That's what makes it dangerous. A man who seizes a legend is a man you can bargain with. A man a legend lands on—" she shook her head. "Voss will hear about this within the week. And so will the Compact. And both of them will decide they know better than you do what you're for."

I looked back down the road. Three hundred people, maybe four hundred now, walking behind us through country that got more burned the further north we went. An old woman carrying a child that wasn't hers. A smith with his tools strapped to his back, refusing to leave them behind because, he said, someone would need iron where we were going. People who had lost everything except each other, and had somehow decided that I was the thing they'd keep.

"They think I'm going to save them," I said.

"Aren't you?"

"That's not—I'm one person, Maren. I'm one person with weather."

"You're one person who made a patrol of Voss's veterans run," she said. "In the north, right now, that makes you the most hopeful thing anybody's seen in a year. Hope isn't rational. Nobody votes on it. It just—attaches." She glanced sideways at me. "To whoever's standing closest when the impossible happens."

"And if I walk away? Just—turn south and vanish again. Seven years worked fine."

We both let that lie where it fell, because neither of us believed it, and we both knew the other one didn't.

"The last time I hid," I said finally, "people died anyway. My mentor died, and I wasn't even there to—"

"You don't owe them salvation."

"No," I agreed. "But I owe them the truth. And the truth is I don't know what I am yet. I found my mother's journal, Maren. I know what she did at Vaelmoor. I know there might be a way to end this that doesn't end with me on a throne or in a grave, and I don't understand it well enough to promise anyone anything. If they follow me believing I'm their salvation, I'll fail them. Guaranteed. The only question is how badly."

Maren was quiet for a long stretch of road. Then: "Then tell them that."

"Tell four hundred frightened people that their miracle is uncertain?"

"Tell them you're a person," she said. "It's harder. But lies of comfort compound interest, Lior. And the bill always comes due."

* * *

That night we camped in the shell of a waystation inn, its roof half-collapsed, its great hearth somehow still standing—a room built for warmth, filled with cold. I sat near the fire because everyone insisted on giving me the best place, which is another thing about being believed in: you lose the argument about seating arrangements before it starts.

They came to me in ones and twos at first. The old woman with the bread—Sera, her name was—brought a child with a fevered cough and asked, not for medicine, but for my blessing. I gave the child willow bark and honey, which I had, and a blessing, which I improvised, and Sera looked at me afterward with such naked gratitude that I had to leave the fire for a while and stand in the dark and breathe.

Because here is the seduction, and I won't pretend I didn't feel it. When they look at you that way, you feel *larger*. All those years hiding in Vell, performing smallness, flinching from my own reflection—and now here was a mirror held up by four hundred people showing me something vast and necessary. It would be so easy to step into it. To let the wolf be real. To decide that the story they needed was the truth, and shape myself to fit it, and never mind the seams.

And here is the horror, which is the same horror wearing different clothes: the moment you accept the larger self they offer, you stop being a person who helps people and become a symbol that consumes them. Every death after that becomes *your* failure rather than fate's. Every hope becomes a debt you never signed for. My mother knew this—I'd read it in her journal, in her own hand, written a century before I was born: *A prophet is a person who has been conscripted by other people's fear. Never let them make you a banner. Banners get carried into battles started by other hands.*

She wrote prophecies, and she warned against belief in them. I've decided that's the most honest thing anyone in my family ever did.

So I sat back down by the fire, and when the smith came to ask—formally, with the awkward gravity of a practical man doing an impractical thing—whether I would lead them north to Harrowgate, I said no.

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, wind moved through broken rafters.

"No?" he said.

"My name is Lior Thackeray. I'm not a wolf and I don't sing—at least, not anything you'd want to hear twice. What happened on the road was real, and I can't take it back, and I'm not going to pretend I'm less than I am. But I'm not leading anyone anywhere. I'm walking north to Harrowgate because my mother left me something unfinished there, and if some of you want to walk the same direction, then walk together. With me, not behind me. There's a difference, and it matters."

Silence. The particular, awful silence of four hundred people deciding whether to be disappointed.

Then Sera, the old woman, laughed—a dry, cracked sound like a gate hinge. "That's exactly what the Wolf-Singer would say," she announced, to general nodding, and the moment broke into murmurs and settling-in-for-the-night sounds, and I realized with a kind of distant, ringing despair that I had just made it worse.

Denial, it turns out, is indistinguishable from modesty, and modesty is indistinguishable from sanctity. Everything I said to shrink the legend became another verse of it.

Maren found me later at the edge of the camp, staring north at a sky bruised with distant fires. She handed me tea without commentary, which is one of the ten thousand reasons I trust her.

"It went well, I take it," she said.

"They've decided my humility is proof of prophecy. I told them I'm not their leader and they nodded like I'd confirmed scripture."

"That tracks." She drank. "My order has a saying: you can't argue with a story once it's wearing your face."

"I could leave. Tonight. Alone, south, gone."

"You could," Maren agreed. "And tomorrow there'd be four hundred people telling stories about the night the Wolf-Singer tested them and vanished into the dark, and half of them would start walking north anyway, because you pointed the direction. Legends don't need the person, Lior. That's the trap. They run on their own. All you control is whether you're standing inside them or watching them from a distance—which, incidentally, is also how you end up on the wrong side of them."

"So I stay and steer."

"So you stay and steer," she said. "Or you go to Harrowgate fast, hand these people to someone with walls, and get to the Wellspring before Voss gets to you. Because he's heard by now. I'd stake my commission on it. And everything he's done—every priest in chains, every village burned—he's done it chasing the thing you're trying to give away."

I looked north again. The fires were too far to feel the heat, but I could feel something else—faint, at the edge of the storm under my skin, like pressure before a change in weather. The magic itself seemed to lean toward whatever was coming, the way animals lean before an earthquake.

"Sera's right, you know," I said quietly. "About the wolf."

Maren frowned. "There is no wolf. You told me."

"There isn't. But my mother's journal calls the storm-bond 'the wolf at the door'—the old tongue's word for the wild gift, the thing that sings. *Wulf-sangar.* It's not a title they invented, Maren. It's the translation. Someone in that crowd knows the old words—or heard them from someone who does. Which means someone is shaping the story on purpose."

The wind shifted. Down in the valley, the distant fires flickered, and for a heartbeat I thought I saw—not lights. Lanterns. A line of them, moving in formation along the far ridge. Too disciplined for refugees.

Too many for a patrol.

Maren saw them a breath after I did, and her teacup stopped halfway to her mouth. "How long since the story left this camp?"

"Three days."

"Then either that's the Compact come to collect their weapon," she said slowly, setting the cup down, "or Voss sent someone who knows exactly what they're looking at—and neither of us is going to sleep tonight."

The lanterns on the ridge began, very calmly, to descend.
