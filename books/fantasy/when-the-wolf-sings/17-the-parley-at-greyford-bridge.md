## 17. The Parley at Greyford Bridge

There is a particular kind of silence that descends on a bridge when two armies are pretending they haven't brought bows to a conversation. I had never heard it before that morning, and I hope—though the Fates have never once taken my hopes under advisement—I will never hear it again.

Greyford Bridge was old grey granite, wide enough for six carts abreast in its prime and now cracked down the middle like an old promise, spanning a river the color of tarnished pewter. Mist came off the water in slow ribbons, which everyone agreed was atmospheric and nobody said aloud was tactically convenient. On the southern end stood Voss's honor guard: forty soldiers in storm-steel, faces like closed doors. On the northern end stood the Compact's delegation: Maren with her courier's satchel, General Ashk with her scarred jaw and her smile that had never once reached her eyes, and me.

I had argued against this parley from the moment it was proposed. Not because meeting Kaelen Voss was dangerous—everything worth doing was dangerous now—but because of what the Compact wanted from the meeting. Ashk hadn't said the word *martyr*. Nobody ever says the word martyr. They say things like *leverage* and *optics* and *what message we send*, and you only understand later, standing in the wreckage, exactly what message they intended to send and whose body they'd planned to send it with.

"Remember," Ashk murmured as we walked out onto the bridge, our boots loud on wet stone, "he'll try to turn you. That's what this is. He wants the Wolf-Singer more than he wants Harrowgate."

"He can't have me," I said.

"No one can," Maren said quietly, and there was something in her voice—not quite agreement, not quite warning—that I chose not to examine until much later, when examining it wouldn't have changed anything.

The mist thickened at the bridge's center, and out of it came Kaelen Voss.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to afford until recently: he was not what I expected. The broadsheets draw him as a monster—crowned in lightning, teeth filed to points, feasting on captured priests. In person he was a broad, weathered man in his fifties with a soldier's rolling gait and grey threading through a black beard, wearing no crown at all, just a plain circlet of iron that looked like something pulled off a plow horse. He stopped ten paces away. His guards stopped behind him. Ours did the same. And then he looked at me the way you look at a door you've been walking past your whole life before finally noticing it's there.

"Lior Thackeray," he said. My name in his mouth sounded wrong, like a bell rung by someone else's hand. "You have your mother's eyes."

"You didn't know my mother."

"I knew *of* her." He spread his hands, easy, unhurried. A man who had decided the outcome of this conversation before arriving and was simply waiting for the rest of us to catch up. "Serane Thackeray sealed the war at Vaelmoor and bought the north sixty years of quiet. And then the Compact let the free cities rot, and the old bloodlines hoarded their gifts or abandoned them, and the storms came back hungrier every winter. Your mother's sacrifice bought time. Time is spent. I'm here to spend something else."

"Charming," I said. "Do you rehearse these speeches, or does the usurpation come with a speechwriter?"

Maren made a small sound that might have been a cough. Ashk's jaw tightened. Voss smiled—a real one, brief and tired—and for one vertiginous moment I understood how a whole country had followed this man off the edge of the world. He wasn't a monster. He was worse. He was reasonable.

"I was a storm-caller," he said. "Did they tell you that? Before your time. Before the purges. I could pull lightning out of a clear sky." He held up his right hand, and I saw the scarring across the knuckles, pale and old, like frostbite. "I gave most of it up to hold what I'd built. What remains, I use carefully. You've seen what my priests do. I won't pretend otherwise. They are weapons because the Compact left me no other kind. Every army is a failure of imagination, Thackeray. Mine included."

"And if I refuse you?"

"Then the war continues, and you know how it continues. You've walked the burned villages. You've buried what we leave behind." He said it without heat, without cruelty, the way a physician recites symptoms. "Join me, and the free cities open their gates unburned. The priests go home to their families. The storms go unchallenged while we rebuild what two centuries of cowardice broke. Or stand with Ashk, and I take Harrowgate in a season, and everything after that is arithmetic."

"The arithmetic includes people," Maren said sharply.

"People have always been the arithmetic," Voss said. "That's the lesson no one survives learning gently."

And then—because I am apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone, because seven years of hiding taught me nothing except how to hide—I stepped forward, into the space between them, and said:

"Neither."

Voss raised an eyebrow. Behind me, Ashk went very still, the way a hunting dog goes still.

"There's a third option," I said. "You haven't read the last verses. Or you have, and you're afraid of them. The storm doesn't belong to bloodlines—to yours or mine or the priests' you've got chained to your artillery wagons. It belongs to no one. It was never meant to be owned. My mother didn't seal the war, she postponed it, because sealing it would have cost—" My voice caught, traitorous thing, on my mother's name. "It would have cost more than she could pay twice. But it doesn't have to cost anyone, if it's done right. Unbound. All of it. No Wolf-Singer, no storm-thrones, no living artillery. Just weather."

The mist seemed to lean closer.

Voss studied me for a long moment, and I watched him decide—not whether I was mad, but whether mad was useful. "Unbinding," he said at last, tasting the word. "Every gift, gone? Yours too?"

"Mine first."

"And the north goes undefended against whatever comes after. Because something always comes after." He shook his head slowly. "You'd disarm us all in a forest full of wolves."

"Then we stop being wolves."

"That," said Kaelen Voss, "is the dream of every child who has ever watched a war, and the reason wars continue." But something in his face had shifted—a hairline crack in the certainty, a man hearing, for the first time in decades, an ending he hadn't written himself. "I'll consider it. Genuinely. Which is more than the Compact offered you, I suspect. Ask them sometime what Ashk's council voted on three nights ago. Ask them what the word *contingency* meant in the minutes."

"Ashk," I said, without turning around. "What's he talking about?"

Silence. The pewter river slid beneath us. And then Ashk said, in a voice flat as a blade laid on a table, "Nothing you need worry about."

Which was, of course, the worst possible answer.

* * *

Here is what happened next, reconstructed from the testimony of survivors, because I saw almost none of it as it happened. I saw only the results.

A crossbow bolt came out of the mist from the northern bank—from *behind* our own lines—and took Voss's standard-bearer through the throat. Then another, and another, six or eight of them in a ragged volley, and the Compact guards on the bridge turned—not toward the arrows but toward Voss himself, blades coming up, and I understood in one cold flooding instant what the contingency had been.

Kill the usurper at his own parley. Let him die reaching for peace, or let him die under a flag of truce—it didn't matter which story they told afterward. Dead men make excellent ink. Martyrs make better banners. A Voss who died at Greyford Bridge would rally every free city faster than a year of victories, and his army, leaderless, would shatter or scatter or come apart in succession squabbles the Compact could pick apart at leisure. And if Lior Thackeray happened to die in the crossfire—well. A dead Wolf-Singer was even safer than a live one. A dead prophecy couldn't choose a third path.

They hadn't planned for me to say *neither* out loud. Plans rarely survive contact with someone stubborn enough to ruin them.

"Down!" Maren slammed into me, shoulder to my ribs, and we went down together behind the bridge's low parapet as the world came apart. Steel rang on steel. Someone screamed—the high animal scream of a man discovering what his body looks like from the inside. Voss's guards collapsed around him, a wall of storm-steel, and bolts thudded into shields and flesh alike.

And through the gap between two dying men, I saw Kaelen Voss.

He'd gone down hard—one bolt in the meat of his thigh, another glancing off his pauldron—and the guards who should have covered his retreat were falling, cut down by arrows from their own side of the river, from men wearing Compact blue who had infiltrated the honor guard's flanking positions during the night. The math was simple and obscene. Forty seconds, maybe less, and the most dangerous man in the north would bleed out on a bridge built by people who'd believed in peace.

Every part of me that had walked those burned villages, that had healed a dying child with a thread of storm-light and sworn *never again to choose a side over a person*—every part of me screamed to stay down. To let it happen. He was a tyrant. He was the reason the roads were lined with ash. The world would be simpler, cleaner, kinder without him, and no jury in any surviving city would convict me for closing my eyes.

But he had looked at me like a door he'd never noticed. And he had listened. And underneath the iron circlet and the arithmetic and the armies, some part of him had wanted—genuinely, hungrily, the way starving men want bread—an ending he hadn't written himself.

If he died here, the third path died with him, and the war would be won by whoever killed best, and my mother's journal might as well be burned for kindling.

"Oh, hells," I said, and stood up.

The storm was already in me—I hadn't called it; seven years of suppression and a month of practice had worn the door thin, and it came through the gap like a dog that has learned to open latches. Lightning walked down my arms. The mist lit blue-white, every raindrop a small furious star, and I put out my hands toward the knot of Compact assassins swarming the bridge's center and *sang*—not the killing song, not the one that levels streets, but the other one, the one my mother's journal had hinted at, the pressure-song, the shove.

Thunder detonated overhead. Not on them. Over them. A single colossal crack of sound directly above the bridge that picked up four armed men like dolls and set them down hard against the parapets, weapons scattering, eardrums surely ringing, and the rest of them froze—because there is a difference between courage in the dark and courage under a sky that has just spoken your name.

"ENOUGH!" I shouted, and my voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, with the storm, and every fighter on both ends of that bridge heard it in their back teeth. "Enough. Drop them. *Drop them.*"

Steel clattered on granite. One by one. The mist drifted, torn by wind I hadn't asked for, and in the sudden strange quiet I knelt beside Kaelen Voss, who was bleeding steadily into the seams of the bridge, and pressed my palm against the bolt in his thigh.

"This will hurt," I said.

"It already does," he said, through gritted teeth, and laughed—actually laughed, a raw bark of a sound. "The Wolf-Singer, saving the wolf. Your mother would be proud. Or furious. Possibly both."

"She usually managed both."

Storm-light ran from my palm into the wound, chasing the bolt-head, knitting what it touched. Small magic, careful magic, deliberate—my second true healing, my third deliberate working in seven years, and it cost me what it always cost, a cold draining ache like winter moving into the bones. Around us, the Compact delegation stood in a ring of profound institutional horror. Ashk's face had gone the color of the river. Maren was staring at me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to, because reading it would have required deciding what to feel about it, and I was busy keeping a usurper alive.

"You understand," Voss said quietly, so only I could hear, as the light faded from my hands and the wound closed to an ugly pink seam, "what you've just done."

"I've saved your life."

"You've ended yourself. Ashk can't trust you. My officers will call it sorcery and treachery in the same breath. Both sides will swear you were the other side's creature by nightfall." He gripped my wrist, hard, with the strength of a man determined to live long enough to be inconvenient. "You've burned every bridge but this one. And this one's on fire."

"Yes," I said. "I'd noticed."

He let go. Struggled upright against his guards, waved off help, straightened his ruined coat with the automatic dignity of a man who had been dressing for disaster his entire career. Across the bridge, Ashk found her voice.

"Thackeray. Step away from him. Now. That is an order from the Compact council—"

"The Compact council voted to murder a man under truce," I said, and my voice carried, storm-touched, to every ear on the riverbank, and I let it carry, because if I was going to lose everything I was at least going to lose it honestly. "I heard the thunder. So did all of you. There's the message you wanted to send. Send it."

Ashk's hand moved toward her sword and stopped—not because of me, but because of Maren, who had stepped, quietly and irrevocably, to stand between the general and me. Courier, spy, recruiter, friend. Whatever she was, she was choosing it in front of witnesses.

"We're done here," Voss announced to the bridge at large, limping backward toward the south bank, his surviving guards forming up around him. But at the edge of the mist he paused and looked back at me, and there was no triumph in his face, and no gratitude either. Only a grim, appraising respect, and something almost like grief.

"Third path," he said. "Prove it exists, Thackeray. Because as of today, neither of us has an army that will follow it—and the ones who still have armies are coming for both of us."

Then the mist took him, and the bridge was ours, littered with the dead of both allegiances, and the river slid on beneath us, indifferent, pewter-grey, going somewhere else entirely.

Maren touched my arm. Her fingers were shaking.

"They'll come for you tonight," she said. "Ashk won't risk a trial. Not after what you said, not with witnesses."

"I know."

"Do you have a plan?"

I looked south, where the enemy I had just saved was limping home to think about mercy. I looked north, where the allies I had just condemned were already composing the story of my treachery. Somewhere beyond both, beneath the drowned abbey of Vaelmoor, the Wellspring waited with my mother's final verse and a price I hadn't yet told anyone about.

"No," I said. "But I know where I'm going. And for the first time in seven years, I'm not running there."

Behind us, on the far bank, a horn sounded—long, low, repeated. Not Voss's signal horns. Not the Compact's either.

Something else was coming to Greyford Bridge. Something that belonged to no army I knew.

And it was moving fast.
