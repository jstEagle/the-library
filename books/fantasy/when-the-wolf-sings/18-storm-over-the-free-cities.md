## 18. Storm Over the Free Cities

### Odile Vane

I have stood at a lot of war councils in my life, and I can tell you the exact moment each one went wrong. It's always the same moment: when someone says the word *unconditional*.

The council chamber of Harrowgate had been a granary once, back before the Compact needed walls instead of wheat. Now it held forty people who could not agree on the color of the sky. General Ashkane of the Riverlands stood at one end of the long table with her fists planted like fence posts, insisting that surrender terms were on offer from Voss and that surrender terms were better than ash. Across from her, the Harrowgate garrison commander—a red-faced man named Pell whose entire strategic philosophy appeared to be *louder*—was demanding a sortie. A sortie. Against an army of sixty thousand with storm-priests chained to its baggage train.

"We fight," Pell said, for the eleventh time.

"We die," Ashkane corrected, for the eleventh time. "There's a difference, Commander. One of them leaves something behind."

"And what does surrender leave behind?" This came from a woman I didn't know, a delegate from the Salt Marshes, still wearing travel mud. "Voss doesn't garrison cities, he harvests them. He takes the mages and burns the rest."

"He takes the mages," someone else said, "and the rest of us live."

I stood by the door where sergeants stand, which is to say I stood by the door and listened and was ignored, which is the traditional privilege of my rank. And I watched Lior Thackeray, who sat at the far end of the table like a person attending a funeral for someone they weren't sure they'd liked.

They'd changed since Greyford Bridge. Not visibly—same storm-grey eyes, same face that looked like it had been carved by someone who got bored halfway through—but there was a stillness in them now that hadn't been there before. Before the bridge, Lior had flinched at their own name. Now they sat inside it like it was a coat that fit badly but fit.

Pell was shouting again. Ashkane was shouting back. The Salt Marshes delegate had produced a map and was stabbing it. And Lior looked up, caught my eye across the room—the exiled watch-sergeant and the exiled storm-caller, the two most irrelevant people in the building—and gave me a small, exhausted smile, as if to say: *look what we've become.*

Then they stood up, and the room didn't quiet, because rooms never do until you make them.

"Both of you are right," Lior said, and their voice did something I'd only heard once before, at the river crossing, when the thunder came down out of a clear sky. It wasn't loud. It was just *inescapable*, the way weather is inescapable. "We fight, we lose. We surrender, we're harvested. Both true. So let's stop pretending those are the only two doors."

The chamber quieted in stages, like rain tapering off.

"There's a third door," Lior said. "It's under Vaelmoor."

### Maren Quill

Here's what nobody tells you about being a courier for the Free Cities Compact: half the job is carrying messages, and the other half is standing in rooms full of powerful people watching them fail to read them.

I'd carried the intelligence north from the drowned abbey myself—Voss's route, his supply lines, the disposition of his captured priests—and I'd watched the Compact leadership do exactly nothing useful with any of it, because doing something useful would have required agreeing, and agreement in the Compact was a currency rarer than storm-magic. By the time the councils concluded anything, the conclusion was usually obsolete.

So when Lior stood up in the granary chamber and said *there's a third door under Vaelmoor*, I felt two things simultaneously, and I've learned not to trust either one.

The first was hope. Real hope, ugly and sudden, like a fish hook behind the sternum. Because I'd seen the journal. I'd sat with Lior in the flooded cloisters while they read their dead mother's handwriting by lantern-light, and I'd understood, before Lior said it aloud, what the unbinding meant. Storm-magic wasn't weather. It was blood. A lineage running back a thousand years through every priest Voss had in chains and every child born with static under their skin, all of it rooted in one place, the Wellspring beneath the abbey where the first singers had cut the bargain. Cut it loose, and the war ended—not won, not lost, *ended*. No more weaponized children. No more inquisitors hunting bloodlines. No more prophecies.

No more Wolf-Singer, either. But that part I tried not to think about, and mostly failed.

The second thing I felt was fear, because I knew these people, and I knew what they would hear. They wouldn't hear *the war ends*. They'd hear *a weapon exists, and here is someone willing to use it*.

General Ashkane leaned forward first. Of course she did. She was the smartest person at that table and the most dangerous for exactly that reason.

"The Wellspring," she said. "You're telling me it's real."

"I'm telling you my mother died sealing a war there," Lior said. "I'm telling you the journal describes how she did it. And I'm telling you the same method works in the other direction." A pause. "Mostly."

"*Mostly,*" repeated Pell, delighted, as though he'd caught a liar.

"The journal uses the word 'price' eleven times," Lior said flatly. "I've counted. It doesn't specify. My mother paid it. I'm prepared to find out what mine is." They looked around the table, and I saw them doing the thing they hated—spending themselves, spending their grief, offering their own throat to a room full of strangers because no one else would. "But I need two things. Time, because the unbinding is a song, and songs take as long as they take. And a distraction big enough that Voss sends his army somewhere that isn't Vaelmoor."

"You want us to give you a battle," Ashkane said.

"I want you to give me an hour," Lior said. "What shape it comes in is your problem."

And then—because I was watching for it—I saw the exact moment the Compact did what the Compact always does. Saw it move through the room like damp through plaster. Pell heard *distraction* and thought *sortie, glory*. The Salt Marshes delegate heard *Wellspring* and thought *leverage, a seat at whatever table comes after*. Ashkane heard everything, weighed everything, and asked the only question that mattered:

"If you succeed, Thackeray—if you pull the magic out of every bloodline in the world—what happens to the priests in Voss's chains? To the ones already bound to his engines?"

The room went very quiet.

"They live," Lior said. "That's the whole point. They wake up ordinary. Ordinary people can be freed. Bound weapons can't."

"And if you fail?"

"Then I'll be dead," Lior said, "and you'll be having this conversation with someone less qualified."

Ashkane looked at them for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, hard note, the laugh of a woman conceding a point she resented—and turned to the map.

"All right," she said. "Show us the abbey."

### Kaelen Voss

My father taught me chess before he taught me to read, and the lesson he taught with it was this: the player who needs to win has already lost. Play so that losing costs you nothing, and victory will come looking for you.

I have built an empire on that principle. I do not need the free cities. I need what is *in* the free cities—the grain, the harbors, the mage-children the Compact is too squeamish to use. If the cities burn, the grain burns with them, and I am a patient man, but I am not a stupid one. So I offered terms. Generous ones. Surrender the mages, keep your lives. Every city that accepted is currently governed by its own mayor and paying me taxes it used to waste on walls.

Harrowgate refuses. Harrowgate has found itself a Wolf-Singer, and hope, in my experience, is the one commodity people will pay any price to keep.

I stood on the ridge above the Greywater with my map-table open and the wind pulling at my cloak, and I reviewed the situation the way my father taught me, asking not *how do I win* but *what does losing cost me*.

Sixty thousand men. Forty-one bound priests, yoked to the storm-engines, fed and watered and dosed with restraint-salts like the valuable animals they are. Supply lines reaching back four hundred miles to territories that produce nothing but obedience. And ahead of me, a fortress-city whose defenders outnumber my engineers ten to one but whose unity I could snap between two fingers.

Because that was the joke of it. The Compact had finally, after twenty years, assembled an alliance capable of stopping me—and they had assembled it around a single person. Around Lior Thackeray.

One person is not an alliance. One person is a hinge. And hinges exist to be broken.

"My lord." Captain Sera, my shadow, my left hand, arrived at my shoulder with the morning's dispatches. "Scouts report Compact forces moving south along the Greywater. Light cavalry, screening formations. They're maneuvering."

"Toward the bridge at Greyford?"

"Toward the fords, yes."

I studied the map. It was almost elegant, what they were attempting—a feint at the fords to draw my center while the bulk of their strength shifted east. Almost elegant. But the Compact had never in its history executed a feint without one faction informing on another, and I had bought my informant in Harrowgate months ago, before the abbey, before the parley, before any of this mattered.

"They mean to fix us here," I said, "while their singer runs north. To Vaelmoor."

Sera didn't ask how I knew. That was why she was Sera. "Do we split the army? Send a column north?"

"No." I moved a single piece on the map—not a knight, not a rook; a pawn, one of the expendable ones. "The singer goes to Vaelmoor because the singer must. Whatever ritual they intend, they cannot perform it while my army lives. They know that. Which means they will come back to us—or send their friends against us—and either way, they leave the Wellspring undefended long enough for us to take it." I tapped the map, over the drowned abbey, over the deep place where the old magic pooled like blood in a wound. "I don't need to kill the Wolf-Singer, Captain. I need to arrive second, at a place they've already opened."

"And if the ritual kills them first?"

"Then the Wellspring stands unguarded by anyone who understands it, and I have a century of captured texts and forty-one living priests to teach me." I smiled, and felt the smile do what smiles do in front of soldiers, which is reassure them that their commander is not afraid. I was not afraid. Fear is a need, and I had burned mine out years ago, in the purges, in the years when I was nobody's heir and everybody's problem. "Break camp. We march on Harrowgate at dawn. And Sera—double the guard on the priests. If the Compact grows desperate, they'll try to take them back, and desperate people are the only kind I respect."

She saluted and went. I stayed on the ridge a while longer, looking north, past the smoke of the burned villages my own army had left like signatures, toward the grey line of hills where a drowned abbey kept its secrets.

Play so that losing costs you nothing.

The trouble with my father's wisdom—which I had learned too late to thank him for, since I had killed him for less than I kill most men—was that it assumed the board stayed still. It assumed the rules held.

Somewhere north of me, a person I had never met was preparing to change the rules. To take the game off the table entirely. Magic out of bloodlines. Priests into people. Empires built on the ownership of power, dissolved overnight—including mine, including the Compact's, including every crown and creed that had ever traded in the currency of inherited gift.

A lesser ambition would have angered me.

Instead, standing in the wind above the Greywater, I felt something I had not felt since I was young and powerless and furious about it.

I wanted to see it done.

Not by them. Never by them. But I wanted to watch the world after, and I was honest enough, alone on my ridge, to admit that if the price of seeing it was everything I'd built—

Well. Losing had never cost me anything I couldn't rebuild.

Except, perhaps, this once.

### Lior Thackeray

They gave me a room in the citadel with a bed I didn't sleep in and a window facing north.

Maren found me there at what the bells insisted was dawn but the sky insisted was opinion. She brought tea, which neither of us drank, and the journal, which neither of us needed—we both had it memorized by now, my mother's cipher unspooling in our heads like a second heartbeat.

"The council voted," she said. "Ashkane holds the Greywater with everything that can march. Pell gets his sortie—he's going to die gloriously at the fords, or not, depending on whether Ashkane's timing holds. The Salt Marshes are evacuating civilians east." She paused. "You get your hour. Maybe two, if the gods are drunk."

"That's more than Mother got," I said.

"Lior." Maren set the tea down and crossed her arms, and I braced, because I knew that posture. It was her *I'm about to say the true thing* posture, and Maren's true things were worse than other people's lies. "Tell me the price."

"The journal doesn't—"

"Don't." Quiet. Terrible. "I've read it too. Eleven times the word 'price.' Eleven times no specification. And I've watched you for two hundred miles counting verses and practicing breaths, and I have watched you say goodbye to things, Lior. To me. To Odile's letters. You pack like someone leaving forever and you think nobody notices."

I looked at the window. North. The hills were invisible in the rain, which felt appropriate.

"If I tell you," I said finally, "you'll try to stop me."

"Yes."

"And if you stop me, Voss wins, and every mage-child in his chains stays a weapon until someone worse than him finds the Wellspring. There's always someone worse, Maren. I've met enough of them." I made myself look at her. Her eyes were wet and furious and I loved her for it, which is the cruelest trick love plays. "The price is mine to pay. That's not concealment. That's arithmetic."

"It's cowardice wearing arithmetic's coat."

"Probably," I admitted. "Both things can be true. Most true things can wear several coats."

She laughed despite herself—a broken little sound—and then she crossed the room and hugged me, hard, the way you hug someone you're angry at, and I held on, and outside the rain kept falling on a city that had maybe two days left to be a city at all.

When she pulled back, her courier's face was back on, the professional mask, but her voice wasn't quite steady. "Odile's riding up from the river tonight. She'll meet us at Vaelmoor."

"She shouldn't."

"She's coming anyway. You know how she is about orders."

"I do," I said. "It's how I survived seven years in a city with no magic and a watch sergeant with no imagination."

Maren went to the door. Stopped. Didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "whatever the price turns out to be—I'd have paid it differently. Slower. With more arguments. But the same direction." A breath. "In case nobody tells you that at the end."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with the rain and the journal and the last verse, the one my mother had written in letters that shook:

*The song ends where the singer ends. Sing anyway.*

Downstairs, the war was making its arrangements. Hinges and pawns and feints, empires and alliances, everyone playing for the board.

I closed the journal, shouldered my pack, and went down to join them—one last charm-seller, heading north to sell the only thing I had left.

The storm was waiting under my skin. It had been waiting for twenty-two years.

By tomorrow night, one of us would be finished.
