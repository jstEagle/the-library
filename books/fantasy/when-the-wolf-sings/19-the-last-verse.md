## 19. The Last Verse

The journal's last page was written in a hand that shook.

I had read my mother's words for three days by then—her careful cipher unraveling under the archivist's lamp like thread pulled from old embroidery—and every page before this one had been steady. Measured. A woman building a road for her child to walk, stone by stone, with all the patience of someone who knew exactly how long the journey would take and how much it would cost.

Then the last page, and her hand shook.

*The unbinding is not a song of power,* she wrote. *It is a song of surrender. The Wellspring does not accept commands; it accepts payment. To unbind the storm from bloodlines, the singer must give up their own gift entirely—the thread that ties them to it, cut at the source. And the Wellspring may not distinguish between the gift and the singer. It may take both.*

*There is no way to know which until you sing.*

*I would spare you this if I could. I could not even spare myself. But if you are reading this, then you have already chosen to come here, and I know whose child you are, and I know you will have weighed it as I did—not against your life, but against everyone else's.*

*Forgive me for leaving you a choice instead of an answer. It was the only inheritance I had left to give.*

I sat in the archivist's tower with the page open on my knees while rain ticked against the shutters, and I found, to my considerable surprise, that I was not afraid.

Not in the way I'd expected to be. Not the cold animal fear that had lived in my chest for seven years, the one that woke me at night convinced my skin was about to split open and let the storm out. This was something quieter. Something almost like relief.

Because here was the truth I'd been circling since Vell: I had never once, in seven years of hiding, managed to actually *stop*. The magic hummed under my skin whether I sang or not. It leaked when I dreamed. It flared when I panicked, and it had leveled a street because a woman I loved was grabbed by the wrist. My mother's seal was fraying, Voss was marching on the free cities with priests chained to his war-wagons like oxen, and every day I carried the gift was another day the storm carried *me*.

The journal offered me what no one else had: an ending. Not victory, exactly. Not survival, necessarily. An ending.

"Is it bad news?" Odile asked from the doorway.

She had a way of appearing when I was least fit for company, which I suspected was deliberate. She leaned against the frame with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd worn for fifteen years—the one that said she already knew the answer and was only giving me the courtesy of lying first.

"It's terms and conditions," I said. "You know how I feel about those."

"Lior."

"It says the unbinding requires my gift." I turned the page toward her, then thought better of it and turned it back. Some things shouldn't be read over a friend's shoulder. "And possibly the rest of me. The Wellspring might not see a difference between the two."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Rain filled the silence. When she finally spoke, her voice had the flatness she used on recruits who were about to do something stupid and needed to understand, precisely, how stupid.

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"I'm going to try," I said. "There's a difference. Legally speaking."

"There isn't, and you know it." She came into the room properly now, sat down across from me, and put her elbows on her knees the way she did before hard conversations. "Seven years I've known you. Seven years of watching you flinch from doorways and count exits and sell copper bells to people who didn't need them, all so you could stay small enough to survive. And now you've finally found a way to stop running, and it's off a cliff."

"When you put it that way it sounds reckless."

"How would you put it?"

I considered. "Necessary with style."

"That's worse."

"Odile." I closed the journal. "Voss has storm-priests harnessed to his engines. Children, some of them. The Compact wants to win the war, Maren wants to win the war, Voss wants to own the war—and every single one of them needs the storm to keep existing so they have something to fight over. There's exactly one person alive who can end the argument instead of winning it, and she's sitting in this chair eating biscuits she stole from your ration pack."

"You stole my biscuits."

"The point stands."

She looked at me for a long time, and I watched her decide things behind her eyes—the way she always did, sorting duty from friendship from exhaustion, filing each where it belonged. Fifteen years of practice. She'd been a watch sergeant in a city without magic, protecting a charm-seller who wasn't, and she'd done it knowing full well it would end badly, because some people choose their doomed causes early and stick with them.

"If you don't come back," she said at last, "I'm going to be very annoyed with you."

"Noted."

"And if you do come back without your gift—" Her jaw worked. "You'll be ordinary. You'll be *safe*, Lior. Do you understand what that means? No one will hunt you. No prophecy will want you. You could plant cabbages."

"I hate cabbages."

"You could learn."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet around the edges, and Odile pretended very hard not to notice, which was its own kind of kindness.

* * *

Maren was harder.

Not because I loved her more—I'd stopped ranking those things somewhere on the road north, somewhere between the burned village and the child with storm-light knitting her fever shut—but because Maren noticed everything, and noticing everything meant I couldn't hide anything, except that hiding things from Maren was precisely the assignment.

We walked the walls of the refugee camp at dusk, because she liked to walk when she thought and I liked to walk when I lied, and the two activities apparently paired well.

"The Compact's council ratified the plan," she said. "Harrowgate holds the eastern gate. Odile's people screen the southern road. Everything depends on timing."

"Mm."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you agree with me in a voice like porridge because you're not listening." She stopped at the wall's edge and looked out over the camp—ten thousand people, cookfires smoking, children chasing a dog between the tents—and her face did the thing it did when she was carrying more than she could say. "Lior. What did the last page say?"

"Nothing useful."

"You're lying."

"Badly, yes. I'm aware. I'm a charm-seller, not a spy."

She turned to face me fully, and the wind coming off the plains pushed her hair across her face, and I thought: *remember this. File it away. Take it down into the dark with you, because it's the last ordinary evening you get.*

"The codex was written by your mother," Maren said. "Every verse. Every date. She knew about the bell, the scholar, the inquisitor, the tunnels—she mapped your whole life a century before you lived it. So tell me the last page doesn't also map this. Tell me it doesn't end where I think it ends."

I could have lied better. I should have. Seven years of practice, and I fumbled it because she was looking at me like that.

"It ends at the Wellspring," I said. "That's all you need to know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

Her hands curled into fists at her sides. "If you walk into that water planning to die—"

"Maren."

"—then you're doing exactly what the Compact wanted, exactly what Voss wanted, exactly what the *prophecy* wanted, and you told me once that the whole point of you was refusing to be what other people decided—"

"*Maren.*"

She stopped. Below us, the dog had caught whatever it was chasing, and the children were cheering, and the world went on being ordinary and enormous and worth the price, whichever price it turned out to be.

"I'm not planning to die," I said quietly. "I'm planning to sing. Those aren't the same thing, and I refuse to let anyone—including me—pretend otherwise. But I won't stand in front of ten thousand people and promise them a tomorrow I can't guarantee. That's what Voss does. That's what the Compact does. Somebody in this story has to be honest about the arithmetic."

"And the arithmetic says?"

"The arithmetic says the storm goes back into the sky where it belongs, and nobody's blood buys it ever again. Mine or anyone's." I shrugged. "It's a good trade. I never liked the gift much anyway. It's damp, it's loud, and it has terrible taste in men—look at Voss."

That got a broken half-laugh out of her, which had been the goal. Maren cried rarely and hated crying, so I made her laugh instead, the way she made me eat when I forgot, the way Odile stood guard when I slept. We had built a whole language of substitutions over these months. I was about to leave her fluent in a dead tongue.

She stepped forward and took my face in both hands, and for a moment neither of us performed anything at all.

"Come back," she said. "Ordinary. Cabbage-farming. I don't care. Come back."

"I'll consider the cabbages."

"Lior."

"I heard you," I said. "I hear everything. It's a curse."

* * *

I left before dawn, which was cowardly, and I knew it was cowardly, and I did it anyway, because goodbyes said in daylight become promises, and I couldn't afford any more promises I might not keep.

Except Odile was waiting at the south gate, because of course she was. She'd known me for fifteen years. She'd counted on me sneaking out the way she counted on rain in autumn.

"You're a menace," I said.

"I'm a sergeant. We anticipate." She handed me a bundle—bread, cheese, a flask, and, wrapped separately with suspicious care, a small copper bell. "For luck. It worked once."

"It rang once and destroyed a street."

"Worked," she repeated, in the tone that ended arguments.

We stood there in the grey light with the army stirring behind us and the long road north unspooling ahead, and there was so much I wanted to say that I said none of it. That was the trick of it, I was learning. Love expressed through concealment. My mother had written a hundred pages of prophecy rather than say *goodbye* outright. Odile had guarded a fugitive for fifteen years rather than ask why. Maren had recruited a weapon rather than admit she'd found a friend. We were all of us cowards in the same direction, and it looked, from certain angles, exactly like courage.

"Tell them I went ahead to scout," I said.

"They'll know that's nonsense."

"Tell them anyway. Nonsense is traditional, at moments like this."

"Yes, sir," she said dryly, and then, quieter: "Lior. Whatever's down there—whatever the water asks—you don't owe it your whole self. Give it the gift. Keep the rest. Fight for the rest."

"I'll fight for the rest," I promised, and it was the truest thing I'd said in days, and also possibly a lie, and I let her believe whichever version kept her standing straight.

I walked north alone. Behind me the camp woke in stages—cooksmoke, voices, a bugle somewhere—and above me the clouds were gathering, low and heavy and familiar, the way they always gathered when I stopped holding them back.

The storm knew. It had always known. It rose to meet me like an old dog hearing footsteps at the gate, and I let it, and we went down together toward Vaelmoor, toward the drowned abbey, toward the water that was waiting to hear the last verse my mother wrote and the first one that would be entirely mine.

The rain began as I crossed the ridge, soft at first, then steady, then singing.

I sang back.
