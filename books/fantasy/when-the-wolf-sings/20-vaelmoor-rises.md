## 20. Vaelmoor Rises

The drowned abbey of Vaelmoor had been drowning for three hundred years, which struck me, as I waded through its gates, as a very committed sort of ruin. Most things give up after a decade underwater. Not Vaelmoor. The arches still stood in black rows out in the flood, their tops breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping whales, and the bell tower leaned at an angle that defied both gravity and, I suspected, several gods. The water around it was the color of old pewter, and it did not ripple when the rain struck it.

It rippled when *I* struck it. Every step I took sent rings spreading outward, slow and deliberate, as if the lake were taking my measure.

"Fine," I told it. "I'm not much to look at either."

The storm above me had stopped being weather somewhere on the road north. It was a column now, a rotating dark thing with lightning walking its edges like a nervous animal in a cage, and every free city for fifty miles could see it. Which was rather the point. Maren would have wanted me to be subtle. Odile would have wanted me to be sensible. Both of them were back at the ridge with the refugees and the fractured remains of the Compact, holding a line against an army with nothing but ditches, desperation, and whatever prayers hadn't been confiscated by Voss's priests.

Subtle and sensible had both died at Greyford Bridge. What was left was me, and a song, and a door at the bottom of a lake.

The journal rode against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, though I no longer needed to read it. I had read the last verse so many times the words had worn grooves in me, the way water wears stone—slowly, patiently, and all the way through. *The unbinding asks the singer's whole voice. What is given to the deep is not returned.* My mother's hand, steady even there, even at the end. She had written it knowing she would never sing it herself. Knowing her child would.

Mothers, I have decided, should not be allowed to leave instructions.

I waded out along the tops of the collapsed cloister wall, following the map in my memory, until I came to the place where the water went from pewter to black. The Wellspring. It didn't look like much. A square of carved stone, maybe ten feet across, sunk below the flood line, with steps leading down into darkness. Old words ringed the rim in the family cipher—the private tongue my mother had used to write prophecies in a book that would one day find its way into the hands of a nervous scholar with kind eyes and excellent taste in contraband.

I could read them now. *Here the storm was bound. Here it may be loosed.*

Or unbound entirely. That was the part the prophecy had never said plainly, because prophecy is a coward's art—it shows you the fork in the road but never tells you which path has the cliff at the end of it. My mother's journal had been braver. Two paths, she wrote. Take up the storm, become what Voss had become, and the war begins again with better weapons. Or sever it—not destroy it, nothing that old can be destroyed, but cut it loose from bloodlines, from inheritance, from the whole rotten idea that power should run in families like a curse. No more storm-callers born. No more children singing thunder before they can say their own names. No more mothers dying to seal wars their children will inherit.

And no more gift. No more storm under my skin. No more anything, really, except an ordinary life in a world that would have no use for me—which, if I'm honest, was the only future I'd ever actually wanted. The universe had simply insisted on making me pay retail for it.

The first horn sounded from the east while I stood at the rim with my boots in the water.

Then the second horn. Then all of them at once, a braided rope of sound pulling tight across the valley, and beneath it a sound I knew from salt-and-ashes villages and burned fields: the deep, arrhythmic boom of captured storm-priests firing on command, artillery with heartbeats.

The battle had begun without me, which was fine. Battles do that. They're very independent that way.

I crouched and put my palm flat against the stone rim. The Well answered immediately—a pressure against my skin like a hand returning a grip, vast and slow and older than language. It had been waiting three hundred years for someone with the right blood to come down those stairs and take hold of it. Everyone assumed someone would, eventually. Voss assumed. The Compact assumed. The whole bloody world was built on the assumption that the storm belonged to whoever was strong enough to carry it.

That was the assumption I had come to kill.

I pulled off my boots. It seemed important, somehow, to go down as myself and not as a soldier. I tied the journal's cord tighter, tucked it inside my shirt, and started down the steps.

* * *

The water did not get colder as I descended. That was the first impossible thing.

The second was the light. There shouldn't have been any—twenty feet down, forty, sixty—but the dark itself glowed faintly, a grey-silver shimmer like moonlight remembering itself, and in that light I could see the stairs widen into a hall, a flooded nave with pillars going up forever into blackness, and between the pillars: threads.

That is the only word I have for them. Lines of pale fire running through the water in every direction, converging, all of them, on a single point ahead—thousands upon thousands of filaments of storm-light stretched taut across centuries, humming a note too low to hear and too deep to ignore. Each thread ran out into the world somewhere, into a bloodline, into a child yet unborn, into Kaelen Voss and his chained priests and every soul who had ever carried the sky under their ribs.

Every thread passed through this room. Every thread was anchored here.

And at the center, coiled around the anchor point like a ring on a finger, hung a shape of woven light that made my chest ache before my mind caught up to why: it looked like a lullaby. Like something half-remembered from a kitchen with a leaky roof and a woman humming over the stove. She had added her own thread here, my mother. Her life, braided into the seal, spending itself slowly across three hundred—no. Across seventeen years. She hadn't sealed the war a century ago. She had sealed it the night I was born, or the night she died, and everything since—the prophecy, the codex, the trail of breadcrumbs through a magic-free city—had been her voice, still singing, long after her lungs gave out.

"Hello, Mother," I said. My voice came out strange in the water, flat and close. "You'll forgive me if I don't do this exactly as written."

Because that was the other thing I'd understood on the road, in the rain, singing back at the sky like a madwoman: the last verse had two readings, and everyone—the Compact, the codex's scholars, probably my mother herself—assumed the price meant death. *What is given to the deep is not returned.* But I had spent seven years selling charms in a city without magic, and if there was one thing a charm-seller knows, it is that the value of a thing is not the same as the cost of it. The verse asked for my whole voice. My gift. The storm in my blood, surrendered willingly, completely, forever.

Not necessarily my life. Unless I was stubborn enough to make the two the same thing.

I intended to be exactly that stubborn.

I reached the anchor point and the threads converged around me, thousands of lines of light crossing within arm's reach, and when I lifted my hand they stirred toward my fingers like cats recognizing the person who feeds them. Somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms and stone, the battle's rhythm changed—and then changed again, closer, faster. Something was coming down through the abbey. Something with horns behind it.

I began to sing.

* * *

There are no words for the unbinding in any living tongue; there barely need to be. The cipher is mostly vowels. You open your throat and the storm opens with you, and for a moment—I will always remember the moment—we were one continuous thing, me and the rain and the lightning walking its cage, and I understood with perfect clarity what Voss could never understand:

Power is not diminished by being shared. Only hoarding diminishes it. Hoarding is the only thing that ever has.

The first thread snapped at the fourth phrase. I felt it let go somewhere north—a storm-priest in Voss's chains suddenly standing empty-handed in the mud, weeping, alive, free—and the note of it rang down the hall and came back to me changed. The second thread went. The tenth. The hundredth. Each release came back to me as a small bright grief, because each one was also mine, was also my childhood, was also the rite of inheritance and the mentor I had killed and the seven gray years of pretending to be nothing. I sang them all loose. I sang my mother's braid loose, gently, apologizing to it, and felt her presence in the weave lean against me once—brief, warm, unmistakable—before letting go.

She had trusted me to choose. That had been the whole prophecy, start to finish. Not a command. An inheritance of choice.

I was halfway through the great braid—the main trunk, the deep root, the part that would mean no storm-caller ever born again—when the doors at the top of the stairwell burst open with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and Kaelen Voss came down into the water.

He came alone. That surprised me, though it shouldn't have. Men like him trust no one to witness what matters. His armor was scorched. His crown—he actually wore it now, iron and thin, the crown of a man who had decided necessity was a metal worth forging—was crooked. And his hands were raised, and around them the remaining threads of the Well bent toward him like iron filings toward a magnet, and his face when he saw me was not rage.

It was recognition. That was the worst of it.

"Don't," he said. Water distorted his voice into something almost human. "Girl. Thackeray. Stop."

"Come to stop me?" I kept singing between the words. It's possible. Everything is possible when you're the instrument and the player both. "You're late. Your army's losing interest in you even now. Feel it? Every priest you chained just put their hands down."

"I felt it." He waded closer, and the threads followed him, eager, starving for a bearer, and I understood with a lurch how easy it would be—how the Well *wanted* him, wanted anyone, wanted to keep existing inside people rather than exist in no one. "That's why I'm here. You're killing it. The finest thing our blood ever made, and you're—"

"Our blood." I laughed, and the laugh came out as thunder, and the whole flooded nave shook with it. "Kaelen. Sweet, deluded man. There is no *our*. That's the entire point. It was never ours. We were just the cage it lived in, and cages aren't owners, no matter how much the bird sings for them."

"You'd make us ordinary." He was close enough now that I could see his eyes, and they were mine, storm-grey, the same eyes, because the same storm looked out of everyone it ever inhabited. "Ordinary. Weak. In a world with men like me in it—"

"In a world with men like you in it," I said, "ordinary is the most radical thing left. Yes."

He lunged for the anchor.

I had wondered, on the road, what I would do in that moment—whether I had it in me to fight him, whether transcendence survived contact with a man trying to steal your inheritance mid-song. I needn't have worried. I didn't fight him. I simply changed key.

The threads turned from him like a crowd turning its back. He grasped at the anchor and his hands closed on nothing, because the anchor was no longer an anchor; it was a doorway, and doorways only open outward. He grabbed for the braid and the braid poured through his fingers like rain through a fist, and Kaelen Voss—usurper, artillery-keeper, corruption wearing a crown of necessity—stood in the flooding light of the Well and screamed at the sky that was leaving him.

I sang the last thread.

Mine.

It did not hurt. That was the strangest thing. Seven years I had feared the storm under my skin, fought it, starved it, buried it, and in the end it left the way a guest leaves who truly loves the house: quietly, gratefully, taking nothing but memories. The hum under my skin that had been my whole life—my curse, my exile, my secret, my legend—unwound itself note by note, and I sang it out, and the water took it, and the deep said *it is not returned*, exactly as written, and I found I was still breathing afterward. Still standing. Still, improbably, me.

The light faded. The threads were gone—or rather, everywhere, dispersed into the air and rain and soil of the world, ownerless, harmless as weather. The nave went dark and ordinary and full of water, like any drowned place.

Above me, distant and ragged, the guns of two armies fell silent in the same minute, because there was suddenly nothing in the valley to fire them for.

Voss knelt in the water where the anchor had been, hands empty, mouth open, listening to a silence where his god used to be. He looked smaller than his statues. He looked, in fact, exactly like what he had spent his whole life terrified of becoming.

An ordinary man, in the rain, with nothing left to command.

"Look at it this way," I told him, hauling myself up the first step on legs that shook like a novice's. My voice sounded strange—small, unmagnified, merely human. "Now you get to find out who you are without it. Same as the rest of us."

I climbed toward the grey light of the surface, and the climb was long, and hard, and I was glad of it—glad of the weight in my arms and the burn in my lungs and the cold that finally, finally felt like weather instead of warning. At the top of the stairs the rain met me, soft and directionless and blessedly stupid, just water falling out of a sky that no longer knew my name.

I stood in the ruins of Vaelmoor with nothing under my skin but skin, and far off across the valley I heard the armies falter, and men begin—to my lasting astonishment—to walk home.

Maren was going to kill me. Assuming she could find anything left of me to kill.

I took one step, and then another, and the world did not end, and I discovered I had absolutely no idea what happened next.
