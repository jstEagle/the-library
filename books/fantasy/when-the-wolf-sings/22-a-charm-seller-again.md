## 22. A Charm-Seller Again

There is a particular honesty in selling things that don't work.

I didn't understand that for most of my life. For seven years I sold charms in Vell that were supposed to be harmless—copper bells and knotted cords and little glass beads that caught the light—and every one of them was a lie twice over. A lie because they had no power. And a lie because I did, and the whole stall was a performance of powerlessness, a stage set built to keep the storm under my skin invisible.

Now my charms are still harmless. But they're honest about it.

"Does it work?" asks a boy of perhaps nine, holding up one of my wind-chimes, a cluster of tin fish on fishing line, the kind that clatters when the harbor breeze comes off the Sound.

"No," I tell him. "It makes noise when there's wind. That's all it does."

He considers this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My gran bought a luck-charm from a man on Corvess Street. She said it worked."

"He was lying or she was lucky. Luck's free. It just doesn't come with a receipt."

The boy laughs, decides he likes me, and buys the chime with three coppers counted out one at a time from a fist that has clearly held them for days. He runs off toward the fish market, tin fish jangling, and I watch him go and feel something I'm still learning to name. Not joy, exactly. Joy implies surprise. This is more like a settled thing, a stone set into a wall where a hole used to be.

The stall sits at the edge of Tanner's Row, which no longer smells entirely of tanning—half the row burned in what the city records now call, with magnificent understatement, *the Incident*. The rebuilt half has new timber, pale as bone against the old black beams, and my awning is new canvas stretched over a frame Odile helped me cut before she rode north again. The city gave me the pitch free for a year. Some clerk decided a person who leveled a street and then helped rebuild it had earned a discount on rent. Vell has always been pragmatic about its miracles.

That's another thing I'm learning: how to be known.

Not famous—I could write a whole chapter on why fame is just fear wearing nicer clothes—but *known*. The neighbors know my name. The woman who sells eels two stalls down knows I take my tea bitter. The watch-sergeant who walks Tanner's Row knows to nod at me and not ask questions, though the questions he isn't asking have changed. Seven years ago the unasked question was *what are you hiding?* Now it's only *how did you sleep?* It turns out that being a person among people is a trade you can fall out of practice with, like a language. I am relearning it slowly, badly, one conversation at a time.

* * *

Maren comes on a Thursday, because Maren does everything on Thursdays—it's the Compact courier's superstition, and after everything, I've stopped teasing her about it. She comes down Tanner's Row in her travel leathers with road dust on her boots and a satchel full of letters, and she doesn't say hello so much as arrive, dropping onto the stool beside my stall like she owns it, the way she has since the tunnels beneath Vell.

"You look terrible," she says.

"I've been up since dawn making honest trinkets. Honesty is exhausting."

"Honesty." She picks up a glass bead, holds it to the light, puts it down. "You're selling these without any enchantment at all?"

"Without any enchantment possible. You know that better than anyone."

"I know." And her voice goes quiet in that way it does when she's thinking about the Wellspring, about the night the sky over Vaelmoor tore open and every storm-priest in Kaelen Voss's chains felt their gift simply... leave them. About the way I came walking out of the flooded cloisters with empty hands and an empty chest, hollowed out like a gourd. "I know better than anyone," she says. "That's rather the problem."

"Maren."

"I brought letters." She thrusts the satchel at me before I can say anything else, and I let her, because we've developed this dance over the months—she arrives carrying something heavy, I refuse to let her put it down alone, and between us we manage not to drop it.

Odile writes every month. Her letters are masterpieces of compression; she was a sergeant too long to waste words. She tells me the north is thawing—not metaphorically, although also metaphorically. With Voss powerless and his coalition dissolved, the storm-bound provinces are doing the slow, ungainly work of governing themselves, which mostly looks like arguing in assembly halls and occasionally throwing turnips. She's taken a post as a caravan guard, which she describes as "quiet," which from Odile means nobody has died recently. Last month she wrote: *The pass above Greyford is clear for the first time in living memory. I stood in it a while. Thought you'd want to know the sky can be ordinary.* I've read that letter eleven times. I keep meaning to tell her I read it eleven times, and I keep not finding the words, which is absurd, because I once sang a storm apart. Words were never the hard part until they mattered.

There's a letter today, and I open it right there at the stall while Maren pretends to inventory my stock.

*Astrid—* (she never calls me Lior anymore; she knew me first as the charm-seller, and names are clothes, and she likes me best in the old ones) *—the Compact archive has finished cataloguing the codex. They asked if I wanted a copy of the final page for you. There is nothing to copy. It remains blank. The archivist says blank pages in prophetic works usually mean the prophecy is unfinished. I think it means it was never a prophecy at all. Come north in spring. The sky here is ordinary and I would share it with you. —O.*

Maren watches me fold it. "She's right, you know. About the last page."

"It's blank because I ended it. The story ends where I stopped writing it."

"That's not how prophecies work."

"That's exactly how prophecies work when you find out your mother wrote the whole thing by hand in a family cipher to give you a map instead of a cage." I say it lightly, and mostly mean it. My mother spent her life sealing a war and her death guiding a child, and the least I owe her is to stop treating her love like a trap. But some mornings I wake and reach for the storm the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore, and those mornings the blank page feels less like freedom and more like a sentence with the ending torn off. Maren knows this. She has the grace to let me have both truths at once, which may be the rarest thing anyone has ever given me.

"The archivist wants to display the codex," Maren says. "Public reading room. Under glass."

"Let them. It's just a book now. A very dramatic book with excellent penmanship."

"People will make pilgrimages. People already do—they come through Harrowgate asking where the Wolf-Singer lives."

"What do you tell them?"

She smiles, crooked and tired and fond. "I tell them the Wolf-Singer is dead. Died at Vaelmoor doing something brave and stupid. Then I sell them directions to a good inn."

"And the truth?"

"The truth keeps a stall on Tanner's Row and charges fair prices for tin fish." She stands, shoulders her satchel, pauses. "Thursday next week?"

"I'll be here."

"You'd better be." And then, because we have survived ambushes and betrayals and the literal end of magic, she permits herself the enormous vulnerability of squeezing my shoulder once before she walks away down the row, dust rising behind her boots, going north to carry other people's words, which is what couriers do, and what friends do too, if you think about it.

* * *

Evenings, I close the stall and walk to the harbor.

Vell is rebuilding itself the way cities do—unevenly, argumentatively, with great energy and poor planning. The burned quarter has scaffolding like bones. Children play in the rubble and have named the largest standing wall *the Thunder Wall*, because children metabolize catastrophe faster than anyone and turn it into a playground. I walked past it the first week after I came back and had to sit down on a crate for ten minutes. Now I can walk past. Progress is measured in strange units.

At the harbor there's a bench facing the Sound, and I sit on it with tea from the cart by the fish market, bitter, the way the eel-seller knows I like it. The water does what water does. Gulls conduct their endless corrupt negotiations over scraps. Across the water the sky builds clouds and spends them and builds more, and none of it answers me, and none of it needs to. That took longest to learn—that I could stand under weather and not be its instrument, that rain could be rain instead of a summons.

Some nights I miss it. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently my trade now. I miss the storm the way you miss a person you loved who was also, frankly, dangerous—the way you miss the whole of them, including the parts that hurt you. The power was mine and I was its, and there were nights under Voss's shadow when calling lightning down on his columns felt like the truest thing I'd ever done. I don't regret giving it up. Regret requires believing there was a better choice available, and there wasn't; my mother's journal was clear, and the Wellspring doesn't negotiate. But grief and regret are different animals, and the grief still visits. It sits next to me on the bench some evenings like an old comrade, and we watch the water together, and eventually it leaves, and I stay.

What I don't miss—this surprises people when I say it—is the hiding. Seven years of performing smallness, of flinching from lantern-light, of loving people sideways so they couldn't be leverage against me. I thought concealment was safety. It was just a smaller cell. The exile I fled north from wasn't the banishment from home; it was the banishment from myself, and I carried the prison with me the whole way, disguised as caution.

The strange arithmetic of my life is that I lost everything I inherited and gained everything I chose. No gift, no lineage, no destiny, no place in either world—the human world that fears magic and the magical world that worships it. Both shut their doors. And somehow, in the wreckage of all that belonging, I built the cheap kind instead. The kind you make yourself, out of Thursdays and bitter tea and letters from a caravan guard and a boy who buys wind-chimes with counted-out coppers. Prophesied belonging would have been a throne. Chosen belonging is a bench by the harbor, and I know now which one I'd fight for.

* * *

It rains the evening the stranger comes, because of course it does. Weather in Vell has no sense of narrative timing, which I respect.

I'm packing up the stall, canvas folded, tin fish wrapped in cloth, when I notice him at the edge of the awning's shadow—a traveler, road-worn, cloak dark with rain. Northerner, by the build and the boots. He stands the way Odile stands, the way soldiers stand when they've forgotten how to do anything else.

"You're the charm-seller," he says. Not a question. Word has traveled, then, as word does. *The one who came back. The one who sells things that don't work.*

"That's me. Everything's packed except my honesty, and that's not for sale."

He doesn't smile. He pulls something from inside his cloak—a bundle of oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age—and sets it on my counter with the care you use for something that has already broken once.

"My village sent me," he says. "North of Greyford. We found this when the floods went down after—" he gestures vaguely upward, at the sky, at everything, "—after that night. It was buried under the abbey road. The elders said it should come to you."

I look at the bundle and my hands go cold in a way that has nothing to do with the rain. I know that knot. My mother tied knots like that; it's a Thackeray seal-cord, the kind you use on things meant to stay closed until the right hands open them.

"There's no one left to send it to," I say quietly. "Whatever it is."

"The elders disagree." He steps back into the rain. "They say the last page isn't blank everywhere. They say there are copies."

The codex is under glass in Harrowgate. Its final verse has been blank for months. I told Maren it was blank because I ended the story.

But my mother was a woman who sealed a war with her life, and I am my mother's child, and I have learned—one war, one Wellspring, one hollowed-out chest too late—that she never once did anything with only one meaning.

The stranger disappears into the rain. I stand there a long moment, the oilcloth parcel sitting on my counter, heavier than anything I've sold all day.

Then I untie the knot.
