## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The candle had burned down to its last finger of wax when Theo Halloran reached the general's cowardice.

He knew it by the smell before he read a word—the guild's vials gave off different odors depending on what they held. Grief was brine and iron. Love came sweet, almost rotten, like pears left too long in a warm room. Cowardice smelled of wet copper, and this one, decanted from some battlefield outside Halden's Reach and sealed in green glass, filled the counting room with the stench of a man drowning in his own mouth.

*Lot 447. One memory, intact. Source: General Aldous Crane. Contents: the retreat at Marrow Ford, unedited. Purchased: yes. Price paid: a barony's worth of silence.* Theo dipped his quill and wrote it into the ledger in his careful clerk's hand, each letter identical to the ten thousand letters that had come before it. *Condition: excellent. The seller wept only once.*

That last line was not procedure. It was habit. The guildmaster liked the ledgers to carry small cruelties in their margins—proof, he said, that the trade was honest. People always wept once. They wept when they understood that forgetting was possible, and they never wept twice.

Theo set Lot 447 aside and took up the next tray.

The widow's first love arrived in a vial no larger than his thumb, rose-colored, glowing faintly like something still alive. He catalogued it with less detachment than he intended. *Lot 448. One memory, intact. Source: Maren Voss, widow of Salt Row. Contents: a boy named Elio; a summer; a stolen apricot; the first time she was ever looked at as if she mattered. Sold voluntarily to settle her late husband's debts.* Price: three months of bread and rent, forgiven.

He imagined the widow waking tomorrow in her narrow bed, feeling the shape of a summer missing from her life the way an amputee feels weather in a gone limb. She would not know what she had lost. That was the mercy of the trade and its obscenity, both at once: the wound healed invisible.

The candle guttered. Theo turned the page.

Three trays remained from the day's intake—a ferryman's terror of deep water, sold cheap; a midwife's knowledge of forty births, purchased by a barren duchess who wanted to *have felt it*, even secondhand; and at the bottom of the cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth behind a false panel that existed in no inventory but his own—

Theo stopped writing.

He looked at the door of the counting room. It was locked. He listened for Brech, the night enforcer, making his rounds on the floor above: heavy boots, pause, heavy boots, pause. The house breathed around him the way old houses did, settling timbers and the tick of cooling wax, and beneath all of it the faint chorus of four thousand memories humming in their glass like a hive kept against its will.

He drew out the oilcloth package.

Inside lay a single vial, and inside the vial, light—not glow but *light*, silver and moving, like moonlight trapped in water. He had carried it for eleven days now. Eleven nights of telling himself he would hand it up to the guildmaster in the morning, log it, price it, be done with it. Eleven mornings of failing.

Because he had checked the ledger of acquisitions, the private one, and found the entry written in the guildmaster's own slanted hand: *Lot 439. One fragment. Source: Her Majesty, Queen Sable Vessia, deceased. Recovered from the body during preparation. Contents unknown. DO NOT PRICE.*

Do not price meant do not sell. Do not sell meant priceless. And priceless meant the guildmaster intended to keep it, or eat it—he had begun consuming his finest inventory that autumn, claiming clarity, though everyone below the third floor said it was only hunger for the lives he no longer had—and either way it would vanish into that gray appetite forever.

So Theo had stolen it. Not from the queen—from the guild. A theft within a theft, which somehow made it feel almost honest.

He had touched it exactly once, the night he took it. Clerk's curiosity. He had uncorked the vial for the length of one heartbeat, and in that heartbeat he had seen: rain on black stone. A door, tall and plain, standing alone in a wall that belonged to no building. And words spoken in a dead woman's voice, patient as tide: *the door stands while my name is spoken.*

Then he had corked it, shaking, and hidden it, and told himself he had seen nothing.

The boots above paused longer than usual.

Theo wrapped the vial, returned it behind the false panel, and was two lines into Lot 451 when the bell at the guildhouse gate began to ring—not the trade bell, which rang twice and stopped, but the alarm, rung hard and continuous, the sound of trouble arriving in official form.

He heard the gate open. Heard voices, and then a voice above the voices, tinny and flat, the voice of a town crier reading from paper because paper could not be argued with.

*"By decree of the Regent's Council, in the name of the Crown of Vessia: all persons gifted in the taking, holding, or altering of memory are hereby declared enemies of the realm. All such persons shall present themselves for registration within seven days. Registered persons will be—"*

The crier's voice hitched. Even hired men hesitated at certain sentences.

*"—examined. Unregistered persons will be sought, seized, and burned. So it is decreed. So it will be done."*

The bell stopped. In the ringing silence after, Theo sat very still with his pen above the ledger, and a bead of ink gathered at the nib and fell, blotting the word *intact* into a small black sun.

Burned.

Not taxed, not exiled, not pressed into the crown's service. Burned, the way they burned ledgers with errors in them. The way you destroyed a thing whose existence embarrassed the arithmetic.

Above him, Brech's boots had gone silent. Then they started again, faster now, toward the stairs, and Theo understood with a cold sliding feeling that the enforcer was going to the guildmaster, and that whatever happened next would happen quickly.

He looked down at his hands, at the ink stains worked so deep into his knuckles they seemed part of the skin, and thought about invisibility. For six years he had been the man nobody saw—the clerk, the quill, the boy who balanced books while better men traded in souls. The guild had sold him that invisibility, he realized now, the way it sold everything else. *Be unnoticed. Be furniture.* There was probably a lot number for it somewhere, a vial of other people's disregard that the guildmaster dosed him with every quarter, billed against his wages. His safety had never been his. It had been inventory, rented, and tonight the crown had canceled the lease.

They would burn the gifted. But first they would burn the records—or seize them—and either way, the ledgers would tell them everything. Every name. Every seller. Every clerk.

And somewhere under the false panel, a dead queen's fragment waited with rain on black stone and a door that stood only while her name was spoken.

*My name,* Theo thought, and the thought arrived whole, fully formed, as if it had been waiting in him for eleven days: *they are unmaking her country, and she hid a way out of it inside a memory, and I am holding it in a candlelit basement like a thief.*

Because he was a thief. He saw that clearly at last. He had been one since the moment he touched the vial—no, since the moment he decided not to give it back. Whatever the guild had made of him, that choice had been his own.

Boots on the stairs now. Two sets. Fast.

Theo blew out the candle, slid the ledger shut, and put his palm flat against the false panel—one heartbeat, two—feeling through the wood the faint silver warmth of the only door out of Vessia.

The door handle of the counting room turned.

* * *

Brech came in with a lit lantern and no announcement, which was how Theo knew things were bad. Enforcers announced themselves precisely so that no one could claim later they hadn't been warned.

"Guildmaster wants the vault opened," Brech said. "All of it. Crown inspectors at dawn." His eyes moved across the dark counting room, over the trays, over Theo, and stopped on the blot of ink drying on the open ledger. "You'll inventory nothing tonight. You'll pack."

"Pack," Theo repeated.

"The guildmaster says the trade moves south tonight. Carts, crates, everything priced." Brech's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "And everything *unpriced* goes in the burn crate."

Unpriced. The word landed in Theo's chest like a coin dropped down a well.

He thought of the oilcloth, the false panel, the silver light moving behind glass. He thought of the crier's sentence, the hitch in the hired man's voice. Seven days. The guild would not last one.

"Yes," Theo heard himself say, in his careful clerk's voice, each syllable identical to ten thousand before it. "Of course. The burn crate."

Brech nodded and went out.

Theo stood alone in the dark with the lantern-light fading under the door, and began, very calmly, to plan a theft.
