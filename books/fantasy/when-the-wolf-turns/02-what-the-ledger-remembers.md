## 2. What the Ledger Remembers

The guildhouse burned the way everything in Vessia burned now—slowly at first, then all at once, as if the fire had only been waiting for permission.

Theo heard it before he saw it: the groan of timber giving up its arguments, the wet hiss where flames met the canal water seeping through the cellar. He was three floors down, in the vault, with a candle stub and the queen's fragment pressed against his palm like a coin too hot to spend.

Above him, boots. Many boots.

He had been deciphering the fragment for six hours. It was not a memory in the ordinary sense—not a scene, not a face—but a *coordinates*, folded into sensation. That was the trick of royal memories: they were written in architecture rather than language. The dead queen had stored her secret the way she'd stored her jewels, in settings designed to be worn, not read.

* * *

*The memory speaks:*

*I am standing in a corridor of white stone, though I have never seen this corridor, and I know—I know the way you know your own heartbeat—that it lies beneath the flooded cathedral on Sable Street, below the third arch, where the water runs green with old glass. There is a door there. Not a door of wood. A door of* *unmaking**,* *the last door, and it opens only for one condition: that my name be spoken aloud, somewhere in the world, by someone who means it.*

*My name is Maren. Say it. Someone must say it.*

*They are coming to take even that from me.*

* * *

Theo read it again by candlelight, lips moving, and felt the shape of it settle into him like a swallowed stone. A door out of Vessia. Out of the country that was being erased from maps, from trade routes, from the tongues of foreign merchants who had begun, politely, to pretend they'd never heard of it.

The crown wasn't just burning the gifted. The crown was unwriting the country itself, name by name, door by door—and the queen, before they killed her, had hidden the one exit in the safest vault in Vessia: inside Theo Halloran's own skull, planted there by a thief's careless hand during a routine purchase four years ago. His theft. Unknowing, but theft nonetheless, and the ledger proved it. Entry 4,471. *One fragment, provenance royal, seller deceased, buyer: T. Halloran (error).*

An error. His whole life now hung on a clerk's error.

The ceiling cracked above him. Plaster snowed onto the ledger table, onto rows of neat columns cataloguing other people's yesterdays. He grabbed the master ledger—the real one, the one that would hang them all—and shoved it into his coat, because some part of him understood, even then, what he would only understand later: that the book itself was the crime. Not any single entry. The whole idea of it.

Then the stairs came down.

* * *

He went out through the water gate, into the canal, because enforcers watched doors and the drowned city had always kept its back entrances wet. The cold closed over him like a ledger closing. He swam with one arm clutching the book to his chest and surfaced behind the tanneries, where the smoke made its own weather, and stood in the shallows watching the guildhouse burn with something he refused to call grief.

Twelve years he had worked there. Twelve years balancing columns of stolen childhoods, and he had told himself it was honest work, clean work, work for people who *needed* forgetting—the widow who could not survive her first love, the soldier who could not survive what he'd done. He had believed the guild was a mercy with a price tag.

Now he stood in the reeking shallows and did the arithmetic he'd always refused to do.

Every memory the guild sold had to come from somewhere. Every forgetting had a donor. And the donors—the ones whose memories were *harvested*, not surrendered, the ones dragged in from debtor prisons and workhouses and orphanages, the ones who woke up lighter and poorer and unable to say why—

Theo had processed their entries. He had assigned them lot numbers. He had once catalogued an eight-year-old's memory of her mother's voice under the heading *Domestic Ambience, Minor Key, Lot 2210,* and gone home and slept soundly.

The guild hadn't bought and sold memories. It had bought and sold *people*, one piece at a time, and Theo had been the man who kept the books tidy so no one had to look at what the numbers meant.

He was still standing there when the hand closed on his collar.

* * *

The guildmaster looked worse than mad. He looked *eaten*. Master Corvus had always been gaunt, but now his skin sat on his skull like wet paper, and his eyes had the particular shine of a man who had tasted his own inventory and found it better than food. They said he'd begun taking doses from the vault after the queen died. Grief, someone whispered. Others said appetite. With Corvus it had never been necessary to choose.

"Theo." His voice was warm. That was the horror of it; it was always warm. "You have something of mine."

"You sold me nothing," Theo said. "I checked."

"I sold you *invisibility*." Corvus smiled, and his teeth seemed too many. "Twelve years of being beneath notice, of walking past soldiers like furniture. You thought that was your nature. It was a product, boy. My best one. And products—" he held out a gray, trembling hand— "*return to the vendor.*"

Soldiers moved through the smoke behind him, patient as tide. Somewhere the guildhouse groaned and shed another floor.

"Give me the fragment," Corvus said, "and I will give you freedom. Real freedom. A new name, papers, passage east. You've seen the entries. You know I can arrange anything."

And Theo did know. He knew exactly what Corvus arranged, and for whom, and what it cost, because he had written it all down in a fair hand with good margins. Freedom from Corvus was just another entry in the ledger, another person converted into inventory.

"No," Theo said.

Corvus sighed, genuinely sorrowful, and that was worst of all. "Then you'll forgive me. I'm told the harvest doesn't hurt. I've never asked anyone."

Theo ran.

* * *

He ran through streets he suddenly could not trust, past walls where the crown's new proclamation was being pasted over older proclamations, the glue freezing in the night air:

*BY ORDER OF THE REGENT: ALL PERSONS OF THE GIFT ARE TO PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION. THE UNREGISTERED WILL BE BURNED. VESSIA HAS NO NEED OF THIEVES.*

*Vessia.* He said the word to himself as he ran, testing it, and felt the queen's fragment stir in him like a fish turning in dark water.

*A door exists while her name is spoken somewhere,* the memory insisted. *Someone must say it.*

Below Sable Street, then. Below the third arch of the flooded cathedral, where the water ran green with old glass. He could be there before dawn if the bridges held, if the enforcers were still busy with the fire, if—

If he could get past the wards.

He didn't know yet how he knew about the wards. The knowledge arrived the way the coordinates had, leaking from the queen's fragment into his own mind, and with it came the price tag, because royal memories were written in architecture and architecture always specified materials.

*One memory per ward,* the fragment murmured. *The door does not accept coin.*

Theo stopped running. Stood in the frozen mud between the tanneries and the river, holding a stolen ledger full of stolen lives against his chest, and counted what he owned.

His mother's face—he had not looked at it in years, but it was there, folded small.

His first theft—a crust of bread, age seven, and the baker's astonished kindness when he was caught.

His name. *Theo.* Given by a woman whose face was already filed away, attached to a history he had never questioned because he'd never needed to.

And deeper, underneath all of it, sealed and unlabeled: the truth of who he was before the guild found him. He could feel it the way you feel a locked room in a house you've lived in forever.

Behind him, horns. Ahead, the cathedral bells, drowned and silent these ten years, but still hanging in their flooded tower like questions no one had answered.

He started running again, toward the water.

Behind him, unhurried, certain, the guildmaster followed—and far off across the rooftops, moving with a soldier's terrible efficiency toward the same address, came torchlight that was not fire at all, but a badge: Captain Ilsa Vey, carrying orders to burn what she would find there, and a purchased memory of her own that had begun, lately, to whisper back.
