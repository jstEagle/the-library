## 3. A Country Being Unmade

Theo counted the wards before he could make himself count what they would cost.

Seven stood between the cathedral's drowned threshold and the door itself, seven circles of the queen's pale script sunk into the flooded nave like mooring rings for a ship that had not yet been built. The water reached his knees, black and still as ink in a well, and each ward glowed faintly beneath it—a drowned constellation, a map of a country that existed now only in the negative space of maps.

He had learned that much from the fragment. Not all of it—the queen's memory was a locked room and he held only one key—but enough. *The door stands where her name is spoken. The wards stand where she is forgotten.* To pass through them, he would have to unremember his way forward, spending himself coin by coin, and the ledger-keeper in him could not stop doing the arithmetic.

*Seven wards. What do I have worth seven?*

The answer sat in the hollow of him like a stone: everything. His mother's face. His first theft, the taste of stolen sugar and shame. His name—*Halloran*, which his mother had said meant nothing, which he had written at the top of ten thousand guild pages until it felt like a brand rather than a gift.

He waded toward the first ward.

Behind him, far off across the drowned district, something burned with a light too steady to be accidental. The guildhouse, probably. Or what remained of it. He thought of Master Corvane sitting in the dark among his eaten years, and felt—not grief, exactly. Grief required that you mourn what had been good.

His fingers found the ward's edge beneath the water. It hummed against his skin like a struck tuning fork, patient, indifferent.

"Mother," he said aloud, to hear it once more while the words still meant anything. "Your face is—" 

And then he stopped saying it, because saying it made it easier to spend, and he did not want this to be easy.

He let go.

The memory went out of him the way heat leaves a cupped hand—not torn, not stolen, but *released*, sliding down his arm and into the ward until the circle blazed white through the black water. For half a breath he still knew there had been something to lose. Then even the shape of the loss dissolved, and Theo Halloran knelt in a flooded cathedral weeping without knowing why, and the way ahead lay open by one ward's width.

Six remained.

He stood, wrung out his sleeves, and walked on.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Vey had burned eleven gifted persons in nine days, and every night she dreamed her brother alive again.

Not the true Tomas—the thin, coughing boy who'd died of lungrot at fourteen—but the Tomas she had bought, the purchased memory that lived behind her left eye like a coal that refused to cool. In it they were children on the sea wall at Harrowgate, and he was laughing at gulls, and the wind smelled of tar and salt, and none of it had ever happened in quite the order or brightness she remembered, because the memory-broker who sold it to her had polished it the way brokers polish everything. She had paid three months' wages for a brother who laughed. Some nights she thought it was the only honest transaction she had ever made.

Now she rode through the drowned quarter with six soldiers behind her and the proclamation heavy in her saddlebag, the one bearing the seal that made burning people legal arithmetic. Ahead, the old cathedral rose from the floodwater like the ribcage of some god-sized animal, its spire snapped off, its bells long looted. And around it, wrongness. The water moved backward there. She had watched it for a full minute before she trusted her eyes: waves crawling upstream, drawn toward the broken doors as if the building were drinking.

"The gifted one came this way," said Sergeant Brannick, squinting through the drizzle. "Fisherman saw a man wading in alone. Mad, walking into that."

*Mad,* Ilsa thought, *or desperate enough to pay any price.* She knew the price such places asked. Everyone who dealt in memories knew, and everyone pretended otherwise.

She should have reported the sighting. Should have ridden back for the pyre-wagons. Instead she found herself dismounting, boots splashing into water that should not flow the direction it flowed, and telling Brannick, "Hold here. I'll scout."

"You shouldn't go in alone, Captain."

"No," she agreed. "I shouldn't." She went in anyway.

* * *

Inside, the nave stretched up into darkness, and Theo stood waist-deep now before the fourth ward, holding his own name in his mouth like a live coal.

He had spent the second on his first theft—the sugar, the shopkeeper's startled kindness, the shame that had taught him how easily things could be taken—and the third on the sound of rain on the guildhouse roof during his first night there, when he'd believed he'd been rescued. That one he regretted least.

But the name. A man could survive without a mother's face; men did it every day, walking past strangers who loved them. But a name was the handle by which the world lifted you. Without it—

*Without it, no ledger can find you,* whispered the fragment of the queen, cool as water in his skull. *That is why I hid mine here.*

"Ilsa Vey."

The voice came from the broken doors, and Theo turned, heart hammering, to see a woman in crown colors wading toward him alone, sword sheathed, hand raised in something that was almost surrender and almost warning.

"That's my name," the captain said. "I'm giving it to you so you know I know what you're about to do. I've read the old texts. I know the wards take names last, if you let them."

"How—" Theo's throat was dry though he stood in water. "How do you know that?"

"Because I bought a memory once," Ilsa Vey said, and her voice did not break, but it bent, the way a mast bends. "From your guild. And the broker threw in fragments of other stock, careless, like chaff with the grain. I have pieces of the queen in my head, ledger-keeper. Pieces she hid in people, hoping someone would carry them somewhere safe." Her jaw tightened. "I carried them for six years without knowing what they were. Now I know, and my crown wants me to burn you for knowing it."

The soldiers' torchlight flickered at the doorway behind her, distant as stars.

"So," she said, drawing her sword—not at him, but laying it flat across both palms, an offering, an abdication. "Finish what you came to do. And when the wolf turns—and he will turn, he's already turning—know that at least one hound bit back."

Theo looked at her, and at the ward glowing beneath him, and understood that terror had quietly become something else sometime in the last hundred yards, the way fear becomes resolve becomes simply *forward*. He pressed his hand to the fourth ward.

"My name," he said. "My name is—"

The water took it before he could finish, and the fourth circle flared, and the fifth waited beyond it, patient as debt.

Behind them, at the cathedral doors, torches multiplied—and with them came a voice Theo's body remembered even though his mind no longer owned the memory attached to it. A voice wet and hungry and familiar, calling his name into the dark.

Calling a name he no longer answered to.
