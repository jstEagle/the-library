## 4. When the Wolf Turns

The water in the flooded cathedral had gone still, and stillness, Theo had learned, was never empty. It gathered. It waited to be spent.

He stood at the edge of the nave where the altar should have been, and instead there was the door—narrow, salt-scoured, standing half-submerged in black water that reflected nothing. Not torchlight. Not his face. The door existed only while the queen's name was spoken somewhere in the world, and tonight it hung in the air like a held breath, and Theo could feel it thinning.

Behind him, Ilsa Vey stood with her sword drawn and her badge torn from her coat, and she was counting soldiers' boots on the stone stairs.

"Six," she said. "And him."

Theo didn't need to ask who *him* was. He heard it in the way the water trembled—the guildmaster's footsteps had always made the world flinch, ever since he'd begun eating his own inventory, memory by memory, until what remained of him was mostly appetite wearing a man's coat.

"Theo Halloran." The voice came down the nave like oil poured on water. "You were always my best clerk. Neat columns. No sentiment. Come back to me and I will let you keep your hands."

Ilsa moved slightly, putting her shoulder between Theo and the dark. "He's not yours."

"Everything in Vessia is mine, Captain. That is what a ledger is." A wet laugh. "Even you. Even the brother you bought back for one night. Did you think the crown didn't know? We sold them the receipt."

Theo felt Ilsa go rigid beside him. He had watched her spend three days getting here, watched her burn her own orders in a brazier rather than read them aloud, watched her hold a purchased memory of a drowned boy the way other people hold sleeping children. The guildmaster knew exactly where to cut.

"You're lying," she said.

"I never lie. Lying is for people who can afford to be wrong."

* * *

Ilsa counted again. Six boots on the stairs, and the seventh set of footsteps belonged to something that had stopped being entirely a man years ago. She thought of her brother—not the memory, which was bought and therefore suspect, but the truth beneath it: that she had joined the crown's service because grief made obedience feel like purpose. Burn the gifted. Burn what cannot be taxed. She had told herself the orders were clean because they came from above, and above was where she'd buried everything she loved.

Now the guildmaster was offering to sell her brother back to her permanently. One fragment, freely given, in exchange for the thief at the door.

She looked at Theo Halloran. He was so thin now. Three wards, three payments—his mother's face, his first theft, his name—and what stood at the water's edge was less a man than an outline of one, drawn by someone who kept erasing and redrawing the lines.

He doesn't even know who he is, she thought. And he's still walking toward the door.

That was the moment the wolf turned. Not with a roar. With a decision.

"No," Ilsa said.

"Captain Vey." The guildmaster's voice softened into something worse than threat. "Your orders are explicit. The gifted die. You are sworn."

"I'm sworn to Vessia." She drew her sword. "Not to whatever's left when Vessia is done being sold."

The first soldier came down the stairs at a run, and Ilsa met him on the third step, and the cathedral filled with the sound of steel and shouting and water splashing against stone that had once known only hymns.

* * *

Theo did not watch the fight. He could not afford to. The door was thinning—he could see it now, its edges going soft as breath on glass—and the queen's name was dying somewhere out in the dark country, in someone's mouth, in someone's last breath, and when it finished dying the door would finish too.

One payment left. He reached into himself the way he had learned to reach into ledgers, past the blank spaces where his mother had been, past the smooth scar where his childhood thefts had lived, down to the last sealed column. The truth of who he was before the guild found him.

He hesitated. Not because it hurt—by now he barely remembered what hurt felt like—but because it was the last thing, and after it there would be nothing between him and the water except the door.

*Who was I?*

The question had no answer anymore. That was the price. That was all the prices, stacked end to end.

He opened the column anyway.

And for one impossible second, before it drained out of him through the ward and into the door, he saw it: a boy on a dock in a fishing town whose name no longer appeared on any map, holding a book he couldn't read, watching ships leave for places with names. A boy who was nobody's clerk. Nobody's thief. A boy the guild had found hungry and taught that everything, even a self, was inventory.

Then it was gone, paid, spent, and the door flared open like a wound of light, and Theo Halloran stood before it knowing nothing about himself except this: the door was open, and he was walking through it.

* * *

Ilsa took a blade across the forearm and gave back worse. Two soldiers down. The guildmaster walked through the fighting as though it were weather, coat dry, eyes fixed on the light at the end of the nave, and Ilsa understood with terrible clarity that she could not stop him—only slow him. So she did the arithmetic every soldier dreads: how much time can I buy, and what will it cost.

Her badge was already on the floor. Her blood was joining it.

She threw herself at his knees.

They went down together into the black water, and she got one arm around his throat and held on while he clawed at her, while the remaining soldiers shouted and waded toward them, while the light at the end of the nave grew brighter and brighter, and she thought: *let this be enough, let this be enough, let this be enough.*

"GO!" she screamed, and did not know, afterward, whether she meant it for Theo or for herself.

* * *

At the threshold, Theo turned around.

He was almost through. The light was warm and smelled like rain on a road he couldn't remember wanting to walk. Behind him the cathedral burned—or was beginning to; torches had fallen, old wood was catching, and the flames ran up the walls like ivy in fast motion. Ilsa was a shape in the water, holding a monster under. Soldiers were screaming things about orders and treason and the queen's name, and one of them, sobbing, said it over and over—*Vessia, Vessia, Vessia*—and each time he said it the door burned brighter, because that was the rule, that was the whole architecture of the miracle: a country exists as long as someone says its name.

The ledger was still strapped to his chest. His life's work. Every memory the guild had ever bought, catalogued in his neat, sentimentless hand: the general's cowardice, the widow's first love, ten thousand small eternities priced and sold. Proof. Leverage. Wealth.

Someone else could carry it through. Someone else could rebuild the trade on the far side of the door, in whatever country came next. It would be so easy. It was the only thing he had left to give.

Theo Halloran—who no longer knew his mother's face, his first sin, or his own name, who had paid for survival with everything except himself—took the ledger off his chest and held it over the black water, and understood that this was the last choice he would ever get to make as anyone at all.

The guildmaster surfaced, roaring, reaching.

Ilsa held on.

Theo let go.

The ledger hit the water and the fire came down the nave to meet it, and pages began to curl and blacken, and names began to free themselves the only way names can be freed—by burning. The widow's first love rose off the page as ash. The general's cowardice went up in smoke and smoke owes nothing to anyone.

The sobbing soldier said *Vessia* one last time, and then, quietly, forgot why he was crying.

The door closed behind Theo like an eye.

* * *

On the far side there was a road, and rain just beginning, and grass growing in a language he didn't recognize but didn't need to. He sat down at the edge of it, dripping, empty, alive.

He tried to remember his name and found a clean white space where it had been. He tried to remember why he was crying and found that too was gone, traded or burned or simply washed away with a country that no longer existed anywhere except—

Except.

Somewhere behind him, faint as a bell underwater, a woman's voice—Ilsa's, or memory's, or no one's—said the name one more time. And the road under Theo's hands hummed, briefly, like a struck string, and held.

He didn't know who he was. But he knew what he had refused to carry, and somehow that was enough to stand on.

He got up. He faced away from the sea. And he began, with no name and no ledger and nothing owed to anyone, to walk.
