## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charm that would betray her was a luck-knot the size of a thumbnail, and Sebastian Kavanagh finished tying it with hands that ached from honest work.

That was the trick of it—honest work. Twelve years of it. She'd learned to let her knuckles crack and her back complain and her fingers blister on copper wire and hemp twine until the calluses told their own story: *tinker, tinker, tinker.* No storm in those hands. Just wire.

"Seb!" A voice cut through the market din. "Seb, you've shorted me again!"

Marta Cole, fishwife and professional complainer, stood at the edge of his stall with three children's bracelets held out in one red fist. Sebastian kept her smile easy, her shoulders loose. In Corvane, ease was armor.

"Shorted you? Those are double-strung, Marta. I use more silver in them than the temple uses in its candlesticks."

"You use *something*," Marta said, but she was already fishing out coins. "My Tammy's fever broke in one night after I hung your charm over her cot. One night. Old Mother Hesk says fever like that runs seven days minimum."

Something cold slid down Sebastian's spine, slow as honey off a spoon.

"Fever breaks when fevers break," she said. "Charms are for comfort. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Marta agreed cheerfully, and dropped the coins into Sebastian's palm, and went off toward the fish stalls without a care in the world, because everyone in Corvane knew that Seb the Tinker sold pretty trinkets and nothing more, and the law of Corvane said that magic was a crime against God and crown and common sense, and no one—not even old Mother Hesk, who had buried two husbands and could smell a lie at fifty paces—had ever once looked at Seb the Tinker twice.

Sebastian counted the coins twice anyway. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady now; that had been the hardest lesson, harder than the binding oaths themselves, harder than the night she'd walked out of Vanehold with nothing but the clothes she wore and a warden's seal burned into her travel papers. The storm inside her had thrashed for years afterward, throwing itself against the oaths like a wolf against a fence, and every time it moved, her hands shook, and shaking hands were the first thing they taught you to watch for in the wards' training yards.

She knew because she'd grown up in them. She knew because Ilsa Vane had been the one teaching.

*Don't think about that,* she told herself, the way she told herself every morning, every market day, every night when the wind came off the Frostreach and moaned in the eaves of her little room above the chandler's shop. *You're Seb. You're nobody. You sell charms that don't work.*

Except tonight, apparently, they worked.

She looked down at her stall—the luck-knots and fever-charms and the little braided cords mothers bought to hang over doorways against bad dreams—and made herself look at them properly for the first time in months. Really look. The copper wire in the nearest knot had gone strange overnight, she realized. Not tarnished. Brightened. There was a faint shimmer along its length, the kind you saw on water just before a squall came up the valley, and when she touched it, something deep beneath her breastbone turned over in its sleep.

Twelve years, and it had never done that. Not once. The oaths were iron; the oaths were stone. She had paid a woman she loved more than her own name to bury her power alive, and the grave had held.

So why did the grave feel like it was breathing?

"Seb! Two dream-ropes, if you've got them left."

She sold them without fumbling. She smiled. She haggled with a cooper over a knot-charm for his daughter's wedding cart and won by two coppers and felt nothing about it either way, which was itself strange, because bargaining usually pleased her—it was the one performance she'd gotten so good at that it stopped being a performance. Tonight the words came out of her mouth and landed somewhere far away while the rest of her listened to that slow, sleeping turn beneath her ribs, like the first deep grumble of thunder past the horizon.

The light began to fail around the sixth bell.

Corvane's market didn't close at dark so much as transform. Lanterns bloomed along the stall-fronts, fat candles in glass chimneys, and the crowd thickened with the evening shift come up from the dye-works and the tanneries, and the smell of roasting chestnuts drowned even the tanneries out. Sebastian lit her own lantern—a plain thing, no charm in it, she'd been careful about that—and worked by its glow, mending a hinge for the spice-seller next door because he paid in cinnamon and she was weak.

It was the chestnut boy who noticed first.

He couldn't have been more than ten, working his brazier on the corner, and his shout cracked across the square like a whip:

"The stars! Where'd the stars go?"

People laughed at first. That was the thing Sebastian would remember afterward, in the long bad nights that followed—how they laughed. The sky over Corvane was famously filthy with hearth-smoke and forge-fume; you got perhaps a dozen stars on the clearest winter night, and the city's children grew up thinking the constellations were stories travelers made up. So when the chestnut boy pointed up and yelled, the market chuckled and someone called out that the lad needed sleep, and life went on.

Then the dyer looked up. Then the cooper. Then everybody.

Sebastian set down her pliers.

She hadn't meant to look. Some part of her, some animal part that had spent twelve years learning exactly which things not to do, understood before her mind did what she would see. But she looked. Of course she looked.

The sky above the market square was black.

Not city-black. Not smoke-black, not the soft brown murk of clouded stars. Black the way the underside of a coffin lid is black. Black with nothing behind it. And it wasn't only over Corvane—she could feel that, though she couldn't have said how, the way you can feel a wall behind you in the dark. The absence went up and up and out, past the rooftops, past the hills, past the whole turning world, and where the stars should have been there was simply *nothing*, a great smooth darkness like a sheet drawn over a face.

The market went quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet—all the small sounds dying at once, leaving a silence with edges.

Then everyone started talking at once.

"A cloud bank—" "It's not a cloud bank, you fool, clouds don't—" "God's wrath, my gran always said—" "Someone fetch a priest—" "Which priest?" "All of them!"

A woman near the fountain began to weep, quietly and steadily, the way people weep when they've decided it's already too late for noise. The chestnut boy had stopped shouting. He stood with his tongs hanging forgotten in his hand, looking up, and up, and up.

And Sebastian Kavanagh felt the thing beneath her oaths open one eye.

There was no other way to describe it. For twelve years the storm in her had been a buried river, a pressure, an ache she'd learned to live around the way you live around a bad tooth. It did not wake. It could not wake. The binding oaths were woven through it like roots through soil, and they did not loosen, ever, not in fever, not in grief, not that one terrible night three years past when lightning had struck the chandler's shop and she had stood in the rain and wanted—with a want that had bent her nearly in half—to catch the next bolt out of the air.

But now it turned toward the empty sky the way a dog turns toward a door before the knock comes, and the iron of her oaths sang. Actually sang, a thin high note she heard not with her ears but somewhere behind them, and beneath her skin, along both forearms, her old scars—faint white ladders from the years before the binding, when the power had leaked and burned on its way through her—began to warm.

"No," she whispered. It came out as steam in the autumn air. "No, no, don't you dare."

The warmth spread. It wasn't pain. That was the worst part. It was *relief*, the feeling of a cramped muscle finally stretching, of breath after long submersion, and twelve years of careful ordinary life tilted sideways under her like deck-planking in a swell.

Because she knew this feeling. She had known it as a girl in Vanehold's training yards, before everything. This was what the air did before a storm. This was the taste of copper and green rain, the pressure-drop, the whole sky leaning in. The storm-caller's first sense, the one they couldn't teach and couldn't bind and couldn't take away: the knowing, hours ahead, that something enormous was coming.

Only the storm wasn't in the sky. The sky was empty. The sky was emptier than any sky in the history of skies.

Whatever was coming was coming from beyond it.

"Seb." The spice-seller, Anselm, gripping his counter with both hands, his cinnamon-dark face gone grey. "Seb, you're a sensible sort. What is it? Comets? Some—some astronomical business?"

"I'm a tinker, Anselm."

"Yes, but you read. You've got books."

She had three books, all purchased secondhand, all chosen with agonizing care to be exactly what a tinker with pretensions might own. She said, "I'll ask at the temple tomorrow," and hated how steady her voice came out, because nothing in her was steady, nothing had been steady since the sixth bell, and the scars on her arms were glowing now, faintly, a pale blue-white like foxfire, visible in the lantern-light if anyone cared to look—

She pulled her sleeves down. Clenched her fists. Thought, with the focus of desperation: *wire, hinges, twine, coppers, Marta's bracelets, the wedding cart, the chandler's rent due at month's end.* The inventory of an ordinary life, item by item, a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

The glow faded. The singing faded. The thing beneath the oaths lay back down—but it did not sleep. She could feel it watching, the way you can feel eyes between the slats of a shutter. Waiting for the sky to tell it whatever the sky had told it.

Around her, Corvane did what Corvane always did with the inexplicable: it got angry. Voices rose. Someone blamed the alchemists' quarter, someone blamed the northern temples, someone blamed the King's new taxes for angering heaven, and a pair of temple acolytes came hurrying through the square ringing a handbell and calling for calm, which produced none whatsoever. By the eighth bell the market had broken apart into knots of argument and fear, and the chestnut boy's mother had come to drag him home, and Sebastian packed her stall with hands that only trembled once.

She was carrying her last crate up the chandler's stairs when she saw the messenger.

He came down the street at a dead gallop, hooves striking sparks off the cobbles, royal livery mud-splattered past recognition, and he did not slow at the corner but took it at a clatter and was gone, heading for the Warden's Hall on the hill. And behind him, seconds later, another rider. And behind *that* one, a third, these wearing the grey-and-black of the Inquisition, and Sebastian stood in her doorway with a crate of harmless charms in her arms and watched the Inquisition ride hard through the streets of Corvane at nine o'clock at night, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like calm:

*They know. Whatever happened to the sky, they know what it means, and it has something to do with magic, and I am standing here glowing like a lamp.*

She went inside. Bolted the door. Lit no candle—she found she could see perfectly well in the dark, which was new, and which she filed away in the growing drawer of things she refused to think about. Through her narrow window, the city lay under that starless black, and somewhere out on the Frostreach, she knew, the border wards ran for six hundred miles, the great woven spells that had kept Corvane safe from the wild magic of the north since before the kingdom had a name.

Her grandmother had helped raise a piece of them, once. Before. Before the purges, before the laws, before the word *storm-caller* became something said only in whispers and court transcripts.

The border wards were anchored to the sky. Every warding-stone was cut to catch starlight, every line of the great weave drawn from constellation to constellation. Any child of the old families knew it. Any child of the old families would understand, instantly and completely, what it meant that the stars had gone out.

The wards weren't failing. The wards were *dead.*

Sebastian sat down slowly on the edge of her narrow bed, in her narrow room, above a chandler's shop, in the body of a woman named Seb, and put her face in her hands.

Under her palms, her scars were still warm. Beneath the oaths, patient as winter, the storm watched the door.

Somewhere across the city, a bell began to ring—not a market bell, not a temple bell, but the deep alarm toll of the Warden's Hall, the one that had sounded, according to the old men at the tavern, only twice in living memory. It rang once. Twice. Three times, and then stopped mid-swing, as though the hand on the rope had been pulled away.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Sebastian had ever heard.

She sat in it for a long time. Then, because twelve years of habit is stronger than dread, she checked the bolts on her door, banked her unlit stove, and lay down in her clothes with her boots beside the bed, facing the window, the way you sleep when part of you is already certain that before morning, someone will come knocking.

The knock, when it came—much later, well past midnight, heavy and stumbling and wrong—was not the one she had been expecting.

* * *

She woke to pounding. Not the measured fist of the Watch or the crisp triple-rap of the Inquisition she'd rehearsed in her head a hundred nights. This was erratic, desperate, the beating of someone using the last of their strength, and it stopped between strikes, started again weaker.

Sebastian was at the window before she was fully awake, one eye to the gap in the shutter. The street below lay empty under the starless dark. Empty except for a shape crumpled against her doorstep, half in the recess of her doorway, trying and failing to rise. Even in the dark she could see too much detail—the gleam of wet cobblestone, the dark stain spreading wide beneath the shape, the glint of a broken sigil-chain dragged halfway into the shadow of the stair.

And pinned under the figure's bloodied hand, catching what little light the street offered and giving back something colder than light, a disc of white metal stamped with the royal seal of Corvane.

The seal was bleeding shadow. Not reflecting it. Bleeding it—a thin dark smoke curling up from the engraved crown and dissolving into the starless air, and every instinct Sebastian owned, every instinct the storm beneath her oaths owned, screamed the same single word:

*Run.*

Instead, her hand was already lifting the latch.

Through the wood of the door, ragged and unmistakable after twelve years, came a voice she knew better than her own name.

"Sebastian. Please. I know you're awake—I can hear the floorboards. Sebastian, please open the door."

Warden Ilsa Vane was dying on her step, and the last star in the sky had not yet finished going out.
