## 2. Charms and Cinders

The morning after the stars went out, Corvane did what Corvane always did: it pretended nothing had happened.

Sebastian heard it in the market before she saw it—the great shrugging machine of ordinary life grinding back into motion. Fishwives arguing over the price of herring. A cooper swearing at a stubborn stave. Two porters complaining about their backs as if the sky had not emptied itself overnight like a purse turned out on a table. The city had looked up, seen the black, and decided collectively that it was weather, or an omen for someone else, or simply none of its business.

She envied them that. She truly did.

Her stall was the third from the corner of Tanner's Row, between a candle-dipper who never spoke and a man who sold birds' nests by the basket and swore they were medicinal. Her awning was faded green canvas, patched twice at the corners. Her sign—*SEB THE TINKER, CHARMWORK & REPAIRS*—had been painted by her own hand eleven years ago in letters deliberately crooked, because crooked letters looked like no one had cared much, and people who didn't care attracted less attention. It was the finest piece of craft she owned, that bad handwriting.

Twelve years of it now. Twelve years since the rain, the trial-that-wasn't, the long road east with Ilsa Vane's voice at her back telling her *keep walking and don't stop until the mountains forget your name*. Twelve years of being nobody, which was the whole point, and which she had gotten so good at that some mornings she woke up and had to lie still for a while to remember who she was supposed not to be.

This morning she arranged her wares the way she always did: copper charms on hooks, small enough to fit in a palm—a luck-knot, a hearth-blessing, a key-whistle against thieves, a fever-catcher shaped like a coiled snake of braided tin. Nothing grand. Nothing that worked, if you asked anyone official. Charmwork wasn't magic, said the law of Corvane; charmwork was superstition with good manners, tolerated the way a beggar is tolerated, so long as it stayed in its lane and kept its hands visible. Every charm she sold was, technically, a trinket. A bit of hammered metal with a rhyme attached.

That was the artistry of it. That was what kept her alive.

She hung the last of the luck-knots at mid-morning and felt it happen again—the thing that had started yesterday, when the stars went out and something under her breastbone had rolled over like a dog hearing its name.

The knot *took*.

Not sort-of-took, not took-the-way-a-trinket-takes, which is to say the buyer feels better and the coin changes hands and nothing actually happens. No. The luck-knot on her hook brightened. There was no other word for it. The dull copper seemed to catch light that wasn't there, and the little rhyme she'd stitched into the binding thread—that nonsense verse about roads and rivers that she'd written specifically to mean nothing—hummed against her fingers when she touched it, faint as a struck tuning fork.

Sebastian stood very still behind her stall, hands flat on the wood, and breathed.

It was the dark, then. She'd suspected it since last night, since the moment every star in the heavens went out at once—not veiled, not clouded, *gone*, like candles snuffed by a hand passing across the world—and the storm she had buried twelve years ago had stirred in its grave and whispered, *someone is drinking*. Magic was water finding its level. If something somewhere was pulling hard enough, everything loose would drift toward it, and everything bound would strain at its moorings. Including, apparently, a luck-knot in a tinker's stall on Tanner's Row.

*Keep it small,* she told herself, the old litany. *Keep it crooked. Keep it boring.*

By noon her stall looked like a shrine to a god of modest fortune, and half of Corvane wanted to worship there.

* * *

It shouldn't have mattered. A charm working too well was a nuisance, not a catastrophe—except that Sebastian Kavanagh had spent twelve years learning exactly how finely calibrated a nuisance had to be before it became a death sentence.

The trouble was the quality of the wanting. Corvane after a starless night was a city full of people who had slept badly and woken worse, and they came to her stall the way the thirsty come to a well they've been told is dry. A carter bought a road-charm for his lame mare, and by evening the mare was trotting. A widow bought a hearth-blessing and came back the next day for three more, saying her fire hadn't guttered once, even in the wind, even with the door open. The candle-dipper beside her bought a key-whistle for her sister's shop and reported, with the flat wonder of a woman who did not believe in anything, that the lock now opened for her sister alone and had bitten a stranger hard enough to draw blood.

"Good tin," Sebastian said each time. "I use good tin."

"You use something," the candle-dipper said, and looked at her a moment too long, and moved off.

And Sebastian smiled her tinker's smile—apologetic, slightly gap-toothed, a smile engineered over a decade to say *nothing here, move along*—and inside, beneath the smile, the fear sat up and paid attention.

Because here was the arithmetic of her life: a charm that worked a little was commerce. A charm that worked *too* well was evidence. And evidence, in Corvane, traveled one direction—to the Office of Inquiry, where clerks wrote things down in ledgers with leather thongs instead of ribbon, and where the men in grey coats read those ledgers aloud twice a year at something called Reckoning, and where names that appeared in three consecutive reckonings stopped being names and became appointments.

She knew. She had watched it happen to a hedgewife in the Low Quarter named Marta, back in her fourth year, whose poultices had worked just a little too well through one wet spring. Marta had been appointed. Marta's cottage had been appointed too, right down to the foundation stones, and the ash had smelled wrong for weeks.

So she should have packed the stall early. She should have sold nothing more, taken down her crooked sign, gone home, and spent the night unpicking every binding-thread she'd ever sewn, drowning her rhymes in the canal.

Instead, the child came.

* * *

She was four, maybe five, carried on her mother's hip with her head lolling the way heads loll only when a body has stopped fighting a fever and started losing to it. Sebastian saw them coming through the crowd the way she saw everything now—with the terrible clarity that the dark sky had left behind, as if the vanished stars had all crowded into her eyes. The child's skin was wrong, grey-gone-glazed, sweat-dark hair plastered to a skull too hot beneath. The mother walked like a woman carrying something already lost and refusing to set it down.

They weren't coming to her stall. The mother was heading past, toward the physic's on Chalk Lane, and Sebastian could have let her pass. Could have watched the grey face bob away into the crowd and told herself, correctly, that fevers broke on their own, that physicks existed, that touching that child was the loudest thing she could possibly do.

Twelve years of discipline said: *let her pass.*

The storm under her breastbone said nothing at all. It just leaned forward, the way a wolf leans when the wind brings a scent it recognizes.

"Madam." Her mouth was speaking before she'd voted on it. "Your girl. Let me see her."

The mother stopped. Turned. Her eyes were the eyes Sebastian had seen a thousand times in this market—suspicious, harried, a woman with no money for physicks and knowing it. "You sell charms."

"I sell repairs," Sebastian said. "Bring her here. Sit her down. I'll look at nothing, I promise you. Just let me hold her hands a moment. Hands are honest."

It was nonsense and the mother knew it was nonsense, but fever makes believers of everyone eventually, and she sat on Sebastian's stool with the child across her knees, and Sebastian knelt in the dust of Tanner's Row and took two small burning hands in hers.

She meant to do almost nothing. That was the plan, the whole plan: a trickle, a whisper, the magical equivalent of blowing on an ember. Enough to ease the heat a degree, no more, and call it the will of whatever saints Corvane still permitted itself to mention.

But the child looked at her.

Fever-bright, unfocused, sunk halfway under already—and still, somehow, the look found her, and held her, the way only the very young and the very old can hold a stranger. And the storm rose up inside Sebastian Kavanagh like floodwater against a door.

*No,* she told it.

It came anyway. Not much. Not a river—a thread of it, silver and cold and impossibly glad, sliding down through her arms and out through her palms, and she felt the fever meet it and *break*, physically break, like ice on a spring pond, the heat shattering into the thread and running out of the small body and into her, where it went nowhere, harmlessly, swallowed by something far deeper than heat could reach.

The child shivered once. Drew a breath that went all the way down. And blinked up at the starless sky with clear, cool, ordinary eyes.

"She's—" The mother's voice cracked. She gathered the girl in, feeling her forehead, her neck, her hands, disbelieving. "What did you—what was—"

"Tin," Sebastian said. Her own voice sounded very far away. "Good tin. Like I said."

The mother pressed a copper coin into her hand as if paying for a miracle with pocket change, and hurried off toward Chalk Lane anyway, because mothers believe in physicks even after miracles. The child, over her shoulder, waved.

Sebastian knelt in the dust a moment longer than made sense. Then she got up, smiling her tinker's smile at no one, and began taking down her wares.

*Pack it up. Now. Today.* Her hands were steady; her mind was screaming. *Every charm off the hooks. Unpick the threads tonight. Burn the rhymes. Be smaller tomorrow. Be nothing.*

She had the luck-knots in a box and the sign half-unhooked when the clerk arrived.

* * *

He came out of the crowd the way ink comes out of water, and she knew him instantly for what he was, though he wore no grey coat—clerks never did. He wore brown, unremarkable brown, and carried a ledger under his arm the way other men carried bread, casually, constantly, an organ of his body. Middle-aged, soft-handed, with the patient, unhungry expression of a man whose appetite was entirely professional.

He browsed her stall for a full minute. Picked up a key-whistle. Set it down. Picked up a hearth-blessing and turned it over twice.

"Clever work," he said.

"I get by."

"The carter's mare." He said it mildly, conversationally, to the hearth-blessing rather than to her. "Lame since autumn. Trotting today. My wife knows the carter's wife." He set the blessing down and aligned its edge precisely with the table's edge. "Funny thing about the mare. Farrier swears there's nothing wrong with the leg. Never was. Says it's like the leg just... agreed to be well."

"Horses are strange animals," Sebastian said. "Especially when they feel a change in the air. All this about the stars—animals have been mad since Tuesday."

"Mm." He produced the ledger, and she watched—actually watched—his pen find a page already bearing entries, and write. She couldn't read it upside down, and didn't try, and hated herself for the effort of not trying. "Name's Pell," he said. "I keep accounts. Curiosities of trade, mostly. You'd be surprised what turns up in the margins." Pen scratch. "Seb, is it? The tinker?"

"Just Seb."

"No family name?"

"Dropped it. Family dropped me first." A lie with a true shape; those were the best kind. "Rough years. You know how it goes."

"Mm." He closed the ledger. Looked at her properly for the first time, and his eyes were kind, which frightened her more than cruelty would have, because kind men with ledgers were still men with ledgers. "Word of advice, from someone who reads the margins. This week, with the sky the way it is—there's folk upstairs looking for causes. Any cause will do. They're not particular." A pause. "A clever tradeswoman might find the week a good one for inventory. Quiet work. Home work."

"I'll bear it in mind."

"Do." He tipped his head, almost a bow, and melted back into the crowd, brown into brown, gone.

Sebastian stood in the emptying light of Tanner's Row with her sign under her arm and her heart going like a smith's hammer. *Inventory. Home work. Unpick every thread.* She would. She was going to start the moment she—

"—Tinker! Ho, tinker!"

A boy of maybe ten, one of the canal-rats who ran messages for bread, skidded to a halt at her stall with the particular urgency of the well-paid. He thrust out a folded scrap of paper.

"Lady at the Candle Street gate said two bits if this reached Seb the Tinker tonight. Said you'd know her." He grinned, gap-toothed, waiting.

Two bits was generous. Two bits was *urgent*. Sebastian gave him the coins and unfolded the paper with fingers that had gone cold for the second time that day.

The note was brief, written in a strong angular hand she had spent twelve years forgetting and had never once forgotten:

*Sebastian. I'm hurt. I know what killed the stars. I need the woman I exiled, not the friend I lost—but I'll take whichever will open the door. Third bell. The blue house on Wren Stile. Come alone. —V.*

Below it, pressed into the wax of a plain seal, was the sigil of the eastern border-ward.

Snapped cleanly in half.
