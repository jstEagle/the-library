## 3. The Warden at the Door

The rain had followed Ilsa Vane for three days.

She had told herself it was weather—ordinary, mindless weather, the kind that fell on Corvane without malice or meaning—but somewhere past the second night she had stopped believing it. It found her. It pooled in the hollow of her collarbone and ran down the seam of her wounded side like a cold hand testing the damage, and when she turned down an alley it seemed to turn with her, patient as a debt.

Twelve years ago she would have known what that meant. Twelve years ago she had been a Warden of the Crown, sworn and sigiled, fluent in the language of omens. Now she was a woman limping through the tinker quarter at dusk with a dead man's seal wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, and every instinct she had left told her one thing: *Sebastian Kavanagh.*

Not Seb the Tinker. She had made inquiries, careful ones, spread across three months like bread crumbs she couldn't afford to drop. A charm-seller on Coppersmith Row who never smiled quite right for his customers. A man with calluses in strange places—the pads of his fingers worn smooth in the pattern of someone who had once held a storm between her hands. A neighbor's child who'd been pulled from the canal two winters back by "the tinker," who swore afterward that the water had gone still around her like something listening.

Ilsa had read the reports and felt something crack quietly behind her breastbone.

*She's here. All this time, she's been here. Ten streets from my office door.*

And now the wards were falling, the stars were going out one constellation at a time, and the only person alive who might understand what was eating the kingdom was the woman whose life Ilsa had spent to keep the peace.

*Some peace,* she thought, and pressed her hand harder against her side, where the bolt had torn through leather and flesh both. The bleeding had slowed to a seep. That was not the same as stopping.

Coppersmith Row smelled of solder and coal smoke and wet wool. Lanterns were being lit in windows—not many, and lower than usual. People were frightened. Three nights without stars did that to a city, even a city like Corvane that prided itself on needing nothing from the sky. Ilsa kept her hood up and her gait unhurried, though every step sent a hot wire of pain up through her hip, and she counted doors until she reached the narrow shopfront wedged between a farrier and a candlemaker.

No sign hung above it. Just a chalked symbol on the lintel—a little spiral, the mark tinkers used to say *repairs done fair.* The window displayed a scatter of wares: copper charms shaped like moths, iron nails sold in bundles for doorways, a row of glass beads that caught the lamplight and held it a half-second too long.

Ilsa stood in the rain and looked at those beads for a long moment, because she recognized them. Storm-glass. Worthless to anyone else. To a storm-caller they were anchors, or they had been once, in another life, in a green valley she still visited in her dreams and always woke from ashamed.

*You could still walk away,* said the part of her that had survived twelve years of service by knowing exactly how much truth any moment could hold. *You could take this to Hale. To the archive. To anyone but her.*

But Hale was half a stranger now, moving through the undercity with shadow in his eyes, and the archive burned its own archivists, and there was no one. There had never been anyone but Sebastian Kavanagh, and Ilsa had known it twelve years ago when she'd stood in the mud outside Thornmere and spoken the words of exile in a voice that hadn't shaken until the girl was out of sight.

She climbed the three steps and knocked.

* * *

From inside: silence, then the soft scrape of a stool, footsteps. A shutter slid an inch. One eye in the gap—brown, sharp, ringed with tiredness—and then the shutter slammed so hard the whole frame jumped.

"Closed," said the voice behind it, roughened by twelve years into something Ilsa almost didn't recognize. Almost.

"Warden business," Ilsa said, before she could think better of the word. She heard her own mistake even as it left her mouth, heard the old authority in it, the tone that had ended a girl's life on a muddy road, and hated herself for it.

Silence. Then, low and bitter: "There's no such thing anymore. Go away."

"The stars went out." Rain ran off the brim of her hood. "Three nights ago. All of them, all at once, and they haven't come back."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came again it was quieter, and worse for that. "I know. I felt it. Like a door opening over the whole world." A pause. "Why are you telling me this? Why are you *here?*"

Because I have nowhere else. Because you're the only one. Because I have carried your name like a stone in my boot for twelve years and tonight I finally set it down.

"Sebastian." She said the true name deliberately, plainly, the way you'd lay a knife on a table between you. "It's me. It's Ilsa. And I'm hurt, and I'm out of road, and I need—" Her breath caught on the word; she forced it through. "I need your help."

Nothing. Rain. Somewhere down the row a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Then the lock turned.

* * *

The shop's interior was small and fiercely tidy: a workbench scarred by decades of soldering, shelves of components sorted with a precision that spoke of a mind that needed order the way other people needed air, a narrow stair to a loft above. And everywhere, threaded through everything, small bright workings—Ilsa's trained eye catalogued them in a heartbeat, wards woven so fine and so *humble* that no inquisitor would ever flag them. A luck-knot over the door. A hearth-charm that kept the fire honest. A threshold-binding older than the shop itself, laid down in patterns Ilsa knew, because she had been the one who taught them.

*You kept practicing.* The realization landed like a blow. *All these years, you never stopped. You just made yourself small enough to hide it.*

Sebastian closed the door and threw the bolt, and then turned, and Ilsa saw her properly for the first time since Thornmere.

She was thirty-four years old and looked older in the lamplight—or perhaps just carved differently, the softness of twenty-two planed away into angles and wariness. Her hair was cropped short, practical, streaked early with grey at the temples. She wore a tinker's leather apron over a plain wool shirt, and her hands—her hands were the same, long-fingered, scarred across the knuckles, hands built for channeling something enormous through something very small.

Those hands were trembling. Ilsa noted it with a strange, distant grief. Not with fear, she thought. With rage held very carefully, the way you'd carry a live coal.

"You're bleeding on my floor," Sebastian said.

"I'll pay for the mopping."

"Don't." Sebastian crossed the room in four strides, caught Ilsa's arm as her knee buckled, and lowered her onto the stool with a strength that surprised them both. "Don't joke. Don't be charming. You don't get to do either." She was already cutting the leather away from the wound with a workknife, efficient, clinical, not meeting Ilsa's eyes. "Crossbow bolt. Who shot you?"

"Does it matter?"

"If I'm going to pull it out of you, it matters enormously, yes."

"Crown hunters. Inquisitorial retinue." Ilsa exhaled slowly as cool air touched raw flesh. "They weren't hunting you. They were hunting *me.* I took something from the royal archive that I wasn't authorized to remove."

"And you brought it here." Sebastian's voice was flat. "To the house of a woman living under a false name, committing a capital offense daily, in a city that burns people like us. You brought the thing they're killing each other over to my *door.*"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ilsa had rehearsed a dozen answers on the road. Every one of them dissolved now, in this small bright room, under these furious brown eyes. What remained was the truth, bare and inadequate.

"Because you're the only person in Corvane who might understand what I'm carrying. And because I owe you—" Her voice failed and she steadied it with effort. "I owe you more than I can ever repay, and I've known that for twelve years, and I chose tonight because tonight is the last night I get to choose anything."

Sebastian stopped moving.

For a moment neither of them breathed. The lamp hissed. Outside, the rain kept up its patient knocking, like something asking to be let in.

Then Sebastian went back to work, jaw tight, and said nothing at all while she probed the wound, poured something clear and biting into it that made Ilsa's vision white out, and drew the bolt-head free with a single merciless motion. She stitched fast and neat, a craftsman's stitches, and bound the wound in clean linen, and only when it was done—only when Ilsa was shaking and pale and anchored to the stool by nothing but pride—did she step back, wipe her hands, and fold her arms.

"Now," Sebastian said. "Show me what a dying woman carries across three days of rain."

* * *

Ilsa unwrapped the oilcloth bundle on the workbench, beneath the lamp, and watched Sebastian's face as the light fell on what lay inside.

Two things.

The first was a disc of black iron the size of a dinner plate, worked with concentric rings of script that should have glowed silver and instead drank the lamplight like a hole cut in the world. Even dimmed—even *dead*—it radiated a pressure Ilsa felt against her teeth. She had carried it wrapped against her skin for three days and had dreamed, every night, of standing on the border of the kingdom looking out at an empty field where the world used to be.

"The border-ward sigil," she said. "The master-anchor for the Great Wards. The wards that have kept the Hollowings out and the wild magic contained since the founding. I pulled it from the vault beneath Wardenhold because our readings showed it failing." She swallowed. "It isn't failing, Sebastian. Look at it. Look at the edges."

Sebastian leaned close. Ilsa saw her go still, saw the exact moment the tinker became something else—a scholar, a survivor, a girl who had once read ward-script fluently enough to frighten her teachers.

"It's not cracked," Sebastian whispered. "It's *gnawed.* These edges—something ate through it. Something ate through a founding-grade anchor." She looked up. "That's not possible. There's nothing in the world with an appetite like that."

"There is one thing." Ilsa's hand hovered over the second object in the bundle and, for a heartbeat, refused to touch it. "The second thing is why they're hunting me. It came off King Aldric's own seal-ring. His privy seal. I was present at court when he pressed it into wax—and afterward the wax didn't stop moving."

She lifted it free: a heavy signet of white gold, the royal wolf rampant on its face. Except the wolf was wrong. Its lines writhed when you weren't looking directly at them, and beneath the gold, in the recesses of the carving, something dark welled up like ink through paper—slowly, patiently, endlessly. It dripped onto the workbench and the wood beneath it went grey and brittle, like frost-burned grass.

Sebastian recoiled. Not far—she was too controlled for that—but Ilsa saw her hand drift toward the storm-glass beads on the shelf, an old reflex, and something in Ilsa's chest twisted at the sight of it.

"What is it?" Sebastian asked. Her voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "What's wrong with him? With the *seal?*"

"That's what I came to find out. Here's what I know." Ilsa made herself speak evenly, laying the facts down one by one like stones across a river, because if she let herself feel them she would never finish crossing. "A year ago, Aldric began to change. Slower speech. Long absences. Decrees signed in his name that contradicted decrees signed the week before. I reported it. My superiors told me the king was grieving and I was overwrought." Her mouth twisted. "Six months ago I stopped reporting and started investigating. I found records in the deep archive—founder-era records, sealed by the first king himself. Records about the crown he wore at his coronation. Records about what it actually *was.*"

"And?"

"It was never jewelry, Sebastian. It was a binding vessel. A guardian-thing the star-priests caught and collared and set to watch the royal line—to watch *through*, to warn against corruption. And somewhere along twelve generations of kings, it stopped watching and started feeding." Ilsa nodded at the gnawed sigil. "The wards didn't fail. They were *eaten.* Whatever wears Aldric's crown has spent a year drinking the kingdom dry—wards, ley-veins, blessings, everything—and the stars going out is just the sky noticing."

The lamp flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

Sebastian stood very still. Ilsa watched her take it in—watched her merchant's composure fracture and reform, fracture and reform—and understood, with sickening clarity, what was coming next. Because she knew this woman. She had helped raise this woman. And there was only one conclusion Sebastian Kavanagh could reach from these facts, and it was the one Ilsa had been dreading since the border.

"The Hollowings," Sebastian said softly. "The magic-eating blights on the frontier. The ones that appeared the same year I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The year you exiled me, the Hollowings began. Everyone said a rogue storm-caller's residue must have drawn them. That was the official finding. *Your* finding."

"I wrote the report, yes."

"Was it true?"

"No." The word cost Ilsa everything she had left, and she paid it. "The Hollowings started eight days after Aldric's coronation. Eight days. I knew the timeline didn't fit. I knew it. But the council needed a cause they could act against, and they had you—a girl whose power had already killed once—and if I told them the truth, they'd have taken you apart looking for answers inside you. Exile was the only shape of mercy the law allowed me." Her voice frayed at last, twelve years of control coming apart at the seams. "So I gave them the lie, and I gave you the road, and I told myself it was love, doing it. And I have never once, in twelve years, believed myself."

Sebastian turned away.

She walked to the window and stood with her back straight and her arms folded, looking out at the starless dark over Coppersmith Row, and for a long time she said nothing, and the silence was worse than shouting. Ilsa sat on the stool and bled quietly through her new stitches and let her have it. She had no right to hurry her.

When Sebastian finally spoke, her voice was steady in the way that ice is steady.

"Do you know what the first year was like? On the road?" She didn't turn around. "Do you know how many times I almost walked back? How many times I got within sight of the border stones and stood there telling myself you'd send word? That you'd find a way to undo it, once things calmed down?" A short, terrible laugh. "I waited for a letter for six years, Ilsa. Six. And then I stopped waiting, and I built *this*—" she gestured at the small bright room, the tidy shelves, the careful life— "out of the pieces, and it was small, but it was *mine*, and I was safe in it. And now you're sitting on my stool, bleeding on my floor, telling me the lie you ruined my life to protect was covering up a *parasite wearing the king.*"

"I know."

"You don't." Now she turned, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "You don't know, because you got to keep your rank and your purpose and your *certainty*. I got a false name and a fear of open skies." She crossed the room and planted her hands on either side of Ilsa's stool, caging her, close enough that Ilsa could see the storm-light—that faint blue flicker Ilsa remembered from Thornmere—guttering deep in her pupils like lightning behind cloud. "So before I decide whether to throw you out into that rain, you're going to tell me why. Why *me.* Out of everyone in this rotten kingdom—why bring this to the woman you exiled?"

And Ilsa, who had commanded garrisons and faced down councils and lied to inquisitors without blinking, found her hands shaking on her knees.

"Because you're the strongest storm-caller born in three hundred years," she said. "And whatever ate the wards and wears the king—it feeds on magic. Everything I found says the same thing. It can't be fought with steel. It can't be starved, because it's already eaten the borders. But a storm—" She held Sebastian's gaze and made herself finish. "A storm isn't drawn magic. It's not stored anywhere for it to drink. It's *made*, live, faster than anything can feed on it. The founder-era texts call it the one fire that doesn't leave ashes. If anything in this world can burn that thing out of Aldric Vane, it's you."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain stopped all at once, the way rain does when something much larger is holding its breath.

Sebastian stepped back. Her face had gone very pale, and very calm, which Ilsa knew—from one terrible afternoon twelve years ago—was the most dangerous expression she owned.

"So that's why you came," Sebastian said. "Not for forgiveness. For *use.*"

"No." Ilsa rose, slowly, her wound screaming, and stood facing her across the width of the small bright room that held both their histories. "I came because the kingdom is dying and you're the only cure I know. And yes, I came because I'd rather you hate me and live than forgive me and die wondering. Both things are true. I've stopped pretending they can't be."

For a long moment, the only sound was the drip of shadow-blood from the king's seal onto the scarred workbench, each drop leaving a small grey ghost.

Then Sebastian picked up a cloth, folded it over the signet with deliberate care, and smothered it beneath an iron pot lid, as if putting out a fire.

"Get upstairs," she said, not looking at Ilsa. "There's a bed and there's willow-bark tea and you'll sleep with the door unlocked, because if your hunters come, they're going to have to come through me." She paused at the foot of the stair, and her voice dropped, rough and quiet. "This doesn't mean anything, Ilsa. It doesn't mean I forgive you. It means I want to see this thing with my own eyes before I decide what it costs."

"Thank you," Ilsa said.

"Don't." Sebastian's hand rested on the rail, and she still wouldn't turn around. "Save your thanks. Because tomorrow you're going to tell me everything in those founder-texts, every word, and then we're going to talk about the part you haven't said yet—the part sitting behind your teeth right now, the reason you flinched when I asked why you really came here tonight." At last she glanced back over her shoulder, and her eyes were dry and burning. "Whatever it is, Warden—you're going to tell me. And then we'll see whether either of us survives the truth."

Above the shop, through the loft's small window, the sky over Corvane was utterly, perfectly black.

And in that blackness, for the space of a single heartbeat, something vast and patient looked back.
