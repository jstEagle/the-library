## 4. What the Wards Were For

The sigil sat on my workbench between a jar of solder-flux and a half-finished music box, and it was the most wrong thing I had ever seen.

I'd made wards in another life—small ones, honest ones, the kind that kept rats out of granaries and frost off seedlings. A ward-sigil should hum. It should sit in the world like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing, some faint insistence of *I am here, I am holding*. Even dead ones kept an echo. Even shattered ones.

This one didn't echo. This one had been *unmade* from the inside out. The silver of it was still whole—the circle unbroken, the runes crisp as the day a warden-smith cut them—and that was what turned my stomach. The border-ward that had stood over Corvane's northern pass for three hundred years hadn't been broken. Breaking leaves wreckage. This left nothing but shape, a skin with nothing under it, like a snakeskin hung on a fence post.

"Say something," Ilsa said. She was still standing by my door with her hand pressed to her ruined side, blood working its way through the field-dressing she'd bound there herself, badly, the way soldiers do when they don't trust anyone else's knots. "You've been looking at it for two minutes. You always did think louder than you talked."

"Don't." I didn't look up. "Don't do that. Don't stand in my kitchen and talk to me like it's been twelve days instead of twelve years."

"It's been twelve years," she said quietly. "I know exactly how long it's been. I can tell you to the hour."

That landed somewhere under my ribs where I didn't want things landing. I picked up the sigil instead of answering her, because metal was easier than her voice, and held it to the lamp.

The runes were intact. That was the second wrongness, the one that finally made me put it back down like it might bite. Ward-runes don't survive their ward dying. They burn out with it, spend themselves, go dark and crumble. These were bright. These were *full*.

"Ilsa." My voice came out flat and strange. "Where are the stars?"

She went very still. It's a particular stillness, a warden's stillness—I'd seen it once before, on a hillside, in the rain, right before she spoke the words that ended my life as I knew it. Twelve years, and my body remembered it before my mind caught up: *she is deciding how much truth you can carry.*

"You've seen them," she said. Not a question. Of course not a question; if I'd been awake at midnight, anyone with eyes had seen it. A sky like a blacked-out room. Every star in heaven gone at once, no clouds, no moon, just—absence, total and deliberate.

"They didn't fall," I said. "I checked the old almanacs this morning before market. Falling stars leave trails. They leave *debris*, sometimes. There were no trails. No light anywhere on the horizon, either, which means it wasn't local. It wasn't even weather." I looked up at her then, and let her see how afraid I'd been since midnight, because fear was the one weapon I had against whatever she'd come here carrying. "Stars don't go out, Ilsa. Stars burn for longer than kingdoms have existed. So either the world ended last night—and clearly it didn't, because you're standing in my kitchen bleeding on my floor—or something *took* them."

The lamp flame guttered. Neither of us moved.

"Eat first," she said. "Or pour tea, at least. What I have to tell you goes down easier with something warm in your hands, and you'll want your hands doing something so they don't do anything stupid."

"My hands," I said, "are extremely well-behaved."

"Your hands," said Ilsa Vane, who had personally watched me put a lightning bolt through a chapel roof at fourteen, "have never once behaved in their lives. Tea. Now. Then I'll talk, and you'll listen all the way to the end before you decide to hate me more than you already do."

It was, I would later understand, the closest thing to an apology she was capable of making: an acknowledgment that I *would* hate her more. She wasn't asking forgiveness. She was asking for sequence.

I made the tea because my hands wanted something to do, exactly as she'd known they would. She sat across the table from me, which was itself an obscenity—Wardens don't sit in exiles' kitchens, Wardens deliver sentences from doorways and leave—but sitting made the wound in her side worse and she knew better than to pretend otherwise in front of me. I'd seen her take a boar tusk through the thigh when I was sixteen and walk four miles on it rather than let me carry her. Pride like hers only bent when the alternative was breaking.

She wrapped both hands around the cup and stared into it for a long moment, steam rising past a face I had spent twelve years refusing to remember and remembered anyway, every line of it, every scar.

"The border wards fell eleven nights ago," she said. "All of them. North, east, the sea-passes, the mountain chains. Fourteen great wards, each one older than the city we're sitting in. The official finding—which I wrote, God forgive me—was structural failure. Age, erosion, a cascade. One falls, the resonance breaks, the rest follow. It happens. It has happened before."

"And?"

"And it's a lie." She said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I wrote it because the Crown ordered it written and because I believed it, and I am very good at believing things the Crown tells me. It's a skill. They select for it." Her mouth twisted. "Three nights ago I went to the north pass myself. I took the fallen sigil out of its housing with my own hands. And I sat down in the snow with three hundred years of archive training and I read what was actually in front of me."

"The runes are full," I said. "They should be empty."

"The runes are full," she agreed, "because they were never *spent*. Sebastian, the wards weren't broken. They weren't eroded. They weren't sabotaged—there's no cutting mark, no counter-sigil, no residue of any working I know or any working in the archives." She lifted her eyes to mine. "They were *eaten*. Consumed. Drunk down to the dregs. Something fed on them, and it fed so completely that it took the magic itself and left the shape behind, the way a wasp larva leaves the husk of the spider it grew inside."

The tea in my cup had gone cold. I noticed this distantly, the way you notice things when the floor has tilted.

"That's not possible," I said. "Magic isn't—you can't *drink* it. It's not a substance. It's—" I gestured vaguely, hating how young I sounded, how much of the theory I'd let rot these twelve years because thinking about magic meant thinking about the storm, and thinking about the storm meant thinking about the rain and the hillside and the harvest and her face under her hood as she read my sentence aloud. "It's a relationship. Between the caller and the world. You can't eat a relationship."

"No," Ilsa said. "You can't eat a relationship. But you can eat a *person*. And every ward ever raised was raised by someone. The power doesn't live in the silver, Sebastian. It lives in the lineage. In the oaths. In the people who gave it." She reached into her coat—slowly, watching me flinch at the movement—and drew out a second object and set it beside the sigil.

A seal. The royal seal of Aldric IV, heavy wax the color of dried liver, bearing the imprint of the crown-and-oak that had stamped every edict of my childhood. Except that where the oak should have been pale gold, it was black. Not inked black, not stained. Black the way the sky had been black at midnight, black the way the space between stars is black—a darkness that seemed to have depth, that seemed, when I leaned too close, to be *looking back*.

I snatched my hand away before I'd consciously decided to.

"It came on an edict," Ilsa said. "Two weeks ago. New tariffs on the eastern provinces. Ordinary, dull, forgettable—the kind of paper that crosses my desk fifty times a year. Except I've handled the king's seals since I was a junior archivist, and this one was wrong. The wax was cold. It's always warm; there's a warming charm laid into the signet, it's been there for six reigns. And the shadow it cast—" She stopped. Started again. "Sebastian, the shadow it cast moved. When the paper was still. Just slightly. Like something behind the wax shifting its weight."

"You're telling me," I said carefully, "that the King of Corvane sent you haunted mail."

"I'm telling you what I found when I stopped being a good little believer and started being an archivist." She pushed the cup aside and leaned forward, and I saw that her hands were shaking now, fine tremors she couldn't soldier through. "There are records, Sebastian. Old ones. Pre-founding, pre-Aldric-the-first, back when this land was nine squabbling kingdoms and the star-priests kept the archives instead of us. Records of a guardian spirit. A crown—not a piece of metal, a *being*—bound to the royal line as protector. It was supposed to watch over the kings. Warn them. Strengthen the borders. The star-priests bound it themselves, willingly, as an act of service." Her jaw tightened. "And somewhere across the centuries, the service curdled. The protector became a tenant. The tenant became a parasite. And the parasite learned to feed."

"On what?"

"Everything." The word came out of her like a tooth pulled. "On the wards. On the ley-lines under the capital—three of them have gone silent since midwinter; I thought it was survey error. On hedge-witches in the countryside whose workings fail for no reason. On charms that sputter. On—" She hesitated, and I watched her choose cruelty or kindness and couldn't tell which one she chose. "On girls in market towns whose storms get out of control. The Crown has been drinking the kingdom dry for years, slowly, a sip at a time, and last week it got hungry enough to swallow the borders whole. And last night it drank deep enough that the sky went dark, because the stars—" her voice cracked, actually cracked, and she hated herself for it, I could see her hating herself— "the stars aren't fires, Sebastian. They're the oldest workings there are. First magic. And something just ate the visible ones over our heads."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, and a neighbor called a greeting to someone, and the ordinariness of it was obscene.

"And the king," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Where does Aldric fit in your story?"

Ilsa closed her eyes.

"A crown needs a head," she said. "The guardian was bound to the royal line—it has to anchor somewhere, in someone, in each generation. For two hundred years the anchoring was gentle. Communion. Partnership. The king dreams, the crown watches, the realm holds. But a parasite doesn't want partnership." She opened her eyes, and they were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, and it was the terror that convinced me, more than any archive record could have. Ilsa Vane does not terrify easily. "Six months ago, Aldric stopped appearing at council. Illness, we were told. Then he reappeared, and he was eloquent, and gracious, and he remembered every name and signed every paper, and I told myself the fever had passed. But his shadow moves late. Half a breath. I've seen it twice, in torchlight, when I was paying attention. And last month I pulled his childhood medical records from the sealed vault, comparing, checking, and Sebastian—the man on the throne has a scar on his left hand from a hunting accident at nineteen. The king who signed the eastern tariffs has no scar. Smooth skin. Perfect. A king grown *new*, like the runes on a eaten ward. Full where they should be spent. Shape without anything inside."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My chair went over backward and neither of us looked at it.

"So the wards are dead, the stars are dead, the lines are dying, and the king is a puppet with something wearing him like a coat." I was shaking. I could hear it in my own voice and despised it. "Fine. Horrific. Apocalyptic. I'll add it to the list. But none of it—*none of it*—explains why you're in my kitchen, Ilsa. Because there are wardens whose whole life is this. There's an entire order. There are archives and armies and the Council of Corvane, such as it is. You walked past all of it, wounded, hunted by your own people by the look of that dressing, to bring a stolen sigil and a haunted seal to a *tinker*. To a convicted storm-caller living under a false name in the one city in the realm that hangs witches. So either you're desperate beyond sense, or—" and here it came, twelve years of it rising in my throat like floodwater, "—or this connects to *me*. To my exile. So say it. Whatever it is, say it now, all of it, and don't you dare manage me. Don't you dare decide how much truth I can carry. That's what you did last time. That's what you've *always* done."

She didn't flinch. That was the worst part. She took it full in the face like rain.

"The rite that sealed your magic," she said. "When you were fourteen. The binding-oaths the star-priests' successors laid on you, the sevenfold sealing that was supposed to cage the storm until you learned control." She swallowed. "It wasn't invented for you. It's in the old archives—it's the *only* defense the old records describe against the Hollow Crown. Containment. Starvation. Cut the parasite off from power and it weakens. The priests used it once, in the founding age, when the Crown first turned—they drove it dormant for a hundred years by sealing away everything it could feed on. And when your storm woke, and the order needed a way to contain you, they reached for the one tool they had." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "They used you as a practice run, Sebastian. Your exile wasn't justice and it wasn't mercy. It was a *test*. They sealed you to see if the sealing worked. And it worked beautifully, and they filed it, and they went back to ignoring the archives, and the Crown kept drinking, and I—" 

"Stop."

"*I helped write the filing.*"

"I said stop!" The lamp flared. Every charm hanging in my workshop—the luck-knots, the fever-beads, the little copper suns I sold to grandmothers—flared with me, a heartbeat of borrowed light, and Ilsa's eyes went wide, because twelve years of suppression shouldn't have allowed that, shouldn't have allowed a single spark, and we both understood at the same moment what it meant: the thing that ate the stars was pulling at my chains too, and the chains were *fraying*.

I gripped the table. Breathed. Made myself breathe, the way they taught me, the way she'd taught me, once, when she was my mentor and not my judge.

"The wards," I said, when I could speak again. "The wards that 'failed.' Who built them, Ilsa? Originally. Three hundred years ago."

She was quiet long enough that I knew.

"Storm-callers," she said. "The northern line especially. The Kavanagh line. Your family raised half the border wards, back when the realm still trusted your kind. Which means when the Crown ate them, it ate—" 

"—my inheritance." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "It ate my family's work. My blood's work. And the order knew, and the order hid it, and you *knew*, because you're the archivist, you're the one who reads everything, you must have known when you stood on that hillside in the rain and sentenced a fourteen-year-old girl to lose her name and her home and everyone she loved—that the power they were so afraid of was the same power the realm would be begging for the moment its walls came down." I leaned across the table. "Did you know? Yes or no."

"Yes," she whispered.

The word hit harder than any storm ever had.

"I suspected," she went on, wrecked, relentless, giving me everything because I'd demanded it, "and I chose to believe the order's version, because the order's version let me keep loving them and keep loving you, and I have had twelve years to learn that those two loves were never compatible, and I am *done*, Sebastian. I'm done. I burned my copy of the false findings. I took the sigil. They'll have marked me by now—I give it a day before the inquisitors start asking after me, two before they start asking after you, because I bought supplies in three towns under my own name like an idiot and because the Crown *already knows what you are*. It felt you at the sealing. It's been circling you for twelve years like a shark around a wound. Why do you think your charms have been working too well lately? Why do you think the fever broke under your hands yesterday?" She pushed the bleeding royal seal an inch toward me, and the black oak-shape seemed to drink the lamplight. "It's starving, and it knows exactly where the biggest unopened meal in the kingdom is sleeping. It's coming for you, Sebastian. Maybe not today. Maybe not this month. But the wards were the lock on the door, and the lock is gone, and you are the last storm-caller with your bloodline's strength left un-drunk, and the thing wearing the king has a hunger three hundred years deep."

Outside, far off in the direction of the palace hill, bells began to ring. Not alarm bells—ceremony bells, the bright cascading peal that meant royalty on the move. A procession. Tomorrow was the Feast of the Founding; the king would ride through the capital in golden light and receive the acclamation of a city that had no idea what wore his face.

Ilsa looked at the window, then at me, and something grim settled into her features—the look of a woman moving to the next item on a list she'd been composing for weeks.

"Then it's time you saw him," she said.

"*Saw* him? Saw the king? Ilsa, I'm a convicted exile with a forged license and a stall of lucky copper suns, I can't just—"

"He rides the Procession Road at midmorning. Fifty feet from ten thousand people. And you need to see his shadow move with your own eyes, because you're the only person alive who'll understand what it means, and because—" she rose, wincing at her side, and met my gaze with the old stubborn steadiness that had once been the axis my whole world turned around, "—because in a few days I'm going to ask you for something impossible, and I won't ask it unless you've seen it yourself. Unless you know. Get your cloak, Seb. We're going to go look at the face of the thing that ate the sky."

* * *

The bells were still ringing as I pulled my hood low and followed her out into the gray morning, the fallen ward-sigil wrapped in cloth beneath my coat and against my heart, where it sat cold and hollow and full all at once—like a promise, like a warning, like a grave with my family's name on it that somebody, someday, was going to have to answer for.
