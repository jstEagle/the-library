## 5. The King's Empty Eyes

The procession was announced by silence.

That was the strange thing about it—Corvane had no love for its king, but it had a healthy appetite for spectacle, and on any ordinary day a royal visit would have brought the crowds out three deep with ribbons and roasted chestnuts and children hoisted onto shoulders. Instead, when the criers went through the streets at dawn proclaiming that His Majesty King Aldric the Second would walk the Grand Processional to the Cathedral of Lawful Order at midday, the city simply... went quiet. Shutters closed. Markets thinned. The same people who had burned the tinker quarter's edge two nights past and then hidden Sebastian in their cellars now drew their curtains as though the king were not a blessing coming down the street but weather to be endured indoors.

"They're afraid of him," Ilsa said.

She stood at the window of the safehouse above the dyer's shop—a widow named Maren who asked no questions and took no payment beyond a promise Sebastian intended to keep—and she watched the emptying avenue with the flat expression she wore like armor. Her wound had been bound in clean linen; her color was better. The sigil of the fallen border-ward sat wrapped in oilcloth on the table between them like a dead thing they hadn't yet decided how to bury.

"People are always afraid of kings," Sebastian said. "That's half of what a king is for."

"Not like this." Ilsa turned. "I've stood honor guard for Aldric at eleven processions. Children threw flowers. Old men wept. He was—" She stopped, jaw tightening, as if the words themselves were evidence of her year of lying. "He was loved. Whatever else I did or didn't do, I never lied about that."

Sebastian looked down at her own hands. They were tinker's hands now, nicked and stained, hands that had spent twelve years learning to be unremarkable. Two nights ago those hands had pulled a warden back from the edge of bleeding out on her floor, and the whole time her magic had strained against its seals like a dog against a chain, and she had hated it, and she had hated that some buried part of her hadn't hated it at all.

"So we look at him," she said. "Up close. That's what you want?"

"That's what we need. If the king is consumed—if it's real—I need you to see it with your own eyes. Not my report. Not the sigil." Ilsa crossed the room and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Sebastian could smell the salve on the bandages, the road dust beneath it. "Because if you see it and still refuse, then I'll know there's nothing left to argue. But if you don't look first, every part of you will find a way to call me a liar. You've had twelve years of practice."

The cruelty of it landed precisely, the way Ilsa's words always did—the woman had never once missed. Sebastian held her gaze and found, infuriatingly, no apology in it. Only the old stubborn certainty that had exiled a frightened girl rather than let the council execute her, dressed up all these years later as honesty.

"Fine," Sebastian said. "But we do it my way. No uniforms. No wardens. If your order has hunters in this city, and we both know it does, one glimpse of Vane gray and we're both in irons before the king finishes his prayer."

"I know how to disappear," Ilsa said.

"You knew how to be obeyed," Sebastian corrected. "It's different. I'll show you."

* * *

Corvane on a processional day, with its shutters drawn and its bells muffled, looked like a city holding its breath. They went out through Maren's cellar and along the undercity ways—drainage tunnels from the old river district, bricked over generations ago and colonized since by smugglers, beggars, and the particular species of child who ran messages for coin and asked no questions. Sebastian had known these tunnels for twelve years the way a spider knows its web. Every third crossing she'd left a charm: a thread of copper bent into a knot that hummed faintly when someone passed who didn't belong. It wasn't magic, officially. Officially it was tinker's craft, a novelty, a toy. Unofficially it was the only magic she'd allowed herself since the sealing-oaths, small enough to hide behind a tradesman's license, and today it earned its keep twice—once flagging a crown patrol on the Merchant Stair, once warning them off a tunnel mouth where two men in plain clothes stood too straight to be anything but soldiers.

"Left here," Sebastian murmured. "There's a grate comes up behind the chandler's on Bellows Row. We'll come out inside the crowd line, thirty feet from the barrier rope. Close enough."

"Close enough for what?"

"For you to tell me whether I'm seeing what you're seeing." She glanced back at Ilsa in her borrowed coat, brown wool, no insignia, her white-streaked braid coiled and pinned under a dyer's cap that made her look like a dockworker's widow. It should have made her ridiculous. It didn't. Even disguised, even wounded, Ilsa Vane moved through the world like someone the world owed an explanation to. "And for me to decide something."

"Decide what?"

Sebastian pushed up through the grate into grey daylight and cold air that tasted of coal smoke and fear. "Whether he's worth saving."

She heard Ilsa stop on the ladder below her, just for a heartbeat. Then the warden climbed out into the light beside her and said nothing at all.

They folded into the thin crowd along Bellows Row. There was no jostling, no laughter, none of the animal warmth of a Corvane crowd. People stood shoulder to shoulder and apart, eyes forward, hands quiet. A mother near them kept her palm flat against her son's chest the entire time, not holding him so much as covering his heart, as if the procession might reach in and take something out of it. Down the row, an old man mouthed a prayer to saints Sebastian's grandmother would have laughed at. Nobody spoke aloud.

Then came the sound.

Not trumpets—there were trumpets, but they arrived second, oddly late, like thunder after lightning. First came the sound of boots: the Royal Guard in their black-lacquered plate, forty of them in perfect step, and their footfalls hit the cobblestones with a rhythm too exact, too identical, each boot landing on the same fraction of the beat until it stopped sounding like marching and started sounding like ticking. Sebastian had seen the Guard march a hundred times from market stalls, half-attentive. She had never noticed the clockwork of it. Had it always been like this? Or had something changed?

Behind the Guard rode the banner-bearers, and behind them—

"There," Ilsa breathed.

King Aldric the Second came down the Grand Processional on foot, as tradition demanded, walking the stones of his capital bareheaded so his people could see his face.

He was smiling.

That was the first wrongness, and it was worse than horror would have been. He looked well. He looked better than well—he looked like a portrait, like the idealized engravings sold outside the mint: silver-gold hair, strong jaw, the famous Aldric blue eyes, upright and hale at fifty-one years. He waved. He inclined his head to the shuttered windows. He paused at the cathedral steps where the Provost of Lawful Order waited and exchanged words that were, by every account of the lip-readers' art, gracious and warm.

And his shadow moved a half-second behind him.

Sebastian saw it because she had spent twelve years teaching herself to watch for exactly one thing: the places where the world leaked. A charm working too well. A fever breaking at a touch. Magic left stains and seams, and she had become, without wanting to, an expert in seams. The king walked, and the sun fell full on him from the east, and his shadow lay long across the cobblestones toward the crowd—and the shadow lagged. When he lifted his arm to wave, the shadow's arm rose a breath afterward, a delayed echo, a reflection that had to think about it. When he turned his head toward the Provost, the shadow's head turned like a follower catching up, unhurried, almost lazy.

No one else saw it. Of course no one else saw it. Shadows lagged for everyone, didn't they? Everyone knew that. Everyone had always known that. The thought arrived in her mind smooth and reasonable and utterly foreign, wearing her own voice, and Sebastian's skin went cold from nape to wrist, because she recognized that sensation. That was what compulsion felt like. That was the taste of someone else's suggestion sliding into your thoughts and calling itself yours. She'd felt it once before, at fifteen, from an inquisitor's truth-spell, and she had sworn never to forget the flavor.

"It's pushing," she whispered. "Right now, at us, at everyone. It's telling the crowd this is normal. Look normal. Nothing is wrong."

"I feel it." Ilsa's hand had found hers without either of them deciding it, gripping hard enough to hurt. Her knuckles were white. "Gods, I've been feeling it for a year. I thought it was my own cowardice."

"It is," Sebastian said, and squeezed back. "It's also him."

Because she had stopped watching the shadow and started watching the man, and the man was worse.

Aldric smiled, and the smile reached his mouth and his cheeks and the crinkles at the corners of his eyes, every muscle performing delight in perfect sequence—but it reached nothing behind them. His eyes were the problem. They were open. They tracked. They moved from the Provost to the Guard captain to the banner of the realm with correct, attentive motion, and they were empty the way a house is empty—not dark, not hostile, but vacant, rooms with the furniture gone, light falling through windows into space where a life used to be. Once—only once, in the whole length of Bellows Row—something flickered in them. A hitch. His smile stuttered at one corner, dropped, reassembled. For a single instant his face did something no portrait ever showed: it *strained*, like a swimmer surfacing, like a man pressing his palms against the underside of ice.

His lips parted. What came out, pitched only for the Provost beside him, was lost to distance—but his throat worked around it, and his right hand, hanging at his side, curled slowly inward until the knuckles went bloodless, and the fingers spelled, clumsily, unmistakably, against the fabric of his royal coat:

*H-u-r-t-s.*

Then the flicker guttered out. The smile returned, seamless. The hand relaxed. And the shadow caught up with him, and settled, and was still.

Sebastian discovered she was shaking. Not with fear—or not only fear. Something had cracked open in her chest and she couldn't have named it to save her life.

"Aldric could spell before he could ride," Ilsa whispered. Her voice had gone ragged. "Finger-cant. All the royal children learn it. His sister invented half the court signs. He—" She swallowed. "Twelve years I served that man. He knighted me. He remembered my father's name. He asked after my mother every winter, every single winter, and meant it."

"Ilsa—"

"He's in there." She turned, and her eyes were wet and blazing at once, and for a moment Sebastian saw not the Warden of the Eastern Marches but the young officer who had stood in a rain-soaked square twelve years ago and pronounced a sentence with tears freezing on her face. "You saw it. Don't tell me you didn't see it. He's drowning in his own body and he's *signaling*, he's fighting—"

"I saw it." Sebastian made herself say it steadily. Made herself hold the image instead of looking away: the perfect face, the lagging shadow, the hand spelling *hurts* while ten thousand charms' worth of borrowed calm pressed down on a city too frightened to wave. "I saw it."

The procession passed. The Guard's boots ticked away eastward toward the cathedral, the banners following, the king walking bareheaded among his people like a bride down an aisle—smiling, waving, hollow, and somewhere far behind his own eyes, screaming.

The crowd began to disperse with the same terrible quiet it had gathered in, shutters opening one by one like eyes deciding it was safe to blink.

* * *

They walked back through the undercity in silence. It lasted until the third junction, where Sebastian's copper knots hung humming on their wires, and then she stopped, put her hand against the damp brick wall, and let out a breath that shook more than she wanted it to.

"I keep trying to make it simple," she said. "That's what I do. Things break, things get mended, things get replaced. Twelve years of hinges and kettle-seals and fever-charms. Simple." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "And now the king of the realm is a puppet with a man stuffed inside it, and the thing pulling the strings is drinking the sky dry, and the woman who ruined my life is standing in a dyer's cap asking me to fix it."

"Yes," Ilsa said. No flinch. No defense. Just the word, offered plainly, like a blade laid hilt-first on a table.

"The rite you described. Pour everything through the Crown and burn it out." Sebastian turned to face her. In the lantern-light of the tunnel her own hands looked pale and steady, which was a lie her body was telling for her benefit, and she appreciated it. "Everything means everything. You said it yourself. My power or my life, and likely both."

"I said likely."

"You said *likely*." She stepped closer. "Here's what you don't understand, Warden. You think you're asking me to die for a king. You're not. You're asking me to go back. To open every door I nailed shut at seventeen and stand in the middle of all of it again—the storm, the village, the harvest, the faces. You want me to pour twelve years of sealed night through that thing wearing Aldric like a coat." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I don't know if I can survive that. But I know I can't survive being the person who watched a man spell *hurts* with his own fingers and walked away."

For the first time since she'd bled on Sebastian's doorstep, Ilsa Vane looked uncertain.

"Then we find another way," she said quietly. "There are archives. The wardens keep records going back nine centuries—rites, counter-rituals, precedents. If there's another path, I'll tear the vaults apart to find it. I swear it."

"Don't swear," Sebastian said. "Swearing is what got us here." She pushed off from the wall and started down the tunnel toward Maren's, then stopped, because at the far end of the passage, where the lamplight failed, someone was waiting.

She knew the silhouette before she knew the face. Broad shoulders, grey traveling cloak, the particular patient stillness of a hunter who has already decided the chase is over. But it was the voice, when it came, soft and echoing down nine hundred years of drainage brick, that stopped her heart.

"Hello, little storm," said Inquisitor Hale. "We need to talk before it uses my mouth again."
