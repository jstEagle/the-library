## 6. Inquisitor Hale

The undercity of Corvane smelled the way old secrets did—damp stone, tallow smoke, and the sour iron of drains that had never once been cleaned in four hundred years. Sebastian moved through it with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, past the shuttered lantern-shrines and the beggars who sold directions instead of asking alms, following a thread of rumor she didn't entirely trust.

*The Warden's clerk was seen going down. Alone. After dark.*

Three days since the procession. Three days since she had stood close enough to King Aldric to count the pores on his face and watch his shadow lag behind him like a dog that had lost interest in its master. Three nights of not sleeping, of listening to the silence where the stars should have been, of feeling that low hum beneath her breastbone—the storm she'd buried twelve years ago turning over in its grave.

She'd told herself she was down here to find out whether Ilsa Vane's story held water. That was half true. The other half was simpler and worse: she couldn't stand her own ceiling anymore. She kept imagining the sky above it, black as a burned page, and every hour she spent under a roof felt like being buried alive at a courteous pace.

The rumor led her to the Cistern Ward, where Corvane's oldest aqueduct had collapsed into a cathedral of broken arches and standing black water. Squatters' laundry hung between pillars like funeral banners. Somewhere below, water dripped with the patience of geology.

She felt him before she saw him.

Not his footsteps—his *weight*. The same pressure she'd felt at the procession, that sensation of air being drunk rather than breathed, concentrated here in the dark until her teeth ached with it. Her suppressed magic rose against her will, pressing at the seals Ilsa's order had carved into her bones, and for one dizzying second the puddles around her feet trembled with rings too small to be caused by anything so crude as sound.

"Inquisitor Hale," she said quietly, because hiding from a man like this was a courtesy he no longer deserved—or a cruelty she no longer had the stomach for.

He stepped out from behind a pillar as if he'd been waiting all evening for exactly that sentence.

He had spared her once. She was seventeen, soaked to the skin, lightning still crawling off her fingers in fat blue worms, half her village's barley field charred to glass behind her—and Hale had looked at her across that smoking ruin, looked at the terrified girl inside the monster the villagers were screaming about, and said, *Run, child. And don't stop running until you're someone else.*

Twelve years later, the man who had given her a new name stood in the drowned dark wearing his old one like a coat that no longer fit.

He had always been lean; now he was gaunt, hollowed, his uniform immaculate but hanging off him the way sails hang off a becalmed ship. His face in the lamplight was gray as the water. But it was his eyes that stopped Sebastian's breath—because they were wrong in precisely the way the king's had been wrong. Not empty. Worse than empty. *Crowded.* As if something behind them were pressed against the glass, looking out, taking notes.

"Sebastian Kavanagh." Her true name, spoken softly, reverently, in the dark. "You kept it. All these years, you actually kept it."

"I've been careful," she said. "Apparently not careful enough."

"No one is careful enough anymore." He laughed—a dry, rusted sound with nothing in it of humor. "That's rather the point."

* * *

She should have run. Every instinct honed by twelve years of invisibility screamed it: turn, walk, lose yourself in the warren of stairs and sluice-tunnels where even an inquisitor needed a map. Instead she found herself stepping closer, over the bodies of dead lanterns and into the radius of his lamp, because there was something in his voice that the thing in the king's voice had lacked.

Something human. Something drowning.

"Hale." She kept her tone level, though her heart was hammering hard enough to hurt. "Why did you have me followed? Why come yourself? You could have sent thirty men."

"Thirty men would have arrested you." He swayed slightly, caught himself against a pillar. Up close she could see what the lamplight had hidden: thin dark lines crawling beneath the skin of his neck, branching like frost ferns or river deltas, moving when he wasn't. "And I needed—" His jaw clenched. Sweat beaded at his temple despite the cold. "I needed to warn you. Before I'm not able to."

"Warn me about what? I already know about the Crown. I've seen the king."

"You've seen the king." He nodded slowly, and the movement had a jerky, puppet-adjacent quality that turned her stomach. "Then you know more than my order does. More than the council dares. Good. Then listen, girl, because I don't know how long I have before I stop being the one talking." He gripped the pillar with white-knuckled fingers. "It's not just him. It was never just him. The wards fell because it ate them—it eats everything that binds. Oaths. Wards. Names." His eyes fixed on hers, and for a moment the crowded darkness behind them receded and it was only Hale again, tired and old and afraid. "Sealing-oaths, Sebastian. What do you think is holding your storm shut?"

The words went through her like cold water through cloth.

"The oaths Ilsa's order laid on me," she whispered. "They're still—"

"Intact. For now. You're a locked door it hasn't gotten around to yet." He smiled, and the smile was terrible because part of it was genuine relief. "But you felt it tonight, didn't you? Under the cistern. Your magic leaning toward it like a plant toward light. It calls to what it means to drink. It's been calling to you since the night the stars went out."

Her hand rose without her permission to her sternum, to the place where the hum lived. "How do you know all this?"

"Because it's in me." He said it flatly, the way a man announces a terminal diagnosis he's had time to make peace with. "I found the first evidence eleven months ago. A ledger in the royal archive showing ward-tithes rerouted to the palace. I brought it to my superiors. They thanked me and promoted me sideways. So I kept digging alone—which was exactly what it wanted. It doesn't fight investigators, child. It *waits* for them. It let me get close enough to the truth to touch it, and then—" He lifted his collar aside.

The lines beneath his skin converged at the base of his skull, where they formed a shape she recognized instantly and would remember until she died: a circlet. A crown of dark filaments, etched into living flesh.

"It's been growing in me for a year," Hale said. "Slowly. It likes to savor the strong ones. I can still hold the door most hours. Some hours I can't." He looked away, toward the black water. "Last week I lost two hours and woke up writing a report I don't remember writing. It was flawless. Convincing. My superiors loved it. That's how it works—not through monsters. Through men doing their jobs beautifully."

* * *

"Why tell me?" Sebastian asked. "Why risk it? If it speaks through you, if it hears everything you hear—"

"It does." He grimaced. "It's hearing this conversation. I can feel it listening, the way you feel weather coming. Which is why we don't have long, and why you need to understand the one thing it will try very hard to prevent you from understanding." He pushed off the pillar and took a step toward her, and she flinched back before she could stop herself. He noticed. He had the grace to look wounded. "I spared you once, girl. Do you remember why?"

"Because you were soft," she said, too fast, too sharp. "Because the law wanted a monster and you saw a frightened kid."

"Because I'd read the old ward-codes." His voice dropped. "Because I knew that a storm-caller's power isn't the danger. An untrained one is. I let you go because killing you would have been easy and leaving you would have been a gamble—and I gambled that you'd learn to hold it. Have you?"

"I haven't used it in twelve years."

"That's not holding it." Gently, terribly gently. "That's sitting on it. There's a difference, and I suspect you're about to learn how wide it is." He exhaled, long and shaking. "Here is what I came down here to say, while I am still the one saying it. The Crown fears you. Not the warden, not the wardens-general, not the priests. *You.* A sealed storm-caller inside the city walls is the one piece on the board it cannot predict, because it has never seen what happens when someone opens that door on purpose, at a time and place of their choosing. Everything else it has eaten was offered to it slowly, by degrees, by men who kept waiting for permission." His hand shot out and caught her wrist—his grip fever-hot, the pulse beneath it stuttering like a failing lamp. "When the door opens, be the one holding the handle."

"Hale—"

"And the other thing." His eyes were wet now, and the crowded darkness behind them was pressing closer; she could see it straining at him like current through a dam. "The other thing is a favor. A debt, if you like, twelve years owed. When it takes me fully—and it will, soon, within days—I will become a very effective instrument. I know the order's safehouses. I know the warden's movements. I know *you*, and everything I know, it will know." His breath came ragged. "There are ways to stop that. Ways that require a storm-caller. And I am asking you, Sebastian Kavanagh, formally, as the man who once gave you your life—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it.

"—to take it back."

* * *

"No." She pulled her wrist free and stepped back, and the puddles shivered. "No. Find another way. There are surgeons, there are—"

"Surgeons?" He laughed, and this time the laugh had something else in it, something that curled at the edges and wasn't entirely his. He heard it too. His face went white and he clamped his mouth shut, wrestling himself still, and when he spoke again each word came out slow and deliberate, a man walking a ledge. "The Crown cannot be cut out. Only burned. You know this. You watched the king's shadow drag a half-second behind his heels, and some part of you already knows what has to happen, to him and to me, and you came down here anyway because hope is the last luxury of the condemned."

"There is always another way."

"For other people, perhaps. Not for me." He straightened, and for a moment—incongruously, unbearably—he looked like the man on the barley field again: tired, kind, immovable. "Do you know what I did after I let you run? I filed a report saying the storm-caller died in her own lightning. I falsified a death record and slept fine for years. And then eleven months ago I found the truth about Aldric and thought, *ah, here it is, the bill.* Every mercy has a price, child. Mine has come due with interest."

The dark lines on his neck were moving faster now. Branching. Climbing toward his jaw.

"I can feel it reading my intentions," he whispered. "It knows why I brought you here. It's amused." His eyes found hers, and in them Sebastian saw the last of the man, clinging like a climber to a cliff-face, and beneath him the drop, and beneath the drop something vast and patient and hungry, looking up. "It wants me to ask you for mercy so it can punish the asking. So I'll ask for justice instead. Inquisitor's privilege. We're allowed to demand the sentence we deserve."

"Hale, please—"

"*Now*, girl." And then his spine arched and his head snapped back and the voice that came out of him was layered, doubled, his own voice and underneath it a chorus of older voices speaking in perfect unison, and the temperature of the whole drowned cathedral dropped as though winter had leaned in through the stones:

**"HE IS A DOOR AND WE ARE ALREADY THROUGH IT."**

Hale's body shuddered. His hand flew to his own throat—not strangling himself, *holding* himself, gripping his jaw shut like a man plugging a breach. His eyes found hers over his own knuckles, and they were clear, they were his, and they were begging.

Sebastian's magic answered before her conscience could.

It surged against the sealing-oaths like a flood against a gate, and this time she didn't fight it—she *aimed* it, dragged it up through twelve years of scar tissue and poured it down her arm, into her hand, and the world went blue-white. Thunder that made no sound cracked through the cistern. The black water leapt. Every dead lantern in the ward flared alight for one impossible heartbeat, and in that heartbeat Sebastian Kavanagh, exiled storm-caller, seller of lucky charms, reached out and touched two fingers to the inquisitor's brow.

She meant to unmake the filaments. Burn the parasite out of him the way she dreamed of burning it out of the king.

What happened instead was that she *felt* him—all of him, the whole architecture of the man, his griefs and grudging mercies and the falsified death record he carried like a stone, and wrapped through all of it the Crown's roots, deep as oak, grown into places no fire could reach without burning the tree. There was no separating them. There never had been. The Crown didn't wear its victims.

It became their marrow.

So she did the only thing left, the thing his eyes had been begging for, and she hated herself with her whole heart while she did it, and she would go on hating herself for it every day of whatever life she had remaining.

The light went out of the lanterns. The thunder rolled away down the tunnels, mourning.

Hale folded to the wet stone like a man finally setting down a weight he'd carried for miles. She caught him—of course she caught him—and lowered him gently, cradling his head above the black water, and for a few seconds his face was peaceful in a way it hadn't been since she'd arrived. Young, almost. The face of a man who had once read old ward-codes late into the night and decided, against all procedure, that a frightened child was worth a gamble.

"Thank you," he mouthed. No voice came. Perhaps none was needed.

Then his eyes sharpened, focused on something past her shoulder, and his dying hand seized her sleeve with sudden desperate strength, and he rasped out three words that turned her blood to ice water:

"She lied to you."

"Hale—who? Who lied—"

But the light was already gone out of him, guttering like the lamps, and the last breath he drew hissed through his teeth in something that might have been a name: *"V—"*

It never finished. The cistern fell silent except for the dripping, patient as ever, and Sebastian knelt in the dark holding a dead man who had spared her once and damned her twice—once with mercy, once with truth—and somewhere above her, far above the drowned arches and the funeral-banner laundry, Corvane lay starless beneath a sky with nothing left to say.

*She lied to you.*

Sebastian stayed until the cold drove her up the stairs, and all the way home through the blind streets, the words rang in her skull like a struck bell, keeping time with the storm that stirred beneath her breastbone, waking.

Tomorrow, she would find Ilsa Vane.

Tomorrow, she would ask her what, exactly, had been in those wards besides exile.
