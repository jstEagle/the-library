## 7. Twelve Years of Rain

The rain began at noon, and by dusk Ilsa Vane understood that it was not rain at all.

She had been in the village of Harrow's Bend for six days, doing what wardens did—walking the boundary stones, checking the old charms nailed above doorways, listening to the small grievances of small places. Harrow's Bend was a wheat village, forty roofs and a mill, tucked into a fold of hills where the king's road forgot to go. The people were plain-spoken and pious in the way of folk who lived close to the soil, and they had been kind to her in the grudging way country people are kind to anyone wearing the grey-and-silver of the Wardens.

They had also, all of them, been watching the girl.

Ilsa had noticed it on her first evening. A child with a scabbed knee who stopped mid-run when Sebastian Kavanagh passed. A miller who fell silent mid-sentence as the girl carried water past his window. Not hostility—nothing so clean. It was the watchfulness of people who had decided long ago to keep something in the corner of their eye, the way you watch a dog that bit once.

The girl herself was fourteen, all elbows and dark hair and a smile too quick for someone so watched. She had brought Ilsa bread on her second day, unasked, and stood in the doorway of the warden's rented room with the loaf held out like a shield.

"You're the one who checks the wards," she'd said. "My mam says I'm not to talk to you. I'm talking to you anyway."

"Why?"

"Because you're the only person in this village who doesn't already know everything about me." A crooked grin. "It's restful."

Isla had taken the bread. She should have sent the girl away. Every instinct trained into her across ten years of service said *distance, warden; distance is mercy*. But the girl's eyes were grey as storm-glass, and there was something underneath the quickness in them—something tired, something braced—that Ilsa recognized because she had seen it in mirrors before her own talent was bound.

"Sebastian," she had said. "That's an old name."

"It's a stupid name. Mam says my father chose it from a book."

"What would you rather be called?"

The girl had shrugged, and for a moment the grin fell away entirely, and what looked out from beneath it was not fourteen years old at all. "Anything. Anyone. Someone whose name doesn't make the whole room go quiet."

That had been four days ago. Four days, and Ilsa had let the girl follow her along the boundary stones asking questions—*what's the capital like, have you ever called lightning, does the binding hurt*—and had answered more of them than she should have, and had told herself it was harmless, that the child's talent was thin country hedge-magic at best, a whisper of weather-sense like half the farmwives in the province claimed.

She had been wrong. She had been magnificently, catastrophically wrong, and the proof of it was falling on the wheat.

* * *

It started as pressure. Ilsa felt it first in the binding-scar behind her ear—the cold knot of her own sealed talent aching like an old break before weather. She straightened in the miller's yard, where she'd been re-inking a faded threshold charm, and looked south.

There were no clouds. That was the first wrong thing. There were no clouds anywhere, and yet the light had gone the color of pewter, and the air tasted of copper and ozone, and every hair on Ilsa's arms stood up beneath her coat.

Then the sky over the southern fields darkened—not clouded, *darkened*, as though a hand had pulled a curtain across the sun—and the rain came down in ropes.

Not drops. Ropes. Water falling with weight behind it, hammering the wheat flat, and threaded through it, flickering without sound, lightning crawled along the ground like something living looking for a way inside. The thunder didn't crack; it *rolled*, continuous, a sound like the sky grinding its teeth, and Ilsa stood in the miller's yard with ink still wet on her fingers and knew—with the certainty that comes to wardens once or twice in a career, the certainty you pray you never need—

*This is a person. This is a person coming apart.*

She ran.

* * *

She found the girl at the edge of the southern field, at the base of the old standing stone the villagers called the Wolfstone—a squat granite thing, lichen-covered, older than any record. Sebastian stood with her back against it, arms flung wide, head thrown back, and she was *screaming*. No words. Just a raw, animal sound torn out of her, and around her the air bent and swam as though the world were seen through water, and every bolt of ground-lightning in the field bent toward her and died an arm's length from her skin, drinking inward, feeding her, and she could not stop it, and she did not know how, and she was drowning in her own power while the harvest of forty families drowned beside her.

Ilsa had bound her own gift at nineteen. She knew exactly what this was. She knew, with sickening clarity, that the girl had perhaps minutes before the storm either burned her out or broke loose entirely—and if it broke loose over a village, over open wheat, over dry August—

She ran into the rain.

The water hit like fists. She lost her hat in three strides, lost her footing in five, went down hard in the mud between the wheat rows and got up and kept running, shouting the girl's name, and the girl couldn't hear her, couldn't hear anything, eyes white and rolling, hair lifting off her skull in a dark corona, and the lightning was no longer dying at her skin—it was crawling *up her arms*, silver veins under brown skin, beautiful and obscene—

Ilsa did the only thing left. She threw herself against the standing stone beside the girl, pressed her palm flat to the granite, and tore the seal off her own binding.

Ten years of caged storm came out of her like a dam failing.

She had forgotten. In ten years of grey discipline and ink and ledgers she had genuinely forgotten what it was—the *joy* of it, the terrible sweetness, power moving through her like her own blood finally remembered. She caught the girl's wild output the way you catch a falling body: arms around her, forehead to forehead, and opened every gate she had, and *pulled*.

For one heartbeat, two, they were one storm together—Ilsa felt the girl's whole self laid open to her, felt the frantic, formless terror of a talent far vaster than the vessel holding it, felt the child underneath screaming *I didn't mean to, I didn't mean to, make it stop*—and Ilsa poured herself into that scream like a mother singing a nightmare away, shaping the chaos, giving it banks, giving it a bed, dragging the storm down and down until the rain thinned to a whisper and the lightning guttered and the pewter light cracked open to ordinary gold afternoon.

Silence. Steam rising off flattened wheat. Somewhere a dog barking, far away, the only sound left in the world.

The girl collapsed into her arms, and Ilsa held her, both of them shaking, mud to their knees, and above them the standing stone was scorched black in the shape of two handprints side by side.

"Well," the girl whispered, hoarse and half-conscious, with the ghost of that crooked grin. "You *have* called lightning."

Ilsa laughed, or sobbed, or something between. Then she saw the villagers at the field's edge—all of them, forty families, come running with scythes and pitchforks and faces gone grey—and she stopped laughing, and gathered the unconscious girl closer, and understood that everything was about to change.

* * *

The Warden's court convened in Harrow's Bend's chapel that night, because protocol demanded a local hearing before transfer, and because Ilsa insisted on it—insisted, over the objections of the two junior wardens dispatched from the provincial seat, on facing the village herself.

She stood before forty families in a room that smelled of tallow and wet wool, and she gave them the truth, because they deserved it and because lying to them would have been the last cowardice available to her.

"The girl's talent broke containment," she said. "It was not her intent. It was not malice. It was an untrained gift of extraordinary magnitude, and it nearly killed her before it endangered any of you."

A miller—Brannoc, the one who went quiet when she passed—stood up. His face was hollow. Behind him, through the chapel window, his flattened fields gleamed in the moonlight like a drowned meadow.

"Nearly killed *her*," he repeated. "Madam Warden, we've watched that child since she was six. We've watched and we've said nothing and we've prayed nothing, and now look." He gestured at the window, at the ruin. "Three seasons of planting. Our seed-corn. Our winter. What do we eat in March, Warden? What do our children eat?"

No one answered him. No one needed to. The arithmetic was visible on every face: a dead harvest in a border province meant hunger, meant winter deaths, meant the slow cruelty of want. And the law was clear besides—an uncontrolled working that destroyed livelihoods drew mandatory sanction. Execution or exile were the only sentences the statute allowed, and the age of the offender changed nothing but the method.

"Bind her," Brannoc said. "Like you're bound, Warden. We've all seen the scar. Bind her and let her stay."

"I can't." Ilsa's voice came out level, which surprised her. "A binding requires a full conclave of wardens and a rite that takes nine days. She'll be transferred to the provincial seat within the week, and the tribunal there will—" She stopped. They all knew what the tribunal would do. Provincial tribunals did not spend nine days and a conclave on a fourteen-year-old farm girl with no family influence. They spent an hour and a headsman.

An old woman near the back spoke then—Sebastian's grandmother, Ilsa realized, the only kin the girl had, a tiny fierce woman with hands like tree roots. "You stopped it," the old woman said. "The village says you walked into that storm and pulled her out of it. So you have authority over her. Take your authority, Warden, and use it."

"I have no such—"

"You have *something*," the old woman said, and her voice cracked like a whip. "You have whatever made you run into the rain for a child you'd known four days. Use it."

The silence afterward lasted a long time. Ilsa stood in it and felt her whole life pivot on a hinge, and thought of the girl's voice in the doorway four days ago—*you're the only person in this village who doesn't already know everything about me*—and thought of the scream inside the storm—*make it stop*—and understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever she chose tonight she would carry for the rest of her life.

Exile. The statute permitted a warden of senior rank, acting alone in remote jurisdictions, to commute execution to perpetual banishment—a provision written for cases of political embarrassment, rarely used, never forgiven. The exiled party lost name, homeland, and legal existence. The sanctioning warden assumed permanent liability: any crime committed by the exile fell upon the warden's own record, forever. It was, the manual said, a sentence shared by two people for the remainder of both their lives.

It was also the only door out of the chapel that didn't lead to a block.

"Sebastian Kavanagh," Ilsa said, and heard her own voice go strange and formal, the voice of the law speaking through her mouth whether she willed it or not, "is sentenced to perpetual exile from the provinces of the Crown, effective immediately. I assume liability for her conduct. So witnessed, so entered."

The old woman closed her eyes. Brannoc sat down slowly, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far. And somewhere in the chapel's shadowed rafters, rain that wasn't there seemed to fall anyway, soft and endless, the memory of a storm settling over everything.

* * *

They left before dawn, because exile parties traveled before dawn, before the village woke, before goodbyes could become scenes.

Ilsa walked the girl—no, the woman now; the law had made her a non-person overnight, and non-persons traveled unshackled precisely because the law no longer troubled itself with them—down the cart-track toward the crossroads where the south road began. Sebastian carried one bundle: a change of clothes, the book her father's name had come from, a heel of bread. Everything else—every roof, every grave, every year—stayed behind.

The girl hadn't cried. That was the worst of it, Ilsa thought. She hadn't cried, hadn't raged, hadn't begged. She'd listened to the sentence in the chapel with her chin up and her hands folded, and when it was done she'd looked at Ilsa across forty bowed heads and her eyes had said, clearly as speech: *you too. You're in the storm with me now. I saw you choose it.*

At the crossroads the sky was going grey-pink in the east, and the wheat fields stretched away gold and drowned, and Corvane lay south, the magic-hating city, the one place in the kingdom where a storm-caller might actually disappear, swallowed by a million strangers who hated what she was on principle and therefore never bothered to look closely.

"This is where I leave the law's protection and become its debt," Ilsa said. "Understand what happens now. If you so much as light a candle with a wish, it lands on my record. If you're caught working magic in Corvane, we hang together. You will never use your gift. Not once. Not to save a life. Not even your own. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you." Flat. Fourteen years old and flat as a blade.

"And you hate me for it."

Sebastian turned then, and looked at her, and Ilsa braced for the hatred she deserved.

What she saw instead frightened her more. The girl's eyes were dry and very steady, and behind the steadiness something enormous was being carefully, deliberately folded up and put away—the way Ilsa herself had once folded away her own storm, scar by scar, year by year.

"My grandmother says loyalty is just love that's found its job," Sebastian said. "So no, Warden. I don't hate you. That's the problem." She adjusted the bundle on her shoulder. "Ask me again in twelve years."

She walked south, small against the vast pale morning, and did not look back, and Ilsa Vane stood at the crossroads of Harrow's Bend until she was a speck, then nothing, and then Ilsa went and reported her own sanction to the provincial seat, took her censure, took her demotion, took twelve years of gray service and colder rooms, and told no one—not her order, not her superiors, not the tribunal that never asked—why she had really commuted the sentence.

Why she had run into the rain.

Some truths you bury so deep even you can't dig them up again. But the crown has a way of making everything surface, and twelve years later—wounded, hunted, bleeding on a tinker's doorstep in Corvane with a fallen sigil in her fist—Ilsa Vane was about to learn that buried things, like storms, always wake.
