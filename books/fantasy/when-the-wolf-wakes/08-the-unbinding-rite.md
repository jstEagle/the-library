## 8. The Unbinding Rite

The temple had no roof left to speak of—just ribs of white stone reaching up into a sky that still refused to remember its stars. Sebastian stood in the doorway with her satchel strap cutting into her shoulder and felt the building notice her.

That was the only word for it. The old star-temples were supposed to be dead things, stripped and salted when Corvane turned on magic a century ago, but this one, half-swallowed by the frost plains' scrub pines, had a pulse of attention in its broken walls. The air inside tasted like the moment before lightning.

"You feel it too," Ilsa said. She was already three steps in, one hand resting on the hilt of a sword she'd barely slept beside for six days. Her wound had been stitched twice and wept through both stitchings; she walked like a woman pretending the ground was level. "The priest who hid these texts chose his ruin well. Even the Crown doesn't like coming here."

"The Crown doesn't like *me*," Sebastian said. "Let's not flatter the architecture."

But it wasn't flattery. As they crossed the threshold, the pressure against her sealed magic eased—not gone, never gone, twelve years of binding-oaths did not loosen for scenery—but it changed, the way a fist becomes an open hand hovering near your throat. Waiting. Asking.

The main hall opened around them like the ribcage of something enormous. Moonlight—thin, gray, starless moonlight—poured through the shattered vault above and fell across a floor worked with constellations, silver inlay gone green with moss but still legible: the Hunter, the Wolf, the Weeping Sisters, all the old figures Corvane had renamed or erased. At the far end, an altar of pale stone bore a shallow bowl shape, and in that bowl lay a drift of books.

Not books. One book, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a bundle of loose pages, and beneath those, a skeleton.

Small. Curled on its side, as if asleep. The robes had rotted to lace.

Ilsa stopped walking. So did Sebastian.

"A priest," Ilsa said quietly. "One of the last."

"He didn't run." Sebastian's voice came out strange. "He stayed with the texts. He died keeping them." She thought of every burned archive, every melted bell, every name scratched out of Corvane's histories, and something in her chest went tight and hot. "They always say magic died fighting. Nobody says it died *waiting*."

She knelt by the altar because someone should, and because her knees decided before her pride could object. "Sleep well," she murmured, in the old tongue her grandmother had used for charms—the tongue that had gotten her family named and marked. It was the first time in twelve years she'd spoken it aloud where anyone could hear.

Behind her, Ilsa made a small sound and said nothing at all.

* * *

They made camp in what had been the acolytes' dormitory—a cell with three walls and a view of the sky—and Ilsa built a fire while Sebastian carried the book to the light. It was bound in gray leather gone soft as skin, hand-sewn, unmarked except for a single stamped figure on the cover: a crown, drawn hollow. An empty circle wearing points.

Her fingers knew it before her mind did. The sigil Ilsa had pulled from the fallen border-ward, bleeding shadow onto her palm. The mark on the royal seal. The thing that had been eating the kingdom from the throne down.

"Where did you even get this?" Sebastian asked. "You said archived texts. Whose archive?"

"Mine." Ilsa fed the fire another stick without looking up. "Wardens keep a second library. Unofficial. Everything the order decides is too dangerous to exist, we copy first and burn the original." A pause. "It's how you survive serving a lie for twenty years. You keep receipts."

"That's a hell of a confession to make casually over kindling."

"I've had six days to practice saying it." She finally looked up, and firelight moved across a face Sebastian had spent a decade teaching herself to hate and two years failing to. "Read it, Seb. Then hate me with better material."

So she read.

The priest's hand was small and precise, the script of a man who wrote for people he'd never meet. The early pages were history, and the history was worse than anything Ilsa had told her, because history came with dates. The Hollow Crown was older than Aldric. Older than the Kavanagh persecutions. It had been bound to the royal line since the first king—*a guardian-spirit, sworn to the bloodline, meant to warn and ward and whisper counsel*, the priest wrote—and somewhere in the deep centuries it had turned, or been turned, fed on ambition until guardianship curdled into appetite. King after king, it drank them hollow and wore them warm. Most reigns it slept fat and quiet. But wards falling, stars dying—that meant it was starving.

And starving things get bold.

Sebastian turned pages until the fire burned low and Ilsa fed it again, and neither of them spoke. Outside, the frost wind combed through the pines with a sound like distant surf. Somewhere under her skin, under the lattice of sealing-oaths she'd paid a fortune in pain to weave, her storm turned over in its sleep.

Then she reached the passage.

She knew it was *the* passage before she finished the first line, because her hands started shaking and wouldn't stop.

*On the unbinding of a consumed sovereign.* The priest had written it the way men write things they hope are wrong. *There is one door out of the Crown, and it opens from within. The parasite is bound into the king's own life-fire; it cannot be cut away without killing him, nor starved without killing the land. But it fears one thing above all others, and rightly: the raw current. Storm-magic is the oldest current, unbound, unbowed—it answers to no crown. If a caller of sufficient strength pours their full gift through the sovereign—through the Crown itself, into the knot where parasite and king are wound together—the current will burn the Crown out of him as fire burns rot from timber.*

*The cost:* and here the pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. *A storm-caller's gift is not water in a jug. It is weather. It is a self. To pour out all of it is to pour out everything the caller is. Some die on the threshold, their hearts unable to let go of so much at once. Some survive, emptied. Of those who survive, none have called so much as a breeze afterward. The rite does not take their power. It takes their power's future. What remains is a person, and nothing more.*

*I have searched forty years for a gentler door. There is none. Forgive me. I was the last to know how to do this and I could find no one brave enough, and now there is no time left to be gentle about anything.*

Sebastian read it three times. On the third pass she realized Ilsa had already read it—Ilsa, who had carried these texts across a kingdom, who had known, who had *known*—

"When?" Her voice came out flat and cold, a blade laid on a table. "When did you know what the cure would cost?"

Ilsa didn't flinch. That was the worst part; she'd learned not to, in twelve years of being hated by exactly one person. "Before I knocked on your door."

"You came to my house. You bled on my floor. You asked me to help free a king—" Sebastian stood, and the book slid off her knees, and she didn't care. "You knew the price was everything. And you let me walk into it thinking it was wards and sigils and clever work."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*"

"Yes." Ilsa rose too, slow, favoring her side. "Because if I'd told you the truth at your door, you'd have shut it, and you'd have been right to. Because the alternative is a kingdom going dark, a king eaten alive, a girl in the palace growing up under whatever wears her father next. Because I am a coward in every way except the ones that matter, and this is the one way I'm not." Her jaw worked. "And because it isn't mine to decide for you. It never was. Not twelve years ago, and not now. I brought you the choice. That's all I had to bring."

"The choice." Sebastian laughed, and it came out ugly. "Do you hear yourself? That's not a choice, Ilsa. That's a knife with two handles."

"It's the only door," Ilsa said simply. "I won't pretend otherwise. I've spent a year pretending, and it put shadow on a royal seal and corpses on the border. No more pretending. Read it again if you need to. Hate me however much you need to. But don't tell me I lied to you tonight, because tonight is the first night I haven't."

The fire cracked. Wind moved through the broken vault overhead, and for one absurd moment Sebastian would have sworn the temple sighed.

She looked down at the book, at the priest's cramped, desperate hand. *No one brave enough,* he'd written, forty years ago, in a world where the sky still had stars in it.

She picked the book up off the floor. Set it gently back on the altar, in the bowl of pale stone, where it belonged.

"We're done for tonight," she said, and walked out into the dark.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She climbed instead—to the top of the temple's broken tower, up stairs missing their middles, until she stood on a ledge of ancient stone with the whole frost plain spread below her, black and silver and endless.

Twelve years ago she had stood on a hill outside her village and watched her first storm come down out of her own chest and flatten a harvest that eight hundred people needed to live through winter. She hadn't killed anyone. That was the sentence everyone clung to, Ilsa hardest of all, when she argued exile over the rope. *No one died.* As if the arithmetic of a catastrophe could be balanced by counting only the dead and not the hungry, the sold farms, the father who took to drink, the mother who stopped speaking to her daughter forever.

Sealing-oaths. Twelve years of them. She had paid a hedge-witch in Vellmark three months' wages and a week of screaming to weave the first lattice, and she had renewed it every year, and each renewal cost more than the last, because the storm grew and the oaths didn't. She had built herself a small life inside the cage—charms, coppers, a stall in Cinders Row, a name that wasn't hers—and she had called it peace.

And now a dead priest was telling her the only door out of the kingdom's nightmare was to open the cage from the inside and pour herself through it.

*What remains is a person, and nothing more.*

She tried to imagine it. Waking with nothing under her skin. No hum, no pressure, no weather waiting. She had been a storm-caller before she'd been anything else; her mother said she'd thundered in the cradle. Take that away and what was left? A tinker. A charm-seller whose charms would finally, honestly, be worthless. Seb the Tinker, minus the trick.

And underneath the terror—she kept searching for it, turning the feeling over like a coin, trying to find the other face—underneath it, horribly, was relief.

That was the part she couldn't say aloud. That some exhausted cellar of her had been waiting twelve years for permission to stop holding it all in. That *nothing more* sounded, in the smallest hours, almost like rest.

"No," she said to the dark, out loud, because saying it made it smaller. "That's not resolve. That's just tired."

Resolve would be doing it because Aldric had a daughter who deserved to know her father fought. Resolve would be doing it because the stars were going out one constellation at a time and children in Corvane were learning the sky as a blank page. Resolve would be standing in front of the thing that ate kings and pouring everything she was into burning it, terrified, grieving, furious—not eager to be emptied, but unwilling to let the world be.

She sat on the cold stone and made herself count the reasons, one by one, the way she counted stitches in a charm. Not to talk herself into it. To know, honestly, whether she could stand in that fire without flinching.

By the time the east grayed, she had her answer, and it frightened her more than the rite did.

She could.

* * *

Ilsa was awake when she came down, sitting by embers, cleaning a sword she'd cleaned the night before. She looked up. She didn't ask. Twelve years of estrangement had at least taught them both that some questions are ambushes.

"The texts mention an anchor-line," Sebastian said. "Page sixty. When the caller pours through, someone has to hold the channel open—keep the current from doubling back, keep the king's life-fire from guttering out with the Crown. It can't be the caller. It has to be someone else, someone steady, someone willing to stand in the storm's eye and not let go." She held Ilsa's gaze. "I'm not asking yet. I'm telling you it exists, so you can start deciding whether you're that person, honestly, instead of volunteering out of guilt and getting us both killed."

Something flickered across Ilsa's face—relief, and shame at the relief, and love, badly hidden, the way it always was. "I'll decide," she said. "Honestly."

"Good." Sebastian sat, warmed her hands over embers that had no right to still be warm. "And Ilsa. If I go through that door and come out the other side as nothing—no power, no storm, just a woman—you'll tell me that's enough. Not kindly. *Tell* me. Because I'll need to believe it, and I won't be able to believe it from myself."

Ilsa was quiet a long moment. "That," she said finally, "is the first request you've made of me in twelve years."

"Don't get used to it."

"I'll make no promises either."

Despite everything—despite the book, the skeleton, the arithmetic of sacrifice—Sebastian felt her mouth twitch. Almost a smile. The first in days.

It lasted until hoofbeats came up the temple road.

Ilsa was on her feet with sword drawn before the sound finished, moving through pain like it was a door she'd learned to open fast. Sebastian pressed back against the wall, heart slamming, and listened. Two horses. Heavy riders. And beneath the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, drifting from the direction of the plains road:

Bells. Many bells, ringing out of rhythm, ringing wrong.

"Not soldiers," Ilsa breathed. "Proclamation riders. They're reading declarations in every town between here and the capital."

Through the broken wall, across miles of frozen dark, a rider's voice carried thin and clear on the wind, pitched for crowds, arriving to no one:

*"—by decree of the council of Corvane, the darkened heavens are no act of God nor nature, but of sorcery—let it be known the perpetrator walks among us, a storm-witch in tinker's guise—seize her, bind her, and the sky shall mend—"*

The words dissolved into distance. In the silence after, Sebastian became very aware of the storm under her skin, turning over, waking, pressing against twelve years of oaths like a wolf against a fence it had finally measured.

"They're blaming me," she said. "For the sky. For all of it."

"The Crown is smart," Ilsa said grimly, snuffing the embers with her boot. "It found the one witch in the kingdom who can kill it—and made her the kingdom's enemy before she could try."

Far off in the dark, the wrong-ringing bells swept on toward the next town, carrying Sebastian's name ahead of them like a lit torch.
