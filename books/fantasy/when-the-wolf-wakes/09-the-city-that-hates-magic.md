## 9. The City That Hates Magic

The proclamation went up on the council house doors at dawn, and by mid-morning it had been shouted into every alley of Corvane.

Sebastian heard it secondhand, the way she heard everything in the tinker quarter—not from criers with their polished boots and official voices, but from Marta the fishwife, who came pounding up the stairs to her rooms above the charm stall with a face gone the color of old lard.

"They're saying it's you." Marta's breath came fast; she was sixty and had never once run anywhere in her life. "They're saying there's a storm-witch hiding in Corvane, that she called down some working that snuffed the stars, that the sky's been dark because of *her*. Seb—Seb, they read out a description."

"What description?" Sebastian kept her voice level. Twelve years of practice. She set down the kettle she'd been filling and made her hands be still.

"Red hair. Young woman. Comes and goes at odd hours. Knows things about people's fevers before they say them." Marta wrung her apron between her fists. "Half the quarter could match that if they wanted to. Half the quarter *wouldn't* say so. But Seb, there were men from the palace there. Inquisitors' men. And the crier said—" She swallowed. "He said anyone sheltering the witch shares her crime. Fire for fire, he said. The council's word."

*Fire for fire.* Sebastian looked past Marta to the window, where the sun should have been climbing over the slate roofs and instead hung like a coin behind smoked glass, dim and colorless, the way it had been since the stars went out. Three weeks now of light that seemed filtered through ash. People had started burning candles at noon. Children had stopped asking when it would end, which was worse than asking.

"It isn't true," Sebastian said.

"I know it isn't true." Marta gripped her wrist, hard, her hands smelling of brine and scale. "But knowing things has never once stopped a mob."

Down in the street, the market was opening as it always opened, stalls unfolding like slow flowers, the tinker quarter shaking off its night. Someone was arguing cheerfully about the price of lamp oil. A dog barked at a cart. Ordinary life, going on over the top of an abyss.

Sebastian had spent twelve years learning how invisible felt. She had worn her ordinariness like armor: *Seb the Tinker*, mender of kettles, seller of charms that were mostly copper wire and confidence. She knew every crack in her own disguise.

And she knew, watching the street below, that the disguise was already failing. Had been failing since the child's fever broke under her palm. Since the clerk with the ink-stained fingers wrote something down and looked back twice on his way out of the market.

The Crown was hungry. The Crown was drinking the kingdom dry through its fallen wards. And what does a starving thing do? It points. It finds a throat that isn't its own and names it the cause.

*I am the distraction,* she thought. *I am the bone thrown to a starving city.*

Marta was still talking—neighbors, cellar doors, the cooper had a space under his floorboards—but Sebastian caught only pieces. Because across the square, at the mouth of Tanner's Row, four men were nailing something to a post. Not a proclamation. Smaller. Hand-lettered, ugly, quick.

A drawing of a woman. Red hair. Lightning scribbled beneath her feet like roots.

Underneath, in capitals: *THE WITCH OF THE TINKER QUARTER.*

The ink wasn't even dry.

* * *

They came at dusk.

Not all at once—that would have been too simple. First came boys, a dozen of them, hurling stones at shutters and running, testing, the way water tests a dam. Then came men with torches unlit, walking the rows slowly, reading door signs aloud to each other. Then came more, and then came wine, and then came the particular roaring laughter that Sebastian had heard exactly once before, twelve years ago, from the far side of a river, coming from her own village as her own harvest burned.

She had been packing for an hour by then. She'd known since morning—had put Ilsa's bleeding body in the cellar bed, had wrapped the fallen border-ward sigil and the shadow-bleeding royal seal in oilcloth and strapped them under her coat, had stood at the window counting torches the way she'd once counted stars.

Forty. Fifty. More gathering at the edges, drawn like moths to a bonfire that hadn't been lit yet.

"Go out the back," Ilsa rasped from the cellar stairs. She was propped against the frame, grey-faced, the wound in her side bound in linen that had soaked through twice. "There's the drainage cut behind the dye-works. You can make the river wall before—"

"I'm not leaving you in a cellar under a building they're going to burn."

"You are absolutely leaving me in a cellar," Ilsa said, "under a building they may burn, because you cannot fight fifty men, and neither can I, and the sigil is worth ten thousand of us both." She said it like a warden giving orders, and then her voice cracked, just slightly, into something older. "Seb. Please."

Before Sebastian could answer, the first torch lit.

It went up at the corner of Cooper's Row—a brand flaring orange against the dead sky—and a voice roared, and fifty voices answered, and the sound rolled down the quarter like a wave hitting a seawall. Glass broke somewhere. A scream that was half terror and half fury.

And then the strangest thing happened. The thing Sebastian would remember for the rest of her life, ordinary or otherwise.

Old Tam the cooper came out of his shop with a smith's hammer in his hand and planted himself in the middle of the row, in front of his own burning-hungry neighbors, and said, in a voice that carried the whole street:

"Not this one."

The mob laughed. Torches wavered. Tam didn't move.

"Not this one," he said again, louder. "Twelve years my daughter coughed blood every winter. Twelve years. Then the tinker sells me a charm for two bits and tells me, mind you, *boil the willow bark, keep her feet warm, and give her this to hold when the fever comes.* And my girl hasn't missed a winter day since. So no." He raised the hammer. "Not this one. Find your witch somewhere else."

For one heartbeat, the whole street held still—the mob, the torches, the smoke beginning to curl from somewhere Sebastian couldn't see, the dead sky pressing down on all of it.

Then a stone took Tam across the temple, and he went down on one knee, and the dam broke.

They came up the row like floodwater. Sebastian saw her own stall go first—the little awning she'd sewn herself, the shelves of copper and glass, all of it kicked apart and fired in seconds. Saw the Widow Anselm dragged out of her doorway by two men while her grandson screamed. Saw fire catch on a roof beam and begin to eat.

And she did the one thing she had sworn off for twelve years.

She reached for the storm.

It came up in her like a well flooding—twelve years sealed under oaths and discipline, twelve years of rain held behind a wall of will, and it hit her palms with a pressure that made her gasp. She hadn't called anything this size since the village. Her skin prickled with static. Somewhere above the smoke, the dead clouds churned, feeling her.

*No,* she told herself, teeth gritted. *Not lightning. Not yet. They'll know. They'll come for me with a hundred men next time, and Ilsa is dying in a cellar, and the rite—*

Instead she pulled the cold. Just the cold, drawn up out of the high dark air, and pushed it down the row ahead of her like a wall.

The fires guttered. Torch flames bent sideways and shrank to coals. Men stumbled backward, arms crossed, breath steaming, suddenly standing in air cold enough to ache. It bought thirty seconds. Thirty seconds was enough.

She got the Widow Anselm loose. She hauled Tam upright—he was bleeding but alive, roaring curses—and shoved him toward his own cellar trapdoor. She saw Marta pulling children through a fence gap, saw the dye-works crew forming a bucket line against the spreading roof-fire, and realized, with a strange floating clarity, that the quarter wasn't breaking. It was *fighting*. Disorganized, furious, outnumbered—but fighting.

Then someone grabbed her arm, spun her around, and she was looking into the face of a man holding a torch, close enough to smell the pitch, and he was shouting, "*Witch! It's her, it's the witch, she froze the fire—*" and his eyes went wide because his torch had just died in his fist, drowned in cold, and everyone within ten feet had seen it.

So much for invisibility.

She ran. Down Tanner's Row, through the smoke, into the maze of alleys behind the dye-works where she'd lived small and quiet for twelve years, and the roar of the crowd followed her, and above it—above everything—a new sound. Horns. Palace horns, deep and brazen, from the direction of the Council House.

The inquisitors were coming. To save the city, or to claim it. With the Crown behind their eyes, there was very little difference anymore.

She went down through the drainage cut in the dark, hands slick on wet stone, toward Ilsa, toward the cellar, toward whatever came next.

* * *

The cellar belonged to the Widow Anselm's late husband's family, and it had been dug to store root vegetables and smuggled brandy in roughly equal measure. Now it held eleven people, three candles, and the entire surviving inventory of Seb the Tinker's stock, crated and stacked by neighbors who had worked in silence, quickly, without being asked.

Sebastian counted heads twice. Tam, bandaged, dozing upright against a keg. Marta and her sister. Four children, including Tam's granddaughter, who had appointed herself guardian of the smallest candle and guarded it fiercely. Two dye-workers, a lamplighter, a boy of perhaps fifteen whom nobody seemed to know and everybody fed anyway. The Widow Anselm herself, presiding from a chair like a queen in exile.

Ilsa lay on a pallet in the corner, feverish, her wound dressed with boiled linen and honey. She'd insisted on helping haul crates until she nearly fainted, and had been ordered to lie down by three separate people, including the fifteen-year-old.

Above them, muffled by floors and earth, the city screamed and burned. Boots passed overhead in rhythms—running crowds, marching patrols, the terrible irregular drumming of collapsing timber. Once, something heavy fell close enough to sift dust through the floorboards, and the candle flames all leaned sideways, and nobody spoke until they straightened again.

"They burned the chapel row," the boy reported, returning from a listening-post at the coal chute. "And the clockmaker's. But the bucket lines saved the granary. And there's criers saying—saying anyone who gives up the witch gets bread ration doubled. Month's supply."

Silence. Eleven faces turned, not toward the boy, but toward Sebastian.

She had been sitting against the wall with Ilsa's head pillowed on her folded coat, keeping the fever magic thin and careful in her fingertips—the kind of healing that left no thunder, only warmth. She looked around the cellar at these people: the fishwife, the cooper, the widow, the stranger-boy. People whose kettles she'd mended. People whose fevers she'd quietly broken, whose luck-charms she'd loaded with a whisper of real working and never once taken credit for. Twelve years of small kindnesses, done in secret, expecting nothing.

Expecting, in fact, exactly this. That someday the secret would cost them. She had told herself it was better they never know her, so that when the reckoning came, they could hand her over clean-handed and sleep afterward.

Tam spoke first. His head wound had given him a magnificent bruise and an air of injured dignity.

"Bread ration doubled," he repeated. He spat, carefully, into the dirt floor. "I've had the council's bread. Tastes like punishment."

Laughter, ragged and startled, the laughter of exhausted people surprised by their own relief. Even Ilsa smiled faintly with her eyes closed.

"The criers also say the witch caused the dark sky," the boy said. "That she ate the stars."

More silence. The candle flames stood perfectly still.

"Well," said the Widow Anselm, adjusting her shawl with great deliberation. "Someone had better tell her to give them back, then. We liked the stars."

The laugh that went around the cellar this time was longer, warmer, and Sebastian found, to her horror, that her eyes were burning. She pressed the heel of her hand against them and stared hard at the candle flame and thought, *this is the worst night of my life and I have never in twelve years felt less alone.*

"Listen to me," she said, when she could trust her voice. "The council wants me. If they find this cellar, they'll find all of you. There's still time tonight to—"

"To what?" Tam asked. "Put you out? Walk you to the council house with a ribbon on?" He shook his head, gingerly. "Woman, I watched you walk into a mob tonight and pull a burning man out of the smoke before you pulled yourself free. Don't ask us again what we'll do. We've done it."

"He means thank you," Marta translated. "He means it badly, but he means it."

Overhead, the horns sounded again—closer now, sweeping the streets in a grid, methodical. Door to door. Cellar to cellar. The inquisitors were patient, and the Crown was hungry, and somewhere out in the smoking dark a clerk with ink-stained fingers was probably pointing at a map.

Sebastian bent low over Ilsa and touched her forehead. The fever was wrong—not the honest heat of infection but something else underneath, something cold moving beneath the warmth, and when Sebastian closed her eyes she could almost hear it, faint as a whisper through a wall.

*Give her back,* it seemed to say. *Give the witch back, and the stars, and the sun. Give me the storm.*

Her blood went to ice water. She pulled her hand away.

"Ilsa," she whispered. "Wake up. It knows where we are. It's not just the council looking anymore—it's *reaching*."

Ilsa's eyes opened. Grey, fever-bright, and utterly clear.

"Then we don't wait for dawn," she said. "Get your storm, witch. We ride for the star-temple tonight."

Above them, directly overhead, every boot in the street stopped at once.
