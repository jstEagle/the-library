## 10. A Crown's Bargain

Sebastian dreamed of rain.

Not the gray drizzle Corvane offered in its grudging seasons, but true rain—the warm, fat drops of the lowlands she'd grown up in, hammering tin roofs, filling the cisterns, singing against her skin like a language only she had ever bothered to learn. In the dream she stood barefoot in the dooryard of a house that no longer existed, arms spread, letting the storm soak through the rough weave of her tinker's coat. Twelve years of dust washed out of her. Twelve years of careful smallness. She could feel it all going down the gutters.

*You miss this.*

She turned. The yard was empty except for a figure standing at the gate, and even in the dream—especially in the dream—her body knew what it was looking at before her mind caught up. The figure wore a king's cloak, ermine-trimmed, far too heavy for the warm rain. Its face was King Aldric's face, or close enough: the long jaw from the coins, the deep-set eyes from the procession banners. But the eyes were wrong. They were the color of a well at midnight, and when she looked into them she saw not depth but absence—a hole shaped like a man, wearing his outline like a coat hung on a hook.

"You're not him," Sebastian said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. She'd expected to wake screaming. Instead she stood in the rain of her childhood with the thing that had eaten a kingdom, and she was *angry*.

"No," the Crown said. It did not move its mouth when it spoke; the words simply arrived, the way thunder arrives, already inside your chest. "He is asleep. He dreams of orchards. I let him. Kindness costs me nothing."

"Kindness." She laughed, and the sound cracked like ice on a pond. "You drank his kingdom dry. You ate the stars. You call that kindness?"

"I call it *hunger*, tinker. You would know something of hunger." The figure took one step through the gate—it did not open the gate; it simply was on her side of it now—and the rain bent around it, avoiding it, as if the storm itself flinched. "You have spent twelve years starving yourself. Do you know how that feels? Of course you do. You are the only living soul who does."

Sebastian looked down at her hands. In the dream they were younger—seventeen again, freckled, faintly luminous at the fingertips where the static used to gather before a storm broke. The light pulsed there like a second heartbeat.

"I sealed it," she said. "Twelve years ago. The oaths hold."

"The oaths hold," the Crown agreed, pleasantly. "*I* held them once. Did you never wonder why they were so easy to swear? Why the words came to you so cleanly, why no priest questioned a village girl swearing herself silent in three languages?" It tilted Aldric's head, and the gesture was almost gentle. "Because I wrote them, little storm-caller. Or rather—I suggested them, through men who thought the ideas were their own. A storm like yours needed a cage, and I have always been very good at cages. I built the first one around myself."

The rain kept falling. Somewhere behind the house, in the dream's soft-focus distance, her mother's voice called a name that wasn't Seb, wasn't Tinker—her true name, the one only Ilsa still used, and even Ilsa said it like setting down something fragile.

"Why?" Sebastian asked. "Why seal me? If you wanted storms, if you wanted magic—why lock mine away?"

"Because I did not want it *yet*. And because—" the well-eyes flickered, and for the first time she heard something beneath the pleasant thunder, something thin and quick, like a wire under tension, "—because you were not ready. Power poured too young curdles. Yours would have spoiled, and spoiled power is useless to everyone." A pause. "You are ready now."

And the dream changed.

* * *

She was standing in Corvane—but not the Corvane she knew. This one was bright. The market square blazed under a full sky of returned stars, every one of them burning, and the stalls around her sold bread and rope and iron nails, ordinary things, and nobody flinched from her. People nodded to her as she passed. An old woman pressed an apple into her hand without being asked. A child pointed and whispered *that's the storm-lady who brought back the sky* —not in fear. In wonder.

In this Corvane, the wards stood. In this Corvane, her name was legal ink on a guild charter: *Kavanagh, Storm-Caller, Licensed.* In this Corvane, she walked down Copper Lane past a shop with her own sign over the door, and through the window she saw Ilsa Vane sitting at the counter, older, gray at the temples, laughing at something Sebastian had said, easy and unguarded in a way the real Ilsa had never once been in twelve years.

"This is what walking away buys," the Crown said, standing beside her now, its shadow pooling wrong on the sunlit cobbles. "No rite. No burning. No temple in the frost. You go back to your stall tomorrow. You mend kettles. In a year—two, I am patient—the oaths dissolve on their own, quietly, the way they were made. Your power returns to you legally, licensed, *wanted*. And the warden lives."

Sebastian's throat went dry. "Lives?"

"She is dying, Kavanagh. Surely you've seen it. The cough she hides. The blood in her handkerchief that she burns afterward so you won't find it." The Crown's voice softened to something almost tender, and that tenderness was worse than any threat. "The wound she took at the pass was clean, but wounds fester in women who refuse to rest. Winter is coming, and she will march beside you into the cold because guilt is a poor physician. She will die in the snow, unreconciled, having ruined you twice—once at nineteen, and once by leading you to this altar. Unless you walk away. Then she lives. Long enough, at least. Long enough for the two of you to finally say the things you've spent twelve years not saying."

The dream-Ilsa in the shop window looked up, met her eyes, smiled.

Sebastian made herself look away. That was the hardest thing she had ever done in a dream, harder than facing the thing itself: turning her back on a door opening onto twelve years of everything she'd lost.

"What do you get?" she asked. "If I walk away. What does the great hungry dark gain from a tinker mending kettles?"

"Time," the Crown said simply. "The wards are eaten. The ley-lines are mine. Another season and I will be strong enough to leave this frail vessel entirely and take the realm itself as my body—every wardstone, every blessed hearth, every prayer. Nothing you could do would stop me then. But you—you are the one variable. The one instrument tuned to cut me." It turned to face her fully, and for a moment the mask of Aldric slipped, and she saw what wore it: vast, cold, and—she realized with a jolt that went through the dream like a bell-strike—

Afraid.

"You fear me," she breathed.

"I *respect* you," it snapped, and the market-square illusion shuddered, the stars guttering overhead like candles in wind. "Do you think I offer bargains to things that cannot harm me? I ate nine kings, girl. I unmade the star-priests in a single night. I swallowed the border wards that stood four hundred years. But you—" the wire-tension in its voice sang tighter, "—you were born under a sky I did not yet own, with a storm in her that I did not make, cannot eat, and cannot predict. When you swore your oaths I told myself: good, contained, manageable. But oaths can be unsworn. And I have watched you these past weeks, tinker. I watched you heal the fevered child. I felt your charms drink from the dying lines and give the power back *cleaner than they took it*. Do you understand what that means? Everything I touch, I corrupt. Everything you touch—"

It stopped. The silence stretched, and in it Sebastian heard the answer arrive on its own, the way weather arrives.

"—everything I touch comes back to life," she finished quietly.

The Crown's borrowed face went very still.

"That is why the stars went out the night the wards fell," she said, thinking aloud now, watching the realization land on the thing like blows. "You didn't just feed. You were *fleeing* something. Or preparing. Because somewhere in this kingdom there was a girl growing up whose nature is the opposite of yours—and when her power started to stir, even sealed, even small, you felt it. Like a splinter. Like a wolf waking under the floorboards." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "You're not offering me a bargain. You're offering me anything, *anything*, to keep me from remembering what I am."

For one heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—the entire dream held still. The false stars. The false Ilsa. The warm false rain waiting beyond the edges of the square.

Then the Crown laughed, and it was the worst sound Sebastian had ever heard: nine kings' laughter braided together, and underneath all of it, thin and high, something that might once have been a guardian spirit weeping.

"Walk away," it said, "and I will show you mercy such as no enemy of mine has ever known. Refuse—" the bright market began to darken at its edges, stalls crumbling, stars snuffing out one by one in exactly the order they had died that night eleven months ago, "—and I will spend the warden's life teaching you what my mercy costs. Choose quickly, storm-caller. Winter chooses faster."

* * *

Sebastian woke in the cellar room behind Maren's dye-shop with her heart slamming and frost on the *inside* of the window glass—in autumn, in a city that had gone cold since the Crown began to starve. Her hands were glowing. Not faintly, not at the fingertips: both hands burned with pale blue-white light from wrist to nail, and the water in the basin beside her bed had frozen solid, cracked upward from the center like a blooming flower.

She stared at it for a long moment. The oaths were supposed to prevent this. The oaths had prevented this, faithfully, for twelve years.

But oaths, the Crown had said, can be unsworn.

And something in her—something that had been pressing patiently against the seals for years the way spring presses against river ice—had heard the whole conversation and understood, before her waking mind had, that the Crown had just shown her the key. It feared her. It feared her *specifically*, enough to write her prison, enough to buy her with dreams, enough to threaten the woman she loved. Fear was information. Fear was a map.

She got up, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and climbed the stairs to the kitchen, where a single candle burned and Ilsa sat hunched over the archived texts from the star-temple, copying passages by hand, her penmanship deteriorating the way it always did after midnight. There was fresh blood on the handkerchief crumpled beside the inkwell. She hadn't burned this one yet.

"Bad dream?" Ilsa asked, without looking up. She always knew.

"It spoke to me," Sebastian said. "The Crown. It offered me a deal—my magic legitimized, the exile erased, your safety, if I walk away."

That got Ilsa's head up. Her eyes were shadowed and sharp. "And?"

"And it made a mistake." Sebastian sat down across from her, laid her still-faintly-glowing hands flat on the table between them, and told her the rest: the sealed oaths written by the Crown's suggestion, the corruption reversed by her touch, the fear underneath the bargain's silk. When she finished, Ilsa was quiet for a long time, staring at the frost-flower pattern creeping across the kitchen window.

"A guardian spirit corrupted by greed," Ilsa said at last, tapping the copied page before her—the passage from the vault, the one the old priest had given them. "And a storm born to burn corruption clean. The texts say the rite needs a caller willing to pour everything through it. But maybe that's not a sacrifice, Seb. Maybe that's just... what you're *for*."

Outside, the wind rose off the frost plains, and somewhere in it—faint, furious, and unmistakably afraid—something screamed.

By morning, there would be riders on the south road. But that night, Sebastian Kavanagh slept without dreaming, her hands glowing softly in the dark like two small defiant stars, and for the first time in twelve years, she was not hiding from what she was.

She was walking toward it.
