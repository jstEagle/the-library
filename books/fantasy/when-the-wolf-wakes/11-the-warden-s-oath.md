## 11. The Warden's Oath

The Wardens' Hall had not changed in twelve years, and that was the cruelest thing about it.

Ilsa Vane stood beneath the vaulted ceiling where the banners of every warden-commander hung in solemn ranks—dyed wool gone soft with age, each one bearing the crossed keys of the Order of the Vigilant Crown. She had sworn her oath beneath those banners at seventeen, kneeling on cold marble while Commandant Orell's voice rolled through the hall like judgment itself. *To watch. To guard. To speak truth to the throne, and truth to the realm, and never let the two diverge.*

She had believed it then. She had believed it right up until the moment believing became inconvenient, and then she had spent a year learning exactly how heavy a silence could grow.

Now she stood before the assembled Chapter—the full convocation, forty wardens in their grey cloaks, seated in a crescent around the raised dais—and waited for the reading of charges to begin.

They had come for her at dawn. Not soldiers; that would have been simpler. Warden-Commander Orell himself, white-haired now, walking with a stick he refused to acknowledge, standing on her sister's doorstep in the drizzle with six wardens behind him and grief already settled into his face like an old scar. He hadn't said *you're under arrest*. He'd said, "Ilsa. Come home and tell us what you've done."

So she had come home.

"The accused will state her name and rank for the record."

The clerk's voice was thin and bored, the voice of a man who had read a hundred confessions and expected nothing new. Ilsa lifted her chin. The wound in her side from the border pass still pulled when she stood too straight; she straightened anyway.

"Ilsa Vane. Warden of the Third Circle. Keeper of the Border Archive at Corvane East."

"And do you understand the charges laid against you?"

"I wrote them myself," she said. "So yes."

A ripple went through the crescent of grey cloaks. Somewhere to her left, a woman muttered something sharp, and someone else shushed her. Ilsa kept her eyes on the empty iron chair at the center of the dais—the Warden-Regent's seat, vacant since the Regent had been summoned to the palace eight months ago and had not returned. Nobody spoke about that. Nobody ever spoke about anything that mattered anymore.

Orell rose from his place at the crescent's heart. He looked older than his banners did, older than stone. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that the whole hall had to lean in to hear it, which was, Ilsa suspected, deliberate.

"Warden Vane. You stand accused of concealment of truth from your Order, of obstruction of the Vigilant Charter, and of—" he paused, and the pause was the worst thing in the room, "—of high treason against the crown you swore to guard. The charge of treason is brought not by this Chapter but by the Inquisition, who request transfer of custody upon conviction." His eyes found hers. "Before we proceed: is there anything you wish to say?"

"Yes," Ilsa said. "Everything. That's rather the point."

* * *

She began at the beginning, because the beginning was the part they would least want to hear.

"Fourteen months ago, I was summoned to the Winter Palace to renew the king's annual health-warding. It's routine—a formality. Every warden of the Third Circle has done it a dozen times." Her voice held steady. She made it hold steady. "When I entered the royal bedchamber, King Aldric was awake. He was dressed. He asked after my family by name—he always remembered names—and he asked me to check the wards on his daughter's wing first, because Princess Elira had been having nightmares.

"Then he turned to the window, and his shadow didn't follow him."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"I thought I was tired," she continued. "I thought my eyes were playing tricks—the light in that room comes from the east, and the angles are strange. So I watched. And I watched. And over the course of that visit I confirmed what I did not yet have the courage to name: the King's shadow moves late. A half-second. Sometimes more. Like something wearing him hasn't fully learned the shape of him yet."

"Objection." The Inquisitor's liaison sat apart from the wardens, a narrow man in black called Serel, with ink-stained fingers and eyes like coins. "The accused is describing fever-dreams and calling them evidence."

"I'm describing what I saw," Ilsa said, "and then I'll describe what I did about it, which is worse. Let me finish."

Orell nodded once. *Go on.*

"I should have reported it that night. I told myself I needed proof—that a warden doesn't walk into the Chapter and accuse the king of being hollow on the strength of a shadow's hesitation. And that was true. But it wasn't the whole truth." She made herself say it plainly, no ornament, no shelter. "The whole truth is that I was afraid. Aldric was a good king. He was *the* good king—the one who ended the tithe-wars, who opened the archives to commoners, who pardoned half the people this Order had locked up. If something was wrong with him, everything was wrong with everything, and I did not want to live in that world. So I gave myself a month. Then another. I ran tests I could run alone. I consulted texts I could consult quietly. And every week I delayed, the delay became its own reason to keep delaying, because reporting it meant admitting I'd waited."

Serel shifted in his seat. "You admit concealment freely?"

"I'm confessing it," Ilsa said. "There's a difference, and I'll thank you not to blur it. Concealment is hiding a thing so it can't hurt you. Confession is dragging it into the light knowing it will destroy you, because leaving it buried destroys more."

She turned back to Orell.

"Three months in, I found the sigil."

At the word *sigil*, several wardens went very still. They all knew what sigil she meant. There was only one sigil in the world that mattered this season, the one whose failure had been announced from every pulpit in Corvane as an act of sabotage, the one whose loss had given the Inquisition license to burn half the tinker quarter and hang four hedge-mages from the Salt Gate.

"The border ward-anchor at Greywatch Pass," Ilsa said. "Officially destroyed by saboteurs on the night the stars went out. I was sent to investigate the ruins—I was the closest archive-keeper, and I knew the anchor's construction better than anyone living, because I helped inscribe it twenty years ago." Her hand drifted toward her coat, and stopped. "Sabotage leaves marks. Cutting, burning, blood. What I found at Greywatch left none. The anchor hadn't been broken. It had been *eaten*—hollowed out from the inside, every thread of binding-magic consumed down to the last filament, the way a moth consumes a coat while leaving the shape of it intact. Something drank a hundred years of ward-work in a single night. And whatever did it came from inside the kingdom."

"You cannot know that," Serel said.

"I know it the way you know fire burns," Ilsa said. "And here is what I did with that knowledge, which is the actual crime, and I want every warden in this hall to hear it clearly." She drew a breath that cost her more than the wound in her side ever had. "I hid the sigil. I hid my findings. For eleven months, I carried proof of the greatest threat this realm has faced since the founding—and I told no one—because the trail of that consumption led, without deviation, without ambiguity, into the Winter Palace. Into the king."

The hall erupted.

It wasn't shouting, not quite—it was the sound of forty disciplined people failing at once to be disciplined, chairs scraping, voices overlapping, one warden near the front saying *she's mad* and another saying *the stars, though, the stars*, and Orell standing with both hands raised until the noise broke against his stillness and died.

"When the stars went out," a warden called—Brannoc, old Brannoc, who had taught her sword-forms, "that same night? You're claiming connection?"

"I'm claiming causation," Ilsa said. "The night the stars went out was the night the Crown finished eating the wards. Whatever extinguished the sky is the same thing that hollows anchors, the same thing that rides our king, and it is starving now that the wards are gone. It's drinking the kingdom dry. Every charm in Corvane working too well, every hedge-mage's candle flaring—those aren't miracles, they're symptoms. Magic is coming *out* of things because something is pulling it. And when the pull reaches the deep wells—" she stopped. Shook her head. "There won't be a kingdom left to save. Just a shape of one, the way the anchor kept its shape."

"Why?" The voice came from the back, young, almost a girl's. A probationer Ilsa didn't know. "Why hide it? Why come forward *now*?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions. Ilsa looked at the girl—at her earnest, frightened face, at the fresh oath-cord still bright on her wrist—and felt twelve years collapse into a single moment, because she had asked that same question once, of someone else, in this same hall.

"Because I was loyal," Ilsa said. "That's the answer nobody wants. I wasn't corrupt. I wasn't paid. I wasn't cowardly—not entirely, not at first. I loved this crown. I loved what it was supposed to be. And love, in a warden, is the most dangerous thing there is, because it lets you confuse protecting the throne with protecting the realm. Every month I waited, I told myself I was guarding stability—that if the truth got out, the Inquisition would tear the country apart hunting witches, that the council would fracture, that Corvane would burn. And maybe all of that is true. But the deeper truth is that I couldn't bear for Aldric to be lost. I couldn't bear for the good king to be the ruined one. So I protected my idea of him instead of my kingdom." Her voice roughened. "I have done this before. Twelve years ago I protected the Order's idea of justice instead of a frightened girl, and I told myself exile was mercy. I have spent my whole career mistaking loyalty for love and calling the wreckage duty. I am done."

* * *

The recess lasted two hours. They put her in a side chamber with a barred window and no guard, which was either courtesy or confidence, and she spent the time doing the thing she had not allowed herself on the walk from her sister's house: remembering.

Sebastian Kavanagh, nineteen years old, standing in this building's antechamber with rain still steaming off her shoulders, watching Ilsa with an expression that wasn't hatred—hatred would have been easier to carry, would have been *deserved*. It had been bewilderment. The look of someone waiting for the person they trusted most to explain how this could possibly be happening.

*I chose exile over execution,* Ilsa had told her. *It's mercy.*

*It's banishment,* Sebastian had said. *You're sending me away because I frighten you, and you're dressing it up as saving my life so you don't have to call it what it is.*

Twelve years, and the girl had been right on the first day. Ilsa had needed a hollowed-out king and a dead sky to catch up to a teenager's clarity. There was a lesson in that, probably, but she was too tired and too close to ruin to find it elegant.

The bolt scraped. Orell came in alone, leaning harder on his stick than he had in the hall, and lowered himself onto the bench across from her with the groan of a man whose body had stopped negotiating.

"They're deliberating," he said.

"I know how deliberations work, Commandant."

"Humor an old man." He studied her for a long moment. "Serel wants you transferred tonight. The Inquisition has a cell under the Bastion with your name already chalked on the door. Half the Chapter agrees with him—you confessed to treason, Ilsa, in front of witnesses. The other half—" he rubbed his face, "—the other half checked the archive seal you described. The hollowing pattern. I had three of our best inscribers examine your notes this afternoon. Two of them came out pale. One of them vomited."

"So they believe me."

"Some of them believe you. Belief is not the same as courage." He leaned forward. "Tell me the rest. Not the hall version. The version with names. Who else knows? Where is the proof now? What are you planning, because I have known you since you were small enough to ride on my shoulder, and you did not walk into that hall to be convicted. You walked in to buy something."

Trust Orell to see the architecture under the rubble. Ilsa weighed the door, the window, the distance to the street. Weighed, also, the fact that if the Inquisition took her tonight, everything died with her—the sigil hidden in the wall of a tinker's cellar, the archived rite-texts, the name *Sebastian Kavanagh* written nowhere but in her own memory.

"The proof is safe," she said. "One other person knows everything, and she is the only person alive who can end this. I need three days, Commandant. Three days, and then the Inquisition can have me, cells and chalked doors and all."

"And if I refuse? If I let Serel take you now?"

"Then you'll be handing the Crown's creature the last witness who can testify to what the king actually is." She met his eyes. "You taught me that a warden's word is the only currency the realm can't counterfeit. Spend mine wisely."

He laughed—short, rusty, genuine. "Gods preserve me. You even argue like your mother." He stood, slowly, joints cracking like green wood. "Two days. There's a postern gate behind the armory that the night-watch doesn't check until the third bell. After that, I never saw you, and if the Inquisition asks, I'll swear you escaped—which will be true enough, since I'm telling you how." At the door he paused, his back to her, his hand on the frame. "For the record, Warden Vane. What you did was wrong. Hiding the truth nearly cost us everything."

"I know."

"But what you did in that hall today—" his voice dropped low, "—was the first time in a year anyone in this Order has spoken like a warden instead of a servant. Go. Before I remember which side of my oath I'm supposed to be standing on."

* * *

They recalled her at dusk for the verdict, and the hall felt different now—or perhaps she did. The candles had been trimmed. The banners hung motionless in air that smelled of tallow and cold stone.

Orell read the finding in a voice like a landslip: guilty of concealment, guilty of obstruction. On the charge of treason—brought by the Inquisition, pressed by Serel with a lawyer's patience—the Chapter returned a verdict Ilsa felt land in the room like a struck bell: *not proven.* Not innocent. Not guilty. A door left open, or a knife left unsheathed; with the Inquisition, they were often the same thing.

Then came the sentence, and it was delivered not by Orell but by the Chapter entire, all forty wardens rising together, which was procedure and had never once, in the history of the Order, failed to feel like a funeral.

"Ilsa Vane. For crimes against the Vigilant Charter, you are stripped of rank, title, and office. Your oath-cord is forfeit." Brannoc came forward, his face grey, and unwound the braided cord from her wrist with hands that shook. Forty years of service, ending with an old man's trembling fingers. "Your key-ring is surrendered. Your name is struck from the rolls. You are marked for arrest on sight by any officer of the crown or the Inquisition." A pause, and then Orell, softer, breaking protocol by a hair: "Ilsa Vane. The Vigilant Charter commands us to watch, to guard, and to speak truth. This Chapter finds that you failed the first two. May the third sustain you."

They took everything. The cord. The keys. The ring with the Order's seal, which left a pale band of skin on her finger she would apparently wear for the rest of her life. They marched her to the postern gate themselves—all forty of them, in formation, as escort, as honor guard, as the last lie either of them would ever tell—and released her into the dark beyond the walls with a warrant for her capture following her like a second shadow.

She stood in the alley behind the armory and listened to the gate close, and waited for the grief to arrive.

It didn't.

What arrived instead was lighter than grief and stranger: the feeling of setting down something she had carried so long she'd forgotten it had weight. No rank. No order. No oath except the one she'd actually kept all along, the private one, the one that had finally, at forty-one years of age, become the only one that mattered.

Somewhere across the dark city, in a cellar under the tinker quarter, a storm-caller named Sebastian Kavanagh was preparing to unbind herself, and the sky above Corvane was a blind black ceiling where stars used to be.

Ilsa pulled her stolen cloak tighter and started walking.

Behind her, at the top of the Bastion tower, a signal-fire kindled—inquisitors' red, three pulses, the summons that meant *mobilize*. They had found the empty cell with her name chalked on the door. By morning, every road out of Corvane would have eyes on it.

She smiled into the dark, and it felt like the first honest expression of her life.

Good, she thought. Let them hunt. They were about to learn what a warden without an order could do.

She turned toward the tinker quarter, and the ruins of the star-temple beyond it, and the woman she owed twelve years—and began, at last, to run.
