## 12. The Road to the Star-Temple

The frost plains began where the road ended.

Sebastian felt the change in her boots first—the packed earth of the king's highway giving way to snow that squeaked and groaned beneath each step, a sound like something small being hurt. Then she felt it in her chest, in the sealed place where twelve years of storm sat coiled behind her oaths: a faint pressure, like a hand pressing against the inside of a window. The temple was calling. Or the storm was answering.

"Off the road," Ilsa said quietly. "They'll watch the road."

They cut east across the white, into a country of low hills and buried fences, where farmhouses stood dark and doorless and the wind moved through them like a tenant who had outstayed the owners. Three days out of Corvane, and already the world had thinned. No carts. No smoke from chimneys. Once they passed a shrine at a crossroads—Corvane's godless little altars, the kind farmers kept so no one could call them witches—and someone had smashed it, methodically, every stone turned over, as if looking for magic hiding underneath.

Sebastian pulled her coat tighter and said nothing. She had been counting things all morning: the hours since dawn, the leagues to the star-temple, the number of times Ilsa had looked at her when she thought Sebastian wasn't watching.

Seven times. She'd stopped counting after seven because the answer was always the same. Ilsa Vane was memorizing her face.

*Good,* Sebastian told herself, and hated how much it cost to think it. *That's exactly what you want.*

* * *

They sheltered that night in the ruin of a waystation, its roof half-fallen, its hearth choked with old ash. Ilsa built a fire anyway, small and shielded, cupping it with her body like a secret. Twelve years ago, Sebastian would have laughed at a warden of the Crown crouched over contraband flame. Now there was no Crown worth the name, only the thing wearing it, and fire was just fire.

Ilsa divided their bread into unequal halves and gave Sebastian the larger one without comment. Sebastian ate it without thanks. They had fallen back into this somewhere on the second day—an old rhythm, wordless, domestic in a way that frightened her more than the hunters did.

"You're favoring your left side," Sebastian said.

"I'm not."

"You've been holding your ribs since we crossed the river."

"It's bruising." Ilsa's voice had the flat finality of a report. "It'll keep."

Everything about Ilsa would keep, Sebastian thought. That was the whole architecture of the woman. Grief, guilt, love—Ilsa filed them all under *later*, and later never came, and meanwhile she walked and fought and gave away the larger half of the bread.

"The pass at Grey Hollow," Ilsa said, turning to the map by firelight. "Two days from here. After that the ground climbs and the temple sits above the tree line like a wound that never closed. We should reach it by the sixth night."

"*We*," Sebastian said.

Ilsa looked up. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadows, older than Sebastian remembered it, though she had been remembering it wrong for twelve years anyway—younger, softer, wearing the mentor's smile instead of the warden's jaw.

"I'm coming with you," Ilsa said. "Into the rite. As anchor. You know this."

"I know you *said* it."

"And you've been arguing with me in your head for three days instead of saying it aloud." Ilsa almost smiled. "You used to do that too. Argue with people who weren't there because the real conversation was harder."

"This isn't an argument about whether I can do it," Sebastian said. "I can do it. I'm going to do it. This is about what happens after."

"Nothing happens after. That's the point of an anchor-line. I hold the thread, you pour the storm, and when it's done—" Ilsa spread her hands. "When it's done, I pull you back."

"That's what the texts say. The texts also say *likely loss of the caller's power, possible loss of the caller*. Your own archive, Warden. Your own translation." Sebastian heard her voice climb and made herself stop, made herself look at the fire instead of the woman. "You read the same words I did. Don't stand there and pretend the sentence has a different ending for you."

Ilsa was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence, moaning through the broken roof beams.

"If you die," Ilsa said finally, "and I'm not there—if I let you walk in alone because it was cleaner, kinder, whatever word you're dressing it in tonight—then I spend the rest of my life knowing I chose my comfort over your life. Twice." Her eyes came up, gray and unflinching. "I won't survive that. So understand me clearly, Seb: I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you how it will be."

"There is no *how it will be*," Sebastian said. "There's a ritual written by dead star-priests for a parasite older than the kingdom. There's a king hollowed down to the walls. And there's a storm-caller who hasn't called weather in twelve years because the last time she did, forty people lost everything they owned." She stood, abruptly, needing to move. "You want to hold my anchor-line? Fine. But don't lie to yourself about what you're holding onto."

She walked out into the cold before Ilsa could answer, and stood in the dark among the ruined fences, and let the wind hit her face until her eyes stopped burning.

*Twice,* Ilsa had said. Sebastian knew what the first time was. The exile. The choice Ilsa had made at nineteen, kneeling in the mud of a drowned harvest field, trading her student's freedom for her student's head. Sebastian had spent twelve years calling it betrayal. Somewhere between Corvane and the frost line, without noticing the exact mile, she had started calling it what it was.

Which made the plan in her chest heavier, not lighter.

Because Ilsa Vane had chosen once already, and it had cost her everything, and she was ready to choose again. And Sebastian Kavanagh had decided—three nights ago, lying awake listening to Ilsa breathe—that she would not let her.

* * *

They walked the next day through falling snow, and Sebastian began laying the groundwork the way she'd once laid charm-work: small pieces, innocent-looking, assembled toward a shape the buyer wouldn't see until it was finished.

At midday she complained, convincingly she hoped, about her ankle. By afternoon she was lagging—a dozen steps, then twenty, then fifty. Not enough to alarm. Just enough to build the habit of distance.

"The temple vaults," she said as they climbed a white shoulder of hill. "Tell me again what the priest wrote about the anchor. Exactly."

Isla recited it from memory, of course; she had the whole rite folded away in that relentless mind. "*One stands within the circle and pours. One stands upon the threshold and holds. The holder must be bound to the pourer by oath or by blood or by love freely spoken.*"

"*Love freely spoken*," Sebastian repeated.

"It's a technical term. It means trust without reservation. The anchor has to hold nothing back, or the line frays and the pourer comes apart with it." Ilsa glanced sideways. "Why?"

"Just checking your translation." Sebastian looked out at the snow. "You're sure you have no reservations, Warden?"

"I've had reservations my entire life," Ilsa said dryly. "Not about you."

And there it was—the opening, laid out neat as a market stall. All Sebastian had to do was say the true thing, the enormous thing, and bind Ilsa to her openly, and then use the binding to break her heart. Because here was what Sebastian had worked out in the cold dark while Ilsa slept: the anchor-line ran both directions. If Ilsa held her, truly held her, love freely spoken, then when the moment came—when the storm demanded more than power, when it reached for the rest of Sebastian—she could sever the line herself. From her side. Cut the anchor loose and walk into the deep part of the rite alone, so that Ilsa would feel the thread go slack but never see the ending.

It was a terrible plan. It required lying to the one person whose whole life was a monument to what lying cost. And it was the only plan Sebastian had that ended with Ilsa alive.

So she said none of it. She smiled instead, small and tired, and let Ilsa believe the smile.

*Forgive me,* she thought, and put another fifty paces of daylight between the lie and the truth. *Or don't. Either way, live.*

* * *

On the fifth night, in the lee of a stone shepherd's hut with one wall still standing, the hunters found their trail.

Sebastian woke to Ilsa's hand clamped over her mouth and Ilsa's eyes very close, black in the dark. Below the ridge, maybe half a league, lights moved in the snowfall—not torchlight. Lantern-light with a wrongness to it, a greenish undertone, like light strained through pond water.

"How many?" Sebastian breathed when the hand lifted.

"Six. Maybe eight. Cavalry left behind—they're walking their horses, which means they picked up our sign at the river and aren't sure of it yet." Ilsa was already moving, rolling their blankets, scattering the cold ashes of their fire with a practiced hand. "We split their certainty. Downstream bed, bare rock, no tracks. We can lose them before Grey Hollow."

They went down through the dark at a pace that hurt, Sebastian's breath sawing, the storm in her chest pressing harder now, excited, *hungry*—it liked the fear, the way a dog likes thunder. Twice she stumbled and twice Ilsa caught her arm, and the second time neither of them let go right away.

"They're following us, not the trail," Ilsa murmured at last, crouched behind a shelf of black stone while the greenish lights swung somewhere below. "They were waiting on this road. Which means the Crown knows where we're going. Which means it knows what you are."

"Then why send six men?"

"Because it doesn't need to kill you." Ilsa's voice was very quiet. "Think, Seb. It offered you a bargain. It wants you *willing*. Six men aren't hunters—they're herdsmen. They're driving us toward the temple faster than we'd have gone ourselves."

Sebastian stared at her. "It's rushing us."

"It's starving. The wards are gone, the city's gone dark, and every day it holds Aldric it burns more of itself keeping him upright. A desperate parasite makes mistakes." Ilsa's mouth curved, and it was not a kind expression. "Let it rush us. Desperate things die sloppy."

They moved again, and the lights fell behind, and near dawn the land began to rise in earnest and the trees gave out altogether, and there it was at last: Grey Hollow, a notch in the mountains stuffed full of cloud, with the old temple road switchbacking up through it, stones worn smooth by feet that had been dead two hundred years.

Ilsa stopped and studied the pass for a long time.

"What?" Sebastian asked.

"Too easy," Ilsa said. "No pickets. No watchers. If I were the Crown, this is where I'd wait." She turned, and in the gray pre-dawn light her face was unreadable, and something cold slid down Sebastian's spine—not at the pass, at the look. At how tired Ilsa suddenly seemed. How settled.

"Warden. What aren't you telling me?"

"A calculation," Ilsa said. "Nothing more. Come on—we make the temple by nightfall, and then it's done talking."

But Sebastian had spent five days learning to read the new-old grammar of Ilsa Vane, the language she'd spoken before exile taught her silence, and she caught the word beneath the word. *Calculation.* Ilsa had run numbers all night, and some of the arithmetic had come out in Sebastian's favor, and Ilsa intended to pay the difference herself.

*No,* Sebastian thought, with a ferocity that startled her, that made the snow around her boots shiver and hiss. *Whatever you've decided to sacrifice, Warden, put it down. I found the same numbers. I got there first.*

She said nothing. She followed Ilsa up into the clouds, one hand pressed flat against her sternum, feeling the storm press back, patient and immense, like a wolf waking slowly beneath the floorboards of her life.

By noon the snow had stopped. By midafternoon the clouds tore open ahead of them, and Sebastian saw the star-temple for the first time—black spires against blue ice, crowned with empty observatories, silent as a held breath.

And on the highest tower, where no lamp should burn, a single light was moving. Waiting. As if someone inside had been counting the days too.
