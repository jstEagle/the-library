## 13. What the Stars Remember

The star-temple did not look like a temple anymore. It looked like a wound in the mountain that had learned, over centuries, to close around its own hurt.

Sebastian felt it the moment they passed beneath the shattered archway—a pressure against her skin like the hush before lightning, but inverted. Not gathering. Waiting. The walls were black glass veined with silver, and where once a thousand stars had been set into the vaulted ceiling above them, only empty sockets remained, each one a small dark crater where a stone had drunk light for an age and now had nothing left to hold.

"Twelve years," she said quietly, "and I never knew this was here."

"You weren't meant to." Ilsa walked ahead of her through the ruined nave, torch held low, her wounded side still bound tight beneath the grey traveling coat. She moved carefully, favoring her right leg from the frost plains, but her eyes swept every shadow with the old warden's habit. "The order kept it that way. A temple to stars nobody could see anymore—after the first century of the Crown, there wasn't much point in worship. People stopped coming. Then people stopped remembering."

"And yet you remembered."

"I archive things." Ilsa's voice was flat, but Sebastian heard what lived under the flatness: shame, worn smooth by repetition until it almost sounded like duty. "That's all I am now. Someone who keeps records other people would rather burn."

The nave opened onto a descent—a spiral stair cut into the mountain's living rock, each step worn concave by feet long dead. Cold air rose up to meet them, carrying a smell Sebastian couldn't name at first. When she placed it, she stopped on the stair.

Ozone. Faint and old, like a storm that had passed through this place centuries ago and left its breath behind.

Ilsa glanced back. "You feel it."

"It feels like—" Sebastian pressed her palm flat against the wall. The black glass hummed against her skin, so faintly she might have imagined it. "Like me. Before I sealed myself. That first hour after a storm breaks, when everything is washed clean and the air still remembers."

Something crossed Ilsa's face—not quite pain, not quite tenderness. She turned back down the stairs without answering, and Sebastian followed, telling herself the ache in her chest was just the altitude.

* * *

At the bottom of the stair was a door of white stone, unmarked, untouched by the rot that had eaten the rest of the temple. Set into its center was a single star-shaped socket, empty as all the others.

Ilsa reached into her pack and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it with the care of someone handling a relic or a confession: a small stone, dull grey until it caught the torchlight—and then it wasn't dull at all. Light moved inside it, slow as honey, silver-white and patient.

"The last true star-stone," Ilsa said. "Recovered from the temple archives before the Crown's people sealed the library. It took me four years to find it and two more to understand what it was for."

"You're going to open it."

"I'm going to knock." Ilsa met her eyes. "Whatever's down here has been alone a very long time, Sebastian. I'd rather not arrive like an inquisitor."

She set the stone into the socket. For a heartbeat, nothing. Then light flooded the door from within, tracing the star's outline, spilling out along seams Sebastian hadn't seen, and the whole face of the stone split down the middle and swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all.

Warmth breathed over them. Real warmth, impossible warmth, smelling of summer grass and rain-wet earth—the smell of a sky full of stars on a night warm enough to lie out beneath them. Sebastian stepped through and forgot how to breathe.

* * *

The vault beyond was a garden.

Not metaphorically. Green moss carpeted a chamber the size of a cathedral nave, lit from no source Sebastian could find—light simply existed here, diffuse and golden, the way it exists in memory rather than in fact. Flowers she didn't recognize grew in drifts among the moss, pale as moonstone. A stream no wider than her hand ran through the center of the room and vanished into the rock, and its water made a sound like distant bells.

Standing at the stream's edge, hands folded, waiting, was an old man.

He was small and thin, dressed in robes that might once have been white and were now the color of old ivory, patched at the elbows with fabric from three different centuries. His beard was long and white and uncombed. His eyes, when he lifted them, were the most startling thing about him: they held light the way the star-sockets overhead must once have held stars, and looking into them was like looking up.

"You're late," he said. His voice was dry and cracked, a door long unopened. "But then, everyone is late. That is the nature of lateness. Come in, come in. Mind the feverfew; it bites."

"You're—" Sebastian began.

"The last priest of the Order of the Veiled Sky. Brother Callum, if titles bore you. They should; mine certainly do." He looked at Ilsa. "And the archivist. Yes. I know why you've come. I've known since the stars went out. I spent the first month hoping I was wrong." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, gentle as a graveside. "I was not wrong."

"The Hollow Crown," Ilsa said. "Everything the archives say—"

"—is incomplete. Yes. Sit." He gestured at the moss, which obligingly thickened and softened beneath their feet as they lowered themselves onto it. Sebastian sank nearly to her wrists; it was like sitting in warm water. "You want a weapon. Every person who comes asking about the Crown wants a weapon. I am going to give you a story instead, and you are going to hate me for it, and then you are going to need it. Are we agreed?"

Neither of them answered. Brother Callum nodded as though they had.

* * *

"Before the first king," he began, settling himself beside the stream with the groans of a man whose joints had outlived his patience, "there was no crown. There was no kingdom. There were scattered peoples, and above them, the sky—and in the sky, the Shepherd."

Sebastian frowned. "The Shepherd?"

"That is what my order called it. Other peoples had other names. The Warden of Lights. The Night-Father. The Quiet Flame." He glanced at Ilsa, and something flickered in his ancient eyes. "Ah. You know that one. Your wardens took their oath-name from it, did they know? Probably not. Institutions forget faster than stones erode." He waved the objection away before Ilsa could speak. "It doesn't matter. What matters is this: the Shepherd was real. A guardian spirit, older than any human law—a mind woven out of starlight and the slow turning of the heavens. Its purpose was simple. It watched. It guided. When the nights grew too dark, when the peoples below lost their way, it bent the light of the stars toward them like a mother tilting a lamp toward a child who cannot sleep.

"And the first king—" Callum's voice roughened, "—the first king wanted more than guidance."

Sebastian sat forward despite herself. "What did he do?"

"He caught it." The words fell into the vault like stones into deep water. "There was a rite—we have fragments of it, enough to make my stomach turn even now. He built the first crown of iron and cold-forged star-glass, and on the longest night of the year, when the Shepherd came closest to the world to do its work, he called it down and bound it. Bound it into the metal. Into the shape of a circlet, because a circlet can be worn, and what can be worn can be commanded."

The stream's bell-song seemed to falter. Sebastian realized her hands had gone cold.

"He enslaved a god," Ilsa said slowly, "to run a kingdom."

"He enslaved a guardian to serve ambition. And it worked, at first—that is the horror of it. Under the Shepherd's stolen power, crops grew in drought, plagues broke, enemies' armies wandered lost in clear weather. The first king called it divine right, and his descendants called it bloodline blessing, and every generation the binding was renewed, and every generation the binding chafed." Callum picked up a handful of stream-water and let it run through his fingers. Where it touched his skin, it glowed briefly, then went dark. "You cannot cage something made of freedom and expect it to stay gentle. Fear curdles. Love, denied too long, turns to hunger. Somewhere in the long centuries—my order believes it was during the reign of the fourth king, though we cannot prove it—the Shepherd's patience finally broke, and what remained was no longer a shepherd at all.

"It became a parasite. It learned that it could take. It learned that magic itself was food, and that kings were doors. And it renamed itself in the only language a caged thing understands: possession." He looked up at them both. "Hollow Crown is what your age calls it. We called it the Grieving Star, because even corrupted, even starving, it weeps. Have you heard it? In the palace, at night? My sources say the servants call it wind in the chimneys."

Sebastian had not been in the palace. But she thought of Aldric's procession, of the king's shadow lagging half a heartbeat behind him, and something twisted in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name.

"Why tell us this?" she asked. Her voice came out harder than she intended. "If it's a corrupted god, if it's grieving—what difference does that make? It's eating our kingdom. It ate the wards. It put out the stars. Knowing it's sad doesn't stop it."

"No," Callum agreed mildly. "Knowing it's sad does not stop a wolf. But it changes how you hunt one." He fixed her with those star-filled eyes. "Tell me, storm-caller. What did you come here believing?"

Sebastian hesitated. The truth was ugly and she knew it. "That I pour my storm through it until nothing's left. Mine or its. Preferably both."

"Yes. The rite of unbinding. I wrote part of it, actually—centuries ago, in a fit of optimism I have since regretted." He sighed. "It will work. Fire burns out fire. But understand what you would be doing. The Shepherd did not choose this. It was seized in the night by a man who wanted its power, chained in iron for a thousand years, fed on fear, taught that love is a trick and freedom is a wound. And now you propose to walk into its prison and burn it alive, and call it salvation."

"What else would you call it?" Ilsa asked quietly. "It killed my king. It's killing everyone."

"It is killing because it was killed first, in every way that matters." Callum spread his thin hands. "I am not asking you to forgive it. I am asking you to grieve it. There is a difference, and the difference may be the only reason any of us survive what comes next."

* * *

Sebastian stood. The moss released her reluctantly. She walked to the stream and stared down into it, watching her own reflection waver there—tinker's clothes, tinker's name, and underneath, twelve years deep, the girl who had once called lightning down on her own village without meaning to.

"You're saying it's like me."

Callum said nothing, which was answer enough.

"My whole life, I've been told the storm in me is a curse. Corvane says magic is sickness. The order said I was dangerous. Everyone agrees on the cure: bury it, bind it, starve it." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twelve years I've done that. And do you know what happens when you cage a thing like that? It doesn't get smaller. It gets louder. Some nights I can feel it pressing against the seals like a hand against a door, and it isn't angry, exactly. It's—" She searched for the word and found it and hated it. "Lonely. It's lonely, and it wants out, and it doesn't care what breaks on the way."

"Now you understand the Crown," Callum said softly.

"I understand it." She turned to face him. "Understanding isn't mercy. If I let it go—if I try to grieve it instead of burning it—it will eat me the way it ate Aldric. It'll eat Ilsa. It'll eat the city that sheltered me when they burned my quarter. Compassion is a luxury for people who aren't standing in the wolf's mouth."

"Compassion is not surrender." The old priest rose too, slowly, joints crackling like green wood in fire. He crossed to the far wall of the vault, where the black glass gave way to something else—a mural, ancient and faded, that Sebastian hadn't noticed in the golden light. Now she saw it: figures painted in mineral pigments, circling a central form.

The central form was a crown. But painted around it, reaching toward it with hands both pleading and offering, were dozens of smaller figures—kings, priests, common folk—and above them all, vast and sorrowful, a great eye of silver light, half-open, weeping lines of pale paint that ran down the wall like rain.

"This is the oldest record we have," Callum said. "Older than the rite. Older than the corruption, maybe. Look at the hands, child. What do you see?"

Sebastian studied it. "They're... reaching for it. Some of them. Not all fighting. Some are—" She stopped. "Some are trying to help it. Or be helped by it. Both at once."

"The rite of unbinding requires a storm poured through the Crown. Fire against fire. That will work—but it will also destroy whatever remains of the Shepherd underneath. Everything it was before the first king's greed. Every night it ever lit for a frightened child. Gone." He touched the weeping eye in the mural with one crooked finger. "Unless. Unless the storm-bearer goes in not as an executioner but as a mourner. Unless, in the moment the fire rises, someone remembers aloud what it used to be. Names it. Grieves it. Gives it back, for one instant, the shape of what it loved being." His voice dropped. "In that instant, the Crown must choose. To keep devouring, and die whole. Or to let go, and end as what it was born to be."

"Or it takes the chance to tear me apart while I'm busy singing lullabies to it," Sebastian said.

"Yes," Callum agreed placidly. "That is also possible. I never claimed this path was safe. Only that it was truer."

Silence filled the vault. The stream rang. Somewhere far above, muffled by a thousand feet of mountain, thunder rolled—or perhaps it was only the weight of the darkened sky settling.

Ilsa spoke from behind her, quiet. "You don't have to decide tonight."

"Don't I?" Sebastian didn't turn around. "Hale is marching here with everything the Crown has left. You're bleeding through your bandages. And somewhere north of us, a man wearing my king's face is drinking the world dry. Tonight is all anyone's giving us." She finally faced the old priest. "Show me the rite. All of it. Both versions—mine and yours. If I'm going to walk into that throne room, I want every tool I can carry, even the ones shaped like grief."

Brother Callum studied her for a long moment, and then he bowed—low, formal, the bow of one priest to another kind of calling.

"Then come," he said, turning toward the depths of the vault, where a second door waited in the black glass, older than the first and marked with the same weeping star. "And pray, storm-caller, that when the moment comes, you find it in yourself to weep for the thing that is trying to kill you. Because I promise you this much—" He paused at the threshold, and his starlit eyes were wet. "It remembers what it lost. Every single day. And no one, in a thousand years, has ever once asked it to."

Behind them, faint through the mountain's bones, Sebastian heard the sound she had been dreading since the frost plains began: horns. Many of them. And under the horns, carried on air that should not carry sound across such distance, a voice like a chimney full of weeping wind, speaking with perfect clarity directly into her ear:

*Little storm. I know what the old man told you. It will not save you.*

*Come anyway. I have been so hungry, and you smell like home.*
