## 14. Hale's Last March

Ilsa Vane had stood watch on the frost plains before, in the years when she still believed the wards would hold forever and her orders came from men who meant them. She had frozen in three different winters, bled in two, and buried a warden once in ground so hard they had to break it with fire. None of that had taught her what she needed now, which was how to hold a mountain pass alone against a man who had once shown her mercy and no longer had a self to show anything with.

She crouched behind a shelf of black stone at the mouth of the Serpent's Throat, the pass that climbed toward the ruined star-temple, and counted the lights below.

Torches. Nine of them, maybe ten, strung along the switchback trail like a rosary of embers. At their head walked a figure in grey robes gone stiff with frost, moving with the unhurried certainty of something that did not feel the cold because it had forgotten cold was ever a thing bodies suffered.

Hale.

Even at this distance, even in the dark where stars ought to be and weren't, Ilsa knew his walk. Twelve years of reports, of sharing campfires on border inspections, of him sliding a cup of bad tea across a table and saying *warden* in that dry voice that pretended he hadn't just told a joke. He had been the one who'd found Sebastian Kavanagh half-starved in the reeds after the storm, twelve years old with lightning still crawling in her hair, and he had looked at the girl for a long moment and then written in his report: *no further pursuit warranted.*

She had never thanked him for that. There had never seemed to be time, and then there had never seemed to be a way to say it without admitting what else she'd done.

Now there was no time at all.

Behind her, up the switchbacks, the temple door glowed faintly gold where Sebastian had gone through it an hour ago with the last star-priest and a satchel of texts older than the kingdom. Sebastian had wanted to argue. Of course she had. She had stood in the snow with her jaw set like her father's had been and said, *I'm not leaving you to die in a pass so I can save a king,* and Ilsa had said the only true thing she could think of:

"You're not saving a king. You're saving everyone who has to live under one."

It had worked, which was its own small shame. Lies worked on Sebastian; Ilsa had proven that twelve years ago. Truths, apparently, also worked, which was worse, because it meant the lie hadn't even been necessary.

The torches were climbing.

Ilsa checked her gear by touch. Two short swords, one long blade taken from a dead inquisitor whose name she'd made a point of learning—Corren, twenty-six years old, from the salt marshes—and a quiver of bolts for a crossbow she was not sure she could still shoot straight with her left shoulder the way it was. The wound from Corvane had closed into a hot wire of pain that ran from collarbone to elbow every time she raised the arm above her heart.

She raised it anyway, once, testing. The wire sang. She lowered it and decided she would simply not miss.

Below, the column stopped at the third switchback. Hale lifted one hand, and the torches dimmed—not blown out, but *dimmed*, as if the flames themselves had flinched. His head turned. Even across four hundred yards of freezing dark, Ilsa felt the turn like a hand laid flat against her sternum.

The Hollow Crown had eaten everything of Aldric except the shell that walked and spoke. In Hale it had gotten less. He had been half-consumed when he begged Sebastian to end him in the undercity; whatever mercy or hunger had stayed its hand since, it had finished the work. What climbed the pass now wore Hale's face the way a glove wears a hand, stretched over something that used the shape only because shapes were useful for walking.

And it knew she was here.

"Then come up," Ilsa said quietly, to no one, to him, to the thing inside him. "You'll find me a poor host."

* * *

They had argued about this in the temple antechamber, by the light of a lamp that burned without oil—the priest's doing, one of the few miracles left in the building.

"Two of us," Sebastian had said. "We hold the choke point together, and when it's done we both come inside."

"And if it's not done?" Ilsa had asked. "If there are more behind them? If the Crown sends the city garrison after? Someone holds until you finish, and someone finishes. That's the arithmetic. You taught me arithmetic, actually—you don't remember, you were eleven, you corrected my supply tallies."

"I remember everything," Sebastian had said, too fast, and looked away, and Ilsa had understood suddenly that the girl had spent twelve years memorizing her exile the way other people memorize scripture: every word said at the tribunal, every step of the road east, every face. Including hers. Especially hers.

So she had lied again, gently, the way you set a bone: "I'll fall back the moment it turns. I've run from worse than ten torches." She had run from nothing worse than this. She intended to run from nothing at all.

Now, watching the lights climb, she arranged her lies in order and found she was tired of all of them.

* * *

The first three hunters reached the throat of the pass at moonrise—or where moonrise should have been; there had been no moon since the night the stars went out, only a blank ceiling where the sky used to be, and the dark pressed close as wool.

They were not soldiers. That was the worst part. She saw it as they came into the reach of her lamp-shield: a gate-sergeant from the north wall, a woman Ilsa had personally commended for holding the granary gates during the bread riots. A clerk with ink-stained fingers. A boy in a courier's coat, sixteen at most, walking in his sleep with his eyes open and empty as wells.

Not an army. A harvest. The Crown was spending people the way a drunk spends coin, and it had gotten to the capital's people first because they were nearest.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, and put a bolt through the boy's knee—not his chest, not yet—took his legs out of the fight, and stepped out of the shadow of the rock with both swords drawn.

The pass narrowed here to six feet of scree between walls of black stone, the old star-temple road cut by hands that had believed light was a form of prayer. It was the best killing ground in forty miles, and Hale had chosen it anyway, which told her he didn't care about losses. Or that some part of Hale, deep down under the rot, remembered this ground from a border survey nine years ago and had brought her here deliberately, to ground she'd know, to give her every advantage the dead man in him still could.

She chose to believe that. She suspected she'd need to, later, for what she meant to do.

The sergeant came first, sword rising in perfect parade form. Ilsa slipped inside the arc, drove her pommel into the woman's temple, and let her drop. The clerk swung wildly; she broke his wrist and put him down with a knee to the jaw. Neither would die. She made sure. She was not fighting these people. She was fighting past them.

Then the pass filled with grey, and Hale was there.

Up close, the consumption was worse than she had imagined. His skin had gone the color of candle-tallow, translucent at the cheeks, and beneath the skin moved shadows that did not correspond to anything in his body—threads of darkness crawling like roots under ice. His eyes were open and his eyes were not his. They were windows painted black from the outside.

"Warden Vane," the Crown said through his mouth, and it used his voice, his dry, tired, tea-joke voice, and it was obscene, it was grave-robbery, it was the single cruelest thing she had heard in a life that included the tribunal where she'd sentenced Sebastian. "You are carrying stolen archives. You are harboring an unbound storm-caller. You are in violation of—"

"I wrote your violations," Ilsa said. "I signed the exile order. I sat in that room and read the charges while you stood in the back with your arms crossed, hating every word, and you let me do it because the alternative was telling the truth about what she was and letting the Crown have her. So spare me the law, Hale. You and I are past law. We're in the part where we account."

The thing wearing him tilted its head, and for one flickering instant the black windows cleared, and it was Hale looking out—Hale exhausted, Hale horrified, Hale mouthing words she couldn't hear but understood anyway: *run, you idiot, run.*

Then it was gone, drowned again, and Hale's sword came up.

He was better than she was. He always had been; he'd trained the border duelists for a decade. But he was fighting in a body that no longer fully obeyed him, and she was fighting on ground she'd walked in dreams for nine years, and grief is its own kind of strength when you stop pretending you aren't carrying it.

She gave ground. Switchback by switchback, she gave ground, bleeding him for every yard, letting the narrow stone do her work, using the crossbow twice at ranges where it was nearly a club. A slash opened her thigh. She returned it across his forearm and watched the wound well black instead of red and felt her stomach turn over. Another took her across the ribs, shallow, burning, and she heard herself make a sound that was not pain exactly, or not only.

Above her, far up the mountain, the temple's gold glow pulsed once. Twice. Beginning.

*Hold,* she told herself. *Whatever it costs, hold. She's almost free of you. Hold.*

Because that was the truth underneath everything, the truth she had finally stopped running from somewhere on the frost plains in the dark hours before dawn: this was not duty. This had not been duty since Corvane, maybe since the beginning. This was atonement, and atonement does not negotiate, and she had twelve years of debt with interest accruing, and the ledger was finally open, and she meant to balance it if it killed her.

It was going to kill her. She had known it since she'd watched Sebastian walk through the temple door and felt the specific lightness of a decision already made. Some part of her had known it for twelve years—that debts like hers didn't get paid in installments. They got paid all at once or not at all.

Her only fear, and it surprised her, was not the dying. Her fear was dying unreconciled. Dying with the words unsaid. Dying the way she had lived: behind procedure, behind rank, behind the cowardly architecture of doing the correct cruel thing instead of the kind difficult one. If she died tonight she wanted—Gods, how badly she wanted—to have said it. Out loud. To the right person. Even if the hearing of it changed nothing. Even if it was too late by twelve years and a continent.

*Survive until she comes down,* she bargained with whatever listened in the dark sky. *Let me say it. Then take whatever you want.*

The dark sky, being empty of stars, said nothing. But the pass went on narrowing, and the Crown kept sending people up it, and Ilsa Vane planted her feet on the ninth switchback and prepared to be very expensive to move.

* * *

They came in waves after that, and memory blurs the way blood blurs in water.

She remembered the courier boy coming at her again despite the knee, dragging himself forward on his sword like it was a crutch, and the terrible patience of the thing driving him. She remembered putting him down gently, almost tenderly, and apologizing to his sleeping face.

She remembered Hale's voice changing mid-fight—sliding from the Crown's flat recitation back into the man's dry cadence for three whole seconds, long enough to gasp: *"Vane. The archive vault. Page ninety. She'll understand—"*

Then the black swallowed him again, and he fought like a machine, and so did she.

She remembered the moment her left arm stopped answering. The rib wound had gone deep after all, or the old Corvane wound had torn loose; either way the arm hung and the wire of pain became a bar and she dropped the shield-side sword and took the long blade in both hands on her right, and the pass got very simple after that. One blade. One line. One job.

She remembered counting. Not enemies—she'd stopped being able to count enemies—but heartbeats, or breaths, or seconds between pulses of gold from the temple above, each pulse stronger than the last, the rite climbing toward its peak, Sebastian climbing with it, and Ilsa measuring her remaining life against the rhythm like a woman counting waves between herself and shore.

Forty-one pulses. Forty-two. On the forty-third, Hale's sword opened her side from hip to floating rib, and the world tilted, and she went down on one knee on the frozen scree with her blood steaming off the stones, and the thing wearing him stood over her and raised the blade for the ending.

And Ilsa Vane, warden without a ward, exiler of innocents, keeper of twelve years of profitable lies, looked up at the dead man who had once spared a storm-child in the reeds, and found she was smiling.

"Do it," she said. "But you should know—I'm glad it's you. If it has to be anyone. You were the last decent thing the crown had, and I think some of you is still in there, and I think—" blood filled her mouth, and she spat it, and kept going, because if these were her last words she was damned if they'd be tidy, "—I think you tried. All those years. You tried, and nobody thanked you, and I'm thanking you now, you miserable, honorable—"

Hale's blade came down.

And stopped.

Stopped a hand's width from her neck, trembling, the whole arm shaking like a branch in wind, and the black windows cracked—cracked like ice, like glass, like a prison wall—and through the cracks Hale looked out one final time with eyes gone human and wet, and his mouth shaped three words she would carry wherever she was going:

*"Tell her—sorry—"*

Then he turned the blade on himself, or the last of him did, and the Crown screamed through his mouth with a sound like a kingdom tearing, and the body folded down beside her onto the frozen stones, and the shadows under the skin went still, and the pass was quiet except for the wind and her own ruined breathing.

Isla lay back against the black rock. The sky overhead was still a blank ceiling, but high above, at the summit, the temple had begun to burn gold through every window, brighter and brighter, a second dawn arriving sideways out of the mountain.

*Hold until she's done,* she had promised herself. *Say it, then go.*

Sebastian wasn't done. The rite wasn't done. And Ilsa's blood was leaving faster than the cold could argue with, pooling warm beneath her, and she pressed both hands to the wound and laughed—a wet, broken, genuinely amused sound, because of course. Of course this was the shape of it. She buys the hours and doesn't get to see what they purchase. She pays the debt and dies before the receipt.

"No," she said aloud, to the dark, to the absent stars, to whatever ledger held her name. "No, I don't accept that. I've accepted every other rotten term for fifty years. Not this one."

She got her knees under her. The world went white, then gray, then merely terrible. She got one foot flat on the scree. Somewhere above her, the mountain rang like a struck bell, and the bell was Sebastian, and the sound it made was the sound of twelve caged years coming unlocked all at once.

Ilsa Vane began to climb.

One switchback. Blood on the stones marking her progress like a trail home. Two. Her vision narrowing to a corridor with gold light at the end of it. Three, dragging her dead arm over the rock, teeth gritted around words she was saving, hoarding them, refusing to spend them anywhere but the right ear—

At the top of the fourth switchback, the temple doors stood open, pouring light, and a figure staggered out into the snow and turned toward her, and even at that distance, even half-blind, Ilsa would have known the silhouette anywhere: storm-caller, tinker, exile, the girl she had ruined trying to save.

Sebastian saw her. Stopped. And started to run down through the snow, fast, reckless, shouting something the wind tore apart—

—and behind her, through the open doors, the light changed. Gold curdled to violet. The bell-note bent into a scream. And the blank sky above the mountain split down its whole length like old cloth, and through the tear, for the first time in a season, a single star came back—

—and it was falling.
