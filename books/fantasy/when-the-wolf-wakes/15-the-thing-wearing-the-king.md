## 15. The Thing Wearing the King

The message came to her in a dream, and it came wrong—sideways, like light bent through cracked glass.

Sebastian stood on a road she knew too well: the frost plains outside Corvane, the same stretch she and Ilsa would cross in two days' time if the maps held. But in the dream the snow was black, and the stars overhead were not merely absent—they had been *eaten*, leaving holes in the dark with ragged edges, like cloth gnawed through by moths. And down the road, at the far limit of seeing, sat a throne.

She knew it was a dream. She knew it was the Crown's country now, that sleeping was walking into its parlor with her boots off. She'd taken precautions—Ilsa's iron ring on one hand, the star-priest's salt in her pocket, a knot of storm-sense coiled behind her ribs like a fist ready to open. Twelve years of practice at keeping her magic small had taught her every trick of hiding there was, and some of them, apparently, worked even here.

But dreams were the Crown's native tongue. It could speak to her whenever it liked.

So when it spoke, and the voice was *not* the Crown's, she almost missed it.

*"Kavanagh."*

Not the honeyed chorus the thing wore for a voice—that layered whisper of every king it had swallowed, beautiful and rotten as fruit gone soft inside. This voice was hoarse. Human. A man's voice, worn down to the grain, the way wood wears where hands grip it year after year.

She turned. The black snow crunched beneath her boots—a real sound, an honest sound—and there, halfway between her and the throne, stood King Aldric.

Or something shaped like him.

He was thinner than his portraits. Corvane minted his face onto every coin, and Sebastian had counted out those coins a thousand times without a second glance: the strong jaw, the deep-set eyes, the crown rendered in tiny raised metal above a brow furrowed by the weight of office. The man on the road had all of that, but hollowed—the jaw sharp enough to cut, the eyes sunk into shadow, the royal furs hanging off shoulders that had forgotten what they were carrying. He looked like a coat someone had emptied and left on a hook.

His shadow did not stand with him. It lay stretched toward the throne instead, taut as a leash, quivering faintly, like a dog straining against its rope.

"You're not real," Sebastian said carefully. "You're the bait on the hook."

"I know." He said it simply, without offense, the way a tired man agrees that yes, it is raining again. "I think I'm real enough for this. It sleeps deeper than it thinks it does. It spends so much of itself drinking—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the ragged holes where stars should be, "—that there are hours when it forgets to hold me under."

"Why show yourself?" Her hand had drifted to her pocket, to the salt. "Why give me your face? Is this the bargain again? The Crown already tried buying me once."

"Not the Crown." His mouth twisted, and for a moment there was something underneath the ruin—something wry, almost boyish, the ghost of a man who had once been young in a kingdom that still had stars. "Me. Aldric. Whatever's left. Listen to me, storm-caller, because I don't know how long I have. When it notices I'm standing in my own front garden, it will pull, and I will go, and you'll wake up thinking this was just a bad night's sleep."

The snow whispered around them. Somewhere far off, in the direction of the throne, something vast exhaled.

"Then talk fast," Sebastian said.

He laughed—a dry, broken sound, more cough than laugh. "Fast. Yes. I've been composing speeches for years in there. In the dark. You'd think a man would run out of words, but no. I've written epics no one will ever hear. Odes to my daughter. Apologies in seventeen movements." The humor guttered out of him like a candle pinched between wet fingers. "Here is what matters. Here is the only thing that matters."

He stepped closer, and up close she could see the wrongness in detail—the way his veins showed dark beneath the skin, branching patterns like frost on a window, like roots. Like the sigil of the fallen border-ward Ilsa had carried to her door, bleeding shadow across her kitchen table.

"The rite you're planning," he said. "I can feel you planning it. It's like weather coming. I've felt storms before, girl—I'm a king; I've stood on walls while my wardens called lightning down on sieges. What's coming feels like that. Only bigger. Only *you*-sized."

"Then you know what it costs."

"I know what everything costs. That's most of what being a king is." His eyes—Aldric's eyes, human eyes, terrified and stubborn—held hers. "I'm not asking you to stop. Gods, no. I'm asking you to hurry. But listen. There's something you need to carry, because I may not be able to say it when you arrive. If I surface at all. Understand—it fights for the mouth. The moment I try to speak through my own face, it slams me down like a door. So I'm saying it here, in the one place it doesn't think to look, because to the Crown a dream is just another pantry."

"What do you need me to carry?"

"My daughter," Aldric said, and the word broke him open.

* * *

Sebastian woke with tears freezing on her lashes and did not remember starting to cry.

The ruined waystation they'd sheltered in was dark except for the banked fire, and Ilsa Vane sat propped against the wall opposite, awake as always, whetstone whispering along the edge of her sword. The sound stopped.

"You cried out," Ilsa said quietly.

"Did I?"

"Twice. The first time, you said 'no.' The second time you said—" Ilsa hesitated, and Sebastian watched her decide whether to finish the sentence. She finished it. "You said 'I promise.' Then you went still as a corpse and slept like one."

Sebastian sat up. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them with distant irritation, as if they belonged to a frightened stranger, and folded them in her lap until the shaking slowed.

"It wasn't the Crown," she said. "That's the strange part. I've been braced for it to come at me again—another dream, another offer, exile's end, legitimacy, your safety, all the lovely poisoned fruit. It didn't come. *He* did."

Ilsa set the whetstone down. In the firelight her scarred face was unreadable, but her whole body had gone alert, the way it did before a fight. "Aldric."

"Aldric." Sebastian hugged her knees, staring into the embers. "Surfacing through the thing wearing him. For a few minutes, maybe less. Long enough to tell me—" Her voice caught on the shape of it. "Long enough to ask me for something no one's asked me for in twelve years."

"What?"

"To be a messenger." She laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "Not a savior. Not a storm. A messenger. Ilsa, do you know what he asked? Not freedom—he didn't waste breath on that, though I heard him want it, it was rolling off him like heat off a forge. Not vengeance. Not even mercy. He asked me to find Princess Elowen—she's at the convent at Saint Verre, the Crown keeps her close, keeps her *quiet*—and tell her that her father fought. That's all. That he fought, and that he never stopped, and that he remembers her birthday, every single one, because it's the only calendar he kept in the dark."

Silence, except for the fire settling.

"He told me about her," Sebastian went on, quieter. "Told me she used to sit under his council table drawing horses while the ministers shouted about grain tariffs. Told me she has her mother's temper and his terrible singing voice. Told me—" Sebastian pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Gods, he *told me things*. Little things. The kind of things only a father knows. He's been imprisoned inside himself for years and what he's been doing with the dark is memorizing his daughter."

Ilsa said nothing for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice had the roughness it got when she was keeping it level by force. "That's how you beat them. Things like that Crown. The old texts say it eats kings because kings are the biggest meal—but a king who loves something bigger than himself..." She shook her head slowly. "It can swallow the throne. Maybe it can't swallow that."

"No," Sebastian agreed softly. "I don't think it can. I think that's why he could surface at all. It holds him down everywhere except the one place it has no grip." She looked up. "And that means something else, Ilsa. Something important."

"That it fears him?"

"That it fears *us*. All of us. Me, you, Hale before it finished eating him, the priest, the outcasts in the cellars of Corvane." She unfolded herself and rose, pacing the narrow room, energy crackling through her that had nothing to do with magic—or everything to do with it. "Think about it. The Crown offered me a bargain. Why would it bargain? It drinks kingdoms. It ate the wards in a single night. It could crush me like a beetle if power were all that mattered. It bargained because it's *afraid*, and it's afraid of exactly the thing Aldric is made of right now: people who won't let go of each other. Love isn't sentimental, Ilsa. It's structural. It's the one load-bearing wall the thing can't chew through."

Ilsa watched her pace, and something moved behind her eyes—an old memory, perhaps, of a younger woman who had once talked like this with her hands before the world taught her to keep them still. "Careful," she said at last, gently. "Hope is a weapon with the edge toward your own thumb."

"I know." Sebastian stopped pacing. Outside, wind dragged across the frost plains with a long low moan, and both of them glanced at the shuttered window out of old habit. "But we leave for the temple tomorrow, and I needed this tonight. Do you understand? I've been carrying the rite like a coffin. Pouring myself out, burning the Crown out, probably dying, and I'd made my peace with it the way you make peace with winter—by not looking directly at it. And then a dead-eyed ghost of a king shows up in my dream and asks me to deliver a message to a little girl who draws horses." Her breath shuddered out. "This isn't a battle anymore. It never was. It's a rescue. There's a man alive in there, Ilsa. Alive, and fighting, and *asking for help*, and I am going to walk into that throne room and give it to him or die trying, and either way I'm going to stop at Saint Verre afterward and tell Elowen Aldric everything he made me memorize. Every word. Even the ones about the singing."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, breath pluming silver, harnesses creaking, the frost plains stretching north and east toward the mountains where the star-temple's broken dome waited like a skull half-buried in the world's forehead.

They had made perhaps three miles when Sebastian felt it.

It began as pressure—not sound, not light, but pressure, the way the air goes strange before a twister, and every hair on her arms lifted though the morning was windless. The horses screamed and bolted against their leads. Ilsa had her sword half-drawn before Sebastian caught her arm.

"Wait. Wait—look."

On the southern horizon, back the way they'd come, back toward Corvane, the darkness was moving.

Not cloud. Clouds had edges, however ragged. This was a wall of absence advancing across the frosted land, swallowing the pale dawn as it came, and threaded through it—Sebastian squinted, disbelieving—were lights. Torches. Hundreds of torches, burning cold and colorless, held aloft by shapes that marched in perfect, terrible unison. At the head of the column rode a figure on a grey horse, and even at this distance, even through the waking world rather than the dream, Sebastian knew the set of those shoulders.

"Hale," she breathed.

"He was consumed." Ilsa's voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used over battlefield triage. "You told me. Half-eaten in the undercity, begging you—"

"Begging me to end him before the Crown spoke through his mouth. I couldn't do it, Ilsa. I gave him mercy the only way I knew how and ran." Sebastian's fists clenched. "Now it's finished the job. That's not a man riding out here. That's the Crown wearing a uniform it used to respect."

The column was too far away to be an immediate threat—but not too far to be seen, and not moving slowly. They were making for the pass. The pass was the temple's only approach, the one place the mountains could be crossed by an army, and between the hunters and the temple there was, as far as Sebastian could see on any map, exactly one defensible position: a narrow gorge where the frost river cut through the hills, wide enough for two riders abreast and no more.

Ilsa saw it the same moment she did. Sebastian watched the calculation happen in the other woman's face—the geometry of chokepoints, the arithmetic of one sword against hundreds—and watched her begin, calmly, terribly, to reach the answer.

"No," Sebastian said.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You were going to say you'd hold the pass." Her voice cracked across the plain like a whip. "Don't. Don't you dare. We go together or—"

"Sebastian." Ilsa turned to her, and the dawn light caught the scars, the iron ring, the twelve years of guilt and love worn smooth as river stone. "Look at me. Someone has to buy you time to reach the vault. Someone has to make sure the Crown's hounds don't drag you off that mountain mid-rite. And someone—" her voice dropped, roughened, "—someone owes a debt twelve years past due. Let me pay it. Please. Of the two of us, I'm the one who knows how to die well."

The dark column crawled closer. Somewhere in the dream-country of Sebastian's mind, a hollow king was still whispering *hurry*, and the wind off the plains carried, faint and cold, the sound of many feet marching in step.

Sebastian opened her mouth to argue—and closed it again. Because far to the north, past the pass, past the gorge, the sun was rising on the star-temple's shattered dome, and time, which had always been the enemy, had just drawn a blade.

"We ride," she said. "And Ilsa—if you die in that gorge unreconciled, I will haunt *you*."

For the first time in days, Ilsa Vane smiled. "Then fight fast, storm-caller."
