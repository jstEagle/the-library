## 16. Storm-Bound

The vault beneath the star-temple smelled of cold iron and old prayers.

Sebastian knelt at the center of the ritual floor, her palms flat against a disc of meteoric stone that had not been warm in three hundred years. Around her, carved into the rock in concentric rings, the names of every storm-caller who had ever knelt in this place spiraled inward like the eye of a hurricane—some of them worn smooth by centuries of kneeling feet, some still sharp-edged, cut so recently that the chisel-marks still held shadow.

Her name was not among them. It would never be among them, if the rite did what the texts said it might.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Ilsa said from the edge of the rings. She had washed the blood of the pass out of her hair but not out of the creases of her hands, and she stood with her arms crossed like a woman holding herself shut. "The priest says the unbinding must be done in calm. We can wait for calm."

"There is no calm coming, Warden." The last star-priest spoke from the shadows near the vault's eastern arch—a voice like wind through wheat, dry and patient and very old. Serael had not given Sebastian another name, and Sebastian had not asked for one. "The Crown drinks deeper every night. Every hour you wait, the king has less left to save."

"And if the unbinding kills her?"

"Then it kills her on her feet," Sebastian said, "instead of in her sleep, waiting."

She had rehearsed this speech for three days, ever since Serael first laid the rite out before them like an anatomist laying out a body: *the sealing-oaths are seven knots; each knot must be untied in order; the storm will come all at once because storms have no patience*. She had told herself she was ready. She had told Ilsa she was ready. She had almost believed it.

But now, kneeling on stone that remembered a hundred storm-callers before her, she could feel them through the rock—not their ghosts, exactly, but their weather. Grief pressed into granite. Twelve years of caged lightning sat behind her sternum like a second heart, beating slower than her own, and she understood suddenly that she had never once stopped being afraid of it. Not in Corvane. Not under a false name with copper charms and a tinker's smile. Every ordinary day she had built had been built on top of a thing that wanted out.

"Seb." Ilsa crouched at the outermost ring, careful not to cross the line. Her gray eyes were the same as they'd been on the day of the exile—steady, terrible, full of something she refused to name. "Say the words you need to say. Then do what you have to do."

*What I need to say.* There wasn't enough language in any tongue for twelve years. So Sebastian said the only true thing she could find.

"When it's over—if there's anything left of me—I want to go back to Corvane. I want my stall. I want Mrs. Halloway's roof fixed properly, and I want to teach Tam's little sister to read. That's what I'm anchored to. Not duty. Not the king." A breath. "A shop with a crooked sign and neighbors who burn my candles when they think I can't see them doing it. That's what I'm coming back to."

Ilsa's jaw worked. Whatever answer she had, she swallowed it, and Sebastian was grateful, because she didn't have room in her chest for both the fear and the answer.

Serael shuffled forward, robes whispering, and pressed three objects into Sebastian's hands: a bowl of water drawn from the temple's frozen cistern; a strip of white linen; and a sliver of black glass that caught no light at all—the shard of the Hollow Crown's prison-seal, recovered from the border-ward ruins, humming faintly like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

"The water is for remembering," the priest said. "The linen is for letting go. The glass is your key—it was made from the same binding-craft that made your oaths. Speak your true name to it, and the knots will hear you."

"My true name." Sebastian laughed, and it came out cracked. "I haven't used it in twelve years. Half the time I forget it's mine."

"A name buried is not a name dead." Serael's eyes were milk-pale, blind or nearly so, and yet they seemed to look straight through her. "That is rather the point of everything we are about to do. Kneel. Begin."

Sebastian dipped two fingers in the water and touched them to her own tongue. Cold. Clean. Tasting of deep places.

"I am—" Her voice caught on the first syllable, snagged like cloth on a nail. She tried again. "I am Sebastian Kavanagh. Daughter of Marisol Kavanagh, weaver. Of the village of Thornmere. Storm-caller of the third order, oath-bound and sealed by decree of Warden Ilsa Vane, in the year the harvest drowned."

The glass shard flared with a light that wasn't light—a darkness so complete it outlined her hands in negative, a silhouette of absence—and somewhere far below the floor, something vast turned over in its sleep.

*First knot,* said the rite. *Speak the oath you swore.*

Sebastian closed her eyes.

"I swore to bury my gift," she said. "I swore that no storm would pass through me again, that I would live small and quiet and harmless, that I would let the sky do its work without me. I swore it with my hand in the rain and my mentor's blade at my throat, and I meant it. Stars help me, I meant every word."

She laid the glass against her collarbone, where the first seal had been driven in like a nail—a scar she had always told the market-women was a smithing accident.

"Untie."

The sound the oath made coming undone was nothing like thunder. It was quieter than that, and worse: a single high note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup, rising and rising until it passed beyond hearing and became pressure instead of sound. Something in her chest *shifted*, a lock turning after twelve years of rust, and warmth flooded down her left arm like water finding an old channel.

Sebastian gasped. Her eyes flew open, and for half a heartbeat she saw the vault lit blue-white from within her own skin—veins of lightning crawling up her wrists, dancing across her knuckles—and then it guttered and sank, coiling back down into the dark.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, it's still *there*."

"It was always there," Serael said. "You cannot kill a storm. You can only postpone it. Second knot. Quickly now—the first will wake the rest, and they will not wait politely."

* * *

The second knot was the oath of silence. The third, the oath of place—she had sworn never to return to the storm-country of her birth, and speaking that one loose made her weep without quite knowing why, tears that steamed where they hit her own glowing skin. The fourth knot was the cruelest: the oath of harmlessness, sworn not to Ilsa or the crown but to the memory of everyone her childhood storm had touched. To speak it aloud she had to say the names. Forty-one names. She got through thirty-eight before her voice broke entirely, and Ilsa—crossing the ritual line without permission, consequences be damned—knelt beside her and held her shoulders and finished the last three names with her, steady as a drumbeat, as if she had been keeping count too.

Of course she had been keeping count. Ilsa Vane kept count of everything. That was the whole shape of her.

By the fifth knot, Sebastian was shaking. By the sixth, she was laughing—high and helpless and half-hysterical—as the power rose through her like floodwater through a cellar stair, filling channels that had lain dry so long she'd convinced herself they were imaginary. Twelve years. Twelve years of copper charms and candle smoke and pretending her hands were just hands, and underneath it all the storm had waited, patient as winter, *faithful* as winter, and now it recognized her the way a dog recognizes its person after a war.

*Come home,* it seemed to say. *Come home, come home, come home.*

The seventh knot was the sealing-oath itself, the master-lock, and Serael's voice reached her from a great distance: *"This one you cannot untie with words alone. This one you must open like a door—with intent, and with something worth walking through it toward. Think of your anchor, child. Think of the shop with the crooked sign."*

Sebastian thought of Corvane. Of lamplight on wet cobblestones. Of Mrs. Halloway's bad hip and the way the baker saved her the end-crusts and how Tam's sister had fallen asleep against her shoulder during the fever-night, trusting a stranger absolutely. Of Ilsa, bloodied in a mountain pass, buying her hours with her body.

*That,* she thought. *That's the door. Open.*

She tore the seventh knot with her bare will, and the world went white.

* * *

There was no temple. There was no floor. There was only the storm, and the storm was *her*—every storm she had ever swallowed, every thunderhead she'd clenched her teeth against, every summer squall she'd watched from windows and refused to claim. It came out of her in a column of light that punched through the vault ceiling, through the ruined temple above, through cloud cover that hadn't existed five minutes earlier, and Sebastian heard—felt—stone crack and buckle around her as twelve years of postponed weather announced itself to a kingdom that had forgotten what weather was.

She was laughing. She was screaming. She could not have told anyone which.

Power like this did not feel like power. It felt like *recognition*—like putting on a coat she'd outgrown at fourteen and finding it still fit, finding it had grown with her, finding that she had never once in twelve years been the smaller thing. Lightning walked her spine. Wind poured out of her lungs with every breath. Somewhere beyond the whiteout she heard Ilsa shouting her name and Serael's dry voice cutting through it, calm as arithmetic:

*"She's losing the boundary. The storm thinks it IS her. Anchor her or lose her!"*

Hands seized hers—Ilsa's, burned-red and bleeding at the knuckles where the discharge had bitten her, gripping anyway, gripping like the pass, gripping like twelve years of guilt made flesh. "Sebastian! Look at me. *Look at me!* You are a woman who sells charms in a market. You are a woman who mends kettles. You are NOT the storm. The storm is a thing you carry, not a thing you ARE—"

But it was already past argument. The boundary between Sebastian and the sky had thinned to gauze, and through it poured everything: the pressure systems of half a continent, the great slow breathing of the sea-storms off the southern coast, the cold electric smell of a front four hundred miles wide turning its attention toward her like a wolf catching a scent. She could feel the whole atmosphere leaning in. She could feel how easy it would be to say *yes*. To call it all down. To be the storm entire, and never again be the small tired woman who counted coins at the end of market day—

And that was when Serael's teachings finally found purchase.

*The storm does not know its own edges,* the old priest had said, in the first lesson, over frozen tea. *It has never needed to. A storm that touches everything touches nothing. But you—you have lived inside an EDGE for twelve years. You know precisely where you end. That is your mastery, child. Not the calling. The knowing.*

*When it comes, don't fight it and don't ride it. Name your edges. One by one. Like counting coins.*

Counting coins. Sebastian knew how to count coins. She had done it ten thousand times at a stall in the Cinders Market with frost-nipped fingers.

*One,* she thought, clawing toward the surface of the whiteout. *The scar on my palm, from the kettle, winter before last. Mine. Not the storm's.*

*Two. The ache in my knee when it rains. Mine.*

*Three. Ilsa's hands on mine, bleeding. Hers. NOT mine. The pain is hers and I will not take it.*

*Four. The shop. The crooked sign. The candle smoke. MINE.*

Each naming was a stitch. Each stitch pulled the gauze-boundary tighter. The storm raged and roared and *grieved*—she could feel it grieving, feel it understanding that the door it had waited twelve years for opened onto a room too small to hold it—and Sebastian grieved right back, tears streaming sideways off her face in the howling wind, because she understood it. God help her, she loved it. It was the truest part of her and she was going to spend every drop of it saving a man she'd met once across a parade square, and then it would be gone, and she would be the small tired woman forever.

*Let it grieve,* Serael had taught. *Grief is not loss of control. Grief is control, wearing its true face.*

So she grieved, deliberately, fully, with the storm pouring through her—and the grief grounded her the way a lightning rod grounds a tower. The column of light narrowed. The cracking stone stilled. The winds that had peeled tiles off the temple roof sank, slowly, grudgingly, to a low circular moan around the vault, circling her like a dog denied the bed but refusing to leave the room.

After a long time—minutes or hours, she couldn't say—Sebastian opened her eyes.

She knelt in a crater of fused glass, a perfect smooth bowl where the ritual floor had been, the carved ring of names melted into a mirror-bright pool around her. Above, through the shattered vault ceiling, she could see the night sky—and the clouds she'd torn open were still open, a ragged wound of darkness, and through it the stars should have been visible.

They weren't. The hole in the sky showed only black.

But the air itself had changed. Charged. *Waiting.* Every hair on her arms stood on end. Somewhere far to the north, she could feel it—the Hollow Crown, vast and starving, feeling her the way a spider feels a fly land at the far edge of the web.

Ilsa knelt in the glass beside her, hands scorched, eyes wet, alive.

"Welcome back," Ilsa said hoarsely. "You were gone eleven minutes. I aged forty years."

"I brought the weather." Sebastian's voice came out as ash and wonder. She flexed her fingers; lightning answered, arcing lazily between them like a cat weaving between ankles. "All of it. Everything I buried." She looked down at her glowing hands and felt the grief and the exhilaration sitting side by side in her chest, refusing to resolve. "It's even bigger than I remember, Ilsa. And tomorrow I give every spark of it away."

"Tonight," Ilsa said, pulling her upright, steadying her when her legs buckled, "you eat something and sleep. Even storms bed down eventually."

But as Serael hobbled forward with blankets and the old priest's blind face turned toward the north, toward the black hole in the sky, the priest stopped, and tilted her head, and listened to something none of them could hear.

"She has woken it fully now," Serael said quietly. "The Crown knows where the storm sleeps. Child—" the milk-pale eyes found Sebastian's, "—it will not wait for you to come to the palace. By morning, it will send everything it has left. It is coming here."

And far away in the dark, faint but unmistakable, carried on the charged air like a promise: hoofbeats. Many. Riding hard.
