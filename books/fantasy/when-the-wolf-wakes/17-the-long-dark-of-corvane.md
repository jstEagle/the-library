## 17. The Long Dark of Corvane

The cold came first.

Ilsa had expected fire when the wards fell—some great conflagration at the border, a pillar of smoke visible from the capital's walls. Instead, three days after Sebastian rode north with the frost plains swallowing her horse's hoofbeats, the cold arrived like a debt collector, patient and thorough, and it came for Corvane from the inside out.

It began with the hearths. A cobbler on Tanner's Row lit his fire at dawn and watched the flames lean sideways, as if listening to something no one else could hear, then gutter down to coals that gave light but no warmth. By noon, half the braziers in the market square were doing the same trick—burning beautifully, warming nothing. By dusk, the fountains had frozen mid-fall, sheets of ice suspended in the air like glass sculpture, and no one could explain it because no one in Corvane believed in magic anymore, and so there was no word for what was happening except *wrong*.

Ilsa stood at her window above the dyer's shop—the same window she'd been carried through with two broken ribs and a shoulder that screamed every time she breathed—and watched the lamplighters work their rounds along Candlemaker Street. The lamps caught. The flames burned thin and blue and useless, shedding light that seemed to fall a little shorter each night, as though the darkness itself was learning to eat it.

*The Crown is starving,* Sebastian had said before she left, standing in the doorway with frost in her hair and twelve years of buried storm beginning to wake behind her eyes. *The wards were its first meal. Now it's drinking everything—every ward, every blessing, every charm laid down by anyone who ever whispered over a candle. It'll pull the warmth out of the city if it has to.*

She hadn't said: *and I'm going alone.* But she'd packed as though she were, and she'd left before dawn, and Ilsa had woken to an empty bed and a note in careful tinker's script: *Don't follow. Don't argue. Come find me if I'm not back by the new moon.*

As if Ilsa Vane had ever once in her life done what she was told.

Her ribs ached. She pressed her palm flat against them, felt the binding wraps the hedge-witch Maren had wound around her torso, and breathed through the pain until it became merely a fact instead of a commandment. Downstairs, the dyer's family was huddled around their own failing fire, and the youngest boy looked up at her when she came into the room, and Ilsa realized with a small, strange jolt that he wasn't afraid of her anymore.

That was new. That was very new.

"They're saying the sky did it," the boy's mother said, wringing dye-water from a bolt of cloth with blue-stained hands. "The dark sky. Sucked all the warm right out of it."

"They're saying a witch did it," the father corrected grimly. "Council posted a decree this morning. Five hundred crowns for information leading to the arrest of the storm-witch Kavanagh." He spat into the fire, which flinched. "Half the quarter already knows where her stall was. Nobody's said a word."

Ilsa said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't burn the whole fragile thing down—that wouldn't tell these good people that the woman they were protecting had ridden straight toward the palace, or that the woman standing dripping in their spare room was a disgraced warden whose face would soon be on the decree boards beside Sebastian's.

But the father looked at her anyway, long and level, and Ilsa understood that he knew exactly who she was, and had known for days, and had said nothing about that either.

"More tea, Warden?" the mother asked.

"Not a warden," Ilsa said. "Not anymore."

"More tea, then," the mother said, as if that settled it.

* * *

By the fifth day of the dark, Corvane began to die politely.

The granaries still held grain but the mills couldn't grind it—no wind, and the waterwheels frozen in the canals. The bakeries fired what ovens they could and the bread queues stretched for streets, orderly and silent and terrifying in their patience, because Ilsa had seen hungry cities before and quiet was always the first stage of the sickness. The council met behind locked doors and issued decrees blaming witches, foreign agents, divine displeasure, and—in one memorable edict—the weather itself, which at least had the virtue of honesty.

And in the undercity, beneath the tanner yards and the old smuggler stairs, the people the decrees blamed gathered around fires that shouldn't have worked, and did.

Ilsa found them by accident, or told herself she had. Her ribs kept her from sleeping flat; on the worst nights she walked, slow circuits of the lower streets, and on the fourth night she followed a smell of woodsmoke down a stair she'd known as a young patrol officer and never had reason to visit since. At the bottom, behind a door marked FLOOD CHANNEL — NO ENTRY, she heard voices. Singing, actually. Badly.

Inside, forty people were crammed into a vaulted channel-house around a bonfire that burned green-gold at its edges, and the fire was wrong in every way Ilsa had been trained to notice, and it was also the warmest place in Corvane.

A witch named Odile—gray-braided, gap-toothed, with the callused hands of someone who had laundered for a living before laundering for a calling—was feeding the fire strips of written charm-paper, one by one, whispering over each. Beside her sat three of Sebastian's fellow tinkers, their workbenches dragged down into the dark, hammering out iron charms in assembly-line rhythm while a hedge-doctor named Bram sorted feverish children into rows. In the corner, a woman Ilsa recognized distantly as a fortune-teller expelled from the Merchant Ward years ago was reading the fire itself, frowning at what she saw in it.

Every person in the room was on somebody's list. Every one of them had spent years being the thing Corvane pointed at and said *there, that's what's wrong with us.*

"Well," Odile said, looking up at Ilsa's warden's coat—she still wore it; she owned nothing else—"the law finally found our parlor. You'll want names, I expect. We've made a list ourselves, if you'd care to trade."

"I've come about the fire," Ilsa said. "It's drawing heat from somewhere. Nothing burns like that without fuel, and there's no fuel in this room worth a damn."

Odile grinned with her gap teeth. "There's fuel enough. Just not the kind your academy teaches." She tossed another charm-paper strip into the flames and the fire leapt, and Ilsa felt the heat of it sink into her broken ribs like a hand laid gently over the wound. "Wards are falling all over the city, Warden Vane. Old ones, little ones, ones laid by grandmothers fifty years dead to keep damp out of root cellars. The Crown eats them. And when it eats a ward, the power has to go *somewhere*, doesn't it? Power always does." She spread her hands at the fire, at the room, at the forty unloved souls warming themselves in the belly of a city that wanted them gone. "We're just catching the drippings off the table."

Ilsa stared at the fire, and the fire stared back, and twelve years of training rearranged itself around a single idea.

"You're siphoning the Crown's leavings."

"We're *scavenging*," Odile corrected comfortably. "Scavenging's honest work. Ask any crow."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, assembled itself the way plans do in desperate rooms—out of arguments, out of scraps, out of the particular genius of people who had never once been trusted with anything official.

Corvane could not be saved. Ilsa understood that within a day of working the problem. The Crown's hunger was too vast, the wards too scattered, the council too busy hunting witches to notice the city freezing around them. The granary district would fail within the month. The aqueducts would freeze solid. Whatever happened at the star-temple—if Sebastian succeeded, if she survived, if either of those things could be made true—would come too late for the poorest quarters unless someone built something ugly and temporary and strong enough to hold.

So they built it.

Maren the hedge-witch knew the old ward-lattice under the city—the forgotten foundation-wards laid by the star-priests centuries ago, before Corvane decided magic was filth, the ones even the Crown's hunger had missed because they'd been buried so deep and so long everyone forgot they existed. Odile could teach thirty amateurs to catch spillover power in a night. The tinkers could forge focus-charms by the hundred, crude and ugly and functional, the magical equivalent of a bucket brigade. And Ilsa—

Ilsa knew how armies worked. How supply lines broke, how panic moved, how to hold a street, how to feed a rumor, how to make forty frightened people believe they were five hundred.

"You want us to defend the city," said Bram the hedge-doctor on the sixth night, in the tone of a man checking whether he'd misheard a diagnosis. "Us. The witches and the tinkers and the fortune-tellers. Defend Corvane. From the Crown. The city that's spent sixty years drowning us in the river whenever the mood took it."

"The city isn't drowning you tonight," Ilsa said. "Tonight the city is freezing, and you're the only ones holding fire."

"It'll drown us again after," Odile said, but she was already pulling out fresh charm-paper. "Mind, I'm not saying no. I'm saying history, is all."

"After," Ilsa said, "there will be a reckoning. I give you my word on that. Not forgiveness handed down from the council—reckoning. Names read aloud. Laws rewritten. I'll stand in front of whatever tribunal they form and testify to every arrest I ever made on false grounds, starting with my own." She let that sit. She thought of Sebastian's face in the doorway twelve years ago, and again three weeks ago, and added quietly: "I owe this city a debt it doesn't know about. Let me start paying it here."

Nobody cheered. These were not cheering people; they had survived precisely by never believing speeches. But Odile began writing, and the tinkers began hammering, and by midnight the channel-house sounded like a smithy having a conversation with a choir, and Ilsa stood in the middle of it with broken ribs and no rank and no future, and felt—for the first time since the night she'd put a girl on a road east with nothing but a sealed letter and her own shame—something adjacent to hope.

* * *

They called it the Lattice, later, in the songs that got sung badly in taverns after everything ended. At the time it was just work, endless and improvised.

The foundation-wards ran in a ring beneath the old city walls, star-priest craftsmanship, dormant for two centuries. Maren mapped them from a crumbling codex she'd bought for bread-money during a famine year and never had cause to open. The tinkers built junction-charms—ugly iron discs stamped with geometric patterns Sebastian would have laughed at—to tap the lattice and route its trickle of power outward: to communal hearth-houses, to the infirmaries, to the bread ovens in Tanner's Row that began, on the ninth day, to actually bake.

The Crown noticed on the eleventh day.

It came for them as a rumor first, because that was how the Crown fought now—it had no soldiers in Corvane, only mouths. Word spread through the loyal districts that the warmth in the poor quarters was bought with children's blood, that witches were draining the living to feed the dead, that the tinker quarter had become a nest of the storm-witch's confederates. Decrees went up naming Odile, naming Maren, naming Bram. And on the twelfth morning a mob came down the smuggler stairs with torches and hammers, forty strong, to burn out the channel-house.

Ilsa met them at the door alone, in her warden's coat, with her ribs strapped and her sword drawn and planted point-down before her like a grave marker.

"Stand aside, traitor," said the mob's front rank, and she recognized faces—fishmongers, a chandler, two off-duty watchmen she'd drunk with a hundred times. Good people. Frozen, frightened, lied-to people.

"No," she said.

"There's forty of us."

"I know. I counted the torches. Forty torches, and every one of them burning cold." She raised her voice to carry down the tunnel. "You've noticed, haven't you? Your fires don't warm. Your candles don't hold. You've been told it's witchcraft, and you're right—but it isn't ours. Something is eating this city's warmth from the top down, from the crown down, and the people in this room are the only ones in Corvane who've figured out how to take some of it back." She stepped aside from the door, deliberately, leaving herself wide open, and gestured inward. "Come in. Warm yourselves. Then decide whether we're the enemy. If you still want to swing your hammers after, I won't stop you—I'm one woman with cracked ribs, and you're forty."

Silence. Wind moved through the tunnel mouth, carrying snow.

Then the chandler—a broad woman named Petra whom Ilsa had once arrested for selling tallow charms during a plague scare—pushed through the crowd, marched past Ilsa close enough to elbow her bruised ribs on purpose, and held her hands out to the green-gold fire.

"Huh," Petra said. "That's warm."

She did not leave. Neither, one by one, did most of the others. Two watchmen stayed at the door and started, without being asked, organizing who could come down and who couldn't, turning away the drunk and the agent provocateurs with a firmness Ilsa didn't have to fake. By evening, the channel-house had a queue.

The unloved had found each other. Now they were finding everyone else.

* * *

On the fifteenth night of the dark, Ilsa climbed to the roof of the dyer's shop because her ribs had finally loosened enough to allow it, and because Odile had told her to look north.

"Fire on the horizon," the witch had said, reading her green-gold flames with an expression Ilsa had learned meant awe rather than worry. "Way north. Past the frost plains. Something's waking, Warden. Something big. The whole lattice's been singing about it for an hour."

So Ilsa stood on the rooftiles in the killing cold, breath steaming, shoulder screaming, and looked north across the black rooftops of the city she had served and betrayed and was now, at last, honestly trying to save.

At first there was nothing. Then the horizon flickered—not lightning, not quite, though it moved like lightning, slow and enormous, a pulsing glow behind the distant mountains where no temple should have been burning. The air pressure changed. Every charm in the city rang at once, faintly, a thousand tiny bells, and below her the channel-house fire roared up green and gold and white, and Odile's laughter drifted up through the floorboards, wild and delighted and afraid.

The glow pulsed again. Closer now. Brighter. And threaded through it, so faint Ilsa might have imagined it, she heard something that stopped her heart: a scream. Vast, layered, ancient—the sound of many voices crying out together, kings' voices, a chorus of the devoured, rising off the plains like a storm-front made of grief.

*Sebastian.*

Ilsa gripped the rooftop edge until the tiles bit her palms, and made herself breathe, and made herself count—forty torches, thirty witches, a hundred iron charms, one city, one promise: *come find me if I'm not back by the new moon.*

Three nights until the new moon.

Below her, Corvane lay dark and freezing and stubbornly alive, ten thousand hearth-houses glowing with scavenged light, the unloved keeping the flame for a city that had never loved them back. Above her, the sky remained empty of stars, but in the far north, above the pulse of that distant fire, Ilsa saw it—one point of light, impossibly small, kindle and hold.

Then a second.

Then a third.

The stars were coming back. Which meant the Crown was dying. Which meant the rite was happening *now*, and Ilsa Vane had promised a woman on a frost plain that she would not follow, and had broken every other promise she'd ever made, and was not going to break this one by keeping it.

She went down the ladder fast, ignoring her ribs, shouting for Odile, for Maren, for horses and coats and the fastest road north.

"Get me to the temple," she said, when they crowded around her. "All of you keep the Lattice burning. If I'm not back—" she paused, and found she was smiling, and didn't understand why until she said it, "—if I'm not back, it means I'm where I'm supposed to be."

The stars were relighting over Corvane, one by one, like windows opening in a long dark house—and Ilsa Vane rode north through the gates alone, toward the last light of the storm.
