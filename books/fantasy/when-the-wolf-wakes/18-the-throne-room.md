## 18. The Throne Room

The parley flag was white wool, dyed gray by the road, and it trembled in my hands not from wind but from the storm beneath my skin trying to answer the palace's call.

They let me through the gates.

That was the first wrongness. Corvane's capital had been locked tight for weeks—ward-stakes driven into every threshold, inquisitors at every corner, a city holding its breath against the dark. And yet when I walked up the processional avenue under my scrap of white cloth, the guards parted like water around a stone. They didn't search me. They didn't speak. Their eyes were open and their faces were slack as bread dough, and behind them the great bronze doors of the palace swung inward before I could knock.

*It wants you here,* said the small cold voice of my own good sense.

I know, I told it. That's why I came.

Because that was the truth of it—the thing wearing King Aldric had spent chapters of dreams courting me, tempting me, offering me back my name and my magic and Ilsa's life if only I'd walk away. You don't make that many offers to someone you can beat. It was afraid of me. A tinker with a false name and twelve years of caged lightning, and something ancient enough to eat a king lay awake at night worrying about what I might do.

So I gave it what it feared: an invitation accepted on my terms.

The throne room doors were oak banded with silver, tall enough to admit a siege engine, and they opened onto a hall lit by a thousand candles that did not flicker. That was the second wrongness. Every flame stood perfectly still, like painted light, like the memory of fire rather than fire itself. The banners of Aldric's line hung motionless along the walls—nine kings, nine generations of stitched gold—and the air smelled of cold stone and, faintly, of rain that never fell.

He sat on the throne at the far end, and he looked *better*. That was the third wrongness and the worst one. In the procession, weeks ago, Aldric had been a husk in royal clothes, his shadow lagging half a heartbeat behind his body. Now he sat straight. His color was good. His hands rested easy on the arms of the throne, and when he smiled at me across two hundred feet of marble, it was a warm smile, a fatherly one, a smile I might have believed in another life.

"Sebastian Kavanagh," he said, and his voice filled the hall without echoing. "Storm-caller. Tinker. The girl who killed her village."

"I saved my village," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word because I hadn't said it aloud in twelve years and it tasted like a lie even though it wasn't. "I buried it."

"You did both." He rose, unhurried, and descended the three steps of the dais. Up close, the hollowness was still there—it lived behind his eyes like a tenant who kept the windows clean. "Come. Walk with me. We have much to discuss before your little rite, and so little time in which the stars will let us have it."

"I'm not walking anywhere with you."

"You are already walking." He gestured, and I realized my feet had carried me ten paces into the hall without my permission. The candles' stillness pressed against my skin like deep water. "Forgive me. Old habits. Kings summon; it is difficult to remember courtesy." He stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could see the fine grain of the Hollow Crown where it sat upon his brow—except it was no crown at all, not really. It was a circlet of black iron grown into the flesh above his ears, its filigree moving very slowly, like frost forming in reverse. "You've come to kill me. Let us be honest, since honesty is so rare in this hall."

"I've come to free him," I said. "You're just in the way."

Something moved behind Aldric's eyes—not pain exactly. Interest. "Yes," the Crown said through his mouth, softer now, the king's warm voice thinning into something older and hungrier underneath. "There she is. There is the storm." It tilted his head, studying me the way I'd once studied a broken clockwork, looking for the flaw that explained everything. "Do you know why I fear you? Not the rite. Rites fail. Callers falter. I have eaten better mages than you, in kingdoms older than this one." The filigree pulsed. "I fear you because of what you did when you were nine years old. Untrained. Unbound. One thunderhead over one valley—and you tore a hole in me that took sixty years to knit."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "But you will. It is the only inheritance I can give you, child, and I give it freely: *memory*."

It reached out and touched my forehead with one finger.

* * *

The world went white, then green.

Green—impossibly green, the green of high summer, of the valley where I was born before the maps started calling it the Blightlands. I stood in my mother's barley field, nine years old and barefoot, and the sky above was the wrong color, bruise-purple, swollen, a thunderhead like a fist closing over the sun. I knew this day. I had spent twelve years refusing to know this day.

And the Crown stood beside me in my own memory, wearing Aldric's body like a borrowed coat, and it said: *Watch.*

I watched.

I watched myself—small, screaming, lightning crawling out of my skin in ropes—as the storm broke. I watched the hail come down the size of goose eggs and then the size of fists, flattening the barley that was to be the village's winter bread. I watched the lightning find old Tomas Hedley in his cart on the ridge road, and I watched him fall, and I watched his horse run home riderless.

"That one you know," the Crown murmured. "Tomas. First of your dead. But he was quick, child—a mercy, as deaths go. Keep watching."

The harvest failed. Of course it failed; I'd known that too, abstractly, the way you know a scar is there. What I had never done was follow the failure forward. The Crown did it for me. Winter came early and hard to a village with no stores, and I watched Mrs. Oren take in three neighbor children because their parents had gone to Corvane to beg credit and never come back. I watched her stretch one pot of soup across eight bowls for six weeks, thinner each week, her own portion shrinking to nothing while she smiled and lied and said she'd eaten at her sister's. I watched her stop smiling in the fourth week. I watched the fever move through the underfed houses in the fifth.

*I watched forty-one people die of a winter I made,* and the Crown stood beside me narrating none of it, needing to say nothing, because arithmetic needed no commentary.

"This is the guilt you carry already," it said at last, when the snow had covered the graves in rows I recognized from my nightmares. "This is the weight you built your whole gray little life upon—the charms, the false name, the vow. You think this is why you must burn me out. Penance." Aldric's face turned to me, kind and terrible. "But you have miscounted, storm-child. Your storm did not stop at the valley's edge."

The scene tore like wet paper.

Now I stood on a merchant road, and I knew it—the north road out of the valley, the one I'd been carried down, feverish, in Ilsa's arms. The Crown walked me along it. At the crossroads inn where we'd sheltered that first night, it showed me the carter whose horses had bolted at the distant thunder and overturned on the ice, breaking his back, ending a trade route three villages depended on, and the lean year that followed, and the family in the second village who sold their daughter's dowry to survive, and the daughter who entered a cloister she hated and died in a plague ship's hold twenty years later because she'd taken a charity berth—

"Stop," I said.

"—and here," the Crown continued, serene as scripture, "the miller of Dunmarrow, whose river flooded that spring because your storm had stripped the high slopes of root-hold—"

"*Stop.*"

"—and his son, who joined the border wardens out of poverty, and died at the fell-gate the very season the wards began to starve. Because of me. Because I was hungry. Which makes him, if we trace the chain honestly—" the Crown smiled with the king's mouth, "—yours."

I tore free of the memory the way you tear free of a nightmare, by deciding to bleed instead. I came back to myself kneeling on the marble of the throne room, palms flat, blood in my mouth where I'd bitten my tongue, and the thousand candles still burning their painted stillness, and Aldric standing over me with his hand extended and his eyes full of patient, bottomless sorrow.

"There," he said gently. "Now you understand why I offer you the bargain again. Walk away, Sebastian. Take Ilsa Vane and go somewhere the names don't reach you. No one need ever count those chains again. I ask so little—one storm-caller's silence, and I will leave your city unharmed. I am not cruel. Cruelty is inefficient." He crouched, and for one vertiginous moment the king's face slipped and I saw the thing underneath, vast and tired and old as the first throne, and it looked at me almost tenderly. "But if you pour yourself into me—if you try—I will show you the rest. There are more chains, child. So many more. Every life your storm touched, and every life those lives touched, outward forever, until the whole world is your grave-marker. Can you stand inside that arithmetic and still raise your hand against me?"

The hall was silent. Even my heartbeat sounded loud.

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave waits for me, whether it comes at the rite or fifty ordinary years from now: I couldn't answer. The arithmetic was true. I had built twelve years of penance on the first row of it and called that humility, and the Crown had simply shown me the rest of the ledger, and every number in it was real, and every real number was mine. My knees wouldn't lock. My hands shook against the cold marble. Somewhere behind my ribs the caged storm stirred and I flinched away from it—for the first time since Ilsa put the sealing-oaths on me, I flinched from my own magic, because it was the murder weapon and the murderer and the orphaned child all at once, and I did not know which of them deserved my loyalty.

"That's it," the Crown whispered, and Aldric's voice wrapped around me like a shroud. "Feel it settle. Guilt is such honest material. It wants only to be put down, and I am offering to take it from you—all you must surrender is the hand that holds the hammer. Kneel here a moment longer. Rest. You were always going to kneel. Every storm-caller kneels eventually; I have watched a hundred of them learn what their lightning costs, and not one of them ever—"

The great doors banged open.

Not opened. *Banged*, slammed inward hard enough to crack one hinge, and the sound ripped through the candle-stillness like a blade through canvas, and for the first time since I'd entered the palace, a thousand flames flickered.

She stood in the doorway with blood down one side of her face and her warden's coat hanging off her shoulder in strips, sword drawn, breathing like a bellows, silhouetted against a corridor full of smoke and shouting. Twelve years of estrangement, one botched exorcism of a conversation, a hundred unsaid things between us—and Ilsa Vane looked across that hall at me kneeling in front of the thing that ate kings, and her face did something complicated and fierce, and she said:

"Get up, Seb. We're leaving."

The Crown turned toward the door, and every candle in the throne room guttered at once, and behind Aldric's hollow eyes something very old began, slowly, to laugh.

* * *

"You brought her *here*?" The Crown's voice dropped the king entirely now; it came out of Aldric's throat in layers, a chorus wearing one man. "I held the gates for you. I opened every door. I gave you the one gift no one else could give—the truth of your own cost—and you let *her* in?"

"She let herself in," I managed. My voice came out scraped raw. "She's good at doors."

Ilsa crossed the marble at a dead run and got an arm under mine, hauling me upright, and her grip was iron and shaking all at once. Blood dripped off her jaw onto my sleeve. Up close I could see what the doorway had hidden: a gash over her ear, a burn along her collarbone, the specific gray-faced steadiness of someone who has decided she is not allowed to fall down yet.

"The pass," I said stupidly. "Hale. The hunters—you were supposed to be—"

"Hale's hunters are ash in a ravine, and Hale is ash with them." She said it flat, fast, a report filed under fire. "I'll tell you the rest later. Right now the plan is the part where we don't die in a throne room." Her eyes flicked to Aldric—to the Crown wearing him—and I watched her take in the black filigree, the still candles, the choir-voice, and I watched her decide to be unafraid of it, the same way she'd decided everything else her whole stubborn life. "Hello, Your Majesty. You're under arrest for treason against the realm of yourself."

"Ah," said the Crown, and the laughter behind its voice went quiet, which frightened me more than any scream. "The anchor. Yes. We wondered which of you would break first." Aldric's head cocked toward me, and the sorrow in his borrowed face was gone, replaced by something surgical. "She came to save you, storm-child. How touching. Shall I show you what she sacrificed to get here? Shall I show you the pass, and the men she burned, and the oath she broke—or would you prefer I finish what I started? The ledger has a final page, you know. Would you like to see what your storm does to *her*, thirty years from now, when the anchoring rite pulls her heart apart to hold yours together?"

"Don't listen." Ilsa's hand closed hard on my wrist, grounding me, her pulse jumping under my fingers. "It lies with true numbers, Seb. That's all it knows how to do. Every word it says is real and every word is poison."

"It showed me the winter," I whispered. "Forty-one people, Ilsa. I counted them. They have faces now."

"They always had faces. That's why you're the one who can end this." She stepped in front of me, between me and the throne, bleeding and rankless and utterly certain. "Guilt's only a weapon if you swing it at yourself. So don't. Swing it at *him*."

The Crown raised Aldric's hand, and the thousand candles went out together, and in the dark the black iron filigree began to glow like veins of cooling lava, and the last thing I heard before the storm in my chest finally answered the storm in the hall was the Crown saying, almost kindly:

"Then let us see, little wolf. Let us see what you wake."
