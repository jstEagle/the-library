## 19. When the Wolf Wakes

The Crown did not show me mercy because it had none to give. It showed me the dead.

They came in the throne room's dark like frost creeping across a window—faces lit from within by starlight that no longer existed anywhere else in the world. A farmer from Millbrook, drowned in his own well when my storm cracked the earth beneath it. The miller's daughter who had been kind to me once, crushed under a collapsing granary roof. Faces I had not thought of in twenty years, rendered with a precision no memory could match, because these were not memories. They were records. The Crown kept accounts. It had been keeping them since before I was born, and now it laid them out before me like a ledger it expected me to settle.

*You are the same as us,* it said, in a voice like wind through a burned forest. *You killed with your first storm. We only learned what kings learned: that power must feed.*

"I was a child," I said. My voice sounded small in the vastness of the hall. "I didn't choose."

*Neither did we. Neither did Aldric. Choice is the story you all tell afterward.*

I knelt on the cold flagstones—I hadn't meant to kneel—and the faces went on and on. Not just mine. Every storm-caller the Crown had ever broken, every warden it had turned, every king it had worn like a glove until the hand inside rotted away. It had been eating this kingdom for three hundred years, patiently, one drought at a time, one war at a time, one dark night of stars at a time. And I understood, kneeling there with grief pressing down on me like deep water, that this was its true weapon. Not the wards it ate or the magic it drank. This. Guilt, weaponized. Sorrow sharpened into a blade and held at your own throat.

It wanted me to believe the rite would be just another killing. That pouring my storm into it would be me, once again, destroying something I loved.

And gods forgive me, for a moment—just a moment—I believed it. I felt my hands go still against the floor. I felt the sealing-oaths I'd unbound the night before humming in my blood like caged thunder, and I wanted, desperately, to bind them back. To walk out of this palace. To go back to being Seb the Tinker, small and safe and harmless, in a city where harmlessness was the only virtue anyone respected.

That was when the doors blew open.

Not opened. *Blown*, off their hinges, splintering across the marble, and through the smoke and dust came a figure limping, dragging, half-carried by two of Ilsa's outcasts—a woman in scorched warden's leathers with her left arm hanging wrong and blood sheeting down the side of her face so thickly I could not tell at first if it was hers or Hale's.

It was hers. Some of it was certainly hers.

Ilsa Vane crossed the throne room the way she had crossed every room I'd ever seen her enter: like the room owed her passage and knew better than to argue. But she was slower now. Each step cost her something visible, and the two witches holding her up were barely holding her up at all.

"You," she said, and her voice came out ruined, scraped raw by smoke or screaming or both. "Sebastian Kavanagh. You absolute—" She stopped, swayed, caught herself. "I told you to wait at the temple."

"The temple was burning," I said. "Hale found it."

"I know. I was at the pass." She looked down at herself, at the blood, as though noticing it for the first time, with mild academic interest. "I held it."

"For how long?"

"Long enough." Her eyes found the Crown—the thing on the throne, wearing the king—and whatever she saw there made her jaw tighten. "Let her go," she said to it. "She's not yours."

*I made her,* the Crown said, and the dead faces flickered around us like candles guttering. *I have been making her since the night of her first storm. Who do you think sent the flood to Millbrook? Who do you think taught her kingdom that magic is a sickness? I built her guilt brick by brick, warden. It is the finest cage ever constructed, and she built most of it herself.*

"That's the trick, then." Ilsa laughed—a terrible sound, wet and short. "Three hundred years of eating kings and your best weapon is telling sad girls they're bad. You're not a god. You're not even a clever monster. You're just very old and very hungry and very afraid of her."

*Afraid.* The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Around the throne, the shadow that moved half a second behind King Aldric's body stuttered—caught—recovered.

"Yes," Ilsa said, and now she was looking at me, only at me. "Afraid. Why do you think it showed you the dead instead of fighting you? Why do you think it offered you bargains in your sleep instead of sending soldiers? Because a storm-caller at full strength, walking willingly into an unbinding rite, is the one thing in this world it cannot survive. And it knows it."

The faces began to scream. All of them at once, the whole ledger of the Crown's sins shrieking through the vaulted dark, and I felt it press down on me—not physically, nothing so honest—pressing on the soft places, the guilty places, saying *you will add to this, you will always add to this, put the storm away, put it away, put it away forever—*

Ilsa let go of her witches.

She shouldn't have been able to stand. I saw the moment she decided to stand anyway, the way her whole body argued with itself and lost, and then she was crossing the last ten feet of marble toward me, leaving a red smear behind her like a signature on a confession, and she took my face in her hands—both hands, even the broken-armed one, and I heard her make a sound she would deny to her grave—and she said:

"Listen to me. Twelve years ago I exiled you to save your life, and I let you believe I did it out of duty because the truth would have been crueler. The truth is I couldn't watch you die and I couldn't watch you stay and I chose the coward's mercy and I have regretted it every single day since. So hear this now, while I'm still upright and still bleeding and still too stubborn to fall down: you do not get to carry those faces alone, and you do not get to carry this alone, and you especially do not get to die alone in a throne room to spare my feelings. I am done letting you go, Sebastian. Done. Whatever comes next, I am in it."

"Ilsa—"

"Shut up. I'm mid-oath." Her thumb brushed my cheekbone, smearing blood I suspected was mine after all. "You need an anchor for the rite. The priest said it. Someone has to hold the line while you pour yourself out, someone outside the storm, someone whose whole self is set against yours like a brace against a breaking dam. You were going to do it without one. You were going to come here alone and burn out and I was going to find your body and spend the rest of my life apologizing to a corpse. No. Not anymore."

"There's no time to teach you the anchor-work—"

"I've been reading your priest's texts for three days." Her mouth twitched, that old dry almost-smile that had undone me at nineteen and was undoing me now, at thirty-one, bleeding out in a haunted throne room. "What did you think I was doing while you sulked in the vault? The line runs through consent, Sebastian. Will given freely, held absolutely. Do you know how long I've practiced giving things freely?" Her voice cracked, just once, just slightly. "Twelve years. Consider it rehearsal."

The Crown screamed louder. The dead faces whirled faster, and the temperature in the room plunged, and somewhere above us the ceiling groaned as frost spread across stone that had never known winter. *If she anchors you,* it hissed, *she dies with you. Is that your love? To drag her into the grave?*

"It's my love to stop deciding for her," I said.

And I meant it. That was the strange, terrible clarity of the moment—that everything the Crown had shown me, all the guilt, all the grief, had been built on one lie underneath: that protection was the highest form of love. My whole life I had believed it. Ilsa had believed it. We had spent twelve years protecting each other into misery, exile into estrangement, silence into ruin, and here at the end, with the oldest evil in the kingdom howling at us, the way through was not more protection.

It was honesty. Just that. Just the thing we'd both been starving on.

"Hold out your hand," I said.

Ilsa Vane, former Warden of the Eastern Marches, stripped of rank and marked for arrest, missing most of the blood a body requires, extended her right hand with the crisp formality of a knight taking an oath. "Anchor-line, per the Rite of Unbinding, as recorded by the last order of the star-priests. State your intent, storm-caller."

"My intent," I said, and my voice shook and steadied, "is to pour everything I am into the Hollow Crown and burn it out of King Aldric, and I intend to survive it, and I intend to come home, and I want you there when I do."

"Noted," she whispered, and gripped my hand.

The line snapped taut between us.

I don't know how else to describe it. One instant her grip was just a grip—cold fingers, warm palm, the callus on her sword-hand I'd memorized a lifetime ago—and the next there was a *line*, a silver thread running from her heart to mine, singing, and I could feel her whole self flowing down it: twelve years of guilt, twelve years of watching the horizon for a storm-caller who never came home, twelve years of loyalty to a crown that had been rotting from the inside, and underneath all of it, patient as bedrock, the love she had never once spoken aloud because speaking it would have made everything true.

"Oh," I said. Foolishly. Wonderingly. "Oh, it's *you*. It was always—"

"Don't get sentimental during a rite," she said, crying openly. "It's undignified."

Then I stopped holding back.

* * *

Twelve years of caged storm does not rise gently.

It rose like the wolf waking—like the name of this whole cursed story finally making sense—uncoiling from the marrow of me where I had buried it, sealed it, sworn it away. It came up my spine like lightning finding ground. It filled my lungs with ozone. For one blinding instant I was nineteen again, standing in a shattered harvest field with rain rising upward around me and Ilsa shouting my name through the thunder, except now I wasn't afraid of it. Now I had an anchor.

"Now," Ilsa breathed against my ear, her hand crushing mine, the silver line blazing between us. "Whatever it shows you, whoever it makes you see—it lies about the ending. Only I know the ending. Hold to me."

I stepped forward, and the storm stepped with me.

King Aldric sat on his throne like a man carved from driftwood, hollow-eyed and gray-lipped, and the shadow behind him writhed as I approached, coiling, striking shapes—serpent, wolf, crown, wolf again—as though it could not decide what to be now that the end had arrived. Up close I could see him clearly for the first time: the fine bones under the wasted flesh, the tremor in his hands, and—flickering, drowning, desperate—one human eye surfacing through the black like a man breaking water.

*Fight,* I begged him silently, as I raised my hands. *Please fight. Just a little longer.*

The Crown struck first. It tore the faces out of the air and hurled them at me—all of them, three hundred years of the eaten, every king and priest and caller it had consumed, and they crashed over me like a wave of screaming, and each one wore a face I knew, my mother, Ilsa, the miller's daughter, myself—

But the line held.

Every time the wave broke over me, I felt Ilsa at the far end of it, iron and certain, *here, here, here*, and I surfaced, and I breathed, and I walked on. Frost exploded from the flagstones under my feet. The banners along the walls ignited with static and shredded into drifting embers. Somewhere far away, alarm bells began to ring across Corvane, because everyone in the city could feel what was happening in the palace—could feel the pressure dropping, the air turning to glass, the sky above the capital beginning—for the first time in months—to *move*.

"This kingdom fed you for three hundred years," I said, and my voice came out doubled, tripled, a chorus of thunder speaking through one throat. "You drank its rivers and its harvests and its children's magic. You wore its kings down to gloves. But you never learned the thing it could have taught you, if you'd bothered to be anything but hunger."

The shadow reared to its full height, taller than the throne room, crowned in darkness, and for one instant I saw it truly—not a parasite, not yet, not originally, but something old and guardian-shaped, starved past recognition, a shepherd that had eaten its own flock. Grief pierced me, sharp and clean. Even now. *Especially* now.

"You never learned," I said, "that a storm is not destruction. It's the sky remembering how to move."

I poured.

There is no adequate word for it. I gave it everything—the first storm and every storm after it, the terror and the exile and the twelve years of small charms sold in small markets, the fever I broke in a child's brow, the ward-sigils I mended in secret, every drop of suppressed, denied, buried power rising through me and down my arms and into the dying king on the throne—and the Crown *screamed*.

It screamed with every voice it had ever eaten. Kings I'd never known poured out of it in death-cries three centuries deep—Aldric's grandfather, and his before him, and back and back to the first king who had taken a guardian spirit and leashed it to his greed, and all of them howled together as my storm flooded through the hollow of their stolen vessel and found the Crown clinging there, black and vast and suddenly, wonderfully, *burnable*.

Ilsa held on. The line sang white-hot between us; I could smell blood, hers, fresh, and I knew what the anchor-work was costing her and I hated myself and loved her and held on anyway, because she would never have forgiven me for letting go, and neither would I.

The Crown thrashed. It threw Aldric's body against the throne, tried to flee deeper, tried the bargains one last time—*I can give you back your magic, I can make you a queen, I can unmake the exile, I can—*

"I know what you can give," I said, gentle now, so gentle, the way you speak to a rabid animal at the end. "But you're not a crown. You never were. You're just cold and starving and so afraid of ending that you became the ending. So end."

I burned it out.

The light was total. I remember no shapes after that, only heat and the taste of rain and Ilsa's voice cutting through everything, steady as a lighthouse beam, *here, here, here*, and the Crown's scream climbing past sound into something that cracked the throne room's high windows and rang across the darkened city like the first bell of a new age—

And then, silence.

Real silence. The pressure gone. The frost gone. The dead faces gone—not destroyed, I think, but released, finally allowed to rest, which was more than the Crown had ever granted anyone including itself.

King Aldric slumped in his throne, breathing—shallowly, raggedly, but breathing, and his remaining eye, his human eye, was open and wet and *his*.

Above the shattered windows, through the smoke, through the ruin, I saw it happen: one star, high over the eastern wall, relit.

Then another. Then another.

"Sebastian." Ilsa's voice, very far away, very close. Her arms around me. We were both on the floor; I had no memory of falling. The silver line between us was fading, its work done, dissolving back into the ordinary space between two people—but I could still feel her at the end of it, faintly, like warmth from a fire after the flames are out.

"Ilsa," I managed. "Did we—"

"Stars are coming back." She was laughing and sobbing and holding my face and checking my pulse with grim professional thoroughness all at once. "You did it. You impossible, reckless—" She stopped. Her expression changed. I watched her feel it, through the thinning thread between us, before I felt it myself.

The storm was quiet.

Not caged. Not sleeping. *Quiet* the way a hearth is quiet after the wood has burnt to ash. I reached inward, the way I'd reached inward every day for twelve years, expecting the thunder, the weight, the wolf—and found only stillness. Ordinary, human, finite stillness. A body. A heartbeat. Nothing else.

"Astrid," Ilsa whispered—my true name, the one almost no one living knew. "Your magic."

I lay in her arms on the broken marble of a throne room, under a sky slowly remembering its stars, and I searched myself one more time, thoroughly, honestly, the way you search a house you already know is empty.

"Gone," I said. And then, because it was true, and because I had promised myself I would never lie to her again: "I think... I think I gave it all. Everything. I'm just—me now. Just a woman."

Ilsa stared down at me, blood-streaked and shaking, and I braced for the grief in her face, the pity, the terrible tenderness reserved for the wounded.

Instead she kissed me.

"Good," she said against my mouth. "Now you'll finally have time to stay."

Above us, the third star lit, and the fourth, and somewhere below, in the corridors of the palace, boots were running—wardens, healers, someone shouting for the princess, someone shouting that the king was alive, the king was *awake*—and the door burst open onto the dawn of a kingdom remade, and the first person through it was a girl of perhaps sixteen with the king's fine bones and his gray eyes wide with terror and hope.

"Father?" she called into the smoking hall. "Is he—"

Aldric stirred on his throne. And I remembered a promise made in a hidden message, a debt I had sworn to honor, and I got one knee under myself, and began to rise.
