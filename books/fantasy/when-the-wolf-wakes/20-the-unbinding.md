## 20. The Unbinding

The throne room had no roof.

Sebastian noticed it first—the absence of stone where a ceiling should have been, the jagged ring of broken rafters opening onto a sky that was not black but *wrong*, a colorless nothing where stars should have burned. Snow drifted down through that emptiness in long, slow spirals, and each flake seemed to hesitate midair, as if even winter had forgotten which way it was falling.

The hall stretched before her, longer than any hall had a right to be. The Crown had been drinking Corvane's magic for a year, and it had drunk the geometry too—corridors of shadow branching off at angles her eyes refused to follow, banners hanging motionless though a cold wind moved through everything else. Her boots echoed wrong on the marble: one step, then two, then silence, as if the room were swallowing sound.

Aldric sat upon the throne.

He looked smaller than he had in the procession. Smaller, and older, and *emptier*—a man-shaped vessel with something vast coiled inside it. His crown was not gold. It was a circlet of darkness so dense it bent the light around it, and it pulsed faintly, like a heart, like a throat swallowing.

*You came.*

The voice did not come from Aldric's mouth. It came from everywhere—from the walls, from the snow, from the hollow behind her own ribs where her storm had crouched for twelve years.

"I came," Sebastian said aloud, because speaking made it real, made it hers. "Let him go."

*Let him go.* The Crown tasted the words, turned them over. *I have worn forty-one kings, little storm-caller. I remember every one of their names. Do you know what I remember about Aldric Veyra? He sang to his daughter. Badly. He could not carry a tune to save his life, and he sang anyway, every night, until she told him she was too old for lullabies.* A pause, long and cold. *I kept his songs. They are mine now.*

"Then you know he's still in there." Sebastian's hands were shaking. She let them shake. Fear was fuel; she'd learned that much in the temple vault, with the last star-priest's voice steady in her ears. "And you know what I am."

*I know what you are.* Something moved through the hall—a pressure, a turning, like the attention of an eye the size of the sky. *I felt you the night the stars went out. I ate the wards, and the wards screamed, and beneath the screaming there was one small sealed thing in Corvane that burned like a wound. Twelve years of oaths wrapped around a storm, and the storm never once went out.*

The snow stopped falling. Every flake hung suspended, and in each one Sebastian saw a reflection—not of herself, but of a girl of eleven standing in a drowned barley field, lightning crawling up her arms while the harvest rotted under floodwater.

*Do you know why I fear you?* the Crown asked softly. *Not because you are strong. Strength I have eaten by the kingdomful. I fear you because you are the only creature in this realm who ever chose to be less than she was. Every king I wore reached for more. You reached for nothing. You buried your power under oaths and tinker's solder and a false name, and you lived.* The suspended snow began, very slowly, to fall upward. *That is a door I cannot open. But you can walk through it yourself. And if you do—if you unbind what you sealed—I will not be able to stop you. So I am asking you not to.*

"Ask again," Sebastian said, "and I'll burn you where you sit."

She walked forward. One step. Two. The throne room swallowed the echo.

Behind her, far back near the shattered doors, Ilsa Vane stood with her hand pressed flat against the anchor-stone they'd carried down from the temple—a shard of the old ward-network, veined with silver light, heavy as sin. The priest had been clear: *the rite needs two. One to pour, one to hold the line between pourer and vessel, or the storm will take them both and the Crown with them, and the realm will be a cinder with a clean king's corpse on its throne.* Ilsa had not argued. Ilsa had simply picked up the stone, and said, "Then I'm holding it," in the voice she used when argument was already dead.

Their eyes met across the impossible distance of the hall. Ilsa's face was gray with blood loss, her side bound in strips torn from her warden's cloak, her arm shaking against the stone's weight. She nodded once.

*Sebastian.* The Crown's voice changed—warmer, silkier, and infinitely more dangerous. *One last gift. Would you like to see what your storm has already done?*

The hall dissolved.

---

She stood in the barley field again, but it was not memory—it was *witness*, rendered in perfect, merciless detail. Rain hammering down. The village below the hill, lanterns bobbing like drowning fireflies. And the storm—her storm, eleven years old and screaming out of her without permission or mercy—tearing the sky open above it all.

*Watch,* the Crown whispered.

She watched. The flood took the eastern fields first. Old Marta Hennick, who had taught her to braid charm-cord, drowned in her own root cellar trying to save the seed stores. The Calloway boy—Tomas, eight years old, who used to trade her river-glass for honey cakes—was swept off the mill bridge; they found him three days later, ten miles downstream. The harvest died. The winter after it, the fever came, and the fever took the old and the small, and every grave in the village churchyard leaned toward the Kavanagh house like flowers toward a poisoned sun.

*Forty-one kings,* the Crown murmured, *and I never once had to show them anything. They came to me hungry and I fed them and they thanked me. But you—you need to be shown. You need to be *reminded*. Because if you pour that storm into me, you will feel all of it again. Every death. Every grief. Twelve years of rain, little caller, all at once, through your own hands. Can you carry that?*

Sebastian stood in the phantom rain and let it hit her.

She had expected it to break her. That had been the Crown's mistake—expecting. She had spent twelve years carrying these deaths already. She had braided them into her charm-cords and soldered them into her tin whistles and poured them out, drop by drop, into every small kindness she'd done in Corvane's market. The guilt was not a wave that would drown her. It was the ground she stood on.

"No," she said. "But I can use it."

The vision shattered like ice.

---

She was running now, down the endless hall, and the shadows were peeling off the walls to meet her—shapes with the faces of dead kings, mouths open in silent screams, reaching with hands that had signed treaties and held children and strangled rivals. She ran through them. They passed through her like cold smoke, whispering in voices that had ruled for nine hundred years: *turn back, turn back, we tried, we all tried—*

The throne rose before her.

Aldric's eyes were open. They were pale, washed-out things, fixed on nothing—and then, as she climbed the dais steps, they *moved*. Tracked her. Focused.

For one heartbeat, a man looked out of them.

His lips cracked apart. No sound came—but his right hand twitched against the armrest, fingers dragging through the dust, and Sebastian saw what he wrote, over and over, in a shaking hand:

*Mara. Tell Mara.*

"I know," Sebastian breathed. "I know. Hold on. I'm getting you out."

The light died from his eyes. The Crown surged up through him like black water filling a lung, and Aldric's spine arched, and the voice that came out of his mouth was no longer silky or warm. It was vast, and layered, and afraid.

*YOU CANNOT CARRY IT.*

"You're right," Sebastian said, and pulled the first oath loose.

---

It was like unwinding a bone from her own body.

Twelve years ago, in a warden's office that smelled of ink and rain, she had spoken the sealing-oaths with Ilsa Vane's hand on her shoulder—oaths woven by three archmages, paid for with Ilsa's own rank, binding the storm down into a cage of words at the base of her soul. She had felt them settle like stones. She had lived with them so long she'd forgotten they had weight.

Now she spoke the unbinding, and the first oath tore free, and she *screamed*—not in pain, but because the sound that came out of her was thunder.

The second oath. The third. Each one ripped away left her vaster and vaster, a well becoming a lake becoming a sea, and the throne room's surviving windows blew inward in a glittering hail, and the dead kings' shadows fled howling into the cracks of the world. Lightning crawled up through the marble under her feet, following her veins, mapping her. The suspended snow ignited. Somewhere far away, she heard Ilsa shouting her name, and the shout sounded tiny, and beloved, and impossibly far.

The last oath was the oldest one—the one she'd spoken herself, alone, the night after her exile, pressing her palm to a border-ward post in the rain: *I renounce the storm. Let it sleep in me until I die.*

She didn't speak the counter-word. She just opened her hand.

The storm woke.

---

There are no words in any language for what happened next, and Sebastian would spend the rest of her life grateful for that, because it meant she never had to try.

She put both hands on Aldric's chest, over his heart, and she *poured*.

The storm went through her like a river through a broken dam—all twelve years of it, every lightning bolt she'd swallowed, every thunderclap she'd bitten down on, every gray morning she'd watched the sky and ached. It went through her and into the anchor-line—Ilsa's voice rising behind her in the anchoring litany, raw and steady, *hold, hold, I have you, I HOLD you*—and through the line into the dying king, and into the thing coiled around his soul.

The Hollow Crown burned.

It fought. Of course it fought. It screamed with forty-one royal voices, and each voice offered her something—her magic legitimized, her name cleared, Ilsa safe, the exile ended, a tower of her own, a *throne*—and Sebastian poured the storm harder, and the offers charred to ash in the lightning. It showed her the drowned field again, the graves, Tomas Calloway's small white face, and she wept without stopping and did not slow down, because the priest had taught her this too: *grief is not the opposite of the rite, child. Grief is the fuel. Burn it clean.*

The Crown's last scream was not a king's voice at all. It was older—thin, high, almost birdlike—and in it, for half a heartbeat, Sebastian heard what it had been before the first king's greed had gotten into it like rot into a tooth: a guardian. A shepherd of stars. Something that had once loved this sky.

*I'm sorry,* she thought at it, and meant it, and burned it anyway.

The darkness around Aldric's head unraveled thread by thread, wailing, and then it was gone—sucked up the channel of the storm and out through the broken roof and scattered into the colorless sky like cinders on wind.

And the stars—

Oh, the stars.

They came back one at a time, the way lamps are lit down a long street. First one, high in the north, trembling. Then three more. Then a handful, then a scatter, then a *flood*—the whole host of heaven reigniting in waves, constellations Sebastian hadn't seen since childhood blooming back into existence overhead, and the light of them fell through the ruined roof and turned the throne room silver, and somewhere in the city below, she knew, people were pouring into the streets to look up and weep.

The storm guttered.

She felt it go the way you feel a fever break—one moment blazing, the next a strange cool quiet. The sea in her drained. The lightning in her veins dimmed to embers, to sparks, to nothing at all, and what was left behind was a silence so complete it rang.

*Ordinary,* she thought, from very far away. *I'm ordinary.*

Her knees buckled. The last thing she saw was Ilsa Vane dropping the anchor-stone and running, limping, flying down the hall toward her, shouting her name—her true name, shouted in front of God and the relit stars and nobody else at all.

Then the floor came up gentle as snowfall, and the dark took her, and for the first time in twelve years, the dark was only dark.

---

She dreamed of the barley field, but dry. Golden. A girl with untamed hair ran through it laughing, and no storm followed her, and when Sebastian called out the girl only waved and kept running, free, and Sebastian understood that this was not a warning or a haunting. It was a goodbye.

When she woke, gray daylight was coming through the broken roof, and snow was falling again—falling *down*, properly, the way snow should. Someone had covered her with a warden's cloak. Someone was sitting on the steps of the dais beside her, holding her hand in both of theirs, and had been, by the stiffness in the air, for a long time.

On the throne, King Aldric slept—truly slept, his breathing shallow but his own, his brow bare and human and lined with ordinary age. Alive. Empty of crowns.

Sebastian turned her head. Ilsa's eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and fixed on her with an intensity that would have frightened her, once.

"The stars are out," Ilsa said hoarsely. "All of them. The whole city's in the streets."

Sebastian tried to speak and found her throat wrecked. She tried instead to reach for the storm—the old reflex, the lifetime habit—and felt, where it had always lived, only quiet. Clean, empty, human quiet. A space shaped exactly like everything she'd given away.

She started to laugh. It hurt, and it cracked, and halfway through it became crying, and Ilsa gathered her up off the cold marble and held her through all of it, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that needed saying.

After a long time, Sebastian wiped her face and looked up at the hole in the roof, at the blue morning sky beyond it, at a single stubborn star still lingering at the edge of the light, refusing to leave before it was sure.

"There's a girl," she rasped. "In the capital. Mara. The king asked—" Her voice broke. She steadied. "I promised someone I'd tell her he fought."

Ilsa helped her stand.

Together, leaning on each other, they walked out of the throne room into the noise of a waking city—and somewhere below, bells had begun to ring.
