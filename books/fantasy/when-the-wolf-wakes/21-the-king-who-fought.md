## 21. The King Who Fought

Ilsa Vane had stood before kings. She had knelt to Aldric at his coronation, back when his hair was still dark and his voice could fill a cathedral without effort. She had delivered reports to him in this same hall, a hundred times, her boots ringing on marble that had been laid by masons who believed the crown would outlast the mountains.

She had never once seen him look like this.

The throne room of Corvane had not been repaired since the night the stars relit. The great windows were still cracked webs of glass, and no one had swept away the scorch marks where Sebastian's storm had come through the walls like judgment given flesh. Workmen had hung canvas where the doors used to be, and the wind moved it in slow, ghostly billows. The Hollow Crown's seat—Aldric's seat—had been carried out and burned in the courtyard three days ago, and Ilsa had watched the smoke rise with something she refused to call joy.

Now Aldric sat on a plain wooden chair placed where the throne had been, wrapped in a grey wool cloak, and he looked small.

That was the word for it. *Small.* The man who had worn the Crown for nine years, who had been eaten from the inside out by a parasite older than the kingdom itself, sat with his hands folded in his lap like an old farmer waiting out rain. His face was gaunt. His eyes, which Ilsa had last seen moving half a second behind his body, tracking nothing, were now present and tired and unbearably human.

"You're staring, Warden," he said. His voice was rough, as if it had been dragged through gravel. In a way, Ilsa supposed, it had been.

"I'm sorry, Your Majesty."

"Don't be." He turned his head slowly, and she saw the tremor in his neck, the way his body had become an unfamiliar country to him. "I'd stare too. At a man who came back from the dead." A pause. "Or almost did."

Ilsa stood at parade rest because she didn't trust herself to do anything else. Her wounds from the pass—the spear-graze along her ribs, the deep bruise blooming across her hip where Hale's hunters had put her into the rocks—ached with every heartbeat. The healers had told her to lie down. She had told them, with some heat, that she would lie down when the realm stopped burning.

"Your Majesty," she said, "the council is assembled."

"I know. I can hear them shuffling." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "Nine years I sat on that throne and never heard them shuffle. Now I hear everything. Every creak. Every whisper. Do you know what the Crown took from me first, Warden? Before the magic, before the memories?" He looked up at her. "It took my hearing. It made the world quiet so I wouldn't notice what was happening inside my own skull."

Ilsa said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either pity or apology, and he deserved neither from her.

"Let them in," Aldric said.

* * *

The regency council was seven people, and Ilsa knew every one of them would have hanged her a month ago.

There was Chancellor Merric Sorrel, whose ledgers had kept the kingdom solvent through the dark winter and whose loyalty to the crown was the only religion he practiced. There was Lady Vess of the Marches, who had lost two sons to the Crown's hunters and had come to the capital with frostbite scars and a face like a closed door. There was Mother Anselm of the rebuilt star-temple, the last priest's oldest student, who spoke for the faith that Corvane had spent a century pretending didn't exist. And four others—soldiers, merchants, a magistrate from the river country—all of them summoned by a king who could barely stand, all of them watching him now with the particular wariness of people who had learned, over one terrible year, that authority could be a mask worn by something hungry.

Aldric rose to greet them. It took him three tries, and Ilsa stepped forward without thinking, offering her arm, and after a moment—a long moment in which the whole room watched—he took it.

"The crown of this kingdom," he said, once he was upright, "has been empty for nine years. You all know it now. Some of you suspected longer." His gaze found Lady Vess, held it. "I will not insult you by pretending I can rule. I can barely walk to the window. What remains of me is going home—to my daughter, if she'll have me—and what remains of my reign is this: there will be no more kings until the realm has decided what a king is *for*."

He named the council then, all seven, and gave them the regency in a voice that grew stronger with each sentence, as though the words themselves were pulling him upright. He gave them the laws he had been drafting in the nights since his freeing—laws written in a shaking hand that Ilsa recognized, because she had seen the same shaking hand sign death warrants under the Crown's compulsion. The repeal of the Edict of Ashes. The legalization of hedge-craft, charm-work, storm-calling, all of it, licensed and witnessed and *legal*. The dissolution of the Inquisition as it had stood, its records opened, its prisoners freed.

At that last one, Chancellor Sorrel went pale. "Your Majesty—the Inquisition's mandate—"

"Was to protect the realm from magic," Aldric said. "And instead it fed the realm's fear to the thing that was eating it. Fear is a food, Chancellor. I know. I was fed on it for nine years." He let go of Ilsa's arm and stood, swaying, on his own. "The mandate is ended. Dissolved. Burn the seals if you like. I'd suggest the courtyard."

No one laughed. But something in the room loosened, the way a knot loosens, and Ilsa understood that she was watching a country exhale.

Then Aldric turned to her, and the room followed his gaze, and Ilsa felt twenty years of discipline hold her spine straight while everything behind it wanted to run.

"Warden Vane," he said. "Come here."

She crossed the floor. Marble under her boots, wind breathing through the canvas doors, seven faces watching.

"A year ago," Aldric said, "you found the border wards dead and you knew why. You carried that knowledge alone. You let the realm believe the wards failed naturally. You let *me* believe—" his voice caught, just slightly, "—whatever the Crown let me believe. You did it because you thought you were protecting the line. Protecting me." He shook his head slowly. "You were wrong. It cost us a year we didn't have. It cost lives. It cost—" he glanced toward the canvas doors, toward the city beyond, where a tinker's shop was being fitted with a new sign even now, "—it cost things I can't name yet."

"Yes, Your Majesty," Ilsa said quietly.

"And you also held a mountain pass alone against thirty of the Crown's hunters so that a storm-caller could reach the temple and save my life." He reached into his cloak and drew out something Ilsa hadn't seen in twelve years: the badge of the High Wardenship, silver and sapphire, heavy enough to leave a mark. "I am told the council wishes to restore your rank. All of it. High Warden. Command of the borders. A pardon for the year of silence, sealed and buried."

He held it out.

Ilsa looked at it.

She thought about the day she had pinned that badge on for the first time, young and certain and so terribly sure that duty was a straight road. She thought about a girl in a rain-soaked village square, twelve years ago, kneeling in the mud while Ilsa read the exile decree in a voice that did not shake only because she had practiced it for three days beforehand. She thought about a mountain pass, and blood in the snow, and the moment—halfway through the fighting, when her sword arm was screaming and the hunters kept coming—when she had realized she was not defending the crown anymore. She was defending a woman. One specific woman, asleep in a ruined temple, who had every right to let her die.

She thought about how the badge had never once felt like love, and how the exile decree had always, secretly, been exactly that.

"No," Ilsa said.

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well.

Aldric blinked. Behind him, Chancellor Sorrel made a small strangled sound. Lady Vess, of all people, tilted her head—just slightly—with something that might, in a different life, have been approval.

"High Warden is a rank of the old order," Ilsa said. "The order that exiled children. That burned the tinker quarter. That looked at a girl who couldn't control what lived in her blood and called it treason." She reached out—not for the badge, but to close the king's fingers around it, gently, the way you'd close the hand of someone sleeping. "Keep it, Your Majesty. Melt it down. Make it into a bell, or a plowshare, or a child's toy. I'm done wearing things that weigh more than they're worth."

"What will you do instead?" Aldric asked. And it was not a king's question. It was a tired man's question, asked by someone who genuinely wanted to know.

Ilsa looked past him, through the cracked windows, toward the lower city, where the outcasts she had drilled in the dark winter were already rebuilding—witches and tinkers and exiles, unranked and unrepentant, organizing themselves into something that had no name yet but had saved the capital twice. Toward a narrow house above a charm shop where a woman with no storm left in her was learning, slowly, how to sleep through the night.

"There's a door I've been standing outside of for twelve years," Ilsa said. "I intend to knock on it properly this time."

* * *

The Kavanagh farmstead lay in the hill country south of the capital, three days' ride, in a valley that the dark winter had spared the worst of. Ilsa rode out on the second morning after the abdication, alone, because some debts you don't carry witnesses to.

Princess Elara Aldraine met her in the dooryard.

Ilsa had expected guards, or servants, or at least the cold formality of a royal household in mourning-for-a-year-that-wasn't-quite-over. Instead there was a young woman of nineteen standing among the bare apple trees with a wood axe in her hands and sawdust on her sleeves, and when she saw the Warden's cloak she set the axe against the fence post with deliberate care, the way her father must have taught her, and waited.

"Lady Vane," Elara said. Her voice was level. Her eyes were not; they were her father's eyes, and they had spent a year learning to hope and unlearning it again, and Ilsa could see both wars still running behind them.

"My lady. May we speak?"

"We may." Elara glanced at the house. "Inside?"

"The garden is fine."

They walked between the apple trees. Frost still clung to the north sides of the trunks. Somewhere beyond the hedge, a neighbor's dog barked at nothing, and the sound was so ordinary that Ilsa's throat tightened unexpectedly.

"I'll come to it plainly," Ilsa said, "because you've had a year of courtiers coming to it any other way. Your father sent me. Not with a letter—he wanted to, but his hand isn't steady enough yet, and he said—" she paused, choosing, "—he said you'd know his words better from someone who'd earned the telling."

Elara stopped walking. She did not speak. She folded her hands in front of her, and Ilsa saw that they were work-roughened, a princess's hands that had spent a winter hauling water and splitting wood like everyone else in the starving country, and that she was proud of them.

"He remembers you," Ilsa said. "Through all of it. Through the Crown, through nine years of being eaten alive from the inside. There were stretches—I won't lie to you—where he was gone. Where the thing wearing him didn't know your name. But he surfaced. Near the end, he surfaced, and the first thing he did—the *first* thing—was send a message out through the parasite's own mouth, risking everything, to say one thing." She made herself hold the girl's gaze. "He said: tell my daughter I fought. Every day. Even when I couldn't remember why. Tell her I fought."

Elara's composure held for four full seconds.

Then it broke, quietly, the way ice breaks on a spring pond—one crack, then another, then the whole surface giving way all at once. She turned away, gripping a low apple branch with both hands, and her shoulders shook, and she made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than sobbing. Ilsa stood where she was and did not touch her and did not look away, because looking away would have been another kind of lie, and this family had been lied to enough for nine lifetimes.

When Elara finally turned back, her face was wet and open and years younger.

"He fought," she repeated. As if testing whether the words would bear weight.

"Every day," Ilsa said. "And the woman who freed him—she nearly died doing it. Gave up everything she was. Your father knows what she paid. He talks about it the way soldiers talk about the ones who pulled them out of the fire." She hesitated. "He also talks about you. Constantly. It's insufferable."

That startled a laugh out of the girl—wet, broken, real. "Is he really coming home? Truly? Not the—the thing—"

"Not the thing. The thing is ash. I watched it burn." Ilsa reached into her coat and brought out the only proof she had carried the whole ride: a scrap of parchment in a shaking, familiar hand, three lines long. *Lara. Coming home. Save me a place at the table. —Da.* "He wrote it himself. Refused help. Took him an hour, I'm told, and he was furious about every minute."

Elara took the paper with both hands, the way you take something holy, and read it, and read it again, and pressed it flat against her chest.

"When?" she whispered.

"As soon as the roads clear. A fortnight, perhaps less."

The princess of a dissolving monarchy stood in her muddy garden holding a scrap of paper, and for a moment she was just a daughter, and the frost on the apple trees began, very faintly, to melt in the strengthening sun.

"Lady Vane," she said at last. "Stay for supper. Please. I—I think my father would want his debts honored properly, and I'm told you're owed several."

Ilsa thought of every meal she had eaten alone in garrison halls, every rank-feast, every toast to a crown that was hollow all along.

"I'd be honored, my lady."

* * *

She rode back north on the fourth day, and the roads were full of spring.

Not metaphorically—actually full of it. Snowmelt roaring in the ditches. Farmers walking their field edges, assessing the thaw. A peddler's cart with new charms hanging from its frame, displayed openly, brazenly, the little wooden tokens turning in the wind—and no one crossing the road to avoid the cart. No one spitting. A boy ran alongside it for a hundred yards just to look.

Corvane rose out of the valley in the late afternoon, and Ilsa reined in on the ridge to look at it, because she was learning to do that now—to stop, and look, and admit that a place mattered to her. The city's roofs were patched with canvas and scavenged slate where the mob-fires had taken the tinker quarter, but smoke rose from a thousand chimneys, honest smoke, hearth-smoke. On the temple hill, the star-temple's broken dome caught the light, and scaffolding crawled over it like patient ivy. They were rebuilding it. Of course they were. Mother Anselm had not asked anyone's permission.

And below, in the recovered streets of the lower quarter, a narrow building with a fresh-painted sign.

Ilsa walked her horse down through the gates, past the guard posts staffed now by half ex-soldiers and half witches, past the market where a fever-child's mother was pressing coins on a tall woman with grey-shot red hair and ink-stained fingers—Sebastian, arguing about the coins, losing the argument, pocketing them with ill grace. She didn't see Ilsa yet. She was laughing at something the boy beside her had said.

Twelve years ago, Ilsa had signed a decree that erased this woman from the world, and had called it mercy, and had gone home and drunk half a bottle of garrison brandy and told herself it was the same thing.

Tonight there would be no decrees. Tonight there was a door with a new sign over it—KAVANAGH'S CHARMS, the letters still bright—and a threshold she intended to cross not as a warden, not as an exile-maker, not as anything the old order had ever allowed her to be.

Just Ilsa. Knocking properly, at last.

She swung down from the saddle, and Sebastian looked up, and the whole street seemed to lean in around them like a held breath—

—and that was when the bells began.

Not the alarm bells. Not the Crown's tolling, which Ilsa would know in her bones until she died. These were the temple bells, high and bright and rapid, the pattern rung only for one purpose in all the old liturgies: *a sign has been given. Come and see.*

From the temple hill, thin with distance, rose a sound Ilsa had heard only once before in her life, on the night the stars went out—but reversed now, rising instead of falling, swelling instead of dying:

Every star-priest in Corvane, singing at once.

Sebastian's hand found hers without either of them deciding it. Above the temple, in the brightening dusk, something new was kindling in the sky—something that had not burned there in a thousand years, according to the oldest texts, according to a dying priest's last whispers.

The first star of the old constellation—the Wolf—was waking above the city, and it was calling them by name.
