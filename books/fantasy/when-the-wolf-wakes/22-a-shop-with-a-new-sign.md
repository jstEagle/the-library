## 22. A Shop with a New Sign

The sign took three days to paint, and Sebastian repainted it twice.

Not because the lettering was wrong—her hands had grown steadier in the months since the unbinding, not weaker, which still surprised her some mornings when she woke and reached for the storm that wasn't there and found, instead of grief, only quiet. No, the problem was simpler than craft. The problem was the name.

*Seb the Tinker — Charms & Mending*, she had written first, out of twelve years of habit, and then stood in the doorway of the little shop on Tanner's Row staring at it until her eyes stung, because it was a mask painted in her own hand, and she was done wearing masks.

She scraped it off with turpentine at dusk while the lamplighter made his rounds and the smell of spring rain came off the cobbles, and she wrote instead:

*Kavanagh's — Charms, Mending, & Honest Weather Advice*

Ilsa found her there an hour later, paint-flecked to the elbow, laughing at nothing in particular, or perhaps at everything.

"It's crooked," Ilsa said, from the doorway.

"The K leans."

"Everything leans. It's Corvane." But Ilsa stepped inside anyway, ducking under the lintel the way she always did, a half-inch too tall for every door in this city and never once complaining about it, and looked around at what they'd made: the shelves Seb had built from salvaged temple timber, the counter polished from a door some mob had torn off its hinges last autumn and never reclaimed, the jars of river-stones and knotted thread and salt in blue paper, all of it ordinary now. All of it just *things*, sold by just a woman.

No storm in any of them. No storm in her.

"You could have straightened it," Ilsa said, meaning the K, meaning nothing at all.

Sebastian dipped the brush and held it out. "Then fix it, Warden."

"I'm not a warden anymore."

"No," Sebastian agreed, warm as banked coals. "You're not. So fix it as a woman who owes me twelve years of honest work."

Ilsa took the brush. Their fingers touched over the handle and stayed a moment longer than the task required, which was, by now, simply how they touched things—each other included.

Outside, Tanner's Row went about its evening. The baker's boy ran past with his arms full of early rhubarb. Old Marta from the dye-house called up something rude and affectionate about the new sign being too fancy for the neighborhood, and someone else laughed, and somewhere down toward the river a fiddle struck up the tune that had been going around all spring—the one everyone called *the Wolf's Reel* and no one could quite remember learning, because songs like that arrived the way stars did now: one by one, quietly, until you looked up and realized the sky was full.

---

The magic had come back to Corvane slowly, the way feeling returns to a numb limb—pins and needles first, then ache, then warmth.

The regency council had spent the winter arguing, which was what councils were for, but even they could not argue with what everyone had seen: the king walking out of his own palace thin as a winter branch, gray-haired at forty, kneeling in the mud of the palace square to tell ten thousand people that he had been asleep inside his own body for a year and that a tinker and a disgraced warden had woken him. He had abdicated two days later. He had kept one thing, though—one request, delivered through channels, through clerks, through Ilsa, until it reached the person it belonged to.

*Tell my daughter I fought.*

So on a bright cold morning in late winter, Sebastian Kavanagh had stood in a schoolyard in the temple district while Princess Elara Aldricsdotter—eleven years old, chin up the way her father's chin went up, eyes red-rimmed and furious at the world for making her cry—demanded, "Did he? Really?"

"He fought every day," Sebastian said. "Even when he couldn't show it. Even when he couldn't *know* it. Whatever that thing took from him, it never got the part of him that was your father. That part stayed fighting the whole time." She had knelt so their eyes were level. "I know because I saw him. At the very end, he surfaced long enough to say it himself. Those were almost his words: *tell her I fought.*"

Elara had cried then, properly, ugly, the way children are allowed to and adults are not, and Sebastian had held her shoulders and let her, and thought: *this is what the rite bought. Not glory. This. One girl getting to grieve a man who is still alive to be grieved.*

Aldric lived in the country now, at a house with an orchard, teaching himself to walk without a shadow that lagged. He sent letters sometimes. Short ones. Once, only once, he had written: *Some mornings I reach for it—the Crown—and feel nothing where it was. Then I remember that nothing can be a gift. I am told you understand.*

She did. She understood better than anyone alive.

Because here was the truth she carried now, folded small against her ribs where the storm used to live: she missed it. Some nights she woke with her skin prickling, dreaming of rain, of the vast pressure of weather wanting to move through her, and she lay in the dark and mourned it honestly, the way you mourn anything that was part of you—even the parts that hurt you, even the parts that burned villages and killed harvests and sent you into exile. You didn't get to choose which pieces of yourself you loved. You only got to choose what you built afterward.

And afterward, it turned out, was not empty.

The charms she made now worked the way charms were supposed to work—modestly, imperfectly, the way a grandmother's knot worked or a horseshoe over a door. A fever charm bought a child a night of easier breathing; it did not break the fever. A luck-knot tilted a bargain by a hair; it did not make fortunes. And yet people came. They came in growing numbers, first the neighbors who had hidden her in their cellars while the mobs burned the quarter, then strangers, then—after the council's Edict of Tolerance passed in the third month, after magic stopped being a crime and became merely a trade, licensed and taxed and boring, which was the greatest victory anyone had ever won—a whole city's worth of people who wanted, more than power, the simple animal comfort of knowing someone was looking out for them.

Sebastian Kavanagh, storm-caller, the woman who had burned out a god wearing a king, sold fever knots at two coppers and gave them free to anyone who couldn't pay, and if that was a smaller life than the one the ballads described, well. She had read the ballads. Ballads were written by people who hadn't been there.

* * *

In April the licensing office finally processed her paperwork, and she walked home from the civic hall with a stamped document declaring her *Kavanagh, S.—Charmer, First Class* and had to stop on the bridge over the Corve because her legs wouldn't hold her and her eyes were doing something embarrassing.

Twelve years ago she had crossed this same bridge at night, seventeen years old, stripped of name and rank, with a warden's seal on her exile papers and frostbite in her heart, and she had promised herself she would never come back. Now the bridge was just a bridge. The water underneath was just water, brown and quick with snowmelt. A woman selling roasted chestnuts nodded at her the way you nod at someone you see most days.

That was all. That was everything.

"Rough morning?" the chestnut seller asked.

"The best one of my life," Sebastian said, and bought a paper cone of chestnuts she didn't want, because some transactions are really thank-yous.

Ilsa was waiting at the shop when she got back, which no longer required explanation between them—Ilsa simply was there, most days, in the way that weather is there, in the way that the north wall of a house is there. She had taken the room above the shop in February, over Sebastian's objections that she should at least pretend to look for other lodgings, and had unpacked exactly four possessions: a whetstone, a broken ward-sigil she refused to throw away, a bottle of very bad brandy, and a second pillow, which said everything that needed saying without either of them having to find words for it.

They were finding words anyway, lately. Slowly. The way ice goes off a lake—not all at once but edge by edge, in the warm afternoons, with a sound like distant singing.

"The clerk said First Class," Sebastian reported, hanging her coat on the peg. "There's apparently a Second Class. And a Third."

"For people whose charms don't work as well?"

"For people who haven't been audited. We're all Second until the audit." She dropped onto the stool behind the counter and spun the ledger open. "Marta ordered thirty fever knots for the dye-house. The Hendersons' roof needs a weather-seal before the rains. And we've been invited—" she squinted at the note pinned under the counter weight "—to something called the Founders' Feast. Council hall. Us."

Ilsa, who was re-hanging the fallen ward-sigil on its nail by the door—the sigil from the border, the one she'd carried bleeding through the dark to Sebastian's door a lifetime ago—went still.

"They're giving you a medal," she said flatly.

"They're giving *us* a medal. There's two names on the invitation."

"I told the council what they could do with their rank. In front of the full session. With witnesses."

"Yes, and I told them I'd think about it, which is different, because unlike you I'm capable of strategy." Sebastian tapped the note. "We go. We smile. We eat their terrible food. And when they hand us whatever it is—a plaque, a sash, a commemorative spoon—we accept it graciously, and then we hang the spoon over the door next to your sigil, and every corrupt old councillor in Corvane has to look at the border-ward they let fall every time they come groveling for our blessing."

Ilsa considered this. The corner of her mouth moved—that slow tectonic shift that, twelve years ago, Sebastian would have given anything to cause, and that now cost her nothing at all except the occasional afternoon.

"You've become political," Ilsa said, with the tone of a woman discovering mold.

"I've become *tired*. It looks similar from the outside."

* * *

The feast was terrible in exactly the ways Sebastian had predicted: too many candles, too much ceremony, a chancellor with a voice like wet velvet who spoke for twenty minutes about *renewal* and *the healing of the realm* without once saying the word *Crown*, because the council still preferred euphemism to history. But there were good things threaded through it, the way silver runs through ore. Witches sat at the high tables now—actual hedge-witches, soil-under-their-fingernails women who eight months ago would have been dragged to Hale's inquisitors. Hale's name was spoken only in the past tense, and mostly with a complicated gentleness; they had put his marker in the wardens' crypt with the inscription Ilsa chose: *He was consumed defending others from consumption. Mercy was his last act and his truest one.*

Halfway through the chancellor's speech, an old woman in dyer's black leaned over from the neighboring seat—Marta, invited, to Sebastian's astonishment and delight, because apparently saving the kingdom earned your whole street a seat at the table—and muttered, "He's going to do the bit about the Wolf constellation again."

He did the bit about the Wolf constellation again.

But when it came time for honors, the chancellor surprised her. He didn't give a speech about the unbinding. Instead he read out the charges that had been filed against Sebastian Kavanagh twelve years ago—unlicensed magic, public endangerment, exile by order of Wardens Command—and then, formally, one by one, struck each from the record, and asked her to stand, and said: "Corvane wronged you. The record is amended. Welcome home."

It wasn't the medal that undid her. It was the word *home*, said in a civic hall, in front of strangers, as a legal fact.

She stood. She managed a bow. She did not cry until later, on the steps outside, with Ilsa's arm solid around her shoulders and the spring night smelling of thaw and woodsmoke, and the sky overhead scattered so thickly with stars that she had to pick out the Wolf one star at a time, nose to tail, like following a stitch-line.

"It's brighter this year," Ilsa said.

"What is?"

"The Wolf. The eye-star. Priests keep writing to me about it—they still think I'm clergy-adjacent, God help them. They say it's never burned this color. Red-gold." Ilsa paused. "Like storm-light."

Sebastian looked up at it a long moment—the eye of the wolf, watching the city that had exiled her and then, clumsily, belatedly, learned her name—and felt something turn over in her chest. Not the storm. Nothing would ever be the storm again. Something quieter. The feeling of a door standing open that she had assumed was bricked shut forever.

"Well," she said. "Let it watch. We're not hiding anymore."

* * *

Summer came, and with it the ordinary miracles of a life she had never expected to have.

She learned that Ilsa cooked like a soldier (adequately, in quantity, at dawn) and danced like a secret romantic (badly, joyfully, whenever the fiddle on Tanner's Row started up). She learned the exact weight of sleep without nightmares, most nights, though the village still visited her dreams in the deep of winter and probably always would, and she had stopped expecting that wound to close all the way. Scars weren't closures. Scars were how the body remembered without bleeding.

She learned the neighborhood's rhythms: which houses needed luck-knots renewed at each solstice, which children came in pretending to need charms fixed when really they needed an adult to notice they were sad. She kept a jar behind the counter labeled *HONEST WEATHER ADVICE — FREE*, which contained no money at all, only slips of paper on which customers wrote their troubles, and on slow evenings she read them and answered as best a former storm-caller turned shopkeeper could. Most answers were variations on *you're not cursed, you're grieving, eat something and sleep*. Corvane found this deeply reassuring. Corvane had been cursed for a year; it knew the difference.

In June, the council ratified the final articles of magical licensure, and among the first hundred names registered was a girl of nineteen from the tinker quarter named Osla Bren, whose talent was so modest it barely qualified—hearth-charms, kettle-warming, the ability to keep bread from staling—and who shook so hard presenting her papers that Sebastian had to pour her tea from behind her own counter.

"I practiced the oath for weeks," Osla whispered.

"I know. I heard you through the floor."

Osla stared. "You live *above* the— oh. Oh no."

"Relax. When I was your age, my practice sessions flattened a valley." She slid the tea across. "Your bread trick is lovely. Never apologize for small magic. Small magic is what holds the world together while the large kind is busy destroying it."

When Osla left, lighter by several pounds of fear, Ilsa came down the stairs drying her hands and said, "You're training them now."

"I'm *feeding* them. Training implies curriculum."

"Mm." Ilsa leaned on the counter, and outside the window the summer light came in gold across the shelves, across the jars and knots and the border-ward sigil on its nail, across the two mugs that lived permanently beside the kettle because neither of them ever bothered putting them away. "The priests wrote again. About the star."

"I know. I answered them."

"What did you tell them?"

Sebastian looked at the sigil—cracked, dead, its ward-lines dark since the night the Crown ate it, kept anyway, because some things you keep to remember what they cost—and then up through the window at the blue afternoon where, invisible in daylight, the Wolf's red-gold eye hung waiting for dusk.

"I told them the truth," she said. "That guardian spirits don't die cleanly. That the Crown screamed with a thousand kings' voices when it burned, and some of those voices might have been grateful. That the Wolf is waking the way anything wakes after a long corruption—slowly, and strangely, and nobody entirely sure what it will be when it's done." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and certain. "And that whatever it turns out to be, it'll find a city ready for it. A city with witches on its registers and exiles on its streets and a charm shop on Tanner's Row with a crooked K on the sign, run by a woman who has already survived the worst thing the sky ever sent down."

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Then: "And if it comes asking for a storm-caller?"

"It won't find one." Sebastian turned her hand over on the counter, palm up, empty, calloused from carpentry and ink stains and ordinary work, and found—as she did every time she checked, and she suspected she would check her whole life long—that the emptiness didn't ache today. "It'll find a shopkeeper. Who makes excellent tea. And who is, at long last, exactly where she belongs."

Below the window, on Tanner's Row, the fiddle started up the Wolf's Reel, and the whole street began, in its unhurried way, to dance.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the night the stars went out, Corvane held no official observance—the council couldn't agree on whether it should be a mourning or a festival, so the city settled the question itself and held both at once, and the boundary between them dissolved somewhere around midnight into the largest celebration anyone could remember.

Sebastian closed the shop early. She and Ilsa ate dinner on the roof with the whole sprawl of the capital below them, candles in every window, lanterns strung across every alley, ten thousand small magics and none at all burning together against the dark. At the hour when the stars had vanished, the city fell silent by common consent—not organized, just understood—and ten thousand faces turned upward together.

And the Wolf rose over the rooftops, nose to tail, blazing.

At its eye, the red-gold star burned brighter than any account had ever recorded, brighter than the oldest priest's oldest text described, so bright it threw shadows on the rooftop, and Sebastian felt Ilsa's hand tighten around hers.

The light pulsed.

Once. Twice.

And then—there was no mistaking it, and no explaining it away, and every soul on every rooftop in Corvane saw it at the same instant—the star *moved*: a slow, deliberate sweep across the sky, nose to tail, the way a great hound circles what it loves before lying down at its feet.

The silence held for one breath, two, three.

Then Tanner's Row erupted, and the whole city with it—but Sebastian didn't cheer. She stood very still on her own roof, in her own home, above her own shop, with her own name on the sign below and the woman she loved beside her, and watched the ancient guardian settle into the sky above the city like a promise finally kept.

"Wolf's awake," Ilsa said softly. "Really awake."

"Yes," Sebastian said, and discovered she was smiling, wide and unafraid, into all that returning light. "Welcome home, old friend. We saved you a place."
