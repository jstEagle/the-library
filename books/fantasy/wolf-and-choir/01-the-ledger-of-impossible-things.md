## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with impossible things, Noor had learned in nine years at the Vellum Guild, was that they never balanced.

Everything else did. Grain futures balanced against warehouse receipts. Salt imports against the harbor tax. The guild's ordinary business was a clean arithmetic that any clerk could manage, and Noor managed it better than anyone — Mistress Corvane said so twice a year, which by her accounting was extravagant praise. But the second ledger, the one locked in the drawer to his left, the one that recorded what the guild actually traded in — that one fought him every night like something alive.

Tonight it was fighting harder than usual.

He dipped his pen, blotted it, and looked again at the entry that refused to close. *One storm-hawk, juvenile, male, acquired from the Saltmarket brokers, condition: poor.* In the column for disposition he had written nothing, because there was nothing honest to write. A storm-hawk could not be sold whole. Its feathers went into weather-charms its wings into fletching for messages that flew true through gales, and its voice — well, its voice went nowhere, because no buyer in the city could pay for a thing they couldn't hear.

Noor could hear it.

It had been crying since sundown. Not loudly — storm-hawks didn't waste breath — but in that thin, sawing register that slid under doors and through walls and settled behind the sternum like a fishhook. Grief, mostly. A little rage. Underneath both, worse than either: expectation. The bird had been promised things by whoever brokered it, promises made in human words it half understood, and now it sat in a cage in the guild's cellar working through the arithmetic of betrayal, and every calculation came out the same as Noor's ledger. It did not balance.

"Stop," he murmured, without looking up from the page. "You'll bring someone."

The crying didn't stop, but it changed key. Curious now. Suspicious. Storm-hawks were not clever the way wolves or ravens were clever, but they knew a voice that answered when no mouth moved.

Noor set down his pen and pressed two fingers flat against the floorboards. Through the wood, through stone, through forty feet of the guildhouse's old bones, he sent the shape of a thought the way his mother had taught him before she taught him letters: *quiet. dark is safe. wait.*

The crying thinned to a whisper, then stopped. In the sudden silence Noor became aware of his own heartbeat and the candle burning down beside the ledger, wax pooling in the brass dish, and the fact that he had just used the gift inside Vellum House on the same night the books were due to Corvane. Stupid. Careless. The kind of mistake that got beast-speakers burned, historically speaking, though history had been quiet on that subject for a hundred years, which everyone agreed meant the problem was solved.

He rubbed his eyes and went back to work. There was comfort in the work; there always had been. Numbers didn't sing. Numbers didn't know your name.

* * *

The first ledger was finished by midnight. The second took longer, because the second required lying, and Noor liked to lie well.

Corvane's rule for the impossible inventory was simple: everything entered, nothing explained. The Vellum Guild traded in favors, relics, rumors, and living assets, and each of these went into the book in the guild's private shorthand — a cipher of Corvane's own design, changed yearly, that reduced a captured river-spirit's name to three characters and a debt owed by a duke to a fraction. Noor kept the cipher in his head the way other men kept prayers. He had never been asked to. That was the arrangement. He was given the strange entries and he rendered them legible and he asked no questions, and in exchange the guild paid him well, housed him in the attic rooms, and never once remarked on how the cats of Copperlane followed him home, or why the rats in the counting-room seemed to arrange themselves when he passed, or how a man who worked alone by candlelight always seemed to know when a shipment had gone bad three days before the smell reached anyone else.

Nine years of not asking. Nine years of her not asking back. It was, he sometimes thought, the most expensive conversation neither of them had ever had.

The storm-hawk entry stared up at him. He dipped the pen.

*Disposition:* he wrote, *one favor called in. Debtor: Aldous Renk, salt-factor, obligation outstanding since Frostmonth. Terms: Renk secures release papers for lot 44 (avian) from the Saltmarket brokers, citing guild authority. Consideration: forgiveness of debt in full.*

There. Clean. Legal, even, if anyone ever checked — and no one checked, because who would care about a bird? The hawk would be free by week's end, out over the breakwater and gone north with the storms it was named for. And the ledger would show a routine settlement of an aged debt, which was even true, in the way that most good lies are true things wearing different clothes.

His mother had called this kind of work *spending yourself*. Every favor traded, every cage quietly opened, every lie told cleanly — each one spent a coin out of an account no auditor could see. The account was his safety. He'd been spending carefully for thirty-one years.

He was blotting the entry when the wall spoke.

Not the hawk. Something smaller, closer, moving inside the plaster the way mice move but with a rhythm no mouse had — a soft, wet, deliberate sound, like a word being formed over and over. Noor went still. He didn't reach for the gift; the gift arrived on its own, sliding up through his palms where they rested on the desk, and the voice resolved out of the house's noise the way a face resolves out of fog.

Rat. Old female, scarred ears, the particular gravel of an animal that had survived four winters in the walls of a building full of paper and poison. She had spoken to him maybe six times in nine years. Rats were talkative among themselves and nearly silent toward humans, even ones like him — too much had been done to them in the name of cleanliness for them to trust any human voice, including the ones that answered.

"*Upstairs-man,*" she said. Her name for him. "*Listen. They are coming to read you.*"

The candle flame stood perfectly straight. Somewhere below, the hawk shifted in its cage, iron rattling once.

"Who is coming?" Noor asked aloud, barely breathing it. "Coming here?"

But the rat's grammar wasn't built for questions answered, only warnings given. She repeated herself, slower, the way you'd speak to a child or a fool: "*They are coming to read you. Smell of fire on them. Song in their throats. Coming to read you.*"

Then the sound of her movement faded into the wall's ordinary creaks, and she was gone, and Noor sat alone in the pool of candlelight with two ledgers open in front of him and the taste of copper in his mouth.

Fire. Song. He knew the words beasts used for the Choir without ever having been taught them — some knowledge predated his mother, predated the gift itself, lived somewhere older than memory. Fire and song. The Inquisitors' Choir hadn't come to a city in his lifetime. Their hymns were the stuff of his mother's worst stories, told low, after the shutters were closed: how the Choir sang down chimneys and into cellars, how their detection hymn found a hidden gift the way a lit lamp finds moths, how the last beast-speaker in Varrow had been burned on the cathedral steps while the crowd wept at the beauty of the singing.

That was a century ago. Everyone agreed it was solved.

Noor looked down at the ledgers. His own handwriting looked suddenly foreign to him — *one favor called in, forgiveness of debt in full* — evidence, proof, a trail in ink of a man who traded with animals. He thought of the cipher in his head, the secrets in the second book, the hawk in the cellar whose freedom he had just purchased with borrowed money. Everything he had built his life out of was written down somewhere. That was the horror of it, the thing he'd never let himself see clearly: a man like him didn't need to be caught doing magic. He only needed to be *read*.

"They are coming to read you," he repeated to the empty room, testing the weight of it. It didn't lighten.

He snuffed the candle, sat a moment in the dark listening — to the house, to the rain starting against the windows, to the small terrified engine of the storm-hawk's heart two floors down — and then he began, very calmly, to do what he did best.

He opened the first ledger to a fresh page and started balancing.

* * *

By the third bell past midnight, Noor had reconstructed his evening as an innocent man would have lived it.

He wrote himself finishing the first ledger at the proper hour. He wrote himself locking the second drawer at the proper time. He invented a small error in the salt column and corrected it, because innocent men make small errors; perfection was a scent hounds followed. He dated everything, initialed everything, blotted everything properly, and then he sat back and read the evening over twice and found it seamless, and hated how easily it came, how fluent he was in the grammar of concealment, how nine years of practice meant that falsifying his own life took less effort than balancing a column of numbers.

The rain thickened. Down in the cellar the hawk had gone silent — sleeping, or trusting, or both. Noor climbed to the attic room he'd occupied since he was twenty-two, with its sloped ceiling and its single window over the rooftops of Copperlane, and he did not sleep. He packed instead. Not much. Coin, the false-bottomed strongbox's contents, his mother's ring, a change of clothes rolled tight. He'd done it before, twice, in other cities, in earlier years, always after some near-thing — a gull that talked too loudly in a market square, a horse that screamed his name in front of a carter — and both times he'd unpacked within a week, ashamed, telling himself the danger had passed.

This time he left the bag by the door.

Toward morning he gave up on sleep entirely and went back down to the counting-room, because waiting in the dark was worse than working in it, and because there was one more entry he wanted to make while his hand was steady. He lit a fresh candle and pulled the second ledger toward him and turned to the back pages, past the inventory, past the debts, to the blank leaves at the end where Corvane recorded nothing and therefore, by the guild's own logic, nothing existed.

On the first blank leaf he wrote, in the smallest hand he could manage:

*Nine years' service. If anything happens to me, let the record show I settled my accounts. Lot 44 goes free regardless — the order stands on its own. Tell no one why.*

He signed it with his initials, N.I., and blotted it, and felt briefly, foolishly better, the way a man feels better writing a will he doesn't expect to need.

Then the hair on his arms lifted, all at once, the way it does before lightning.

It wasn't thunder. It was music — faint, distant, coming up through the soles of his feet from the direction of the harbor, a sound almost too far away to be heard and yet unmistakably *shaped*, rising and falling in long deliberate phrases, dozens of voices braided into one. Noor had never heard the Choir. He knew it instantly anyway, the way the hawk had known its cage, the way the rats know poison. Some things announce themselves across any distance.

The song swelled, held a note that seemed to bend the rain, and faded — not ending, he understood, merely passing. Moving through the city street by street. Reading.

In the walls around him, every rat in Vellum House went still at once. Two floors down, the storm-hawk began, very softly, to scream.

Noor closed both ledgers, locked the drawer, and stood in the candlelight with his hand flat on the wood, feeling through sixty feet of stone and timber the small frantic heartbeat of the bird he'd bought free, and listened to the beautiful, terrible music come one street closer.

They were coming to read him.

He blew out the candle and waited to find out what the dark would say.
