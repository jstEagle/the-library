## 2. A Date Written a Century Ago

The Vellum Guild did not send Noor Iversen to appraise ordinary estates. Ordinary estates went to Tam and the other clerks with their ink-stained cuffs and their honest, plodding arithmetic. Noor went to the estates that had been *seized* — the ones where the Inquisitors' Choir had already come and gone, leaving behind that particular silence, the silence of a house whose owner had been taken away in the back of a black wagon while the neighbors drew their shutters and pretended not to hear the singing.

He arrived at the Aldous Marchetti estate on a morning of thin gray rain, and the first thing he noticed was that the birds had gone.

Not fled. *Gone*. The garden's hedges stood full of empty nests, and the dovecote door hung open on a coop of nothing, and when Noor stood very still and listened with the part of himself he was never supposed to listen with, he heard the absence the way you hear a missing tooth — a small, constant wrongness where something used to be.

The Choir had sung here recently. The birds had understood what that meant.

"Ledger-keeper." The bailiff, a squat man named Ferro with the complexion of boiled ham, met him at the door with a manifest already thrust out like a shield. "Inquisitors took the master three days ago. We're to inventory everything. Everything gets valued. Everything gets *recorded*."

"Then everything gets recorded," Noor said, and unslung his satchel.

He had done this eleven times in his four years with the Guild, and the routine was the same each time, and the routine was the only reason he could do it at all. Begin with the rooms furthest from the study. Work inward. Let the house tell its story in the order houses tell stories: servants' quarters first, where the truth about a man lived in the quality of his boots and the state of his kitchen; then the public rooms, where the lie of him lived in the portraits and the silver; and the study last, always last, because the study was where a man kept the self he thought no one would read.

The house was cold. The fires had been let to die, and the rain came through somewhere upstairs with a patient dripping that sounded, to Noor's ear, like a quill touching paper over and over and over.

He catalogued. He was good at cataloguing. It was, he had long ago admitted to himself in the privacy of his own skull, a kind of hiding — a man who spent his days assigning numbers to other people's lives did not have to assign a number to his own. A silver fruit-knife, hallmarked, three crowns. A painting of a woman with a lapdog, the dog's eyes repainted sometime in the last decade by someone who had loved it, two crowns, though Noor noted in the margin, in the small cipher only he used, *dog's eyes wrong, grief in the brushwork, do not sell to the man on Candle Street who beats his horses.*

Mistress Corvane would never see that margin. That was the arrangement. Corvane read the columns; Noor read everything else.

It was in the third-floor linen room — of all the absurd places, pressed between bolts of funeral cloth that had never been needed for the funeral that had instead become a wagon ride — that he found the first wrong thing.

A book, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, and the cord tied in a knot Noor's fingers knew before his mind did, because it was a knot that beast-speakers used, a knot that could be untied by a hand but not by teeth or claw, a knot that said: *if you are reading this, you already know what you are, and I am sorry.*

Noor stood in the cold linen room with the rain dripping upstairs and the empty nests outside and his own heart going like a trapped bird, and he did not untie the knot.

He untied the knot.

The book inside was a ledger. Of course it was a ledger. It was bound in the Guild style, columns ruled in faded red, the leather gone the color of old ash — and that was the first thing, the color, because Noor knew leather, knew every tannery's mark from here to the coast, and no tannery made leather that color. That color was made by fire. Someone had burned this book, or tried to, and it had not let itself be burned, and the char had become its binding the way scar becomes skin.

The first page bore a date a hundred years and some months gone, and a title in a cramped, urgent hand:

*Codex of Ashes. Being a true account of things not yet come to pass, kept so that when they come, someone will know they were known.*

Noor's mouth had gone dry. He was a professional. He appraised impossible things for a living — that was the Guild's whole trade, the reason the Vellum Guild existed in the gray space between the merchant houses and the law: someone had to put a price on a saint's fingerbone, a bottled storm, a love letter that answered back. He had held impossible things in his hands before.

He had never held one that held *him*.

He carried the Codex down to the study — carried it openly, because a ledger-keeper carrying a ledger was the most invisible act in the world — and set it on Marchetti's desk beneath the window with its gray rain light, and he began, with the discipline of four years of routine, to read.

* * *

The entries were prophecies. Noor had seen prophetic texts before; every appraiser had, and the Guild's standing instruction was to value them as curiosities, ten crowns at most, because prophecy was the one impossible thing that never held its price. Prophecies were vague. Prophecies were poetry. Prophecies were the desperate embroidery of frightened people stitching meaning onto whatever the century happened to produce.

These were not vague.

*On the third of Thaw-month, in the year the harbor freezes late, a child will be born in the Salt Quarter with the gray eyes of the tide, and the midwife will die of a joy she cannot explain, and the Guild will record the birth as a death because the father pays for the erasure.*

Noor sat back. Then he went to the manifest, and found the entry for Marchetti's personal papers, and found among them — because the Choir had left them, uninterested, worthless — a bundle of Guild birth-notices from sixty years back. He checked. He checked twice. The entry existed. A child, Salt Quarter, recorded as stillborn, father's payment noted in the margin in a clerk's hand.

A hundred years ago, someone had written down a lie that a Guild clerk would make sixty years later, in the exact terms the clerk would make it in.

Noor's hands were not steady, but his training was, and he did what a ledger-keeper does: he kept reading, and he kept a tally.

The Codex was not one prophecy. It was dozens, maybe hundreds, marching down the ruled columns year by year, decade by decade — and here was the thing that made the vertigo climb up through the floor of him and settle behind his eyes like a second pulse: the entries were in *two hands*. The first was the cramped urgent hand of the title page, a human hand, ink and quill, a person writing fast and writing scared. The second hand was different. It was not written so much as *pressed* — the letters too deep, the strokes too even, as though the pen had been held by something that understood the idea of writing but not the anatomy of it, something translating from a language with no alphabet into one, laboriously, the way a musician transcribes a song by ear.

Two authors. A hundred years of entries. And the entries did not fade, or scatter, or go poetic. They came forward through the decades like a debt accruing interest, each one dated, each one precise, each one — Noor checked, and checked again, and felt the floor of the world tilt a degree further — *accurate*.

The harbor freezing late. The midwife. The false birth record. The fire at the Tannery Row in the year of the double eclipse, which had started, the official record said, from a lightning strike, and which the Codex said would start from a lantern knocked by a cat whose name would be given, and the cat's name was given, and Noor found the name in a fire-brigade survivor's deposition that Marchetti had apparently collected as a curiosity and the Choir had not bothered to seize.

The Codex was not prophecy the way the ballads meant prophecy. The Codex was *bookkeeping*. It was a ledger of the future kept with the same dry precision Noor brought to silver fruit-knives and grief-struck lapdog portraits, and somewhere in the last hundred years, every entry in it had come true.

He turned pages with hands that had gone cold despite the room. The two hands alternated, argued almost — the human hand writing entries and then, in the margins, in smaller script, the pressed hand *correcting* them, refining them, the way a senior partner corrects a junior's figures. A collaboration. A century-long collaboration between a person and something that was not a person, or not a person in any way the law of the city would recognize.

And the entries grew darker as they came forward. The early ones were small: births, fires, debts, a wedding, a drowning. The later ones bent toward the Choir. *In the year the new Inquisitor wears gray, the singing will move from the temples to the streets, and the beasts will go quiet in a ring around the city, one mile out, as though behind a wall.* Noor had walked that ring. He had felt it. He had told himself for years it was his imagination — that the animals of the countryside fell silent at the city's edge for ordinary reasons.

It was in the book. It had been in the book for ninety years.

The final ruled page was near the end of the binding, and Noor knew it was the final one the way you know the last step of a staircase in the dark — there was a different quality to the paper's resistance under his thumb. The last entry was written in the pressed hand alone. The human hand had stopped contributing decades before; whatever collaboration this had been, the person had ended and the other author had gone on alone, faithful to a dead partner's project, keeping the books of a future with no one left to read them with.

The last entry bore a date.

Noor read the date.

He read it again, because the mind does that, the mind hands you a thing and then asks you to hold it up to the light and confirm it is real, and he confirmed it, and it was real, and it was tonight. Not the night of the seizure, not the night of Marchetti's arrest, not some anniversary. Tonight. The night on which he, Noor Iversen, ledger-keeper of the Vellum Guild, was sitting in a dead man's study cataloguing a dead man's silver while the rain dripped upstairs like a patient quill.

The entry was short. The pressed hand had grown surer over the century, the letters almost elegant now, an animal that had learned to write:

*On this night, the Wolf comes to the book. He will not know he is the Wolf. He will be reading, and he will be afraid, and he will be right to be afraid, because they are already looking for him, and the book is the reason.*

And beside it, in the margin, in the cramped human hand — the dead partner's hand, reaching across a century to underline a single word the way Noor underlined values in his manifests — one word:

*Wolf.*

Noor did not move for a long time.

The rain dripped. The house settled around its own emptiness. Somewhere below, Ferro was hammering a Guild seal onto a crate of confiscated linens, and the sound came up through the floorboards like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

*Wolf.*

He knew the word. Everyone knew the word, the way everyone knows the words for things that live only in stories — the Wolf, the Choir's own founding myth, the beast-speaker of the old tales who would come at the end of the pact and either break it or keep it, and no two tellings agreed which. The Inquisitors sang the story into children at temple school: *the Wolf is the lie the beasts told, and when the Wolf speaks, the silence ends, and when the silence ends, the wars return.* The Wolf was the name the Choir gave to its own fear. The Wolf was why the gift was outlawed. The Wolf was the reason Noor had spent thirty-one years folding himself small, thirty-one years of never letting his mouth move when the gulls spoke to him over the fish-market, thirty-one years of answering the rats in the walls only in the dark and only in a whisper and only ever, *ever*, pretending afterward that he had not.

He was not the Wolf. He was a clerk. He was a man who assigned three crowns to silver fruit-knives and noted in margins which buyers beat their horses. He was careful. He was *careful*, that was the whole architecture of his life, every beam of it load-bearing carefulness, and the Wolf of the songs was the opposite of careful, the Wolf was the thing carefulness existed to prevent—

*They are already looking for him, and the book is the reason.*

The vertigo came fully then, and Noor let it, because fighting it in a dead man's study with a bailiff on the stairs was how a man dropped a century-old book and made a noise. He let the floor of the world tilt and he held the Codex in both hands like an anchor and he understood, with the terrible clarity of the already-written, what he was feeling.

It was the feeling of reading your own name in a debtor's column. It was the feeling of an entry you never made appearing in your ledger in your own hand. It was the sense — precise, professional, unbearable — of being *recorded*. Somewhere in the past, someone had entered him. Someone had ruled a line, dated it, and written him into being, and tonight was the date of the entry coming due, and he had walked into this house on routine Guild business because of course he had, because that was how the entry had been written, because he had never had a choice, because the choice had been transacted a hundred years ago by a hand that pressed too deep into the paper—

No. He put the thought down like a heavy crate. He was a ledger-keeper. He knew what books could do and what they could not. A book was ink. A book was a claim, and claims could be audited, and audited claims could be *disputed*. If the Codex said tonight, then tonight was an entry, and entries were not fate. Entries were *assertions awaiting reconciliation*.

He was going to reconcile it. That was the whole of his plan, born whole in the rain-gray light of Marchetti's study: he was going to take the book apart the way he took apart a fraudulent manifest, line by line, and find the error in it, because there was always an error, because no book balanced perfectly, because a hundred years of accurate entries was not prophecy but the most meticulous, most patient fraud ever committed, and frauds had authors, and authors could be found.

And if there was no error—

He did not finish the thought. He was careful. He never finished the dangerous thoughts.

* * *

He finished the appraisal. That was the discipline of him, that was the load-bearing beam: the Choir had taken the man, but the books still had to balance, and Noor Iversen balanced them. Silver fruit-knife, three crowns. Portrait with the repainted dog, two. Linens, funeral cloth, never needed. He worked through the afternoon with the Codex wrapped again in its oilcloth at the bottom of his satchel, against his hip, a weight like a second heartbeat, and he gave it no entry in the manifest.

That was the first crime. He understood that it was. A ledger-keeper who failed to enter an asset had committed the oldest sin of his trade, and he committed it deliberately, in full knowledge, with a steadiness that frightened him more than the book did — because the steadiness meant some part of him had decided already, some part of him that did not consult the careful architecture, had looked at the word *Wolf* and the word *tonight* and had said: *not them. Not the Choir. This one is mine.*

He told Ferro the estate was a poor one, mostly linens and sentiment, worth less than the wagon hire. Ferro grunted and was relieved, because Ferro had been dreading the arithmetic of a rich man's goods. Noor signed the manifest. The Guild seal went onto the door. The rain had stopped, and the birds were still gone, and the empty nests stood against the graying sky like small open mouths.

He walked back through the city with the Codex against his hip and he told no one.

He told no one at the Guild, where Tam asked him how the Marchetti job had gone and Noor said *poor estate, sad business, the usual* and Tam, who was honest and plodding and kind, believed him, because Tam believed everyone, and Noor felt the lie go between them like a coin passed in the dark.

He told no one Mistress Corvane, though she looked up when he brought the manifest, and her eyes — quick, gray, priced-everything eyes — rested on him a half-second longer than usual, and she said, "You've found something," in the tone she used for assets, and he said, "A sad house, mistress. Empty nests and funeral cloth," and she laughed, because it was a good line, and let him go.

He told no one the walls of his own room, where the rats lived, the small gray neighbors who knew what he was and kept his secret with the loose honor of the desperate — he did not ask them anything, did not listen to anything, sat with his candle and his cold supper and the Codex open on his knees, and read it through from the ash-colored first page to the last, and made his tally, and found no error.

Every entry. A hundred years. Every entry had come true.

The candle burned low. The city outside went about its night, carts and quarrels and the far-off bells of the temple where they taught children to fear a Wolf, and Noor sat with the book of himself in his lap and finally, near midnight, did the thing he had been not-doing since the linen room.

He spoke — barely a breath, barely a sound, into the dark of his rented room, to no one, to the walls, to the small gray listeners he trusted more than any person in the city:

"Tomorrow," he whispered, in the old tongue, the tongue with no alphabet, the tongue that was a crime, "I'm going to ask about the one who wrote this."

And from inside the walls, in the dark, in the same tongue, small and dry and old, a rat's voice answered him — and Noor Iversen, who had spent thirty-one years being careful, heard what it said and felt the last of the careful architecture shift on its foundations:

*"We remember the writer. She burned. And the one who finished the book —"* a pause, a small gray hesitation, like a clerk deciding how to phrase a loss — *"— the one who finished the book has been asking the walls of this city about you for a very long time."*
