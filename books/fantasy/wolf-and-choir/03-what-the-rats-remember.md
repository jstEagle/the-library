## 3. What the Rats Remember

Noor did not sleep.

He sat at his desk until the candle burned down to a coin of wax, and then he lit another from the first and sat some more, the Codex of Ashes open in front of him like a wound he couldn't stop looking at. The date glowed on the final page in that faded iron-gall brown: tonight's date. His name beside it. *Wolf.*

Around him, Vellum House ticked and settled the way old buildings do — timber contracting, pipes knocking, the night clerk Tam coughing two floors up in a room that smelled of cheap tobacco and cheaper gin. Ordinary sounds. A building full of ordinary people doing ordinary crimes for an extraordinary profit.

And beneath all of it, threaded through the walls like water through limestone, the rats were waiting.

They had spoken to him once already tonight, which was itself unprecedented. Dock rats didn't volunteer information; they sold it, hoarded it, traded it the way everything in this city was traded. For one of them to speak unprompted — *we remember the writer* — meant either that they were afraid, or that they wanted something badly enough to spend their fear.

Both, probably. Both was usually the answer.

*"The one who finished the book has been asking the walls of this city about you for a very long time."*

Noor turned that sentence over the way he turned over ledger entries, looking for the hidden column, the second set of numbers behind the first. Someone had finished the Codex. Someone had added the last entry — his name, tonight's date — after the original writer died. And that someone had been looking for him.

He should burn the book. He knew this the way you know a stair is rotten even before your foot goes through. A century-old prophecy naming him as the Wolf of the pact, in a city where the Inquisitors' Choir could sing a secret out of a man's chest like a splinter — the only safe place for that book was a fire.

Instead he wrapped it in oilcloth, tied it with ledger twine, and hid it inside the hollow spine of the Guild's 1841 shipping registry, because no one in the history of commerce had ever voluntarily opened the 1841 shipping registry.

Then he put on his coat and went down to the docks to commit the second most dangerous crime of his life.

* * *

The harbor at three in the morning smelled of tar, dead fish, and the particular sweetness of rot that the tide brought in twice a day and took credit for twice a day. Noor walked the length of Pier Nine with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and when he reached the breakwater where the pilings made their dark lattice against the black water, he crouched, and he knocked.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. It meant nothing to any human ear. To the ears under the pier it meant: *a speaker, alone, paying.*

They came out of the dark the way they always did, which was to say all at once and never quite where you were looking. Gray shapes along the pilings, gray shapes in the shadow of a beached skiff, a dozen pairs of eyes catching the distant lamplight like wet stones. The big male came forward — scarred across the muzzle, one ear chewed to lace, a veteran of a hundred territorial wars fought entirely below human notice. Noor had known him for nine years. He'd never learned the rat's real name, because rats kept their true names the way humans kept their ledgers: private, and load-bearing.

*Speaker.* The voice arrived in his head dry and sardonic, like paper turning. *You pay in cheese again, or have you finally learned what we actually eat?*

"I bring bread and dried fish and I don't want to hear about it."

*An insult, but a consistent one.* The rat sat back on his haunches. Around him the others settled into stillness — not calm, Noor noted. The stillness of animals deciding whether a thing is a trap. *You came at a bad hour, speaker. We don't gather like this anymore. Not since the singing started in the south parishes.*

Noor's stomach dropped half an inch. "What singing?"

*The kind that finds things.* The rat's whiskers flattened. *Two nights past, in Saltmarket. A woman who kept bees and talked to them. They sang her street at midnight and her gift lit up like a lamp in a dark room, and by dawn there was ash where she'd been.* A pause. *The bees came to us after. Bees are terrible gossips, but they were very upset.*

Saltmarket was four districts away. Four districts, two nights. The Choir was moving north, toward the guild quarter, toward the river, toward him, though of course the rat didn't know that last part. No one knew that part. That was the entire architecture of his life: thirty-one years of load-bearing secrecy.

"Tell me about the writer," Noor said. "The one who wrote the book."

The change in the rats was immediate and total — a ripple through the gathered bodies, ears flattening, the big male taking a step back into the shadow of the piling. For a long moment the only sound was the water slapping stone.

*Why do you ask?*

"Because I'm holding her book. Because my name is in it. Because someone finished it after she died, and that someone is asking the walls about me." He kept his voice level, but the rats heard heartbeats the way humans heard voices, and he knew they heard his speed up. "I need to know how she died. All of it. Don't make it gentle."

*It isn't gentle,* the rat said. *Nothing we remember is gentle. That is mostly what memory is for.*

* * *

Her name had been Aldith Marrow. She had been a chandler's widow who kept a shop on Cindergate Lane and talked to pigeons, and the pigeons told her everything the city saw from above, and she wrote it all down in a book bound in ash-colored leather.

"She wasn't hiding," the rat said. They had settled now, the whole congress of them arranged around Noor on the breakwater like a jury, or an audience, and the big male spoke while the others fed small corrections and details into the telling — a word here, a scent-memory there, so that the story arrived the way rat stories always arrived, layered, communal, chewed over until it was soft enough to swallow. "That's what nobody understands about her. She hid for forty years like everyone else. And then near the end she stopped. Started leaving the book where it could be found. Talking to sparrows on her windowsill in daylight. She said—" the rat's voice shifted register, going careful, quoting — "*'A pact that only survives by silence isn't a pact. It's a hostage situation.'*"

Noor closed his eyes. He could see the shop. He hadn't ever seen the shop, and he could see it anyway: jars of tallow, wicks hanging like pale hair, a heavyset gray-haired woman writing by lamplight while pigeons crowded the sill like feathered clerks.

"What happened?"

"The Choir came for her on a Tuesday. There was no hymn, that far back — they hadn't invented the finding-songs yet, or they hadn't needed them. They just came with rope and a warrant signed by someone whose name we've forgotten, which tells you how much names matter." The big male's tail curled and uncurled. "She ran. Through the fish market, down to the water. The beasts tried to help her — every animal on the waterfront, we all tried. Gulls mobbed the Inquisitors. Horses threw their riders. The dock dogs got loose and did what dock dogs do. And it bought her maybe four minutes, and then she was caught, and they burned her on the customs house steps at noon so everyone could watch."

Silence, except the water.

"And here is the part," the rat continued, softer now, "that we do not tell speakers, because speakers get sentimental and then get stupid. The beasts did not rise up for her. You understand? Every animal within a mile watched her burn and did nothing, afterward. We bit and clawed and delayed while she lived, and when she stopped living, we went back to our holes. Because what else was there? She was one woman and we were ten thousand animals and none of it mattered. That is the arithmetic of being voiceless. You can spend everything you have and buy four minutes."

Noor found that his hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against his knees. "Someone finished her book."

"Yes."

"Do the rats know who?"

Another long pause. This one had a different texture — not reluctance exactly. Fear with its coat brushed and its boots polished.

"We know what we saw," the rat said at last. "In the year after the burning, a stranger came to the customs house steps, at night, and knelt where the ash was, and collected it. Into a jar. A human shape, hooded, walking wrong — too quiet, like something wearing a person. And it spoke to us." The rat's whiskers trembled. "Speakers speak our tongue the way you do it, speaker. Learned. Careful. Like a merchant pronouncing foreign words. This thing spoke it *native*. Old. Older than the pact. It asked us questions about Aldith Marrow for six hours, and it paid us in answers instead of food, and every answer it gave us was true, which was somehow worse. Then it finished her book, whatever finishing means, and it left the city, and it has been coming back every few decades to check the walls. Asking. Always asking the same question."

Noor's mouth had gone dry. "Which question?"

"*Has the Wolf come yet.*"

The word landed in his chest like a dropped anchor. Around him the rats had gone utterly still, all those small bodies watching him with a hundred eyes that reflected nothing, gave nothing back.

"You understand why we gather like this now," the rat said. "Not for bread. The thing that walks too quietly came back this winter. First time in eleven years. It is asking the walls again. And last week, the walls started answering differently — because of *you*, speaker. Because a man in Vellum House has been talking to rats in the dark for nine years and thinking no one heard." The big male leaned forward. "We don't know if the thing that finished the book is your friend or the other kind of thing. But we know this: it knows your building. It stood outside Vellum House three nights ago and listened to the walls for an hour. And two days ago, men in gray coats with silver throats came to the harbor master and asked for the shipping manifests of every guild house on the river. Silver throats, speaker. Do you know what wears a silver throat?"

Noor did. Everyone did, in the way everyone knew about executions — abstractly, from a distance, grateful for the distance. The Inquisitors' Choir wore silver throat-bands that carried their hymns without their own voices, so that the finding-songs could pour through a district for hours without tiring the singer. Gray coats. Silver throats. The Choir wasn't coming north.

It was here.

"When?" he managed. "When did they ask the harbor master?"

"Yesterday, at low tide. And they bought the manifests of the Vellum Guild first."

* * *

He walked home fast, not running, because running drew eyes, but at the pace of a man with somewhere to be, which he was, everywhere, permanently, starting now. The sky over the harbor had gone from black to the color of dirty slate. Gulls were waking on the masts, arguing about nothing, and for the first time in his life Noor heard their noise as testimony — *she burned, she burned, we watched, we did nothing* — a hundred years of witness compressed into birdsong that no human ear could parse and no human law could punish.

Aldith Marrow had hidden for forty years and died anyway. Had spent her silence like a miser and been burned at noon in front of a crowd that included ten thousand animals who loved her and could do nothing that mattered.

And she had left the book where it could be found. On purpose. *A pact that only survives by silence isn't a pact.*

"No," Noor said aloud, to the empty street, to the gulls, to whoever was listening — the too-quiet thing, the walls, the century. "I'm not her. I keep my head down. I balance books. Whatever she started, I'm not—"

From a rooftop above him, a pigeon said, in the flat tone of a bird reporting facts: *"Gray coats. Six of them. Walking up Cordwainer Street toward the guild quarter. Singing low."*

Noor stopped walking.

The pigeon hopped along the gutter, keeping pace, relentless. *"Low singing. The kind that makes the walls hum. They're checking doorways. They're checking everything."*

Six blocks away. Maybe seven. At this hour the streets were empty and sound carried off the water like a rumor off a tongue, and the Choir's finding-hymns were designed for exactly this: an empty city, a low song, every hidden gift lighting up like a lamp in a dark room.

Like a lamp in Vellum House.

Noor broke into a run.
