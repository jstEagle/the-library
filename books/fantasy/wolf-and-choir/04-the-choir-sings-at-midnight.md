## 4. The Choir Sings at Midnight

Sera Voss had heard the Hymn of Unveiling performed four times in her life, and every time it made her want to weep, which was a problem, because Inquisitors were not permitted to weep.

Not because of cruelty. Because of acoustics.

She stood on the quarterdeck of the *Canticle* as the harbor opened before them — black water, blacker sky, and the city of Vellum Reach stacked up its hills like books shelved by a drunkard. Behind her, forty voices of the Choir stood in their crescent formation on the deck, robed in ash-grey, faces bare, mouths closed. Waiting for her downbeat.

"Remember what you are," she said, though they all knew. They had drilled the doctrine since childhood: *We do not hunt. We listen.* It was even true, in the way that a knife does not hunt but merely arrives where cutting was always going to happen.

She raised her hand.

The city did not know yet that it was being read.

* * *

The hymn began low, in the men's section, a single sustained tone like the hum inside a struck bell. Then the women entered a fifth above it, and then the children — the Choir still trained children, because children's voices carried no guile and the hymn required purity more than power — and the chord bloomed outward across the water like ink dropped in milk.

Sera felt it move through her own chest, the way she always did. The Hymn of Unveiling was not a spell, whatever the pamphlets said. It was an argument. It said: *everything hidden is a debt, and debts come due in sound.* A detection hymn did not seek magic. It sought concealment. It sang the shape of the lie, and anything living that maintained a lie found itself vibrating in sympathy with the note — burning, as the field manuals put it, like a lantern behind a curtain.

In her eleven years as an Inquisitor, Sera had watched the hymn flush out a hedge-witch who had been quietly curing fevers in a fishing village for thirty years. She had watched it find a boy of nine who could make bread rise by singing to it. She had watched it find things worse than either, once, in the border provinces, and she did not let herself think about that night, except at night.

Forty voices. One city. Somewhere in those hills, if the intelligence was right, a beast-speaker was hiding.

The reports had been fragmentary. Dock rats behaving strangely — organized, which rats should never be. A gull flock over the fish market that had scattered on command, or so a frightened witness swore before recanting. And now this: a seized estate in the Merchant Quarter whose inventory included a book the archivists could not date, delivered to the Vellum Guild for appraisal, because the Vellum Guild would appraise anything, that was rather their reputation.

Beast-speaking had been outlawed for a hundred years. Not suppressed — outlawed, erased, sung out of the world during the Choir Wars until the beasts themselves fell silent, and good riddance, said the doctrine, because a beast with a voice was a beast with grievances, and grievances became wars, and wars became the drowned marshes and the empty villages that Sera had walked through as a girl, counting doorframes with no doors.

She believed the doctrine. She wanted that noted, somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven kept. She believed it the way you believe a load-bearing wall.

The hymn swelled into its second movement, and the city began to answer.

* * *

It was always like this — the terrible beauty of it. From the harbor, Vellum Reach lit up in sympathy, not with fire but with resonance. Sera closed her eyes and read the city the way other people read a page.

Ordinary silence everywhere, the great grey wash of ten thousand honest sleepers. Then small flares. A woman in the Tanners' Row who kept a luck-charm that was slightly more than a charm — a warm amber smudge, barely worth a clerk's time. A cellar in the Salt District where something fermented under a blessing that had gone feral. A child, somewhere up in the Weavers' Terraces, whose dreams glimmered faintly wrong, and Sera marked the street in her mind with the weary certainty that she would have to visit that house within the year, and take the child somewhere for training or for forgetting, and the parents would cry, and the law was the law.

And then —

She opened her eyes.

"Inquisitor Ondrey," she said quietly. "Do you hear the guildhouse?"

Ondrey, second voice of the left crescent, singing even as he listened — the hymn required them to sing and scan simultaneously, which was why Choir training took nine years — shook his head a fraction. "Nothing, First Voice."

Hmm.

The Vellum Guild sat on its hill above the Merchant Quarter like a fat black ledger dropped from the sky. Sera had reviewed its file on the voyage. Mistress Odile Corvane, proprietor; licensed dealer in curiosities, relics, and appraised impossibilities; taxes paid punctually; bribes paid punctually-er. Guilds like hers were how forbidden things moved through the world without anyone technically touching them. The Choir tolerated the Vellum Guild the way a body tolerates a spleen — with suspicion and vague unease, unable to prove it did anything necessary.

And tonight, as the hymn rolled over the city in its third movement — the searching movement, the one that went door to door in sound — the guildhouse returned nothing.

That was not the same as returning clean.

A clean building sang back honestly: stone, timber, glass, the dull animal murmur of sleepers. A guilty building flared. But the Corvane guildhouse did neither. It returned a smoothness, a practiced quiet, like a witness who has rehearsed his testimony. And beneath the smoothness — so faint that Sera doubted her own ear, and Sera never doubted her own ear — there was something else.

A second voice.

Not loud. Not shaped. More like the memory of a voice, the way a bell keeps humming after you've gripped its rim. Something in that building was holding very, very still, the way a held breath is still louder than breathing.

"Again from the bridge," she said. "Movement three. Let me hear the Merchant Quarter alone."

The Choir obeyed. Forty throats bent the note, and the hymn narrowed to a beam, and Sera stood at the rail with her eyes shut and her fist pressed to her sternum, listening to one hillside of one district of one sleeping city.

There. Under the guildhouse. Below the ground floor, below the cellars — a vault, the file said, the guild kept a deep vault — something answered the hymn with silence so dense it rang.

And threaded through that ringing silence, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain:

a human timbre, trembling, forcing itself flat.

Someone down there was suppressing a gift right now. Suppressing it well — well enough that Ondrey heard nothing, well enough that any Inquisitor in the provinces would have sailed home satisfied. But Sera Voss had been gifted herself since before she could talk; she heard the choir in rain and the argument in wind; and she knew what it sounded like when a person clenched their whole soul around a secret and squeezed.

It sounded like this. Like a note being strangled in the throat of the dark.

* * *

The hymn ended at moonset, as the rubric required. Unveilings were performed at night because gifts burned brighter against darkness, and because terror traveled better in the dark — the second reason appeared in no rubric but every Inquisitor knew it. By dawn, everyone in Vellum Reach would know the Choir had come. Doors would be swept. Cellars would be emptied of charms. Confessions would arrive at the temporary chapter-house like birds to a feeder.

Sera stood in the chapter-house — requisitioned customs hall, smelling of tar and cold incense — while her clerks set out the night's harvest on the long table. Three amber smudges. One feral blessing. One child, Weavers' Terraces, flagged for follow-up. She initialed each report without reading closely; she had heard everything already, in the music.

"The guildhouse," said Clerk Fenner, sliding the last sheet toward her. "No resonance logged. Clean return."

"No," Sera said.

Fenner waited. Fenner was good at waiting; it was most of his job.

"Log it as unresolved." She tapped the page. "The return was too smooth. Stone doesn't sing that evenly. Write: *acoustic anomaly, unclassified, requires physical inspection.*"

"You think they're hiding something."

"I think," Sera said, "that I have heard honest buildings my entire career, and that one was lying about being a building." She pulled the guild's file toward her and opened it to the staff roster. "Who has vault access?"

"Mistress Corvane. Her senior appraiser — a Noor Iversen. Two bonded porters. A clerk of record, one Tam Wicke, listed as indebted."

"Everyone is indebted," Sera murmured. She looked at the name *Iversen* for a moment longer than the name deserved. There was nothing there. A number, a hire-date, a notation of competence. But the hymn had left an afterimage in her ear, that strangled human note under the vault, and she had learned to trust afterimages the way sailors trust a change in the wind's taste.

"Send a courtesy notice to Mistress Corvane," she said. "The Choir will call on the guild tomorrow regarding the estate appraisal. Standard procedure. Give her a day to decide what kind of night she wants to have had."

"And if she refuses inspection?"

Sera closed the file. Outside, dawn was coming up the color of old vellum, and somewhere across the city a gull was screaming — screaming, she noticed, in a pattern, two short cries and a long one, repeated, repeated, like a signal, like a sentence, like something with grammar — and she made a private note to have the harbor birds watched, and told herself the small cold feeling underneath her ribs was only fatigue.

"If she refuses," she said, "then we'll know exactly how much the silence cost, and who paid it."

* * *

She slept badly, which was usual, and dreamed of the border provinces, which was not permitted, and woke before the bells with the hymn still humming in her blood. That was the price of the gift they'd trained into her: the music never fully stopped. Some nights it was almost sweet. Most nights it was the sound of the world refusing to be quiet, and her job was to make it quiet, and some mornings she woke not knowing which side of the song she was on.

Today was a working day. She dressed in the ash-grey, bound her hair, and pinned the silver tuning-fork brooch at her collar — the Choir's sigil, a fork struck and forever ringing. In the chapter-house yard, her escort mounted up: six riders, grey cloaks, instruments slung like weapons, which in a sense they were.

Vellum Reach in daylight was a city of paper. Paper banners, paper ledgers, paper promises blowing through the streets when the wind came off the harbor. The Vellum Guild crowned it all, and as the riders climbed the Merchant Quarter's cobbled spine, Sera watched the guildhouse grow, and felt again that hairline crack in the porcelain of the night.

The doors were already open. Of course they were. Mistress Odile Corvane stood on the top step in wine-dark silk, hands folded, smile calibrated to the half-degree, a woman who had clearly spent the interval between notice and arrival deciding precisely how much fear to display and had settled on just enough to seem human.

"Inquisitor Voss." Her voice was warm as wax. "The Guild of Vellum is honored. We keep a clean house."

"I'm sure you do," Sera said, dismounting. "Clean houses are my favorite kind to read."

And behind Corvane's shoulder, in the dim interior of the guildhall, Sera saw him for the first time — a young man with ledgers under his arm, standing very still among the shelves, with the particular stillness of someone listening to a conversation he had no right to hear. Their eyes met for less than a heartbeat.

He looked away first. He looked away *carefully*, the way you close a door so the frame doesn't catch.

Sera smiled at Mistress Corvane, and stepped across the threshold, and thought: *there you are.*

Whatever you are. Whatever note you're strangling in the dark.

Let's hear it.
