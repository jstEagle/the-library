## 5. Mistress Corvane's Bargain

The Inquisitors left before dawn.

Noor heard them go — not with his ears, which were ordinary enough, but with the building. The guildhouse had its own small population of beasts: mice in the granary, a colony of starlings under the eaves, an old tomcat who had appointed himself master of the loading dock and taxed every delivery of dried fish that came through it. When the Choir's horses clattered away down Vellum Street, the starlings stopped holding their breath, and the mice resumed their small economies, and the cat stretched out across the threshold like a fallen king.

Noor sat at his desk and did not move for a long time.

He had stood among the shelves last night with the ledgers pressed to his chest while Sera Voss's voice moved through the front room like water through cloth. He had listened the way he always listened — carefully, completely, cataloguing — and what he had catalogued was this: she was dangerous not because she was cruel but because she was *thorough*. She asked about shipments. She asked about storage. She asked whether the Guild kept any "living inventory," and Corvane had laughed, a real laugh, warm as a hearth-fire, and said the Vellum Guild traded in paper, Inquisitor, not flesh.

It was a lie so smooth Noor almost believed it himself.

Which meant one of two things. Either Mistress Corvane had forgotten what slept in her deepest vault — or she had decided, last night, in the space of a heartbeat, that the wolf was not worth mentioning.

He did not believe in forgetting. Not where Corvane was concerned.

* * *

She sent for him at midmorning.

Not a summons — Corvane never summoned anyone, it was bad for morale and worse for negotiation. Instead a note arrived on his desk, delivered by Tam the junior clerk, a nervous young man with ink-stained cuffs and debts he thought no one knew about:

*Noor — my office, when you've balanced the morning column. Bring the Ashwater appraisal. We have things to discuss. — M.C.*

Tam lingered after delivering it, hovering the way he did when he wanted to ask something and hadn't yet found a price for the asking.

"Anything else?" Noor said.

"No. Yes." Tam glanced at the door. "The Choir woman. She was asking about you."

Noor kept his eyes on the ledger. "Was she."

"Not by name. She asked how many staff I kept. Whether any were... local-born. Whether any had been with me long." Tam swallowed. "I told her you'd been with me nine years and were the best appraiser in the city. Was that wrong?"

*You told her exactly where to dig,* Noor thought. *Thank you.*

"That was correct," he said. "Loyalty is the cheapest insurance we sell. Go back to work."

Tam went, looking relieved to have been given something to do with his fear.

Nine years, Noor thought, alone in the office after. Nine years of balancing impossible books, of trading favors for caged birds, of listening through walls and saying nothing. Nine years, and in one evening he had looked at an Inquisitor for less than a heartbeat and she had seen him.

Or Corvane had sold him without even opening her mouth.

* * *

Corvane's office occupied the top floor of the guildhouse, behind a door of black oak carved with the Guild's motto: *EVERYTHING HAS A PRICE; EVERYTHING HAS A BUYER.* Inside it smelled of cedar, lamp oil, and the particular dry sweetness of very old vellum. Maps hung on the walls — trade routes, mostly, though Noor knew three of them marked smuggling channels and one marked nothing that existed at all, a fictional archipelago used to launder money through invented tariffs.

Mistress Odile Corvane stood at the window with her back to him, hands folded behind her, watching the street. She was past sixty and built like the guildhouse itself — narrow, tall, load-bearing. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned with a quill made of silver, a gift from a dead king, though which king she refused to say.

"You met the Inquisitor," she said, without turning.

"I saw her arrive. I was working."

"You were standing in the stacks holding four ledgers like a breastplate." Now she turned. Her face was pleasant the way a contract is pleasant — all terms, no warmth. "Sera Voss is third chair of the Inquisitorial Choir. Do you know what that means?"

"It means she sings well."

Corvane smiled thinly. "It means she can hear a hidden gift from forty feet through stone. It means that last night, standing in my front room, she heard something in this house. Something faint. Something *wrong*, in her word for it." She crossed to her desk and sat, and laced her fingers together on the blotter, and looked at him the way she looked at assets she was deciding whether to keep or liquidate.

"And she heard it coming from you, Noor."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the starlings rustled. The old cat yawned somewhere below, vast and indifferent.

"Mistress—"

"Don't." She raised one finger. "Don't insult us both. You think I hired a book-keeper who happens to have a gift for numbers? I hired a beast-speaker who happens to be able to count. Nine years ago you walked into my interview and lied to my face about your references, and the gull on my windowsill told me everything you'd said to it that morning on the docks. *Nice old woman, sharp as a fishhook, pays in secrets.*" She leaned forward. "I have known what you are since before you finished your first week. I let you believe otherwise because a secret shared is a debt owed, and I collect debts, Noor. It's the family business."

The floor seemed to tilt. Not vertigo this time — something colder. He had spent nine years performing normalcy, rehearsing his silences, and the whole performance had played to an audience of one who held season tickets.

"The rats," he said quietly. "The storm-hawk. The warnings through the walls. You knew."

"I suspected the hawk. I *knew* the rest." She opened a drawer and drew out a single sheet of paper, laid it on the desk between them, and turned it to face him. It was a bill of sale. Dated nine years ago. For one clerk, Noor Iversen, purchased — there was no other word — from a debtor's prison in Ashharbor, price listed as *one favor, value undisclosed.*

"You bought me," he said.

"I *rescued* you. The distinction matters to people with feelings." She tapped the page. "Every year on the anniversary, I could have torn that up. Every year I didn't. Do you know why?"

"Because it was worth more intact."

"Because *you* were worth more intact." For just a moment something moved behind her eyes that was not commerce — something older and tireder. "Do you imagine I enjoyed sitting in that room last night while a woman whose order burned my grandmother's cousin sang hymns in my hallway? I hate the Choir, Noor. I hate what they did to this city. But hatred is not a business plan. Survival is. And survival right now requires that the thing she heard last night stop existing. Or stop being findable."

"So you're dismissing me."

"I'm offering you a bargain."

She rose and went to the wall panel behind her desk — the one Noor had always assumed hid a safe — and pressed three points in sequence. The panel swung open on darkness, and cold air breathed out of it, air that smelled of stone and deep water and something else, something animal, musk and fur and long confinement.

"There is a vault beneath this building," Corvane said. "Deeper than the cellars. Deeper than the flood-tunnels. Your grandmother's cousin wasn't the only thing the Choir ever took — sometimes things were taken *from* the Choir, or bought before they could be taken, or simply... fell into the market." She turned back to him. "Three weeks ago a trapper sold us a living asset. Wolf. Female. Old — older than any wolf has a right to be. Grey as ash, eyes like a winter harbor. She hasn't made a sound since she was brought in. Not a whimper, not a growl. The handlers say she's broken." Corvane's mouth curved. "But you and I both know that silence isn't always broken, Noor. Sometimes it's *waiting*."

His heart had begun to beat against his ribs like something caged. "What do you want me to do?"

"Appraise her. Tonight, after close. A buyer comes in four days — a buyer with deep pockets and a Choir seal on his letters of credit. I need a valuation. Condition, age, temperament, market price. I need it honest, because if I misprice her and the Choir finds out, they won't fine me, they'll *dissolve* me." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from Corvane was more frightening than anger. "And you will do it well, because here is the other half of the bargain: you appraise the wolf, and I tear up that bill of sale. Free and clear. And when Sera Voss comes back — and she'll come back, she's already circling — I will tell her that whatever she heard in this house was the pipes. Old buildings sing. I'm very good at telling stories buyers want to hear."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I put that bill of sale on this desk in front of her myself, and I tell her I've been harboring a beast-speaker for nine years without knowing, and my tears will be genuine, and my guild will survive, and you—" she shrugged, a small economical motion "—you'll burn. Quietly. They don't make spectacles of clerks."

The lamp flame steadied itself. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, Noor felt the mice go still, all of them at once, the way animals do when they sense a predator passing above.

He thought about running. There were flood-tunnels; he'd mapped them in his head years ago out of habit. He thought about the marshes, the exile colonies, the stories of drowned uprisings. He thought about the Codex of Ashes upstairs in the sealed archive, with tonight's date written in it a century ago, and his own name beside the word *Wolf* — a fact he had told absolutely no one, which now felt like the last coin in his pocket.

And he thought about a grey wolf in a dark vault who hadn't made a sound in three weeks, waiting.

"All right," he said.

Corvane studied him for a moment, then nodded once, briskly, business concluded. She tore the bill of sale in half, then in half again, and dropped the pieces into the cold hearth beside her desk, where they curled and blackened.

"Free and clear," she said. "See how easy honesty is, when the price is finally stated?" She retrieved a heavy iron key from the panel's inner shelf and held it out. "Third door past the cistern. Don't touch her through the bars — she bites through fear and dignity both, and I'm told her teeth are still excellent."

Noor took the key. It was colder than the room deserved.

"One more thing," Corvane added, as he reached the door. "Whatever you hear down there — however she answers you — remember that in four days she belongs to the Choir. Whatever kindness you're planning, price it first. Sentiment is the one commodity this guild doesn't stock."

He went down.

Down past the cellars where the wine racks stood like organ pipes. Down past the cistern, where black water reflected the lantern in a long trembling line. Down a corridor that narrowed until the stone on either side was older than the city above it, older than the guild, older perhaps than the law that had outlawed his voice.

The third door was iron banded with bronze, and it bore a lock built for something larger than wolves.

Noor fit the key, turned it, and pushed.

The dark inside exhaled. Straw. Stone. Cold. And then, from the far corner, from the deep shadow where the lantern light gave up and died, two eyes opened — pale, patient, winter-harbor grey — and fixed on him with an attention so complete it felt like being read aloud.

Noor stood in the doorway with the lantern shaking slightly in his hand.

"Hello," he said, in the human tongue, because he was still afraid.

And in the silence that followed, he heard — not with his ears, not through the walls, but directly, the way one mind touches another across a distance too small for sound — an answer, dry and old and amused, in the grammar of beasts that no human law had ever managed to outlaw:

*Took you long enough, little clerk. I've been reading your name in the dark for a hundred years.*
