## 6. The Wolf in the Vault

The stairs down were older than the guildhouse above them.

Noor had worked in Vellum House for nine years and never once been asked to descend them. Everyone knew there was a vault beneath the guild — Mistress Corvane spoke of it the way merchants speak of weather, casually, as a fact of the trade — but no clerk ever went down. The ledgers said *vault, subterranean, restricted* and nothing else. Noor had catalogued a thousand impossible things: feathers that fell upward, letters addressed to the dead, a jar of last winter's thunder. None of it had prepared him for a staircase.

Corvane walked ahead with a lantern, her keys making a sound like slow rain. She had not spoken since they left her office. That was its own kind of speech.

"Nine steps per landing," she said finally. "Forty landings. I counted them once, when I was younger than you are now."

"Why am I going down?"

"Because I asked you to appraise an asset," she said, "and this asset cannot be brought up to you."

The air changed at the twentieth landing. It grew colder, then warmer, then carried something animal underneath — musk and straw and old stone breathing. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse through a wall, Noor felt the thing he always felt near caged creatures: a voice. But where the storm-hawk's whisper had been a thin wire of sound, this was a chord. Deep, layered, patient.

He stopped on the landing.

"Keep walking," Corvane said, not turning.

"It's awake."

"Of course it's awake. It has been awake for a very long time." She lifted the lantern higher. "That is rather the point of it."

* * *

The vault was not what he expected. He had imagined iron and darkness, chains and damp. Instead the lowest chamber was wide and dry, floored with sand over old flagstones, lit by a shaft of pale light from some mirrored channel far above — a trick of architecture, daylight bent down like water poured into a well. The light fell across the center of the room, and in the center of the room stood a cage.

Not a small one. A cage built like a chapel, iron ribs rising into arches, tall enough that the creature inside could almost stand at full height without touching the roof. Almost. Whoever built it had measured a wolf and then subtracted exactly enough.

She was grey and white and enormous, and she was watching him before he saw her eyes find him — no, she had been watching since the stairs. Her gaze had weight. It settled on his chest like a hand.

"Noor Iversen," Corvane said, "meet the asset. Asset — meet my ledger-keeper. Value her honestly. You know how I hate flattery in the books."

Then she left. The lantern-light shrank up the stairs and around the turns, and Noor stood alone in the bent daylight with a wolf who looked at him the way scholars look at a text they wrote.

"You're afraid," she said.

Her voice arrived inside his skull the way voices did when he listened properly — not through ears, but through the place behind his sternum where his gift lived. Except no voice had ever been like this. The rats chattered; the gulls complained; the hawk had whispered. This voice was a cathedral of a voice. It filled the vault.

"I'm not afraid," Noor said. Which was a lie, and she knew it, because her ear twitched.

"Liar. Good. Honest liars are the only ones worth talking to." She paced two steps along the bars, favoring her left foreleg — old injury, badly set, decades ago by the look of the scar. "You hear me. Not the way the jailers hear me — they hear nothing, they think I'm mute, they write *does not vocalize* in their little reports. You hear me the way I mean to be heard." She sat, folding her ruined leg beneath her. "Say something back. In the old grammar. I want to hear whether you butcher it."

"I don't know what you—"

"Your mouth says one thing and your blood says another, boy. Speak."

And Noor, who had spent his whole life keeping his gift folded small and silent as a knife in a boot, heard himself answer in the way beasts spoke to each other — not words, but meaning shaped into sound, a low tone in his chest that said: *I hear you. I mean no harm. I am listening.*

The sound embarrassed him the moment it left his body. It had been years since he'd used that register. It had been years since anyone alive could have heard it and understood.

Ysolt closed her eyes.

"Yes," she said softly. "There you are."

* * *

He should have gone straight to work. Appraise the asset: species, age, condition, provenance, market value. That was the job. That was the whole shape of his life — reduce the impossible to a column of figures, keep the numbers honest so his secret could stay hidden inside them.

Instead he found himself asking, "What's your name?"

"Ysolt."

"How long have you been down here?"

"Longer than your guild has kept honest books. Shorter than it matters." She tilted her head. "You didn't come down here to make conversation. You came with a book under your coat. I can smell the vellum. Old vellum. Ash-cured."

His hand went to his chest before he could stop it. The Codex of Ashes rode against his ribs where he'd hidden it that morning, wrapped in oilcloth, because he could not leave it in the catalogue room and could not bring himself to show it to anyone and could not, apparently, stop carrying it.

"Sit down," Ysolt said. "Take it out. Read me the last entry."

"The last entry is tonight's date."

"I know what it is. I wrote it."

"You wrote—" Noor laughed, once, without humor. "It's a century old. The ink is older than my grandmother's grave."

"Ink keeps. Wolves keep better." She rose and came to the bars, close enough that he could see her eyes clearly for the first time — pale gold, ringed with grey, the eyes of something that had watched a great deal and forgotten none of it. "A century ago I dictated prophecies to a man named Aldric Vane. He had a gift like yours and half your sense. His hand, my words. We wrote the Codex together — his script, my foresight. Half those pages are mine, Noor Iversen. Every prophecy about the pact, every line about the Wolf. Mine."

"That's impossible."

"So is a ledger that trades in bottled thunder, but here we both are." Her muzzle wrinkled, not quite a snarl — something more tired. "Show me the page. Show me the handwriting. If Aldric is dead, then seeing his hand again is worth the indignity of your doubt."

Noor unwrapped the Codex with hands that would not quite stay steady. He turned to the final entry and held it up to the bent light.

Ysolt read it for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its irony, the cathedral emptied down to one candle.

"That's his hand," she said. "He pressed too hard on his descenders when he was frightened. Look — every *g*, every *y*. He was frightened when he wrote this." A pause. "I told him what to write, and even then he was frightened. Because he understood it before you do."

"Understood what?"

"Read it aloud."

"*On the night the Choir seals the city,*" Noor read, "*the Wolf will come down from the paper world. He will carry the pact in his blood and the debt in his name. He will speak, or everything silenced will speak instead.*" His own voice sounded strange in the vault, flattened by stone. Below the words, in smaller script, a single name: *N. Iversen.*

"And?" Ysolt said. "What does the entry say after the name?"

He looked. He had read this page forty times and he had never once been able to make himself read past the name. Now he made himself.

"*He will refuse the name three times.*"

The silence afterward was total. Even the drip of water somewhere in the deep had stopped, or Noor had stopped hearing it.

"One," Ysolt said.

"What?"

"That was one." She settled back on her haunches, and something almost gentle entered the vastness of her voice. "You've already refused twice today, if it helps. Once to yourself, on the stairs. Once just now, when your heart said *not me* loud enough that I heard it through six feet of iron. But aloud, in front of a witness — that was the first."

"This is a game to you."

"Nothing is a game. Games end." She pressed her scarred foreleg against the bars. "I have been in this cage for sixty years, boy. Before that I ran messages between the packs and the last free beast-speakers during the wars. I bargained my voice away with the rest of them, and then I broke the bargain, and this is what breaking it costs. Do you imagine I stayed sane down here by playing games? I stayed sane by waiting. Prophecy is patience with teeth."

"I'm not your Wolf. I'm a clerk. I balance books. I traded a storm-hawk's freedom for a shipping favor on Tuesday — that's the largest thing I've ever done with my life."

"Good," Ysolt said. "Keep doing things that size. The pact doesn't need heroes. It needs someone honest enough to write down what things actually cost." Her eyes held his. "That's why it's you. You thought the name chose you because you're special. It chose you because you're a keeper of accounts. Someone has to tell the truth about the debt, and everyone else in this city is either lying or singing."

* * *

He stayed until the bent daylight shifted — hours, maybe longer. They talked in fragments. She asked questions about the city above, about the harbor, about how many gulls nested on the customs house (he didn't know; she seemed disappointed). He asked what the pact said, and she said, "Not yet. First you have to stop pretending you aren't the one it's written to." He asked who put her in the cage, and she smiled with all her teeth and said, "Ask your mistress sometime why the Vellum Guild's founding charter has a wolf's signature on it."

When he finally rose to go, his legs were stiff and his mind was full of loose pages.

"At the door," Ysolt called, "one more thing."

He turned.

"The date in the Codex. Tonight's date. You checked it against three calendars, didn't you? You checked it four times."

"How did you—"

"Because I would have." She laid her chin on her paws. "Go home, Noor Iversen. Count your dates. And when the Choir comes to sing — and they are coming, soon, I can feel their hymns in my bones the way you feel weather — remember that you met me first. Remember that whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten you with, there is a wolf under this city who waited a hundred years for you specifically."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"No," she agreed. "But you took the book out of your coat when I asked. That's how every pact begins — with someone showing what they were hiding."

He climbed the forty landings with the Codex against his chest, and behind him, fading with every step, he could feel her attention still resting on him like a hand between his shoulder blades — patient, ancient, absolutely certain.

At the top, Corvane was waiting with his appraisal form and a pen.

"Well?"

Noor looked down at the blank column marked *VALUE*. Everything in him wanted to write *priceless* and everything professional wanted to write *negligible*. He wrote: *Asset is conversant, aged, injured, and knows things about this guild's history that are not in any ledger I have access to. Recommend no sale until provenance is established.*

Corvane read it twice. Something flickered across her face — he'd never seen it before and couldn't name it now.

"Interesting," she said. "You'll want to establish provenance, then. Come to my office tomorrow. Bring nothing."

She walked away, and Noor stood in the corridor holding his pen, understanding suddenly and completely that he had just written something into the record that could not be unwritten — and that somewhere below the city, a wolf was counting.
