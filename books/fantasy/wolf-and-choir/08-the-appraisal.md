## 8. The Appraisal

Corvane called it *an opportunity*.

She said it the way she said everything — as though the word itself were coin, and saying it made it legal tender. "An opportunity, Iversen. Do you know how long the Guild has waited for a buyer of this caliber to walk through our doors?"

I knew. I kept her books. I knew exactly what caliber of buyer walked out of them too, which was most of them, and what they paid, and what she'd paid to keep the lamps lit in Vellum House for another quarter. The numbers didn't lie because numbers couldn't afford to. That was the one honest language left in the building, and I was its last fluent speaker.

"The Choir," I said. Not a question. I'd heard them at the harbor gates since dawn — that low, patient humming that got into the mortar of the city like damp. Three days since the midnight hymn, and the song hadn't stopped so much as changed key. It lived in the walls now. Some nights I could feel it pressing on the back of my teeth.

"An emissary." Corvane smoothed the front of her coat with both hands, a gesture I had catalogued over six years of employment: she did it when she was about to spend someone. "Inquisitor Second Class. Traveling under a trade delegation's flag, which means we are expected to be flattered and discreet in that order."

"And the asset?"

"Is a wolf."

The word sat between us. Neither of us reached for it.

"You want me to value her," I said.

"I want you to value her *honestly*." Corvane's eyes were pale and quick, like gulls deciding. "That is what you're for, Iversen. The Choir can smell a padded ledger from across a room — it reads like a lie, and lies read like gifts to them. You will inspect the asset, assess her condition, provenance, age, temperament, and training, and you will write the appraisal in your own hand, in the ledger, where the Inquisitor can watch you do it. And then you will smile, because you are very good at smiling lately, have you noticed? You've been practicing."

I hadn't noticed. Apparently I had.

"And if the price is honest," I said, "it will be enormous."

"It will be *justified*. There is a difference, and the difference is your job." She turned to the window. Below, in the counting yard, guildsmen were hauling trestles and hanging lanterns for an evening viewing, and beyond the wall the harbor lay flat and gray, and somewhere out on that gray water the Choir's barge sat at anchor like a closed hymnal. "You've been strange since the vault, Iversen. Distracted. Misfiling. Don't argue — Tam covers for you badly and I read everything twice. So consider this your correction. You will appraise the wolf, the Choir will buy her or not, and either way the Guild survives the season. Am I understood?"

Every instinct I owned told me to say yes. Six years of instincts said: *she has known about you for years and she has not yet sold you, therefore she will not sell you today.* But there was a newer instinct, three days old, that had been born in the dark of the deepest vault with Ysolt's breath on my face, and it said something else entirely. It said: *she has known about you for years and has simply been waiting for a buyer worth the risk.*

"Yes, Mistress," I said, and went to sharpen my pen.

* * *

They brought Ysolt up from the vault at dusk, in a wagon cage draped with sailcloth, through the yard where the lanterns were already lit, and the first thing I noticed was that the beasts of the city had gone quiet.

Not silent — I would have welcomed silent. Quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone dangerous enters it. The gulls that normally screamed over the counting-house roof were standing in ranks along the gutter, heads turned toward the yard. Rats lined the drainage channel in a gray ribbon, motionless. Even the cart-horse in the far stall had stopped chewing and stood with its ears fixed forward, trembling very slightly, like a man hearing his own name spoken by a stranger across a crowded hall.

They had all come to see her. Or to be near her. Or — and this thought arrived cold and complete, the way true things do — to be *counted*. Because something in them knew what tonight was. Something old in their blood understood that a wolf was about to be priced, and pricing is the oldest human sacrament, older than any pact, and they had come to witness it the way the devout witness an execution.

Ysolt stepped down from the wagon cage into the torchlight, and the yard exhaled.

She was magnificent and I hated that I thought so, because magnificence is exactly what gets a creature valued. She moved like weather deciding where to go next. Her coat carried the gray of deep water. Her eyes found mine across the yard — one flick, less than a heartbeat — and in that flick there was nothing at all. No recognition. No plea. A hundred years old and patient as arithmetic, she gave me a stranger's face, and I understood the instruction in it: *we have never met. Look at me the way they expect.*

So I did what I was for.

I walked her perimeter with my ledger open. I noted the condition of her claws, the set of her hips, the old scarring along her left flank that I already knew the story of — the iron collar of the war years, the one she wore for a decade and described to me once in the grammar of the old pact, a grammar that has no word for *collar* and so describes it as *a ring of the-not-sky that never lifts*. I measured her height against a marked stave while a guildsman held the chain, and she stood perfectly still for me, and stillness from her cost more than any scream would have.

"Temperament?" said a voice at my shoulder.

Inquisitor Sera Voss was smaller than I remembered from the midnight hymn, and younger, with ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing had quite defeated. That detail undid me a little. Ink-stained fingers. She kept ledgers too. We were, in some horrible administrative sense, colleagues.

"Cautious," I said. "Intelligent. She watches hands, not faces."

"Why hands?"

"Because hands hold things." I wrote it down as I said it, and watched her watch me write, and felt the double life pull tight around my throat like a well-fitted collar. Every word I put in the ledger was honest — that was the trap of it, that was the craftsmanship of the trap. Honest entries. Accurate weights. A fair description of a magnificent animal. Nothing in the ledger mentioned that I had sat with her in the dark and heard her say my name in a tongue that predates names.

"A fair assessment," Voss said. "You know beasts, Master...?"

"Iversen. Ledger-keeper."

"Ledger-keeper." She tried the word like a wine she wasn't sure about. "And what does a ledger-keeper charge for a century-old wolf?"

I looked at the page. This was the moment Corvane meant when she said *opportunity*: the number. Get it wrong high, and the Choir walked, and Voss started pulling threads out of curiosity, and threads lead to frays, and frays lead to fires. Get it wrong low, and the sale happened fast — fast being the only kind of escape plan that had ever worked in the history of escape plans — but fast also meant the wolf left tonight, in a wagon cage, onto a barge, into the Choir's keeping, and every plan I had half-built in three sleepless nights died at the dockside.

Get it right, and we bought time. Time was the only currency I had left. Time was going to cost me everything.

I did the arithmetic honestly, because that was my gift, the one nobody outlawed: her age against her rarity, her provenance — seized, undocumented, which lowered her legally and raised her mystically — her condition, the market among collectors of the impossible, the Choir's own institutional hunger for anything that might sing back. I wrote the number. It was enormous. It was justified. The difference was my job.

Voss read it upside down without asking permission, which told me something about her. "High."

"Honest."

"Honesty is a style," she said mildly. "Like any style, it can be affected." She straightened, and her eyes went past me to the wolf, and for one moment something moved behind her face that I recognized, horribly, because I saw it in my own mirror: the look of a person listening to something no one else can hear. "She's watching your hands, Master Iversen."

"She watches everyone's hands."

"No," Voss said. "She's watching *yours*."

* * *

The viewing proper began after supper. Corvane staged it beautifully — candles enough to make the yard feel like a church, Ysolt displayed on a raised dais of grain pallets with the sailcloth pulled back, wine circulated among the buyers like gossip. There were four of them: a furrier with soft predatory hands; a menagerie agent counting coins under the table with his thumb; a fat collector of esoterica who kept saying *remarkable* in a voice like a hinge. And Voss, who bought nothing and missed nothing, circulating with her notebook and her stained fingers, asking each guest cheerful questions that were actually knives.

I stood at the ledger table and recorded bids.

That was my penance, and Corvane knew it, and maybe that was part of the correction too: make the boy write it down. The furrier opened at forty marks. The collector went fifty-five. The menagerie agent, sensing prestige rather than profit, climbed to seventy before running aground. I entered each figure in my fair round hand, and each stroke of the pen felt like swallowing a coin — cold, hard, wrong-sized. Somewhere behind my ribs a voice I refused to listen to kept repeating the only question that mattered: *how much time does seventy marks buy, and is it enough?*

It would have been easier if Ysolt had struggled. If she'd thrown herself at the bars I could have hated the buyers cleanly. Instead she sat in the torchlight like a monument, letting herself be valued, and once — only once, when the collector leaned close to admire her teeth — her eyes slid sideways and found mine, and she spoke. Not aloud. Through the old channel, the one that feels less like sound and more like remembering:

*Write me expensive, little scribe.*

*I am writing you honestly,* I answered, hands steady on the pen, face empty. Three days of practice and we could almost converse without my pulse giving us away. Almost.

*Honest is what I asked for,* she said. *A century ago I asked a room full of men for honesty and they gave me a treaty. Mind the difference.*

*There is a plan.*

*There is a boy with a plan. Those are different things also.* But something in the old channel shifted — amusement, dry as ash, or maybe just acknowledgment — and then the collector wanted her paw examined and she turned away to be magnificent for him.

Seventy marks became eighty. Eighty became ninety-five. Voss did not bid, but she stood near Voss's silence the way sailors stand near weather, and I understood with growing cold that the Inquisitor wasn't here to buy. She was here to establish a baseline. Whatever the Guild sold this wolf for, Voss now had a documented market value, a paper trail, a ledger entry in my own hand — and paper trails are how hunters hunt when they're patient. She was buying *us*, in installments, and paying nothing.

At the interval Corvane drew me aside behind the candle table, wine in hand, smiling for the yard.

"Well?"

"Ninety-five. The Choir hasn't bid."

"The Choir doesn't bid at auctions. The Choir makes offers the auction cannot refuse." She sipped. "After the third bid, Iversen, you'll close the book and present it. Standard theater. Then the Inquisitor names her figure privately, tomorrow, and we negotiate, and either way the Guild eats this winter." Her eyes flicked to mine, sharp as a clerk's. "You're doing well. The honest face suits you better than the guilty one. Keep wearing it."

Back at the table, the fat collector raised to a hundred and ten, and the furrier shook his head, and the menagerie agent had discovered the bottom of his purse, and I closed the ledger with a click that sounded, in the hushed yard, like a small bone breaking.

*Enough time,* I told myself. *Ninety-five marks of time. Tomorrow's negotiation, the day after's paperwork, a week of transfer arrangements — a caged wolf moves slowly, cages move slower, barges need manifests—*

A week. Maybe. I had been planning for a week's worth of maybe, sleeping two hours a night, sketching routes through the flooded undercity with Tam's help, memorizing tide tables. A week of maybes balanced against a hundred years of patience, and somewhere in that equation a woman with ink-stained fingers was writing everything down.

* * *

The yard emptied by degrees. Lanterns guttered. Guildsmen struck the dais and folded the sailcloth, and the buyers' carriages ground away through the gate one by one, and the city's beasts — the ranked gulls, the gray ribbon of rats, the trembling horse — watched the whole dismantling with the fixed attention of a congregation after the sermon.

Ysolt was to go back down to the vault at moonrise. They wheeled the cage wagon to the yard's dark end to wait for the vault crew, and for a few minutes she was unattended, and I crossed the yard with my ledger under my arm like a man doing final accounts, which was even true.

I stood at the bars. Close enough that no one watching would think it strange — the keeper checking his asset one last time.

"Tomorrow," I said under my breath, "they'll make an offer the auction can't refuse."

*Then tomorrow we are sold,* she said, unhurried. *Good. Sold things travel.*

"Not to the Choir. Not on that barge."

*Little scribe.* Her breath fogged the bar an inch from my hand. *You cannot falsify your way out of a city full of listeners. I have watched cleverer men than you try, and I attended their executions, and I took notes — it is a habit of mine, taking notes. What is your week of maybes worth against a hymn that hears a heartbeat change key?*

"Then tell me a better plan."

For a moment the great head turned, and the torchlight caught her eyes, and what was in them was not encouragement. It was the look of someone checking a calculation made a century ago and finding, reluctantly, that it still holds.

*Yes,* she said. *Here is the better plan. When they come to move me — whenever they come, tonight or tomorrow or the day the deal closes — the vault crew will be one man short. Tam will be sick. He is already arranging to be sick; he guesses too much, your Tam, but he owes the Guild nine marks and guilt is cheaper than gold. And the man who takes his place at the vault door will be a man who owes you his sister's medicine, and he will turn his back at the wrong moment, and between one wrong moment and the next, the cage will find itself unlocked.*

My mouth went dry. "You've been planning this since before I came to the vault."

*I told you,* Ysolt said. *Half the prophecies are mine. Prophecy is only planning with good handwriting.* She settled back from the bars, giving me the stranger's face again, because boots were crossing the yard — two vaultmen and the wagon driver, coming to take her down into the dark. *One thing remains, and it must be yours, not mine. The ledger entry. Tonight, before you sleep, you will amend the appraisal page.*

"Amend it how?"

*You will know how,* she said. *You are the honest one. That is why I chose you a hundred years ago, little scribe, before you were born — I chose the Wolf, and the Wolf keeps honest books.* And her eyes held mine through the last sentence, and there was nothing amused in them at all. *Do it tonight. Because tomorrow morning, when the Inquisitor opens your ledger to check your figures, the entry she finds must already be wrong.*

The wagon wheels groaned; the cage rolled toward the vault stairs; the ranked gulls on the roofline lifted all at once, wheeling, crying, settling again.

I stood in the emptying yard with the ledger under my arm, feeling the moral weight of it settle into my bones like the Choir's hymn had settled into the walls: I had spent three days learning to write honestly in service of a lie, and now I was being asked to corrupt the one clean thing I had left — the books themselves, my own fair hand, the last language I trusted.

Tam would guess. Voss would read. Corvane read everything twice.

And somewhere below the yard, down at the bottom of the deepest stair, a hundred-year-old wolf was already asleep, dreaming in good handwriting, while I climbed back to my desk to decide exactly how a careful man begins to forge his own soul — one amended line at a time.
