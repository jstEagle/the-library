## 9. Sera Voss Hears Something

The wolf's voice was exactly as advertised.

Sera Voss stood at the back of the appraisal chamber with her hands folded behind her and let the sound wash over her, cataloguing it the way she had been trained to catalogue everything: by pitch, by weight, by the shape of the silence it left behind. The creature was magnificent in the way that only caged things are magnificent — a gray she-wolf with frost at her muzzle, chained to an iron floor plate that hummed faintly with warding. She sat perfectly still while the buyers circled her, but she was not silent. She never stopped speaking. That was the thing about beasts who had bargained their voices away a century ago: what remained was not silence but something quieter than silence, a subsonic grief that ordinary humans could not hear and therefore called nothing.

Sera heard it. It was her gift, though *gift* was the wrong word for it — the Choir preferred *instrument*, and the truth was closer to *wound*. Her left ear had been opened when she was nine years old, in the Choir's seminary at Vail, in a ceremony they called the Unsealing and she privately called the cutting. Since then she had heard the world's second register: the prayers of dogs, the complaints of horses, the long liturgical boredom of gulls. And beneath all of it, when she listened hard, the residue of the stolen voices — a hundred years of muffled screaming pressed down under the Pact like flowers in a book.

The wolf was louder than most. The wolf was practically shouting.

"Note the dentition," said Mistress Corvane, gliding between the buyers with a ledger tucked under one arm like a psalter. "Complete, unbroken. Note also the coat — you'll find no mange, no scarring of consequence. This animal has been maintained."

*Maintained.* Sera almost smiled. Only a guild-trader would use the vocabulary of upholstery for a living relic.

One of the buyers, a narrow man with the gray robes and gray face of a Choir quartermaster, leaned close to the cage. "Does it speak?"

"It understands," Corvane said smoothly. "Speaking is another matter. The old stock lost the knack, as I'm sure Your Reverence knows better than I."

The quartermaster did know. Sera watched him know it — the small tightening around his eyes, the reflexive touch of two fingers to the ear he'd had opened too. But he leaned in anyway, and made a low humming note in his throat, a probe-tone, third harmonic, the kind the Choir used to make hidden things flinch. Standard practice. If there were any latent gifts in the room — any unregistered listeners, any beast-speakers — the tone would catch them the way a struck bell catches dust.

Sera felt the probe pass through the chamber like a hand through water.

And then she heard something answer.

It came from the direction of the guild's clerical desks — a row of them against the far wall, where three scribes worked with their heads bent over valuation sheets, quills scratching. One of them, a lean young man with ink-stained fingers and the careful posture of someone trying very hard to be furniture, had flinched. Not visibly. Sera doubted anyone else in the room had noticed. But she had heard his breath change — one held beat, half a second, the involuntary hitch of a body suppressing a response — and in that same half-second, through her opened ear, she had caught a thread of something impossible.

A voice answering the wolf.

Not aloud. Not in any register the quartermaster's probe could reach. It had been underneath, subvocal, shaped in the old grammar — the grammar the Choir had spent a century burning out of the world. A single phrase, maybe two. She couldn't reconstruct the words; it had been too quiet, too fast, gone before her mind could close around it. Like hearing someone whisper your name across a crowded cathedral and turning to find no one looking at you.

But the direction had been clear. And the direction had been a clerk.

"—the reserve price," Corvane was saying, "reflects the animal's provenance. We have documentation—"

Sera stopped listening. She moved along the wall, unhurried, a woman admiring the vault's stonework, and let her gaze travel across the clerks with the mild professional interest of someone checking arithmetic. Three scribes. An older woman with a widow's hump, deaf in the way most people were deaf, harmless. A boy of perhaps sixteen, chewing his quill, bored past the point of fear. And the young man — dark-haired, precise, ink to the second knuckle — whose breathing had gone carefully, deliberately even, which was itself a confession. Ordinary men breathed. Only people hiding something breathed *on purpose*.

His nameplate said N. IVERSEN, LEDGER-KEEPER.

She filed it. She was very good at filing things.

* * *

Afterward, in the guest cell the guild provided — clean, warm, insultingly comfortable — Sera sat on the edge of the cot and reconstructed the moment the way she'd been taught to reconstruct hymns: note by note.

The wolf had been singing her grief, as wolves did. That was expected. The Choir had purchased dozens of the old stock over the years, mostly for study, and their voices all ran to the same themes — loss, chains, the names of dead packmates. Grief was noise. Noise was safe.

What wasn't safe was an answer. Because the Pact of Silence ran both ways, and the human side of it had been enforced with fire for a hundred years. No living human should have been able to speak the old grammar. The last confirmed beast-speaker had burned in Vail eleven years ago — Sera had attended the burning, had stood in formation with the other acolytes while the Choir sang the sealing hymn, and she remembered thinking even then, even at nineteen, that the man had looked less like a monster than like someone reading aloud from a book everyone else pretended didn't exist.

Someone was reading aloud again.

She took out her field journal — plain vellum, unmarked cover, the Choir's discipline of documentation applied to herself — and wrote, in her own cipher:

*Appraisal, Vellum Guild, 14th hour. Subject: female lupine, aged (est.) beyond natural span. Voice active, high amplitude. Probe-tone deployed by Qrmstr. Havel. Response detected: one (1) human voice, subvocal, old grammar, fragmentary. Source: clerical staff. Identity uncertain. Confidence: high.*

Then she crossed out *identity uncertain* and wrote beneath it: *Identity probable. N. Iversen, ledger-keeper.*

Not because she was certain. Because certainty was a luxury the Choir paid for and patience got for free. She would be certain later, after the ledgers told her what they always told her.

Because that was the other thing — the thing that had brought her to this city in the first place, riding ahead of the main Choir detachment with sealed orders and an open ear. For six months, the central archive in Vail had been receiving anomalies from the Vellum Guild's exported records: valuations that didn't reconcile, transfers that canceled themselves, a paper trail so clean it squeaked. Someone at Vellum House was keeping books the way a surgeon keeps a wound — dressed, monitored, kept from festering. Honest fraud, if such a thing existed. Fraud with a conscience.

Fraud was easy to find. Conscience was easier. Both left marks in ink.

Sera closed her journal and lay back on the cot and listened, because listening cost her nothing and told her everything. Through the floor she could hear the guild settling into its nighttime metabolism: the tick of the great case clock, the sigh of the boiler, rats moving in the walls with the brisk purpose of small creditors. And somewhere below — deep below, in the vault levels — the wolf, still singing. Slower now. A lullaby, almost. Or a message.

She let herself hear it fully, the way the Choir had trained her to, opening the wound of her ear until the whole building's second register poured in.

And there — faint, threading beneath the wolf's voice like a second instrument joining a solo — the human voice again. Steady now. Patient. Speaking the old grammar with the fluency of long habit.

*Iversen,* she thought. *You are answering her.*

She should have reported it immediately. That was procedure; that was doctrine; that was the hymn she'd sworn at her Unsealing. Instead she lay in the dark and listened, and found — to her considerable professional discomfort — that the sound was beautiful. The wolf and the man, conversing across iron and law, in the language the whole world had agreed to forget. It sounded like the first page of a very old book. It sounded like something that had been true before the Choir decided otherwise.

She shut her ear. Sat up. Lit the candle.

Sentiment was a leak, and leaks sank ships, and she had not survived eleven years in the Inquisitors' Choir by being anything but watertight. Tomorrow she would request access to the guild's ledgers — politely, as a buyer verifying provenance, with none of the apparatus of an investigation. Corvane would grant it, because Corvane granted everything that could be granted cheaply. And somewhere in those columns of figures, in the margins and the misfilings and the entries that reconciled too perfectly, she would find the handwriting of the voice she'd heard tonight.

Every liar has a hand. Every hand has a habit. She had once convicted a smuggler in Vail on the angle of his serifs alone.

Sera trimmed the wick, opened her journal one last time, and added a final line to the day's entry:

*Begin audit. Follow the ink.*

In the walls above her head, faint and unhurried, a rat paused mid-scuttle — and listened back.
