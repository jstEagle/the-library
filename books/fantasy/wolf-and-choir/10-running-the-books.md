## 10. Running the Books

The trouble with falsifying a ledger is that a ledger is a promise made of ink.

Every entry in the Vellum Guild's books was a small vow: *this happened, at this hour, for this price, witnessed by this hand.* Mistress Corvane liked to say the guild traded in impossible things, but the ledgers themselves were the most impossible thing in the building — a hundred years of transactions recorded so cleanly that an auditor could reconstruct the guild's entire soul from them in a single night. Which was precisely what Sera Voss intended to do.

I had learned to keep books the way other people learned to lie: thoroughly, and with a straight face. Now I had to unlearn it in reverse. Somewhere between the appraisal and tonight, Ysolt's transfer had to vanish — not be hidden, because hidden things could be found, but *unhappen*. The wolf would move from the deep vault to the river barge to the marsh road without ever appearing on paper to have moved at all.

Ink, I was discovering, could be a weapon or a confession. Sometimes both in the same line.

* * *

I started where all fraud starts: with the boring parts.

Ysolt's sale to the Choir's buyer was scheduled in three days. The bill of sale existed as a draft in Corvane's own hand — which meant I couldn't touch it without her noticing, and I couldn't leave it intact without the Choir tracing the wolf directly into my hands. So I did what any bookkeeper does with an inconvenient fact. I buried it in context.

A guild like ours never sold one thing. It sold *lots* — bundles of assets moved together so that no single item carried its own history. I began drafting a transfer manifest: eleven items leaving Vellum House by barge, bound for a warehouse upriver that the guild used for goods too strange to store in the city proper. A crate of storm-glass. Two rolls of vellum cured from something the manifest politely declined to name. A locked strongbox of Choir-confiscated charms awaiting resale. And, listed ninth of eleven, under the description "livestock, aged, one head" — Ysolt.

Nine of eleven. Deliberately forgettable. Auditors read firsts and lasts; middles were where truth went to sleep.

The problem was the ninth item itself. "Livestock, aged, one head" was a lie, and lies in a ledger have a smell. Sera Voss had spent her career smelling them. What I needed wasn't a false description — it was a true one that pointed nowhere. I sat with the page for an hour before I found it:

*One (1) animal, provenance disputed, valuation pending second appraisal.*

Every word true. Every word useless. Provenance *was* disputed — Ysolt claimed to be a century old and the author of half a book of prophecies, and no assessor alive could confirm it. Valuation *was* pending; I was the second appraiser, and I intended to pend indefinitely. The entry was a door that looked like a wall.

I wrote it out in my steadiest hand, blotted it, and felt the particular nausea of a man who has just committed his first crime against paper. It is a different crime than theft. Theft takes a thing. Falsification takes the *record* of a thing, and the record is the only place most things are allowed to exist.

Then Tam knocked on the counting-room door, and I understood that the crime had only begun.

* * *

Tam Wicker was twenty-six years old, owed the guild four months' wages in advances he'd signed for against his father's debts, and possessed the two qualities I needed most: he was meticulous, and he was desperate. He also noticed things. That was the quality I feared.

"I've been reconciling the vault inventory," he said, setting a folio on my desk like a man delivering a verdict. "Against your appraisal sheets."

He didn't look up right away. When he did, his eyes were red-rimmed and steady, the steadiness of someone who has decided not to blink first.

"There's a wolf," he said. "Down in the deep vault. I've been carrying feed down twice a week since winter. Nobody told me what she was *for*." He paused. "And now there's a manifest with 'livestock, aged' on it, and the feed schedule says the animal eats venison, and Master Noor — livestock doesn't eat venison."

The candle between us guttered. I let it.

"What do you want, Tam?"

"That's the wrong question," he said. "The right question is what do *you* want, because you've been writing around something for three days. Your hand changes when you're lying. Did you know that? The descenders go shallow." He turned the manifest toward me and tapped the ninth line. "Here. And here. And here."

I had been caught by a clerk with good eyes and bad prospects, and there was exactly one move available, the same move Corvane had once made on me: tell him enough truth to make the silence expensive.

"The Choir is buying that animal," I said. "You've seen what they do to things that sing."

His throat worked. "My sister sang. In the cathedral choir, when we were young. Before the last purge." He looked at his hands. "They didn't burn her. They made her *listen* while they burned the choirmaster, and after that she never sang again. She's alive. She lives in Ashfall with her husband and she hums when she thinks nobody can hear, and it's the worst sound I know."

I hadn't expected grief to be the currency. But grief was always the currency, in this city; coin was just its receipt.

"I'm moving her off the books," I said. "Off the manifest, off the guild's hands, out of the Choir's reach. If it works, she was never here. If it fails, everyone who touched the paper burns with me."

"And you're telling me this why?"

"Because you've already guessed, and a man who guesses is more dangerous than a man who knows. A knower can be managed. A guesser invents." I slid the folio back across the desk. "Also because you reconcile the vault inventory, and next week someone will ask why the feed ledger shows a carnivore that the manifests say never existed. I need those pages to agree with each other. Or rather — I need them to disagree *correctly*."

Tam was quiet for a long time. Outside, the harbor gulls were making their evening argument with the tide, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through stone, I could feel the rats in the walls going still — listening the way they always listened when they smelled fear on me.

"If I do this," Tam said finally, "I want it in the open. Not the crime — the *reason*. Write me a note. Your hand, your seal if you have one, saying what you told me. Dated. If this goes wrong, I want the world to know I was lied *with*, not lying."

It was the most honest demand I'd ever heard in that room, and it terrified me, because a dated note in my hand was a confession waiting for a reader. But refusing it would cost me the only ally whose loyalty wasn't purchased.

I wrote the note. I sealed it with the little wax stamp Corvane had given me for internal memoranda — irony being free of charge in Vellum House — and I handed it over and watched him fold it into the lining of his coat like a man hiding a splinter of ice against his heart.

"Now teach me," he said. "How do you make a ledger lie?"

"You don't," I said. "You make it *stammer*."

* * *

Here is what I taught him, working through the night by three candles and a pot of chicory gone bitter from sitting:

A clean ledger convicts. Numbers that agree perfectly, columns that foot exactly, dates that march in order — that's not accuracy, that's *authorship*, and investigators know authorship when they see it. Real commerce is messy. Shipments arrive late. Weights get re-estimated. Clerks transpose digits and correct them badly. So the art of concealment is not to erase the mess but to *curate* it — to build a false record that fails audit in all the places a real record would fail, and passes it everywhere that matters.

We gave the ninth-of-eleven shipment a late arrival. We gave the barge master a fee negotiated verbally and entered two days after the fact, with a smudged correction. We split Ysolt's feed costs across three accounts — provisions, veterinary, sundries — so no single column grew suspiciously fat. Tam, who had kept the feed ledger honestly for a year, now had to keep it dishonestly *in the shape of honesty*, and he turned out to be a natural. Desperation, I was learning, is a kind of talent. It concentrates the mind the way fear concentrates a prey animal's hearing.

There was one moment, past midnight, when he stopped copying and looked at me with the ink still wet on his fingers.

"Do you hear that?" he whispered.

I listened. Under the floorboards, under the cellar, under the deep vault where Ysolt lay chained in the dark — a voice. Not words. A pressure, a low sustained tone like a bow drawn across a cello string made of night. She was singing to herself, down there, the way prisoners do, and the walls were carrying it up to us like a secret told through a pillow.

"She does that," I said. "When she thinks no one is listening."

"Is that what the Choir wants to buy?" Tam asked. "That?"

"No," I said. "They want to buy the fact that she still *can*."

He went back to copying. His hand shook for a page, then steadied. People are braver than their own estimate of themselves; they just need a task small enough to start with. Copying a column is a small enough task. Loyalty gets built out of tasks.

* * *

Toward dawn, the mistake happened, and it was mine.

I was drafting the cover entries — the fake correspondence between the guild and the upriver warehouse confirming space for "eleven lots" — when I wrote the date wrong. Not falsified. Wrong. I wrote the date of the *prophecy*. Tonight's date, the one inscribed a century ago beside my name, the one I had been trying not to think about since I found it, surfaced from under my skin and onto the page like a bruise rising.

I stared at it. Then I scraped it with a knife, sanded it, rewrote the correct date over the ghost of the old one — and knew, with a bookkeeper's certainty, that the ghost would still be legible under lamplight held at an angle. That was how Sera Voss worked. Angled light and patience.

"Scrapings show," Tam said quietly, watching me do it. He'd seen forged documents before; his father had dealt in them, which was how the debts started. "You want to fix a date, you don't scrape. You cut the whole leaf out and recopy the page, and you age the new paper with tea and a warm iron, and even then—" he shrugged. "Even then, a good examiner counts the leaves. A missing leaf is a wound. A scraped date is a scar. Scars at least look like they healed."

"Where did you learn that?"

"From watching my father hang for it," he said, without drama, the way you'd report weather. "The Inquisitors don't catch forgers by finding the forgery. They catch them by finding the *fear* in the handwriting. Fear makes people over-correct. Over-correction is a signature."

So we left the ghost. We left the scar. We built our stammers around it and hoped the examiner would read the whole sentence instead of the single wounded word.

By the time the windows went grey, the false structure stood complete: a shipment that had never been planned, documented in papers that looked tired and ordinary, moving a wolf who officially did not exist toward a warehouse that officially had room for her, on a barge whose master had been paid in advance and told nothing except the departure hour. Three days until the Choir's buyer arrived to purchase an animal the guild would claim had already been transferred. Two days until the barge sailed. One day of margin, which is to say: none.

Tam gathered his coat. At the door he stopped, and for a moment neither of us spoke, because we both knew what the night had made us. Accomplices. Co-signatories on a debt that could only be repaid in one currency.

"Master Noor," he said. "The note you wrote me. You dated it yesterday."

"Yes."

"Why yesterday?"

"Because if this goes wrong tomorrow," I said, "I wanted it to look like I trusted you before the danger, not after."

He considered that with the seriousness he brought to everything, then nodded slowly. "That's either very kind or very clever."

"In my experience," I said, "it's usually both or neither."

He left. I sat alone in the counting room with the ledgers, and the light came up slow and dirty over the harbor, and underneath the floorboards Ysolt's song faded as she slept, and I put my palm flat against the wood and felt for it anyway.

The rats came to the wainscot gap and looked at me with their black bead eyes, and one of them — the old scarred one I thought of as the Auditor — said, in the compressed grammar of its kind: *Counted. Counted again. They count too.*

I know, I told them. I know.

* * *

I should have gone to bed. Instead I did the thing that paranoia does: I reread my own work looking for the flaw, and found seven, and fixed four, and invented two new ones fixing them, and was deep in the fifth when the bell at the street door rang — early, wrong, insistent.

Through the counting-room window I saw the knock answered. Saw the visitor's cloak before I saw the face: grey wool, travel-stained, cut in the austere line of Choir issue. Not Sera Voss herself — too short, too young — but a junior examiner, one of the readers they send ahead of the reading, the way surgeons send probes.

She presented a document to the door-clerk. The clerk, poor man, bowed and scurried upstairs toward Corvane's study, and the examiner stepped inside Vellum House, dripping rain, and stood in our entrance hall looking around with the mild, terrible patience of a person cataloguing a room she intends to take apart.

On the hall table, in plain view, lay the current copy-book of outgoing correspondence.

Including, third from the top, a letter to the upriver warehouse confirming space for eleven lots.

She didn't pick it up. She didn't need to. She read it where it lay, top to bottom, unhurried, and then she took off her hood, folded her hands, and waited to be announced — a guest, patient as ink drying.

Upstairs, a door opened. Down in the deep vault, Ysolt went silent — fully silent, the silence of an animal that has heard a predator's footfall and decided not to exist.

And Tam, arriving for the morning shift, came through the street door behind her, saw her standing there, saw me watching from the stair, and did the bravest stupid thing I have ever witnessed: he walked past her, climbed to the counting room, and said, in a voice pitched to carry no further than my ear —

"She read the letter. All of it. What do we do now?"

What we did now, I told him, was the only thing left to falsifiers when the examiner arrives before the forgery is dry.

We ran the books faster.
