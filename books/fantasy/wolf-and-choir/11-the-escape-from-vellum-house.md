## 11. The Escape from Vellum House

We ran the books faster, and the books ran us.

By noon we had moved Ysolt three times on paper — sold to a buyer in Marrowgate, returned unsold, then consigned to a salt merchant who did not exist and whose fictional debts I had been cultivating for two weeks like a garden. Tam carried the false transfer slips down to the strong room with hands that only shook a little. Upstairs, Sera Voss read our ledger with one gloved finger tracing each line, and I stood at my desk doing sums that meant nothing, listening through the floorboards to the rats beneath the guildhouse, who told me everything.

*She has stopped believing the paper,* the oldest rat reported, in the flat gray grammar of its kind. *She reads with her ears now.*

That was the trouble with Inquisitors. You could forge ink. You could not forge silence.

I went down to the vault at the changing of the watch, when the corridor guards changed their boots for slippers and their vigilance for gossip. Ysolt was standing at the bars, and she did not look like a wolf being appraised anymore. She looked like a wolf who had done arithmetic.

"They will come tonight," she said. "Not to buy. To take."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her eyes were the color of old ice, and just as patient. "Then say what you are going to do, Noor Iversen, because I have watched men plan escapes for a century, and the ones who survive are the ones who can say it plainly."

"Tonight," I said, "when the tide turns and the undercity gates open to drain the flood channels, the vault lock fails. Old iron. A shame. You go out through the cistern way, up through the gull roost on Saltspire, and by morning you're over the harbor wall." I swallowed. "And I am gone before anyone knows the books were lies."

"And the clerk?"

"Tam comes with us."

Ysolt made a sound that was almost a laugh, if laughter were made of gravel. "You have burned your ledgers, bought your freedom with fiction, and now you propose to add a debt-ridden clerk to your cargo. The Wolf of the prophecies is a bookkeeper who cannot bear to leave an entry unfinished."

"It isn't sentiment," I said, and heard the lie even as it left me.

"No," she agreed. "It's worse. It's honesty. That's why the century chose you." She turned back into the dark of her cell. "Be at the lock before the tide. If you are late, I will break it myself, and then everyone in this city will know exactly what a wolf sounds like when she is no longer pretending to be merchandise."

* * *

The tide turned at the hour past midnight, when even the Choir sleeps — or so I told myself, though I knew better. Inquisitors do not sleep. They listen.

The last thing I did at my desk was the thing I will be judged by, whatever judgment comes: I wrote the true ledger. Not the guild's. Mine. A small notebook, wrapped in oilskin, that listed every lie I had told Corvane, every coin I had moved, every date and door of the escape. If I drowned in the undercity, I wanted someone someday to know that the numbers had been honest somewhere.

Then I put the notebook inside my coat, took the vault key, the file I'd spent three weeks wearing into the lock mechanism, and went down into the dark to end my life as I had known it.

Tam was waiting at the cistern junction, pale as paper in the lantern light, holding the sledgehammer he'd insisted on bringing. "The guard rotation changed," he whispered. "Corvane doubled the night watch. She — Noor, she knows something's wrong. She's been in the counting room since dusk."

"She doesn't know about tonight."

"Nobody knows anything about anybody," Tam said, with the bitter clarity of a man who had finally understood his own profession. "That's the whole trade, isn't it? We just never thought we'd be the goods."

I put the file to the lock. Iron sang softly under steel, a sound like a small bird complaining, and behind the bars Ysolt's eyes opened in the dark, already awake, already watching the corridor behind us.

That was how I learned we had run out of time. Not by sound. By her stillness.

Lantern light came down the stair. More than one lantern. Boots — good boots, the kind Inquisitors wore, and under them, softer, the house slippers of a woman who owned the building.

Mistress Corvane came around the corner first, which surprised me until it didn't. Behind her walked Sera Voss, and behind Sera walked four of the Choir, gray-robed, their hymnals open, their mouths already shaping the first syllables of a detection song.

"Noor," Corvane said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "I want you to understand that I tried every other entry first."

"You sold me."

"I priced you." For one moment the old woman's face cracked, and underneath was not cruelty but exhaustion, deep as the vault itself. "They came to me this evening with the guild's charter in one hand and a warrant in the other. I have forty people under this roof whose wages I sign. You are one person, and you were always leaving." She looked away. "I'm sorry that it's tonight. I would have given you more time, if time were mine to give."

"It never was," said Ysolt, from the cage.

Sera Voss stepped forward, and the Choir behind her breathed in together, and the hymn began.

I have described the detection song before as a lantern. That was cowardice. It is a hand. It goes into you and closes around whatever you have hidden and lifts. I felt it find my gift the way a fisherman's hook finds the soft part of a fish's mouth — not painful, precisely. Just certain. My beast-speech rose in my throat like floodwater, every word I'd ever spoken to a rat or gull or horse lighting up along the hymn's melody like lamps along a street, and Sera Voss's head tilted, listening, and I saw her hear it —

Tam hit her with the sledgehammer.

Not her. The lantern. He swung wild and desperate and clipped the nearest Choirman's raised hymnal, and the song broke mid-phrase, and in that broken half-second of silence I did the only magic I have ever performed without thinking: I screamed down through the stone floor in the tongue of beasts, *NOW, ALL OF YOU, NOW*, and the undercity answered.

Rats came up out of the drains like a brown flood with intentions. Gulls threw themselves against the high windows. The horses in the guild stable kicked their stalls to splinters, and the guard dogs — the Choir's own trained silence-hounds — sat down and began to howl, and I will swear until I die that they were howling words.

"GO!" Tam shoved me toward the cistern gate. He wasn't running with us. He had planted himself between us and the Choir like a comma in a sentence that refused to end. "The gate, the flood channel, GO — I'll be right behind you—"

He would not be right behind him. We both knew it. He swung the hammer again anyway.

I got the gate open. Ysolt came through the ruined lock like water finding its level, shouldered past me, and grabbed Tam's collar in her teeth — actually grabbed him, hauling him stumbling backward toward the channel — and then the hymn found its footing again.

The second verse of the detection song is not a hand. It is a fist.

It closed on Tam first, because Tam had no gift to hide except courage, and courage rings like a bell under that music. He dropped the hammer. Two Choirmen took his arms. He did not struggle, and he looked at me across the cistern, and he mouthed — no sound at all, saving all his voice for later, for whatever room they would put him in — two words:

*The books.*

Meaning: burn them. Meaning: save them. Meaning both; meaning, knowing Tam, that he had misfiled something crucial on purpose days ago and I would only understand it when I needed it most.

Then the current took us.

* * *

The flood channel runs beneath Vellum House the way a secret runs beneath a reputation. When the tide turns, the sluices open and the black water comes through the undercity in a slow cold muscle, and everything that lives down there rides it. I rode it badly. Ysolt rode it like she'd invented it, swimming beside me with her teeth set gently in my coat collar whenever the current pulled, steering me the way you steer a boat.

Behind us, muffled by stone and water, I heard Vellum House make noises I had never heard it make. And above those noises, fainter and fainter, the hymn — still singing, but singing after us now, chasing, and I understood that the song could follow anywhere I went as long as I carried what it sought.

So I stopped carrying it. Or tried. I clamped down on my gift the way you clamp a bleeding wound, and the beasts around me in the dark felt the clamp close and began, one by one, to call to me anyway. Rats on the conduit walls. Eels in the water, offended by our splashing. Something huge and blind in a side tunnel that spoke in pressure waves rather than sound and asked only, *which way, brother, which way* — and I answered without opening my mouth, because a gift under a fist is not a gift extinguished, it is a gift whispered.

Left at the drowned chapel. Right at the place where the old aqueduct had fallen in. Up through the storm grate where the gulls nested, because the gulls had seen the Choir seal the street exits and were telling me so in short furious sentences, *blocked, blocked, blocked, sky open, sky open* —

We surfaced in the Saltspire roost at half past two in the morning, soaked, shaking, alive. Below us the city lay under a curfew of lamplight, and moving through the streets in perfect formation came the gray robes of the Choir, thirty of them, sealing intersections, and on every corner a Cantor stood with an open hymnal, and the detection song passed from mouth to mouth like a bucket line fighting a fire.

"They've sealed the landward gates," I said. "Harbor next. Then they comb district by district."

"Then we do not stay in districts." Ysolt shook the water off her coat and looked east, toward the marshes beyond the walls, where the exiled beast-speakers lived — or lived is perhaps generous. "There are people who owe the prophecy a debt, and people the prophecy owes. Both kinds will be looking for you now."

"Not yet." I was looking south, at the harbor, where the prison barges sat, where Tam would be by sunrise if he wasn't already. Every calculation I ran ended at those barges, and every calculation ended the same way: too many guards, one wolf, one clerk's hammer left behind in a flooded vault.

Ysolt followed my gaze and sighed, a long silver breath in the cold air. "You are going to do something stupid and book-shaped about the clerk."

"He kept my books. I keep his."

"That is not how debt works between people."

"It's the only way I know how it works."

For a while neither of us spoke. The gulls muttered around us, trading rumors — the Choir had taken three speakers in the Marsh Ward already; the Cathedral bells had been silenced for tomorrow, a rare and terrible sign; something was gathering out past the harbor bar, though whether ships or whales the birds could not agree.

And then, from below, from every drain and gutter and cellar grating in the Salt Quarter, the rats began to sing.

Not the way the Choir sings. There was no melody. It was testimony — hundreds of small gray voices saying the same thing in the old flat grammar, over and over, rising through the stones like groundwater: *they-took-one, they-took-one, they-took-the-bookkeeper, they-took-the-bookkeeper.* They were telling the whole undercity what had happened to Tam. They were making him a fact that could not be forged or filed away.

Ysolt's ears flattened. "Noor. Listen to what they're saying at the end of it."

I listened. Past *they-took-one*, past the name the rats had given Tam (*the-kind-hands*, which undid me worse than any hymn), there was another phrase threading through, older, slower, coming from deeper down than rats ever go — from the eel-dark places, the drowned foundations, the parts of the city where the flood never drains.

*Come-down, come-down, come-down.* A voice I didn't know. A voice the rats themselves seemed afraid of.

"The undercity wants to meet you," Ysolt said quietly. "The things that live below the rats. I had hoped you'd have years before that invitation arrived."

"Why?"

"Because," she said, starting down the seaward side of Saltspire with the gulls wheeling overhead like punctuation, "everything old in this city has been waiting a century for the Wolf, and everything old does not care that you haven't decided to be him yet."

Below us, the Choir sealed the last street. Above us, the sky was beginning to turn the color of an unwritten page.

I had burned down my life in a single night — my employer, my clerk, my ledgers, my name — and the fire had barely started. What remained of Noor Iversen went down into the dark with a wolf, following the voices of things that had never signed any pact at all.
