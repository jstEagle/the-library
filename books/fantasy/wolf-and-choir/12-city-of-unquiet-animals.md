## 12. City of Unquiet Animals

The gulls found him before dawn.

Noor had slept — if it could be called sleeping — in the hollow behind a chimney on the roof of the Saltworks chapel, wrapped in Ysolt's warmth and nothing else, because everything else had burned. His coat was still in Vellum House. His ledgers were in Vellum House. Tam was in Vellum House, or worse, in the Choir's custody, and Noor had run through the flooded undercity with the taste of sewer water in his mouth while his whole life went up behind him like a page held to a candle.

He woke because a gull screamed directly into his ear.

*Thief. Thief. Thief.*

"Noor," he said hoarsely. "My name is Noor."

The gull tilted its head, one yellow eye considering him. *Thief,* it repeated, but without conviction now. Gulls were not subtle creatures. They stole from each other constantly and considered it a profession.

"You're one to talk."

*Fish-thief. Egg-thief. Roof-king.* The gull hopped closer, feathers rough with harbor grime. *You smell of wolf and ink. Wolf-ink-man. The old ones smelled of wolf too.*

Noor sat up so fast his head spun. "What old ones?"

But the gull had already lost interest in conversation, having remembered that the tide was out and the mudflats were full of worms, and it launched itself off the chapel roof with a cry that might have been mockery.

Beside him, Ysolt stirred. In three days of hiding she had healed enough to walk, though the wound Sera Voss's crossbow bolt had torn across her shoulder still wept through the poultices the rats had brought — actual poultices, chewed yarrow and river clay, delivered by rats who would not say where they'd learned the craft. Her gray fur was dull in the pre-dawn light. She looked, for the first time since he'd known her, her age: a hundred years and more, carried in the set of her muzzle and the deep amber patience of her eyes.

"You spoke to the bird," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"It spoke to me first."

"Mm." She stretched, wincing. "Then you're ready to hear what this city actually sounds like. You've been hearing it your whole life, Noor. You just didn't know you were allowed to listen."

* * *

She took him down through the city the way its beasts traveled — not by street but by seam.

Down the fire-stairs of the tannery district, where the walls were stained black with runoff and every drain grate breathed steam; across the rooftops above Cooper's Row, moving between chimneys on paths worn smooth by generations of cats; down again into the undercity through a broken culvert the maps had stopped marking two hundred years ago. Ysolt moved slowly, favoring her shoulder, but she knew these routes the way Noor knew ledger columns — intimately, structurally, as a grammar of connections.

And everywhere, the city spoke to him.

He had always heard beasts, but he had always heard them at a distance, filtered, the way you hear neighbors through a wall — fragments, impressions, mostly complaint. Now, moving at their pace through their territory, he heard the whole texture of it, and it was not what he had imagined.

It was hunger. Constant, ambient, structural hunger, the way a building has a hum. The cats of Cooper's Row were starving because the ships had stopped coming in spring and the grain rats had been poisoned in the granaries and the poison worked its way up the food chain like a slow dark rhyme. A tomcat named — named, they all had names, that was the thing no human ever understood, they all had names — a tomcat called Bracken was teaching his last surviving kitten which mushrooms were safe, because safe meat no longer existed below the third story.

The sewers were worse. The rats there spoke in a clipped, economical dialect Noor had to concentrate to follow, and what they said was: *the water is wrong, the water has been wrong since the tanneries upstream changed hands, our pups are born thin, we bury more than we raise.* They said it without self-pity. They said it the way clerks read figures — this is the state of the account, here is the deficit, here is what remains.

And underneath the hunger, underneath even the poison, there was memory.

That was the part that undid him. On the second night, sheltering in the belfry of a drowned church where the undercity floods met, an ancient rat with half a tail and eyes like wet garnets told him about the Choir Wars. Not the version from the histories Noor had catalogued at the Guild — the version with dates and generals and the heroic silencing. This version had names in it too. Names of beasts who had bargained their voices away to end a war humans had started and could not finish. Names of the ones who had refused, and what the refusal-squads of the early Choir had done to them in the marshes and the forests, before the pact made such things unnecessary.

"We remember," the rat said, in its dry clerk's voice. "That is the whole of our magic now. You took the songs and left us the remembering. We have had a century to get very good at it."

Noor had cried, quietly, in the dark of the belfry, where only Ysolt could see, and she had let him, and had not offered comfort, because comfort was not what the moment required. What the moment required was witness, and he gave it, and the old rat inclined its head as though receiving payment on a debt long outstanding.

* * *

On the fourth day, Ysolt began to teach him.

They were in the flooded crypt beneath the drowned church, sitting on a stone sarcophagus dry enough to bear their weight, water lapping black and patient around its base. She had brought him here, she said, because the acoustics were honest. Every word spoken in the crypt arrived exactly as it was sent.

"The pact isn't a document," she began. "I know that's how Corvane's kind would file it, and I know that's how the Choir enforces it, but it isn't paper. It's a grammar. Do you understand what a grammar is?"

"Rules for making meaning."

"Rules for making promises." She paced the narrow stone, three steps and turn, favoring the shoulder. "When the beasts gave up their voices, the grammar was how we kept something back. Every silenced creature retained one sentence — the terms of the bargain, held in trust. None of us can speak it alone. It was designed that way. One voice can't unmake what ten thousand voices sealed. But the grammar survives, in fragments, passed down. And a beast-speaker — a true one — is the hinge. The place where the two grammars meet."

"I don't want to be a hinge."

"Nobody wants to be a hinge. Doors want to swing free." Her tail flicked. "Say after me. The first clause."

She spoke, then, in the old tongue — not the beast-speech he knew, the fluid language of scent and posture and sound, but something older, formal, cadenced, each phrase shaped like a knot tied in rope. He heard it twice before he understood that she was asking him to repeat it in his own throat, and when he tried, his voice came out strange to himself, doubled, as if a second speaker stood behind his teeth.

"What did I just say?"

"'We were many, and we chose to be silent, so that the children of both kinds might live.'" Ysolt settled onto her haunches. "That's the opening clause. Every beast in this city carries it. Ask them sometime. Ask the gulls, ask the rats, ask the oldest cat you can find. They will all recite it identically, to the syllable, and none of them knows why."

He asked them. Over the following days, working the rooftops and the flood-channels, gathering food and information in equal measure, he asked. And every creature answered the same way — the gulls shrieking the clause in their harsh grammar, the rats reciting it in their dry precision, Bracken the tomcat mumbling it half-asleep in a warm chimney corner, word-perfect, tone-deaf, faithful. A century of enforced silence, and the promise had survived in ten thousand mouths like a seed banked against winter.

"They're waiting," Ysolt said, watching him come back across the rooftops at dusk, his face gone strange. "Not for me. For the Wolf. The grammar needs a reader. It has needed one for a hundred years, and the prophecies were my way of making sure that when the reader came, he couldn't pretend he hadn't seen his name in the book."

"I've read the book. I'm still pretending."

"You're alive. Pretending is what the living do." She rose, shook herself, winced. "Tomorrow I teach you the closing clause. It's harder. It costs more to say."

"What does it cost?"

Ysolt looked at him with those ancient amber eyes, and for once she didn't answer, and he understood that the answer was the lesson, and that he wasn't ready for it yet.

* * *

On the sixth day, the city told him it was afraid.

He felt it first in the rats — a change in the quality of their chatter, the clerkish calm giving way to short, urgent exchanges, movement patterns tightening, stores being shifted deeper into the walls. Then the cats vanished from the rooftops, all of them at once, as if a bell had rung that only they could hear. The gulls wheeled higher over the harbor than Noor had ever seen them fly.

*What is it?* he asked the old rat with the half tail.

*Men with bells,* the rat said. *Men with bells walking the seawall, singing into the water. The fish are going mad. Something is being sung shut.*

He climbed to the highest point of the Saltworks chapel roof as evening came down, and looked toward the harbor, and saw it: lantern-light along the seawall, ranks of white-robed figures spaced at intervals like notes on a staff, and rising from them, faint across the water, the sound of the Choir singing — not the detection hymn that had hunted him through the guildhouse, but something vaster, slower, a hymn with the weight of a closing door.

The seal-hymn. The plan had called it that, in the documents he'd appraised years ago and never thought he'd need to understand: the ritual by which the Choir closed a city, sealing every hidden gift inside, making escape impossible and discovery inevitable.

"They know," he said, when Ysolt came limping up beside him. "Voss knows I'm in the city. They're not searching anymore. They're locking the box."

"Yes." Ysolt's voice was flat and old. "And Noor — the seal does something else. Listen."

He listened. Beneath the great hymn, beneath the hunger and the memory and the endless clerkish accounting of the city's discarded, he heard it: a stirring, a restlessness moving through ten thousand throats. The stolen voices. The ones held in trust. The seal-hymn pressed against them like a hand pressing on a bruise, and all across the harbor, in the water and the warehouses and the drowned places, the beasts were beginning to scream — not in pain, exactly, but in the agony of a word trying to be spoken and finding its own mouth locked.

"The appointed date," Noor said. His voice came out small. "How far?"

Ysolt turned her head toward the cathedral spire, visible even from here, a needle of black against the burning sunset.

"Three nights," she said. "So tonight, wolf, you learn the closing clause. And tomorrow, whatever it costs, we go to sing it home."

Below them, in the darkening streets, the city's forgotten voices rose together in a single trembling note — waiting, aching, almost awake.
