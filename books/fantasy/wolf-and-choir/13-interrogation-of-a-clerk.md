## 13. Interrogation of a Clerk

The cell they gave Tam Wicker was, by the standards of the Inquisitors' Choir, almost merciful. A chair. A table. A lamp that burned without flickering because Sera Voss had personally checked its wick. No chains — chains were for people who might run, and Tam Wicker had the posture of a man who had never run anywhere in his life, including away from debt collectors, fire, or his own bad decisions.

He sat with his hands flat on the table like a schoolboy at an examination he hadn't studied for.

Sera sat opposite him and did not open with a question. That was the first lesson of interrogation, learned in the Choir's training halls beneath the Cathedral of Song: never open with a question. Open with silence, and let the subject fill it. Most people could not endure more than forty seconds of silence from another human being. Clerks, she had found, lasted longer. They were used to silence. But they were also used to being *correct*, and that was a different weakness entirely.

She let the silence run to fifty seconds.

Tam shifted. "I filed everything," he said. "Before you ask. Everything's filed."

"Good," said Sera. "Then this will be brief."

* * *

She had spent two days on the ledgers before she ever requested the clerk, and she would spend no time pretending otherwise. Interrogation, done properly, was not a fishing expedition. It was arithmetic. You gathered your sums and then you went looking for the man who'd made them come out wrong.

The sums were these.

In the Vellum Guild's transfer records, over a period of eleven days, there existed a pattern so faint that three junior Inquisitors had read straight past it. Entries transposed. Not forged — forgery left seams, and Noor Iversen (if it was Iversen; she was careful with names she couldn't yet prove) had left no seams. What he had done was subtler. He had moved dates. Shifted entries across page-breaks so that a transaction recorded on one folio appeared, when read in sequence, to belong to a different week. A wolf purchased on the ninth, entered as sold on the fourth. An appraisal dated after the buyer had already left the city. Small things. The kind of things that happened naturally in any large ledger, through honest error, all the time.

Except that they happened four times. And except that every one of them concerned the same asset class: living cargo.

And one thing more. On the eleventh day, in the margin of the freight schedule, in ink that matched the rest of the entry perfectly, someone had written the word *salt* where the word *silt* belonged. The undercity barges ran silt routes. The harbor runs took salt. It was a single letter. It was, Sera thought, the most beautiful mistake she had ever seen, because it was not a mistake. Someone had rewritten history in real time and gotten one letter wrong under pressure, and that letter pointed down, toward the water, toward the flooded quarter where the Choir's hymns echoed badly and their lanterns drowned.

Someone had moved a living asset out of Vellum House through the silt gates, and dressed it as salt freight, and then run.

She wanted the clerk who had touched those pages.

* * *

"Tell me about your duties," Sera said now, pleasantly.

Tam told her about his duties. He told her at length. He told her about the ink requisitions and the folio numbering system and the way Mistress Corvane liked her margins ruled wide enough for corrections, and Sera listened with the appearance of total absorption, because the fastest way to learn what a man was hiding was to listen to everything he wasn't.

"You worked closely with Master Iversen," she said, when he paused for breath.

"No one worked *closely* with Master Iversen." Tam said it before he could stop himself, and then went pale, realizing it sounded like something. "I mean — he kept his own books. The impossible acquisitions. That's Guild shorthand, miss, not — it's just what we call the ledger."

"The Ledger of Impossible Things." She smiled. She knew exactly how her smile looked under this lamp; she had been told once, by a colleague, that it was like watching a door open onto a room you didn't want to enter. "And you never saw inside it?"

"I'm a third clerk. Third clerks don't see inside anything."

"Third clerks see *everything*," Sera said gently. "That's what third clerks are for. You fetch, you carry, you file, you observe. The senior staff forget you're in the room. It's the most valuable position in any house." She leaned forward slightly. "So tell me what you observed."

* * *

Here is what Sera knew about guilt: it behaved like water. It found the lowest point and pooled there. In twenty years of reading people the way other people read ledgers, she had learned that the guilty rarely lied about the big things. They lied about the small ones, the ones nearest the pool, and the lie always sat slightly wrong, like a misfiled page.

So she fed him small things.

"The night the Choir arrived in the city," she said, "the eleventh. Where were you?"

"Here. At the guildhouse. Everyone was — we all heard the hymn, everyone stopped working—"

"And Master Iversen? Did he stop working?"

A pause. One beat too long. "I don't know. I wasn't watching him."

"You weren't watching him." Sera turned a page in her notebook, unhurried. "On the twelfth, a barge manifest crossed your desk. Salt freight, harbor route. Do you remember it?"

"It was a normal manifest."

"It was a normal manifest," she agreed. "Signed off normally. Filed normally. And yet when I pulled the harbor master's duplicate copy this morning, the tonnage didn't match. Your copy lists six barrels. The harbor master counted five." She watched his hands. Flat on the table still, but the fingers had gone white at the tips. "One barrel, Mr. Wicker. Where did it go?"

"I wouldn't know. Barrels get miscounted all the time—"

"On a sealed manifest?" She let the question hang, delicate as a held note. Then, softer: "You've been very helpful. Let me be helpful back. I am not interested in a third clerk who was frightened and did what a senior colleague told him to do. Fear is not a crime. Concealment is. And you are concealing something, and we both know it, and we will sit in this pleasant little room until the lamp burns out if that's what honesty costs tonight. So." She folded her hands. "What did Master Iversen ask you to do?"

* * *

What Tam Wicker actually said, in the end, was nothing.

That was the strange part, the part Sera would turn over for hours afterward. She broke him — she felt it happen; there was a moment around the second hour when his breathing changed and his hands came off the table and into his lap, the posture of a man surrendering — and then he gave her nothing. He cried a little. He apologized for crying. He said, "I can't," twice, in a voice stripped of all performance, and then he said nothing else at all, and no technique she possessed could extract a syllable more.

It was loyalty, she realized. Not fear. Fear she could work with; fear negotiates. This was a man protecting someone, and doing it badly and bravely and without any plan for how to survive the protection. It was the least profitable form of courage she had ever encountered, and it unsettled her more than defiance would have.

She released him to holding and sat alone with the lamp and her notes.

* * *

He had given her one thing, though he didn't know it. He'd given it in the first ten minutes, before the breaking, when he was still performing innocence: *"Everything's filed."*

Because that was the lie. She had gone back to the guild archive that evening with a lantern and a warrant, and she had gone shelf by shelf, and she had found the misfiled page.

It was tucked into a folio of tallow contracts from nine years ago — deliberately boring, the kind of place no eye returns to. A single sheet from the current year, out of order, out of sequence. An appraisal record. Asset designation: *living, unclassified*. Appraiser's initials in the corner: **N.I.**

And in the valuation column, in Iversen's neat, upright hand, a figure. Sera stared at it for a long time.

Every appraiser in the Guild valued living assets by market comparables — price per pound, per wing-span, per voice, depending on the commodity. Every valuation she had ever seen followed the same formula. But this figure matched no formula. It was too high by half, and it was not round, and when she worked backward from it — she did the arithmetic three times, on the back of the tallow folio, with the lamp burning low — the number resolved into something else entirely. It was a ransom. Or a debt. Or a price paid by a man to his own conscience, entered honestly into a dishonest system, which was the single most incriminating thing a person could do with ink.

No forger values honestly. Forgers value what the buyer expects. This man had looked at a caged creature and written down what he believed it was worth — not what it would sell for, but what it was *owed* — and hidden the page among the tallow because he could not bring himself to falsify the figure itself.

Sera sat back in the dark of the archive.

She had hunted beast-speakers for eleven years. She had heard them sing magic out of hiding places, had stood in rooms while the detection hymn passed through walls and made hidden gifts burn like lantern-lightning behind plaster, and she had taken them in pairs and dozens, and she had believed — professionally, cleanly, the way one believes a doctrine — that they were corruptions. Errors in the pact. Voices that had refused to stay silenced.

But she had never before read one. Never seen the inside of one rendered in ledger-form: the scrupulousness, the guilt priced and carried, the terrible inability to lie about value. Her subjects had always been songs and screams and running figures in the rain. This was different. This was a mind she could follow line by line, and the line said: *this man believes the creature in the cage deserves more than the market says.*

The Wolf of the Codex. The name had come up out of the seized estate records like a fish surfacing — prophecies were above her pay grade, filed to the doctrinal office, stamped and forgotten — but the clerks talked, and clerks' talk said the Codex named a Wolf who would break the pact, a beast-speaker, here, now, on a date the Choir had already circled.

She looked at the initials again. *N.I.*

Not a corruption. A bookkeeper. A man whose entire crime was that he could not stop valuing things truthfully.

"Well," Sera Voss said aloud, to the tallow shelves, and her voice sounded strange in the dark. "You're real."

* * *

She wrote the report herself, by hand, because this one would not go through juniors. She laid out the pattern — the four transpositions, the salt/silt letter, the misfiled appraisal — and she drew the conclusion in language the doctrinal office could not ignore:

*Subject is not fleeing the city. Subject has moved a high-value living asset to a location within city limits accessible via the silt routes. Subject remains close to the asset. Recommendation: seal the undercity exits quietly; post listeners at the floodgates; make no arrests until the appointed date, when the hymn will confirm.*

Bureaucratic elegance. That was how you caught a good man — not with torches, but with paperwork. You let him keep believing he was invisible while you built the room around him, entry by entry, and on the appointed night you simply closed the cover.

She signed it. She blotted it. And then she sat for a moment longer, because there was one thing she had not written down, one observation with no column in any report form she owned.

During the interrogation, in the hour when Tam Wicker finally broke and gave her nothing, the lamp between them had guttered — once, softly, though she had checked its wick herself — and in that instant Sera had heard, at the very floor of her hearing, beneath the threshold where the Choir trained its ears to listen, something that answered.

Faint. Wrong. Impossible. Not in the room. Underneath it. Below the cell, below the foundations, somewhere down in the dark wet arteries of the city where the hymns went badly.

A voice. Not a beast's, exactly. Something between — a voice shaped like a man's but pitched to carry the way animal voices carry, and it was not singing, and it was not speaking to her. It was speaking to the rats.

She had not written it in the report. She did not fully know why. She told herself it was because unverified phenomena belonged in addenda, and addenda could wait.

But her hand had hesitated over the page, and Sera Voss had been a hunter long enough to know what hesitation meant. It meant the listener had heard something she was afraid to transcribe.

She sealed the report, called for the runner, and stood at the window of her office watching the silt-gate district smoke its evening fires, all unknowing, all unguarded, while somewhere below the streets a man who valued things too honestly taught the rats to keep watch.

Five days to the appointed date.

She opened a fresh notebook, and began, at the top of the first page, a new file. Not a case file.

A listening file.

*Subject: the Wolf.*
