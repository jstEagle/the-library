## 15. What Justice Costs Off the Books

We left the city at low tide, through a sewer mouth that smelled of tar and old fish, and the gulls guided us along the mudflats like a choir of jeering angels.

*Left. Left. Left,* they said, wheeling above us in ragged arcs, because to a gull everything is a direction and an insult. Ysolt moved beside me with her head low and her limp carefully disguised, which fooled me not at all and fooled no one else either. The mud took her paws to the wrist with every step, sucking, releasing, taking its toll of strength she did not have to spare.

*How far?* I asked her, in the old grammar she had been teaching me — the one made of posture and pressure and meaning that arrived whole rather than in pieces.

*Past where the reeds stop pretending to be land,* she said. *There's a village that isn't on maps because the mapmakers drowned.*

I didn't ask what she meant by *drowned*. I was saving my questions for the people we were going to find. Beast-speakers. Survivors of the last uprising — the Marsh Choir, Ysolt called them, though there was nothing left of any choir in them but the name, she said. People like me who had refused the silencing and paid for it, or people who had taken the silencing and lived anyway and hated themselves for it.

Allies. That was what I needed. Nine days until the appointed date, nine days until the pact came due and the beasts' stolen voices woke as a war-cry if I didn't speak it first, and I could not stand alone in a cathedral against the entire Inquisitors' Choir. I needed witnesses. I needed voices that weren't mine. I needed, more than anything, someone who had done this before and could tell me where the traps were.

That was the arithmetic I carried across the flats. It was simple ledger-work: assets on one side, liabilities on the other. What I had: a wolf, a codex, a gift, and no home. What I needed: allies, a route into the Cathedral of Song, and enough time.

The marshes do not do simple arithmetic. I learned that before sundown.

* * *

The village announced itself by smell first — woodsmoke and peat and something medicinal, bitter, boiled. Then by absence. No dogs barking. No chickens complaining at our approach. In a settlement of beast-speakers, I realized, there would be no beasts to speak to. They would have sent them away, or eaten them, or simply stopped listening, the way you stop listening to a song that has cost you too much.

The houses were built on stilts above the black water, connected by walkways gone soft with rot, and the people who came out onto those walkways to watch us come were gray-faced and quiet and old. Not old in years — some of them were younger than me — but old the way a burned house is old.

A woman stood at the end of the main walkway, and she was the oldest of all of them. She had a fishing spear held loosely, the way you hold something when you've decided whether to use it. Her left arm ended at the elbow, wrapped in waxed cloth.

"Noor," Ysolt said quietly, beside me, using my name in the human way because these people would need human words. "This is the place."

The woman watched Ysolt for a long moment. Then me. Then Ysolt again.

"You're walking a wolf into a village of people the wolves' prophecies got killed," she said. "Explain yourself fast, bookkeeper."

I hadn't told her I was a bookkeeper. But then, I supposed I looked like one. Ink-stained cuffs, ledger-callused fingers, the particular hunched carriage of a man who has spent his life making columns agree.

"I'm looking for—" I began.

"Help," she said. "Everyone who comes here is looking for help. You're the third this year." She turned her head and spat into the water. "The other two are buried behind the smokehouse. One asked too loudly. One couldn't stop singing."

My mouth went dry. Behind me, Ysolt made a sound very low in her chest, and thirty people on thirty walkways flinched at once, a single animal motion, and I understood that I was not looking at a village. I was looking at one wound wearing thirty faces.

"My name is Noor Iversen," I said. "I keep— kept— the ledgers of the Vellum Guild. And in nine days, unless I speak an old pact aloud in the Choir's cathedral, every silenced voice in this city wakes up screaming, and the city burns. I came to ask you to stand with me when I do it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere out in the reeds a bittern boomed, that ancient foghorn note, and nobody so much as glanced toward it.

Then the woman laughed. It was the worst laugh I have ever heard — no humor in it at all, just the shape of laughter worn hollow, like a shell.

"The cathedral," she said. "He wants to sing in the cathedral."

"Maren," someone said from a walkway behind her, a warning.

"Oh, let him talk." Maren sat down on the edge of the walkway, spear across her knees, and looked up at me with eyes the color of marsh water. "Let him tell us his plan. I want to hear how it goes this time. Sit down, bookkeeper. You look tired. We'll feed you first, because we're not monsters, and then you can tell us about your nine days, and then I'm going to show you my arm."

They fed us eel stew in a longhouse that stank of peat smoke and camphor, and while we ate, Maren talked, and what she talked about was arithmetic.

* * *

"You think the uprising failed because we lost," she said, when the bowls were empty and the young ones had drifted to the edges of the room to listen without seeming to listen. "You've read the histories. The histories say the Marsh Choir rose in the spring and the Choir of Inquisitors put us down by autumn, and that's the story, tidy as a ledger line. Rise, suppression, martyrdom. Very clean." She turned her stump slightly, deliberately, where I could see it. "Here's the untidy version.

"We didn't lose in autumn. We lost on the second night. Do you know what the Inquisitors did when the first village declared for the beasts? They didn't burn it. Burning makes martyrs; the Church had learned that much. They sang the *drowning hymn*. You know it?"

I shook my head.

"Of course you don't. It's not in the hymnals anymore. It's a detection hymn's ugly cousin. Where the detection hymn finds a hidden gift and makes it burn like a lantern, the drowning hymn finds it and *pulls*. It drags the gift up out of you whether you want it or not, and it doesn't stop, and you can't close your ears to your own voice being used. Every beast-speaker in that village heard themselves call the marsh beasts to war in the Choir's own words, in their own mouths, and the beasts came, and there was a battle, and the dead on both sides were ours. Ours and theirs. And every survivor knew that their voice had been the weapon." She smiled, and it was terrible. "You don't need to burn people after that. They burn themselves. Quietly. For years."

"That was forty years ago," I said. "The law hasn't softened. The Choir is stronger now. Sera Voss herself—"

"I know who Sera Voss is." Something moved behind Maren's eyes. "She trained under Inquisitor Hale. Hale wrote the modern drowning hymn. Why do you think I know its name?" She leaned forward. "Bookkeeper. Let me see your books. You say you have nine days, a wolf, and a speech. What else?"

"A codex of prophecies. Written by the wolf beside me, a century ago. A date. A clause."

"And allies. That's why you're here. How many did you imagine? Fifty? A hundred? Enough bodies between you and the halberds that the halberds get tired?"

I said nothing, which was answer enough.

Maren nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a figure she'd already entered in her own ledger. Then she stood, and the room stood with her, and I understood that whatever came next had been decided before I arrived — maybe before I was born.

"Come outside," she said. "It's time you saw the rest of the inventory."

* * *

They showed me one at a time, because that was how they had agreed to be seen.

A man named Aldous whose throat had been opened by the drowning hymn twenty years ago and never healed right — he could whisper, barely, and he communicated mostly through a slate he wore on a cord, and his slate said: *I sang once. Never again.* He showed me the slate the way another man might show a scar.

A pair of sisters, twins, who had spoken bird since they could breathe. When the Choir came for their town, they had each swallowed needles — not to die, Maren explained, but because a gift channeled through a body in agony comes out wrong, garbled, useless to a hymn trying to seize it. They had both kept the scars in their throats. They could still speak to sparrows. They chose not to. *It hurts the birds to hear us cry*, one sister wrote on her palm, and showed me, and closed her hand.

A boy of perhaps fifteen, born in the marshes, never registered, never sung at — the freest beast-speaker in the province, and when I reached toward him with the old grammar, gently, the way you'd extend a hand to a strange dog, he went white as bone and walked off the walkway into a house and shut the door, and his mother came out and looked at me with such hatred that I stepped back and nearly went into the water myself.

"He's not angry at you," Maren said quietly. "He's angry that you exist. That the thing he is exists outside these stilts, out in the world, walking around in daylight, talking to wolves. Every time someone like you shows up alive and free, it reminds him there's a door out of here, and that staying is a choice, and that the choice costs what it costs." She looked at the shut door. "Don't blame him. Blame the hymns."

Last of all, at the far end of the village, past the smokehouse and the two graves behind it, Maren took me to a hut on its own, apart, and inside the hut was an old man lying on a pallet, breathing with great care, like a man carrying water across ice.

"Gerrit," Maren said. "Show him."

The old man opened his mouth.

Nothing came out. Not silence the way a stone is silent — silence the way a bell tower is silent, a shaped and waiting emptiness, an architecture of sound with no sound in it. And I felt it through the old grammar, felt the shape of what should have been there, and it was vast. This man had not merely been a beast-speaker. He had been a *voice*. The kind the Choir Wars had been fought over. The kind they'd built the drowning hymn for.

"They took it at the battle of Reedfleet," Maren said. "Not his gift. His gift's still there — you can feel it, can't you? They took his *use* of it. Sang it shut. Permanently. There are eleven ways to silence a speaker, bookkeeper, and ten of them heal. This is the eleventh." She crouched by the pallet and took the old man's good hand. "Gerrit led the Marsh Choir. Gerrit wrote the petition to the beasts. Gerrit stood in the market square of Reedfleet and spoke the pact aloud, publicly, in both tongues, exactly as you're planning to do — and the beasts came to witness, and the hymn came down on all of them at once, and the harbor ran red, and the pact died with everyone who heard it. Forty years. He has spent forty years learning to live without the half of himself they drowned."

The old man looked at me. His eyes were clear. His mouth moved, and no sound came, and through the grammar — faintly, terribly, like light through blackout curtains — I felt him *push* a word at me, the only word he had the strength to push:

***No.***

And then, weaker, with the last of it, the whole sermon in two syllables:

***Please.***

* * *

I walked back along the walkway alone. Ysolt had been kept at the village edge — the villagers tolerated her the way you tolerate weather — and I found her standing hip-deep in the black shallows with the evening coming down, her ears turned toward me before I spoke, because she'd heard everything. She always heard everything.

"Well?" she said.

"They said no." My voice sounded far away to me. "They begged, Noor. A man gave me the only word he has left and it was *please*. Please don't. Forty years ago someone stood where I'm going to stand and said what I'm going to say, and it bought a massacre and nothing else. The math is the math. Speaking gets people killed. Silence keeps them alive. That's the whole ledger." I crouched at the water's edge and stared at my reflection, which stared back with the face of a man who had already written his death warrant in a century-old book and was now discovering that the margins were full of footnotes. "Maybe they're right. Maybe the bravest thing — the *justest* thing — is to stay quiet, stay small, stay alive, and let the date pass. Let the clause fail. If I do nothing, the prophecy does nothing. The voices sleep. Nobody burns."

Ysolt waded closer. Water streamed off her forelegs.

"Say the rest of it," she said.

"There is no rest of it."

"There is. Say it."

I said it. It came out small. "But I heard them. Last night, under the city. All of them, under the hymn. They aren't sleeping, Ysolt. They're *waiting*. And waiting isn't peace — it's a debt accruing interest. And if I stay silent, I'm not choosing survival. I'm choosing *when* the war happens. Their silence isn't quiet. It's a held breath, and the city is standing on its chest." I looked up at her. "Maren's ledger balances. Gerrit's ledger balances. Every one of them balances, if you never count the beasts as anything but collateral. That's the entry they've all made for forty years. *Beasts: acceptable loss.* And I've made that same entry myself — I hid, I falsified, I traded a woman in a vault to buy time. I know how the column works." I stood. Mud sucked at my boots. "But somebody finally has to refuse to write it."

Ysolt regarded me for a long moment with her amber eyes, and then she did something I had not seen her do. She bowed her head — not submission, nothing so simple — the bow of one professional to another.

"Then you'll go to the cathedral alone," she said. "Because they will not come with you. Not one of them. And neither will the villages beyond the marsh, and neither will anyone whose memory reaches back further than their fear. I told you a century ago, when I inked the clause: the Wolf speaks alone, or not at all. I wrote it that way because I'd seen crowds, Noor. Crowds are how the Choir knows where to point the hymn." She turned toward the darkening east, toward the city, where the distant towers had begun to light their lamps one by one, like a row of candles being counted. "Nine days. And tonight the tide turns early, and we have a long walk back, and somewhere behind those lights an Inquisitor is reading your handwriting."

Behind us, from the village, a slate clacked against wood — Aldous, at his window, writing one more line for anyone gifted enough to read it at distance.

I read it.

*I hope you're faster than we were.*
