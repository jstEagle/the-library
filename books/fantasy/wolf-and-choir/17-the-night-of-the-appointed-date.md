## 17. The Night of the Appointed Date

The date arrived the way debts do: not all at once, but all at once, everywhere, in every ledger at the same hour.

Noor knew it was coming before he opened his eyes. The gulls had stopped sleeping. He could feel them through the floorboards of the marsh house — hundreds of them wheeling over Blackwater Harbor in the dark, their minds a single shrill ribbon of *soon, soon, soon*. The rats had gone silent two days ago, which was worse. Rats only went quiet when they were counting.

Beside him on the hearthstones, Ysolt lay with her head up, ears forward, watching a door that hadn't moved in hours.

"It's tonight," she said.

"I know."

"You slept anyway."

"Ysolt." Noor sat up. His ribs had knit wrong where Sera Voss's choir-whip had caught him in the escape from Vellum House; they ached when he breathed deep, and he breathed deep now, deliberately, because today was the last day pain would be private. "I've been running since I was twelve years old. Sleeping is how I run."

She made a sound low in her chest — the old grammar's word for *fool*, and also for *brother*. The pact-language was full of words like that. Words that held two truths and refused to choose.

He'd spent three weeks in the marshes learning them. Three weeks among the broken beast-speakers who had begged him not to speak — old Maren with her burned throat, the twins who flinched at birdsong, the boy named Pell who could talk to eels and hadn't spoken to anything else in eleven years. They had told him what the last uprising cost. They had told him what the hymn did to a city full of waking voices. And then they had given him everything they had left anyway: Maren's memory of the old covenant-words, the twins' knowledge of the Choir's patrol-song, Pell's map of the flooded undercity drawn in eel-grease on a scrap of sailcloth.

"They think I'm going to die," Noor said now, pulling on the guild coat Corvane had once paid for and he had never returned. It still smelled faintly of vellum and lamp oil. Of a life.

"The prophecy says you might," Ysolt said. "It doesn't say you will. I wrote the ambiguity myself. A hundred years ago I had a very good reason for it."

"And what was the reason?"

She looked at him with one amber eye. "So that whoever read it would have to decide. Prophecies without decisions are just weather reports."

* * *

They came into the city before dawn broke, because there would be no dawn worth the name. The sky over Blackwater was the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom, and beneath it the harbor lay flat as poured pewter — too flat. No tide-tick. No fish-rise. Every animal in the water was holding its breath.

The first hymn began at the moment the sun should have risen but didn't.

Noor heard it start at the edge of the Saltmarket: a single sustained note, high and clean, rising from the Cathedral of Song like steam from a kettle. Then a second voice joined it, a fifth above. Then ten. Then a hundred. The Choir had been singing Inquisitors into the city for days, and now they were all singing at once, and the sound wasn't loud so much as *total* — it didn't fill the air, it replaced it.

And underneath the hymn, the harbor began to scream.

Not the gulls first, though the gulls went quickly enough — their stolen voices stirring under a century of silence like embers under ash, tearing out of them in ragged half-tones that no gull throat had made since the Choir Wars. Noor staggered against a fish-stall and pressed his palms over his ears, which was useless, because it wasn't coming through his ears. It was coming through the gift. Through the thing he had hidden his whole life and could hide from nothing anymore.

*It hurts,* the gulls were saying, in voices cracking apart like ice. *It hurts, it's coming back, we don't want it back, make it stop—*

Because that was the clause. That was the dark arithmetic Ysolt had confessed in the marsh grass while Pell wept beside the eel-pools. If the Wolf failed to speak the pact by the appointed date, the beasts' voices wouldn't return as gifts. They'd return as a war-cry. A hundred years of stolen song coming home all at once, in pain, in fury, carrying the memory of every cage and hook and burning — and a city built of timber and pitch sitting right in the middle of it.

"Listen to me," Noor said to the nearest gull, crouching on the wet cobblestones while market-folk fled around him. He reached down into the grammar, into the old words Maren had taught him, and spoke aloud, which he had almost never done in daylight in his entire life. "*Still. Still. Not yet. Hold your voice one more hour.*"

The gull's rolling eye fixed on him. Its scream guttered, steadied, became something almost like speech.

"*Wolf,*" it said. "*You came.*"

"I came. One more hour. Then you can say everything you've ever needed to say."

He stood up and found the street staring at him. Fishwives, dockhands, a child with a herring still on its string. They had heard him speak to the bird. Some had heard the bird answer. In the old days — in any day until three weeks ago — that was a pyre. But the hymn was shaking crockery off shelves, and a cart-horse down the lane was screaming in a voice no horse had owned in living memory, and people were too busy being afraid of the impossible to arrest this particular impossibility.

A woman grabbed Noor's sleeve. "What's happening? What's *wrong* with them?"

"Nothing's wrong with them," Noor said. "Something's finally right. Get indoors. Bar your doors. And if you hear a wolf in the streets — don't run. She's on our side."

* * *

The undercity took them the rest of the way.

Pell's map was good, though Pell had drawn it for eels and eels don't mind water. The floods had swallowed the lower lanes generations ago, and Noor waded chest-deep through black channels lit by Ysolt's eyes and the sick green glow of Choir-lanterns bleeding down through grates overhead. Above them the hymn rolled on, wave after wave of it, and each wave made the water tremble and the drowned city groan, and each wave dragged the beasts' voices another inch toward the surface.

Rats ran the walls alongside them, dozens of them, keeping pace. Not speaking. Just present — a gray honor guard of the discarded, escorting him through the bones of the city that had outlawed them.

"They're frightened," Noor said. His voice echoed strangely off brick. "I can't tell if they're frightened of me or for me."

"Yes," Ysolt said. She swam better than he did, a dark head cutting the current ahead of him. "That's usually both."

They surfaced in the Cistern Row, one district shy of the Cathedral Hill, and that was where Noor saw the seals.

Choir-soldiers stood at every mouth of the square — not many, maybe thirty, but arranged in rings, and each ring was singing. Low harmony, the kind that didn't carry but *closed*: Noor felt it press against his gift like a hand pressing down on a bell. Inside the rings, nothing magical could move without ringing like a struck glass. The whole city was being sewn shut with thread made of song.

"We can't go through that," Noor said. "They'll feel me the moment I cross the line. I'm not hiding anything anymore. I'm the loudest thing in Blackwater."

"No," Ysolt agreed. She shook water from her coat, unhurried, and Noor realized she wasn't looking at the soldiers. She was looking at the cathedral spire above the rooftops, where the hymn came from. "We won't go through. We'll go *up*. Every seal is built to catch what creeps. Nothing about tonight has ever been quiet."

"What do you mean—"

The bells.

He understood one heartbeat before they rang. The Choir had silenced the cathedral bells a century ago along with everything else — melted some, cracked others, left one hanging mute as a monument. But bells remember their own weight, and the hymn shaking the city had been shaking the tower too, all night, harder and harder, and somewhere up in that bronze-dark dark, the great cracked bell called the Penitent had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this much vibration.

It rang.

The sound went through Noor like a spear of pure noise, through the hymn, through the seals, through the water standing in the streets — and every Choir-soldier in Cistern Row flinched, their harmony breaking for one ragged instant, thirty faces tilting upward in involuntary awe.

One instant. It would have to be enough.

"Run," Ysolt said, and they ran.

Up the processional stair, through the ring of singers whose mouths were still open on broken notes, past a young soldier who turned, saw Noor's face, saw the wolf beside him — and stepped aside. Stepped *aside*, eyes wide, hands falling away from his weapon, and Noor would think about that boy for the rest of his life.

The cathedral doors stood open. Of course they did. Tonight the Choir wanted the whole world listening.

* * *

Inside, the nave was a throat of stone and sound.

The hymn lived here at its full body: hundreds of white-robed singers ranked in the galleries, Inquisitors in ash-gray walking the aisles, and above the altar, suspended in a cage of silver wire that Noor recognized from a hundred guild manifests — *one live asset, voice-class, provenance disputed* — hung something that made his heart stop and restart.

Feathers. Storm-colored feathers. A storm-hawk, the first creature he had ever freed, back when he was a clerk balancing books by candlelight and thought the price of mercy was a favor.

It was screaming. Its true voice, torn loose by the hymn, and the sound was thunder learning words.

"Noor." Ysolt pressed close against his leg, her fur wet, her breath fast. "The altar. You have to reach the pulpit. Whatever happens—"

"I know the terms. I wrote out the pact-words forty times in the marsh mud."

"That's not what I was going to say." She looked up at him, and in the hymn-light her eyes were the same amber as the ink in the Codex of Ashes, and he understood suddenly that she had known this night a century before he was born, had written it down, had carried it alone through a hundred years of cages so that it could end either here or nowhere. "Whatever happens — I was glad it was you. Out of everyone the century could have chosen. I was glad."

"Ysolt—"

The gallery doors burst open behind them, and the hymn changed key.

Ash-gray robes flowed down the central aisle, and at their head walked Inquisitor Sera Voss, and she was not singing. That was the terrible thing. Everyone in the cathedral was singing except her, and her silence cut through the music like a blade through silk, and her eyes — those patient, reading eyes that had decoded his falsified ledgers line by line — found him across sixty feet of nave and did not waver.

"Wolf," she said, and her voice carried without effort, the way truth carries. "The Choir sealed this city to keep you from that pulpit. I have read your books, Noor Iversen. Every false entry. Every clever line of ink." She began to walk toward him, unhurried, and the hymn bent around her like current around a keel. "I know exactly what you're capable of. So before you take one more step — tell me why I shouldn't believe that everything in that Codex ends with this city burning."

Above them, the storm-hawk screamed. Below, the great cracked bell shuddered on its rope, gathering itself for another note. And in Noor's chest, under the wrong-knit ribs and the terror and the strange calm underneath it all, a hundred borrowed voices waited for him to open his mouth.

He stepped forward.
