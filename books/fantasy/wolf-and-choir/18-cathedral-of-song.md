## 18. Cathedral of Song

The hymn began before we reached the doors.

It came down the streets like weather, like a tide with a melody in it, and every living thing in the city flinched at once. I felt it in my teeth. I felt it in the places where my gift lived, the low rooms of me where the voices of animals had always made their home — the hymn went through those rooms like a landlord with a lantern, opening doors, looking for squatters.

*Hold still,* Ysolt said. She walked beside me on the flooded avenue, gray as the water, and even now, even fleeing, she moved like something that owned the ground it crossed. *Don't hide. Hiding is a shape. Shapes get seen.*

*Then what do I do?*

*Sing back,* she said. *Badly. Off-key. A wolf howling wrong is just a dog. A dog is nothing.*

So I did. I reached into the noise of the harbor — the gulls screaming their stolen screams, the rats in the seawall chattering their stolen chatter — and I pulled it all into me and let it out crooked, mangled, a drunk choir of everything I was. The hymn passed over me like a searchlight passing over a beggar. It found nothing worth stopping for.

It found Ysolt, though. Of course it did. She was a wolf the size of a pony walking openly through a city under seal, and she made no attempt at all to be anything else.

*They'll come for me,* she said, unbothered. *Good. While they're looking at me, no one looks at you. That was always the arithmetic, Noor. You're the ledger-keeper. Do the sum.*

*I don't accept the sum.*

*You never do,* she said, and there was something in it that was not quite fondness and not quite grief, the sound a century makes when it sighs. *That's why it's you.*

The Cathedral of Song rose out of the mist at the head of the processional avenue, and I had lived in this city my whole life without ever once seeing it properly. That was the trick of it, I understood now. The Choir had built their seat the way they built their hymns — to be heard and not seen, to fill the ear and slip the eye. Tonight the seal-hymn made it visible, the way a scream makes a throat visible. It was white stone and it was vast and its doors were open, and the hymn poured out of it like light from a wound.

We went in through the open doors, because there was nowhere else to go, and because the date was tonight, and because the prophecy had never once offered me a second option.

* * *

Inside, the cathedral was full of sound and empty of people.

The nave stretched away into gloom, pillars like a forest of pale trees, and the hymn lived in the vaulting above us — I could see it, now that I had stopped hiding, now that I had stopped pretending I was only a man. It moved in the air the way heat moves over a road. It had structure. It had *parts*. Somewhere above, in the organ loft or the singing galleries, the Choir was sustaining it, note after note after note, and every note was a question asked of every hidden thing in the city: *What are you? What are you? What are you?*

And the city was answering. I could hear it. That was the horror of the hymn, the thing no one had ever told me because no one untrained had ever heard it whole — the hymn didn't only search. It *listened*. And everything it touched answered whether it wanted to or not. The rats in the cathedral walls were answering. The pigeons in the belfry. The old blind cat that lived in the woodpile behind the guildhouse, miles away, was answering, her small cracked voice dragged up out of her by the melody like a splinter drawn from a wound. The whole city was a choir tonight, and it had never once been asked if it wanted to sing.

*They built it to find us,* Ysolt said. *A century ago we called it the Threshing Floor. You separate the wheat by making everything scream.*

*How do we stop it?*

*We don't stop it,* she said. *We answer it better.*

We had made it perhaps forty paces up the nave when the singing galleries went quiet.

Not all at once. It was like watching a wave flatten — the hymn thinned, wavered, and the silence that replaced it was worse, because silence in that building had a texture, and the texture was *attention*. I knew before I turned. Some animal part of me, older than my gift, older than my name, knew.

Sera Voss stood at the head of the nave, alone, her white choir-cloaks dark with harbor rain, and in her hand was not a weapon but a small silver tuning fork, and she was looking at Ysolt the way an appraiser looks at a forgery she has just realized is worth more than the original.

"You walked through my seal," she said. Her voice carried in the empty nave without effort, without echo, as if the building had been built to hold exactly that voice and nothing else. "A hundred of my Inquisitors are singing the Threshing Hymn above us, and you walked through it like a woman crossing a market square."

"She's old," I said. "Old things don't flinch."

Sera's eyes came to me. I had imagined this meeting a dozen times in the last weeks, in the ledgers of my fear, and in every version she was a monster, a functionary, a beautiful instrument of a beautiful machine. Standing in front of her, I understood that I had appraised her wrong, and I was a guild man, and being wrong in an appraisal had always been the unforgivable sin.

She was tired. That was the first thing. Tired the way a bell is tired after a century of ringing.

"And you," she said, "are the second voice."

* * *

"You heard me," I said. "At the appraisal. That's what this is. That's why you're here."

"I heard something at the appraisal," Sera said. She began to walk down the nave toward us, unhurried, her boots silent on the stone. "A wolf answering a wolf's question in a wolf's grammar, and then a second voice under it — wrong-shaped, small, human, *impossible*. I have been an Inquisitor for nineteen years. I have heard every kind of hidden gift this law was written to burn. I have never heard a human speak beast-tongue as a native. So I did what I always do." She stopped ten paces away. "I read the ledgers."

"Corvane burned them."

"Corvane burned the *true* ones." Something moved across her face that was almost a smile and was mostly grief. "Do you know what I found instead, Master Iversen? I found the false ones. The ones your clerk Tam misfiled for you, the ones with the beautiful little errors. And I sat in a ruined counting-house for two nights with a candle, doing arithmetic." She raised the tuning fork slightly, an officiant elevating a relic. "You falsified a wolf's transfer. You falsified it *badly on purpose in places and perfectly everywhere it mattered*, which is the signature of a man who is honest in his bones and lying with his hands. And every error pointed the same direction. Every road in your ink led to tonight, and to this building, and to the word the Codex of Ashes writes beside this date."

She knew about the Codex. Of course she knew about the Codex. The Choir had seized estates before the Guild ever had; they had probably catalogued the prophecies a century ago and simply *waited*, patient as interest accruing.

"You've come to stop me," I said.

"I've come to do my office," she said. "There's a difference. I've spent nineteen years telling myself there's a difference." She looked at Ysolt, and her voice changed — softened in a way that was somehow worse than the hardness. "A hundred and four years old, if the Codex's margins are honest. The last free voice of the old pact. Do you know what the Hymn of Finding does to a voice like hers, when we complete it? It doesn't kill. Killing is a mercy we stopped affording beasts after the Wars. It *files* her. It folds her voice into the Choir's own, and she sings with us forever, and she knows it, forever, and can do nothing."

The nave was very quiet. Above us, faintly, the hymn had begun again, building toward whatever its completion was, and I could feel it pulling at the edges of me like a tide pulling at a moored boat.

"Then don't complete it," I said.

"It is the law."

"The law is a ledger entry," I said, and heard my own voice crack on it, guild-man's grammar, counting-house courage. "I've kept ledgers my whole life, Inquisitor. I know what they are. Lines of ink that say *this happened* — and every one of them written by someone with a hand and a choice. Someone wrote the law. Someone can strike it through."

"By that argument," Sera said quietly, "anyone may strike through anything."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly the argument. That's the whole argument. That's what I climbed a cathedral to say."

And she hesitated.

I saw it. I was a man who had spent his life reading small marks on difficult pages, and I saw it: the half-second where her grip on the tuning fork shifted, where her eyes went up to the singing galleries and came back down, where nineteen years of office met one night of arithmetic and the sums did not agree. She was not a monster. She was a listener — that was the whole horror of the Choir, that they built it out of listeners, gifted ears turned to the one purpose of silencing what they heard. And the hymn above us was forcing her to listen right now, forcing her gifted ear wide open, and through that open ear was pouring everything the hymn touched: the rats, the pigeons, the blind cat, the gulls on the harbor screaming their stolen screams, the whole unquiet city answering the question *what are you* with a century of pain.

I watched her hear it. I watched her hear it *properly*, maybe for the first time — not as signal to be sorted, hidden thing to be found, but as what it was. Testimony.

"Inquisitor," I said, gently, the way I'd speak to a frightened animal, because that was what she was. "You hear it too. You've always heard it. That's why they gave you the white cloak — they take the people who hear best and they point them."

"Stand back," she said, and her voice was not steady anymore. "Both of you. The completion is in six minutes and I am *ordered*—"

The hymn surged.

* * *

Later — in the new ledgers, in the records we keep now of what is chosen — I will write this part honestly, because Ysolt has asked me to, because she says a record that flatters its subject is just a different kind of lie.

I froze.

The hymn crested above us, the completion beginning, the Threshing Floor turning, and every hidden voice in the city was dragged up into the light at once, and mine with them — I had stopped hiding, I had sung back badly, but the completed hymn was past all that, it was past craft, it was a hand closing around the base of my skull — and I froze. A ledger-keeper's instinct, the oldest one: when the audit comes, stand very still and hope they count someone else.

Ysolt did not freeze. Ysolt had been written about for a hundred years and had written half of it herself, and she knew what the text said, and she moved like something that had read its own ending and found it acceptable.

The Choir's completion was not a sound, exactly. It was a falling — a note the size of the nave, coming down like a portcullis, meant to close over every found voice and fold it in. I felt it coming for me the way you feel a door closing on light.

Ysolt put her body in its path.

She did it the way she had done everything — without ceremony, without speech, without asking. One moment she was beside me, gray and enormous and patient; the next she was between me and the hymn, head up, throat bared, and she *sang into it*. Not a howl. The old grammar, the pact-grammar, the first tongue, the one the beasts had bargained away and she had never entirely surrendered — she poured it upward into the falling note like water thrown at a fire, and the note *broke around her*, and the breaking of it went through her like a spear goes through a body.

The sound she made was not a sound. I have no ledger line for it. It was a hundred years leaving.

She came down on the cathedral stones like a felled tree, and the hymn above us staggered — I heard it stagger, heard the hundred Inquisitors in the galleries lose the thread, because the thread had run through her, because the completion had been tuned to the last free voice and the last free voice had just spent itself breaking it.

I was on my knees beside her before I knew I had moved.

*Noor.* Her voice in my head was very small now, very far away, a harbor light seen from a losing ship. *Don't do the thing where you refuse the name. Not now. There's no time left to be modest in.*

"You're not—" My hands were in her fur and there was blood, there was so much blood, a century's worth of it arriving all at once. "Ysolt. *Ysolt.*"

*The pulpit,* she said. *The pact is a spoken thing. It was always a spoken thing — that was the whole crime, that's why they took our voices, because the pact lives in the throat and not the page. Pages can be seized. Throats have to be answered.* Her breath was a wet, ragged bellows. *Get up, Wolf. Get up and be what I wrote.*

"Stay," I said, and it was not an order, it was a beggar's arithmetic, the last coin I had.

*Ledger-keeper,* she said, and the ghost of the old dry humor moved through her like wind through a ruined house. *Do the sum.*

* * *

I got up.

Sera Voss was still standing ten paces away, and the tuning fork had fallen from her hand, and she was looking at Ysolt's body on the stones with an expression I will carry to my own grave: the expression of an appraiser who has finally, at the end of a long career, valued something correctly and too late.

"The completion is broken," she said. Her voice was almost nothing. "They'll re-tune. They'll begin again. You have minutes, Master Iversen. Perhaps less."

"Then arrest me," I said. "That's your office. That's the law. I'm a beast-speaker standing in your cathedral with the blood of the last free voice on my hands. Strike me through. It's what the ink says."

She looked at me for a long moment. Above us the hymn was gathering itself again, note by note, a machine reassembling its own wheel.

"I spent nineteen years," she said, "listening for hidden things." She bent, and picked up the fallen tuning fork, and did not strike it. "And the whole time, the loudest thing in the city was the sound of everything we'd silenced, still speaking. Still testifying. Still waiting for one person to answer." She stepped aside. The whole nave opened before me — the pillars, the pulpit, the great rose window above it going pale with the hour before dawn. "The law says I stop you. The law is a ledger entry. Someone wrote it."

She did not say *strike it through*. She didn't have to. She was a listener, and I was a speaker, and between us, in the ruined acoustics of that terrible beautiful building, the whole argument had already been made.

I walked toward the pulpit with Ysolt's blood drying on my hands, and behind me I heard her say, quietly, to the galleries above, to the hundred Inquisitors re-tuning their wheel:

"Hold the completion. That is an order. Hold it and *listen*."

And I climbed the steps, and the city outside was screaming, and the date was tonight, and there was only one line left to write.
