## 19. The Wolf Speaks

The pulpit was older than the cathedral.

I knew that because I had catalogued its twin, once — a carved oak lectern seized from a marsh chapel and sold to a collector in the Vellum Guild's better years, before my time. The Guild's ledger had described it as *carved with beasts in attitudes of praise*, and I had laughed at the phrase, because what else would you call it. Beasts did not praise. Beasts endured. That was the whole architecture of my life: endurance, filed under other names.

The carvings on this one had been chiseled away. Someone, sometime in the last century, had taken a hammer to every animal face on it until only smooth wounds remained, wood scarred pale where the shapes used to be. My hands found them as I climbed — little craters of erased testimony — and I understood that this pulpit had been built for exactly what I was about to do, and that they had known it too, whoever *they* were, a hundred years ago. They hadn't burned the pulpit. You don't burn a pulpit; you need somewhere to stand while you tell people what God wants. They had just removed the faces. Made it safe. Made it speak only in human.

The steps were twelve. I counted them because counting kept me upright. Ysolt's blood was drying tacky between my fingers, and every step sent a white wire of pain up through my ribs where Sera's hymn had found me — not a wound exactly, not yet, but the memory of one being written into me in real time, ink still wet. The detection hymn didn't cut flesh. It cut the part of you that hid. And I had been hiding since I was six years old, so there was a great deal of me to cut.

At the top I turned around.

The Cathedral of Song at night, lit by a thousand candles and one impossible hymn, is not a building. It is an instrument that has forgotten it is also a room. The Choir stood in the galleries in their grey vestments, a hundred throats holding the completion-hymn like a bow held drawn, arrow nocked, aimed down at everything alive in the harbor. Below me, the nave floor was empty of people but not of presence: rats along the wainscoting, gulls battering the high windows, cats poured in through broken grates, dogs that had followed the screaming through streets sealed by song. The beasts had come to the cathedral the way water comes to a low place. Nobody had called them. The hymn itself had called them — by hurting them.

And outside the walls, the city. The harbor. Every caged thing, every muzzled thing, every voice that had been bargained away a century ago and had begun, tonight, at the appointed date, to claw its way home. I could hear them through the stone. Not words. Pressure. A tide made of throats.

Sera Voss stood at the foot of the pulpit steps, her hand raised toward the gallery, holding the Choir at *hold*. She was looking at me the way I imagine you look at a ledger entry that contradicts every other entry in the book — not with hatred. With the hunger of someone whose entire accounting has just failed to balance, and who needs, more than revenge or victory, to know *why*.

"Whatever you're going to say," she said, "say it in human. If you say it in the other tongue, they will complete the hymn, and the hymn does not distinguish between speakers and beasts. It unmakes both."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

"Noor." My name, from her mouth, sounded like an error she'd found in her own handwriting. "You are wounded. You are alone. The wolf is dying on your cathedral floor. Whatever the prophecy promised you—"

"The prophecy didn't promise me anything." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "It conscripted me. There's a difference, Inquisitor, and you of all people should know it. You read ledgers. Read this one."

I put my bloodied hands on the pulpit's scarred edge, over the places where the faces used to be.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about speaking in two tongues: it isn't translation. Translation is ferrying meaning across a river, and something always drowns in transit or arrives soaked. Speaking in both tongues at once is standing in the river and discovering the two banks were never separate — that human language and beast-language are not different languages at all but one language sung at two depths, and the pact, the ancient bargain, the law that had outlawed my gift since before my grandmother's grandmother, was nothing more than the claim that the depths don't connect.

They connect. They had always connected. I was the connection, walking and bleeding.

Ysolt had taught me the grammar in the vault and in the undercity and in the long starving weeks among the rooftops: how beast-speech carries its tense in the body, its case in the direction of the ears, its truth-value in whether the throat is open or closed. How humans hide inside their words, and beasts cannot hide at all, which is why the pact had been signed in the first place — because the old wars were fought between creatures who could lie and creatures who could not, and the liars won, and then felt bad about it, and wrote the bad feeling down as law.

The pact was not a treaty. It was a confession that got promoted.

So when I opened my mouth on that pulpit, I did not begin with argument. Argument was for people who could be argued with, and the Choir's whole art was arguing magic out of existence. I began where all honest ledgers begin.

"In the matter of the pact between humanity and the silenced," I said, and heard my own voice do something it had never done — carry in both registers at once, the human word riding the beast-tone underneath it like a keel under a sail, "*I am entering the following into the record.*"

The hymn faltered. One note. One throat in the upper gallery lost its nerve, and a hairline crack ran through the completion-chord, and in that crack I heard a dog in the nave whine, and a rat behind the wainscot go still, and the tide outside press closer against the stones.

"The pact was made in the year the wars ended," I said. "The terms as recorded: the beasts surrendered their voices. In exchange, humanity swore — swore, on whatever humanity swears by — that no beast would be hunted for having a voice, because none would have one. That silence would buy safety."

I looked down at Sera. "Read me the amendment, Inquisitor. You know it by heart. Your order enforces it."

She said nothing. Her silence was an answer, and the whole cathedral heard it, because cathedrals are built to make silences audible.

"There is no amendment," I said. "There never was. For a hundred years we have been taking their silence and spending it — on laws against the ones who kept a whisper of it, on cages, on fires, on hymns that hunt children in their beds. The beasts paid in advance. We never paid at all. And now the term has come due, and the voices are coming home, and the question of this night—" my ribs sang their white wire of pain, and I let it into my voice, let it be evidence, "—the question of this night is not whether the beasts will take those voices back as a war-cry. That was the prophecy's threat-clause, written by someone who loved us enough to plan for our worst faith. The question is whether anyone will stand in the space between and offer the alternative."

"And the alternative is?" Sera asked. Quietly. Genuinely asking. I will give her that until I die.

"Witness," I said. "Not war. Not silence. Witness."

* * *

Then I stopped speaking human.

Not entirely — I want to be precise about this, because precision is all the Guild ever taught me and it turns out to be the only thing worth carrying out of a burning life. The human words continued. But underneath them, in the register the Choir's own training had taught Sera to hear, I opened the other depth, and I spoke the pact again, clause by clause, in the tongue of the ones who had signed it away.

*I, of the speaking kind,* I said in the old grammar, my body doing the tenses, my open throat carrying the truth-value, *acknowledge the debt. The voices you lent to end the war were never payment. They were collateral. We took them as payment anyway. This is entered into the record.*

The crack in the hymn widened. Two more throats dropped out. Somewhere above me an Inquisitor was shouting something about heresy and completion, and the shouting bounced off stone and came back smaller than it went in, because this room had been built for exactly one kind of sound and it was deciding, tonight, to learn another.

*We do not ask you to forgive,* I said. *Forgiveness is a human currency and we have debased it. We ask you to witness, and to be witnessed. Come forward. Say what was done. Let it be entered. Let it be true in a place with walls, in a book with pages, in a law with names.*

And the city answered.

Not all at once. First the gulls — a ragged cry through the high windows, and the cry was not a cry, it was a deposition: *the nets, the hooks, the oil dumped in our feeding water, the chicks that starved with full seas below them.* Then the rats, a rustling chorus from the wainscot, testifying to poison and trap and the boot and the fire in the granaries, each small voice precise as a clerk. The dogs baying in the nave gave the account of the kennels. The horses stamping in the square beyond the doors told of harnesses. Cat-voices from the rafters. Oxen lowing miles off, carried on the hymn's dying acoustics. And under all of it, far out in the harbor, the great deep register I had no bones to hold — whales, or things older — giving testimony in a tone that cracked mortar dust from the ceiling.

Every silenced voice in the city, answering at once. And here is the thing the prophecy got wrong, or maybe the thing it hoped for and couldn't write without jinxing it: none of it was a war-cry. It was worse than a war-cry. A war-cry you can answer with armies. What came through those walls was *accounting*. A hundred years of entries, read aloud, itemized, dated, in the voices of the creditors. The Choir had spent a century perfecting the art of making hidden things burn like lanterns.

Tonight the lanterns testified back.

* * *

The hymn shattered.

I don't mean it faded. I mean it broke the way glass breaks — all at once, from a single flaw, after holding impossibly long. The single flaw was Sera Voss. I saw it happen. She stood at the foot of the pulpit with her gifted ear pinned open by her own doctrine, forced to hear what she could hear better than any of them: the pain in the testimony, the truth-values all reading true, the debt all reading real. Her hand, which had been holding the *hold* order, turned slowly palm-upward — the conductor's gesture for *release* — and the completion-hymn, unheld, unfinished, met a hundred years of unpaid testimony and came apart mid-chord.

A hundred grey vestments stood in the galleries in sudden ordinary silence, and in that silence the last line of my speech arrived on its own, the way the last line of any honest entry does — not composed but discovered.

"The pact is broken," I said, in both tongues, my blood on the wood, my voice wrecked. "Not by the beasts. By the keeping of it. As of tonight there is no law of silence. There is only what we choose to enter next. I renounce secrecy — mine, yours, everyone's. Let the record show that the Wolf spoke, and the pack answered, and no one burned."

I stepped back from the pulpit. My legs informed me they had been borrowed against and were calling the loan.

Below me, Sera Voss looked up, and her face was doing something complicated that I would spend weeks understanding: she was watching a law die and finding that she was relieved, and ashamed of the relief, and relieved by the shame, a whole cascade of balances correcting at once. Around her, Inquisitors were kneeling — some of them, a third of them, the listeners, the ones who had always heard too much — while others backed toward the doors already rehearsing the reports that would call this night a riot, a seduction, a trick.

None of that mattered. What mattered was on the floor of the nave, where a grey wolf lay in a spreading pool of her own old blood, and her eyes were open, and they had been open the whole time, and she had heard every word.

*Well spoken,* Ysolt said, in the grammar that needed no air. *Badly timed, badly dressed, half-dead. But well spoken.*

*I had a good editor,* I said.

And as I started down the twelve steps toward her — toward the blood, toward the debt, toward the first page of whatever came next — the great doors of the Cathedral of Song swung inward, and the city poured in: not soldiers, not mobs, but clerks and fishwives and dockhands and children, humans drawn out of their houses by the sound of a century of testimony, come to see whether the world had ended or merely been corrected.

Behind them, in the doorway, small and singed and free, holding a misfiled ledger page like a passport, stood Tam.

He looked at the kneeling Inquisitors, the shattered hymn, the wolf, the blood, the boy from the ledgers standing on the pulpit stairs, and he said, in the voice of a man who has finally guessed exactly as much as there is to guess:

"So. Do I file this under assets, or miracles?"
