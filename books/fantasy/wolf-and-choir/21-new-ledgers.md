## 21. New Ledgers

The sign above the door still said VELLUM GUILD, because I hadn't been able to afford a new one. Underneath it, in paint Tam had mixed himself from lampblack and linseed oil, someone had added a second line in letters that were deliberately, defiantly crooked:

*AND HOUSE OF OPEN TERMS.*

Tam was proud of the crookedness. "Straight letters look official," he'd said. "Official looks like a lie waiting to happen." I had decided not to argue with him about typography. He had earned his opinions the hard way — three weeks in a Choir holding cell, during which he had given up nothing except a misfiled page he claimed, to this day, to have misplaced honestly. Sera Voss's final report had named him *a witness of no further interest*, which Tam had framed and hung over his desk.

The guildhouse smelled different now. That was the first thing anyone noticed when they came up from the street. For sixty years the place had smelled of vellum dust and sealing wax and Mistress Corvane's clove cigarettes, a smell like money being counted in the dark. Now it smelled of wet fur and river water and the tallow candles Ysolt preferred because their smoke didn't sting her nose. It smelled, Tam said, like a barn that had learned to read.

I was at my old desk by the window, balancing the morning's entries, when the first client of the day arrived: a harbor gull with a fishhook through her foot and a grievance against the fishmongers of Salt Row.

"Terms," she said, in the flat grammar of birds, which sounds to human ears like crying and means something closer to contract law. *"Hook. Blood. Three days' work lost. They owe."*

I wrote it down. That was the whole method, really — the innovation nobody had believed would work until it did. A beast states its claim. I translate. The human party answers. We write the terms where both sides can see them, in ink, on paper, filed in a ledger that anyone may read on request. No secrets. No seized estates. No century-old pacts enforced by silence and threat.

The fishmonger, a broad woman named Hesk who had lost two crates of herring to gulls the previous summer, came down from Salt Row at noon and stood in my office glaring at the gull perched on my windowsill.

"It's a bird," she said.

"She's a claimant," I said. "Her name is Skerrit. She works the harbor shallows, drives fish toward the nets of boats that share their catch. She wants the hook removed, a poultice, and compensation of one herring per day for three days."

Hesk looked at me the way people had looked at me for weeks now — half wonder, half the suspicion that they were being conned. Then she looked at the gull, and the gull looked back with one yellow eye, patient as a magistrate.

"One herring," Hesk said finally. "And she stays off my roof."

*"Roof is mine anyway,"* said Skerrit, but she said it quietly, the way one concedes a point for the sake of the record.

I wrote the terms. Both parties made their marks — Hesk with a signature, Skerrit with a slash of her beak through the ink I poured into a saucer. And then, because the new house had rules, I carried the page across the hall to the vault.

* * *

Ysolt lay on her side in what had once been the deepest cell in Vellum House, the room where Corvane had kept her caged and appraised her like an asset. We had done what we could to unmake the cage without tearing down the building: the bars were gone, the door stood open day and night, and the floor was deep with rushes and the furs Tam bought at scandalous discount from a tanner who owed the old guild money. But Ysolt's hind leg had not healed right. The Choir's hymn-shard, the one Sera's junior Inquisitor had loosed in the cathedral as she fell, had gone deep, and beast-flesh mends slowly around human iron.

She spent most days lying in the rushes, and most nights standing at the open door looking out at the city she could not yet run through. She signed nothing quickly anymore. She read everything twice.

"The gull again?" she said as I knelt beside her with the page.

"Herring dispute. Settled fair."

"Read me the terms."

I read them. She listened with her ears moving independently, the way she did when she was checking a statement for lies, though there were no lies in a herring settlement. When I finished she said, "Witnessed," and pressed her paw print beneath Skerrit's beak-slash, and that was the co-signatory's part, the part we had written into the house's charter in the first week: *no term between human and beast shall stand without a beast's witness.* Half the city thought it was theater. The other half had begun, slowly, to bring their disputes to a door they would once have burned.

"You favor that leg," I said, as she settled back. "You've been favoring it since Tuesday."

"I have been favoring it since the cathedral," she said. "You notice more on Tuesdays."

It was the closest thing to a joke she made. Her humor had come back before her strength, thin and dry as winter grass. I checked the wound while she let me — she always let me, and never liked it — and found it clean, knitting at its own stubborn pace. A hundred years old and healing like something that intended to outlast the century too.

"Corvane's memorial is Saturday," I said.

"I know. The rats told me."

Of course they had. The rats told Ysolt everything now; they had adopted her the way orphans adopt a lighthouse. "Are you coming?"

She was quiet a moment. Outside the open vault door, evening was coming down over the rooftops, and somewhere above us Skerrit was flying home to a roof she technically owned.

"I will stand at the back," Ysolt said. "Wolves do not do well at memorials. Too much grief in one room smells like weakness, and I am tired of smelling like anything."

* * *

Mistress Odile Corvane had no grave, because she had left instructions that her ashes go into the harbor, off the pier where she had conducted her first trade at nineteen years old — a stolen horse, according to Tam, traded back to its owner for double its worth and a promise of silence. So the memorial was held on that pier, on a cold bright Saturday with the water slapping the pilings, and perhaps forty people came, which was thirty-nine more than any of us expected.

They were an odd congregation. Clerks and debtors she had quietly forgiven over the years. Two Inquisitors, plain-clothed, who stood apart and left early. A woman from the Fishmongers' Guild who claimed Corvane had once fixed a price-fixing scandal in her favor out of pure spite for a rival. And beasts, along the pier's far rail, where the gulls lined up in a row so straight it looked rehearsed, and a seal bobbed in the gray water, and the rats — dozens of them, more than I had ever seen gathered in one place — sat along a mooring rope like a jury.

Tam spoke first. He had written a eulogy and abandoned it, and instead he just said: "She sold me out. And then she burned the true ledger rather than let the Choir have it, and that act bought every one of us our lives. I have decided those are both true, and I have decided to stop trying to make them fit together." He looked out at the water. "She'd have appreciated the terms."

Then it was my turn, and I found I had nothing prepared either. What I had instead was the margin.

I had brought the new master ledger — the first book of the reopened house — and I turned to the inside back cover, where the margins lived. In the old guild, margins were where Corvane recorded things that could never appear in the body of an entry: the bribe implied, the favor owed, the name crossed out but not forgotten. I had kept the habit, but changed its purpose. Our margins now held the debts that couldn't be priced.

"In the matter of the estate of Odile Corvane," I said, reading aloud so the gulls could hear it and carry it, "the following entry stands in the margin of this house's books, permanent and unpriced: *Paid in full — one true ledger, burned; one life, traded for many; one secret, kept until it could be given away safely.* The house acknowledges the debt. The house does not attempt repayment, because she would find that vulgar. Instead the house keeps her margins."

I tipped the urn over the pier's edge. The ashes went out onto the water in a gray ribbon, and the tide took them toward the open sea, and the seal dove under the ribbon and came up on the far side of it, which might have been accident and might have been ceremony. With the seals you never knew.

Ysolt stood at the back, as promised, up by the pier's root where the cobblestones began. She did not come closer. But when the ashes scattered, she lifted her head and sang — low, brief, four notes, the old grammar's word for *account closed*. The gulls took it up and carried it out over the harbor, note by note, passing it along like a ledger line traveling down a row of clerks, until the last of it faded somewhere past the breakwater.

Forty humans stood on a pier listening to wolvesong over Odile Corvane's ashes, and not one of them called the Choir.

That was the measure of how much had changed. Or of how much we were gambling hadn't changed only temporarily. I hadn't yet learned to tell the difference.

* * *

Sera Voss came to the house on Monday.

I saw her through the window before she reached the door — walking unhurried, out of uniform, wearing a gray coat that made her look like what she now was: a private citizen with an excellent memory and a resignation letter on file. Tam saw her too, and his pen stopped mid-entry, and the whole front office went quiet in the way rooms do when a predator walks in, even a retired one.

"Voss," I said, when she stood at my desk. "We're not buying indulgences today."

"I'm not selling any." She sat without being asked, which was very like her. Up close she looked thinner than she had in the cathedral, and older, as if resigning had cost her weight the way wounds cost blood. "I'm here on house business. Actual business. Don't look like that — I've read your charter. Anyone may bring a claim."

"A former Inquisitor bringing a claim to a house of beasts."

"A former Inquisitor," she said, "who can still hear things other people can't. You of all people should understand that the gift doesn't resign when you do."

I waited. She took a folded paper from her coat and laid it on the desk between us, and did not unfold it.

"There are others," she said. "Inquisitors who knelt in the cathedral that night, and Inquisitors who ran, and Inquisitors who are still singing the old hymns in the provincial chapters because no one has told them the law is dead — or because they've decided not to believe it. The hymn isn't a song, Iversen. You know that better than anyone. It's an architecture. It took a hundred years to build and it doesn't fall because one woman filed a report."

"What's the claim?"

She unfolded the paper at last. On it, in her precise hand, was a transcription — musical notation interleaved with words in the old grammar, the grammar Ysolt had been teaching me on rooftops all those weeks ago. I recognized maybe a third of it. Enough to feel the floor tilt.

"This is a fragment of the detection hymn," she said. "Recovered from the provincial chapter at Greywater. Except it's been altered. Someone has rewritten the architecture — inverted the harmonics. The original hymn found hidden gifts and burned them out. This version finds gifts and *marks* them. Quietly. The bearer never knows. The singer keeps a list."

My mouth had gone dry. "A list of beast-speakers."

"A list of everyone," Sera said. "Speakers, listeners, anyone the new hymn can tag. And the fragment is recent, Iversen. The ink analysis puts it within the month. Someone is building the next Choir out of the wreckage of the last one, and they're starting with a song designed to be invisible." She leaned back. "I can't investigate it. I resigned. I have no warrant, no chapter, no authority. But your house takes claims from anyone, and as of this morning I'm claiming victimhood — I heard the altered hymn sung outside Greywater eleven days ago, and it marked me. I felt it. Like a hook going in behind the ear."

Behind me, from the direction of the vault, I heard the soft weight of paws on stone. Ysolt had come to the doorway without my noticing, favoring her leg, her head lowered, her nostrils working as she read the paper's scent from across the room.

"That melody," she said. "I know that inversion. It's mine."

Sera turned. For a moment the two of them regarded each other — the hunter and the prophesied wolf, the woman who had struck her down and the beast who had forgiven it, or at least outlived it.

"A century ago," Ysolt said, "when I bargained our voices away, I built a failsafe into the pact's grammar. A way to mark bad faith. To mark *anyone* who sang against the silenced." Her ears flattened. "Someone has found it. Someone is using my own insurance policy as their ledger."

The office was very quiet. Out on the street, ordinary traffic went by — carts, voices, a dog barking at nothing. Skerrit landed on the windowsill with her three herrings' worth of dignity and cocked her head at the folded paper.

"So file the claim," I said at last, and pulled a fresh page toward me, and dipped the pen. "Let's get it on the record."

Sera slid the fragment across the desk. And in the margin, before I wrote anything else, I noted the date — and recognized it, the way you recognize a footstep behind you in the dark.

It was ninety-one days from the appointed date. Ninety-one days from the night the pact was due to be spoken, the deadline written in a dead century's hand.

Whoever was rebuilding the hymn knew the calendar too.
