## 22. The First Blank Page

The guildhouse smelled of wet ash and new paper.

Three weeks after the night the hymn broke, the masons were still pulling charred beams out of the east wing where the Choir's retreat had caught fire — Sera Voss insisted it was an accident of collapsing scaffolding, and no one in the city had the appetite to argue with her about it. The west wing stood untouched. The ledgers survived. Of course they did; Corvane had built Vellum House to outlast everything except its own conscience, and even that had taken a fire to finish.

I sat at my old desk by the window with a candle I no longer needed. Morning light came through glass that Tam had replaced himself, crooked in its frame, letting in a draft that smelled like the harbor. Gulls argued on the roofline. Below them, in the yard, a cart horse complained to a sparrow about the quality of the oats — I heard it all now without trying, the way you hear your own name. The gift didn't hide anymore. It didn't roar either. It was just there, like hearing, like breathing, like the ache in my ribs where Ysolt's weight had nearly broken me carrying her out of the cathedral.

She was healing. Slowly, the way old things heal.

*Come up,* she said from the roof, where she'd spent every morning since the physicians let her climb stairs. *Bring ink.*

*I always bring ink.*

*Bring the good ink. The one that doesn't smell like regret.*

Tam bought that ink from a stationer on Cordwainer Street who charged double for anything described as moral. I brought it anyway.

* * *

Ysolt lay along the ridge of the roof with her grey flank pressed to warm slate, one ear tracking the gulls, the other tracking me as I came through the attic hatch and out onto the leads. Her foreleg was still bound. The wound where the hymn's edge had caught her — a hymn can cut, when it breaks; everyone learned that the hard way — was closing at last, but she walked with a hitch she pretended not to have, and I pretended not to notice, because that was our arrangement and we honored our arrangements now. That was rather the whole point of us.

In her jaws, held with the exaggerated care of a creature who could crack bone but chose not to, was the Codex of Ashes.

I stopped halfway across the roof.

"You've had that the whole time."

*Since the marshes,* she said. *Since before. Since the woman who owned it died and I carried it out of the smoke, and then I carried it a hundred years, and then I put it in a seized estate's library because I thought — * She paused. Wolves don't sigh, exactly, but they do something with their breath that means the same thing. *I thought if I ever found the Wolf, he would find his way to it. Prophecy is a lure, Noor. You set it where the quarry will walk.*

"You used me as bait."

*I used a book as bait. You were the one who read it.* She nudged it toward me across the slate. It was lighter than I remembered, or I was stronger than I'd been. The cover was scorched at one corner, ash-grey leather gone soft as cloth, and the clasp hung open because the clasp had been broken since before either of us was born. *Open it.*

"I know what's in it."

*You know what's in the first part. Look at the end.*

I looked at the end. I had looked at that final page so many times in the last months that I could have recited it: tonight's date, my own name, the word *Wolf*, written in ink that had dried a century before my mother met my father. But past it — past the entry that had conscripted my whole life into someone else's sentence — the pages continued. Blank. Dozens of them. I had never once turned past the prophecy, because why would you? A prophecy ends. That's what makes it a prophecy.

Except it hadn't ended. Someone had left room.

"What is this?"

*The second half,* Ysolt said. *The half I never wrote. When I dictated the first half to that poor burned man — may his name be spoken freely now, and often — I told him the future because the future was all I had. I knew what the Choir would do. I knew what the pact would cost. I wrote down every terrible thing I could see coming, because terror is very easy to transcribe.* Her good ear flicked. *But I left the rest blank on purpose. Because I couldn't see past the Wolf speaking. Not because it was hidden from me. Because it hadn't happened yet, and I refused to write it in advance. A hundred years ago I understood something I should have told you sooner: that the difference between a prophecy and a ledger is only whether anyone gets to answer back.*

I sat down on the slate beside her. The city spread out below — the harbor with its cranes, the cathedral with its broken hymn-arches still draped in scaffold cloth, the rooftops where pigeons conducted their endless petty commerce. Somewhere in it, Tam was arguing with a lumber merchant about the price of shelving. Somewhere in it, Sera Voss was writing the last report of her career, though I didn't know that yet. Somewhere in it, in the marshes, the survivors who had begged me not to speak were waking to a world where their children could hear them, and not all of them were glad, and not all of them were sorry, and all of them were going to have to learn to live inside the silence they'd chosen being over.

"So this is what you meant," I said. "*Records of what is chosen.*"

*That's what I asked for. In the vault, when you asked what I wanted done with the Codex, and you thought I said destroy it because you weren't listening properly.* There was no rebuke in it. There was, possibly, a small amount of rebuke in it. *I want a sequel, Noor. Not prophecies. Records. Things that actually happened, written down after, by someone who was there, in a hand that admits it was a hand. The beasts kept our history in memory for a century because we had no other place to keep it. Memory is a terrible archive. It flatters itself. It loses the boring parts, and the boring parts are where the truth lives.*

"Ledgers," I said.

*Ledgers,* she agreed, with deep satisfaction. *You are the finest ledger-keeper I have ever known, and I have known three centuries of accountants, most of them dead by their own arithmetic. Write it honestly. Write the parts where you were a coward. Write the part where Corvane—*

"I will."

*And write the part where I lied to you in the vault, and the part where you forgave me too quickly because you needed an ally more than you needed the truth. All of it. If this house is going to be a house of terms, then the first term is that nobody edits themselves into a hero.*

I opened the Codex to the first blank page. The paper had yellowed evenly, patient as an unlit candle. My pen hovered over it, and for a moment — just a moment — I felt the old vertigo come back, the sense of being already written, of moving through a story some dead century had composed for me. The word *Wolf* sat thirty pages behind my hand like a brand.

Then I thought about what a ledger actually is. Not fate. Not a cage. Just a promise that someone, later, will be able to check.

I wrote:

*On this day, nobody was silent.*

* * *

It took the rest of the morning to write the first entry, and I will reproduce none of it here except to say that it began with the storm-hawk — the first impossible thing I ever traded for, back when I still believed my gift was a debt I was hiding rather than a voice I was withholding — and that the hawk herself reviewed the draft, perched on my windowsill, and demanded two corrections regarding the size of the bribe I claimed to have paid on her behalf. History, it turns out, has editors.

By noon the yard below had filled with the usual traffic of the new Vellum House: a delegation of harbor rats with a grievance about grain tariffs, which Tam took down in shorthand while visibly trying not to scream; a mule with a contract dispute; two crows acting as notaries, because crows will witness anything for shiny objects and are, annoyingly, incorruptible about the actual witnessing. A century of silenced testimony was coming out of the woodwork of the city, and most of it, it must be said, was about oats.

Ysolt watched from the roof until her leg tired, then came down and installed herself in the front hall, where she had appointed herself doorkeeper, arbiter, and terror of dishonest petitioners. A wolf who has read a hundred years of human ledgers turns out to be an excellent judge of character. She could smell a false claim the way she smelled fear, and she had no patience whatsoever for either.

Near evening, a knock came that made the whole hall go quiet.

Sera Voss stood on the step in plain clothes, no choir-grey, no insignia. She held a document case under her arm the way she'd once held a baton — out of habit, not need. Behind her the street was empty, but I could hear, faint and far off, the sound of singing: not a hymn, not a hunt. Just people. The cathedral choirs had reopened that week, and for the first time in a hundred years, the audition requirements said nothing at all about what kind of throat the singer had.

"Inquisitor," I said.

"Not anymore." She held out the case. "My final report. Filed this morning. You're named in it."

"As a criminal?"

"As a witness." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; Sera Voss did not smile so much as permit her face to briefly acknowledge an event. "There's a copy for your records. The Guild keeps records now, I'm told. I thought you'd want the primary source."

I took the case. Inside was her report, forty pages in a hand so precise it looked printed, and beneath it — this she had not mentioned — a single sheet of music. Unfinished. A few bars, then blank staves.

"For the new hymn," she said, before I could ask. "The one they're writing for the reopening. They asked former Inquisitors to contribute, which is either reconciliation or a very elaborate insult, and I haven't decided which. I got as far as the opening phrase and stopped." She looked past me, into the hall, at Ysolt, and inclined her head — the smallest possible bow, from a woman who had once sung cities open. "I find I can't write the next note alone anymore. Apparently that's the whole lesson. Took me long enough."

Ysolt's tail moved once against the floorboards. *Tell her the wolves will listen to her draft,* she said. *Tell her we always listened better than her colleagues did.*

"She says the wolves will listen."

For a moment Sera Voss closed her eyes, and I understood that I had just handed her something she had been trying to confiscate her entire career: permission.

"The date," she said, recovering, brisk again, turning to go. "The anniversary. Next year. The cathedral wants to mark it — publicly, formally, with the beasts present and the terms read aloud. They'll want you to speak."

"They can want."

"They'll also want it entered in the record beforehand. Draft text, submitted for review." She paused on the step, and this time the almost-smile arrived in full. "Old habits. Review the text before it becomes history. I told them I knew someone who'd insist on it."

After she was gone, I stood in the doorway a long time, holding the report, listening to the distant singing and the near argument of the rats, who had escalated their tariff dispute to the level of personal insults and were demanding arbitration.

Ysolt limped over and leaned her shoulder against my leg — the uninjured side, always the uninjured side, pride being the last wound to close.

*Well?* she said. *What will you tell them?*

"That the review committee meets Thursday," I said, "and that the Wolf will attend, and that the Wolf's notes are already late."

*Your notes are never late.*

"These ones are." I went back inside, to the desk, to the candle I still lit out of habit and the daylight I no longer needed. The Codex lay open to the second page of the sequel, which I had headed that morning with a title and nothing else. I dipped the pen. Outside, the gulls settled; the rats reached a provisional settlement, pending appeal; somewhere across the city, in a cathedral full of scaffolding and hope, someone sang a phrase no one had written yet.

I wrote the heading, and under it, the first line of the first record of the world as it was actually chosen:

*On this day, nobody was silent.*
