## 1. The Last Tollhouse

There are three things a smuggler learns before he learns anything else worth knowing.

First: never carry what you can't afford to lose. Second: never sleep in a bed you didn't pay for with coin you didn't steal. And third — this is the one they carve into your bones, usually with a belt buckle or a broken bottle — never trust a road that's been made legal. Legal roads have wardens on them, and wardens have ledgers, and ledgers are where freedom goes to be itemized and sold back to you by the yard.

I was thinking about all three of these lessons at roughly four in the morning, crouched behind a deadfall of pine on the high shoulder of Thornfell Pass, watching three of Queen Aurelian's new road wardens pick their way up the old toll road with lanterns lit like it was a festival instead of a manhunt. The queen had been on her throne for two years now, and in those two years she'd done something no monarch before her had managed: she'd made the mountains boring. Paved roads. Posted tariffs. Hung signs at every pass reading *BY DECREE OF THE CROWN, ALL GOODS MUST BE DECLARED* — as though the mountain itself could read, and as though anything born under Thornfell had ever once declared itself honestly to anybody.

The guild used to be forty strong when I signed my name — well, my mark; Maren says I held the quill like a dagger, which I took as a compliment — in the ledger. Forty men and women who could move silk, salt, uncut garnet, fugitives, and occasionally a bishop's illegitimate daughter across three kingdoms without so much as disturbing a raven. Now we were five. Me, Maren, Ferro, Isolde, and Tam, and Tam only counted on days when his hands were steady enough to count with.

Five. Against a queen with a mint, an army, and a very new enthusiasm for roads.

And yet here I was, at four in the morning, in the cold, doing the thing everyone said was finished, because the alternative was admitting they were right, and I've never been able to afford that particular luxury.

* * *

The cargo tonight was salt. Not glamorous salt — not the grey fleur-de-sel that the coastal lords paid silver for — but rock salt, cut from the mines below Greyhollow, bound for the valley villages where the winter had come early and mean and the Crown's tariff on salt meant children were seasoning their bread with ash rather than pay it. That's the part the ballads leave out, if anyone were fool enough to write ballads about men like me. Smuggling sounds romantic until you're hauling sixty pounds of it up a scree slope at midnight because some clerk in Greyhollow decided salt was worth more than the people who needed it.

Ferro had the other half of the load. Ferro always took the heavier half, on account of being built like something the mountain had rejected for being too stubborn even for rock. He was somewhere below me in the dark, moving quiet for a big man, which was the only graceful thing about him. We'd split at the fork below the treeline because the wardens had started working pairs on the toll road, and one pair could be slipped, but two pairs converging meant arithmetic, and arithmetic was how smugglers died.

The lanterns below me stopped. One of the wardens raised his light and swept it along the rocks, and I pressed myself flat against the deadfall and did the thing you learn to do, which is become nothing. Not hide — hiding is for rabbits and guilty men. Become nothing. Become the cold stone and the dead pine and the dark that the mountain keeps folded in its pockets.

"Wind's picking up," one of them said, voice carrying thin through the frozen air. "Says here there's a storm coming down off the high passes by tomorrow night."

"Won't matter," said the other. "Nobody crosses Thornfell in winter anyway. Dead road. Captain just likes us walking it so the reports say we walked it."

That stung more than it should have. *Dead road.* I lay there in the dark with frost forming in my beard and felt that phrase land somewhere under my ribs and stay there. Dead road. Dying guild. Last route. Everyone said it now — the factors in Greyhollow, the tavern-keeps, even old clients who still wanted the work done but said it apologetically, the way you speak to a man at a funeral, kindly, quietly, already looking past him at the buffet.

They walked on. I counted to two hundred out of habit — Maren taught me that; she says patience isn't waiting, it's *measuring* your waiting — and then I rose and moved, up and over the shoulder of the pass, along the route that existed on no map because I had taken the map it existed on and fed it to a stove eleven years ago.

* * *

The drop point was the ruined chapel at Hollowmere, which is not a chapel anymore and hasn't been since before my grandmother's grandmother, but the villagers still call it that because people hold onto names the way drowning men hold onto driftwood. Widow Ansk waited there with her mule and her fear, both of them trembling.

"You came," she said, like she hadn't quite believed it.

"I said I'd come."

"They said the guild was finished. They said nobody would—"

"People say a lot of things." I shrugged the pack off my shoulders and set it down between us. "Salt's cut with nothing, weighed honest, forty pounds. You'll want to get it home before the storm. And Ansk — don't take the toll road. Take the sheep track above the falls. It adds an hour and takes ten years off your life, but you'll keep both."

She pressed coins into my hand — actual coin, worn and honest, saved up over months, I could tell by the way each piece felt like it had been handled a thousand times before it reached me — and I took it, because pride is a fine thing but Maren would flay me alive if I came home empty-handed with debts on the ledger. Then she looked at me with that expression people give you when they want to ask something kind and can't find the shape of it.

"The others," she said finally. "Are there many left?"

"Enough," I said.

It wasn't a lie, exactly. Five is enough, if what you need done needs doing carefully. It's just not enough for what the guild used to *be*, and we both knew it, and she climbed onto her mule and went off into the dark toward the falls, and I stood alone in a dead chapel with forty pounds less salt and exactly as much future as I'd had before.

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about watching something die slowly: it doesn't feel like tragedy while it's happening. It feels like bookkeeping.

When I got back to the tollhouse — our tollhouse, the one the guild had squatted in for six generations back when tollhouses were things you could actually collect tolls at, before the Crown decided collecting money was its own monopoly — Maren was awake, because Maren was always awake, sitting in her chair by the hearth with her hands folded and her blind eyes aimed at the fire like it owed her money.

"Well?" she said.

"Delivered. Paid. Wardens on the toll road, two pairs, but they're soft. They think the road's dead."

"The road isn't dead. The road is *waiting*." She turned her face toward me. Blind since before I met her, and I have never once caught her missing anything. "There's a difference, boy, and empires forget it every time. Roads don't die. They go to sleep, and they remember who walked them."

"Maren."

"Don't 'Maren' me. Sit down. We need to talk about the ledger."

I sat down. Nobody in the history of the guild has ever successfully not sat down when Maren said sit down. She kept the ledger — the real one, the one with the debts — in her head, mostly, though there was a physical copy too, inked in her cramped precise hand, and I knew without asking which page we were on. The bad page. The page where the guild's name was written next to numbers that had been getting larger for three years.

"How much?" I asked.

"To the Greyhollow factors? Two hundred crowns. To the ferry-wives at Coldbrook, ninety. To Tam's tab at the Drowned Bell—" she paused, and I swear the blind woman managed to convey an eye-roll, "—eleven crowns and a great deal of dignity we will never recover."

"And our take from tonight?"

"Eighteen crowns, minus what you spent on the mule feed, minus what you'll claim you spent on the mule feed."

"Sixteen," I said, with dignity.

"Fifteen," she said, "because I can smell a lie the way you smell rain coming, and yours smells like mule feed." She smoothed the ledger page with her fingers, reading it the way she read everything, by touch and memory. "Paloma. Listen to me. At this rate we settle the debts by spring. Barely. If nothing goes wrong. If the wardens stay soft. If Tam stays sober, which — " she gestured vaguely at the general concept of Tam — "and then what? What do we do in spring, when the books are clean and the guild has nothing left to be for?"

I didn't have an answer. I poked the fire instead, which is what I do instead of answers. Sparks went up the chimney and out into the night, brief and bright and gone.

"We keep the routes alive," I said finally. "All of them. Even the ones that don't pay. Because the day we stop walking them is the day we forget them, and the day we forget them, they're gone, and they don't come back. Roads remember, Maren. You said it yourself."

"That was a metaphor, boy."

"So am I."

She laughed — short, dry, real — and for a moment the tollhouse felt like it used to feel, back when it was full of boots and arguments and someone always singing badly in the kitchen. Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and it was just two people and a fire and a dying trade, keeping each other company in the dark.

* * *

Ferro got back an hour before dawn, snow in his beard, and put the rest of the salt money on the table without a word, which was his way of saying the run had gone fine. Ferro communicated primarily through the placement of objects. Coins on a table meant success. A broken strap laid across a chair meant he was displeased with the quality of the straps. Once he'd set a dead chicken in front of Isolde after an argument about dinner, and they hadn't spoken for a week, and the chicken had been delicious.

"Two pairs of wardens on the toll road," I told him. "Soft ones. They think the pass is finished."

"Pass isn't finished," Ferro said. He said it the way he said most true things: flatly, like reporting weather. "Storm's coming down off the high ice. Big one. Road'll close for a month."

"A month of no runs," I said. "A month of debt."

"A month of *alive*," Ferro said, and looked at me, and I understood him, the way you understand Ferro, eventually, if you're patient. A closed pass meant no wardens either. A closed pass meant the mountain taking its own back. There was a version of safety in that, and a version of grief, and Ferro had never in his life distinguished between the two.

Isolde arrived last, near midmorning, smelling faintly of somebody else's perfume and carrying news the way she carried everything — tucked under one arm, wrapped in charm, priced for later. She'd been down in Greyhollow, working contacts, and she spread the word out on the table like a card sharp laying down a winning hand.

"Queen's doubling the garrison at the north forts come spring," she said. "New road charter going through the council — they want a paved way over Thornfell itself, can you imagine, a paved road over *our* pass, with tollgates and everything. And—" she paused, enjoying herself, which was Isolde's one unforgivable habit, "—they've posted new bounties. For smugglers. Specifically. Our names aren't on it yet, but the description of 'the Blackwood ring' is flattering. Apparently I'm 'an unknown female accomplice of dangerous cunning.'"

"They got the dangerous right," Ferro said, and Isolde blew him a kiss, and that was the whole conversation, and it was somehow warm.

I looked at the three of them around the table — Maren with her ledger, Ferro with his silence, Isolde with her perfume and her knives — and I thought: this is what's left. Four people and a mute old building and a craft the whole world has agreed to bury. And I felt the thing I always felt, the thing I couldn't have explained to Widow Ansk or to the queen or to any of the clever people in Greyhollow who said the guild was finished.

Weary pride. That's the only name I've got for it. Pride like a stone you carry in your boot — it hurts with every step, and you refuse, absolutely refuse, to take the boot off.

* * *

The storm came down off the high ice that evening, early and angry, exactly as Ferro had promised, the way Ferro promises things, which is by not mentioning them again until they arrive. By full dark the wind was screaming down the pass hard enough to shake the tollhouse shutters, and the snow came sideways, and the world beyond the windows simply ceased to exist.

We battened down. Fed the fire. Tam, who had materialized sometime during the afternoon smelling like the inside of a barrel, told a story about a bear he'd once outrun, a story that grew longer legs every telling, and fell asleep mid-sentence with his mouth open, which Maren said was the most honest he'd ever been.

I took the last watch, because I always took the last watch. Old habit. In the good years there'd been a rotation, eight of us trading the small hours, and the last watch had been coveted because it meant dawn, and dawn meant the pass opening gold and blue and safe. Now the last watch was just mine, every night, because there was no one to trade with, and dawn was just dawn.

I sat by the window with a cup of tea going cold in my hands and watched the blizzard erase the world, and I did the accounting I do every night, the one that never balances. What we had against what we'd lost. Four souls against forty. Eighteen crowns against two hundred in debt. One pass against a kingdom that wanted it paved, taxed, and forgotten.

And underneath the accounting, quieter than the wind, the question I never let myself ask in daylight:

*For what?*

For the routes. For the memory of them. For the idea that a road should belong to the people who walk it, not to whoever holds the paper. For salt in the bread of villages the Crown had priced out of its own mercy. For a craft older than the Crown itself — and it was, whatever the royal histories said; Maren had documents, Maren always had documents, and some nights she talked in circles around things she wouldn't show me, about charters and oaths and something the guild had once been sworn to guard beneath the peaks, and then she'd stop and fold her hands and say *another time, boy*, and another time never came.

The tea went fully cold. The fire settled. Somewhere in the loft, Tam snored like a landslide with opinions.

And then, through the roar of the storm — so faint I thought at first I'd imagined it, the way you imagine voices in wind — I heard a sound from outside.

A knock. Small. Fists, maybe, or the flat of a small hand, striking the tollhouse door twice, pausing, striking twice again. Patient. Deliberate. The knock of someone who had knocked on doors before and knew that persistence outperforms volume.

At first I thought: nobody. Nobody is out in this. Nothing living crosses Thornfell in a blizzard; even the wolves den up and wait it out. It's the wind, or it's a branch, or it's the mountain settling, the way old mountains do, muttering in their sleep.

The knock came again. Twice. Pause. Twice.

I set down the cup. I took my knife off the shelf out of habit, and then, feeling slightly ridiculous, put it back — because whatever was knocking sounded small, and cold, and alone, and I have never in my life been able to leave that particular sound unanswered. It's the flaw in me. Ferro says I collect strays the way other men collect debts, and Ferro, who communicates through the placement of objects, once placed a stray dog's bowl next to mine at supper without comment, so he would know.

I unbarred the door, and the storm lunged in like it had been waiting all night for exactly that, white and shrieking, and I squinted into it, hand raised against the cold —

— and there, on my doorstep, half-buried already in drifting snow, stood a child.
