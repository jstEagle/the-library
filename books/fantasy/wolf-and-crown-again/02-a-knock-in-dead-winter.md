## 2. A Knock in Dead Winter

The blizzard came down off the high passes three days early, the way bad news always does, and I was glad of it. Glad, mind you, because snow meant the road wardens stayed in their barracks at Greyhollow with their boots drying by a fire they hadn't paid for, and every day the queen's men sat warm and stupid was another day my ledger stayed balanced.

I'll not pretend the tollhouse is much to look at. One room below, one above, stone walls two feet thick that the guild raised back when raising things was still worth doing. A hearth, a table scarred by forty years of knives and coins and elbows, a window that looks out on the pass like an old dog watching a road it no longer has the legs to chase. The toll bar rotted through before I was born. Nobody's paid me a toll in years. That's the joke of it — I keep the tollhouse, but there's nothing left to toll. Just me and the mountain and whatever the mountain decides to send down.

That night it decided to send a blizzard, and then, somewhere inside the blizzard, a knock.

* * *

You have to understand what a knock means out here. In Greyhollow, a knock is a neighbor wanting sugar or a bailiff wanting taxes. Out here, past the last shepherd's hut, past the shrine nobody tends, a knock on your door in dead winter is either death or a miracle, and in my experience the two travel together more often than priests like to admit.

I heard it over the wind, which should have been impossible. Three knocks, spaced even as a heartbeat. Not frantic. Not desperate. Patient, the way you'd knock if you knew — really knew — that someone was home.

I took down the knife I keep under the loose flagstone by the hearth, because I'm a smuggler first and a sentimental man a distant second, and I put my eye to the shutter gap.

Nothing. White roaring nothing.

Then something moved at the bottom of the door, small and dark, and I swear on Maren's good eye — the one she has left — my heart did something embarrassing.

I opened the door six inches against the wind, and the wind shoved back like it had a grudge, and there on my step stood a child.

Not a big child. Seven winters old, maybe eight, though he was so bundled in rags and frost that guessing was a fool's game. His lips were the blue of deep ice, his eyelashes had frozen into little white combs, and he was looking up at me with an expression I've only ever seen twice in my life: once on a man standing at the edge of a cliff deciding whether to jump, and once on myself, the night the guild's charter burned and I understood I was the last one who'd ever carry it.

He didn't beg. He didn't cry. He just looked at me, and then he lifted one small mittened hand and knocked again — on my open palm, this time, three knocks, patient as a heartbeat — as if to say, *there, you see, I do know how it's done.*

Well. What would you have done? Don't answer. You'd have done exactly what I did, which is curse fluently in three languages, haul him inside by the collar, and start boiling everything boilable.

* * *

Here's what I know about thawing a frozen child, learned that night by trial and error and no small amount of terror: you don't put them by the fire, you warm them slow; you don't give them wine, whatever the ballads say; and you talk to them the whole time, nonsense, anything, because a voice is a rope and you're throwing it down a well and praying something grabs hold.

So I talked. I told him about the tollhouse, about the pass, about how the queen's new road wardens couldn't find their own backsides with both hands and a map. He lay under every blanket I owned, shivering like a wet cat, and watched me with eyes that were — I noticed this later, when the frost melted off him — a strange pale grey, almost silver, the color of the sky just before snow.

He didn't shiver forever. Around midnight the shaking stopped and he slept, and I sat across the room with my knife across my knees feeling like the world's worst smuggler, because here's the thing about taking in strays: strays come with owners, and owners come with questions, and questions are the one cargo I've spent my whole life not carrying.

The boy slept until dawn. Then he woke, looked around the room like a magistrate taking inventory, and got up without a word and walked to my map.

Now. Every smuggler keeps maps, but mine aren't maps, not properly. They're memory. Forty years of routes drawn in charcoal on hide and slate and the backs of debt notices: the Hollow Way that runs under the falls, the Shepherd's Stair cut into the cliff face where no warden would think to look up, the crack behind the frozen waterfall wide enough for a man with narrow shoulders and no fear of dying. Routes that exist nowhere else. Routes I have never shown another living soul, because showing them is how routes die.

The boy put his finger on the blank stretch of slate above my hearth — the part where I'd never drawn anything, because there's nothing there, just scree and the Widow's Tooth and a drop that kills goats — and he began to draw.

I want to be precise about this, because precision matters when the world goes wrong. He drew, in charcoal, in lines steadier than any seven-year-old has any right to make, the Hollow Way. Under the falls. Exactly under the falls, the exact bend where the ceiling dips and you have to walk bent double for thirty paces, the exact place where the wall sweats cold water even in August. Then he drew the Shepherd's Stair, all forty-one steps, and then — and this is the part I still can't write without my hands going cold — he drew a route I have never walked. A branch off the Hollow Way, heading deeper east, under the Widow's Tooth itself, marked with a symbol I didn't know: a circle, and inside the circle, a wolf's head, crowned.

"There's nothing under the Tooth," I said aloud, to a child who didn't speak my tongue. "I've looked. There's rock and there's dead air and there's a view that'll stop your heart."

He turned and looked at me. And he answered.

It wasn't common speech. It wasn't Low Thornic or High, wasn't trader's cant or the miners' sign-talk or anything the caravans ever brought through. It was a string of sounds, low and rolling, vowels that seemed to go on longer than breath should allow, and I'd swear before any judge you like that the hearth-fire leaned toward him while he spoke. Leaned. Flames bending sideways like grass in wind, in a closed room, in winter.

Then he stopped, and shrugged — actually shrugged, a small apologetic shrug, *sorry, wrong words, let me try yours* — and pointed at the crowned wolf, and then at himself, and then at me, and then he went back to drawing as if none of it had happened.

I sat down on my own floor, which is undignified in a man of fifty-one, and I looked at the map of roads under my mountain that I alone was supposed to know, drawn by a half-frozen stray in charcoal, and I felt something crack in me. Not break — crack. Like lake ice in spring. Like something underneath, moving.

For eleven years since the charter burned I have been the last of the Blackwood line of the Thornfell smugglers' guild, and I have told myself that's a fine thing, a proud thing, a craft kept alive by one stubborn man. But pride is just loneliness wearing its good coat, and that night, watching a mute child finish drawing my secrets better than I could have drawn them, I took the coat off for the first time in a decade and didn't know what to do with my arms.

* * *

We ate porridge. He ate like a wolf cub, fast and watchful, keeping one hand curled near the bowl the whole time as if I might repossess it. When he finished he carried his bowl to the basin and washed it, unasked, which told me something: whoever raised him taught manners before language, or instead of it.

"Paloma," I said, tapping my chest. "Paloma."

He considered this with the gravity of a treaty negotiation. Then he tapped his own chest and made a sound. Two syllables, soft ones, starting in the back of the throat. I tried to repeat it and mangled it so thoroughly that something happened I hadn't seen in eleven years in that house:

He laughed.

It was a small laugh, rusty, like a gate opening after a long winter, and it did more damage to my defenses than the map had. I laughed too, mostly from shock, and we sat there in the grey morning light laughing at my terrible pronunciation, two fools in a tollhouse at the end of the world, and outside the blizzard kept howling like it had opinions about our happiness.

By afternoon the storm had spent itself. I climbed to the loft window and looked down the pass, and what I saw settled the matter of what happens next, because matters like this never settle themselves.

Snow, yes, but under the snow, on the switchback trail from Greyhollow, tracks. Boot prints, military spacing, at least four men, coming up. Wardens don't patrol in weather like this unless they're looking for something specific, and wardens don't look for something specific unless somebody paid them to.

I came down the ladder slower than I'd gone up.

"Well," I said to the boy, who was studying my maps again with his fingertip hovering over the crowned wolf. "It seems you're either very valuable or very dangerous, and in my experience the two are priced the same."

He looked up at me. And then he did the thing that decided it, though I fought the decision for three more days out of pure professional cowardice. He reached out and took my hand — my scarred, smuggling, coin-counting hand — and folded my fingers closed over his small ones, and held on.

Three knocks, patient as a heartbeat. That's how he'd arrived. This was different. This was a grip.

Boots on the switchback. A day away, maybe less. I started packing.

And that's how it stands, or stood, that winter morning: the last smuggler of Thornfell, packing his grandfather's routes into oilcloth, preparing to run a cargo he couldn't price, couldn't weigh, and couldn't in honesty abandon — toward the one person alive who might know what language the boy speaks, and why the Crown wants him badly enough to march men through a blizzard.

Maren. Blind Maren, who reads with her fingers and remembers what the rest of us were made to forget.

If anyone knows what a crowned wolf means, it's her.

Gods help me, I was almost looking forward to finding out.
