## 3. Words Older than Stone

Maren lived three days' hard walk south of the tollhouse, in a hollow beneath a rock face the old maps called the Listening Stone, on the theory that wind through its cracks sounded like voices. The new maps called it nothing at all, because the Crown had stopped mapping the passes years ago. Why chart roads you mean to let die? A dead road needs no warden. It just needs everyone to agree it was never there.

I agreed loudly and often. I also knew forty-one ways through country that officially did not exist.

The boy walked.

That was the first miracle of the journey, and I want it on record that I noticed. He couldn't have been more than six or seven winters old — small for it, even, the way children get when hunger has been doing arithmetic with their bones — and he kept pace with me up switchbacks that made grown mules lie down and refuse. He never complained. He never asked how far, because asking requires language, and whatever tongue lived in him, it wasn't mine.

He did talk, though. That was the thing.

He talked to himself as we climbed, a low running murmur like water under ice. He talked to the crows. He talked once to a dead tree, laying his palm flat against the bark for a long moment before nodding, satisfied, as if the tree had said something worth hearing. And at night, wrapped in my spare blanket by the fire, he sang — quietly, half-asleep, songs in no key I'd ever heard, melodies that went where they pleased and dragged my heartbeat after them whether it liked the route or not.

"You're a cargo," I told him the second night. "The strangest cargo I ever ran, and I once smuggled a live bear over Grey Saddle for a man who paid in cheese."

The boy looked at me. Then he said something — four syllables, liquid and strange, with a sound in the middle like a stone dropping into deep water — and patted the ground beside him.

"That wasn't an answer. That was an invitation."

He patted the ground again, patient.

I sat down. I'm not proud of how quickly I sat down. But there's a kind of loneliness that gets into you after enough years alone in high country, the kind that settles in the joints like cold, and I'd have sat down for anything warm that wanted company, even if the company spoke bird-language and stared at me like I was the puzzle.

We sat. He hummed. Somewhere below us, a day's march back along our trail, men were climbing through snow looking for him, and above us the stars came out in their usual indifferent thousands, and none of it — not the hunters, not the blizzard, not the crowned wolf burned into his cloak clasp — none of it seemed to weigh on him at all.

Children are either very brave or very stupid about danger. Or maybe they know something we've forgotten about what danger actually is.

* * *

The Listening Stone announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and rosemary and the particular must of old paper, which is a smell I'd bet money could raise Maren from the dead if she ever got around to dying. Her door was set into the rock face like a splinter, low enough that I had to fold myself nearly double to enter, and inside it was warm and dim and stacked to the rafters with the accumulated memory of Thornfell.

Books, mostly. Ledgers, journals, survey charts, song collections, heresy trials, tax rolls. Paper the Crown had ordered burned, paper the guild had bought off burning-wagons for decades, paper nobody else alive thought mattered. Maren sat in the middle of it all in her chair by the fire, a small woman folded up like a letter, her white eyes fixed on some middle distance only she could see.

She turned her face toward the door before I knocked. She always did. "Paloma Blackwood," she said. "You walk heavier every year. Guilt does that. So does boots two sizes too big bought off a corpse."

"They weren't a corpse. He was sleeping."

"Mm." Her nostrils flared. "And who is this? Because you brought someone, and they're small, and they're breathing fast, and—" She stopped.

I watched her stop. I'd known Maren since I was nine years old, since she taught me my letters by dragging my fingers over carved boards, and I had never once seen her stop mid-sentence. Sentences were her terrain. She held them the way I held the passes.

"Come here, child," she said softly. "Into the light. Don't be afraid. Old women can't bite. We haven't the teeth."

The boy crossed the room without hesitation — again, that instinct of his, walking toward strangers as though strangers were doors and all doors opened — and stood in front of her chair. Maren reached out and took his hands. Turned them over. Ran her thumbs across his palms the way she read, slow, deliberate.

Then she took his chin in one hand and tilted his face up, though she couldn't see it, and asked him a question.

Not in Common. Not in Low Thallic, or High, or the river-trade cant I'd picked up running contraband down the Vess. She asked him a question in sounds I had never heard her make — short and hard-edged, with that dropped-stone sound in the middle, and I felt the hair on my arms stand up because I'd been hearing those shapes for six days out of the mouth of a child.

The boy answered. Fluently. Eagerly. His whole face lit like a lamp.

Maren's hands began to shake.

I have seen Maren calm through a raid, a flood, and the death of her brother. I have seen her recite shipping manifests from sixty years past while soldiers searched her shelves. Her hands did not shake then and they shook now, and she let go of the boy's chin and sat back in her chair and pressed both palms flat against her knees like a woman holding herself onto a ship's deck in weather.

"Where," she said, in Common now, very carefully, "did you find this child?"

"Tollhouse. Blizzard. Door." I crouched down across from her. "Maren. What did you just say to him?"

"I asked him his name." Her voice had gone thin and precise, the voice she used when precision was all that was holding something terrible shut. "In Thornic. Old Thornic. Pre-kingdom."

"Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning," Maren said, "that I learned it from clay tablets three thousand years old, Paloma. Meaning that I can count the people alive today who speak a word of it on one hand, and I am one finger, and the others are scholars who learned it from me. Meaning that this child just answered me in a dialect older than the kingdom whose soldiers are hunting him, and he answered in a *regional variant*, and I know which region, because the tablets that use that variant were dug out of the deep vaults under Mount Cern and sealed behind royal writ before your grandfather's grandfather drew breath."

The fire popped. The boy climbed into my lap like it was assigned seating, and I found my arms had already gone around him.

"He said his name," Maren continued. "It's a name. A real one. *Vael-ith-karn.* 'Keeper-of-the-Waking.' It's a title, Paloma. It's a *duty-name*. Children don't have names like that. Names like that belong to offices. To orders."

"What order?"

"I don't know yet." She turned her blind face toward the wall of books, toward three thousand years of paper, and I swear the room leaned in with her. "But I'm going to. Light the lamps. All of them. We're going to be here a while, and you're going to tell me everything, starting with why the boy has a wolf-and-crown sigil on his clasp, because you didn't mention it but he's holding it right now and I can feel the engraving from here, and Paloma—" her voice cracked, just slightly, just once— "*no one living should own that seal.*"

* * *

It took until well past midnight.

I gave her the whole account, start to finish: the knock, the frostbite, the maps he'd drawn in the ash of my hearth — roads only a guild-runner would know, including two I'd never shown another living soul — the wardens on the switchback, the flight south. Maren listened the way she did everything, completely, her head tilted, occasionally reaching out to touch the boy's sleeve as if to confirm he was still there. He fell asleep against my chest somewhere around hour two, breathing slow, one fist still closed around the clasp.

When I finished, Maren was silent long enough that I checked whether she'd dozed off.

"You drew the ash-maps?" she said finally.

"From memory of what he drew first."

"Show me."

I sketched them in charcoal on the back of an old toll manifest while she sat forward in her chair, hands hovering over the paper but not touching, reading the movement of my wrist. When I finished the third one — the one that showed a road running straight through the heart of Mount Cern, a road that ended at nothing, at blank stone — her breath came out slow and controlled, the breath of a woman counting.

"Do you know what these are?" she asked.

"Routes?"

"Routes, yes. And accurate — gods, accurate down to the switchback counts, which means he didn't invent them, he *remembers* them. But look at where they go, Paloma. Look at where they all go." Her finger traced the air above the page. "Every one of them converges. On the same point. Under Cern. These aren't trade routes. Trade routes connect places people want to go. These are approaches. This is a siege map drawn from the inside. This is how you reach something buried — or how you defend it."

"The wardens were hunting him," I said. "Grey cloaks. Inquisitor's warrant, by the look. They called him—"

"A stolen vessel." Maren said it flatly, before I could. "Yes. I imagine they would."

"You've heard the phrase?"

"I've *read* it." She rose, moving through her stacks with the sure, gliding steps of a lifetime's practice, fingers reading spines the way other people read signs. "Sit still. Talk less. Think about whether you actually want to know, because there are things, Paloma, that the Crown erased so thoroughly that knowing them again is itself a crime. There are books in this room I keep behind false bindings for exactly that reason. I have spent forty years deciding what deserves to survive and what is too dangerous to let breathe, and I will tell you now, honestly, that I never once imagined I'd meet the answer walking through my door wearing one boot."

"One boot?"

"He lost a boot somewhere on your mountain and you gave him two socks instead, you sentimental disaster, I can feel them, they're wool."

She pulled a box from behind a shelf of hymnals — the best hiding place in any collection; no soldier on earth voluntarily opens a book of hymns — and carried it back to the fire. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were clay tablets. Maybe thirty of them, each smaller than my hand, each dense with wedge-cut script.

"These came out of Cern," she said. "Seventy years ago, before the sealing writ. A mining crew broke into a gallery and the Crown confiscated everything and hanged the foreman for talking about it. My teacher bribed a records clerk. Thirty-one tablets survived. They're fragments of something larger — a chronicle, or a law-code, or a prayer cycle, we never determined which. What we know is that they're written in Old Thornic, the language of the mountains before the First King came north with his army and his new god, and that within three generations of his coronation, Old Thornic was gone. Not evolved. Not absorbed. *Gone.* Every speaker. Every text. Every carved inscription chiseled off the standing stones and replaced with royal script."

"People lose languages," I said. "It happens. Conquest happens."

"Conquest happens," Maren agreed. "Conquest doesn't happen like *this*. Languages die messy, Paloma. They linger in place-names, in lullabies, in the grammar of conquered grandmothers scolding conquered children. You cannot fully kill a tongue in three generations unless you kill it *deliberately*. Unless every speaker is accounted for. Unless every text is gathered and burned by men making lists." She laid one tablet in my hands. It was lighter than I expected, cool, worn smooth at the edges by centuries of handling. "This isn't conquest. This is erasure. Someone wanted the world to forget that this language had ever existed — and they wanted it forgotten *specifically*, thoroughly, with paperwork. Do you understand what kind of fear produces that much effort?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the point. Neither does anyone. That's what they bought." She turned the tablet over in my hands, guiding my thumb to a line of wedges near the bottom edge. "Read me nothing. Just feel. There. That line appears on eleven separate fragments. It's a refrain, or a legal formula. My teacher and I worked forty years on it and got maybe half. It says something like — *the crown remembers, therefore the crown rules* — no. Closer: *what sleeps beneath keeps the crown above*. Something about sleep, and keeping, and a debt owed downward. A debt, Paloma. In a legal formula. Carved in stone. Buried under a mountain. Guarded by a sealed gallery and a hanged foreman."

The boy stirred in my lap. Without waking, without opening his eyes, he murmured something in his sleep — soft, liquid, that dropped-stone sound — and Maren went rigid.

"What did he say?" I whispered.

"He repeated the formula." Her voice had gone very quiet. "Word-perfect. From a fragment he has never seen. He *finished* it, Paloma. He recited the clause my teacher died without finding. The clause that comes after the debt." She swallowed. "It says: *until the keeper wakes it*."

The fire settled. Outside, wind moved through the cracks of the Listening Stone, and for the first time in my life I heard what it was supposed to sound like — voices, dozens of them, far away, talking in a language older than any kingdom.

"And the Crown knows he exists," I said slowly, "because somewhere, someone read these same stones and has been waiting a thousand years for a keeper."

"Worse." Maren reached out and found my shoulder, gripping hard, her blind eyes aimed at my face with terrible accuracy. "The Crown didn't wait. The Crown *made sure*. You don't erase a language that thoroughly unless you're terrified of it coming back. You don't hang miners over a gallery unless there's something in the gallery. Whatever this child is, whatever he carries — the founders of the current royal line built their house on top of it, locked the door, ate the key, and wrote the history books themselves. And now the door is knocking from the inside."

The boy slept on, peaceful as Sunday, his fist tight around a seal no one living should own.

"So what do I do with him?" I asked. It came out smaller than I meant it to. Six days I'd been running on instinct and stubbornness, and suddenly I was tired all the way down, tired in the place where Maren's words had just poured cold water. "Maren. I'm a smuggler. I run brandy and untaxed iron. I don't—I can't guard the answer to a thousand-year-old crime against the entire power of the Crown."

"No," Maren agreed. "You can't."

"Thanks. Very comforting."

"But you're not asking the right question." She released my shoulder and turned her face toward her walls, toward the false-boundary hymnals and the confiscated truth of the world, and when she spoke again it was in the voice she used for readings — the archivist's voice, the witness's voice, the one that had survived every purge by being too careful and too stubborn to die. "The question isn't what you can guard. The question is what you *inherited*. Because your guild didn't start as smugglers, Paloma. Whatever you think the Blackwood ledger says, whatever your grandfather told you around this same fire — the guild is older than its ledgers, and it had a first oath, and I have spent forty years trying to find it, and I think—" her hands trembled again, and this time she let them— "I think it just walked through my door speaking the proof."

The boy sighed in his sleep and burrowed closer.

"Maren," I said. "What did the guild used to be?"

She smiled, and there was no comfort in it at all.

"Get the strongbox down from the third shelf," she said. "Behind the false binding of the *Book of Hours*. It's time you read the founding charter."
