## 4. The Guild Ledger

I have been blind longer than Paloma has been alive, and I will tell you what the sighted never believe: darkness has a texture. The dark of the tollhouse cellar is not the same as the dark of a snowed-in pass, and neither is the dark behind your own eyelids when you know there is still light in the room. That last one is the worst. It is the dark that reminds you what you've lost.

The ledger room beneath the tollhouse smelled of tallow, dust, and old paper—the smell of everything I had left of my guild. I ran my fingers along the shelf spines by touch, counting them the way other people count rosaries. Forty-one ledgers. Toll records going back two hundred years, route maps drawn in code, the confessions of dead smugglers who wanted their sins written down somewhere honest before they went to whatever gods take people who lie for a living.

Paloma came down the stairs the way he always did, too quiet for a man his size, and stopped at the bottom the way he always did, like he wasn't sure he was allowed to be down here.

"Maren." His voice was rough from three days of listening to a child speak nonsense at the hearth. "You sent for me."

"I did." I pulled out the chair at my reading table—an old habit, offering seats I couldn't see anyone take—and heard him settle into it, wood creaking. "How's our guest?"

"Small. Hungry. Still talking that gibberish."

"It isn't gibberish."

A silence. I felt it stretch between us the way a rope stretches before it parts.

"I know you noticed," I said. "You've been noticing for days. You notice everything, boy, it's why Ferro says you're insufferable and why I say you'll live longer than he will."

"He says that about everyone," Paloma said.

"He means it about you." I folded my hands on the table. "Here's what I mean to tell you, so stop fidgeting—I can hear you fidgeting, don't argue. Three nights ago I sat with the child while you slept. He talked in his sleep. And I recognized four words."

"You don't recognize words, Maren. You—"

"—recognize sounds, shapes of sound, rhythms. Yes. Thank you for the reminder, as if I could forget." I kept my voice level. It had taken me years to stop flinching at well-meaning cruelty. "But I have spent sixty years memorizing what this guild wrote down, and some of what this guild wrote down is older than the guild itself. There are fragments. Prayers, mostly. A lullaby. A road-blessing that Tam still mumbles when he's too drunk to remember it isn't common speech. The child used three of those four words in the right order, Paloma. In a sentence. As if they were his mother tongue."

"And what tongue is that?"

I stood, because this next part required me to move, required my hands on the thing itself. "It doesn't have one. Not anymore. Not anywhere south of the peaks. Because it was killed."

I crossed to the far wall, where the shelf sat apart from all the others—the shelf we archivists called the Iron Shelf, though it was only pine stained dark with oak-gall ink. On it lay a book wrapped in oilcloth, bound shut with a strap and a wax seal that no living guildmaster had broken. My fingers found the seal by memory: the wolf-and-road sigil, worn shallow now from generations of thumbs touching it and pulling away.

Every archivist before me had been told the same thing I was told: *you may keep it, you may dust it, you may not open it until the guild asks you to.* Sixty years I'd kept it. Sixty years I'd wondered.

"The charter," I said. "The true one. Not the pretty fiction we show the wardens when they inspect us—not the story about a merchants' brotherhood formed under old King Aldric's road laws. The founding document. The oath our first masters swore, back when Thornfell wasn't a border but a kingdom of its own."

Paloma had gone very still. I could hear it—a held breath has its own sound, if you've listened to enough people decide whether to run.

"You're saying we open it."

"I'm saying a half-frozen child walked through a blizzard to your door speaking a language the Crown spent a century burying, drawing maps of roads that officially do not exist, and you want to sit here discussing whether or not to read a book." I worked the strap loose. "Help me carry it to the table. It weighs more than regret."

He laughed—one short breath of it—but he came, and together we laid the charter flat among the candle stubs. The oilcloth came away crackling. Underneath: boards of black oak, corners capped in lead, and across the front, embossed deep enough that even I could feel every line of it, the wolf-and-road.

"When was the last time this was opened?" Paloma asked.

"Nobody knows. The seal's original. Which means either every archivist before me was more obedient than I am—which I doubt—or the moment never came." I set my palm flat on the cover. "Or the moment came and passed them by, and they died wondering."

"And you think it's come for us."

"I think," I said, "that the Crown's inquisitors are paying gold in every market town from here to Greyhollow for word of 'a stolen vessel,' which is a phrase that comes out of a chancery, not a constable's mouth. I think the queen's new road wardens aren't building roads at all—they're sealing the old passes, the ones only our routes use. And I think someone above us both decided long ago that whatever this book says was worth killing to keep unread." I broke the seal. It crumbled like sugar. "So yes, boy. I think it's come."

* * *

The charter began the way charters begin, with invocations, and Paloma read them aloud over my shoulder because that was our arrangement—I kept the archive, he lent me his eyes when the matter was too fine for fingers alone. His voice was low and getting lower as he went.

"'In the last year of the Wolf-Kings,'" he read, "'when the snows came red off the high fields and the crowns of men were counted in ash—we, the wardens of the roads of Thornfell, being the last sworn servants of the true crown, do bind ourselves thus.'"

"Wardens," Paloma said. "Not smugglers."

"Keep reading."

"'We swear to hold the passes against all comers, crown or clan, while the kings sleep below. We swear to guard the door beneath the mountain, and the thing upon the stone within it. We swear that no man wearing the stolen circlet shall cross our roads while one warden draws breath. And should the kings wake—"'

His voice stopped.

"Should the kings wake," I prompted.

"They're to be met as witnesses. Not weapons." He said it slowly, tasting each phrase. "'Not as conquerors shall we wake them, nor as slaves, but as witnesses to what was done, that the truth may stand before the throne and shame it.' Maren, this is—" He turned pages; I heard the parchment whisper. "There are names here. Hundreds of names. Signatures."

"Every warden who ever took the oath, I'd guess. Down the centuries."

"No." His finger traced the page—I heard the dry scrape of skin on vellum. "They're grouped. Look—each group ends the same way. *Died at Redford Pass. Died at the Hollow Chapel. Died at the Ice Ford.* Dates, Maren. They're dates. These aren't signatures, these are casualty lists." A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want finished: "The last entry is dated nine hundred and forty years ago. After that, nothing. The list just... stops."

"Because the wardens lost," I said. "Or were bought. Or forgot. Somewhere in there, the sons of the sons of the men who signed this looked at the mountains and saw not a duty but an opportunity. Roads became routes. Tolls became tariffs. Guarding the passes became knowing them better than the law did. We didn't fall, Paloma. We *drifted*. Generation by generation, oath by forgotten oath, until the wardens of the Wolf-Kings were a gang of thieves running brandy past excise men and calling themselves a guild."

I heard him close the book. Gently. The way you close a coffin.

"There's more," he said. "Loose pages inside the back board. A letter."

"Read it."

Paper unfolded. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice you use walking on ice.

"'To whoever keeps this book. We are the last. The Crown's men came at midwinter with fire and the king's pardon for any who renounce the old oath. Most took it. We did not. We go up the mountain tomorrow to make our stand at the door itself, as our fathers swore. If any find this after—know that the guild did not break faith. It slept. As the kings sleep. Wake us if you can.'" A pause. "'The sign beneath the peak remembers. So must we. —Warden-Captain Edda Thorn, with eleven souls remaining.'"

Silence filled the cellar, thick as the tallow smoke.

"Edda Thorn," Paloma said finally. "Thornfell is named for her."

"Or she for it. Nobody knows anymore." I sat back down, because my legs had opinions about standing. "Do you understand what I'm showing you? Our guild—the guild you've given your whole life to, the craft you're proud of, the last route of a dying trade—it isn't dying, Paloma. It died nine hundred years ago, at a sealed door under this mountain, holding pikes against the ancestors of the queen whose coin we now dodge. Everything since has been a ghost walking. Us. We're the ghost."

"That's a grim way to look at it."

"That's the *accurate* way to look at it." I reached across the table until my hand found his sleeve. "And here is the part that should frighten you more than inquisitors ever could. The charter says the wardens swore to guard something beneath the peaks. Something worth a thousand-year war to hide. Something the child—who speaks the language of the people who built that door—walked out of a blizzard to reach." I squeezed his arm. "He didn't come to us by accident, boy. He came to the last house in the mountains where a warden might still answer a knock."

Paloma was quiet a long time. When he spoke, it was barely above a whisper.

"What do we do?"

"We do what wardens do." I turned my face toward the sound of his breathing, toward the warmth of another human soul in my dark. "We ask him where the door is."

Above us, faint through the floorboards, the child began to sing.

* * *

Later—I couldn't have said how much later; time goes strange in that cellar—Paloma carried the charter back to its shelf, and I stayed at the table because my knees had filed a formal complaint. I heard him wrap the oilcloth. Heard him hesitate.

"Maren. Why show me this? Why now? You could've kept the seal unbroken. Let the guild die comfortable."

Because I am a coward, I almost said. Because I wanted someone else to share the weight. But I owed him better than that, and anyway he'd have heard the lie. Everyone thinks blindness teaches you to hear truths. It only teaches you to hear lies, and then you spend the rest of your life wishing you couldn't.

"Sixty years ago," I said, "the archivist before me—a woman named Bette, who taught me letters by tracing them into my palm—told me something on her deathbed. She said the guilt of the guild wasn't that we fell. Falling happens to everyone. The guilt was that we *forgot we were supposed to feel it*. She made me swear that if the old blood ever came knocking—if anything speaking Thornic ever crossed our threshold—I'd break the seal and put the choice in the hands of whoever was left." I heard the oilcloth settle, the shelf take the weight. "You're what's left, Paloma. You and Ferro and Isolde and poor soaked Tam. Four smugglers and a blind woman, holding a debt nine centuries overdue."

"And if I'd rather just pay the excise and forget all this?"

"Then you'd be the most honest smuggler in guild history, and I'd help you burn the charter myself." I smiled, and knew he couldn't see it, and smiled anyway. "But you won't. Because I watched you take in a starving child in the worst winter in twenty years, when feeding him meant less for the rest of us, and you didn't hesitate. Men who hesitate about bread don't hesitate about kingdoms."

He snorted. "Ferro hesitates about bread constantly."

"Ferro would give you the shirt off his back and complain the entire time. Different animal entirely." I gathered my cane, found my feet. "Go up. Sit with the boy. And Paloma—listen to him tonight. Really listen. Not to the words. To the songs. If half of what's in that charter is true, then that child isn't speaking gibberish."

"He's remembering."

"He's *carrying*," I corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole history of this guild. A rememberer might let things go. A carrier was given the weight on purpose." I found the stair rail under my hand, cold and smooth as ever, worn concave by two hundred years of palms. "Which brings me to my last question, and think carefully before you answer, because I already know mine."

"Ask."

"The charter lists what the wardens guarded. The door. The thing upon the stone. But it never says *what* the thing is." I started up the steps, slow, one hand on the rail. "Bette told me once that her predecessor refused to write it down, even in cipher, even in the iron room. Said some knowledge is safer carried than kept. So here's my question, boy, and it's the only one that matters tonight:

"If the thing under the mountain wakes up hungry—whose fault is it going to be?"

Behind me, Paloma said nothing at all. Above us both, faint through the beams, the child sang on in a language older than stone, and for the first time in sixty years of listening, I wished I couldn't hear.
