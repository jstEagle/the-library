## 5. Riders in Grey

They came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Bad news should have the decency to arrive at night, or at least during a storm, when a man has some hope of blaming the weather for the way his stomach drops.

Instead it was a bright, cold morning with the kind of light that makes everything look honest, and six riders in grey came up the pass road at an easy trot, their cloaks dusted with frost, their breath steaming like they'd been sent by someone with a sense of theater.

I was splitting wood behind the tollhouse when I heard them. That's the thing about a smuggler's ears — you learn to hear hooves before you hear anything else, and you learn the difference between a merchant's tired plod and soldiers' rhythm. Soldiers ride in time with each other even when they don't mean to. It's the sound of people who've been told what to do so long that their horses do it too.

Six of them. Grey cloaks, unmarked. No royal crest, which meant inquisitors, because the Crown's honest soldiers wear their colors like they're proud of them, and the Crown's dishonest ones wear nothing at all.

I set down the axe. Slowly. You don't run from inquisitors; running is a confession with legs.

Inside, the boy was sitting by the hearth where he always sat, cross-legged, watching the fire with that absolute attention of his, as if the flames were telling him something worth writing down. Maren was in her corner with her braille-cards, fingers moving over the punched holes like she was reading a heartbeat.

"Company," I said quietly.

"How many?" Maren asked. Blind as she was, she still asked *how many*, not *who*. She'd been a guild archivist too long to care about faces.

"Six. Grey."

Her fingers stopped. Just for a moment — a hitch, like a needle skipping on a record — then kept going. But I caught it. After thirty years of reading Maren's hands instead of her face, I catch everything.

"Inquisitors don't ride this far north," she said. "Nothing up here worth inquiring into."

"We both know that's never been true."

* * *

I met them in the yard, alone, hands visible, wearing my best expression of a man with nothing to hide, which is a real skill and harder than lockpicking. The trick isn't to look innocent. Nobody looks innocent. The trick is to look *boring* — the kind of man whose whole life could be searched and would yield nothing but debts and disappointment.

Their leader rode a dun mare with a soldier's rolling gait. He was maybe fifty, lean as a winter branch, with a face like a ledger — every entry recorded, nothing forgiven. His eyes did the thing inquisitor eyes do: went over me, then past me, then over me again, cataloguing. The tollhouse. The woodpile. The smoke from my chimney, thin and domestic. The axe leaning against the block.

"Paloma Blackwood?" His voice matched his face. Flat, precise, no wasted syllables.

"That's me. Last of the Blackwoods, if you count a name nobody else wanted."

"I don't count anything." He dismounted without hurry. "Captain-Inquisitor Vael. This is a census."

"A census." I scratched my jaw. "Funny. We had one of those last year."

"The Crown conducts many censuses, Master Blackwood. Roads. Livestock. Population." He produced a paper, though he didn't hand it to me — just let me see the seal, black wax, the kind that means the document outranks whoever reads it. "This one concerns persons. Specifically, a child."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the weather.

"A child?"

"A boy. Eight, perhaps nine years old. Dark-haired. Found or stolen — the reports differ — from the low country in autumn. He may have come north." Vael folded the paper away. "You're the last habitation on the high route. If he passed through Thornfell, you'd have seen him."

"High route's dead," I said. "Nobody comes through here but me and ghosts."

"Then you won't mind if my men look around."

Now here's the thing about saying yes and the thing about saying no. Say yes, and you're cooperating, and cooperating men get remembered, and remembered men get visited again. Say no, and you're interesting, and interesting men get dug into like badgers. There's a third option — the guild option — which is to be so helpful and so tedious that they want to leave just to escape your conversation.

"Mind? Captain, I'd welcome it." I spread my arms at the yard, the tollhouse, the grey sky. "It's been four months since anyone's stood in this yard who wasn't either dying or selling me something. Look anywhere you like. Mind the third stair in the back hall; it's rotten and I haven't fixed it because there's been no point."

He studied me for a long moment. I held the boredom like a shield, boring as a fencepost.

"Third stair," he said finally, and gestured, and two of his men dismounted.

* * *

While they searched — thoroughly, professionally, the way men search when they've done it a thousand times and found things a thousand times — Vael walked the yard with me, and we had the conversation you have with an inquisitor, which is a conversation the way chess is a conversation: every move a threat wearing politeness.

"You were guild," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Was. Guild's done. You know that better than I do — your road wardens took the passes two winters back."

"And yet you live here. Alone, at the end of a dead road, in a tollhouse the Crown abandoned." He glanced at me sideways. "Sentimental?"

"Poor. There's a difference, though I grant it's narrow."

"The Thornfell guilds moved contraband for three hundred years, Master Blackwood. Salt, steel, scripture, sometimes people. Your charter was revoked, your routes seized, your brothers scattered or pardoned." He said *pardoned* the way a priest says *absolved* — with weight, and a hook in it. "Some of your former colleagues have found the Crown's forgiveness very comfortable."

"I wouldn't know. I don't write letters."

"No. I'm told you never wrote letters. Not once in thirty years." Something flickered under the flat voice — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. One craftsman noting another's discipline. "That's a rare quality. Most men can't bear silence. They have to fill it. With talk. With paper. With names."

"Silence keeps better than most goods," I said. "Low spoilage."

The ghost of something crossed his face that might have been amusement in a man who'd been issued a different one. Then it was gone, and we walked, and his boots crunched the frost in perfect time.

"The child," he said. "If you saw him. A boy alone, in winter, in these mountains — you understand he'd be dead within a week. Whatever pity you might feel for him, whatever instinct to shelter the helpless—" he said *pity* like it was a disease, "—the kindest thing you could do is tell me. Because the people looking for him are not looking to help him. And they are patient."

"What is he, Captain? Some lord's bastard? Debtor's son?"

"He's property," Vael said, "of the Crown. Stolen property. Which makes whoever holds him, knowingly or otherwise, a thief."

And there it was. The word *thief*, laid down between us like a card, and us standing in a smuggler's yard, both of us knowing exactly how much that word weighed coming out of a Crown mouth, given what the Crown had spent three hundred years taking.

"Property," I repeated. "A child."

"Everything is property, Master Blackwood. That's what order means." He turned and looked up at the peaks, white and silent and full of things buried deep. "You know what I admire about mountains? They don't pretend. Everything down there belongs to someone. It's only a question of who collects."

* * *

His men came out of the tollhouse after twenty minutes. I watched their faces and learned two things: they hadn't found the boy, because the boy was where I'd put him — the old salt cellar beneath the hearthstone, a smuggling cavity older than the guild itself, air-fed through a flue crack, big enough for a child, too small for a man to bother checking twice — and they *had* found something, because the taller one murmured to Vael and Vael's eyes came back to me with new arithmetic in them.

The hearth. Of course. Two places set for breakfast. I'd cleared one, but you can't clear the smell of porridge, and you can't un-warm a second chair.

Vael didn't say anything about it. That was worse than saying something. He just looked at me the way you look at a locked door you've decided to come back for.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "Nothing. Ghosts and a lonely old man." He swung up onto his mare. "We'll ride on to the valley villages. But I'll tell you what I tell everyone on this route, Master Blackwood, because the Crown pays for information, and it pays well. Fifty crowns for word of the child. Five hundred for the child delivered. And a full pardon — written, sealed, unconditional — for any guild man who assists."

"Fifty crowns," I said. "For a rumor."

"For truth. Truth's expensive. Lies are cheap; the market's flooded with them." He gathered his reins. "Think about what five hundred crowns buys, out here. Think about what a pardon buys. Think about who else knows what you know, and whether they'll all think as slowly as you do."

They rode south, six grey shapes shrinking down the white road, and I stood in the yard until they were gone, and then a while longer, because my legs had opinions about moving that I didn't share.

* * *

I got the boy out of the cellar and fed him, and he ate like he always ate — fast, watchful, one eye on the door — and then Maren and I sat at the table while he played with the fire-tongs, arranging them with a precision that I tried very hard not to find strange.

"They'll be back," Maren said. "Or they'll send others. Vael's the type who circles."

"He suspects. He doesn't know."

"He doesn't need to know. He needs to wait. Men like that, waiting *is* knowing." She turned her blind face toward the boy, toward the small sounds of him. "Paloma. What are we doing?"

"That," I said, "is an excellent question, and I've been drafting answers since they crested the ridge, and every one of them ends badly."

"Then pick the ending badly in the right direction."

I looked at her. Thirty years I'd known Maren, and she had this habit of cutting straight through the knot to the rope underneath.

"Option one," I said, ticking fingers she couldn't see. "We keep him hidden here until spring. Problem: Vael will post watchers on the pass, and we eat through our stores by midwinter, and the boy's already coughing from the cellar damp. Option two: we take him down to a village and lose him among people. Problem: fifty crowns is a year's living to a valley family, and mothers have sold more for less. Option three—"

I stopped. Option three sat in my chest like swallowed ice.

"Option three," Maren prompted.

"There is no option three."

"There's always an option three. It's the one you thought of first and hated."

She was right, damn her. Option three was the one that had been sitting in the back of my skull since Vael said *property*, and it was this: nobody hides a vessel of anything for free. The Crown doesn't spend inquisitors on lost children. Somewhere under those grey cloaks was a reason with teeth, and the only way to survive a reason with teeth was to learn what it was before it bit.

Which meant the archive. Which meant the guild's true ledgers, the ones Maren kept in the dark room under the tollhouse floor, the ones I'd never been allowed to read and had never asked to read, because some knowledge in the guild was load-bearing and you didn't pull it out to look at it.

"Maren," I said slowly. "What was the guild *for*? Really. Before salt and steel. The founding. The original oath."

Her fingers went still on her cards again, that same skipped-beat hitch.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because a captain-inquisitor just rode four days into the dead country hunting a mute boy, and called him a vessel, and offered a pardon for guild help — which means somebody upstairs thinks the guild knows something. And because you went white — well, whiter — when I said *grey riders*, and you've gone quiet twice tonight, and Maren, you taught me that patterns are evidence."

For a long moment the only sounds were the fire and the boy humming — that low, wordless humming of his, the tune that made no sense and wouldn't leave your head.

"The dark room," Maren said at last. "Tomorrow. Bring a lamp and your nerve."

"That's ominous."

"Good. Ominous means I'm being honest."

The boy looked up from his tongs then, and I swear on the guild's broken charter that he looked at Maren first and me second, like he'd understood every word, like he'd been waiting for us to catch up to something he'd known all along. Then he went back to arranging the fire-tongs — and I saw, for the first time, what he was making with them.

Two lines crossed. A boundary mark. An old one.

The kind you find carved on boundary stones in the high passes, on stones so old the kingdom never put a date on them.

I didn't sleep that night. I lay in the loft listening to the wind work the eaves and thinking about fifty crowns, and five hundred, and the price of truth in a flooded market, and about the fact that somewhere down the road, in some warm valley inn, Vael was buying drinks for frightened people and paying in silver for whispers.

Let him buy. Let him spend.

Because I'd spent thirty years learning what the Crown called order, and I knew its true market rate down to the copper. And whatever slept at the bottom of this — whatever made inquisitors ride into winter and children speak in dead tongues — I was starting to suspect that the Crown wasn't afraid of a stolen boy at all.

It was afraid of what the boy remembered.

And tomorrow I was going down into the dark room to find out why.
