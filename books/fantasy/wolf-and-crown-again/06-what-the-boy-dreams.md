## 6. What the Boy Dreams

I want to tell you about the night the fire knelt.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start with the ordinary part, because the ordinary part was strange enough: the boy slept like a stone that had been dropped in a well. No tossing. No turning. He'd lie down on the pallet by the hearth where Maren's old cat used to sleep — the cat being dead these two years, though I still catch myself setting out a saucer of milk some mornings, which is the kind of thing you don't tell anyone — and he'd go under so completely that for a while each night I checked his breathing. Just to be sure. A man who runs contraband through mountain passes learns to check things twice. Locks, ledgers, alibis, and now apparently the respiration of a foundling who'd walked out of a blizzard speaking a language older than my guild's oldest grudge.

Three nights he slept like that. On the fourth, he got up.

I want to be clear that when I say "he got up," I do not mean it the way you mean it. I mean his body stood, opened its eyes, and left the room, and whatever was driving it was not the boy. I've seen sleepwalkers before. Ferro's nephew used to wander the tollhouse at Crag's Foot with his arms out like a scarecrow, and you could steer him back to bed with a hand on the shoulder and he'd remember nothing by morning. This was not that. This was a body moving with purpose, and the purpose was not the boy's, or if it was the boy's, then there was a great deal more boy than I had bargained for.

* * *

It started small. The first time, he only sat up.

I was doing what I did most nights those weeks, which was sitting at the table with a candle stub and the winter ledger, pretending the numbers would improve if I stared at them hard enough. They never did. Smuggling is a business of margins, and our margins had been eaten down to the bone by the queen's new road wardens and their tidy little checkpoints. That particular night I was trying to make the arithmetic of a dying trade look like something else, and I heard the pallet creak, and I looked up, and the boy was sitting upright with his eyes open.

Except they weren't open, exactly. His eyes were open the way a door is open onto a dark room. Nothing behind them was looking out. He sat facing the hearth, spine straight as a plumb line, hands flat on his knees, and he watched the fire with an attention no sleeping child should have.

"Oi," I said softly. "Back to bed, little one."

He didn't respond. Of course he didn't; he didn't respond to anything I said in daylight either, except once, when I swore after stubbing my toe, and he'd looked at me with an expression I can only describe as scholarly disapproval, as though he were filing my profanity away for later study. But this was different. In daylight, when he ignored me, he ignored me the way children ignore adults — willfully, with his whole face. Now he ignored me the way a mountain ignores weather.

I went over to him. I put my hand on his shoulder, meaning to guide him back down the way you'd guide Ferro's nephew, and the moment I touched him I took my hand back again like I'd grabbed a kettle off the coals.

Not hot. Not cold either. It was — I've thought about how to describe this for longer than I'd like to admit, and the best I've come up with is that his shoulder felt *occupied*. Like putting your hand on a door and feeling, through the wood, footsteps on the other side. There was someone home in that small body, and it wasn't the child I'd fed stew to that evening, and every instinct the guild ever taught me — every instinct for reading a room, weighing a stranger, knowing when a deal has a knife in it — all of it stood up and said *careful*.

So I didn't steer him. I sat down across from him instead, on the floor, like an idiot, in my nightshirt, and I watched the fire with him.

And here is the thing I keep circling back to, the thing I told Maren the next morning and she went quiet in that way she does, folding her hands like she's holding something fragile: the fire watched him back.

I know how that sounds. I ran contraband for twenty years; I am professionally skeptical of everything, including my own eyes, especially my own eyes after midnight. But I know flame. Every smuggler knows flame. You learn to read a fire the way a sailor reads water — where it burns fast, where it hides, what it tells you about the draft in a room, whether the wind outside has turned. And the flames in my hearth, that night, leaned toward the boy. All of them. Every tongue of fire in that grate bent a few degrees eastward, toward the pallet, against the draft, against everything I knew about how chimneys work, and they held that lean, steady, like men bowing to a lord they're afraid of.

Then he lay back down, mid-watch, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and the fire straightened up and behaved itself, and I sat on the cold flagstones for another hour telling myself I'd imagined it.

I'm a good liar. I've made a living at it. But I couldn't sell that one to myself.

* * *

The second time was worse, because there was singing.

Two nights later — I mark the days by the ledger entries, which is a grim way to measure a life but an accurate one — I woke past midnight to a sound from the main room. Not crying. The boy hadn't cried once since the blizzard brought him to my door, and I'd stopped expecting tears and started wondering what kind of child doesn't cry, and what happened to teach him not to. No, this was singing. Low, at first, more hum than melody, and I lay in my bunk above the storeroom listening to it and feeling my chest do something complicated.

Because it was beautiful. That's the honest truth of it, before all the rest. It was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard a human throat make, and I include in that judgment the famous singer at the Gilded Hart in Greyhollow whose voice supposedly made a duke weep into his wine, and whom I once watched, from the gallery, make three separate merchants bid against each other for her favor. This was nothing like that. Her voice asked you for something. This voice didn't ask. It stated. It moved through the dark little tollhouse like water finding its level, and it was in no language I knew, and yet I understood it the way you understand thunder — not the words, but the meaning underneath, which was old, and large, and patient.

I came down the ladder slow, the third rung and the seventh both groaning the way they always do, announcing me to any listener, which is a flaw in a smuggling house and a comfort in a haunted one.

He was standing at the hearth. Standing, this time, arms at his sides, bare feet on the flags, and the song was coming out of him steady as water out of a spring, and the fire —

All right. The fire.

The fire had gone down to embers, as fires do by that hour, and as he sang the embers rose. Not flared — rose. They lifted off the coals, each one a small red seed hanging in the air, and they arranged themselves. I want to choose my words carefully here because I've had a month to choose them and I still don't trust them. They didn't arrange themselves into a shape, exactly. They arranged themselves into an *attention*. A ring of floating coals, hovering at the height of the boy's eyes, and the light they threw was warm and wrong and wonderful, and the shadows of the room fell outward from him like rays on an old coin, and in the middle of it all stood this scrap of a child in a shirt I'd cut down from one of mine, sleeves rolled four times, singing to the dark in the voice of something that remembered the world being made.

And I did the bravest thing I have ever done in my life, which was nothing. I stayed on the ladder. I held the rail, and I watched, and I let him sing, because some animal part of me understood that interrupting would be the worst mistake of my career, and my career had included the time I sold salted pork to a company of mercenaries who'd specifically asked for unsalted.

The song changed. I felt the change before I heard it, the way you feel a storm turn. The melody dropped, note by note, down and down, into registers I didn't know voices could hold without breaking, and the floating embers dimmed, and the shadows grew long, and the meaning underneath the music shifted too — from patience to something harder. Grief, maybe. Or a list. It sounded like a list. Like someone reciting names, hundreds of them, thousands, in a voice that refused to hurry because every name deserved its full weight, and the weight of it pressed down on the room until the timbers overhead ticked and settled, and my own breath came shallow, and I gripped the rail and thought, with perfect clarity: *this house is too small for what is happening in it.*

Then he stopped. Mid-name, mid-note. And the embers dropped back onto the coals all together with a soft rushing sigh, like a congregation sitting down, and the boy swayed, and I came off the ladder faster than dignity allows and caught him before he hit the flags.

He weighed almost nothing. Children are mostly bird, I'd forgotten that. I carried him back to the pallet and his eyes fluttered, half-open, and for one second — one — they were amber. Not brown catching firelight. Amber, lit from inside, banked gold like a coal wrapped in ash, and then gone, and he was just a boy again, asleep, breathing, hollowed out.

I sat by the pallet until dawn. I am not a man who prays; prayer requires believing someone's listening, and my trade rather depends on nobody listening. But I sat there and I talked to him anyway, quietly, the way you talk to a sleeping comrade after a bad crossing. I told him his name — I'd taken to calling him Wren, because he was small and brown and had turned up in winter, and because a man needs something to call a child besides "the boy" or "it." I told him nobody was going to take him anywhere he didn't want to go. I believed maybe half of that when I said it. By morning I believed all of it, which is how belief works in me: backwards, through the mouth, arriving at the heart last.

* * *

I told Maren the next day. She's blind, our Maren, has been since before I joined the guild, and she reads the world through her hands and her ears with an accuracy that has ruined many a confident liar. I expected her to laugh at the fire story. Instead she listened with her head tipped, fingers laced, and when I finished she said, "Sing it back."

"I can't sing it back, Mare, I don't know the—"

"You've a better ear than you admit, Lior Quintana, or you'd never have survived the Sable Pass with a cargo of untaxed brandy. Hum what you remember."

So I hummed it. Badly. Four bars of a song I'd heard once, filtered through terror, rendered by a man whose singing makes dogs leave the room. And Maren went white — I could tell because she gripped the table, and her knuckles are always the map of her mood — and she said, very quietly, "Where did you hear that?"

"You know where I heard it."

"No." She turned her face toward me, and her clouded eyes were terrible. "That is not a song. That is a *roll of the dead*. That is the Lament of the Broken Crown, and the last living throat that sang it whole burned in Greyhollow square nine hundred years ago, and every copy of it was burned with her, and the Crown called that a mercy." She found my wrist and held it, hard, for a small blind woman. "Lior. What did you bring into my house?"

And I said, "A child. I brought a child, Maren. Out of the snow."

She held my wrist a moment longer. Then she let go, and smoothed her skirt, and became practical, because Maren's answer to every apocalypse is tea. But her hands shook pouring it, and I pretended not to see, because that's the courtesy we owe each other in this guild: we pretend each other's fear is invisible so that everyone can keep functioning.

* * *

The third time, I was ready for it. Ready is a strong word. I had a plan, which is what ready looks like when it's wearing a smuggler's coat.

The plan was this: I wouldn't touch him, I wouldn't wake him, and I wouldn't stop him. I'd watch. Whatever rode him at night, it hadn't hurt him, hadn't hurt the house, hadn't hurt me — unless you counted the hurting of certain certainties I'd been leaning on for forty years, such as *fire behaves* and *children are small*. So I banked the hearth low, set a candle where I could see the whole room, and waited up in the chair with a blanket over my knees like the old man I apparently now was.

He rose a little past midnight. Silent. No creak from the pallet even, which bothered me more than the singing had — a body that moves without sound through a house I know every board of. He crossed to the window, the north one, the one that faces up-valley toward Thornfell's high shoulders, and he stood looking out at the snow, and I realized the shutters were open. I'd closed them at dusk. I close them every dusk; it's the first law of a hidden house. They stood open without a sound, swinging gently, and the cold came in and the candle didn't gutter, and that was somehow the detail that frightened me most. Fire and wind agreeing to be polite. The whole night holding its breath around a child in a borrowed shirt.

He raised one hand and pressed it flat against the window glass, against the frost, and outside — I saw this, I swear it on the guild charter and everything else we hold sacred — outside, the falling snow *stopped*. In a column. A column of stillness rising from the ground below the window up past the eaves, snowflakes hanging in the air like stars, while the blizzard blew on around it, and the boy stood with his palm against the glass and the column stood with him, and somewhere far up the valley, faint as memory, something answered his silence with a sound I felt in my teeth and did not hear with my ears.

Then he turned. Looked straight at me. Eyes open, amber, ancient, and he smiled — a real smile, a child's smile, sweet and gap-toothed and utterly at odds with everything else — and he said a word.

One word. In that old language, round and heavy, and I don't know what it meant. But I felt it land in my chest like a coin dropped down a well, and I felt it settle somewhere deep, and I understood — the way you understand a contract's true terms only after signing — that whatever it meant, it concerned me. It was addressed to me. Possibly it was a thank-you. Possibly it was a summons. With the old blood, Maren says, those may be the same word.

Then he yawned, hugely, like any six-year-old alive, walked back to bed on his own, pulled the blanket up to his chin, and slept like a lamb till morning.

In the morning the shutters were closed. The frost on the north window bore one small handprint, melted into the glass itself, not the frost — into the *glass*, a print fused in the pane like a maker's mark — and I stood looking at it for a long time with my tea going cold, and then I did what any sensible man does with evidence of the impossible.

I covered it with a curtain, and I went to find Maren, because if the world was going to break, I wanted it broken in front of a witness who kept better records than I did.

Maren touched the handprint with her fingertips and said nothing for a long while. Then she said, "There are wardens on the passes asking after a stolen vessel. Ferro heard it at the Waymeet, from a carter who'd been questioned twice in one week." Her fingers stayed on the glass, on the small melted shape of a child's hand preserved in window-glass older than both of us. "They're not calling him a runaway, Lior. They're not calling him a lost boy. *Vessel.* As in something carried. As in something with contents."

"And if they come here?"

Maren turned her blind eyes toward the sound of my voice, and she smiled the way old soldiers smile.

"Then we'd best decide, quickly, what this guild still owes its oaths," she said. "Because I think they're about to come collecting."
