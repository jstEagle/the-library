## 7. Brothers at the Table

There is a particular silence that falls over a room when four people who trust each other with their lives are asked to trust each other with something else entirely.

I'd heard it before, once, when Ferro proposed we run the Saltmere road during a royal amnesty. I heard it again now, in the back room of Maren's archive, where the three of them sat around a table scarred by forty years of knives, coins, and arguments, and the child slept on a pallet of blankets by the hearth like a stone dropped into deep water.

Tam arrived drunk, which was expected. Tam arrived drunk *and* on time, which was not, and I took it as an omen of some kind. He folded himself onto the bench across from me, blinked at the boy, and said, "Whose is that, then?"

"Nobody's," I said. "That's rather the problem."

Ferro sat with his arms crossed and his shoulders doing what they always did, which was occupying more of the world than strictly necessary. Isolde perched on the table's edge, sharpening a knife she did not need sharpened, because Isolde only sharpened knives when she was thinking or lying, and I had learned over eleven years to tell the difference mostly from the angle.

Maren stood by the shelf of ledgers, her hands flat against the wood like she was taking its pulse.

"I'll make this short," I said. "The Crown has posted wardens on every road through Thornfell. They're calling it a survey. It isn't a survey. Inquisitors came to Lowmark asking after a stolen vessel—gold for informants, pardon for guildsmen who talk. And three days ago a child walked out of a blizzard to my door speaking a language that died before the kingdom had a name, drawing maps of roads that officially do not exist."

"Roads you know," Ferro said.

"Roads I know."

"And the language," Isolde said without looking up from her knife, "that Maren says was erased on purpose."

I glanced at Maren. She nodded once.

"So." I spread my hands. "Here's what I'm not saying: I'm not saying he's ours. I'm not saying he's safe. I'm saying the queen's men want him badly enough to reopen roads we spent thirty years keeping shut, and anything the Crown wants that badly is either worth a fortune or worth a grave, and in my experience those are frequently the same thing. So we vote. We hide him, we move him along to someone else's problem, or we hand him over and collect. All in favor of hiding him, say so now, and understand what you're saying. Because if we keep him, we're not smugglers anymore. Smugglers are invisible. Men who defy an inquisitor are targets."

The silence again. The hearth ticked. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the tollhouse roof like a dog at a bone.

Tam broke it, because Tam always broke it. "I vote we feed him first. You can't make decisions about a person on an empty stomach. That's how wars start."

"That's not a vote, Tam."

"It's a motion."

Isolde finally set down her knife. "Let me ask the obvious question. What happens to us if we say no? Not the guild. Us. Paloma, you've got a face the wardens have seen at two checkpoints. Ferro, your name is on a debt-bond with the Saltmere factor. Tam—" she paused, delicately, "—Tam cannot keep a secret longer than a pint lasts. And I have a sister in Greyhollow gaol whose freedom currently depends on me being *useful* to certain people. If we take this child in, every one of those threads becomes a rope around all our necks. I'm not arguing against. I'm arguing that everyone votes knowing exactly what they're hanging themselves with."

That was Isolde's gift and her curse: she could see every thread in a web, and she could never stop pulling at them.

"Ferro," I said.

He'd been staring at the boy the whole time, jaw working like he was chewing something too big to swallow. When he finally spoke, his voice came out low and gravelled. "My mother used to tell a story. Old one. About the wolves under the mountain." He shifted, embarrassed, as though admitting to a superstition were worse than admitting to a crime. "How the first kings didn't conquer Thornfell. How they begged the wolves' pardon, and the wolves let them pass. My mother told it like it was history, not a story." He looked up at me, and there was something raw underneath the gruffness. "You're telling me this lad speaks the tongue those wolves would've spoken. Before it was cut out of the world."

"Maren thinks so."

"Then somebody cut it out." His fists tightened on the table edge until the wood creaked. "And whoever does a cutting like that doesn't like witnesses. I vote we hide him. Not because it's smart. Because my mother raised me better than to hand a child to butchers."

"Well," Tam said, into the quiet that followed, "now I feel like a coward for voting porridge."

"You haven't voted at all," Isolde said.

"Aye, I have. Same as Ferro. Hide him." He scratched his beard. "Though I want it noted that I don't understand a word of what's happening, and that's usually the position I find most comfortable."

"Isolde?"

She picked the knife back up. Ran the whetstone along it once, twice, watching the edge catch the firelight. I watched her watch it, and I thought: *she's calculating, and the calculation has her sister in it, and none of us can help her with that.*

"If I say yes," she said quietly, "and this goes wrong—and it will go wrong, because everything this guild touches goes wrong eventually—I need you all to remember that I said yes. That I chose this. Whatever I do later, whatever I have to do—" her voice caught, barely, a hairline crack in good masonry, "—remember that I started here."

"We'll remember," I said.

"Then yes. We hide him." She slid off the table and stood, and for a moment the sharpness fell away and she just looked tired, tired the way soldiers look tired, down somewhere below the skin. "Gods help us. Yes."

Maren spoke from the shelves, soft as dust settling. "Four votes cast. Unanimous. Then it's decided by charter, and the old law applies: what the guild shelters, the guild defends, and no member sells shelter while breath remains." She turned her blind face toward the pallet by the hearth. "He's guild now, whether he knows it or not. Whether any of us survive knowing it."

Tam raised his flask toward the sleeping boy in a solemn toast. "To the little lord. May he be worth the hanging."

"To the little lord," Ferro echoed, and then, because he was Ferro, he got up and put another log on the fire, and pulled the blanket higher over the boy's shoulder, grumbling the whole time about drafts, about children, about the general ingratitude of the universe.

Isolde caught my eye across the table. Something passed between us—not peace exactly, but an acknowledgment. Two people standing on the same ledge, agreeing not to look down.

* * *

We settled him in properly that night. There wasn't much ceremony to it. Ferro carried the pallet up to the small room behind the archive—the one that had been Maren's reading room before her eyes went, back when there'd been books worth reading aloud to her—and Tam insisted on hanging his lucky copper above the door, "for luck, obviously, and also because if the inquisitors search, they'll think a place with a lucky copper over the door belongs to honest folk, which is the finest disguise in the world."

"The finest disguise in the world is not being here," Isolde said.

"Which is why yours works so well," Tam said, "since you're never anywhere."

It went on like that. It always went on like that. Bickering as mortar; we'd built thirty years of survival out of it.

I stood in the doorway a long time after the others had gone down, watching the boy sleep. He'd turned toward the wall, knees drawn up, one hand curled near his mouth. Ordinary. Heartbreakingly ordinary. Except that twice in the night, the hearth-fire downstairs guttered though there was no draft, and both times it happened, the boy sighed in his sleep—a small sound, almost musical, like the first note of something.

I didn't sleep much myself. Around the third bell I gave it up and went down to the common room, and found Ferro already there, sitting alone with a lamp and a whetstone of his own, working the edge of the old warden's pike the guild kept over the door. Ceremonial, mostly. Nobody had sharpened it in living memory.

"You think it'll come to that?" I asked.

"No," Ferro said, and drew the stone along the blade with a long, even rasp. "I think it'll come to something worse. Blades, you know where you stand." He looked up. "Get some sleep, Paloma. Tomorrow you should ride to Maren's sister-house at Dunmarrow and fetch the old charts. If we're hiding the cub, we hide him proper—routes, bolt-holes, all of it. And take Isolde."

"Why Isolde?"

"Because if anyone's going to meet the enemy on the road," Ferro said mildly, "better she meets them away from the child." A pause. "And because I don't trust her today, and I'd rather not have to distrust her in front of him."

I looked at him a long moment. He kept working the pike, unhurried, certain, a man building something heavy and necessary out of iron and patience.

"You know," I said, "for a gravedigger, you're a suspiciously wise man."

"I dug graves for a reason. Most of what gets buried deserves it. Question's always been who decides." He tested the edge with his thumb and nodded, satisfied. "Go on. Bell's in four hours."

* * *

I rode out at grey dawn with Isolde silent on the second horse, and the last thing I saw of the tollhouse was Tam in the doorway, raising his flask to us, and Ferro beside him, arms crossed, and between them, at the window of the little room, a small pale face watching us go.

The boy raised one hand. Not waving, exactly. More like a blessing. Or a warning.

I touched my heels to the horse and didn't look back again, because I am a professional, and professionals know that some things you only get to see clearly once.

The road to Dunmarrow ran high along the ridge, and by midmorning the weather had turned fine and cold, the kind of day where the mountains show you their teeth and call it a smile. Isolde rode close, and after ten miles of silence she said, abruptly:

"When I vote yes, I mean it. You believe that, don't you?"

"I believe you mean it in this moment."

"That's all anybody has," she said. "Moments. Chains of them." She was quiet a while. "If it comes to my sister or the boy—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"—I'd like to believe I'd never have to choose. Help me make sure I never have to."

It was the closest thing to a confession she'd ever offered me, and I took it as such, and we rode on through the cold bright morning, two liars and an honest moment between them, toward Dunmarrow and the charts that would tell us how to disappear.

Behind us, in the high passes we couldn't see, the first grey riders were already crossing into Thornfell.
