## 9. Smuggler's Roads

There's a moment every smuggler lives for, and it isn't the money. The money's fine. Money buys the coal and the beer and Maren's ink, and I won't pretend I don't count it twice. But the real thing—the thing they can't tax, can't hang, can't buy off you with a pardon—is the moment when a warden patrol is chewing up the valley behind you like a dog after a rabbit, and you step sideways into a crack in the mountain that no living soul knows but you, and the noise of pursuit goes from a roar to a rumor to nothing at all.

I lived for that moment three times before noon on our second day out of Hollow Chapel.

The first time was pure luck dressed up as skill, which is how most of my best work gets done. We'd come down off the shrine ridge in grey dawn light, Ferro carrying the boy on his shoulders like a sack of turnips that occasionally sang, Isolde walking point with her crossbow strung and her mouth set in the line she gets when she's decided to be useful instead of sharp. Tam brought up the rear, which was a mistake, because Tam's idea of stealth is walking quietly while complaining loudly.

"Road wardens," Isolde said. She didn't point. You learn early not to point at things you don't want seen.

They came up the switchbacks below us—six of them, grey cloaks, the queen's new road corps, the ones the tollhouse gossip said were paid double to find "a stolen vessel." Six men moving like soldiers, which meant they moved like men who expected the mountain to cooperate with them.

The mountain does not cooperate. That's the whole trade, really. The Crown builds roads where the mountain allows, straight and wide and honest, and then wonders why smugglers exist. Smugglers go where the mountain *insists*.

So I took them left, off the trail, through a stand of black pines so dense we had to turn sideways, and down a sheep track that wasn't on any map because the last shepherd who used it died before the queen's grandmother was crowned. It dropped us into a dry streambed, and the streambed took us north under the noses of the patrol, close enough that I could hear one of them complain about his boots.

Ferro looked back at me once. He's a man who measures everything in weight—cargo, risk, loyalty—and what passed between us was simple: *this is real, then.* We're actually running. Actually fugitives. Actually dying for a child who draws maps of roads only I know.

I shrugged. What else is there to say? You're either a smuggler or you're a corpse that used to be one.

* * *

The boy, it turned out, knew things he shouldn't know.

We stopped at midday in a hollow under an overhang, sharing hard bread and harder cheese, and while the others argued about whether to push for the Grey Stair tonight or hole up till dark, the boy knelt in the dirt and started drawing again. He'd been doing it since the chapel—roads, always roads, but now with little marks beside them. A circle here. Three lines there. A shape like a tooth.

"That one," Isolde said, crouching, "is the Widow's Tooth. The rock spire above the Coldford cut." She glanced at me. "How does he know that? Nobody knows that."

"Smugglers know that," I said.

"How many smugglers are left?"

"One," said Ferro.

"Two," said Isolde, wounded.

"Two," Ferro conceded, "and a drunk."

Tam raised his flask in acknowledgment. Even his drinking had become ceremonial lately; he hadn't been properly drunk since the ford, and I think the guilt sat in him heavier than the gin ever had. He watched the boy draw with an expression I'd never seen on him—something like shame trying very hard to be attention.

I knelt by the drawings. The marks weren't random. Circles at the springs. Teeth at the rock spires. Three lines at the places where the old paths forked. It was a language—not Thornic, not quite, but something older underneath it, the way bedrock sits under topsoil.

"You're telling me which ways are safe," I said to him.

He looked up at me. His eyes did the amber flicker sometimes now, mostly at dusk or in dreams, and I'd stopped pretending I didn't see it. But this time his eyes were just a child's eyes, tired and certain, and he tapped the drawing once: a long line through the mountains, avoiding every mark.

"The Grey Stair's marked," Isolde said slowly. "Three lines. Forked paths."

"And the Coldford cut has a tooth," I said. "Rockfall country." I stared at the dirt map a long moment, feeling something cold walk up my spine. A thousand years ago, somebody had walked these passes and made this same map in this same dirt-language, and that somebody's memory was sitting in front of me wearing a child's boots with a hole in the toe.

"All right," I said, standing, brushing off my knees. "New route. We skip the Grey Stair."

"We skip the *Grey Stair*?" Tam squawked. "That's the easy way down!"

"The easy way down is where six men with crossbows will be waiting, because the easy way down is on their maps too. The boy says the Coldford, high route, past the Widow's Tooth, down through the Splits."

"The Splits haven't been run in forty years," Isolde said.

"They've been run in forty years by people who didn't know where the safe handholds were." I tapped the boy's little tooth-marks. "He does."

Ferro grunted. From Ferro that was a coronation.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about mastery: it feels like falling and landing at the same time, over and over, faster than fear can catch up.

The Coldford high route is a shelf of stone barely wider than a coffin lid in places, running along a cliff face above a river that sounds like permanent thunder. Wardens can't use it. Goats barely tolerate it. I ran it once at nineteen with a pack of untaxed brandy and a warrant out for my mentor's head, and I'd spent twenty years half-convinced I'd invented the good parts of the memory.

I hadn't. It was worse. It was better. The wind came up the gorge and tried to peel us off the rock like bark, and below us the river roared loud enough to drown a scream, and I went along that shelf with my fingers reading the stone the way Maren reads her ledgers—by touch, by habit, by love—and behind me my family followed, trusting me absolutely, which is either the finest or the most terrible thing a person can give you.

Halfway across there was a gap where the shelf had collapsed sometime in the last two decades. Four feet of nothing, with the river grinning far below. I got across, planted myself, and reached back, and one by one I pulled them over: Isolde, light as a heretic; Tam, wheezing and blaspheming; Ferro, who simply stepped across like it was a puddle, the boy still on his shoulders, both of them looking down into the thunder without a flicker of fear.

The boy looked at me when his feet touched stone. Tapped his chest. Then pointed back the way we'd come, down the gorge.

"What?" I said. "What is it?"

Isolde answered for him, flat and grim. "Wardens. On the low road. They've kept pace with us."

Of course they had. Vael wasn't a fool—I'd known that since he stood in my tollhouse being polite, which is the most frightening kind of cleverness there is. He couldn't follow us onto the shelf, but he could shadow us, patient as winter, waiting for us to come down to ground he chose.

Fine. Let him wait. I'd spent my whole life being underestimated by men with maps.

* * *

We came down through the Splits at dusk—a maze of broken stone where an ancient landslide had shattered a whole flank of the mountain into house-sized blocks, leaning against each other like drunkards holding each other up. The boy led us through it. Not me. Him. He'd stop, tilt his head like a bird, choose a corridor between two fallen giants, and he was never wrong. Twice he made us backtrack for no reason I could see, and both times we found fresh boot prints in the scree exactly where we would have walked.

"He hears them," Isolde whispered.

"Or remembers them," I said. "Hard to say which is worse."

Near the bottom, wedged in a crevice, I found something that stopped me cold: an iron lantern-hook, green with age, hammered into the rock at exactly the height a walking person would want it. Below it, another. A chain of them, descending. Someone had lit this route once, ages ago, for travelers. For *guests*.

The Wolf-Kings' road-wardens, Maren would have said. Our guild, before our guild. Guarding the roads, marking the safe ways, hanging lights for strangers in the dark.

I put my hand on the cold hook and felt the whole weight of what we were doing shift slightly, like cargo settling. We weren't just running anymore. We were traveling a road our own guild had built and then forgotten, following a child who remembered it better than we ever had.

"Paloma." Ferro's voice, low, from the rear. "Smoke. Behind us. Two fires, maybe three. They've stopped for the night."

"Good," I said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much I meant it.

"Good?" Tam croaked.

"Good," I said. "Because tired men with torches make mistakes, and tomorrow I'm going to take this company down a route that doesn't exist, and by the time Vael figures out where we've gone, we'll be at Maren's door with answers instead of apologies."

The boy tugged my sleeve. He pointed east, through the last gap in the Splits, toward a horizon going purple with evening. Far off, faint as a scar, the peaks above Thornfell's deep country caught the last light.

Then he looked at me and said—not in his own tongue, not entirely, but in words worn smooth by a thousand years, words I somehow understood the way you understand a mother's tone before you understand her sentences:

"*Home-road. Fast. Before they wake all the way.*"

And somewhere behind us in the dark, a warden's horn sounded—one long note, uncertain, searching—while ahead of us the mountain opened its oldest path like a hand unclenching, and I, Lior Quintana's least humble narrator, grinned into the wind like the fool I have always been, and led my family home.

* * *

We walked through the night.

Not because I wanted to—my knees filed formal complaints around midnight—but because the boy wouldn't sleep. Every time we stopped, he stood facing back the way we'd come, listening, and whatever he heard made his jaw set in a way no child's jaw should set. So we walked, and I navigated by stars and memory and the occasional green-with-age lantern hook appearing out of the dark exactly when my confidence needed feeding.

Around the coldest hour, Isolde fell into step beside me.

"He's getting stronger," she said quietly. "You've noticed."

"I've noticed."

"At the Splits. Those words he said. That wasn't a boy remembering. That was something old borrowing his mouth." She was quiet a moment. "Paloma. What happens when it doesn't need to borrow anymore?"

I didn't have an answer, so I gave her the honest currency of the trade: "Ask me at Maren's door."

She snorted, but she stayed beside me, and after a while she said, "For what it's worth—that shelf crossing. The gap. You didn't hesitate."

"I hesitated plenty. Internally. Very dignified hesitation."

"Externally," she said, "you were the man the guild used to sing about, back before my time. The one who could take anything over any pass." She bumped my shoulder with hers, which from Isolde is roughly equivalent to a sonnet. "Don't let it go to your head."

Too late. It had already gone there, swelled up warm and ridiculous, and I carried it the rest of the night like contraband of the best kind—lighter than it should be, worth more than its weight, dangerous if anyone found out I was hoarding it.

Dawn found us on a ridge above the hidden vale where Maren kept her archive, and dawn also found the boy finally asleep, slung across Ferro's back like a king on a bier, one small hand closed around Ferro's collar. And dawn found something else, too, because the mountain has never yet given me a gift without a bill attached.

Far below, at the mouth of the vale, where there should have been nothing but frost and silence—

Tents. Small, disciplined, grey. And a figure standing apart from them, looking up at our ridge, who even at that distance I would have known anywhere by the stillness of him.

Vael had not been following us.

Vael had been *waiting* for us.
