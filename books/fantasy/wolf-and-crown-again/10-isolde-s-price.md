## 10. Isolde's Price

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only gets done at night, alone, by lamplight, when everyone else in the tollhouse is asleep and the wind is doing its best impression of a dying animal. It's not sums. I was always good at sums; Ferro still comes to me to check his counts, which says less about my talent than about his. No, this other arithmetic has no numbers in it. It's the kind where you put a life on one side of a scale and an oath on the other, and then you stand there like an idiot waiting for one of them to weigh more, knowing all the while that they weigh exactly the same and always will.

My sister weighs the same as everything.

Her name is Wren. She'll come up later, so you may as well have it now. Wren Blackwood—no relation to Paloma, before you ask, though the coincidence amused us both when we first met, two people with crow-bird names finding each other in the smugglers' guild like it was some kind of omen. Paloma took it for fate. I told him fate doesn't bother with birds. He said that was exactly why he liked me.

That was four years ago. Fate, as it turns out, doesn't bother with sisters either.

* * *

I want to be clear about something before I tell you what I did, because I intend to keep telling this story honestly even if no one ever hears it: I did not decide anything that night. That's the coward's version and also the true one. I didn't sit down at the table of my conscience and choose betrayal over loyalty. I sat down and told myself I was only writing a letter. Letters are not decisions. Letters can be burned. Everyone knows letters can be burned—that's practically their defining feature, along with being slow and smelling of tallow smoke.

The letter was already written by the time I admitted I'd been writing it for three days.

It lived inside my head the way a splinter lives under skin, working deeper every time I forgot about it and then remembered again. *To Captain Aldric Vane, Third Wardens, Greyhollow garrison.* I knew the address because I used to know the man. Not well. Enough. A year ago—gods, nearly two now—I'd traded him information twice, small things, nothing that bled: a shipment route we were abandoning anyway, a name of a fence in Lowmarsh who'd earned whatever came to him. In exchange he'd looked the other direction on two occasions, and once he'd bought me a drink and told me his wife had died in the winter fevers, and I'd said something suitably hollow, and we'd understood each other the way two people understand each other across a card table: completely, and about nothing that matters.

Then Wren got arrested, and understanding became currency.

She wasn't smuggling. That's the part that sits in my teeth like grit. Wren kept a mending stall in Greyhollow's lower market, patched sails and coats and tents, never asked where anything came from because asking was bad for business and she wasn't curious by nature. The wardens picked her up in a sweep after someone robbed a tax wagon on the north road—a robbery I know for a fact was done by the Vetch boys out of Millbrook, because Ferro and I watched them do it from a ridge half a mile off and agreed they were amateurs who'd hang within the season. They haven't hung. They bought their way clear inside a month. Wren, who owned nothing worth buying with, stayed in the Marshgate cells while her case went nowhere, which is how the Crown punishes people who can't pay: not with sentences but with suspension, a life held underwater just to see how long she kicks.

Vane made the offer through a middleman, the way these things are done. *Your sister's file has developed complications. Complications resolve faster for cooperative parties.* Which is warden-speak for: give me something, and paper moves. I've seen paper move. I've seen it move fast when the right hand pushes.

And then Paloma knocked on my door with snow in his hair and a mute child wrapped in his own coat, and asked the guild—asked *me*, with those tired dark eyes—to help hide the boy from the very institution holding my sister.

You see the shape of it now. You see why the letter wrote itself.

* * *

Ferro voted yes. Of course Ferro voted yes. Ferro would vote yes to adopting a plague rat if it shivered hard enough. Tam voted yes too, mostly because Tam votes however the last person to pour him wine voted, and Paloma poured. So the decision was made before Isolde opened her mouth, which suited me fine, because I don't trust my mouth on important occasions. My mouth has gotten me thrown out of two taverns and one wedding.

But here is what nobody understands about voting yes: saying yes costs nothing. Yes is free. Yes is the easiest coin in any purse. What costs is lying down afterward in the dark next to the people you've just lied to, listening to Tam snore like a landslide with opinions, and knowing that tomorrow morning you're going to walk into town and buy ink.

I lay awake most of that first night running the ledger. That's what I call it—the ledger. Every choice since the child arrived went in it, debits and credits, and the books refused to balance.

Credit: the boy is eight years old, maybe nine, and he eats like a sparrow and watches fires like they owe him money. Whatever the Crown thinks he is, he is also a small cold person who fell asleep against Maren's shoulder on his second night and drooled on her sleeve, and Maren let him, and Maren—who hasn't let anyone touch her since I've known her—pretended afterward that it hadn't happened.

Debit: inquisitors don't hunt children. Inquisitors hunt *things*. Vael's men came through the valley asking after "a stolen vessel," and the word vessel does work that the word child refuses to do. I've run contraband for eleven years. I know cargo. I know the difference between a thing that's carried and a thing that carries. When the grey riders described what they wanted, their horses got nervous, and horses are honest in ways their riders aren't.

Credit: Paloma. Paloma, who found me half-dead of fever in a ditch outside Thornfell and nursed me back without once mentioning the debt, because that's how he makes debts—he makes them unpayable so you stay. Paloma, whose whole ridiculous guild is five people and a blind woman and a tollhouse falling down around its ears, and who talks about it like it's a kingdom.

Debit: Wren. Wren in Marshgate going into her ninth month of detention, coughing—I heard that from the middleman too, delivered like seasoning, *your sister's health has declined somewhat*, the vulture—and Wren who taught me to read by lantern light when we were girls because our father thought letters were for priests and landlords and we'd need neither. Wren, whose only crime was being poor in the wrong market on the wrong day.

You understand the problem with the ledger now. Every credit is real. Every debit is real. The scale doesn't tip; it just groans.

* * *

On the fourth day I walked into Greyhollow to buy supplies, which was even true. Salt, lamp oil, arrowheads—don't ask why a tollhouse needs arrowheads, ask the road wardens who've started inspecting freight like it owes them rent. I wore my market face, the one that's bored and slightly cheated-looking, and I did my trading, and I haggled a miller down from an outrageous price on flour purely for practice, and then I stood in the square with my basket on my arm and looked at the notice board.

The new notices were up. The queen's seal, fresh wax, red as a wound. *By order of Her Majesty's Inquisition: information concerning a missing person of interest. Reward: two hundred crowns.* Two hundred. For perspective, the guild's entire outstanding debt—the debt Paloma spent this whole autumn bleeding himself gray to service—is three hundred and forty. Two hundred crowns for pointing. Not fighting, not capturing, not risking. Pointing.

Below it, older, weathered, was a wanted poster for the Vetch boys, reward forty crowns each, and beneath that a faded proclamation about toll reform that everyone had long ago stopped reading. The board was a little museum of what the Crown valued. Forty crowns for armed robbers. Two hundred for a child. I did the sum standing there in the cold, and the sum was obscene, and obscenity is clarifying.

Except.

Except that two hundred crowns isn't a price for a child. It's a price for silence, and silence is what I'd be selling, and I've sold silence before and slept fine. What I couldn't stop looking at was the smaller line at the bottom, the standard clause they stamp on everything: *Information leading to the resolution of ongoing matters will be weighed favorably in related proceedings.*

Related proceedings. Somewhere in a warden's filing cabinet was a folder with BLACKWOOD, W. on it, gathering dust and complications, and somewhere in a garrison office was a man named Aldric Vane who could make dust disappear.

I stood in that square until my feet went numb, holding a basket full of salt and oil and death, letting two pieces of paper argue with each other in my chest.

* * *

I'm not going to pretend the guild oath tipped anything, because it didn't, and honesty is the one luxury I'm allowing myself in this account. The oath is words. Good words—I helped write our local version, actually, years back, sitting on a keg in Ferro's cellar while Paloma dictated and Tam kept interrupting with amendments involving ale. *We carry what must be carried. We leave no one on the mountain. We do not sell each other.* That last line, the no-selling one, was mine. I insisted on it. Paloma thought it went without saying and I told him nothing goes without saying, that's the whole point of saying.

So when I finally sat down that night in the loft above the stable, with paper I'd bought in Greyhollow and ink I'd bought in Greyhollow and a candle I'd—well, you follow—the no-selling line was right there in my head, wearing my own handwriting, tapping its foot.

I wrote anyway.

Not much. That was the discipline of it, the thing I'm almost proud of: I kept it small. Small enough to deny. I wrote:

*A. V.—the item your masters seek passed through my district. I cannot say more yet. I can say it is not what the notices imply. Consider whether the Crown's interest here serves the Crown or serves certain careers. An answer regarding the BLACKWOOD matter would clarify my ability to be useful.*

Read it again. Go on. There's no betrayal in it anywhere, if you squint. It's a feeler. It's a merchant testing a price. I am not informing; I am *negotiating*. I have not given up the child; I have mentioned his existence to a man whose entire profession is hunting him, in exchange for—what? Nothing yet. A question. A door left ajar.

That's what I told myself at the time, and I record it here because the self-deception is part of the truth and I refuse to flatter myself in my own accounting. But I'll also record this: my hand shook on the last line, the one with Wren's surname in it, and shaking hands don't shake for feelers. Shaking hands know.

The delivery was the easy part, insultingly easy. Old habits. There's a loose stone behind the shrine-post at the crossroads chapel two miles south, and under it a cavity the size of a fist, and messages left there get collected by a carrier who doesn't know he's a carrier—a tinker who checks that stone for "lost valuables" every market week and sells what he finds to whoever pays, including, twice a year, people connected to the garrison. The guild used it for decades. It's a stupid system and it worked perfectly, which describes most of the trade.

I dropped the folded paper in, replaced the stone, and walked home in the dark feeling precisely nothing, which frightened me more than guilt would have. Guilt at least is company.

* * *

Maren caught me. Of course Maren caught me; I don't know why I bothered pretending otherwise, except that self-deception demands its rituals.

She was in the archive room the next evening when I came down for lamp oil—she keeps her own stores there, don't ask, blind women and their territories—and she said, to the room in general, in that mild voice she uses for stating facts that gut you: "You've been walking to the crossroads."

"I've been walking," I said. "Periodically one walks."

"Mm." She turned a page she couldn't see, the way she does, keeping her hands honest. "When Ferro walks to the crossroads he hums. When Tam walks anywhere it's a miracle. When you walk there, you come back lighter on the way out and heavier on the way home." She tilted her blind face toward me. "Whatever you're carrying, girl, it's gaining weight."

I could have lied to her. Lying to Maren is famously easy—she can't watch your face—and famously pointless, because she hears the load-bearing walls in a sentence. I stood in the doorway with the oil jug in my hand and said, "If I told you I'd done something for the right reasons, would that count for anything?"

"No," she said. "Right reasons are what everyone has. Even Vael has those, I'd wager. Reasons are cheap. Tell me instead: when it breaks, and it will break, who do you hope is standing nearer to you—us, or them?"

And there it was, the question under the question, and I hated her for it with a love that surprised me. Because the honest answer, the answer that rose in my throat before my cleverness could catch it, was *you*. Us. The five of them and the blind woman and the sparrow-child who drools on sleeves. I'd sell a piece of my soul to save my sister and when the selling was done I'd want to come home to the people I'd endangered, and that—write that down in the ledger too, in the debit column, in letters a mile high—that is the ugliest arithmetic there is.

"Us," I said.

"Then you have time," said Maren, and went back to her page. "Time is the only mercy in this trade. Spend it deciding, not justifying."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell her, because I hadn't finished discovering it myself:

That night, lying in my bunk with the wind worrying the shutters, I made myself imagine it whole. Not the letter, not the negotiation—the end of the road. If Vane bites, if the paper moves, if Wren walks out of Marshgate thin and coughing and alive into my arms—then what has paid for her? Vael's men ride up a road someone described to them. Maybe not my description; I gave nothing usable, remember, remember—but roads lead somewhere, negotiations have conclusions, and the conclusion of every negotiation I've ever conducted is that both sides eventually collect.

They'd come for the boy. And between the boy and the grey riders would stand Paloma, and Ferro, and drunk brave useless Tam, and old Maren with her pikes-and-patience, and they would stand there because of a vote I sat silent through, silent as a stone, letting them commit themselves to a grave I'd helped dig.

Wren would live. That was the whole design; I won't pretend otherwise, I won't dress it up as ambiguity. My sister would live, and the cost would arrive wearing familiar faces, and I would spend the rest of my life being the person who did that math correctly and hated the answer.

Unless—

Unless Vane doesn't bite. Unless the tinker finds the stone empty, or finds it and sells to the wrong buyer, or the letter simply drowns in garrison paperwork the way ninety of a hundred papers do. There's a version of this where nothing happens. Where I bought a lottery ticket against my own family and the ticket loses and I burn the memory of buying it and we go on, the six of us and the child, until the inquisitors find their own road or the winter ends or the world gets kinder, whichever comes first.

I lay there choosing to believe in that version the way you choose to believe a rope bridge will hold: not because of evidence, but because the alternative stops your legs.

And then, near dawn, the child started singing in his sleep again.

You hear it through two floors and a winter wind, which should be impossible, and it isn't loud—it's the opposite of loud, it's the sound getting quieter around itself, like the night leaning in. The hearth below does something to its flames when he sings; I've seen it, we've all seen it, and none of us has said a word about it to each other because some things you don't say out loud or they become real faster. And lying there in the dark, listening to a language older than any kingdom pour out of a sleeping boy I'd offered up to the machinery of the state, I felt the ledger finally, mercifully, tip.

Not toward loyalty. Don't mistake me. It tipped toward terror.

Because I realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Vael might be right. That the Crown might genuinely be hunting a weapon, and that I might be the only person in this tollhouse cynical enough to see both truths at once: that the child is a small cold sparrow-boy who needs feeding, *and* that he is a sealed door with something vast sleeping behind it, and that doors like that don't care who opens them or why.

My sister is in a cell. My family is asleep around me. And I have set a letter loose in the world like a spark in dry grass, and sparks do not negotiate.

Three days. The tinker hits the crossroads chapel in three days. Three days to decide whether I ride down and fish that paper back out of the stone—or whether I add a second page.

I lay awake until the sky went grey, doing the only sum that mattered, the one with no numbers in it, and for the first time since the blizzard brought that knocking to our door, I prayed—not to anything in particular, the gods of the mountain having long since stopped taking my calls—but to whatever keeps ledgers in heaven:

*Don't let him answer.*

Two mornings later, Ferro came in from checking the snares and said, casual as weather, "Riders on the south road. Grey cloaks. Making for the chapel crossroads."

The oil jug slipped out of my hands and didn't break, which struck me then, and strikes me now, as the single worst piece of luck I have ever received.
