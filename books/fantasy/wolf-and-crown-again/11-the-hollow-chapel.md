## 11. The Hollow Chapel

The storm broke at dusk the way storms do in Thornfell—all at once, like a debt called in by a creditor who'd been polite too long. One moment we were picking our way along a goat track above the tree line with the boy strapped to Ferro's back like a sack of turnips, and the next the sky simply gave up pretending and dumped half the mountain on our heads.

"We need shelter," Isolde shouted over the wind, which was the sort of observation that made her so valuable in a crisis. Next she'd tell us we were wet.

"I know a place," I said, because I always know a place. It's the one professional skill that hasn't been taxed out of existence yet. "Half a mile down, off the trail. Old shrine."

"A shrine," Ferro said flatly. He had the boy's weight on his shoulders and a hundred pounds of his own bad attitude besides, and still managed to sound like a man being asked to sleep in a brothel. "You want us to pray our way through a blizzard."

"You don't have to pray. Just don't spit on anything holy. The old gods are dead, but their floors are dry."

We went down single file, Maren's hand on my shoulder—she couldn't see the trail, but she could feel the mountain better than any of us could see it, which is either a gift or a curse depending on how much you trust mountains—and within twenty minutes the storm went from trying to kill us to merely insulting us, because the trail bent into a fold of rock and there it was, exactly where my memory said it would be: a low stone mouth in the hillside, half-swallowed by ivy gone black with frost.

The Hollow Chapel. Smugglers' name for it. Nobody remembered what the actual worshippers had called it, mostly because nobody remembered the worshippers at all.

* * *

Inside, it was dry as a ledger page and dark as one too. Tam got a fire going from the cache of old wood some long-dead smuggler had stacked against the wall—guild courtesy across the centuries, one of the few traditions worth keeping—and the light crawled up the walls and found things to look at.

Which is when the trouble started. Not the shouting kind of trouble. The quiet kind, which is worse.

The boy stood up from beside the fire, walked to the far wall as if someone had called his name, and stopped.

"Pup," Ferro rumbled. That was what we'd taken to calling him—not to his face exactly, since he didn't answer to anything, but because Ferro names everything like he names his mules. The boy didn't turn. He just stood there, small hands hanging loose, staring at the wall the way you stare at a door you're afraid will open.

"Ferro," Isolde said softly. "Look at the wall."

I looked.

I've spent thirty years reading marks on stone. Trail blazes, guild signs, chalk ciphers left by men who'd been dust two generations before I was born. I can read a wall the way a priest reads scripture. So believe me when I tell you that what the firelight showed us was no ordinary mural, and no ordinary age.

It covered the whole eastern wall, floor to vault, painted in pigments that should have faded to nothing centuries ago but hadn't—as if whoever mixed them had used something that refused to forget. And the picture was this:

Wolves. Enormous, crowned wolves, running through mountains I knew the way I know my own scars—the Three Sisters, the Grey Stair, the notch we now call Widow's Tooth, all of it, rendered before any of those places had the names we gave them. The wolves weren't monstrous. That's what struck me first, before the rest of it landed. They looked tired. Grieving. A procession of them descending toward a valley, heads low.

And below them, men.

Men with torches. Men with spears and nets and hammers. Men holding aloft, in careful, reverent detail, a broken crown—one half in each raised hand, snapped clean at its center, held up like a trophy and an accusation at once.

And the men were killing the wolves. All of them. Panel after panel, the same story told over and over with the patience of someone who wanted no room left for misunderstanding: wolves cornered on crags, wolves burning in dens, wolf-pups—small enough that my stomach turned—carried away in cages while the mothers—

"All right," Ferro said quietly. "That's enough of that."

He moved to pull the boy back from the wall, and the boy let him, but his eyes never left it. His lips were moving. Making no sound, or making sound below hearing—I genuinely couldn't tell which. Maren heard it anyway. Blind Maren, who navigates the world by her ears the way I navigate it by my feet, cocked her head like a bird and went very still.

"He's counting them," she whispered.

"What?"

"The panels. Or the wolves. Something. He's—" She frowned, and Maren frowning is a rare weather event. "No. He's *reading* it. Lior, he's reading it like text. Like it's words."

"It's a painting."

"It's a painting to us." She rose, slow, and felt her way toward the wall until her fingers touched cold stone, hovering just shy of the images themselves, the way you hover near a sleeping animal. "Paint fades. Words survive. Whoever made this built it to be read, and they built it to be read by someone who speaks—" She stopped herself. We all knew what she almost said. The word from the tollhouse, the word from her books. Thornic. The language older than the kingdom, the language that had been scraped out of every record, every stone inscription, every memory the Crown could reach. Erased on purpose. She'd told me that in chapter and verse, and I'd nodded the way you nod at old women's warnings, politely, without believing the knife was already in your ribs.

"Well," Tam said into the silence, from the safety of the fire, "it's very nice. Very historical. Can we eat now?"

Tam has survived forty years of smuggling through the flawless strategy of treating every crisis as an interruption of dinner. Under other circumstances I'd have admired it.

* * *

We ate. Of course we ate. You don't get to be sentimental on an empty stomach; the mountain doesn't allow it. Ferro portioned out dried sausage and hard cheese and the last of the good bread, and the boy ate sitting with his back deliberately to the mural, which nobody commented on, because we were all pretending very hard that we hadn't noticed him doing it.

But I kept looking at the wall. I'm only human. Well—only smuggler, which is a related species.

The more I studied it, the more wrong it looked. Not the subject matter. The *order*. There were older marks beneath the paint—I could see ghost-lines where something else had been, scraped or washed, and the mural laid over it like fresh snow over old blood. Somebody had painted this wall twice. And the under-layer, whatever it had been, had been erased with the same thoroughness the Crown had applied to every other trace of the Thornic tongue.

"Isolde," I said. "You grew up south. Temple schools. What do the sanctioned histories say about the founding?"

She didn't even look up from cleaning her knife, which is how I knew she'd been waiting for the question. "That the first Aurelian king unified the wild tribes under heaven's mandate. That he found the mountains lawless and full of beast-cults, cleansed them, and forged the crown as a symbol of order rising from chaos." A pause. Her knife paused with her. "There's a famous fresco in the Grand Basilica at Greyhollow. The Founding. King triumphant, crown raised high, kneeling tribesmen."

"Raised whole?" I asked.

"What?"

"In the basilica. Is the crown in the picture whole?"

Isolde's knife resumed its work, slower. "Whole," she said. "Of course whole. It's always whole. On the coins, on the banners, on the seal stamped into every warrant that's ever been nailed to a guild door." She looked up, and her sharp face had gone sharp in a different way, the way it does when she's angry at something she can't stab. "Broken at the center. In every image I've seen since I was six years old."

"And here," Maren said, "in a shrine old enough to predate the basilica by a thousand years, it's broken."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire cracked. Wind worried the doorway like a dog at a bone.

Here's the thing about history that nobody teaches you in temple school: it isn't what happened. History is what somebody paid to have written down, and everything else gets buried, burned, or repainted. I've run contraband past royal checkpoints my whole life, and I will tell you the deepest secret of my trade, free of charge: the most valuable cargo that ever moves through these mountains isn't silk or salt or steel. It's *memory*. That's why the guilds were tolerated for a thousand years and why they're being strangled now. Roads move goods. But smugglers' roads move *everything*, including the things the powerful would prefer stay lost.

And somewhere in the last three days, the thing the Crown preferred to keep lost had started walking around in the body of a mute child who drew maps of roads that officially don't exist, and now stood shivering in front of a wall that called the kingdom's founding a massacre.

"Paloma." Maren's voice pulled me back. Her fingers were tracing the air near the lowest panel, the one where the caged pups were carried east, toward the coast, toward the capital. Beneath it ran a line of script—angular, dense, nothing like any alphabet of the Four Kingdoms. "This text. Read it to me."

"Maren, I can't read it. Nobody can read it. That's rather the point of erasing a language."

"*Read it,*" she said, in the voice she uses when she means *obey*. "Sound it out. Badly. Wrong. I don't care. I want to hear it in the air."

So I did. I sat cross-legged under a thousand-year-old crime scene and sounded out dead syllables like a child at his letters, mangling every third character, and it was gibberish, pure gibberish, except—

Except the boy turned around.

He turned around slowly, the way the hearth-flames had bowed when he sang in his sleep, the way everything near him did lately, and he looked at me with those dark eyes that were starting to worry me more than any warden patrol, and then he opened his mouth and corrected me.

Not in common speech. In the dead tongue. Fluent, liquid, effortless—the way water finds the shape of the riverbed it hasn't flowed down in a thousand years. He spoke maybe ten words. Then he pressed his palm flat against the panel with the caged pups and said one more word, clearly, deliberately, looking straight at me as if daring me to misunderstand.

And I didn't. Understand it, I mean. I didn't understand the word—but I understood him, the way you understand a scream without needing a translation.

*Remember.*

"That's it," Ferro said, standing up with the finality of a man closing a ledger. "Tomorrow at first light we go over the Grey Stair, down to Lowmark, and we find a priest or a peddler or anyone who'll take the lad off our hands somewhere Vael's grey riders aren't. This is bigger than us, Paloma. Whatever he is, whatever this wall is—we're five smugglers and a drunk. We bury people. We don't bury kingdoms."

"He's right," Isolde said, and it cost her something visible to agree with Ferro, which is how you knew she meant it.

Maybe they were right. Maybe I'm a fool. But I looked at the boy's hand on the painted cage, and I thought about the guild charter Maren had shown me—that we were never mere thieves, that the first wardens had sworn oaths to guard something beneath the peaks, that every tollhouse and hidden pass and smuggler's code we'd inherited was the fossil of a promise somebody made a thousand years ago and somebody else had worked very hard to make us forget.

The guild was dying. Everyone said so. The queen's roads, the queen's wardens, the queen's gold—it was finished, and I'd spent the autumn grieving it like a man grieves a father.

But maybe it wasn't dying. Maybe it was *waiting*. Maybe it had been waiting a thousand years for exactly this: a wall full of truth, a child full of memory, and one tired smuggler stubborn enough to stand in the gap between them.

"First light," I said, and watched relief move through my people like wind through wheat. "We head for the Grey Stair." Then, because I'm honest even when I'm foolish: "And then we decide what we do with the truth. Not before. Nobody sells that boy, nobody sells that wall, and nobody talks about any of this in a border town. Agreed?"

"Agreed," they said—Ferro grim, Isolde quick, Tam around a mouthful of cheese, Maren with her face turned toward the mural as though she could see it after all.

The boy took his hand off the wall.

And that night, when the others slept, I sat up watching the fire and watching him, and I saw him do a strange thing. He crept to the mural one last time in the ember-light, stood before the broken crown held up by all those triumphant hands, and reached up—too short to touch it, stretching on his toes—and traced the break in the air above the paint. Twice. Left hand. Right hand.

Then he looked back at me, and for the first time since the tollhouse door, he smiled. Small. Sad. Certain.

It should have warmed me. Instead it chilled me to the marrow, because I realized I'd been asking the wrong question all week. I'd been asking *what is this child?*

The real question, the one written on that wall in a language the Crown drowned in blood, was simpler and much worse:

*What is coming to meet him?*

At dawn, Tam volunteered to go ahead into Lowmark to buy provisions and listen for warden patrols. Alone. Cheerfully. Whistling.

I let him go. Gods forgive me, I let him go.
