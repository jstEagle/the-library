## 12. Tam Talks Too Much

There are three kinds of drunk, and I have been all of them professionally.

The first kind is the happy drunk, who loves every soul in the room and tells them so until they leave. The second kind is the weeping drunk, who loves every soul in the room and tells them so until they wish he hadn't. And the third kind—the kind I was aiming for that night in Greyhollow's lowest tavern, the one with the crooked sign and the beer that tasted like it had been filtered through an old boot—is the quiet drunk. The one who drinks to put a lid on something and finds the lid keeps sliding off.

I had been trying for the third kind for two days when the fourth kind found me.

But let me back up, because a story without its road behind it is just a man shouting in a barroom, which—well. Which is also what this is. Bear with me.

* * *

We'd come down out of the high passes the week before, the whole ragged company of us, running from grey riders and carrying a child who sang to fireplaces. That sentence would have gotten me laughed out of any tavern in the borderlands a year ago. Now it was just Tuesday.

The plan—and I want it on record that the plan was Isolde's, because she'll deny it later, and because it was a bad plan, and bad plans should always be attributed correctly—was to lie low in Greyhollow while Paloma scouted the old tunnel mouths on the Thornfell side. Greyhollow is the sort of town that exists because the mapmaker got tired and drew a circle there. A garrison of bored wardens, a market that smelled of wet wool and turnips, and eleven taverns for four hundred souls, which any honest mathematician will tell you is at least six too few or seven too many, depending on your temperament.

My temperament said too few.

I was supposed to be keeping my head down. Those were Ferro's exact words, delivered with the finger-point that makes his whole arm shake: *Tam. Head. Down.* Ferro communicates primarily in nouns when he's worried, and he'd been worried since the ford, since before the ford, since the day I met him thirty years ago hauling brandy casks through a snowdrift with our then-captain laughing at us both.

And I want to be clear: I *tried*. I stayed in the rented room above the cooperage for a day and a half. I counted the cracks in the ceiling. I inventoried my regrets, which took most of the afternoon and left me hungry. By dusk on the second day I had concluded that a man can keep his head down only so long before his head develops opinions of its own, and mine has always been the mutinous sort.

So I went down the back stairs and out into the grey slush and found myself, by a route I could not afterward reconstruct, sitting at the corner table of the Drowned Pike with a mug of that boot-flavored beer in front of me and a stranger across from me who kept buying the next round.

That's the thing about being the last of anything. Last apprentice of a dying guild, last keeper of roads nobody travels anymore, last man alive who remembers where the smugglers' caches are buried along the Thornfell spine—I'm not proud of what I am, but I am *aware* of it, the way you're aware of an ache in a tooth. You keep touching it. You keep checking whether it still hurts.

It does. It always does. So you drink.

* * *

The stranger's name was Corvan, he said, though names in border towns are like coin purses—you carry whatever fits the moment. He was big through the shoulders in the way of men who've carried heavy things up mountains, with a face like a door somebody had kicked. Grey eyes. Not grey like the wardens' cloaks; grey like slate after rain, flat and patient. He drank slow. He listened better than anyone I've ever met, and I include Maren in that, and Maren listens with her whole body, ears and hands and the tilt of her head, because her eyes quit working years ago.

This Corvan listened the way a snare listens.

But I was three mugs past noticing snares. I was at the stage of the evening where the lid comes off and everything underneath comes up at once, like a cellar flooding, and what was underneath was this:

We were going to die. All of us. Ferro with his shaking finger, Isolde with her sharp tongue and her sharper silences, Paloma with his maps and his stubbornness and that look he gets when he thinks nobody's watching him watch the boy. We were five fools and a mute child playing hide-and-seek with the Crown of Aurelian itself, and the prize at the end of the game was either a hole in the ground or a hole in history, and I had seen the murals in that chapel and I hadn't slept properly since.

So when Corvan leaned in—friendly-like, shoulder-to-shoulder, the way soldiers sit—and asked what brought a weathered old road-man like me down to Greyhollow in dead winter, I did the arithmetic of grief and loneliness and cheap beer, and I came up with the wrong answer.

"Treasure," I said.

Corvan didn't laugh. That should have warned me. Men laugh at treasure talk; it's the oldest boast in the world, older than crowns, and every tavern in creation has heard a thousand of them. But this man just tilted his head, door-face and slate eyes, and waited.

"Under Thornfell," I said, because the flood was up now and the gates were broken and there was no use pretending I was a man who kept secrets. "Old treasure. Older than the queen's grandmother's grandmother. The guild knows where."

"The guild," he said. His voice was mild as milk. "Thought the smugglers' guild was finished. Everyone says so. Road wardens on every pass, new tolls, new laws." He turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table, precise, like a man adjusting an instrument. "Everyone says it's over."

"Everyone," I said, and here I made my second mistake of the night, though I didn't know it yet, "is wrong. There are things under those mountains that don't care whose law it is. There are doors down there older than the Crown, and the guild was founded—" I stopped. Some small drowning part of me grabbed a rope. "Founded to move goods. Brandy, mostly. Very old brandy."

"Very old brandy," Corvan repeated.

"Century-old. Possibly older. Extremely valuable brandy."

He smiled then. It was a careful smile, the smile of a man who has decided not to show all his teeth. "And these doors. The ones that don't care about the queen's law. Who guards them?"

"Guarded themselves, last I heard," I said, and reached for the beer, and found the mug empty, and Corvan signaled the keeper with two fingers, and the keeper brought two more, and I should have stood up right there. I know that. Sitting here in the cold morning of my memory, I know exactly where I should have stood up, pushed back the bench, wished him good health, and gone home to count ceiling cracks until dawn.

Instead I drank the fourth beer, and somewhere in the drinking of it I told him about the chapel murals. Not all of it. Not the child, thank every god that ever got drunk and dozed off—I never said a word about the child, and I cling to that like a drowning man clings to a plank with nails in it. But the murals. Crowned wolves slaughtered by men holding a broken crown. A shrine in the hills that nobody tended, telling a story that contradicts every history book in the kingdom, painted by hands a thousand years dead.

Corvan listened to all of it with his slate eyes steady, and when I ran out of words he was quiet a long moment, and then he did the thing I still dream about. He opened his purse.

Silver. Not copper, not the greasy low-value coin of casual kindness. Silver, stacked, counted out on the scarred tabletop in little towers, ten coins and then ten more, deliberate as a mason laying brick.

"For the brandy," he said. "When you find it. Consider this an advance."

"I can't take—"

"You can." He slid the stack across. His hand, when it passed close to the candle, had a burn scar across the knuckles, old and straight, the kind you get from rope under load. "Consider it a gift between travelers. And consider—" and here his voice dropped, not menacing, worse than menacing, *reasonable*, "—that a man who talks about treasure in taverns might someday want to talk about other things too. To someone who'd pay better than silver. Someone patient. Someone who understands that old things under mountains belong to whoever's strong enough to dig them up. The Crown pays well for strong men, Tam. Or whatever your name is."

He knew. Of course he knew. Had known since I sat down, probably since I walked in, maybe since before that. The whole evening had been a net and I'd swum into it singing about brandy.

I took the silver. That's the part that shames me most—not the talk, though the talk was bad enough to curdle milk—but the taking. My hand went out and closed around those coins like they were warm, like they were bread, like they were the first honest weight they'd held in years, and Corvan watched me do it and nodded slowly, the way you nod at a horse that's finally accepted the bit.

"Wise," he said. "Drink up. Winter's long, and Greyhollow's full of listeners."

Then he stood, dropped a coin on the table for the keeper—gold, I noticed, gold, which buys a lot of listening—and walked out into the slush without looking back, and the door swung shut behind him, and I sat alone with twenty pieces of silver and the sudden, ice-water certainty that I had just done the worst night's work of my life.

* * *

Here's the thing about guilt. It doesn't hit like a hammer. It hits like weather. It's already everywhere before you notice it, and by the time you feel it, it's soaked through everything, and there's nowhere dry to stand.

I sat in the Drowned Pike until they lit lamps, turning the coins over. Literally turning them over, thumbing the edges, looking at the stamps. Border mint, recent year, good weight. Warden-issue pay, or near enough that it didn't matter. Payment for informants, if you wanted to name it plainly, and I've never much seen the point of not naming things plainly—it's the one virtue I've got left, plain speaking, though you'd never guess it tonight of all nights.

Had I sold them? Had I actually sold them?

I hadn't given a name. I hadn't given a route. I hadn't mentioned the boy, or the vault, or Maren's charts, or the tunnel mouth Paloma had marked on his map with a charcoal cross and told us all to forget we'd seen. I'd talked about brandy and murals and doors, vague as fog, and fog doesn't hang anybody.

But fog shows a tracker where the ground dips. And Corvan hadn't needed names. He'd needed confirmation—that we were real, that the rumor had meat on it, that the trail through Greyhollow wasn't cold. Twenty silver to confirm what his masters already half-knew. Cheap. Cheap as sin, which is to say it costs nothing going in and everything after.

I thought about Tam-the-younger, thirty years back, hauling casks through snowdrifts. He wouldn't have taken the coins. He'd have thrown them in Corvan's face and dared him to make something of it, and probably lost some teeth doing it, and slept fine after. Old Tam, current model, takes the coins because winter is coming and his boots have holes and a man gets tired of being loyal to things that are already dead. That's what I told myself, anyway, sitting there with the lamp smoking and the beer gone sour in my gut. That the guild was dead already, that loyalty to it was just nostalgia wearing armor, that twenty silver was twenty silver and feelings were free.

The lie had a crack in it shaped like Ferro's shaking finger. *Head. Down.*

I'd had one job. One noun. And I'd traded it for beer and an audience.

* * *

I didn't go straight back to the cooperage. I walked. Greyhollow at night in the slush, lamplighters working their rounds, a dog barking itself hoarse somewhere, the mountain looming black over the rooftops like a closed fist. I walked past the garrison wall and saw the grey cloaks moving on the rampart, torches spaced regular as teeth, and I told myself: they're always there. That means nothing. Wardens garrison towns; it's what wardens do; the sky is blue and wardens stand on walls.

But there were more of them than there'd been two days ago. I'd bet my remaining teeth on it. And down by the south gate, where the road runs toward the passes, there were horses saddled in the yard, standing ready in the cold, breath steaming. Nobody saddles horses at midnight unless somebody expects to ride.

I gave the coins away.

Not all of them—I'm a coward, not a saint, and a man needs beer money even in penance, especially in penance. But I found a beggar huddled in the chapel porch on Tanner's Row, a woman with a cough like a landslide, and I pressed half the silver into her mittened hand and told her it was from Saint Aldric, patron of fools, and she looked at me like she knew exactly which fool, and blessed me anyway, which was more than I deserved.

The rest I pitched down a storm drain, one coin at a time, listening to each one ring against the stone below, and I swear on my mother's grave—which is a rock cairn above the Kettle Pass, and she'd have approved of the venue—each coin rang a different note, and together they sounded almost like the opening bars of a song I'd heard a mute child sing to a fireplace.

I walked home after that with my heart going like a drum in a parade, and I stood outside the cooperage a long time in the falling snow, looking up at the window where the candle burned, where Ferro was no doubt awake—he sleeps like a cat, one ear open—and deciding.

Tell them. Don't tell them. Tell them and be cast out, or don't tell them and carry it. Both weights felt the same in my hands, and both were heavier than silver.

In the end I climbed the stairs, and Ferro was indeed awake, sitting by the banked fire with a whetstone and his knife, and he looked up at me with those old soldier's eyes that miss nothing except what they choose to, and he said, "You smell like the Drowned Pike."

"Aye," I said.

"Head," he said.

"Down," I said. "I know it, Ferro. I know."

He grunted and went back to his knife, and I lay down on my pallet in my boots because I didn't trust myself to sleep, and I stared at the ceiling cracks, and somewhere in the small hours I decided—firmly, soberly, with the best intentions of a man lying to himself in the dark—that I would tell Paloma everything in the morning. Every word, every coin, every note the storm drain sang. First light. Straightaway. Before it could matter.

And I would have, too. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep for men like me: Tam meant to confess.

But morning came early, and it came with hooves.

I woke to Ferro's hand clamped over my mouth and his knife already drawn, and through the shutter-slats I saw the street below filling with grey cloaks, silent as ash falling, warden patrols where no patrols had ever walked at dawn, and at their center a rider dismounting at the cooperage door—a tall figure in a grey travelling coat, unhurried, patient, slate-eyed even from this distance, even through the dark.

Corvan. Or whoever Corvan had been. Standing in the snow beneath our window, looking up, and smiling his careful smile like a man adjusting an instrument.

"Tam," Ferro breathed against my ear. "What did you do?"

And the worst of it—the very worst, the part I'll carry into whatever grave they dig me—was that I didn't know how to answer. Because I'd only talked about brandy.

Only that. Only ever that.

Below us, the rider raised one gloved hand, and the wardens began to move toward the stairs.
