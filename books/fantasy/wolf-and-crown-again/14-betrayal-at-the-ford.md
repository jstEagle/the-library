## 14. Betrayal at the Ford

I want it on the record — mine, at least, the only record that will survive this story — that I did not ignore the child's warning. I am a smuggler, not an idiot, and when a mute boy who speaks dead languages points past your shoulder with the gravity of a falling stone, you turn around and look.

What I saw was smoke. Not cookfire smoke. Wardens' signal smoke, three plumes in a ragged line along the western ridge, which meant riders, which meant they had cut ahead of us somehow, which meant —

Well. We'll get to what it meant.

"Off the trail," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended, the way it does when the fear has already done its arithmetic. "Down. Now."

Ferro didn't argue, which told me everything about how bad he thought it was. Ferro argues the way other men breathe; silence from Ferro is a scream from anyone else. He swung the boy up onto his shoulders and took the slope like a landslide with opinions, and Isolde followed with the mule, and I went last because somebody always goes last, and in our guild that somebody is the one holding the ledger of who gets left behind if it comes to that.

We dropped through the pines toward the sound of water. The map in my head — Maren's charts overlaid on twenty years of my own boots — said there was a river below us, the Greywash before it widened into the shallows they call Miller's Ford, and past the ford, the old drover road that ran blind through the hills for two days without a single tollhouse or warden post. If we crossed by nightfall we'd be ghosts again. Ghosts were the only thing I knew how to be.

The problem, as the boy had so eloquently pointed out, was behind us.

* * *

We reached the ford at dusk with the light going copper and the water running loud enough to cover conversation, which is why I didn't hear Tam coming up beside me until he was already talking.

"Paloma." His breath smelled like the cheap plum brandy he swore he'd given up in Hollow Chapel, and his eyes had the wet shine of a man rehearsing something. "About what I said. In that town. You know I wasn't — I mean, a man talks. A man's got to talk or he dries up inside like a—"

"Tam." I kept my eyes on the far bank. "Later."

"There might not be a later." He laughed, but it was the wrong laugh, the kind you use to push something uphill. "That's all I'm saying. A man ought to say things while there's ears to hear them."

I looked at him then. Old Tam, sixty if he was fifty, hands like knotted rope, the best lock-man the Thornfell passes ever produced back when his fingers still bent all the ways fingers should. He couldn't meet my eyes. He was looking at the river like it owed him money.

"You talked in that border town," I said. It wasn't a question. I don't know why I said it softly instead of shouting it. Maybe because shouting would have made it real, and some part of me — the part that had pulled him out of two avalanches and one marriage — wanted ten more minutes of not knowing.

"I talked," Tam said. "God forgive me, boy, I talked."

The word *boy* did it. Twenty years I'd carried that name from him, worn it like a coat, and in that moment it turned to iron and I understood that whatever came next, the man who'd taught me to pick my first lock had just picked the lock on all of us.

Before I could decide whether to break his jaw or his heart, the horns started.

* * *

They came down both banks. That's the detail that still wakes me at night — not the numbers, not the crossbows, but the *geometry*. Wardens at the ford upstream and downstream both, torches unhooded in a coordinated instant, and riders closing the treeline on our side of the river. Nobody surrounds a ford like that by luck. Somebody had told them exactly where we'd be, exactly when, and exactly which side of the water we'd need.

Which meant the ambush hadn't been built for the place we were. It had been built for the place we were *going*.

"Tam," Isolde said quietly, beside me. Her voice had gone very calm, which anyone who knew her understood to be the most dangerous weather she had. "How many did you tell?"

"Just the one man. Just once. I swear it on the guild—" He stopped. Swearing on the guild, with horns blowing, is a joke even drunk men know not to make.

"They paid you in silver," I said. "Maren heard. Silver and a listener with soft boots."

"It was for the debt. Paloma, the guild's debts, the ones I never told you about, the ones from before your time — they had my mark on them, and the collector found me in that town like he'd been *planted* there, and I thought, one name of a treasure nobody's ever seen, one drunk story, and Tam sleeps warm the rest of his life—"

"The treasure doesn't exist, Tam."

"I know that now." His voice cracked clean down the middle. "I didn't know they were hunting a *child*."

And there it was. The whole rotten arithmetic of it, laid out in the torchlight: an old man's debts, a stranger's kindness, and a boy of six winters whose only crime was carrying something in his skull older than every crown on the continent.

The child stood between Ferro's legs, watching the torches come down the bank with that terrible calm he had, the calm of someone who had maybe seen sieges before, in dreams too big for his body. He tugged Ferro's sleeve and pointed — not at the wardens, not at the ford, but at a bend of the river upstream where nothing was visible at all except dark water and darker rock.

"He says there's a crossing," Ferro rumbled. "Upstream. Don't ask me how he knows. The boy knows roads like you know roads, Paloma. Like they're *in* him."

"Then we take his crossing." I turned to the others. "Listen. They're set for Miller's Ford. They'll expect us to run the drover road or die here. So we do neither. Ferro takes the boy and the mule upstream, Isolde with them. I stay here and give them a smuggler worth the name."

"No," said Isolde.

"It wasn't a vote."

"Everything in this guild is a vote. It's the only thing that's kept us alive longer than we deserve." She planted herself in front of me, arms crossed, torchlight carving her face into sharp planes. "You go first. I stay. I'm faster than you in broken ground and colder under fire, and we both know it, and you hate that I know it."

She was right, which I hated. But Tam stepped forward, and there was something in how he moved — sober, deliberate, the brandy-shake gone from his hands — that stopped us both.

"No," Tam said. "It's me stays."

"Tam—"

"I sold the road. Let me buy it back." He was pulling off his boots as he talked, the mad old fool, barefoot in the shallows like a man going wading on a summer day. "They'll follow a runner easier than a group. And I'm the slowest thing in this company, so let me be the bait that's *meant* to be caught. Give me your flare-powder, Paloma. All of it. I'll go up the east bank loud as a festival and blow the scree-slope behind me when they bunch up. You've got maybe four hundred heartbeats. Use them."

"That's a death," I said. Plainly. Because he deserved plainness, finally, after a lifetime of his lies.

Tam smiled, and for one moment he wasn't old and he wasn't drunk and he wasn't ashamed; he was the man who'd lifted seven-year-old me over a wall the first night of my apprenticeship and said *up you come, little ghost*, and I loved him the way you love the person who teaches you the shape of your own courage.

"All my life I sold roads," he said. "Let me die owning one."

* * *

I gave him the powder. That's the sentence I keep turning over. I gave him the powder, kissed his filthy forehead, and lied to him that we'd come back for him, and he lied right back that he'd meet us at the drover camp with wine, and we both knew we were trading farewells dressed as promises, because that's the liturgy of smugglers — we are a faith built entirely out of beautiful lies told kindly.

Then we went upstream, fast and low, while Tam went east, singing. Gods help me, he was *singing* — some bawdy old pass-song about the miller's wife, at the top of his lungs, crashing through brush like a herd of cattle, waving a torch he must have soaked in resin. The horns shifted. The torches on the east bank turned. The trap, built with such precision for the five of us, began to chase one old man through the dark.

The child's crossing was real. Of course it was real. There was a spine of submerged rock under the bend he'd pointed to, invisible from the banks, the kind of secret water holds close, and we went across it single file with the Greywash shoving at our knees and the boy on Ferro's shoulders humming — *humming*, low and steady, and the river went quiet around us. Not calm. Quiet. Obedient. Isolde crossed last and stared at the water behind us like it had personally insulted her.

On the far bank, we crouched in the reeds and looked back.

Tam had led them onto the scree. I saw the torch bobbing among the pursuing lights, saw the lights gather the way wolves gather, confident, converging — and then the world cracked open. The flare-powder went off with a roar I felt in my teeth, and half the mountainside above the east bank decided, after ten thousand patient years, to come down and see what all the noise was about. Torches scattered like sparks. Screaming, small with distance. And one voice, rising over all of it, laughing — actual laughing — old Tam singing his miller's-wife song at the avalanche as it took him, and I put my face in the frozen mud of the riverbank so the others wouldn't hear what sound I made, though of course Isolde heard. Isolde hears everything.

When I looked up, the child was watching me. Not with pity. Pity I could have survived. He watched me with recognition, the way you look at someone standing in a room you've stood in yourself, and he reached out and touched my cheek with one cold finger, and for half a heartbeat I felt something pour through that touch that wasn't words but was *older* than words — grief, vast and unhurried, the grief of someone who had counted the dead of whole centuries and never once gotten used to it.

He grieved *with* me. That was the horror of it. This child who had known Tam for eleven days grieved like a king over a battlefield.

* * *

We walked all night. Nobody spoke until the grey hour, when the drover road finally took us under its trees and the pursuit, such as remained of it, fell away behind the ridge lines.

It was Isolde who broke the quiet, because of course it was.

"One man," she said. "One listener with soft boots. That's not enough to build tonight. Tonight needed maps. Timing. Knowledge of which ford, which hour, which route." She wasn't accusing. That was the worst part. She was *reasoning*, out loud, in front of all of us, because she'd decided we deserved the truth even if it cut. "Vael didn't find us, Paloma. Vael was *aimed*."

"I know."

"And Tam's slip happened in a border town nine days ago. Nine days is enough for silver to travel. Enough for a letter." She paused, and the pause had a blade in it. "You know who can write letters."

Ferro stopped walking. The boy, asleep against his back, didn't stir.

"Don't," Ferro said. One word, gravel-heavy. "Don't say it while she's standing right there unless you're sure."

"I'm not sure," I said. "That's what frightens me. I'm never sure anymore. Someone drew Vael a map of our souls, and everyone at this fire has had reason to. Tam sold a story. Isolde has a sister in a Crown cell. I—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "I have a ledger full of names the queen would pay a kingdom to read. Any one of us could have done it. Maybe all of us did it a little, in small ways, without knowing."

Isolde's face didn't change. Later, so much later, I would learn what it cost her to keep it still in that moment, and I would owe her more than I have ever been able to pay. But that night I only saw a woman who didn't flinch, and I mistook the stillness for innocence, and the mistake nearly killed us all.

"So what do we do?" Ferro asked.

I looked down the drover road, black under the branches, running west toward the deep country where Maren's oldest charts marked tunnels that predated the guild, predated the Crown, predated maybe everything. The child had pointed us there once already, in his sleep, drawing shapes in ash that Maren had gone white looking at.

"We stop being hunted," I said. "We stop running their roads altogether. Tomorrow we go underground — the Deep Road, the one Maren swears exists and I swear is a fable. And we don't tell another living soul which door we use. No letters. No fires. No songs in taverns." I looked at each of them, and I hated how much I loved them, and how much that love had become a weapon anyone could pick up. "From tonight, the company is five. Four." My voice snagged on it. "Four."

Behind us, far off in the eastern dark, I thought I heard — or dreamed I heard, which amounts to the same thing in the mountains — one last thread of Tam's song, thin as wire, riding the wind down out of the scree.

The child opened his eyes. He was looking back the way we'd come, at the sound, or at whatever rode beneath it, and his lips moved around silent words in that buried language, and this time — though I have told no one this, though I barely admit it to myself — I could almost feel what the words were shaped like.

Not a warning this time.

A naming. A remembering. As if somewhere behind us in the ruin of rock and torchlight, something had just been added to the long list the boy carries, the list of the dead of Thornfell, and would carry now until someone made the Crown answer for every name on it.

Mine included, if I failed.

I turned west, and we walked into the dark together, four of us and a ghost's song fading behind, and none of us — not one — suspected yet how thoroughly the net was still closing, or that the next door out of the mountains would open onto the one man in the world we least wanted to see.
