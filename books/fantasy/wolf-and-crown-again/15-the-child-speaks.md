## 15. The Child Speaks

I had buried Tam at dawn, under a cairn of river stones because the ground was frozen too hard for anything kinder. I said the words we say over smugglers — *the road remembers him* — and then I stopped talking, because the road had also gotten him killed, and I didn't much feel like flattering it.

By midday we were moving again, up the eastern flank of the pass where the trees thin out and the rock starts doing that thing mountain rock does: pretending to be scenery while quietly planning your death. Ferro carried the boy on his shoulders, which surprised me until I thought about it. Ferro is the kind of man who expresses grief by picking up heavy things and refusing to put them down. The boy was the heaviest thing left to carry.

Isolde walked behind us with her hood up and her silence wrapped around her like a second cloak. She'd been quiet since the ford. Quieter than usual, and Isolde's usual is already a kind of weaponized quiet. I told myself it was mourning. I told myself a lot of things that day.

Maren rode the mule because her feet were done pretending, and she kept one hand on the boy's ankle as they went, the way you'd steady a lantern in wind. She couldn't see him. She touched him instead, constantly, small corrections and reassurances, and he let her, which from a child who flinched at strangers was its own kind of miracle.

We made maybe six miles before the weather turned on us.

It came off the high snowfields without warning — not a storm so much as a white opinion, the mountain's considered view on our continued existence. Visibility dropped to arm's length. The temperature fell fast enough that my beard crackled when I breathed through it. And somewhere behind us, faint under the wind, I heard what I'd been dreading since the ford: horns. Not hunting horns. Wardens' horns, the long flat note they use to talk to each other across distances, three blasts, pause, three blasts. *Found them. Hold them.*

"They're herding us," Isolde said, and her voice came out flat as slate. "West ridge is cut. They want us on the scree."

"Then we disappoint them," I said, because that's the family business. Disappointment, delivered at speed, through terrain no sane person would enter.

The problem was there wasn't anywhere to go. I knew these passes the way you know the back of your hand, except the back of your hand has never tried to kill you. Every route I could name, I could also name who'd be standing on it. Vael wasn't chasing us anymore. He was funneling us, the way you funnel water toward a mill wheel, and every path out of his funnel ended in either a patrol or a cliff.

Except one.

There's a place up there the guild maps call the Kettle — a bowl of stone below the old watchline where the wind eddies and the snow drifts forty feet deep and nothing lives, not even lichen. Smugglers used it once, generations back, as a hiding hole when the wardens swept the pass. You go in through a crevice behind a fallen slab, and you sit in the dark and the cold and you wait, and if you're lucky the searchers look at the Kettle and see exactly what it looks like: nothing. A dead place. Why would anyone hide in a grave?

Because dead places are the only ones nobody checks. That's the whole trade, really. That's always been the whole trade.

"We hole up in the Kettle," I said. "Wait out the sweep."

Ferro looked at me the way he looks at men who suggest spending money. "It's a frozen pit."

"It's a frozen pit they won't search."

"And if they do?"

"Then we'll be very cold corpses, and the queen will have saved on burial costs."

He grunted, which from Ferro is a signature on a contract. We turned upslope into the teeth of the wind and climbed.

* * *

The crevice was narrower than I remembered, or I was wider, or both. We squeezed through single file, Maren passing the boy along hand to hand like something breakable and precious, which — I was starting to understand — was precisely what he was, though not in any way I could have named yet.

Inside, the Kettle was worse than outside. The wind couldn't reach us, which meant the cold just sat there, patient, ancient, waiting. We got a fire going in a hollow of stone, small and mean, shielded so no light leaked out. We huddled around it. We ate hard bread and harder cheese. We did all the things people do when they're trying to be alive a little longer.

And then we waited, and the waiting did what waiting does. It peeled things open.

Ferro sharpened his axe with a whetstone, slow strokes, rhythmic, a man praying the only way he knows. Isolde sat apart with her back to the rock and her eyes on the fire, and I caught her looking at me twice, opening her mouth, closing it again. There was something lodged in her she couldn't cough up. I'd seen that look before, on men about to confess to crimes, on men about to commit them. I didn't know which she was. I still don't, entirely. But I noted it, the way you note a creak in a floorboard — not alarming yet, but filed away.

Maren held the boy against her side under her cloak and hummed to him, an old guild song about a mule that outlived three owners, and the boy listened with his whole body, the way children listen when language is still music and not yet machinery.

That was when the horns changed.

They'd been circling, distant, conversational. Now they came closer, and closer, and then stopped altogether, and the silence afterward was worse. Silence means the searching is done and the finding has begun.

Boots on stone, above us. Voices, muffled by rock. The scrape of something heavy — a shield? a pike butt? — dragged across the slab that hid our entrance.

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. The fire was banked to embers, barely a red secret in the dark. Ferro's hand found his axe and stayed there. Isolde went very still, the stillness of a woman running arithmetic she doesn't like the answer to.

And the boy stood up.

Maren reached for him and missed. He walked past her, past the fire, to the inner wall of the Kettle — the far wall, the one that isn't a wall at all but a tumble of ancient stone older than the guild, older than the road, stone we'd never had reason to look at twice — and he put both palms flat against it.

"Boy," Ferro whispered, sharp. "Get back here."

He didn't get back here.

The voices above us grew louder. I counted at least six. There was a sound like someone testing the slab, leaning weight against it, and I did the sums every smuggler does in that moment — how many of us, how many of them, how many steps to the crevice, how long to die — and the sums came out bad. Bad the way they'd come out at the ford. Bad the way they keep coming out, ever since a half-frozen child knocked on my door in the worst winter in twenty years and rewrote the arithmetic of my entire life.

And then the boy spoke.

Not Thornic. Not the liquid, layered tongue he'd sung to the hearth-flames, the one Maren called pre-kingdom, the one that was supposed to be extinct. He spoke common. Plain, clear, unaccented common, in a voice that was his voice and somehow also not, pitched low and resonant in a way no seven-year-old's throat should manage:

**"They are waking."**

The words went through that stone room like a pick through ice.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because I've turned it over enough times now that I could almost convince myself I imagined parts of it. I didn't. Here is what happened.

The boy's eyes flashed amber.

Not reflected firelight — I've seen firelight in eyes my whole life, and this wasn't it. This came from inside, from behind, from some depth in him that a child's skull has no business containing. Amber like resin. Amber like the heartwood of a tree a thousand years felled. It lit for perhaps two heartbeats, and in those two heartbeats the whole Kettle changed color, the shadows going gold, the embers of our little fire guttering flat as though something larger had asserted precedence.

And the stone moved.

Not much. An inch, maybe two, along a seam I had never noticed in thirty years of knowing that rock. Enough that a breath of air came out of the gap — and the air was wrong. It was warm. In a frozen pit above the snowline, in the dead heart of winter, warm air breathed out of the mountain, and it smelled of iron and pine smoke and something else, something I can only call *attended*, the smell of a house where someone is home.

Above us, the voices stopped.

A long moment. Then boots retreating, quickly, the scrape of the slab abandoned, the horns sounding again — different now, a pattern I didn't know, urgent, falling away westward. Whatever the wardens heard or felt through that stone, whatever their officers decided, they left. All of them. At speed.

The boy took his hands off the wall. The seam closed — I watched it close, ground itself shut like a jaw finishing a word — and the amber went out of his eyes, and he turned around and looked at us, five grown people pressed against the far wall of a hidden vault like mice, and he said, in that same borrowed-depth voice, softer now:

"You are late."

Then he swayed, and Maren caught him before he hit the ground, and he was just a child again — limp, breathing fast, eyes closed, ordinary dark eyes with no amber in them at all.

* * *

For a long time nobody said anything. The fire recovered its nerve and started crackling again. Somewhere far off, the last horn faded into wind.

Ferro broke first, because Ferro always breaks first; his silences have shorter tempers than he does. "What in the nine names was that."

"That," Maren said, holding the sleeping boy, her blind face turned toward the sealed wall with an expression I couldn't read and she couldn't see anyway, "was a door answering."

"Doors don't answer."

"This one did." She smoothed the boy's hair. "Paloma. Come here. Touch the wall."

I didn't want to touch the wall. I want that on the record. But I'm the one who opened doors for a living, so I crossed the Kettle and put my palm where his had been, and the stone was warm. Body-warm. And under my hand, faint as a pulse through a wrist, I felt it: a rhythm. Slow. Deep. Patient beyond all reason.

Something on the other side of that wall was asleep, and had been for a very long time, and had just rolled over.

I took my hand back and wiped it on my coat, which is not a dignified thing for a guild-master to do, and I didn't care.

"The charter," Maren said quietly. "The true founding. *Wardens of the road and of what lies beneath it.* I showed you the page, Paloma. I told you what we were before we were thieves." Her voice did something complicated. "I thought it was a figure of speech. Guilds love figures of speech. *Beneath the peaks.* I thought it meant the mines."

"And now?"

"Now I think the guild was founded as a lock," she said, "and we have spent a thousand years congratulating ourselves on being excellent thieves, when all along we were the tumblers."

Isolde laughed. One short, hard syllable, with no humor in it whatsoever. "Wonderful. Our whole trade is a hinge."

"Our whole trade," I said, "is whatever I decide it is. That hasn't changed." Even as I said it I heard how thin it sounded, and so, judging by her face, did Isolde.

Ferro hadn't sat down. He was standing over the boy with his arms folded, looking down at him the way you look at a dog that has just done something impossible — pride and alarm fighting for the same territory. "He spoke," Ferro said. "Common. First words, and they're a prophecy. Most children start with *mama*."

"He's not most children," Maren said.

"No," Ferro said. "He's not. And I'd like to know what he is, and I'd like to know it soon, because I just watched a mountain move for him, and I've spent my whole life believing mountains only moved for other mountains."

"He said they're waking," Isolde said. She was looking at the wall. "Who's *they*, Paloma?"

"I don't know."

"You always know. It's insufferable. You know every pass, every toll, every warden's drinking habits—"

"Not this." I looked at the seam in the stone, invisible now, gone back to being ordinary rock, and felt the warmth fading off my palm. "This is older than the roads. Older than the guild. Maybe older than the Crown, and the Crown is old enough to have forgotten what it did to get that way." I heard myself and stopped. I hadn't known I believed that last part until I said it. That happens, sometimes, in the mountains. The altitude shakes truths loose that you'd been carrying without noticing the weight.

Maren turned her face toward me. Blind eyes, seeing more than the rest of us combined. "You felt it," she said. "Under your hand. Tell them what you felt."

"A heartbeat," I said. "Slow one."

"How slow?"

I did the arithmetic and didn't like the answer. "One beat for every hundred of mine. Maybe more."

"Then 'waking' is a generous word," Maren said. "More like... turning in sleep. Nearing morning." She pulled the cloak tighter around the boy. "But mornings come. Eventually. To everything."

* * *

We didn't sleep much, which was fine, because none of us wanted to dream in that place.

Near midnight — or what I guessed was midnight; time goes strange in a hole in a mountain — the boy woke, and he was himself again, small and silent and hungry, and he ate his share of bread and leaned against Maren and was a child. I watched him for a long time. I kept looking at his eyes, waiting for the amber, and it didn't come, and that should have comforted me.

It didn't. Because here's the thing I couldn't stop thinking, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone:

When he spoke, I was afraid of the wardens. When his eyes lit, I was afraid of *him*. And those are not the same fear, and only one of them goes away when the horns fade.

He was still a child who liked Maren's humming and slept curled against her side and had once, weeks ago, fallen asleep holding my sleeve in a fist, and I had sat there an extra hour rather than wake him. None of that had changed. All of that was still true. And simultaneously, that child had put his hands on a wall and spoken with a voice that came from underneath the world, and the world had answered, and I had seen ten centuries of patience blink open behind a pair of dark eyes.

Awe is supposed to be a clean feeling. Nobody tells you it curdles. Nobody tells you that the same sight that makes the hair on your arms rise in wonder can make it rise in terror two seconds later, and that the difference is nothing but which way your mind tips, and that your mind tips whichever way it's weakest.

Mine tipped toward terror. I'm a smuggler. We're built low to the ground. Wonder is for priests and poets; my trade runs on knowing exactly how badly things can go.

And things could go very badly indeed. Because the boy had said *they are waking*, plural, and he had said *you are late*, which meant there was a schedule, and schedules mean appointments, and appointments mean that sooner or later something under this mountain was going to expect us — all of us, the guild, the Crown, everyone — to show up on time.

And behind us, in the dark, Vael was still out there. Vael, who wanted the boy. Vael, who would now know — his wardens would have reported the horns, the retreat, the strange stillness — that something had happened in the Kettle that his doctrine had predicted all along. I could hear him already, that reasonable voice of his: *You see? The vessel responds to the old places. The old blood stirs. Give me the child, and we end this before it begins.*

He'd even be sincere about it. That's the worst part. Men like Vael are always sincere.

I sat by the dying fire and turned my palm over and looked at it, at the hand that had touched a sleeping thing's door, and I thought about Tam under his cairn of river stones, and about the murals in the hollow chapel, crowned wolves slaughtered by men holding a broken crown, and about Maren saying *we were the tumblers*.

The boy stirred in his sleep and murmured something in Thornic, soft, half-words. Maren shushed him gently and rocked him, and the fire bowed — actually bowed, flame bending sideways in perfectly still air — and straightened again, and nobody commented, because commenting had passed beyond all of us sometime around dusk.

Ferro sat down beside me. Big, warm, immovable Ferro, smelling of whetstone oil and woodsmoke.

"Paloma," he said, very quietly, so the others wouldn't hear. "Tomorrow. When we're moving. You and me need to talk about what we do if keeping him stops being brave and starts being suicide."

"Ferro—"

"I'm not saying give him up." His jaw worked. "I'm saying I've carried him on my shoulders for two days and I'd carry him to the sea. And I'm saying I watched you wipe your hand on your coat tonight like you'd touched a corpse. So one of us is lying, and it might be both of us."

He got up, checked the crevice, checked it again, and lay down with his axe in reach, and was snoring within minutes, because Ferro's conscience sleeps better than mine does.

I stayed up. Somebody had to keep the first watch, and somebody had to sit with the fact that the mountain had a heartbeat, and the heartbeat was quickening, and the boy had told us we were late.

Somewhere below my palm's memory of warm stone, something old turned over again in its sleep.

And began, I think, to dream of us.
