## 16. Ferro's Doubt

I've carried a lot of things down the mountain roads in my day. Casks of unlicensed brandy. A fugitive countess in a pickle barrel, once, which she complained about for six hours. My own weight, which has never gotten any lighter and has recently begun to feel like a personal grudge.

But I have never carried anything like the look Paloma gave me when I told him we ought to give the boy up.

Let me back up. It's easier that way. Everything's easier if you back up first and look at the whole road instead of the rockslide in front of you.

* * *

We'd been holed up in a shepherd's hut above the Greywater for two days. Two days of cold mutton, cold wind, and colder silence, because Tam was dead, Isolde wouldn't meet anyone's eyes, and Paloma had that look he gets when he's decided something and is waiting for the world to argue with him.

The boy sat by the hearth, which wasn't lit, because lit hearths draw eyes, and drew in the ash with one finger. Little roads. Little passes. The same ones he'd drawn the first night at the tollhouse, the same ones Maren had stared at like she'd seen a ghost wearing her own face.

I don't trust things like that. I want to be clear about that up front. I'm not a learned man — Maren's the learned one, gods keep her, and the gods can have the job — but I know what I know, and what I know is that when something arrives out of a blizzard with no name and no tongue and a map of your own secret roads in its head, that something is not a child. That something is a debt, and debts always come due, and the collector is never the one who pays.

"So," I said. I'd been working up to it for a day and a half, chewing it over like gristle. "We should talk about the obvious thing."

Paloma didn't look up from the map he was pretending to study. "The obvious thing."

"The boy."

"Ferro—"

"Hear me out. I know you're going to do the thing where you look at me like I kicked a puppy, so hear me out first." I set down the mutton bone. It was a good bone. I'd gotten most of the meat off it, which was more than the hut deserved. "Inquisitors came to the tollhouse offering gold. Now they've got Tam's big mouth and — whoever else's ears, and I'm not saying names, but I noticed Isolde went for a walk the night before the ford, and I noticed she came back looking like a woman who'd swallowed a hornet's nest. They know where we are, Paloma. They knew where the ford was. They knew the timing. That's not luck. That's arithmetic."

"Ferro." Paloma's voice had gone quiet, which with him is worse than shouting. Quiet means he's already decided and is now deciding how much of you to take down with him. "He's eight years old."

"He's a beacon." I pointed at the boy, who was still drawing his little roads in the ash, serene as a saint on a coin. "Every hour he's with us, we're a lit torch in a dry field. You want to know how many of us are left? Count. Go on. Maren's blind and slow. Isolde's — Isolde. I'm old and fat and my knees sound like the ice on the Greywater in spring. And Tam's in a shallow grave because he stood in a ford with a crossbow against twelve wardens so the rest of us could run." My voice cracked on that last bit, which I hated, because I'd planned this speech to be cold and reasonable, and cold and reasonable men don't crack. "Tam died for a stranger. I'd like to not die for the same stranger. That's all. That's the whole speech. Give me a medal."

The boy looked up. He looked at me the way he looks at everything — like he's reading a letter written in a language he almost remembers. Then he went back to his roads.

Paloma stood up. He's not a big man, Paloma. Never has been. All wire and shadow, the kind of frame smugglers build when they've spent twenty years fitting through gaps the law can't follow. But when he stood up in that hut, with the grey light through the shutter slats cutting him into strips, he looked like something the mountain had grown on purpose.

"Sit down, Ferro."

"I am sitting down."

"Then stay sitting, because if you stand up we're going to have a problem, and I don't have the energy for a problem." He came around the table and crouched so we were eye to eye, which he does, which is a trick, because it makes you feel like the unreasonable one. "You want to hand an eight-year-old boy to Vael. To the queen's inquisitors. You've met Vael. You've seen his eyes. You want to hand that child to a man who calls him a *vessel*."

"I want to hand him to someone, yes."

"To be what? Opened up? Cut apart to see what's inside? You heard what the wardens said at the ford. *The vessel. Take the vessel whole.* Whole, Ferro. They want him whole the way a butcher wants a pig whole — it's about the cuts, not the pig."

I looked at the boy. He'd drawn the whole Thornfell range now, all the passes, and he was putting a little circle under the eastern shoulder of the Crownspire, right where — right where Maren's charts said the old tunnels went. Right where nothing was supposed to be.

"He's not a pig," I said. "That's my point. He's not a pig and he's not a boy, either, maybe. He's something else, and things that are something else get people killed. I've been in this guild since I was fourteen, Paloma. Fourteen. I carried your first cargo. I carried Maren out of the Kettle Pass when the snow took her eyes. I dug the tollhouse out of three avalanches with my own two hands, and one of those times it was to save your stubborn hide. This guild is four people and a blind woman, and you're asking us to die for a child who sings to hearth-fires and draws roads nobody taught him. That's not loyalty. That's a funeral with extra steps."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight. You could've set it on a scale.

Paloma didn't shout. I almost wish he had. Instead he said, very evenly, "Say his name."

"What?"

"Say the boy's name."

"He doesn't have a name. That's my whole—"

"He has one. He had one before he ever walked out of that blizzard. He just can't tell us in words we know. So until he can, his name is *the boy*, and *the boy* is under this guild's roof, which means — say it with me, Ferro, you know the words, you've known them forty years — *the road remembers who walked it*."

"That's a smuggler's oath, not a—"

"It's the only oath we've got left. The guild's four people and a blind woman, you said. Fine. Then the guild's small enough that every one of us gets a vote, and I'm telling you my vote, and then you can cast yours, and if yours wins, I'll — " he stopped. Jaw working. I'd never seen him stop mid-sentence before. Paloma doesn't stop mid-sentence. Paloma finishes sentences the way avalanches finish slopes. "If yours wins, I'll think on it. That's the most I can promise."

"That's nothing."

"It's everything I've got."

* * *

I went outside after that, because the hut had gotten too small, which is what happens in huts when the truth gets loose in them.

The Greywater valley was white to the treeline. Somewhere down there was the ford, and Tam's grave, and probably a warden patrol or two sniffing along the banks. Somewhere beyond that was Greyhollow, and the queen, and a machine of law and steel that had chewed up kingdoms older than ours and never once gotten a tooth stuck.

And I stood there in the cold thinking: I am not a good man, but I am an honest one, and honest men have to say the ugly thing out loud or the ugly thing wins by default.

Here's the ugly thing. Here's what I couldn't say in front of Paloma, because he'd have looked at me the way he looked at me, with those dark eyes full of a disappointment I've been dodging for forty years like a toll collector.

I'm scared of the boy.

Not of what he is. Of what he might be. Of the songs that make flames bow. Of the way the wardens' captain went white when the kid looked at him. Of the little circle he keeps drawing under the Crownspire, like he's got an appointment there. Of the amber flash in his eyes when he said *they are waking* — I wasn't there for that, but Isolde told me, and Isolde doesn't embellish, Isolde is many terrible things but she is not a liar, and the way she said it made my skin crawl like ants in the bedding.

*They are waking.* Who's they? What's waking? And when it's done waking, is it going to be grateful to the four fools who kept it warm? Or is it going to look at us the way the Crown looks at us — as obstacles, or worse, as furniture?

You can't be loyal to a question. That's what I wanted to tell Paloma. You can be loyal to a man, a guild, a road, a grave. But you can't bleed for a question mark.

Except.

Except I keep coming back to the ford. To Tam. Tam, who was drunk more often than sober and useless more often than drunk, and who, when the wardens came out of the trees, didn't run. Tam, who planted himself on the ford's one narrow stone with a crossbow older than Paloma and yelled — I heard it, I was thirty yards downstream — he yelled, *"Road remembers!"* and loosed until they cut him down.

Tam didn't die for a question. Tam died for a boy he'd known three weeks, a boy who used to sit on his knee and let him sing bawdy songs at him in two languages, neither of which the boy understood, and the boy would laugh anyway, because laughter's the one language that needs no translation.

Maybe that's the answer, then. Maybe that's the whole answer. You don't bleed for what the boy is. You bleed for what he laughed at. You bleed for the knee he sat on.

Gods, I hate it when I argue myself into a corner. It's like losing a wrestling match with your own shadow — you don't even get the dignity of an opponent.

* * *

Isolde found me out there. Came and stood at my shoulder without a word, which is her way. We watched the valley together for a while. She'd brought two cups of something hot that claimed to be tea the way a mule claims to be a horse.

"You're going to vote to give him up," she said.

"I'm going to vote what I vote."

"You're going to vote to give him up, and Paloma's going to look at you like you died, and Maren's going to say something quiet and terrible, and then you're going to lose the vote and hate yourself for casting it." She sipped her terrible tea. "I know the shape of it. I've been standing in that shape for days."

I looked at her. She was watching the treeline, jaw set, eyes red-rimmed. Isolde, who doesn't cry, who once took an arrow in the shoulder and complained about the archer's form.

"Something you want to tell me, Isolde?"

A long pause. Long enough that the wind filled it twice.

"No," she said. "Not yet."

"Not yet."

"There's a *yet* in it. That's all I can give you right now. A yet."

I should have pushed. Forty years of reading people on dark roads, and every instinct I have said *push, Ferro, push now*, because a woman who says *not yet* is a woman carrying a letter with a seal on it. But I was tired, and Tam was dead, and my knees ached, and I told myself the yet could wait until morning.

That's the thing about doubts. They don't just live in you. They spread. I was carrying mine, and Isolde was carrying hers, and I'd bet my good boots Paloma had his own locked up behind that quiet voice of his, and Maren — Maren knew things none of us knew and carried them like stones in a sack.

Four people and a blind woman, and every one of us carrying a separate weight, and not one of us setting it down where the others could see it.

That's how companies die. Not in fords. In silences.

* * *

The vote happened at dusk, by lamplight, because Paloma insisted on lamplight. "The guild votes by lamplight or not at all," he said. "Firelight's for stories. Lamplight's for decisions." Which is the kind of thing that sounds wise until you realize he made it up on the spot, but nobody called him on it, because we needed it to be true.

He laid it out plain. The boy stays, or the boy goes to the nearest warden post with an escort and a white rag on a stick. Majority rules. Maren abstained — she said her eyes had voted enough in her life, which was either humility or the sharpest thing anyone said all night.

Isolde voted with Paloma. She didn't explain it. She just said, "He stays," in a voice like a door closing, and I watched her hands, and her hands were shaking, and I thought: *there's the yet, there it is, walking around in the open.*

Paloma voted, obviously, with a speech I won't repeat because it was good and I'm still angry about how good it was. Something about how the guild was founded to guard something beneath the peaks, and maybe this was that something, and maybe the oath was older than any of us and we'd just been too comfortable to feel it pulling.

Then it was me.

I looked at the boy. He was sitting on Maren's knee, of all places, the blind old woman's arms around him, her chin on his head, and she was humming something — one of his songs, the hearth-song, the one that makes flames bow — except she was humming it wrong, human and off-key, and he was letting her. Correcting nothing. Just leaning into it.

And I thought about Tam yelling *road remembers* with a crossbow bolt in his chest. And I thought about a warden captain calling a child a *vessel*. And I thought about my knees, and my age, and the brandy casks, and the countess in the pickle barrel, and forty years of a road that never once asked me to be good, only quick.

"The boy stays," I said.

Paloma didn't gloat. That's the worst part. He just nodded, once, like I'd paid back a loan instead of reversing a verdict, and said, "Thank you, Ferro," and I had to turn away and pretend to check the lamp oil because my eyes were doing something I don't allow.

But I'll tell you what I said after, because it matters, and because I'm an honest man even when honesty costs.

"I vote he stays. But I'm telling you all now, and I want it in the ledger, Maren, write it down: if it comes to a choice between that boy and one of us — one of *us*, flesh and name and grave — I don't know what I'll do. I'm not promising to be a hero. I'm promising to be here. That's all I've got."

Maren's blind eyes turned toward me, and she smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, gentle as a landslide.

"That's more than the last wardens promised," she said. "And look how long they lasted."

I didn't know what she meant. Not then.

I found out two days later, when Paloma stood at the mouth of a crack in the mountain that wasn't on any chart and said the words I'd been dreading since the tollhouse:

"We're taking the deep road."
