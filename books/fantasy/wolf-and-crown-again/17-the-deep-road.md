## 17. The Deep Road

The crack in the mountain wasn't on any chart, and that was the first thing wrong with it.

Every smuggler worth their salt learns the maps of Thornfell the way priests learn scripture—by heart, by candlelight, by rote. I'd been carrying those passes in my head since I was nine years old, when Ferro hoisted me onto his shoulders so I could memorize the notch of a ridge by feel because drawing it would get us both hanged. There are forty-one ways through Thornfell. I know all of them. The mountain has no secrets from me.

Except this one.

The opening sat in the shadowed flank of a ravine that every chart called a dead end, behind a curtain of ice that looked like any other curtain of ice, except Maren had stood before it for an hour with her palms flat against the stone, reading the rock with her fingers the way other people read letters.

"It's here," she'd said finally. "The old script. It's carved all over the face of it."

"What's it say?"

Her hands kept moving. "It says *the road remembers who walks it.*"

"That's not ominous at all," Isolde said.

So we broke the ice. And now, on the morning of the third day since the ford, with Tam two days dead and his absence sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, I stood at the mouth of a passage older than my guild and prepared to lead four people into the dark on the strength of a blind woman's fingertips.

"Last chance to object," I said. "Ferro?"

"I've objected twice already." He hefted the pack with our remaining food and the lantern oil, which was not nearly enough of either. "You didn't listen either time. Why start now?"

"Isolde?"

She looked back down the ravine, toward the lowlands, where somewhere behind us Vael's wardens were combing the passes with dogs and patience. Her sister's name hung unspoken between us—I hadn't confronted her about the letter yet. I hadn't decided if I could afford to. A company of five is a small thing; cut out one thread and the whole weave comes apart.

"I'm coming," she said. "Obviously."

"Maren?"

"The charts say the tunnel predates the guild." She said it like a woman reporting the weather. "Everything above us is young. Everything below us is true. Go on, Paloma. Ask me the real question."

I hesitated. "How deep does it go?"

"All the way down." She smiled that landslide smile again. "All the way back."

* * *

We went in single file: me first with the lantern, then the boy holding Maren's hand, then Ferro, then Isolde with the rear-guard, which was where she insisted on walking and where I pretended not to understand why—so she wouldn't have to see our faces when she thought about what she'd done.

The first hundred yards were ordinary smuggler's work: natural fissure, widened by tools, drainage cut along the left wall in the old style. I've walked a thousand yards just like it. Then the walls changed.

The stone went smooth. Not chiseled-smooth, not water-smooth—*finished*, the way metal is finished, as though the rock had been poured rather than cut and had hardened into its final shape while someone patient watched it cool. The lantern light slid across it without catching. My boots made almost no sound. Even our breathing seemed quieter than breathing should be, as though the tunnel had opinions about noise and we were wise enough to share them.

"Paloma." Ferro's voice came out flattened, the echo dying too fast. "What guild built this?"

"No guild built this."

"You said—"

"I know what I said." I stopped, held up the lantern, and turned in a slow circle. The light found no seam, no tool mark, no joint where one section met another. The tunnel simply continued, curved and dark and complete, like the inside of something's throat. "I said it predates the guild. I was wrong. It predates the *idea* of the guild. Maren, how far down do these run according to the charter?"

"Farther than anyone's ever needed to go," she said. "And then farther."

The boy tugged Maren's hand. He'd been silent since the ford—silent the way a held breath is silent—and now he pointed forward, down the throat of the mountain, and made a sound I hadn't heard from him before. Not words. Not quite music. A low, pleased hum, like a man greeting a house he grew up in.

"He knows it," Isolde said quietly. She'd come up close without my noticing, which was its own kind of warning about her nerves. "Look at him. He knows this place."

"Then let's hope it knows him back." I started walking again, because standing still in the dark with your doubts is how smugglers die, and because if I stopped moving I'd have to think about Tam, and there wasn't room for that yet. Grief is heavy cargo. You carry it in shifts.

* * *

We descended for hours.

That's the thing nobody tells you about going under a mountain: time stops meaning anything. There's no sun to cheat by, no birds, no change in the wind. Just the lantern, the walls, the small sounds of people trying to be quiet. I counted steps for the first mile and then lost count somewhere past four thousand and never picked it up again, and after that I measured our progress by other things—the temperature dropping until our breath fogged, then rising strangely warm, then dropping again; the air going stale, then sweet, then carrying a smell I couldn't name until I could, and once I named it I wished I hadn't.

Iron and snow.

There's no place under a mountain that smells of snow. But there it was, faint as memory, threading through the iron tang of deep stone, and when I mentioned it to Maren she nodded like I'd confirmed a suspicion.

"We're getting close to something the cold got into a long time ago," she said.

"How long?"

"Longer than the Crown." She walked with her free hand trailing the wall, reading. "Paloma, may I tell you a story? It'll pass the miles."

"Ferro hates stories."

"Ferro can suffer like the rest of us."

"I hate stories that don't end," Ferro said from behind us, which is the closest he's come in twenty years to asking for a story, but Maren took it as granted anyway.

"When I was a girl," she began, "before the fever took my eyes, I could read. Properly, I mean—not just guild cipher but book-hand, court-hand, all of it. And in the tollhouse archive there was a fragment. A page from something older, copied out by some archivist eight hundred years dead who complained in his margins that the original was already crumbling. It described the roads through Thornfell. All forty-one of them." Her hand paused against the wall. "And then it described a forty-second road. One that wasn't for smuggling, or trade, or war. It called it the *witness-road*. It said the road existed so that the mountain could see what was done to it, and remember, and testify."

"Testify to who?" Isolde asked.

"That's the part that crumbled." Maren resumed walking. "But I've thought about that word my whole life. *Witness-road.* Roads go places. So I always asked myself—what's at the end of a road built for witnessing?"

Ahead of us, the boy hummed his low note, and for a moment I swear the tunnel hummed back.

* * *

We camped in a widening where three passages met—or seemed to meet; the geometry down there played tricks, and twice I caught myself certain we were heading uphill when my legs said down. We ate cold. Nobody argued about lighting a fire. The dark down there wasn't empty the way surface dark is empty; it felt occupied, attentive, and even Ferro, who once slept soundly through a border skirmish, kept his axe across his knees and his eyes on the passages.

I took first watch. Of course I did. I always take first watch, partly because I sleep worst and partly because it's the one duty nobody can accuse me of shirking, and a guildmaster who can't be accused of shirking gets to keep his authority a little longer. That night, though, I wasn't watching the dark. I was watching the boy.

He wasn't sleeping either. He sat beside Maren with her arm around him, and he was looking down the main passage—the way we'd come, or the way we were going; down there I genuinely couldn't say—with an expression I couldn't parse. Not fear. Longing, maybe. The look you'd give a house you were dragged away from.

I crossed to them, quiet as my name.

"Can't sleep?" I whispered.

Maren shook her head slightly. "He's listening."

"To what?"

"I don't know. But whatever it is, it's getting closer, and it isn't frightening him, and that frightens me." She pulled him closer. "Ask him something. Anything. It helps, sometimes, when he answers."

I crouched. The boy turned those dark eyes on me—no amber in them tonight, thank all the mercies—and waited.

"What's down there?" I asked. "At the end of the road?"

He considered me for a long moment. Then he reached out and touched my chest, right over the heart, with two small fingers, and pressed gently, the way you'd press on a bruise to check whether it still hurt.

Then he pointed down the passage.

"That's not an answer," I said. "That's a bill."

Maren laughed softly. "Children don't lie, Paloma. That's the trouble with them."

* * *

Somewhere in the second day underground—we were guessing at days by now, keeping a tally scratched into a lantern handle—the walls started talking.

Not talking. Carving. The smooth finished stone gave way, gradually, to surfaces worked with images, and the images told a story in a grammar none of us could read but all of us could follow. Wolves first: wolves running, wolves standing, wolves crowned, rendered with such economy that a single curving line became a spine, a shoulder, a king. Then men among the wolves—not fighting them, walking with them, small figures beside great ones, and I understood suddenly, with a lurch, that I was looking at the founding image of my own guild. Road-wardens. Oath-takers. Us, a thousand years before us.

Then the story turned.

The carvings showed fire. Showed men with the broken-crown sigil—the Crown's oldest mark, the one on every warden's warrant, the one stamped on the pardon papers that bought Tam's silence and Isolde's letter. The little men with the broken crown moved through the carvings like a stain, and everywhere they passed, the wolves lay down. Not fought. Lay down. And the small figures without crowns—the wardens, my guild's grandmothers and grandfathers—stood in lines between the wolves and the broken-crown men, holding pikes.

Isolde stopped before one panel and didn't move for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was stripped bare of all its edges.

"They're guarding them," she said. "In the carving. The wardens are standing between."

"Yes," Maren said.

"And the ones with the broken crown—" She stopped. Started again. "Paloma. Whose side does the charter say we were on?"

I thought about lying to her. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger heaven keeps on Lior Quintana: I thought about it, and I weighed it, and I chose the truth, mostly because she'd already chosen it once herself and paid for it with a letter.

"The charter says we guard what's beneath," I said. "It doesn't say from whom. I think—" I touched the carved line of wardens, my predecessors, standing in their row. "I think it says from everyone. Including whoever holds the Crown. Including us, if it came to it."

"So we're not running from Vael down here," Ferro rumbled. "We're running *into* the thing he's afraid of."

"We're taking the only road he doesn't have a map for," I said. "Same as ever. The politics are new. The craft isn't."

He grunted, which from Ferro is a standing ovation.

The boy, meanwhile, had gone to the last panel and put both hands against it. This one was different—older, maybe, or more important. It showed a door. A great sealed door in a mountainside, with figures sleeping behind it, drawn as shapes within shapes, and above the door a line of script that repeated around the whole panel like a border.

Maren's fingers found it and read it aloud, syllable by careful syllable, translating in the halting way she did with the old tongue.

"*Sleep is not death,*" she recited. "*Sleep is custody. Wake us not for vengeance. Wake us for witness.*"

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The lantern burned. Somewhere far off—farther than sound should travel, deeper than we'd yet gone—something answered the silence with a note too low to hear properly, felt in the teeth rather than the ears.

The boy hummed it back, perfectly.

* * *

On what we decided was the third night, the passage narrowed, and my claustrophobia—which I have denied having my entire adult life, in the same breath I deny being superstitious—came home to roost.

It wasn't a proper squeeze; we could still walk upright, mostly. But the walls closed to shoulder-width, the ceiling dipped until Ferro had to stoop, and the air thickened until each breath felt borrowed rather than owned. And the stone pressed in with that awful finished smoothness, close enough to touch on both sides at once, so that walking became a kind of being-held, and every instinct I own screamed that being held by a mountain is the same as being swallowed.

I kept talking. That's my trick for it—talk, and the fear can't get a word in edgewise. I told them about the first route I ever ran alone, age fourteen, carrying contraband tea through the Wren's Tooth pass in a false-bottomed milk churn, and how I'd sold the tea, drunk the profits, and lied to Ferro about it, and how he'd known, because he always knew, and how instead of beating me he'd made me carry the next churn *with him* and drink nothing for six days.

"You were a little bastard," Ferro agreed fondly, from behind me, his voice squeezed thin by the stone.

"I was an *artist*."

"You were a thief with good posture."

Behind him, I heard Isolde laugh—a short, cracked thing, half sob—but a laugh. First one since the ford. I clung to the sound in the dark like a handhold.

The narrow lasted perhaps half a mile. When it opened, it opened all at once, into a gallery so tall the lantern light died before it found the roof, and so wide we could only see one wall at a time. Pillars rose out of the dark, dozens of them, carved from the living rock in the shape of wolves seated on their haunches, their heads tilted, their ears pricked, all of them facing the same direction: down.

Toward the end of the road.

We stood in the entrance and let the mountain look at us. That's what it was like. Being looked at. Forty generations of smugglers have walked the surface passes of Thornfell believing the mountain indifferent, and every one of them was wrong, and I was the first in a thousand years to stand inside its attention and feel it.

"Well," said Isolde, into the silence. "This is either the most important thing we'll ever do, or the stupidest."

"Those aren't different categories," Ferro said. "Not with him leading."

The boy stepped forward. Past me. Past the pillars. He walked out into that vast dark gallery with the certainty of someone crossing their own doorstep, and he stopped in the center, and he raised his arms slightly from his sides, and he sang.

One note. Low, and long, and older than kingdoms. It went out into the dark the way a stone goes into a well, falling and falling, and the dark took it, and gave something back.

From far ahead—from the end of the witness-road, from whatever door stood waiting there—came a grinding, groaning answer. Stone moving against stone. Something old shifting in its sleep, or turning over to look.

Or beginning to wake.

Maren's hand found my sleeve in the dark. Her blind eyes were wet.

"Steady, Paloma," she whispered. "Whatever opens—it was opened for him. Try to make sure it doesn't close on the rest of us."

I lit the second lantern. I checked the oil. I did the arithmetic of a man about to spend everything he has, and I found, to my complete lack of surprise, that it didn't balance, and that I was going forward anyway.

"After me," I said. "All of you. Stay close, stay quiet, and whatever you see down there—"

"Paloma," Ferro said.

"—whatever you *see*—"

"Paloma." His voice had changed. I turned.

At the far end of the gallery, where the pillars converged and the dark pooled deepest, a thin line of grey light had appeared. Not lantern-light. Not torchlight. Cold, clean, ancient light, seeping up from beneath a door that had just begun to open—the color of snow at dawn, the color of the sky through ice.

The boy was already walking toward it.

"Stay close," I said again, uselessly, to everyone, and followed the child down the last mile of a road a thousand years wide.
