## 19. Isolde Confesses

There is a particular kind of silence that follows you through a tunnel like a debt collector. I had been carrying mine for six days by the time we reached the wardens' bones, and it had grown teeth.

I should explain. My name is Isolde Vane, and I am—was—the guild's lockwoman, which means I was paid to open things that other people wanted kept shut. Doors. Strongboxes. Sealed letters. I was very good at my work. So good, in fact, that when Inquisitor Vane—no relation, though the irony has not escaped me—came to me in a border town inn with a letter bearing my sister Wren's handwriting and her prison seal, I did what I do best.

I opened something that should have stayed shut.

But let me back up, because Paloma will want the whole confession on the record, and Paloma keeps records the way other men keep grudges: carefully, and forever.

* * *

It started with the letter. Of course it did. It always starts with something small—a scrap of paper, a name spoken too loudly—and ends with bones in a tunnel.

Wren and I were born eleven months apart in a coal town called Ashmoor, where the air tasted like pennies and the only two futures available to girls were the pits and the pillow houses. We chose a third option: we ran. I went up into the mountains and learned to pick locks. She went down to Greyhollow and learned to forge seals. Between us we could open any door in the province, and for ten years we did, cheerfully, profitably, until the queen's new magistrates decided that forgery was treason and forgery *for smugglers* was treason twice over.

They took her in autumn. I found out in early winter, when a boy with a runner's legs and no memory of faces delivered a note to my dead-drop behind the tannery in Millbrook.

*I'm alive. They're feeding me. The man with the grey eyes says it depends on you.*

That was all. No signature. But I knew the hand—I'd taught it to her myself, the slant of the W, the little hook on the r. And I knew the grey eyes. Everyone in the passes was learning about the grey eyes that winter. Inquisitor Corvus Vael, the queen's hound, the man who had burned three safehouses in Thornfell and left the ash neatly swept.

The trap was so obvious it was almost insulting. Go to the shrine at Coldwell Cross, alone, or your sister stops being fed. Any guild member worth their salt would have smelled it from a mile off and walked away, grieving but alive, because that is the arithmetic we're raised on. You don't trade one life for five. Ferro would have said it flat out: *she's gone, girl, and feeling bad about it won't un-hang her.*

But here is the thing nobody tells you about arithmetic: it doesn't work when the number on the other side of the scale is someone who used to sleep curled against your back because our childhood bed was too narrow for two, someone whose laugh you can still hear, someone who once bit a magistrate's horse to give you time to run.

So I went to Coldwell Cross. Alone. And I sat across from Vael in a freezing chapel while he poured wine neither of us drank, and he made me an offer so clean it hurt.

Not gold. That's what I need everyone to understand—not Paloma most of all. He offered me nothing but a piece of paper and a truth. The paper was a pardon under the queen's seal for one Isolde Vane and one Wren Vane, full remission, signed and waiting. The truth was this: "Your guild is already dead, Mistress Vane. It simply hasn't lain down yet. I don't want the child harmed—I want him *found*. Tell me where they'll go when they run, and both of you walk out of this winter breathing."

He even told me about Tam. Not asked—told. "The old man talks when he drinks. I expect I won't need you long." Which meant he was running two lines to us, and I was the backup, and somehow that was the part that made me sick rather than the part that made me complicit. Being redundant in your own betrayal. There's a humiliation in that I still haven't found language for.

I gave him the Hollow Chapel route. Not the deep road—I swear on whatever's left of my soul, I didn't know about the deep road then, none of us did—but the surface route, the shrine, the ford at Marrow Water. Everything a hunter needed to set his net.

And then I went back to the company and ate supper with them and laughed at Tam's jokes and watched Paloma teach the boy card games the boy couldn't understand and didn't need to, because he cheated anyway, some instinct older than rules—and every hour of it was like swallowing glass.

* * *

Tam died at the ford.

I keep trying to build a wall between those two facts—my letter, Tam's body floating in the shallows with his coat full of stones the wardens put there out of some grim professional courtesy—but the wall won't hold. Vael came to the ford because I told him where the ford was. Tam died buying time against men who knew exactly where we'd be. Therefore, by any chain of custody that matters, Tam's blood is on my hands, and no amount of telling myself that Vael would have found us anyway—that he had Tam, that he had half the underworld of three provinces singing for silver—will wash it.

Paloma suspected immediately. Of course he did. Smugglers suspect betrayal the way farmers suspect rain; it's not pessimism, it's weather-sense. I watched him count us at the ford—Ferro bleeding, Maren holding the boy, me with wet boots and dry eyes—and I saw the arithmetic move behind his face. Someone sold us. Someone here.

He didn't say anything. That was worse than if he'd accused me. He just started watching, the way he watches a pass before committing the caravan to it, patient and total, and I spent four days in that gaze like a moth in a jar.

Which brings us to the tunnel. The deep road. Maren's charts, the ones older than the guild, older than anything, and the descent that went down and down until the torchlight turned the color of old honey and the stone around us stopped looking like something dug and started looking like something *built*. Ferro grumbling. Maren going quiet in that way she does when her fingers are reading more than her eyes ever could. The boy humming—always humming now, songs in that dead language that made my fillings ache.

And then the formation.

You've heard the others describe it by now, I imagine, in their various ways—Ferro probably grunted, Paloma probably made it poetry. Let me tell you how it landed on me, the lockwoman, the woman who opens things.

A thousand years ago, men and women stood shoulder to shoulder in this tunnel, facing that sealed door, pikes in their hands, and they never left. They starved or froze or bled standing up, and nobody ever moved them, and the mountain kept them the way amber keeps flies. The first guild. Our guild, if you squinted—road-wardens, Maren said, sworn to guard what slept below. Sworn to keep it *shut*, maybe. Or to keep it *safe*. The charter wasn't clear, and Maren read it three times with her fingertips and got quieter each time.

I looked at those dead wardens and thought: they kept their oath for a thousand years without anyone holding their sisters hostage. What's my excuse?

I broke in a tunnel lit by honey-colored light, surrounded by the honored dead, somewhere underneath everything I'd ever believed about my own cleverness. And Paloma, being Paloma, noticed.

"Isolde." His voice in the deep place didn't echo so much as settle, like snowfall. "Walk with me."

* * *

We walked ahead of the others, past the formation, until the dead were shadows behind us and the sealed door was a rumor ahead, and there in the dark he waited. Paloma Blackwood is not a patient man, whatever he thinks—he's a patient *performer*—but he waited now, torch held high, and let the silence do his asking for him.

"You know," I said finally. Because what else is there? Once you're standing in front of a millennium of corpses who kept faith better than you did, prevarication feels obscene. "About the ford."

"I know someone wrote a letter." He said it evenly. "Vael showed me a copy after. He likes showing people things. It's how he kills them—one revelation at a time."

"Wren." My voice cracked on her name like ice on a boot heel. "My sister. He has her in Greyhollow, and he had a pardon written for both of us, and he—" I made myself look at him. Made myself stand in it. "I gave him the chapel route. I gave him Marrow Water. Tam is dead because I opened the door, Paloma. I'm the lockwoman. Opening doors is what I do. Even when they should stay shut."

The torch hissed. Somewhere behind us, faint as breath, the boy was humming.

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Did it work?"

"What?"

"The pardon. Did he hand it over? You and Wren, walking free?" His tone wasn't cruel. That was the terrible thing—it was genuinely curious, the way he'd ask whether a lock had a false bottom.

"No. He said he'd deliver it after. After the child." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "After we handed him a mute boy to whatever happens to stolen vessels in Crown custody. That was the price. Not the letter first. *After.*"

"And you took it anyway."

"I took it anyway."

He nodded slowly, and I braced for the verdict—for exile, for a knife in the dark, for the cold arithmetic Ferro would have applied without blinking. Instead Paloma lowered the torch a little and said, "Do you know why I never accused you?"

"Because you weren't sure."

"Because I was sure," he said, "and it didn't matter. Listen to me, Isolde. Vael didn't buy you with gold. He bought you with the one coin we can't mint—your sister's heartbeat. Every person in this company has a price like that. Mine is this boy now, gods help me. Ferro's is the guild. Maren's is the truth. Tam's was drink and an audience. Vael knows it. It's the whole art of inquisition—you don't break people, you find the thing they can't watch break, and you hold it over the fire." He paused. "What matters isn't that you opened the door. It's what you do standing in the doorway."

"That's very generous," I managed, "for a man whose oldest friend is floating in Marrow Water."

"It's not generous." His jaw tightened, and I saw the grief come up in him like groundwater—Tam, dear stupid brave Tam, who had once carried Paloma four miles through a fever with broken ribs. "It's the furthest thing from generous. I want to hate you. I've been trying to hate you since the ford and it won't take, because I keep picturing Wren, whoever she is to you, and knowing I'd have done the same. That's the worst part, Isolde. Not forgiving you. Recognizing you."

I cried then. Ugly, silent, tunnel-dark crying, the kind you learn to do so the sentries don't hear. He let me. When it was spent, he said:

"When we're out of this—whatever 'out of this' turns out to mean—we get her back. You and me. We pull Wren Vane out of Greyhollow if I have to smuggle her out inside a wine barrel, and then you and I are square, and not one minute before. Do you understand? Forgiveness isn't mine to give you for free. Earn it. Help us finish this."

"And if finishing this gets us all killed?"

"Then we'll be in excellent company." He tilted the torch toward the formation behind us, toward the thousand-year dead who had kept their post. "Look at them. They guarded a door for something they never even explained to their children. We know what's down here. We know why. That makes us luckier than they ever were."

It was not forgiveness. I want to be precise about that, because precision is my trade. It was a contract. Terms stated, consideration named, payment deferred. Paloma Blackwood forgave me the way he ran contraband: with a manifest, a deadline, and no trust extended beyond the next milestone.

I have never respected him more.

* * *

When we returned to the others, Maren was standing at the sealed door with both palms flat against the stone, and the boy was beside her, small hand pressed to the rock beneath hers, and he was singing.

Not humming anymore. Singing—full-throated, wordless in our tongue but worded in his, in that language older than kingdoms, and the door—

Gods help me, the door answered.

Deep under the mountain, under our feet, under the bones of the wardens, something vast ground against something vaster, a sound like a millstone the size of a cathedral turning for the first time in a thousand years, and dust fell from the ceiling in golden threads through the torchlight, and Ferro swore in three dialects, and Maren whispered, "It remembers the song. It *remembers*."

I looked at Paloma. He looked at me. Whatever was on my face—terror, wonder, guilt, all of it braided together—he must have recognized it, because he said quietly:

"Whatever's on the other side of that door, Isolde—no more letters. No more doors opened for anyone. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said. "From here on, I only open things for us."

The seam of the great door split with a crack like winter ice giving way, and cold air breathed out of the darkness beyond—air that smelled, impossibly, of snow and iron, of a winter that had been waiting a thousand years to be exhaled.

The boy stopped singing.

In the sudden silence, from far down in the dark, we all heard it: footsteps.

Or something like footsteps.

Something waking.
