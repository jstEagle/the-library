## 21. What Sleeps Below

The dark on the other side of the door was not the same dark we'd come through.

I've spent my whole life in the under-places. Smugglers learn darkness the way priests learn scripture — every grade of it, from cellar-black to the deep blind of a tunnel that hasn't seen torchlight in three generations. This was none of those. This was *old* dark, patient dark, the kind that had been waiting so long it had forgotten what it was waiting for.

The cold came out to meet us first. Not mountain-cold, not the damp chill of stone and seep-water, but something drier. Cleaner. It smelled of snow that had never fallen on anything, and iron that had never rusted, and beneath both, faint as a memory of a memory, something like juniper smoke.

"Well," said Ferro behind me, "that's not foreboding at all."

"Shut up," Isolde whispered, but there was no edge in it. Her hand had found my sleeve without her seeming to notice she'd done it.

The boy walked past all of us.

That's the thing I keep coming back to, even now, even after everything. We stood at that threshold like sensible people, hearts hammering, torches guttering in air that moved with no wind to move it — and the child simply went forward, small bare feet silent on stone that should have been freezing him to the bone, the way you'd walk into your own kitchen. Like he'd been coming down this road his whole life and we were merely the escort he'd been saddled with.

"The charts say the vault's beyond the second gallery," Maren said. She couldn't see any of this — her fingers read the wall instead, trailing over carvings so old they'd been worn to suggestions. But her voice was steady, the way it always is when her hands are busy. Blind Maren, calmest of us all, because the dark had never been able to frighten her out of anything. "Paloma. The charter says the wardens were sworn to the kings *in their sleeping*. Not dead. Sleeping."

"I heard you the first time."

"And you didn't believe me."

"No," I admitted, and stepped through. "But I'm here anyway. That's the whole trade, isn't it? Doing the job before you're sure."

* * *

The gallery opened around us like the inside of a held breath.

My torch found walls first — smooth, fitted stone, no mortar, blocks the size of carts set so precisely you couldn't have slid a knife between them. The smuggler in me did the arithmetic before the rest of me could stop it: no mortar means no quarry within a hundred leagues could cut them; no cart-road runs to Thornfell that could carry them; therefore either the mountains were younger than I'd been taught, or the men who built this place had ways the Crown's masons would sell their teeth for.

Then the torchlight climbed higher, and I stopped doing sums.

Wolves.

Carved wolves, ranks of them, running along the walls in friezes that wrapped the whole gallery — but they weren't hunting, and nothing hunted them. They ran beside men. Shoulder to shoulder. And the men carried no weapons I could see, only staves tipped with something that caught the light like a lantern catches dawn. Above them, carved across the vault of the ceiling in a ring so wide I had to turn in a full circle to follow it, ran a single line of script in that old Thornic lettering Maren had gone grey over. The boy looked up at it and hummed — two notes, low, almost bored — and I swear on every ledger I've ever cooked, the sound came back off the ceiling a half-beat later, like the room was answering him.

"Don't do that," Ferro told the boy, very seriously.

The boy looked at him and did it again.

* * *

The second gallery narrowed, then opened, and that's where we found the first of them.

I want to tell this part properly, because everyone who wasn't there gets it wrong, and because I got it wrong myself for the space of about ten heartbeats. Ten heartbeats in which I thought: *the guild charter lied, Maren's charts lie, and these are corpses, and we are standing in the biggest tomb on the continent, and the thing Vael wants is a grave-robbery away.*

They stood in niches cut into the walls, dozens of them, rank upon rank receding past the reach of my torchlight. Tall figures. Armored figures — no, not armored, I corrected myself as the light steadied. Dressed. Long robes worked with patterns I recognized with a jolt: the same running-wolf borders from the friezes above, stitched in thread that still held color the way fresh blood holds color. Each figure stood upright, hands folded, face lifted slightly as if listening for something just out of earshot.

And every one of them was sheathed in ice-glass. Not ice — I know ice, I've slept under it half my life. This was clearer, harder, with a depth to it that made my eye slide off whenever I tried to look straight through. You could see them perfectly and not see *into* them at all, both at once.

"Paloma." Maren's voice had gone thin. Her hand was pressed flat against one of the niches' edges. "There's an inscription. It's the oath. It's the *oath*, Paloma, word for word, the founding words of our charter — 'we keep the road while the kings keep the dream.'"

Nobody said anything for a while. Tam would have said something. That was the trouble with Tam; there was always something. The silence where he should have been had its own weight, and we all stood in it.

Ferro reached out — slowly, the way you'd reach toward a strange dog — and touched the glass over one figure's folded hands.

"Warm," he said. His voice came out strange. "It's warm, Paloma. Like skin under a blanket."

"They're alive," Isolde breathed. She'd gone pale as tallow. "All of them. All this time. Under the mountain, all this—"

"How many?" Maren asked, and her fingers kept moving along the wall, counting by touch, faster and faster, and then slower, and then they stopped. "I lost count past two hundred. There may be more galleries."

Two hundred sleepers. Two hundred kings and queens and whatever else they'd been, standing their watch in glass coffins of warm ice, while a thousand years of history piled up on the rock overhead. While kingdoms rose and wrote themselves deeds to everything. While a guild of smugglers forgot what it had originally been smuggling — which, apparently, was nothing. Which had apparently always been *nothing*, because the cargo was never goods at all. The cargo was the secret itself. We'd been carrying an empty box down the generations, guarding a door most of us didn't believe in, and the box turned out to be full.

Full of people.

"Not monsters," I said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice. "Everyone always says monsters under the mountain. It's not monsters. It's *them*. It's the ones they took everything from."

The boy had stopped in front of one niche in particular. He stood on his toes to look up at the sleeper there — a woman, tall even standing, silver-haired, her face composed in that listening expression — and he pressed one small palm flat against the glass, the way you'd touch a fevered forehead.

And the flame of my torch bent toward him. Just slightly. Just enough.

"All right," I said, and my voice only shook a little, which I counted a professional triumph. "All right. Forward. Slowly. Nobody touches anything else, nobody sings, nobody—"

"Wasn't me," said Ferro.

"—nobody does *anything* except walk."

* * *

The heart of the vault was a round chamber, and it was smaller than I expected. I don't know what I'd pictured — a hall big enough to hold two hundred kings, some cathedral of the deep places. Instead: a modest room, intimate almost, lit by veins of that same ice-glass running through the walls so the whole space glowed with a light like moonrise underwater. No dust. After a thousand years, not one grain of dust. The air tasted like the moment after snowfall.

In the center stood a table. One slab of black stone on a single pillar, plain as a butcher's block, and I laughed — actually laughed, one sharp ugly bark of a laugh — because after everything, after the tunnels and the ambush and Tam dying in the ford shallows with warden arrows in him, after the door that sang, after two hundred sleeping kings lining the halls like lanterns —

On the table lay a crown.

Not the crown. Not at first glance. At first glance it was a battered circlet of dark metal, unlovely, the kind of thing a peddler might sell you as "genuine royal, sir, recovered from the wars." It was broken. One side swept up in a curve like a wolf's ear, fine workmanship under the tarnish, and the other side ended in a jagged stump where something had been torn away. Torn, not worn. Snapped off with force.

"That's it," Isolde whispered. "That's the crown from the murals. In the chapel. The broken one — the men were holding the broken—"

She stopped. I watched her get there. I watched Ferro get there a breath behind her, and Maren a breath behind him, and I felt it arrive in my own chest like a swallowed stone.

The murals in the hollow chapel showed men holding a broken crown over slaughtered wolves. We'd read it one way: the crown broke in the killing, the symbol of a fallen house. Every history book in every schoolroom in the kingdom tells it that way. The Wolf-Kings fell, their crown shattered, the victorious line took up rulership of a chastened land.

But crowns don't break in battle. Crowns are made to be broken *on purpose* when a dynasty ends — snapped ceremonially at the last king's funeral, the pieces buried separately so no pretender can reunite them. Any herald knows it. Any *smuggler* who's moved stolen regalia knows it better.

So if this crown was broken deliberately, a thousand years ago, in a ritual meant to end a line forever—

Then why was it whole enough to be lying here? Why had someone gathered the pieces? And why did the jagged stump fit, exactly, precisely, with the shape my own eyes had measured on the head of every coin the royal mint had struck for the last century?

Because it wasn't broken at all.

It was *halved*. Half a crown, and somewhere south of here, in a palace, on the head of a nineteen-year-old girl who had been taught since birth that her authority flowed down unbroken from the founders of her house — somewhere south, the other half sat. Soldered onto new metal. Reinvented. Worn.

"They didn't destroy it," I heard myself say. "When the Wolf-Kings fell. They couldn't destroy it — maybe it can't be destroyed, maybe it wouldn't burn — so they broke it and hid the true half here, under guard, and took the broken half south and *built a crown around it.*" My voice was climbing and I let it. "Every coronation. Every edict signed with the royal seal. Every gallows they've hung smugglers from and every toll they've levied and every village they've burned for harboring the old blood — all of it, all of it justified by a crown that's *half theirs and half stolen,* and the stolen half is the half that matters, the half that—"

"Paloma." Maren's voice, quiet. "Breathe."

I breathed. The boy tugged my coat. I looked down. He pointed at the crown, then at himself, then made a gesture I'd learned on the road — the one he used for *sleeping*, palm against his tilted cheek.

"Yes," I said. "They're asleep. And that's theirs."

He shook his head. Pointed at the crown again. Then at his own chest, over his heart. Then he frowned, frustrated, little fists clenching, and finally he did the thing he does when common gestures fail him: he sang. Three notes this time, soft, and the ice-glass veins in the walls brightened with each note like coals breathed upon, and in the round chamber's glow I watched the shadows of the sleepers in the outer galleries shift — just shift, nothing more — like sleepers turning over in bed.

"He's the key," Ferro said flatly. "That's what Vael meant. Stolen vessel. He's not carrying treasure, he's carrying *permission.*"

"He's a child," Isolde snapped.

"He's both," Maren said softly. "Most terrible things are. So are most holy ones."

I picked up the crown.

I want it recorded — by whoever keeps the ledgers of this new age we're stumbling into — that I hesitated. That I knew every rule of my trade, and the first rule is *you don't touch the merchandise*, and that I picked it up anyway, because some things you have to hold before you can believe them, and because if the choice was going to be mine, I wanted it made with my own hands and not handed to me by fear.

The metal was warm. Of course it was. Everything down here was warm. Warm like a living thing, warm like the glass over two hundred sleepers, warm like the small hand of a boy who'd walked into my life out of a blizzard and rearranged the entire architecture of the world.

Half a crown, warm as a heartbeat, light as a bird's bones.

"So," Ferro said, into the ringing quiet. "Now what?"

And that was when the light in the walls changed.

Not dimmed — *shifted*, rippling outward from the round chamber through the galleries in a slow wave, and beneath my feet I felt it rather than heard it: a pulse, deep in the stone, slow and vast, like something enormous turning over in a sleep that had lasted ten centuries.

From the outer gallery, faint and clear, came a sound I will hear until I die.

A breath. One long, unhurried breath, drawn through ice-glass, by lungs that had waited a thousand years for permission.

Then another. Further down the ranks. Then another.

The boy looked up at me, amber-eyed in the under-light, and smiled — and it was not entirely a child's smile — and said, in that voice like a bell rung underwater:

"*She is nearest.*"

Behind us, down the long gallery of sleeping kings, the first pane of ice-glass began, softly, to crack.
