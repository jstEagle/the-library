## 24. Battle in the Dark

**Paloma**

I have been in fights before. Smuggler's fights, which is to say short, ugly affairs conducted in doorways and ditches by men who mostly wanted to go home. I had never been in a battle. I want that distinction on the record, because what happened in the deep road beneath Thornfell was not a fight. It was a battle, and battles are just fights with the exits removed.

The torchlight did strange things down there. We stood among the Bones of the Wardens — a thousand years of guild-fathers dead in formation, pikes still in their fists, guarding a door no one had ever asked them to stop guarding — and when Vael's wardens came down the tunnel with their lanterns, the light caught all those ancient faces at once, and half of Ferro's crossbowmen froze solid.

"Can't shoot the dead," Tam would have said. Tam was two days dead himself now, at the ford, buying us the time we were spending so carelessly.

"They're not shooting at you," I told my people. "They're shooting at *us*. The dead are on our side tonight."

Isolde snorted. Even now, even here, she found a way to make it sound like an insult. "First time in guild history," she said, and loosed her first bolt.

* * *

**Vael**

Inquisitor Corvus Vael had served the Crown for nineteen years, and in all that time he had never once doubted that he was the right hand of order. He thought in ledgers. Every crime was a debt; every punishment, a payment. It was a clean way to live.

The clean way to live ended somewhere around the moment he saw the dead wardens.

His torches — forty men, each carrying flame into a dark older than the kingdom they served — swept across ranks of corpses standing at attention, and his soldiers began to pray aloud, which was against regulations, and he found he could not bring himself to enforce the regulation.

"Forward," he said instead, because a man who has only one tool uses it even when it is wrong. "The child. The woman archivist. The smugglers. Take them alive."

He meant it, too. That was the thing Paloma Blackwood had never understood about him, would perhaps never understand: Vael did not want a massacre. He wanted custody. He wanted the vessel of the old blood locked in a white room where learned men could study it and the realm could sleep soundly, and if the boy died gently in that room twenty years from now, well — the ledger balanced.

What Vael wanted less than anything, though he would not have admitted it under torture, was for the door behind them to be open. It was open. Cold air came out of it like a held breath finally released, and it smelled of snow and iron and something else, something like a forest that had died before his grandmother's grandmother was born.

"Blackwood!" he called into the dark. "Last offer stands! Amnesty. All of you. Even the traitor woman. Give me the child!"

From somewhere among the tombs, a voice answered him — dry, tired, almost friendly.

"You keep offering me things I don't want, Corvus. It's starting to feel like courtship."

* * *

**Paloma**

Then it was noise. I won't pretend to give you tactics, because there weren't any. There was torchlight jerking across stone, there was the flat crack of Ferro's crossbows, there was Isolde moving through the smoke like a rumor with a knife, and there was me doing the one thing I am genuinely good at, which is knowing where the walls are.

That sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. In the deep road there are alcoves cut into the rock every thirty paces — old smuggler's niches, cut by hands that predate the guild, or maybe *were* the guild — and I had spent our descent memorizing every one of them. So while Vael's wardens advanced in their neat regulation line, my people kept dissolving sideways into the stone and reappearing behind them, and a neat regulation line in a tunnel full of ambush niches is not a line for very long.

"Ferro! Left niche, three down!" A bolt sang out of the dark and a warden went down screaming, alive but done. "Isolde, they're wheeling left — let them wheel, there's a drop behind them." And there was, an old ore-chute, and two of Vael's men discovered it the hard way, with yells that got shorter and shorter until they stopped.

We were winning. That's the part I can hardly write. Forty trained wardens against six smugglers, an old blind woman, and a mute child, and we were *winning*, because the mountain itself was on our side, because every stone in this place had been laid by people who fought the Crown and lost and had apparently decided, after a millennium, to have another go.

Which is, of course, exactly when the gods started laughing.

Because Vael was not stupid. Stupid men lose cleanly. Vael looked at his dying line, and he did the arithmetic, and he sent ten men not at us at all — but straight up the middle, at the small huddled figure by the vault door, where Maren stood.

* * *

**Maren**

Maren Ovesdotter had been blind for fifty years, and in that time she had learned every trick the darkness offered. She heard the ten men coming the way other people hear their own names called: the change in the echo as the tunnel narrowed, the wet click of boot leather, the little collective breath soldiers take before they do something unforgivable.

She also heard the boy breathing beside her. Quick. Frightened. His small hand had found hers without either of them deciding it, and his fingers were cold as the vault air, and he was humming — that same fragment of Thornic melody he always hummed when the world pressed too close, the one that made hearth-flames bow.

"No," she told him softly, in common speech, because some things must be said plainly. "You stay behind me, love. This is guild work."

She had spent sixty years in the guild's service and had never once drawn blood. She knew the charter by heart — the true charter, the one that named them road-wardens of the Wolf-Kings, sworn to guard what slept below — and she had always assumed her part in the oath would end the way archivists' parts end: with the ledger closed and someone else carrying it forward.

The first soldier reached her and grabbed her shoulder, meaning to shove the old woman aside.

Maren drove Maren's own letter-opener — nine inches of good Thornfell steel she had carried for four decades and used exclusively on correspondence — up under his jaw.

The second soldier hesitated, and hesitation killed him, because Isolde came out of the smoke like winter itself.

But the third and fourth came together, and Maren was old, and old women are slow, and she understood with perfect clarity — the clarity she usually reserved for corrupted manuscripts — that she could dodge, or she could stand.

Behind her, the boy was singing louder now. The vault door drank the sound. Somewhere in the dark behind that door, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the ice-glass tombs rang like struck bells.

So Maren stood. She spread her arms the way she'd once seen a mother hen do in the tollhouse yard, absurdly, magnificently, and she said, in pre-kingdom Thornic, the words of the founding oath — *"None pass who mean the kings harm"* — and the pike took her through the body and out again, and she did not fall so much as fold, slowly, deliberately, into the boy's arms, arranging herself so that her bulk shielded him a final time.

"Ledger's yours now," she said, in common speech, so there'd be no mistake. "Mind the entries."

* * *

**Paloma**

I heard her say it. That's the worst of it — I wasn't fifty feet away, I was close enough to see the torchlight on her face, and she didn't look afraid, she looked *annoyed*, the way she looked at a misfiled scroll, and then she folded down around the boy like a cloak, and something in my chest tore along a seam I hadn't known was there.

I have watched men die at the ford. I have watched Tam die laughing, bless his drunken heart. But watching Maren die quiet, on purpose, choosing it the way she chose which books to save from a fire — that broke something in me that I suspect will stay broken, and I find I don't entirely want it mended.

What I did next was not brave and not clever. I screamed, is what I did, a raw ugly sound with no words in it, and I came off my ledge at the four nearest wardens with a smuggler's knife and sixty years of grudges, and Ferro later swore I took down two of them alone, which is a lie, but it's a kind lie and I've decided to keep it.

The fight curdled after that. Chaos is a soft word for it. Torchlight everywhere, shadows lunging, Isolde bleeding from the scalp and grinning about it, Ferro roaring orders nobody followed, wardens and smugglers tangled together among the standing dead so that killing anyone required first checking whether they were already a thousand years deceased. One young warden threw down his sword entirely, sat down against a tomb, and put his hands over his ears, and nobody — not Vael, not Ferro, not me — touched him. Some part of all of us understood that the boy's song had gotten into him, that the vault was singing back now, low and vast, and that a man can only stand so much history at once.

Vael himself fought toward the door. Of course he did. I saw him carve through the melee with that terrible economical competence of his, eyes fixed past all of us on the threshold, on the cold breath of the deep place, on the shape of the kneeling old woman and the small figure she'd died around.

And I saw the boy lift his head over Maren's body.

* * *

**Isolde**

Isolde Vane had betrayed these people. She needed everyone to remember that, especially herself, right now, with blood in her eye and a warden's sword coming apart under her knife. She had sold them to Vael for her sister's life, and she had told herself the ledger balanced, and then Maren Ovesdotter — who had known about the betrayal, Isolde was suddenly certain, had known and said nothing and taught her Thornic declensions anyway — folded up around a child and died saying *the ledger's yours.*

There was no balancing that entry. Isolde understood that with a clarity that felt almost holy. You didn't pay it back. You paid it *forward*, forever, and the account stayed open until you died, and maybe not even then.

So she stopped fighting like a woman protecting an exit and started fighting like a woman defending a grave, which is a different animal entirely, ask any soldier who's ever made the mistake of crossing one.

"Paloma!" she shouted, driving her knee into a warden's gut. "The boy — look at the boy!"

* * *

**Paloma**

Everyone looked at the boy. I think even Vael did, mid-stride, torch high.

He was standing over Maren with her blood on his small hands, and he was not crying. His eyes had gone amber — fully amber, lit from inside, twin coals in the torch-dark — and his song had changed. It wasn't the frightened fragment anymore. It was a whole verse, long and rising, in that language older than any kingdom, and the vault door was answering it, groaning wider, and the cold pouring out of the deep was full of voices now, faint and many, like a choir heard through a wall of ice.

The fighting faltered on both sides. Men lowered weapons to cover their ears. The standing dead of the old guild seemed — I know how this sounds — to lean inward, attentive, a thousand years of patience arriving all at once at its purpose.

And beneath everything, beneath the shouting and the dying and the ringing tombs, came a new sound from the deep: a long, slow inhalation, the kind a body makes when sleep ends and the mind begins, one by one, to take its accounts.

Ferro grabbed my arm. His face was grey. "Paloma," he said. "Something's opening its eyes down there."

I looked at the boy. The boy looked at me, amber-eyed, ancient-eyed, and for the first time since the blizzard night he knocked at my door, I was afraid of him.

The first tomb in the deep place began to crack.
