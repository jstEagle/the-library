## 26. Audience with Ash

The first thing I felt upon waking was cold. Not the cold of the vault—that was nothing, a blanket I had worn so long it had become my skin—but a colder thing entirely: the absence of my own name in the mouths of the living.

A thousand years is a long time to sleep. It is longer still to wake and find that no one remembers you were ever real.

I opened my eyes to torchlight and small faces.

Understand: I had expected this moment for centuries before the sleep took me. We all did. The Wolf-Kings were not fools; we knew what we were sealing ourselves into, and we knew—some of us hoped, some of us dreaded—that someday someone would come down the deep road and break the ice-glass of our dreaming. I had rehearsed this scene in my mind through ten generations of nightmares. In most versions, the ones who came wore crowns and knelt. In some they wore armor and carried hammers. Once or twice, in my darkest rehearsals, they came with fire, and I woke only long enough to watch my people's bones burn a second time.

In none of them did they look like *this*.

There were six of them standing at the edge of my sleeping-place, and not one of them was kneeling. Not one of them was armed—at least not visibly, though the big one with the broken nose held his hands in a way that told me he could be armed very quickly if he chose. A woman stood slightly ahead of the others, dark-haired, road-worn, her coat scorched along one shoulder where someone had recently tried to kill her. She held a torch in one hand and, absurdly, a smuggler's iron hook in the other, and she was looking at me the way no one had looked at me since the world was young.

She was looking at me like I was a problem.

Not a god. Not a ghost. Not a weapon or a relic or a miracle. A problem—a large, dangerous, inconvenient problem that had just been dumped into her night, and gods help us all but she was already calculating what to do about me.

I decided immediately that I liked her.

* * *

Let me tell you what dying feels like when you are what I am, because it matters to what happened next.

We do not die cleanly. The old blood does not end; it thickens, slows, goes down into itself like a river freezing from the edges toward the heart. When Vael's ancestors came over the passes with their broken crown and their blessed fire, I fought at the Ford of Ashes until the ford ran red, and then I fought in the high valleys, and then in the shrine-halls, and then alone, which is how you know you have lost. They cut me down on the steps of my own hall with a spear through the chest, and I lay there in the snow watching my breath stop being mine, and I thought: *this is it, this is the whole of it.*

It was not. The old blood pools. It waits. My sisters and brothers who survived the slaughter—we gathered what remained of our people and went down, down beneath the mountain, into the vault our road-wardens had kept secret even from us until the end, and we lay down in the ice-glass cradles our last smiths had made, and we let the freezing take us slowly, by choice, because the alternative was extinction and the freezing was only a kind of patience.

Patience. Ha. I have been patient for a thousand years and I can report to you with authority: patience is overrated. It is what cowards call waiting when the waiting has gone on too long to admit it was fear.

But I digress. Waking.

Waking is like surfacing from deep water into a storm. Everything hurts. Everything is too loud, too bright, too *now*. The boy helped—the mute child, the little vessel, the living memory of everything my people were. He stood at my cradle with his amber eyes wide open, and when I rose he did not flinch, and when I spoke my first word in ten centuries he answered it in Thornic, the true tongue, the tongue the Crown burned out of every book and every mouth, and the sound of it in a child's living voice nearly undid me right there.

Then I got a proper look at the rest of them, and the near-undoing passed, replaced by something more useful.

Fury.

* * *

You should know something about fury, when it lives as long as mine has. Fresh fury burns. Old fury calcifies. Mine had lain in the ice so long it had become geology—strata upon strata of rage compressed into something dense and dark and patient, and now, waking, it began to come apart layer by layer, each one older and colder than the last, and the deepest stratum was not rage at all.

It was grief.

Because I looked at these mortals—these brief, warm, trembling creatures—and I saw what they carried. The woman with the torch had a guild-mark scarred into her palm, half-effaced, the way marks get effaced when an organization stops believing in itself. The big one bore the posture of a man who had been hit often enough that he'd stopped expecting anything else. There was a drunk at the back reeking of cheap spirit and older shame. A blind woman—blind, and yet she had navigated the deep road, which meant she had memorized it, which meant somewhere in her head lived maps my wardens had died to protect. And one other, a sharp-eyed woman who would not meet my gaze, whose guilt came off her like smoke off wet wood.

Six mortals. That was what remained of the war for the mountains. Six frightened, exhausted, half-broken people and one strange child.

And against them? Against them stood a thousand years of silence. A dynasty built on our graves. A crown on a young queen's head that had been hammered, I knew without asking, out of the metal of my own—out of the crown they pried from my brother Aldric's broken head at the Gates of Morning while he still breathed. Every charter, every border stone, every prayer spoken in the Crown's temples rested on that theft, and the theft rested on the massacre, and the massacre had been so thorough that these six people standing before me did not even know their own guild had once been *mine*.

They didn't know. That was the thing that cracked the oldest stratum of my fury open and let the grief breathe through it. They had come down the deep road past the bones of my wardens—my wardens, dead in formation, pikes still gripped, guarding a door none of them remembered why—and they had looked at those bones with sorrow but not recognition. Grief for strangers. Mourning without inheritance.

A thousand years, and they had stolen even the memory of the crime, so thoroughly that the victims' own descendants mourned us as strangers.

* * *

I stood up out of the cradle. My body remembered itself slowly—muscle by muscle, sinew by sinew, a thousand years of stillness peeling away. I will confess that the first thing I did, before any speech, before any judgment, was stagger, catch myself on the edge of the stone table, and notice that my hands had gone the color of ash. The old blood spends freely when it wakes. I had perhaps days of full strength. Perhaps less.

So much for the terrifying immortal queen. I leaned on a table like a drunkard and faced them, and I saw the woman with the torch register my weakness, and I saw her decide—not quickly, not easily, but visibly—to say nothing about it.

Another point in her favor. Or against her, depending. With mortals it is always difficult to tell.

"You are not worshippers," I said in Thornic, and watched the words land. The boy translated them into the common speech without moving his lips—I felt him do it, felt the memory in him turn my meaning over like a page. "And you are not thieves. So explain to me what you are."

Silence. The blind woman tilted her head, listening to a voice she could not see. The drunk swayed. The guilty one's hand crept toward her belt knife and then, wisely, away.

It was the woman with the torch who answered.

"Smugglers," she said. Her accent mangled the Thornic vowels but she'd clearly caught the shape of my question through the boy's translation, or maybe she simply read faces well. "What's left of one. Paloma Blackwood. This is Ferro, Isolde, Tam, Maren. The boy—" she hesitated, and something in her face shifted, softened, guarded all at once, the way people guard the things they love most, "—the boy doesn't have a name we know yet. He came to us out of a blizzard. He's why we're here. He started singing to your door and the door listened."

"And you followed a singing child into the mountain," I said. "Why?"

"Because the Crown wants him dead," she said flatly. "And because I've spent my whole life running things through these passes and never once asked what they were for. Figured it was time." She shrugged, a small, hard, human gesture. "Also we were cornered and it was the only road left."

Honesty. All three layers of it—the noble reason, the curious reason, and the naked, desperate truth underneath. Mortals rarely offer the third layer voluntarily. I found myself revising my estimate of her again.

* * *

I walked among them. Slowly—I had to walk slowly, my legs were still remembering their argument with gravity—and I studied each face as I passed, the way I had once studied petitioners in the great hall at Cindermarch, back when the hall had a name and a roof and a kingdom attached to it.

The big one, Ferro, met my eyes and did not look away. Good. Fear is honest but defiance is more informative. He was afraid—of course he was afraid, I was a dead queen risen out of legend wearing ash for skin—but his fear had a spine in it. He glanced repeatedly at the boy, and at Blackwood, and I understood that his courage was not his own; it was borrowed from whatever he loved and would not see harmed. That is the best kind of courage. It is also the most fragile.

The drunk, Tam—he could not meet me at all. His eyes skittered everywhere, to the torches, the floor, the tombs, anywhere but my face, and his lips moved around words he hadn't said aloud yet. Guilt. Fresh guilt, recent guilt. Something done in a tavern. I marked him and moved on.

Isolde. The guilty one. When my shadow crossed her she went rigid, and her hand shook, and she stared at a fixed point on the floor as if the floor might save her. She had done something. Recently. Something that put them all in danger, if the scorch-marks on Blackwood's coat and the state of their flight meant what I suspected. I filed her away. Not for punishment—punishment is cheap, any tyrant can afford it—but because a company with an unconfessed betrayal in its middle is a company that breaks at exactly the wrong moment, and I needed to know where the crack was.

Maren, the blind archivist, was the strangest. She could not see me, and so she did what sighted people never think to do: she *listened* to me, wholly, the way you listen to weather. And when I paused near her she spoke, quietly, in accented but careful Thornic:

"The charter says we were your road-wardens. The ledger says we failed you. I've read both my whole life and never understood either." Her chin lifted. "Tell me if I'm wrong."

By all the buried stars. A thousand years, and the truth had survived in one blind woman's keeping like an ember banked in ash, and she had tended it without knowing what it was, and the first thing she wanted upon meeting its source was not absolution. It was *correction*. Accuracy. The record set straight.

I touched her shoulder. She startled, then steadied.

"You are not wrong," I said. "You failed. And you were betrayed. Both are true, and neither is yours to carry. But we will speak of that later."

Later. I said *later* as if I had later to give. As if my body weren't spending itself like a candle in wind. Let them believe in later. Belief is scaffolding; you build with it until the real walls hold.

* * *

Then I turned to the center of the room, to the stone table, and I let myself look—for the first time since waking, truly look—at what lay upon it.

My crown. Or rather, its twin. Its *sister*, forged in the same fire, by the same smiths, in the year the compact bound the nine holds together. The Crown's founders took Aldric's crown off his corpse and melted it into theirs, yes, but they could not melt what it meant, and so for a thousand years the thing on that table has been the true one by every law older than their law—the crown of covenant, of shared burden, of the nine holds speaking as one voice. They left it here because they could not destroy it and could not wear it and could not admit it existed.

Blackwood followed my gaze to it. I saw her understand, in the slow way of practical people, what it was and what it implied.

"That's why they hunt us," she said. Not a question. "Not the boy. Not really. Him waking you—it's because of *that*. Because if anyone ever sees it—"

"If anyone ever proves it," I corrected. "Seeing is nothing. Your queens have seen truths before and buried them under convenient wars. Proof is another matter. Proof walks. Proof speaks. Proof"—I gestured at the boy, at Maren, at the bones of my wardens still standing their eternal watch up the tunnel—"has witnesses."

"And what do you intend?" Ferro rumbled. First words he'd given me, and he chose challenge. Good instincts, that man. Wrong target, good instincts. "You wake up after a thousand years. You're a king—or a queen, whichever. What do you want? Your land back? Your throne? Because if that's the shape of this, I'll fight you here and now and save the Crown the trouble."

"Ferro," Blackwood said sharply.

"No. Let him ask." I stepped closer to him, close enough that he had to tilt his head to keep my eyes, close enough that he could see the ash-color of me, the cracks the long sleep had left. "Ask it properly. Ask it like a man deciding whether to die for a stranger."

He didn't blink. "Fine. What do you want, dead queen?"

Dead queen. I almost laughed. The last person who called me that lost a hand for it, and here was a broken-nosed smuggler saying it like a tavern insult, and the mountains themselves seemed to hold their breath to hear it.

"What do I want," I repeated. And there it was—the moment I had rehearsed for ten centuries, the moment of judgment, the weighing of souls at the threshold of the new age. I looked at each of them. The smuggler who followed a song out of stubbornness. The warden-heir who demanded honesty from legends. The archivist who wanted the record corrected. The traitor shaking beside her secret. The drunk drowning in his. The child who was all of them and none of them, the vessel, the witness, the last living syllable of my people.

"I want," I said, "what was taken. Not the throne." I spat the word; it tasted of spear-points and snow. "Thrones are how we lost. Nine kings, each clutching his own seat, too proud to stand together until the enemy stood over all of us. No. What I want is smaller and harder. I want the record set straight. I want the massacre acknowledged—in the temples, in the schools, in the mouths of queens. I want the names of the dead read aloud until they are boring. I want your Crown to kneel not to me but to *history*, which is a far more terrible sovereign than I ever was." I let the fury show, just a seam of it, the deep stratum cracking. "And I want it done by living hands. Not mine. Mine are finished—you can see it as plainly as I can. Yours are not."

Silence, of the ringing kind.

"So you're not taking the throne," Blackwood said slowly.

"I am taking *nothing*," I said. "That is precisely what makes me dangerous. Kings take. It is all kings know. I am going to *witness*. And I am going to make your young queen witness too, whether she wishes it or not, and what she builds from that witnessing will be hers—hers and yours and the clans' and the guilds', or it will fail, and the mountain will bury this room deeper than before, and I will sleep again, and this time I will not wake."

Tam made a small sound. Everyone ignored him, including, mercifully, the ceiling.

* * *

It was then that the torches guttered—all of them at once, in unison, a breath drawn through the vault—and the boy cried out, a thin sharp sound, and clutched at Blackwood's coat.

I felt it too. Up the deep road, faint as a heartbeat through a wall: boots. Many. Torches. Steel. The inquisitor's people, coming down the tunnel my wardens had died to seal, and behind them something worse—the specific, grinding confidence of a man who believes absolutely that he is right.

Vael. I did not know his name yet. I would learn it soon enough, and learn it well. But I knew his type the way you know weather in old bones: the righteous hammer, the true believer, the man who burns archives to save them.

Blackwood looked at me across the vault, torchlight jumping between us, and asked the only question that mattered:

"How fast can you move?"

I looked down at my ash-grey hands, at the thousand-year rust on my limbs, at the candle of myself burning low and fast.

"Not fast," I said. "But I have been patient for a thousand years, smuggler, and I promise you this—" the vault doors began to boom somewhere above, iron on ancient iron, "—whatever comes down that stair, it will find that the dead remember how to stand in formation."

Above us, the booming stopped. And then, muffled but unmistakable, drifting down through a thousand years of silence: the sound of a door answering.
