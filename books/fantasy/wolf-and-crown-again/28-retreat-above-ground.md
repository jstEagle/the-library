## 28. Retreat Above Ground

Here is what nobody tells you about waking a thousand-year-old king from her tomb: the tomb takes it personally.

The first crack came through the ice-glass like a gunshot, and then the whole vault started to sing — a low groaning hum in the stone itself, the sound a mountain makes when it decides it's been holding its breath too long. Dust fell from the ceiling in ropes. Somewhere above us, tons of rock were doing arithmetic about gravity, and the numbers weren't coming out friendly.

"We need to leave," I said. "Now would be good."

Ferro was already moving, because Ferro has one speed when things go wrong and it's *away from the wrong thing*. He had Maren over his shoulder like a sack of turnips, which she objected to loudly despite being barely conscious, blood seeping through the bandage Isolde had strapped across her ribs. A crossbow bolt meant for the boy had found her instead. She'd been shielding him with nothing but her own stubborn body and her blindness, which is either the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever seen, and knowing Maren it was both.

Isolde had the boy's hand clamped in hers. The boy — my boy, though I hadn't quite admitted that to myself yet — wasn't running. He was standing in front of the first sarcophagus, watching the ice-glass split, watching the woman inside take her first breath in ten centuries.

"Boy." I grabbed his shoulder. "Walk."

He looked up at me, and those amber eyes — still glowing faintly, like coals someone forgot to bank — blinked once, twice, and then he took my hand.

And Sera opened her eyes.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of my entire miserable life now: I nearly ran. Not away from Vael, not away from the collapsing roof, but from *her*. Because what climbed out of that coffin wasn't a corpse and wasn't quite a person. It was a woman tall as Ferro, hair the color of old silver, skin grey-white as frost on a windowpane, and eyes the same amber as the boy's except hers held a thousand years of looking at things. When she looked at you, you felt *inventoried*.

She looked at the collapsing ceiling. She looked at the boy. She looked at me, at my smuggler's leathers and my shaking hands and the knife I'd forgotten I was holding.

"You," she said. Her voice sounded like stone grinding on stone, but underneath it there was something almost amused. "You are afraid of everything, and yet here you stand."

"Occupational hazard," I said, which is what I say when I have no better answer, which is most of the time.

Then the ceiling came down between us and the far wall, and conversation was over.

* * *

The deep road out was Maren's chart, shouted from Ferro's shoulder while he jogged. That's the kind of sentence I never thought I'd write, but the world had gone strange, so we all went strange with it.

"Left at the third pillar," Maren rasped, blood on her lips. "Not the second. The second floods."

"The second *floods*?" Ferro bellowed, taking the left so hard I heard something in his back pop.

"It flooded in the reign of Aldric the Third," Maren said faintly. "Probably it has gotten over it."

We ran through corridors that hadn't felt feet since before the Crown existed. The vault behind us was coming apart in stages — first the groaning, then the cracks spiderwebbing overhead, then whole slabs letting go with crashes that hit us like physical blows, pressure waves shoving air down our necks. The Wolf-Kings' other coffins were cracking too, I saw as we passed. Ice-glass splitting. Frost sublimating off preserved faces like breath in winter. Whatever the boy's waking had started, it hadn't stopped at one king.

"Are they all—" Isolde started.

"Don't know," I said. "Don't care right now."

That was a lie. I cared enormously. But caring and surviving don't fit through the same doorway at the same time, and the doorway was getting smaller.

Behind us, deeper in, I could hear shouting. Vael's men. Some of them had been inside the vault mouth when the mountain started its tantrum, and their shouts were turning from commands to screams. I didn't hear Vael himself. Part of me hoped he'd made it out. A larger part, the part that remembered Tam's body at the ford and Maren's blood on the floor, hoped he hadn't. I'm not proud of that part. But I've stopped pretending it isn't there.

Sera kept pace beside me without seeming to run. It was deeply unsettling. The ground shook, dust choked the dark, and this ancient woman moved through it like she was walking a garden path, her bare grey feet finding stone where stone was and air where it wasn't. Once she reached out and pulled me back by the collar two full seconds before a slab the size of a hay wagon came down exactly where I'd been about to step.

"How did you—"

"I remember this road," she said. "I cut part of it."

Of course she did. Why wouldn't the road predate my guild? My guild predates every kingdom. Everything predates everything, if you go back far enough, and we had apparently gone back plenty far enough.

The boy stumbled. I scooped him up without thinking — he weighed nothing, all ribs and cold — and he wrapped his arms around my neck and pressed his face against it, and I felt his heartbeat going fast as a bird's. Whatever he was, whatever slept inside him, in that moment he was just a child in a falling-down hole in the world who wanted his — well.

His whatever-I-was.

"Almost there," I told him, lying again, because that's what you do with children and horses and yourself.

* * *

The last stretch was the worst. Maren's charts showed an ascent shaft — a chimney of rock the old wardens had cut with hand tools back when hand tools and patience were the only technologies on offer — and we reached it just as the corridor behind us gave up entirely. The collapse chased us up the shaft. I mean that literally. We climbed hand over hand in the dark with the rock shuddering around us, breathing dust, Ferro hauling Maren up ahead of him on a rope harness, Isolde climbing with a broken wrist she refused to acknowledge, and Sera ascending the chimney like water runs uphill, effortless, impossible, her amber eyes twin lamps in the black.

The boy I'd tied to my back with a smuggler's hitch. He sang into my ear the whole climb — not the hearth-bowing songs, just small sounds, little notes of comfort, like a mother cat talking to kittens. It shouldn't have helped. It helped enormously.

Light appeared above us. Actual daylight, thin and grey and the single most beautiful thing I'd seen in my life, filtering down through a crack in the mountainside no wider than a coffin lid.

"Ferro! Up top — widen it!"

"If I blow my shoulders out doing this, you're burying me with honors," Ferro growled, but he got there first, jammed his shoulder into the crack, and pushed. Rock grated. Snow spilled down. And then his arm disappeared to the shoulder into open air, and he hauled himself out, and then Isolde, and then I passed the boy up to waiting hands — Isolde's, shaking, careful — and then Maren, and then I came out into the light myself, flat on my back in three feet of snow, gasping like a landed fish.

Sera emerged last. She stood at the lip of the crack, silhouetted against a white sky, and looked around at the peaks of Thornfell with an expression I couldn't read. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Or both wearing each other's clothes.

Then the mountain exhaled one final time, and the crack we'd come through sealed itself with a boom that rolled down the valley like thunder learning a new job. Dust geysered out of the fissure. Snow slid off the pines in white avalanches of their own.

Silence.

"Well," said Ferro, spitting out grit. "Anyone else hungry?"

It was, in fairness, a fair question. We'd been underground for the better part of two days, running on hardtack and terror.

* * *

We made camp an hour downslope, in a hollow beneath an overhang where the wind couldn't find us. Isolde built a fire with hands that wouldn't stop trembling — she hid it well until she tried to strike the flint, and then I took it from her gently and did it myself, and she let me, which frightened me more than the tremble. Isolde doesn't let people do things for her. Isolde once broke a customs man's nose for offering her a coat.

Maren lay by the fire with her eyes closed — they were always closed; she'd been born that way — and her breath shallow. Isolde had done what field surgery she could. It wasn't enough and we all knew it. What Maren needed was a surgeon, a warm bed, and about a month of soup, in roughly that order.

"You're staring," Maren said. "I can feel it. Blind, not deaf."

"Just checking you're alive."

"Barely. Don't get sentimental, Paloma, you'll strain something." A ghost of her dry smile. Then, quieter: "Did it open? The door?"

"It opened."

"And what was inside?"

I looked across the fire at Sera. She sat slightly apart, watching the flames with an expression of intense scholarly interest, occasionally reaching toward them and pulling her fingers back when they got too close. Fire, apparently, was new information.

"A problem," I said. "A very old problem, awake now, and sitting twenty feet away learning what fire does."

"Hm," said Maren, and drifted off to sleep, which is the archivist's version of filing something under *later*.

The boy curled against my side. He'd stopped singing. He watched Sera too, with something like recognition, and she watched him back, and between them passed some current I couldn't see but could feel, like standing near a lightning strike that hadn't happened yet.

"She saved your life in the shaft," Isolde said quietly, settling next to me. "In the tunnel too. Twice I saw her move stone off your path before it fell."

"I noticed."

"So she's not what the stories say."

"The stories say the Wolf-Kings ate children and drank the blood of kings."

"Do they?" Sera asked.

I jumped about a foot. She had crossed the distance between her seat and our fire without making a sound, which should be impossible for a woman her size, and crouched at the edge of the light, head tilted.

"Do the stories say that?" she repeated. There was no anger in it. Only curiosity, vast and patient, the curiosity of someone who had all the time in the world and then lost ten centuries of it anyway.

"Some do," I admitted.

"What do *you* say?"

I thought about the murals in the hollow chapel. Crowned wolves slaughtered by men holding a broken crown. I thought about the guild charter Maren had shown me, the oath we'd inherited without knowing its weight. I thought about the vault, and the coffins, and the massacre the whole kingdom was founded on top of like a rug swept over a grave.

"I say," I said slowly, "that the stories were written by the winners. And I say the winners cheated."

Something shifted in her face. Not a smile. Something older than smiling.

"Yes," she said. "They did."

* * *

We talked until the fire burned low. Well — I talked. She asked questions. An endless, gentle, surgical interrogation. What year was it. Who sat the throne. What had become of the passes, the roads, the clans. Each answer landed on her like a blow she refused to show, though I learned to read the tells: a tightening at the jaw, a slow blink. When I told her how many Wolf-Kings remained — one, as far as we knew, plus whatever was stirring in the other coffins — she closed her eyes for the length of ten heartbeats.

When I told her about the boy, about the hunters, about the queen's inquisitors and the word *vessel*, she opened her eyes and looked at the sleeping child for a long time.

"He carries what we could not keep," she said finally. "Our memory. Our names. The truth of what was done to us. They hunted the vessels for a thousand years and called it pest control." Her gaze came back to me. "And you. You are not clan, not crown, not court. Why do you stand between him and them?"

I opened my mouth to give the clever answer, the wry deflection, and found I didn't have one. So I gave the true one.

"Because he knocked on my door," I said. "And nobody else answered."

Sera studied me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, like a judge accepting testimony.

"That," she said, "is the first honest reason anyone has given me in a thousand years. Before or after the sleep."

High praise. From her, probably. From anyone, actually.

* * *

I took first watch, because I always take first watch, and because I wanted to be alone with my thoughts and the thoughts were terrible company.

Below us, somewhere under forty fathoms of rock, Vael was either dead or digging. I knew Vael now — knew him the way you know a man after he's chased you across half a country. He was thorough. He was convinced. Convinced men dig. And when he dug out — if he dug out — he'd go to Greyhollow, to the young queen, and he'd tell her that the Wolf-Kings lived, and that the truth of her crown was awake and walking, and then every soldier, warden, and ambitious lordling in the province would come to Thornfell looking for us.

We had: one wounded archivist, one broken-wristed traitor-turned-ally, one gruff giant with a bad back, one drunk (Tam's absence sat in camp like an empty chair), one ancient resurrected king, one mute child full of somebody else's memories, and me.

Against: a kingdom.

"Good odds," I muttered to the snow. "For the kingdom."

The boy stirred in his sleep beside the dead fire and murmured something in that old language, and Sera — who apparently didn't sleep, or slept so rarely it counted as a hobby rather than a necessity — translated softly from across the hollow:

"He says: *the road goes east.*"

East. Toward Greyhollow. Toward the queen.

"Tell him," I said, "that roads go wherever I say they go."

Sera considered the sleeping child, listening to some reply I couldn't hear.

"He says," she reported, with what I swear was the ghost of amusement, "*you will see.*"

Wonderful. Prophetic children. Just what a dying smuggling operation needs.

I looked east, where the sky was starting to bruise grey at the edges, where the provincial capital sat fat and complacent behind its walls, ruled by a young queen who didn't yet know her crown was a lie with jewels on it. In a day, maybe two, Vael would reach her. After that, every hour we waited was an hour she spent sharpening.

"Ferro," I said quietly. "Wake up."

He surfaced instantly, knife already half-drawn, because that's Ferro. "What."

"Gather everyone. We're marching."

"Marching where?"

"Greyhollow."

He stared at me. Across the ashes, Isolde lifted her head. Even Maren, half-conscious, turned her face toward my voice.

"You want to walk the last Wolf-King into the queen's own city," Ferro said flatly.

"No," I said. "I want to walk the truth into the queen's own city. The Wolf-King's just the only one carrying it."

Above the eastern peaks, the sun came up over Thornfell for the first time in a thousand years on a morning that included one of its kings, and I pulled my coat tighter, and we started walking.
