## 29. Sera Among Mortals

I had been dead for a thousand years, and I want to state for the record that death was considerably more comfortable.

Not the dying — the dying had been teeth and iron and my brother's scream cut short, and I do not recommend it. But the sleeping. The sleeping was excellent. No hunger. No cold. No small talk. A thousand years of dreamless dark, and then a child's song cracked the ice-glass like an egg, and I woke to find myself blinking at a smuggler with soot on his jaw and an expression like a man who has just watched his grandmother climb out of a pickle barrel.

"Hello," he said.

I considered my options. There were none. My body was a ruin of old wounds wrapped in new flesh, my kingdom was ash, my crown was — well. We would come to the crown. So I said, in the tongue of my people, "Where is the rest of me?" and he looked at the mute boy, who translated into something rougher and newer, and the smuggler said, "You're all of you there is."

All of me. One king, out of nine. One witness, out of a nation.

I laughed. It came out as a cough, because lungs that have slept a millennium are not built for laughter, but it was a laugh underneath. What else was there to do? Weep? I had done my weeping before the ice took me, and I had promised myself, in that last red hour beneath Thornfell, that if I ever opened my eyes again I would not spend them drowning. Fury keeps. Grief spoils. I intended to be useful.

* * *

The first thing I learned about being alive again was that bodies are tyrants.

Hunger arrived within the hour — not the clean, noble hunger of a hunting king, but a gnawing, undignified cramp that made my hands shake and my thoughts orbit bread. Bread! I had commanded ten thousand spears. I had spoken with the mountain itself, in the days when the mountain still answered. And now the entire architecture of my mind bent around a loaf.

"There's stew," said the big one — Ferro, the others called him, though mostly they called him nothing at all, since he seemed to regard speech as a toll he resented paying. He shoved a tin bowl at me with the wariness of a man feeding a wolf he'd found in his woodshed. Which, to be fair, was approximately what I was.

The stew was barley and salt pork and something rootlike, and it was the finest thing I had ever tasted, and I am including in that assessment the coronation feast where they served me swan in saffron and the whole hall sang my name. That feast had been about me. This stew was just stew, hot and ugly and made by someone who expected nothing from me except that I eat it. I ate three bowls. I would have eaten the tin if no one was watching.

"You eat like Ferro," said the sharp-tongued woman — Isolde, all knives and watchfulness, though her knives were pointed inward more often than out. I was learning to read these people the way I once read battlefields: where they stood, what they guarded, which way their grief faced.

"I eat like someone who has been dead a thousand years," I replied, through the boy. His name was Fen, I had learned. He translated for me with a solemnity that sat strangely on a child, as though he were carrying water in a cup too large for his hands. When he spoke my words in their tongue, something moved behind his eyes — recognition, or memory, or the echo of both. He was a vessel. I knew what that meant better than anyone. I had helped build the vessels, in the last days, when we understood we were losing and began hiding our selves in whatever would hold us.

He should not have been a child. That was the Crown's work, or the work of men who thought like the Crown: take the memory, pour it into something small and portable, something that could be carried off and studied or burned. They had never managed to catch him. Instead a smuggler had.

Paloma Blackwood. I turned the name over as I ate. Last apprentice of a guild that had forgotten what it was. His ancestors had been my road-wardens — sworn to me, sworn to the passes, sworn to the door. And when my enemies came with fire and a broken crown, the wardens had died in formation at the deep door rather than open it, and their descendants had kept the roads and lost the reason, generation by generation, until all that remained was tolls and routes and a charter nobody read.

And yet. A thousand years of forgetting, and when a strange child knocked on a tollhouse door in a blizzard, a smuggler had taken him in. I had ruled men who would not have done that. Kings, with crowns and oaths and armies. I filed this away. It mattered. I did not yet know how, but it mattered.

* * *

The cold was the second tyrant.

We traveled by night along the high paths — Paloma's paths, the smugglers' veins of the mountain, and I will grant the man this: he knew my mountains better than any living soul, perhaps better than the living mountain knew itself. But the wind above the treeline had teeth now, late-winter teeth, and my resurrected body remembered every wound the ice had politely numbed. My knee, which had taken a mace at the Ford of Ashes. My shoulder, where a Crown arrow had gone through in the second year of the war. The long scar across my ribs that I had earned personally, from the man whose grandson's grandson's grandson now wore a crown forged from mine.

I did not complain. Complaining is a habit of the young, and I was very old even before the sleeping. But the cold got in anyway, past dignity, past discipline, into the marrow, and on the third night I caught myself shaking beside the fire like a plucked bird, and Ferro looked over at me with an expression of profound disgust and threw his own cloak at my head.

"I don't need it," he said, which was a lie; the man had the insulation of a barn and the circulation of a banked forge, but still.

"I am a king," I said, through Fen, with what I felt was appropriate gravity.

"You're a skeleton somebody soup'd back together," Ferro said. "Wear the cloak."

I wore the cloak.

It smelled of horse and pipe-smoke and some resinous salve he worked into leather, and it was warm, and I sat there under a dead man's — no, a living man's cloak, a grumpy, loyal, unlettered man who had voted to hide a stranger's child and had never once asked what was in it for him — and I felt something crack in me that had nothing to do with ice-glass.

Here is what no chronicle tells you about kingship: it is loneliness wearing ceremony. For two hundred years I was the Wolf-King, and every hand extended to me wanted something, and every knee bent carried a knife behind its owner's back or fear behind its eyes, and I loved my people the way a shepherd loves a flock — genuinely, but from slightly above, always slightly above, because that was the shape of the office. And here was a man who did not know what kneeling was, offering me his only cloak because I was shivering, and expecting nothing, absolutely nothing, in return except that I stop making that noise with my teeth.

"Thank you," I said.

Ferro grunted, which among his people, I was learning, constituted a sonnet.

* * *

Fen slept against my side that night, and I watched the fire and listened to mortals breathe.

They breathe so loudly. All of them. A thousand years of silence had made me forget that the living are never quiet — the wet bellows of the lungs, the creak of joints, the muttering of dreams. Maren, the blind archivist, slept sitting up with her ruined hands folded, and she breathed like a woman reading aloud. Isolde barely breathed at all, shallow and controlled even in sleep, a sentry's breathing, and I marked that too: whatever she had done, whatever debt rode her, she had not yet forgiven herself for it. Guilt has a sound. I had heard it in the tents of generals the night before battles they did not believe in.

And Paloma. Paloma slept like a man who had forgotten how, badly and gratefully, one hand near the boy even unconscious, the other near the knife even unconscious. A warden's sleep. The old training, worn down to instinct after forty generations, like a riverbed that remembers the glacier.

I had come back expecting to find ruins. I had prepared speeches for ruins. I had rehearsed, in the long dark, the words I would say to the ashes of my murderers' empire: *you built your house on my bones, and here are the bones.* I had imagined I would find either slaves or savages — the descendants of the victors, bloated and blind, or the descendants of the scattered, broken and forgetful.

Instead I found a smuggler who fed a frozen child, a drunk who died buying time at a ford — Tam, they told me, and I drank to him privately, because a man who spends his last coin on strangers is worth a king's toast — a traitor who betrayed for love and confessed for love, a doubter who stayed anyway, a blind woman who had kept the truth alive in the dark for decades without knowing why, and a mute boy who carried my people's memory in his small skull and sang to hearth-flames until they bowed.

Not ruins. Embers. Buried deep and banked hard, but embers.

I had spent my life believing that greatness lived in halls and thrones and lines of spears. Lying under a stolen cloak in a smuggler's camp, listening to mortals snore, I began to suspect that greatness was smaller and stupider and far more durable than that. It was a bowl of stew. It was a cloak thrown without thinking. It was staying.

* * *

On the fifth day, it snowed, and I discovered the third tyrant: boredom.

There is a great deal of waiting in being alive. Waiting for the snow to ease. Waiting for the boy to wake. Waiting for wounds to close and weather to turn and word to come up the pass. As a king I had never waited; waiting was what heralds and quartermasters were for. Now I sat in a hollow beneath an overhang while the world went white, and I discovered that immortality had left me entirely unequipped for an afternoon.

So I talked. To Fen, mostly, in the old tongue, quietly, while the others pretended not to listen. I told him the names of the peaks — their true names, the ones the mountain had answered to, not the Crown's cartography with its victories stamped across every valley. He repeated them back to me, haltingly, and each time he got one right, something in the air around us warmed, as though the mountain were eavesdropping and pleased.

"That one," I said, pointing through the snow-haze at a ridge like a broken tooth, "is called Hjarn-vael. The Wound That Watches. Your maps call it Mount Prosperity." I let the bitterness show. "Your queen's grandfather renamed it after the silver mines. The silver was dug out of a graveyard. Nobody living knows that."

"Fen knows," Fen said. Not translating. Speaking for himself, in the old tongue, clumsy but true. "Fen dreamed it. Before. The digging. The screaming under the snow."

I looked at him for a long time. He looked back, amber-eyed in the grey light, and I saw what he was: not a vessel merely, not a jar for memories, but a witness. The difference matters. A jar can be shelved. A witness must someday speak.

"Yes," I said. "And when we reach Greyhollow, everyone will know. That is the only vengeance I want. Not fire. Not crowns. Just the knowing. Let them choke on it gently."

Ferro, who had been pretending to repair a boot buckle, said without looking up: "Vengeance doesn't feed anybody."

"No," I agreed. "But neither does forgetting. I have tried both. Forgetting takes longer and starves more people."

He grunted. Then, after a silence long enough to grow moss in, he passed me a strip of dried meat without being asked, and I understood that in his language, this was concession, agreement, and apology all at once, and possibly also a marriage proposal. These people. I was beginning to love them, which frightened me more than the vault had.

* * *

That evening, Isolde sat across the fire from me and studied me the way she studied everything — like a lock she intended to pick.

"You don't want your throne back," she said. It was not quite a question.

"My throne is a hole in a mountain full of my dead friends," I said, through Fen. "No."

"The Crown will believe you do. Vael already believes it. The queen will believe it. You're marching on Greyhollow with a growing mob of smugglers and deserters. From where they sit, that's an army."

"It's a grievance with legs," I said. "There is a difference. Armies obey. Grievances only walk."

"And when they get there?"

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Then we will see whether the young queen is her ancestors' daughter or her own woman. I have read her reign in Maren's papers, such as they reach the passes. She is not cruel. Cruelty is almost worse in a ruler than competence, because cruelty is efficient. She hesitates. She asks questions she shouldn't. Her council despises her for it." I smiled, and felt the scar tissue pull at my mouth. "She reminds me of me. Before. That is either the best news in a thousand years or the worst."

Isolde snorted. "Kings comparing themselves favorably to queens. Some things survive the ice."

"Everything survives the ice," I said. "That is rather the problem."

Across the fire, Fen had fallen asleep against Ferro's shoulder — Ferro, who claimed to dislike children and had not moved a muscle in an hour lest he wake him — and Paloma was cleaning his blades with the methodical tenderness of a man saying his prayers, and Maren was murmuring the old charter under her breath like a lullaby she'd finally learned the words to, and the snow came down soft and endless on the roof of the world.

I had wanted, waking, to burn everything down. I had wanted it so badly I could taste it, older than the ice, hotter than the fire. And here I was instead, wearing a smuggler's cloak, planning a revolution made entirely of testimony, loving people I had known for six days.

The old kings would have called it weakness. The old kings, I reflected, were all dead, and I was not, and there was probably a lesson in that.

Then hooves — far off, up the pass, many of them, moving fast through snow that should have muffled them — and Paloma's head came up like a hound's, and Isolde was on her feet with a knife already drawn, and Ferro woke without seeming to wake at all.

"They've found the trail," Paloma said quietly. "Greyhollow knows we're coming."

Good, I thought, rising on old knees that ached like the world's oldest argument. Let them know. Let the whole rotten edifice know that its foundation just stood up and started walking.

Let us see if the young queen kneels to history, or orders it shot.
