## 30. March on Greyhollow

There is an old smuggler's saying: *a road is only illegal until enough people walk it.*

We'd lived by the inverse of that truth for a thousand years, my guild and I. Every pass we kept open in the dark was a small confession that the Crown's roads were a lie — that the mountains belonged to no king's signature, whatever parchment said. We were proof of the lie, walking evidence, and so we had to be criminals. That was the arrangement. That was always the arrangement.

Now I was about to break it in the other direction.

We came down off the high passes on the third morning after Sera's waking, and the world below us looked exactly as it always had, which was the most obscene thing about it. Smoke from chimney fires. A tithe-wagon crawling the king's road toward Greyhollow with four horses and six guards to move what was probably grain and definitely not worth six guards. A shrine at the crossroads with the Queen's face painted fresh on its little board — young Aurelian, serene, gilt-cheeked, watching over travelers who paid her tolls whether they believed in her or not.

The boy stopped in front of that shrine and stared at it for a long time.

He'd been quiet since the vault. Quiet the way a full cup is quiet. He walked with Sera now instead of me, mostly, matching his short legs to her long stride without either of them appearing to notice, and when he looked at things he looked at them the way you look at a room you're deciding whether to buy. He looked at the Queen's painted face like that.

"What do you see?" I asked him, because I'm a fool who talks to children who can't answer.

He reached up and touched the wet paint. It came away on his finger, gold and white. Then he held it out to me, and his eyes did their amber flicker — brief, like sun through moving water — and though he still had no words for it, I understood him perfectly.

*This isn't real.*

"No," I agreed, wiping his hand clean with my sleeve. "It very much isn't."

Sera had watched all this without comment. She watched everything without comment, which was somehow worse than commentary. She was wearing Ferro's spare cloak, which fit her like a sail on a rowboat, and she moved through the winter morning with the unhurried certainty of weather. People on the road stepped aside for her without knowing why. Dogs didn't bark at her. One old woman selling chestnuts took one look, went pale, and pressed a hot bag into the boy's hands and would take no coin, crossing herself twice, muttering something about *the mountain walking.*

Word travels strange in the passes. Faster than riders, sometimes, because it travels as rumor, and rumor doesn't have to stop to eat.

* * *

Ferro found me at midday, at the back of our strung-out little column, where I was doing what I do best: counting heads and pretending not to worry.

"Thirty-one," he said. "Counting the ghost, the boy, Maren in the cart, and your drunk."

"Tam's dead, Ferro."

"Then thirty." He chewed on that. His face was a landscape of old scars and older stubbornness, and grief had carved another valley into it since the ford — Tam's death sat in him like a stone sits in a boot; you don't mention it, but every step reminds you. "Thirty people following a smuggler, a corpse-king, and a child. Paloma. What exactly are we doing?"

I watched the column. Isolde was up front, scouting, moving through snow like she'd been born in it, which she practically had. Behind her came miners from the Kettlevein pits — six of them, still in their pit-leathers, faces gray with rock dust that never washes out — who had simply fallen in beside us at the Hollow Chapel two days back, wordless, carrying picks. Behind them, a family of charcoal-burners whose son had been taken by a press-gang last spring. Two deserters from the Greyhollow garrison, which told you something about morale in Greyhollow. An old toll-collector who'd been dismissed when the Crown centralized the roads and hadn't eaten properly since. A widow. Three brothers who ran contraband salt and had owed me money for years and had apparently decided this was the strangest possible way to settle the debt.

None of them had made a speech. That was the thing Ferro couldn't see, standing there wanting a plan with a shape to it. They hadn't joined anything. There was nothing to join. They were just... walking the same direction, and the direction happened to be toward the capital of the province that had bled them all.

"We're not doing anything," I said. "That's the point. The Crown does things *to* people. We're just refusing to be done to, out loud, where it can be counted."

"That's not a plan."

"No," I agreed. "It's better than a plan. Plans can be countered. This is just arithmetic."

He grunted, which from Ferro is a treaty. Then, gruffer: "Maren wants you. She's been asking since dawn and I've been telling her you were busy being cryptic."

"I am never cryptic. I'm merely economical with clarity."

"She also says if you use that voice on her she'll recite the entire founding charter from memory. In Thornic. Twice."

Gods bless blind old women with perfect recall. I went to the cart.

* * *

Maren lay propped among sacks with her bandaged shoulder and her sightless eyes aimed at the sky, and she caught my wrist before I'd even finished climbing up. Her grip was still strong. The wound Vael's warden had given her, shielding the boy in the dark among the tombs, had gone hot and then cold and now sat at some uneasy truce, and I chose not to think about what that truce cost her daily.

"You felt it too," she said.

"Felt what?"

"Don't be a smuggler at me, Paloma Blackwood. You've been smiling at the horizon all morning and you don't smile unless the roads are talking." She turned her face toward me. "The guild-road network. It's waking up. Isn't it."

She was right, and I'd been avoiding saying it because saying it makes it real and making it real makes it fragile. But here's what had been happening, quietly, all down our line of march: at every crossroads shrine, someone had scratched a mark. The old guild-marks. The *road-open* sign that hasn't been legally cut into a stone in three hundred years. At the Kettlevein fork, someone had left a lantern burning in the hollow of the waystone — the signal for *safe passage tonight*. At the river bend, a fisherman had waved us across his ferry and refused payment and said, only said, looking at Sera the whole while: "My grandmother sang songs about the crowned wolf. I thought they were nursery things."

"The network remembers," Maren said, when I'd told her all this. "Of course it does. We spent a thousand years building a system for passing messages under the Crown's nose, child. Do you know what we actually built? A nervous system. And it has been lying dormant, waiting for a reason to exist." Her mouth did something complicated. "We built it for the Wolf-Kings originally, you know. The charter says so. Guard the roads, guard the secret, guard the sleepers. And then the secret ate the purpose and all we guarded was our own smuggling profits." She laughed, dry as old paper. "And now the purpose walks up the road wearing Ferro's cloak, and every dead drop between here and Greyhollow is lighting up like festival lamps."

"So it's not just thirty people."

"It was never going to be just thirty people. Thirty is the seed. The soil's been fallow for centuries, Paloma. Everyone the Crown squeezed — and the Crown squeezes everyone — they all know the old signs. The miners' grandmothers taught them. The deserters learned them from informants. The whole mountain has been waiting a thousand years for someone to say the word *open* again."

I sat with that a while. Below us, the tithe-wagon crawled on toward Greyhollow, and the painted Queen smiled her gilt smile over empty crossroads.

"And when we get to Greyhollow," I said slowly, "and the garrison comes out to arrest us—"

"It won't be thirty people standing behind you," Maren said. "It'll be everyone who ever paid a bribe at a gate and hated it. Which is to say, everyone."

* * *

That night we camped in the ruins of Tollhouse Nine, which had been my post once, before the Crown burned it in the first sweep of wardens — my home, or the nearest thing I'd had to one, back when the guild still had enough members to need a roof. The walls stood hip-high. Someone had sheltered sheep in it since. The hearth I'd warmed my hands over for eleven years was a black ring of stones full of sheep dung.

I stood in the middle of it while camp went up around me — fires lit, picket set, Isolde's watch rotation running smooth as oiled rope — and I felt... I don't have a good word. Grief and vindication in the same bowl, like blood and milk.

Sera came to stand beside me. She didn't ask questions; I suspect she'd seen ten thousand ruined buildings and knew the taxonomy of grief well enough.

"They burned it in autumn," I said. "Two years ago. New road laws. The wardens gave us a day's warning, which was considered generous." I nudged a hearthstone with my boot. "I dug my ledger out of the ash. Everything else went. My mother's letters. The maps. Eleven years."

"You rebuilt nothing," she observed. Her voice is strange to hear — deep and unhurried, like water moving under ice. She speaks common tongue the way she seems to speak everything: as if she invented it and is being patient with it.

"Nothing to rebuild. The guild was dying anyway. The Crown didn't kill it. It just... refused to let it live, and we did the rest ourselves, slowly, out of tiredness." I looked at her sideways. "You slept a thousand years. Does it seem quick to you? All of it. Kingdoms rising and falling?"

"Everything seems quick," she said, "when you are not the thing that endures." She picked up a hearthstone — my hearthstone — weighed it in her hand like a woman judging bread. Then she set it back precisely where it had been, aligned with the others. "Your house will stand again. Not this one. The one you are building by walking."

"Wise words from a dead queen."

"I was never a queen," Sera said, and there was iron under it, sudden and cold. "Queens rule. I kept. Remember the difference when they offer you my head in Greyhollow, smuggler. They will offer. It's what crowns do. And I want to see what you say — not because I doubt you, but because *you* should hear yourself say it."

She walked off toward the boy, who was showing her, gravely, how Ferro had taught him to whittle. Leaving me alone with a dead fire and a living sentence.

* * *

Deserters came in during the night. That was the new thing, the thing that changed the shape of everything.

Four of them, from the Greyhollow garrison itself, slipping into camp an hour before dawn with their kit bundled in blankets. Their sergeant — a heavy, honest-faced woman named Brakka with a scarred lip and a voice like a landslide — gave her account to me, Isolde, and Ferro around the fire, while Sera sat slightly apart with the sleeping boy against her side and listened to everything, the way she listens to everything.

"There's nine hundred in Greyhollow," Brakka said. "On paper. Real number's nearer seven hundred, what with sickness and desertion and the council skimming pay for two hundred men who don't exist. And half the seven hundred haven't been paid in three months. The Queen's ministers say the war in the south eats the treasury. Meanwhile the granaries are half-empty because the tithe goes south too, and the price of bread in Greyhollow doubled before midwinter." She spat into the fire. "Captain Rennick keeps order because the men fear him more than they hate hunger. Barely."

"Why come to us?" Isolde asked, sharp as ever. "Desertion's a hanging. Why hang faster?"

Brakka looked past her, at Sera. At the boy curled against her. Something moved in her scarred face — not reverence, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman seeing a story her grandmother told her put on flesh.

"Because yesterday the whole garrison saw the beacon," she said.

"What beacon?" Ferro asked. "There's no beacon."

"The old beacon on Wolfrest Tor. Dead three hundred years. Lit at moonrise, burned all night, blue-white flame, no fuel anyone could find." Brakka's voice dropped. "Rennick sent a patrol up. They came back saying the brazier was full of snow and cold as a grave, but the hillside was covered in footprints going *up* and none coming down. And every man in the garrison knows what the beacons meant. The old songs. *When the mountain lights, the mountain wakes.* Half the barracks sang it as children." She met my eyes. "Paloma Blackwood. I don't know what you woke up down there. But I know that when we marched out this morning to hunt you, my squad walked slow. Very slow. And when the others weren't looking, we stopped walking altogether."

Silence around the fire. Somewhere out in the dark, a wolf sang — actually sang, long and clear, and I noticed nobody reached for a weapon, and I noticed Sera almost smile.

"How many others feel the same?" I asked.

"In Greyhollow?" Brakka shrugged, vast and weary. "Ask the bread queues. Ask the families of the twelve miners the Kettlevein company buried in a collapsed shaft last summer because shoring timber costs money and corpses don't complain. Ask the weavers whose looms the Crown taxed into idleness. Captain Rennick holds seven hundred men because they're fed and afraid. Starve them and scare them both a little more and—" she made a gesture, tipping her hand, spilling something invisible.

Isolde and I exchanged a look over the fire. Years of smuggling together teaches you a whole conversation in a glance. Hers said: *this is bigger than us.* Mine said: *it was always going to be.*

* * *

We marched at first light, and I finally understood what we were.

Not an army. Armies have banners, and we had a bedsheet Ferro had nailed to a spear with a wolf's crown daubed on it in lamp-black by the charcoal-burner's daughter, who turned out to have a deft hand and no fear whatsoever. Not a rebellion, either — rebellions have demands, and ours was simpler and stranger: *look. Look what's true. Now decide what you are.*

A procession. A witness walking. Thirty became forty at the Kettlevein turnoff, where the pit-foreman came out with sixty miners who'd walked off shift without permission, still wearing their lamps, so that our column glittered along its flanks like a vein of ore learning to walk. Forty became ninety at the river, where the ferryman brought not just himself but every boat-hand and net-mender in the village, and the fisherwomen sang something low and old as they crossed, and Maren, riding in the cart, wept openly and told me it was Thornic, actual Thornic, a road-blessing from before the Crown, preserved in a fishing song like a fly in amber.

By evening of the second day, standing on the last ridge above the Greyhollow valley, I looked back down our own trail and saw torches. Not pursuing us. *Joining* us. Strings of them, coming up out of the valleys and the pit-villages and the hams along the king's road, converging on our column the way streams converge on a river, and the sound that came up the slope behind us was the sound of hundreds of feet and low talking and here and there a snatch of song, and it did not sound like an army mustering. It sounded like a household getting ready for a funeral. Or a wedding. With crowds like ours it could go either way.

The boy stood next to me at the ridge-top. He'd grown somehow, these two weeks — not in body, in *stance*, shoulders settling, chin level, as if the weight he carried had finally distributed itself evenly through his bones. He pointed down at Greyhollow: the provincial capital, walls and roofs and smoke under a bruised sunset, the citadel on its rock with the Queen's banner hanging motionless above it.

Then he pointed at the citadel, and then at Sera, and then he pressed his small fist against his own chest, and his eyes went amber, and for the first time in days he made a sound that wasn't a word but wasn't nothing either — a note, low, that the wolves on the far slopes answered, all of them at once.

"He says they're waiting," Sera translated quietly, beside us. "All of them. Down there. The ones who know. They're waiting to see which way we walk."

"Which way are we walking, then?" I asked her.

She looked at Greyhollow a long moment, and I saw reflected in her ancient face every wall that had ever been built against the truth, and every one that had ever come down.

"Straight at it," she said. "But unarmed. Let them aim at open hands and learn what it costs them."

Below us in the valley, torchlight was kindling along Greyhollow's walls — watch-fires, alarm-beacons, the garrison turning out. A horn blew, thin and hard in the cold air. Then another. From the citadel, riders spilled from the gate, a dozen of them, galloping hard up the valley road toward us with a herald's banner flying white above their spears.

"Parley," Isolde said, materializing at my elbow with her spyglass already up. "Or a trap dressed as one. Either way — they're coming to talk to *you*, Paloma. Not her." She lowered the glass and gave me a look I knew well, the look she saved for the moments before a door opened onto something with teeth. "The Queen's seal is on that banner. Whatever they're carrying, it comes straight from Greyhollow's throne room."

I straightened my collar, spat on my hands, and put on the face I'd worn into a hundred negotiations with people holding all the power and none of the sense.

"Well," I said, as the hoofbeats came thundering up the ridge toward us, and the column behind me went silent as a held breath, "let's hear what a frightened crown thinks we're worth."
