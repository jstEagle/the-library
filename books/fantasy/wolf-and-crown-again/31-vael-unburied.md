## 31. Vael Unburied

I have died before. Twice, if you count the fever that nearly took me at fourteen, and once more properly, I think, when the mountain came down on my head. The third death was the worst of them, because it didn't take. It left me in the dark with all my sins, and they glowed down there like lanterns.

Let me tell you how it happened, because the court version will be different. The court version will say Inquisitor Vael was buried by traitors in a coward's ambush beneath Thornfell. The truth is that I ordered the storming of a vault older than my queen's dynasty, and the vault objected.

The door had opened onto darkness that breathed. Cold air, snow-smell, iron. And then torchlight on ice-glass, and shapes inside it—tall shapes, crowned shapes—and I understood in one white instant that everything I believed was true, which is the most dangerous thing that can happen to an honest man.

Then the mountain shrugged.

Not collapsed. Shrugged. As though some vast shoulder under the stone rolled over in its sleep and dislodged us like fleas. The last thing I saw clearly was Paloma Blackwood's face, lit from below, wearing an expression I will spend the rest of my life trying to forget: not triumph. Pity. He pitied me, standing in the mouth of the truth I'd spent twenty years denying, and he pitied me, and then ten thousand tons of Thornfell came down between us like a curtain falling on the only play that mattered.

* * *

Waking in the dark is a craft. Most men panic; panic spends air. I lay still and inventoried. Left leg, broken, certainly—the pain had a shape I recognized from a horse accident years ago. Ribs, cracked, two or three. Skull, bleeding but sealed. Air: moving, faintly, which meant somewhere above or beside me the mountain had left a throat open to the sky.

I talked to myself down there. Not prayers—I'd stopped praying around the time I started reading interrogation transcripts for pleasure—but arguments. I laid out my case against the dark the way I'd lay out a case against a prisoner. *You are Inquisitor Dain Vael. You have served the Crown of Aurelian blood for nineteen years. You have broken forty-one heresies and buried three of them so deep even the archivists flinched. You are not permitted to die in a hole with an unverified report.*

The dark did not care for my case. The dark heard a thousand better ones, from better men, and kept them all.

Time went strange. I drank meltwater that dripped from a ceiling I couldn't see. I ate—gods forgive me—a warden's ration biscuit I found in the pocket of a man whose hand I was holding at the time, because his hand was the only warm thing in the world and I couldn't make myself let go of it until I'd taken what I needed. I told myself it was practicality. Down there, in the dark, practicality and cannibalism are neighbors who share a fence, and you learn not to look too hard at the boundary.

I sang. That surprised me. Old Thornic counting songs, the kind the road-wardens' children used to sing before the Crown made the roads safe and the singing unnecessary. My mother's voice, teaching me numbers with dead kings' words. I hadn't thought of those songs in thirty years, and down in the tomb of the people whose language they were, I sang them like a child asking the dark not to eat me.

Somewhere in the fourth day—or the fourteenth; the dark keeps its own books—I stopped being afraid of the Wolf-Kings and started being afraid of something worse: that I had been wrong. Not about their existence. About everything else. About which side of history I had been holding shut.

* * *

They dug me out on the ninth day. I know because Sergeant Corren told me, and Corren does not lie, mostly because he lacks the imagination.

Six wardens with shovels and one with a rope, working off Tam's bragging and Isolde's letter and my own last known position, triangulated to the vault mouth and found it half-buried under a fall no explosion could account for. They said later they expected corpses. What they found instead was a hand sticking out of the rubble, fingers curled, and when Corren grabbed it, the hand gripped back.

"Sir," he said, when they'd pulled me into the grey daylight, "sir, we thought you were dead."

"I was," I said. My voice sounded like gravel being sifted. "I've since reconsidered."

They wrapped me in blankets and fed me broth, and I lay in a warden tent looking up at canvas and thinking: *canvas. Sky-colored canvas. I would kill for this tent. I may yet.*

My leg they splinted badly and set properly later. My mind they could do nothing for, because the thing wrong with it wasn't damage. It was knowledge. I had seen the ice-glass coffins. I had seen the crown on the stone table—older than ours, truer than ours, whole where ours was a forgery built from fragments. I had seen the boy's eyes go amber as a lamp, and I had heard, through the grinding of the world, a woman's voice begin to speak after a thousand years of silence, and the first word of it had gone through me like a nail through wet wood:

*"Who."*

Just that. One syllable. A question. The oldest authority in the mountains, waking, and its first act was to ask who had dared.

And I had dared. With warrants. With wardens. With the righteous certainty of a man who believes his crown is the axis the world turns on.

Corren asked me, gently, whether I wanted a priest. I laughed until my ribs screamed, and then I said, "Bring me a horse and a fast escort. There's someone who needs to hear this before anyone else does, and if she hears it from a rumor first, we're all dead."

"From the Queen?" Corren asked.

"From the Queen," I said, and tasted the strangeness of it, like biting foil.

* * *

Greyhollow in winter is a city pretending to be a capital. The provincial seat: grey walls, grey slate roofs, a palace that is really a fortified customs house with delusions. Queen Aurelian had come west herself—everyone said it was to inspect the new road wardens, everyone said it with the particular careful blankness of people discussing a monarch's real motives in code. She was young. Twenty-three. She had been on the throne four years, and in that time she had done nothing unforgivable, which in a queen is either a great achievement or a great delay.

I was carried to her on a litter, because I refused to meet her sitting down and my leg refused to let me stand. Compromise: I stood, leaning on Corren's shoulder, in the long audience chamber with its banners of the broken-crown sigil, and I waited while the chamberlain announced me, and I felt every eye in the room cataloguing my state—filthy, half-starved, one leg bound in splints, eyes sunken like a man who has argued with the dark and lost on points.

She dismissed the court. All of them. Even the chamberlain. Even, after a moment's hesitation, her own lady-in-waiting. Then it was the two of us and the banners and the fire, and the young Queen of the Aurelian line looked at her Inquisitor and said, "Report."

So I gave it to her straight, because she deserved that much and because I no longer had any appetite for the other thing.

"The Wolf-Kings live," I said. "Or slept, and now wake. They are preserved beneath Thornfell in glass older than any craft we possess. There is a child—a vessel—who carries their memory. The smuggler Blackwood has taken him to the vault. The vault opened for the child's song. I saw the first king wake. She spoke." I paused, because the next part required the pause. "Your Majesty, the crown on your head was forged from hers. Broken and reforged. The founding of your house rests on a massacre your own archives were built to bury. I have seen the murals. I have stood in the chapel where they painted it before anyone thought to paint over it. Your crown is stolen goods."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked, the building settling, indifferent.

Aurelian did not scream. She did not call for my arrest, though I watched her consider it—you could see the thought move behind her eyes like a fish under ice. She was quiet for a long time, and when she finally spoke, her voice was level and very slightly too fast, the way voices get when the speaker is holding something down.

"You saw this yourself."

"I saw it myself."

"And the others? Blackwood, the woman, the archivist?"

"Alive, as far as I know. Buried on the far side of the fall, or escaped through the deep tunnels. I couldn't say which."

"And the king. The one who woke. Describe her."

I described her. Tall, silver-grey, eyes like banked coals, a face that had been beautiful the way winter is beautiful. I described how she had looked at us—not at me, at the boy, at Blackwood—with an expression I could only call *assessment*, and how she had asked her one-word question, and how the boy had answered in that old tongue, fluently, terribly, a child's mouth full of a nation's memory.

When I finished, the Queen walked to the window. Greyhollow's roofs ran grey to the mountains, and beyond them Thornfell stood white and enormous, catching the low sun like a blade laid on a table.

"My father told me our crown was forged in victory," she said, without turning. "He told me the story of the Wolf-War the way you tell children about weather. Long ago. Necessary. Over." A breath. "He never told me why the archives have no records from the first hundred years. He never told me why the old shrine roads all terminate at bricked-up doors. I used to think it was neglect."

"It was policy, Majesty."

"Yes," she said. "I'd worked that out in the last minute, thank you, Vael."

There it was. Panic—not screaming, not running, but the true kind, the kind at the heart of power, which looks exactly like stillness. I knew it well; I'd put it into enough prisoners. She was doing the arithmetic I had done in the dark: *if this is true, then everything is conditional.* Her throne, her law, her right to send wardens and collect taxes and sign death warrants—all of it resting on a foundation that a woman under a mountain could dissolve with a sentence.

"What do you recommend?" she asked me.

And here is where I should tell you what I recommended, and here is where I confess that I hardly know anymore what I did recommend, because the man who stood in that chamber was not entirely the man who had entered the mountain. Nine days in the dark rearrange a person. I had gone in certain and come out with certainty's corpse in my arms.

"Destroy the evidence," I heard myself say, "and you confirm it forever. Every brick over every door, every burned archive, every silenced witness—they become proof. She is proof. You cannot arrest a thousand-year-old king out of existence." I shifted my weight off the bad leg and made myself finish. "Meet her. Before she comes to you. Parley, not pardon—pardon implies she needs one. And whatever you do, do not send soldiers into those tunnels again. The mountain has opinions, and it has just expressed one."

She turned from the window then, and looked at me, and I saw the choice arrive in her face like weather. She was decent—that was always the trouble with Aurelian; her council complained she was too decent, too slow to hang, too fond of asking questions before signing orders. Decency, faced with the end of absolute power, is not a shield. It's just a slower knife.

"Parley," she repeated. "At the pass, I suppose. Where she can bring her smugglers and I can bring my army, and we can both pretend this is diplomacy."

"Majesty—"

"Go and be healed, Inquisitor," she said. "You'll ride with me to Thornfell Gate when I summon you. I find I want my one honest witness where I can see him." A small, terrible smile. "Assuming you're still honest. Nine days in a tomb changes a man."

"Nine days in a tomb clarified a man," I said. "It's the daylight I'm less sure about now."

She almost laughed. Almost. Then the chamberlain was admitted, and the court flowed back in like water finding its level, and the Queen of the Aurelian line became smooth and remote and unreadable again, asking after my health in a voice designed for it, while behind her eyes I watched the panic pace its cell, patient, calculating, waiting to see whether it would be fed with fire or with truth.

* * *

That night I could not sleep, so I sat by my sickroom window and looked at the mountain, and I wrote in my report-book the things I could not say in the audience chamber. *The crown is stolen. The guild was founded to guard the secret and forgot, and then remembered, and the remembering cost them a drunkard's life and a woman's sister and nine days of my own. The child speaks for the dead. The dead speak reasonably. This is the worst part: she woke and asked 'who,' and when she learns the answer, she would be justified in anything, and instead I believe—gods help me, I believe she will ask for something smaller. Something worse. Something like acknowledgment.*

Below, in the courtyard, a courier mounted hard and rode east through the gates, carrying dispatches I had not authorized and could not stop. Word travels faster than shame. By the time the Queen reached Thornfell Gate, half the province would have heard some version of the tale—the Wolf-Kings rise, the smugglers found a god under the mountain, the Inquisitor was buried alive and came out speaking treason.

Half-mad, they called me. Let them. Madness is only prophecy that arrived early.

I closed the book. Out past the walls, Thornfell held the last light, white and silent and patient as a debt, and somewhere inside it a woman with a thousand years of grievance was deciding what to ask of us, and a tired smuggler was deciding what to answer for us, and a child with amber eyes was learning to say words like *covenant* and *witness* in a language that had been murdered and had gotten back up.

I had spent my life keeping doors shut. Now every door I had ever locked was opening at once, and I was going to have to stand at one of them—at the pass, before both armies—and testify.

The Queen would summon me within the week. I could feel it coming the way you feel a storm in a splinted bone.

When she did, I would ride to Thornfell Gate, and I would tell the truth in front of gods and soldiers, and pray to whichever of the sleeping kings was listening that truth would be weapon enough—because if it wasn't, the alternative was already mustering in the grey dawn below my window, and it wore the royal colors, and it was very large.
