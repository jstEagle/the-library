## 32. The Queen's Fear

They tell you, when you are nine years old and the crown is set on your head for the first time—too heavy, always too heavy, a circlet sized for your father's skull and padded with wool so it won't slip over your eyes—that the weight you feel is duty.

Nobody tells you it might be guilt. Nobody tells you because nobody knows. That is rather the point of a founding myth: it is a story told by the winners to the children of the winners until even the winners' children cannot remember there was ever another ending.

I am Aurelian, third of my name, Queen of the Vale and the Lowlands and—by ink on parchment no one has read in two hundred years—the Thornfell Marches. I was crowned at nine, widowed at twenty-two, and ruling alone ever since, which means I have spent more of my life listening to men explain things they do not understand than I have spent sleeping. I mention this not for sympathy but as evidence. Evidence matters now. It is all that matters now, since Vael came stumbling out of the mountains looking like a man who had seen the bottom of the world and been asked to describe it to his creditors.

* * *

He arrived at Greyhollow three days ago, dug out of a collapsed vault by his own wardens after being presumed dead, half his face scalded by frost and stone-dust, his left hand bandaged to the shape of a club. He would not see the physicians. He would not eat. He stood in my audience chamber—still in his ruined grey coat, still shedding mountain grit onto my grandmother's carpet—and asked to speak with me privately, and when the chamber had emptied he knelt, which Vael does not do, and said:

"Your Majesty. The Wolf-Kings live."

And then, because he is Vael and thorough even in ruin, he told me everything. The smugglers. The child. The tunnel older than the guild. The door that answered a song. The figures in ice-glass. And the crown—the true crown of Thornfell—sitting on a stone table beneath a thousand years of deliberate silence, while the thing on my own head was forged from its melted gold by men who had just finished slaughtering its owners.

I did not scream. I want that noted somewhere, by someone, eventually, if there is ever an honest historian: I did not scream. I thanked him for his service. I ordered the vault sealed and garrisoned, doubled the watch on every pass, and dismissed him to the infirmary with a promotion he will never live long enough to enjoy, because I have read enough history to know what happens to men who bring kings bad news twice.

Then I went back to my chambers, locked the door, took off the crown, and sat on the floor holding it like a skull, and I did not sleep.

* * *

Here is what I knew before Vael opened his mouth. Here is what every monarch knows and no monarch says:

Our histories of the Wolf-War run to eleven volumes. They are beautifully bound. They contain maps, battle orders, correspondence between my ancestor Cassian the Unifier and his generals. Eleven volumes, and not one line about what happened to the Thornic people afterward. Not one grave listed. Not one census. The war ends with Cassian's coronation and the next page begins the tax reforms, as though a thousand-year-old kingdom had simply been absorbed into ours the way one absorbs a debt or a wife.

When I was nineteen, still a princess, still allowed curiosity, I asked the Archivist Royal why the histories stopped where they stopped. He looked at me with something I mistook at the time for boredom and now recognize as fear, and said, "The relevant records were consolidated, Your Highness." Consolidated. I have since learned that "consolidated" is what archivists say when they mean burned, and "burned" is what they say when they mean worse.

So no. I was not surprised. That is the worst part—the part I cannot put down, the stone I keep turning over in the dark hours. Vael told me my crown was stolen and my throne built on a massacre, and some cold clerk behind my ribs checked the ledger and found nothing there contradicted it. I have known since I was nineteen that there was an empty shelf in our past shaped exactly like this. I chose not to look at it, because looking would have required deciding what to do, and I was busy. There were harvests. There were border disputes. There was a husband dying slowly and a council full of vultures timing their descent. A queen can be busy her whole life. It is the most respectable form of cowardice ever devised.

* * *

My council met within the day, of course. News travels faster than sense.

Lord Chancellor Hesperin arrived first, as he arrives everywhere first, a soft grey man with soft grey hands who has served the Crown for forty years and served himself for longer. Behind him came Marshal Duvane, whose neck is thicker than her principles, and old Bishop Corvel, who believes in God the way other men believe in weather: generally, and only when it inconveniences someone else.

Hesperin did not ask if the report was true. Mark that. Four decades at the right hand of power, and his first question was not *is it true* but *who else knows*. I counted the questions as they came, because counting kept me calm:

Who else knows? (Vael. The wardens who dug him out. Perhaps the smugglers.)

How many wardens? (Eleven. All sworn.)

Can they be sworn deeper? (A pause. From Hesperin, a pause is a paragraph.)

What remains in the vault? (The kings. The crown. The murals. The child.)

And then Hesperin folded his soft hands and said the sentence I had been waiting for the way you wait for a tooth to finally crack:

"Your Majesty, these difficulties resolve themselves, given sufficient resolve."

Meaning: burn it. Burn them. Fire in the vault, fire in the passes, a fever that carries off a mute child in the night, an accident on the ice for the old woman archivist, and a quiet pension for anyone left standing who needs one. We have done it before, his eyes said. We did it once so thoroughly that you, the heir of the men who did it, do not even know we did it. Give us a week and your crown will be honest again—or rather, it will be dishonest in the traditional manner, which is all anyone has ever required of it.

Marshal Duvane wanted to know how many soldiers the Wolf-Kings could raise. Bishop Corvel wanted to know whether dead kings could be excommunicated posthumously, which I believe was a genuine theological question and which I declined to answer, mostly because the answer—"they were never in our church to begin with; we are the ones trespassing"—was already forming itself in my mouth and would have ended the meeting.

I adjourned without deciding. I watched relief move through the room like oil through water—they read adjournment as assent, the way clever servants always do—and when the doors closed I sat alone with the map of my kingdom and looked at Thornfell, that ragged grey spine across the north of it, and thought: *you have been in my kingdom the whole time. You have been the wound under the map, and I inherited the scar tissue and called it skin.*

* * *

That night I did something no one advised and no one forbade, because no one imagined it. I sent for the archives. Not the public histories—the deep collection, the sealed shelves under the Archivist Royal's keys, the ones I had been formally forbidden from reading at nineteen and informally forbidden ever since by everyone's careful silence.

I went myself, past midnight, with one candle and no escort, because a queen asking questions through intermediaries gets answers curated by whoever loves her least, and I wanted whatever raw thing was down there.

The Archivist Royal is a young woman named Sef, sharp-faced and insomniac, who was clearly expecting anything except the actual Queen descending her stairs in a plain cloak. She recovered well. She said, "Your Majesty," and then, when I held up my hand, "the relevant materials are—" and I said, "Consolidated. Yes. Show me what survived the consolidation."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she lit lamps I suspect had not been lit in a generation, and she showed me.

There was less than there should have been, and the less was damning. A supply requisition from the war's last winter: grain and salt for the Thornic settlements, cancelled by order of the Crown, the cancellation annotated in Cassian's own hand—*no further provision necessary.* A garrison roster showing four thousand soldiers stationed in the high valleys for three years after the fighting ended, against no enemy. A letter, fragmentary, water-stained, from a captain named Aldric Vos to his brother in the lowlands, and I will carry the legible portion of that letter until I die:

*They tell us it was plague. Brother, I have seen plague. Plague does not fill a valley with pikes planted upright in rows, one for each of the dead, and leave the pikes standing. Whoever comes after us should know it was not plague.*

Whoever comes after us. I am whoever comes after us. Nine hundred years late, holding a candle in a basement, I am the recipient of that letter, and the sender is dust, and the valley of pikes is a footnote that was consolidated into nothing, and the crown on my head—

The crown on my head is made of theirs. Melted down, Hesperin's predecessors hammering the shame into a shape pretty enough to wear, so that every coronation for a millennium has been a reenactment of the theft, witnessed and applauded, the original crime recast as sacrament. Every oath I have sworn on that circlet has been an oath sworn on evidence. I have ruled for thirty-one years with a confession on my forehead and called it glory.

I sat on the archive floor until my candle drowned in its own wax. Sef brought me a second one without being asked, set it down beside me, and said nothing, which was the single most intelligent act of political judgment I have seen from any servant of the Crown this year. Then she sat down on the floor too, at a respectful distance, and waited, and eventually she said, very quietly:

"My grandmother was Thornic, Your Majesty. Nobody here knows that either."

* * *

In the morning I summoned them again, and this time I decided.

Or—I want to be honest in at least one place, and this chapter of my life may be the only one that survives—this time I arranged the room so that the decision would make itself. Because here is the truth about corruption, the thing they do not teach you in the histories, the thing I understood standing in that archive among the ashes of other people's honesty: corruption rarely arrives as temptation. It arrives as arithmetic. It arrives wearing your own voice, saying *yes, the crime was real, and yes, it was terrible, and yes, someone must pay for it eventually—but must it be now, and must it be you, and must the payment come due on your watch, with your name on the ledger? Destroy the evidence and the crime stops existing; preserve it and the crime becomes yours. What kind of ruler chooses to inherit a sin she did not commit, when burning one document frees ten thousand people from grief?*

It is a beautiful argument. It is almost airtight. It has persuaded better people than Hesperin, and Hesperin is not a good person, merely a persuasive instrument that good people built and forgot to unstring.

But the argument has a hole in it, and the hole has a name, and the name is *witnesses*. The Wolf-King sleeps below the mountain whether I seal the door or melt it shut. The boy walks and sings and carries a thousand years of memory in a skull small enough to hold in two hands. Paloma Blackwood—whoever he is, some smuggler with a guild of four and a grudge—stands at a pass with an army gathering around him, ready to say aloud what Captain Vos scratched into a letter nine centuries ago. The truth is already out of the vault. Burning documents now would not erase it; it would merely ensure that when it emerged—and it would emerge, truths of this size always emerge, usually at the least convenient moment available—it emerged wearing my fingerprints on the cover-up instead of my ancestors' fingerprints on the crime.

There are two ways for a dynasty to end. One is in blood. The other is in a signed confession and a renegotiated covenant, and only one of those leaves anything standing afterward.

So I chose—not the good choice, I am not pretending to virtue, but the survivable one. I ordered the vault sealed and *garrisoned*, yes, but garrisoned to protect it, with written instructions that the contents be preserved and that no harm come to the sleepers, sealed under my private seal and countersigned by Sef, whom I trust precisely because she has something to lose. I ordered Vael confined to quarters—not arrested, not yet, because arresting him admits the report is credible, and credibility is currency I am not yet ready to spend. And I sent a rider north under a flag of parley, inviting Paloma Blackwood and his companions to meet me at Thornfell Gate, under truce, to discuss terms.

Terms. As though I were negotiating with a foreign power. Which, I suppose, I am—the past turns out to be a foreign country after all, and it has ambassadors, and one of them is nine years old.

* * *

Hesperin came to me privately after the council dispersed, and he was gentle, which frightened me more than any threat could have. Gentle is what he uses when the knives are for later.

"Your Majesty has been through a shock," he said. "These old stories unsettle us all. But consider what is actually at stake. The realm holds. Three million souls eat bread because the Crown keeps the peace. If the Crown falls—if its legitimacy dissolves—you will not get justice for the Thornic dead. You will get forty years of civil war, and their grandchildren will die in it alongside yours. Is that what they would have wanted?"

And there it was. The arithmetic again, dressed in concern, and the worst of it—the reason I lay awake again that night staring at the canopy—is that he is not entirely wrong. I have read the chronicles of the Succession Wars. I know what a realm looks like when the story holding it together fails: provinces at each other's throats, granaries burned, a generation of boys fed to ditches. Justice delayed nine hundred years versus suffering delivered immediately to people who did nothing. Any decent ruler weighs that. Only a corrupt one finds the scale always tips toward her own convenience, and the difference between us, Hesperin and I, is narrower than either of us would like, and both of us know it.

"You are advising me," I said, "to commit the same crime a second time, in order to avoid paying for the first."

"I am advising Your Majesty to govern," he said. "Governing is choosing which sins are affordable."

"And who pays for them."

"That," said Hesperin, with the first honest smile I have ever seen on him, "has never been the sovereign's problem."

I dismissed him. I sat with the crown in my lap again—I have taken to doing that, some grim new habit, weighing it nightly like a merchant weighing suspect coin—and I thought about the girl in the archive saying *my grandmother was Thornic,* and about Captain Vos and his pikes in the valley, and about a mute child I have never met who is currently, at this moment, carrying my kingdom's conscience around the frozen passes in a body that needs feeding.

Here is what I have concluded, alone, at night, with the weight of nine centuries balanced on a wool-padded circlet: the crime was real. The cover-up was real. My throne stands on both. None of that is mine—but the choice now is mine, wholly mine, and I find that I would rather kneel to the truth while my knees still work than be dragged down by it later in front of a larger crowd.

I am going to the pass. I am going to offer Paloma Blackwood everything—pardon, restoration, wealth, a seat at my right hand—and if he takes it, perhaps the old bones can stay asleep and the old crime can stay a secret and the realm can hold together a little longer, patched, imperfect, lying, alive.

And if he refuses—if he stands up in front of both armies and says the words, *your crown is stolen, your kingdom is founded on a graveyard*—then I will have to decide, in front of everyone, in real time, with no adjournment possible:

Whether I am my ancestors' daughter.

Or their repentance.

The trumpets will sound at dawn tomorrow. I have laid out the white cloak, the one I wore for my coronation, because whatever happens at Thornfell Gate, I intend to arrive dressed for judgment—mine or theirs, I no longer pretend to know whose.

God help me, I think it might be mine.
