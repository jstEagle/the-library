## 33. Parley at the Pass

They gave us three days to reach Greyhollow, and I used two of them, because a smuggler who arrives early looks eager and a smuggler who arrives on time looks like he owns the place.

We came down out of the high passes with better than four hundred souls behind us—miners from the Kettlevein pits with their faces still black from the last shift they'd ever work for the Crown's coin, road-wardens gone gray in the queen's service until they'd seen what her service actually cost, smugglers of every stripe and vintage, including three of my old brothers-in-trade who'd sworn oaths to me over campfires that would have made Maren blush if she could have heard them. She couldn't. Maren was back at Thornfell with Tam's grave and the guild's dead, keeping the charts and the fire, because somebody had to be the memory, and she'd told me once that memory was heavier than any pack I'd ever carried. She carried it anyway. That was Maren.

The boy rode on Ferro's shoulders most of the way, which is to say Ferro pretended to grumble about it and the boy pretended not to notice, and both of them were lying, and everyone knew it, and that is more or less how families work.

And Sera walked.

I want you to understand what that looked like. Here was a woman who had ruled mountains when my people were learning which end of a flint to hold, a king whose name had been scraped off every stone in three kingdoms, and she walked at the rear of our column like any pilgrim on the road, bareheaded in the wind, stopping sometimes to look at things—a frozen waterfall, a shrine with its wolf-carvings chiseled away but the shapes of the chisel marks still legible, a child selling roasted chestnuts who stared up at her with his mouth full. She bought chestnuts. She counted out the coins wrong, because coinage had changed six times since her day, and the child patiently corrected her, and Sera of Thornfell, terror of the old world, learned how many coppers made a copper's worth.

Isolde watched all of this from beside me with an expression I couldn't read, which from Isolde was usual, except that usually I *could* read her and chose not to admit it.

"You keep looking at her like she's going to dissolve," I said.

"I keep looking at her like she's the reason my sister is in a Crown cell," Isolde said. "Or the reason she'll get out. Depending on the next month."

"That's fair."

"Nothing about this is fair, Paloma. That's rather the point." She adjusted the strap of her crossbow, which she did when she was thinking, which she did constantly, which meant the strap had been adjusted approximately nine thousand times since the vault. "Vael dug himself out yet?"

"Word came this morning. His own wardens pulled him from under the rockfall. Half-buried, half-mad, fully alive." I turned a coin across my knuckles, an old habit, the guild's oldest prayer. "He reported to Greyhollow by fast rider. Which means the queen has known for—" I did the arithmetic, didn't like it, stopped doing it. "A while."

"And still she offers parley instead of arrows."

"Still she offers parley," I agreed.

Ferro, walking behind us with a sleeping boy on his shoulders, rumbled without opening his eyes: "Because she's scared."

"She should be," Isolde said.

"No." Ferro opened his eyes. "Scared queens burn villages. This one's asking to talk. That's not fear. That's a woman standing at a door deciding whether to open it or board it shut." He shrugged, gently, so as not to wake the boy. "I've stood at that door. You always think boarding it shut is safer. It never is. It just means you're in the dark with whatever you were afraid of, and now nobody can see you either."

I looked at him. Big Ferro, who had spent twenty years arguing that feelings were weather—real, unavoidable, and best endured in silence—and here he was handing me philosophy like it was nothing.

"The war changed you," I said.

"The war hasn't happened yet," Ferro said. "That's what scares me."

* * *

Greyhollow rose out of the valley fog like a jaw of yellow stone, all towers and terraces and the great crown-spire of the provincial seat where the queen's justice was dispensed in the morning and regretted by evening. We made no move on it. That wasn't the plan and wouldn't have worked anyway; four hundred angry people are a mob, and mobs die against walls. But we took the pass above the city—the Thornfell Gate itself, the old gate, the one carved with wolves that some pious mason a thousand years ago had tried to deface and had only managed to give them expressions—and we camped there in the ruins of the old tollhouse, which had been mine, once, in another life, before everything.

Sera stood under the gate arch a long time that first evening, reading the defaced carvings with her fingertips.

"They were good masons," she said. "My brother's work, some of this. He signed his stones with a little badger. He thought it was funny. A badger, guarding the wolf-gate." Her voice was level. Her hands were fists. "They left the badger. It's still there, look. They hated us enough to erase us and they kept his joke."

"Sera—"

"They kept the joke, Paloma." She said it very quietly, the way you say a thing you cannot say loudly without screaming. Then she let go of the stone, and her hands were hands again, and she asked me what the chestnut child had been called, and whether he'd be warm enough tonight, and we went down to the fires.

* * *

The queen's herald came up the pass at midmorning under a banner of truce, riding a white horse that clearly wished it were anywhere else. He was young, polished, and terrified in the specific way of a man who has been told exactly one thing by his sovereign and suspects it isn't the whole truth: *Ride to the gate. Tell the smuggler the queen will meet him at the boundary stone at dusk. No escort beyond ten. He may bring ten.*

Ten and ten, then, at the old boundary stone between the mountain law and the valley law—a flat granite slab older than either, older probably than the argument it arbitrated, sitting in the middle of the pass road like a table waiting for guests.

I picked my ten the way you pick a hand of cards you expect to lose with: Ferro for the obvious reasons. Isolde, because if this went wrong I wanted the best shot in the company standing somewhere with sightlines. The boy, because leaving him behind felt like leaving my own heartbeat in a tent, and because—though I didn't say this to anyone—the boy was the whole reason for the meeting whether anyone acknowledged it or not. Sera, obviously. And six others whose names matter to this story mostly by their absence from later parts of it, so I'll spare them.

Dusk came down purple and cold. The queen's party arrived precisely on time, which told me something; kings and queens are late on purpose, to make you wait, to make you feel small. Aurelian arrived on time because she wanted this conversation to happen between equals or wanted it to look that way, and I still don't know which, and I suspect neither does she.

She was younger than I expected. Everyone always says that about her, so let me say it differently: she was younger than the crown she wore, and the crown was heavy, and she wore it anyway, straight-backed, into the wind, at the top of a pass held by four hundred people who wanted her government gone. Twenty-three years old, maybe. She dismounted herself. She waved off the chair someone tried to bring her. She stood at the boundary stone in a plain traveling cloak with just the crown on her head and looked at Sera for a long, long moment.

Then she bowed her head. Not deeply. Not a curtsy. An acknowledgment, soldier to soldier, of something recognized.

"You're real," Aurelian said to Sera. "Vael swore it on his life and I ordered him confined until I could verify, because Vael has been wrong before, and the things he described—" She stopped. Started again. She was good at starting again; I noticed that about her, later, and it mattered. "You're real."

"I am," Sera said. "And you are the one wearing my crown."

The wind moved through the pass. Nobody breathed much.

"It was never called yours," Aurelian said. "In any record. In any chronicle. In the founding charters of my line, the First Crown was forged new, at the coronation of Aldric the Unifier, from gold of the—"

"From gold of the deep vault," Sera said, "which Aldric emptied after he burned my people in their halls. Yes. I know the charter. I wrote the original oath your charter was copied from, girl, word for word, and I noticed every word he stole. Do you want to know what he changed?" She stepped closer, and even I, who had walked beside her for a hundred miles, felt the old weight come off her like heat off a forge. "One word. One. My oath said the crown belongs to the mountain and is *held* by the king. His scribes wrote *owned*. Held and owned. Everything your kingdom is, everything it has done for a thousand years—the roads, the taxes, the gallows, the wars—all of it grew out of one stolen word."

I have heard silences in caves that were less complete than the silence after that.

Aurelian's hands had gone white on the boundary stone. When she spoke, her voice was careful, controlled, and I recognized the care, because I'd used it myself in rooms where losing control meant dying. "If that is true—"

"If," said Isolde, from behind me, softly, with her crossbow not quite casual in her arms.

"If that is true," Aurelian repeated, with a flicker of anger that did her credit, "then I am the thief's heir and my throne is a fence's stall. I have spent my reign being told that my authority flows from Aldric's covenant. You are telling me the covenant is counterfeit."

"Yes," Sera said.

"So what do you want?" And there it was—the question underneath every question, the one she'd ridden up here carrying under that plain cloak. "Revenge? Restoration? Your throne back? Name it. If you've marched four hundred men to my gate, you've come to name a price."

Sera looked at her for a long time. Then she looked at me.

Which was unfair, frankly. I'm the smuggler. I run contraband and dodge wardens and I was standing at a boundary stone holding a hat I didn't even have because I'd lost it in the vault collapse, and the oldest monarch under the sky turns to me like I'm the one with the answer.

But I knew why. Because the boy was mine. Because the choice was mine. Because Sera had already decided what *she* wanted and it wasn't a throne, and the question that remained—the live wire, the loaded spring—was what *I* wanted, and everyone on both sides of that stone knew it.

Queen Aurelian turned to me too.

"Paloma Blackwood," she said, and my name in a queen's mouth sounded strange, like a coin from a country I'd never visited. "Last apprentice of the Thornfell guild. I've read the file. There isn't much of it. You were good." Almost a smile. "Here is what I offer. Full pardon—for you and every soul in your company, retroactive, sealed, unconditional. The guild restored under royal charter, legitimate, protected, the passes yours to keep as wardens with the Crown's blessing and the Crown's gold. Land. A house—I'm told you people collect tollhouses; pick one. And wealth sufficient that your grandchildren will be bored by it." She paused, and her voice gentled, and that was the worst part, because the gentleness was real. "All of it. For one thing."

"Don't," Ferro said quietly.

"For Sera's head," Aurelian said. "Delivered to me, alive or dead, and the past stays buried, the charters stay true, and every man in your company walks free and rich into a kingdom that forgets you were ever anything but loyal."

The wind came down the pass again, and somewhere behind the queen's escort a horse shifted and stamped, and I stood there holding nothing in my empty hands while she offered me everything I had spent my whole ragged life pretending I didn't want.

Pardon. Guild. Home. Gold. Legitimacy—the one thing no Blackwood had ever held, the thing my mother had died landless outside the walls of a town that used her and forgot her, the thing they tell smugglers' children doesn't exist for people like us. All of it. Priced at one head.

The same choice. Again. The ford, the vault mouth, the threshold—every road of my life had led to this exact fork, and every time the offer got bigger and the price stayed the same: the boy, or the old woman, or the truth. Sell the past and buy the future. Hand over what sleeps and go home rich.

I looked at Sera. She met my eyes, and she didn't plead, didn't nod, didn't do anything at all except stand there with the wind pulling at her hair like she was already a monument, already history, already the thing on the mural with its throat cut—and she let the choice be mine, wholly mine, because she was a king and that's what kings do, they put the blade in your hand and step back.

I looked at the boy. He was watching the queen with his head slightly tilted, the way he watched unfamiliar animals, deciding.

"Your Majesty," I said, "can I ask you a question? Off the ledger?"

Her eyebrows moved. "Ask."

"When Vael came to my tollhouse in the winter, hunting the boy—he offered me the same deal. Smaller numbers. Same shape. And I asked his sergeant, afterward, what happens to informants when the Crown gets what it wants." I let that sit. "Do you know what happens to them?"

"Informants become inconvenient," Aurelian said. "Yes. I know."

"And you'd be different."

"I would be different." She said it steadily. She believed it. Gods help me, standing there in the wind, I believed she believed it. "I am not my council. I am not my grandfather. I am trying to hold together a kingdom that is coming apart, Mr. Blackwood, and you are offering me a choice between civil war and a lie. I am asking you to help me choose the lie, because I genuinely believe fewer people will die."

There it was. Not villainy. Arithmetic. The same arithmetic Vael had carried, the same arithmetic every decent person does terrible math with, and it would have been so easy—so *easy*—to take her hand, take the charter, take the gold, tell myself the lie was mercy.

The boy tugged my sleeve.

He looked up at me, and he pointed—not at the queen, not at Sera—at the boundary stone. At the worn old words cut into its face, so ancient and foot-worn that no one reads them anymore. He traced them with his finger, and his lips moved in that other language, the old language, the one that was erased on purpose, and then he looked at me and said, in his small hoard of common words, carefully, like coins counted out:

"Held. Not owned."

Maren's teaching. Or the dream's. Either way, mine now.

I turned back to the queen, and I felt lighter than I had since the blizzard, since the knock at the door, since the beginning of the whole cursed winter. Lighter, and colder, and certain.

"Here's my answer, then," I said. "No."

Something moved through the queen's escort—hands near weapons, horses sensing it—but Aurelian raised one finger without looking, and they stilled.

"Not because I'm a good man. I'm a smuggler; ask anyone. And not because I trust the Wolf-Kings to be kinder masters than the Crown—they might not be, and I'll say that to their face too, and have. I say no because you just offered to buy the truth and sell it back to me as a lie, and I've been poor my whole life, Your Majesty, but I have never once been cheap." I took a breath. The cold air tasted like snow and iron, like the vault, like the beginning of things. "The guild will take your charter, though. Write it up. Wardens of the passes, lawful, chartered—we'll take that gladly. But the crown comes with us, and the truth comes with it, and Sera stands free, and if you want to know what your kingdom is founded on, you'll have to walk down into the dark and look at it yourself. Same as we did."

Aurelian studied me for a long moment. And then, horribly, she almost smiled—the smile of a chess player watching an opponent make a move she hadn't priced in.

"A charter for the guild," she said. "Truth for the crown. And if my council refuses—if they force my hand—"

"Then we're at war," Ferro said, "and you'll lose, because your soldiers can read murals too."

The queen looked at each of us in turn—Sera, the boy, me—and something passed behind her eyes like weather, decision forming and unforming, and she said, quietly, mostly to herself:

"My council will demand I attack. Tomorrow, probably."

"Probably," Isolde agreed cheerfully.

"Then I have until tomorrow," Aurelian said. She mounted, gathered her reins, and paused. "Mr. Blackwood. For what it's worth—I'm glad you're cheap."

And she rode down into the dark toward Greyhollow, toward her council, toward the war that was already assembling itself around her like wolves around a wounded thing, and I stood at the boundary stone with nothing in my hands and everything on them, and behind me Ferro exhaled like a man setting down a mountain.

"Well," he said. "Now we fight."

Above us, on the gate arch, the old carved wolves grinned their thousand-year-old grins, and far below, torches were kindling along the walls of Greyhollow—one, then ten, then dozens, lighting up the dark like the first stars of a very long night.
