## 34. Refusal

The parley field sat between the two hosts like a wound that hadn't decided whether to bleed. On the south side, the queen's army—pikes ranked in the morning frost, banners snapping, ten thousand boots packed into the frozen mud of Greyhollow's western flats. On the north, our ragged thousand: smugglers, miners, deserters, a handful of clan fighters with wolf-tooth charms, and one ancient king wrapped in Ferro's spare coat because she'd complained—actually complained, in a voice like a glacier learning sarcasm—that being dead for a millennium gave a woman a chill.

I walked out alone.

That had been my condition, and to my lasting surprise, they'd both accepted it. Aurelian wanted a spectacle. Vael wanted me close enough to arrest. Sera wanted—well, Sera wanted whatever Sera wanted, and none of us had the standing to ask twice.

The frost crunched under my boots. Somewhere behind me, Ferro muttered a prayer to a god smugglers don't officially believe in, and Isolde said something sharp and quiet that made him snort. The boy wasn't there. Sera had insisted on that. *The vessel does not stand in the path of armies,* she'd said. *That is how vessels break.* So he was back at the pass with Maren, who was still bandaged from the vault and still, against all medical opinion and my own pleading, refusing to die. Stubbornness ran deep in the guild. It was the only inheritance we'd ever been sure of.

Aurelian's pavilion had been struck for the occasion. She stood on the bare ground like she wanted it understood that even her negotiations were conducted without cover. Young—gods, she was young. Twenty-two, maybe. The crown sat on her head the way a yoke sits on an ox, and I remember thinking, with a strange lurch of pity, that she wore it better than it deserved.

Inquisitor Vael stood at her shoulder, grey and neat and freshly dug out of a mountain. There was still stone dust in the seams of his gloves. He looked at me the way a man looks at a debt he intends to collect personally.

"Paloma Blackwood," the herald called, though there was no need. Everyone in both armies knew the smuggler's face by now. Half of them had bought contraband from it.

"Your Majesty," I said, and gave her the smallest bow the law would recognize. "You sent an offer."

"I did." Her voice carried. She'd been trained to project across courtyards, and it worked across battlefields too. "Full pardon. Restoration of the Thornfell guild's charter, with all lands and toll rights. A seat on the provincial council. A house in Greyhollow with a roof that doesn't leak." A ripple of something—not quite laughter—moved through our lines at that. My tollhouse leaked like a confession. "And in exchange, one item. The head of the creature calling itself Sera of Thornfell. Delivered before sundown."

*Creature.* I had to hand it to her council. They'd coached her well. You don't call an ancient king a king when your own crown is made from the wreckage of hers.

I let the silence stretch. Silence is a smuggler's first tool. Fill a pause too fast and men think you're afraid; hold it, and they start wondering what you know that they don't.

"Before I answer," I said, "I'd like to say a few words. To your soldiers. And to mine. And to the ones in the middle who haven't decided whose they are."

Aurelian's jaw tightened. "This is a parley, not a pulpit."

"Then arrest me for preaching." I spread my hands. "You've got ten thousand men and I'm one unarmed smuggler standing in the open. If my voice frightens you, Majesty, that's worth the armies knowing too."

That did it. Pride is a lever even on queens—especially on young ones who've been told all their lives that pride is the same thing as strength. She gestured, curt, and I turned to face the field.

* * *

Ten thousand faces is a lot of faces. I'd like to tell you I wasn't afraid, but I'm a smuggler, not a liar. My mouth was dry as old chalk, and my hands wanted to shake, so I kept them still the way Ferro taught me—*still hands, Lior, still hands, a man with twitchy fingers dies at the ford*—and I looked at the front rank of the queen's line, at a pikeman no older than the boy, and I spoke to him. Just him. The rest could listen in.

"Soldiers of Greyhollow. My name is Paloma Blackwood, and I've spent my whole life on roads your king's charter says don't exist. I've carried salt past your tolls and letters past your censors, and I'm not going to stand here and tell you I'm an honest man. I'm not. I'm a *useful* one, which is different, and today it happens to matter more.

"I'm going to tell you what I saw under Thornfell. Not what I was told. What I *saw*. Because your queen has offered me everything I've ever wanted to buy one head, and I want you to understand what I'm refusing, and why, and then you can decide what kind of army you're standing in."

I heard Vael shift. I heard the creak of ten thousand men leaning forward despite themselves. Curiosity is the one contraband no border ever stopped.

"There is a vault beneath the mountain," I said. "Older than the Crown. Older than any road, any charter, any law your grandfathers' grandfathers swore by. My guild's own founding charter says we were never smugglers at all. We were road-wardens. Sworn to the kings of Thornfell. Sworn to *guard* something—and when the kings fell, we were left guarding the secret instead.

"And what's in that vault is this: the Wolf-Kings themselves. Not bones. Not ghosts. Them. Sleeping, preserved, waiting. And on a stone table in the middle of them, a crown."

I let that sit. Somewhere in the queen's ranks, a man coughed, and the cough sounded enormous.

"Here's what your histories say. That the Wolf-Kings were tyrants. That they warred against the first King of the realm, and lost, and that their crown was broken and their line ended, and that the realm's own crown was forged new, in righteousness, to replace a corrupted thing. I believed that too. I grew up on it. Every child in these mountains grows up on it.

"Now look at what the murals in the hollow chapel show—murals older than your histories, painted by men who had no reason to lie to anyone, because they expected no one to ever see them. They show the Wolf-Kings *surrendering*. Laying down arms. Crowns offered up in peace. And they show men—your founding heroes, the ones on the coins in your pockets—taking a hammer to the Wolf-King's crown and *reforging it*. Not melting it down in shame. Reforging it. Wearing it.

"Your crown is her crown. Bent, hammered, set with new stones, but the same gold, the same make. I've held both. I've *weighed* them." I raised my voice, and it cracked across the frost. "The authority that sits on your queen's head was cut from the skull of the woman standing behind me, after her people were slaughtered under a flag of parley. That's what the vault is. That's what the guild was sworn to hide—not a treasure. A *crime*. A thousand years old, and still warm."

The line didn't move. But the line *breathed*. I could see it, a shudder passing down ten thousand men like wind through wheat, and I knew—I knew the way you know a pass is open before you've walked it—that the murals had done more work in five minutes than our whole ragged army could do in a year.

"The child," I went on, quieter now, because the field was so quiet I could have whispered and been heard at the rear. "You've heard about the child. The one the inquisitors call a stolen vessel. A weapon of the old blood. I've fed him, clothed him, taught him my roads. He is a vessel, that part's true—but not of any weapon. He carries the *memory* of the Wolf-Kings. Everything that was done to them. Everything they saw. He sang a door open that ten generations of my guild couldn't unlock, because the door was never locked against thieves. It was locked against *forgetting*.

"And the king you're calling a creature—she woke, and do you know what she did? She didn't call for vengeance. She didn't raise the dead. She stood unarmed between two armies yesterday and asked to *talk*. I've met a great many powerful people. Your inquisitors. Your governors. I've smuggled for half of them. Not one of them, given her power and her grievances, would have done anything but burn. She asked to talk. Remember that, when they tell you what she is."

I turned back to Aurelian. She had gone very still. Vael had gone very still in a different way, the stillness of a man calculating angles.

"So here is my answer, Majesty, and I give it in front of your army so there's no mistaking it, and no taking it back." I took a breath. "No.

"I won't deliver her head. Not for a guild, not for a pardon, not for a house with a sound roof. And I'll tell you the real reason, the one beneath the reason. It's not loyalty to a king I've known for three weeks. It's this: I've spent my whole life on the wrong side of the law, and I finally understand why. Because the law was built on a lie, and everything that guards the lie—every toll, every warrant, every gallows—is just the lie wearing armor. You're offering to make me honest inside a dishonest thing. I'd rather be a smuggler under a true crown than a warden under a stolen one."

I heard Ferro make a sound behind me, half groan, half cheer, strangled by discipline.

"And one thing more." I looked at the pikeman again. "Soldiers. You have a choice today that your founders never gave the Wolf-Kings. They were given a parley and a massacre. You've been given the truth and a queen who might still choose better. *Might.* I'm not asking you to desert. I'm asking you to remember what I said, and when the order comes—and it will come—ask yourselves whose crown you're pointing those pikes for. Ask where it came from. Ask why the vault had to be sealed for a thousand years if there was nothing down there but a defeated enemy."

I bowed to Aurelian, properly this time, because whatever else was true, she was young, and the day was about to get much worse for her.

"That's my refusal. Sundown's a long way off, but you can keep your gold."

* * *

I walked back. It's strange, the things you notice. I noticed that my knees were steady until the third step and then weren't, and that Isolde fell into step beside me without a word, and that Ferro's hand on my shoulder weighed about as much as a collapsed tunnel.

"You absolute bastard," Ferro said, thick-voiced. "You absolute *magnificent* bastard."

"He gave away the whole thing," Isolde said. "Charter, vault, the crown. Every card."

"He was never holding cards," Ferro said. "He was holding a mirror."

Sera waited at the head of our line, and when I reached her she looked at me for a long moment with eyes the colour of deep ice over deep water.

"You told them everything," she said.

"Everything I saw."

"You told them the crime. You told them the crown's true make. You gave your queen's soldiers permission to doubt her in front of her." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not a smile. The ancestor of a smile. "A thousand years ago, my councillors advised me to keep such knowledge sealed, for the sake of order. My seal bought me a massacre." She turned to face the queen's host, ten thousand strong, and the wind pulled at Ferro's coat on her shoulders. "You spent truth like coin, smuggler. Now we learn what it buys."

Across the field, the queen's council had closed around Aurelian like a fist. I could see it from where I stood—see the chancellor leaning in, see Vael's mouth moving, see the young queen's face going from white to something harder. I'd seen that face before. Not on her. On cornered men deciding they had nothing left to lose.

"Ferro," I said quietly. "Get the clan fighters to the left flank. Isolde, the deserters will waver first—put our steadiest people behind them, not to hold the line. To *catch* it, if it breaks our way."

"And if it breaks the other way?" Isolde asked.

"Then we catch that too," I said. "Best we can."

Down in the queen's ranks, a horn sounded. Not the parley horn.

Sera rolled her shoulders, slow, like a mountain settling, and I heard her say, almost to herself, in that old tongue the boy speaks and she alone remembers:

*One more time, then. But this time, no one kneels to me with a sword at their back.*

The pikes of Greyhollow came down to level, all along the line, with a sound like the mountain itself clearing its throat.

I'd told them the truth. Now we'd find out what it was worth.
