## 38. What the Guild Becomes

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a tollhouse when the tolls stop being collected in secret.

I had spent thirty years perfecting the other kind of silence—the kind you keep so the queen's wardens don't hear your mules on the switchback at moonset, the kind Maren kept when she was reading a ledger she didn't want anyone to know existed, the kind Ferro kept whenever anyone mentioned feelings within earshot of him. Smuggler's silence. It has a texture to it, like felt wrapped around blade-hilts.

This new silence was nothing like that. This was the silence of the Thornfell high road at midmorning, open to the sky, with sunlight pouring down the pass and a caravan of forty wagons rattling through it without a single man looking over his shoulder.

I stood on the old tollhouse porch—*my* porch, or what had been my porch until three months ago, when a boy who couldn't speak and a woman older than the kingdom between them decided that everything I owned belonged to everyone now—and I listened to that silence, and I'll be honest with you: part of me hated it.

Not most of me. Most of me was standing there with my arms crossed watching grain carts and wool bales and one extremely nervous silk merchant from Greyhollow move through the pass lawful as Sunday churchgoers, and most of me was quietly, stupidly proud. But there's always a splinter of the old self left in the wood, isn't there? Mine sounded like this: *Paloma Blackwood, you have become a customs official.*

Gods wept. A customs official. My mother would have come back from whatever afterlife smugglers go to just to spit on my boots.

"You're doing the face again," Isolde said, coming up beside me with two cups of something hot that claimed to be tea and was mostly pine needles and optimism.

"What face?"

"The face where you look like a man chewing a lemon and deciding whether to swallow."

"That's my thinking face."

"No," she said, pressing a cup into my hands, "your thinking face is smaller and more tragic. That's your *mourning* face. You're mourning yourself."

Isolde had been sharp-tongued before her betrayal, before the ford, before Tam died in the shallows buying us eleven seconds with his body and his bottle both, and she'd been sharper since. But she'd also been different in a way that took me weeks to name. She stopped flinching. Whatever price she'd paid Vael, whatever debt her sister's freedom had cost her, she'd walked into our camp at the foot of the mountain and confessed it all to our faces, and then she'd asked for work, and Ferro had grunted—which, from Ferro, was a full pardon and a knighthood combined—and somewhere in the winter that followed, the flinch went out of her. She stood straight now. It suited her badly and I told her so regularly.

"I'm not mourning anything," I said. "I'm adjusting."

"To what?"

"To being respectable. Do you understand what's happened here? Look." I gestured at the road with my cup, slopping pine-needle tea on the railing. "That wagon train came up from Greyhollow, through the low gate, past the checkpoint at Hollow Bridge, over the ford where Tam—" I stopped. The ford still caught in my throat sometimes, like a fishbone. "Past the ford. Nobody paid a bribe. Nobody hid a cargo. There were no false bottoms, no forged seals, no bribed clerks. They just... drove. In daylight. Singing, Isolde. The carter is *singing.*"

"He is off-key."

"He is *lawful,*" I said, "and it's making me itch."

Isolde watched me over the rim of her cup with those grey eyes that missed nothing, which was how she'd managed to betray us so effectively back when betrayal was her trade. "You could leave," she said mildly. "The compact doesn't hold anyone. Sera was very clear. Wardens by choice or not at all."

"And do what? Go back to running brandy through the passes for men who now pay their tariffs honestly?" I snorted. "Half my old contacts are dead, the other half are wardens, and the guild ledger—" I paused, because the guild ledger was its own small grief. "The ledger's in a museum case in Greyhollow. Under glass. Maren visits it every week and stands there like a widow at a headstone."

"Does she?"

"She says she's 'auditing history.' She says it very loudly, so the museum staff get nervous and offer her tea."

Isolde laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, which was still rare enough that I noted it like a good weather sign. Then her face settled into something more careful. "And you? What do you tell the museum staff when they offer you tea?"

"That I don't drink with institutions."

She looked at me a moment longer, the way she did when she was deciding whether to say the true thing or the easy thing. These days she usually said the true thing. It was exhausting. "You're allowed to miss it, Paloma. Who you were. What we were. Nobody thinks less of you for grieving a life, even a crooked one."

I didn't answer right away. Below us, the caravan wound along the high road, and the lead carter's off-key song drifted up the slope—some harvest tune from the valley farms, the kind of song that only gets sung by people who aren't afraid. The last time I'd heard singing on this road, it had been a mute child sleepwalking through a blizzard, singing in a language dead a thousand years, bending hearth-flames toward him like flowers toward a sun that shouldn't exist.

The boy. Right. I suppose I should explain about the boy, since he's rather the hinge this whole story swings on, though these days he's easier to explain than he used to be.

His name is Wren now. He chose it himself, or chose it the way children choose things—by pointing at a bird and refusing to answer to anything else until we gave in. He's seven, maybe eight; Sera said the vessels were never made older than that, because memory needs an unfurnished house. He speaks common speech now, haltingly, with a Thornic accent nobody alive should have, rolling his r's like the mountain itself talking through a child's mouth. He can read. We found that out when Maren left a charter lying open on the table and he read it aloud in the original pre-kingdom script, and Maren dropped her tea, and Ferro swore so comprehensively that Isolde wrote some of it down for future use.

He lives at the tollhouse. With me. Which brings me, in the long way around, to what the guild became, and why I was standing on my own porch mourning myself on a bright spring morning while lawful commerce sang its terrible songs below.

* * *

The Mountain Compact, as dictated by Wolf-King Sera of Thornfell on the second day after the Battle of the Gate, runs to forty-one clauses. I know because Maren transcribed it, then annotated it, then annotated her annotations, and the final version—copied out fair by four scribes, sealed with wax from three clans and the crown and, at Sera's insistence, a thumbprint of mine that I still think was a joke at my expense—hangs in the great hall at Greyhollow behind glass thicker than the museum case that holds my old ledger. Symmetry. History has a cruel sense of humor.

Clause One: the crown renounces absolute authority. Clause Two through Nine: the division of power among crown, guilds, and mountain clans, with courts that answer to all three. Clause Fourteen: the roads of Thornfell opened to free passage, tolls set by council, collected openly. Clause Twenty-Two: amnesty for all crimes committed under the old smuggling economy, provided the perpetrators take up honest labor or wardenship. Clause Thirty-Three: the Wolf-Kings' vault sealed and honored as witness-ground, guarded but never entered save by consent of the living vessel.

Clause Forty-One is short. Maren calls it the clause that makes the rest possible. It reads, in full: *No law of the compact may depend upon forgetting.*

Sera dictated that one last, after a long silence, looking at the boy. I've thought about it often since. A thousand years ago somebody built a kingdom on forgetting—on a massacre buried, a language erased, a crown melted down and recast so that no one would ever ask where the authority in it came from. And here was the last of the kings who'd been slaughtered, awake for a handful of days, and instead of demanding her throne back, she demanded the opposite: that nothing ever be built on not-knowing again. Then she went back to sleep in her ice-glass, leaving a seven-year-old boy and a reformed smuggler to hold the door.

Which is how the Thornfell Wardens came to exist, and how I came to be wearing, gods help me, a badge.

It's not really a badge. It's a wolf-tooth on a cord—old stock, dug out of a warden-barrow by clan hands who knew which barrows were grave-goods and which were armory, polished by Maren until it gleamed. Every warden wears one. When I pinned the first batch of them on shoulders in the great hall—Ferro's, Isolde's, Maren's, a dozen ex-smugglers', a dozen miners', two deserters from Aurelian's own army who'd stood on the wrong side at the Gate and picked the right side halfway through—I thought about the men in the deep road. The original guild. Dead in formation at a sealed door, pikes still gripped, a thousand years on watch because their oath outlasted their flesh.

We weren't them. I want to be clear about that, because Maren keeps writing ballads that suggest otherwise and I keep burning them. Those wardens died guarding a secret. We live keeping an open one. Different trade entirely. Same mountains, same oath-shaped hole in the heart, but the cargo changed: we carry truth now, up and down the passes, and unlike brandy, it spoils if you hoard it.

* * *

The training yard at Hollow Bridge was loud that afternoon, the way yards get when Ferro is teaching and losing patience at a fixed rate.

"Again," Ferro said.

The recruit—a miner's daughter named Brakka, seventeen, forearms like ship cable—picked herself out of the mud for the ninth time and squared up. "You're enjoying this."

"No," Ferro said. "But the river bandits will, if you fall down that easy. Again."

I leaned on the fence and watched. This was another thing I was still adjusting to: Ferro happy. It didn't look like other people's happiness. It looked like Ferro, except with a purpose larger than the bottom of a bottle and a scowl that had softened maybe two degrees, which from Ferro was practically a smile. He'd argued hardest for giving up the boy, back at the beginning—stood in the firelight and said we'd all die for a stranger—and then he'd been the first to step aside at the threshold when it mattered, and then he'd carried Maren three miles through the dark when she was bleeding out from shielding Wren with her own body, and somewhere in all of that the argument had gone out of him and something steadier had moved in.

He'd told me once, drunk on the good wine we confiscated from a tariff-dodging baron—yes, we still catch tariff-dodgers; some things are eternal—that he'd spent his whole life expecting the world to end badly and arranging his affairs accordingly. "Then the world didn't end," he'd said, baffled, as if the world had broken a contract. "Now what am I supposed to do with the wine cellar?"

Teach children to fight river bandits, apparently. The world is strange.

Brakka threw him again—badly, into the mud again—but she got up faster the tenth time than the ninth, and Ferro nodded, which meant the lesson was over, which meant she'd earned dinner. The recruits gathered around him, six of them, mixed as a market-day crowd: miners, a shepherd, one of Aurelian's deserters, and a former colleague of mine from the old days named Doss who'd taken the amnesty and hadn't stopped looking over his shoulder yet. Give him a year. The tooth on the cord takes a year to feel like it belongs to you.

Wren sat on the fence beside me, legs swinging, watching Ferro with the serious attention he gave everything. He'd asked to learn the staff. Ferro had said when he was older. Wren had pointed out—in his careful, accented common, each word placed like a stone in a wall—that Sera had fought wars when she was younger than him. Ferro had said that Sera was a wolf-king and Wren was a boy, and Wren had considered this and replied, "So was she, once," and Ferro had walked away and had a private conversation with a bottle about it.

"They're getting better," Wren said to me now, nodding at the recruits.

"They're getting less bad," I corrected. "Important difference. Keeps expectations honest."

"Maren says honesty is the whole law."

"Maren would say that. She helped write it."

He was quiet a moment, watching the yard, and then he said the thing children say that lands like a thrown knife because they aim without knowing where the soft parts are. "Do you miss being a smuggler?"

I looked at him. His eyes were brown. Mostly. On certain evenings, near dusk, near the vault-road, near certain songs, they flashed amber, and the oldest memory in the world looked out of a child's face, and I remembered that the friend sitting next to me on this fence had once been a sealed vault of a murdered people and was now, by choice and by covenant, just a boy learning his letters. He'd chosen that. Sera had offered him witnesshood forever, relichood, a place of honor behind glass, and he'd said—he'd *told* her, seven years old, staring up at an immortal—"I already did that job. I want to do a different one." Out of the mouths of vessels.

"Some days," I said honestly, because Clause Forty-One applies at kitchen tables too. "Some days I miss it like a tooth. But a missing tooth teaches you about the ones you've got left. Come on. Maren wants us at the archive before dark, and if we're late she'll make us listen to the ballad."

"The sad one or the funny one?"

"There's a funny one?"

"I wrote it," Wren said, sliding off the fence. "It's about you becoming a customs official."

* * *

Maren's archive lived in what used to be a shrine to the Crown's founding saints—the Hollow Chapel, scrubbed clean of its lies, its murals of crowned wolves slaughtered by crowned men left exactly where they were, because Clause Forty-One cuts both ways and forgetting is forgetting even when the forgotten thing deserved better than worship. Now the chapel walls held shelves. Now the altar held ledgers. Now the saints' niches held clay jars of ink and one jar of Ferro's emergency brandy, which everyone pretended not to know about.

Maren sat at the center table with documents fanned around her like a cardsharp's hand, blind eyes aimed at the middle distance, fingers reading a page in raised script one of the clan scribes had pressed for her. She was smiling, which was either good news or catastrophic news.

"You're late," she said. "Both of you. Sit. Listen to this." She held up the page. "Petition from the Greyhollow weavers' guild. They want the compact court to rule on whether the old smuggler trails through the north passes should be mapped publicly or left as commons knowledge, unwritten."

"What did you rule?"

"Nothing yet. It's not mine to rule. It's ours—all five seats. That's rather the point of the compact, dear, do keep up." She turned her face toward me, and her voice did the thing it did when she was about to hand me something heavy wrapped in something light. "But I wanted you to hear the question. Think about what's being asked. For three hundred years, knowing those trails was contraband. Knowledge smuggled. Men killed each other over maps thinner than onion skin. And now a guild of weavers files paperwork asking, politely, in triplicate, whether the secret paths might belong to everybody." She set the page down. "You did that."

"We did that. And Sera did that. And a lot of people who died at the Gate did that."

"Yes, yes, collective struggle, very edifying." She waved it off. "My point, Paloma, is narrower. Your old guild spent centuries as the shadow of the law. Everything it knew, everything it was, existed because the crown made the roads closed and the people hungry. You were the symptom. And now the disease is cured, and the question in front of this council is what a symptom does when the body heals." Her fingers rested flat on the page. "I think it becomes part of the body. I think the weavers are asking the right question. I think the trails should be mapped, taught, given to every clan and cartwright in the mountains. And I think it will hurt you to vote for it, and I think you should vote for it anyway, and that is why I called you here before the session instead of letting you find out in front of everyone."

I sat with that for a while. Wren sat with me, which helped, because children don't perform patience; they simply have it or lose interest, and he stayed, which was its own vote.

The old trails. Every one of them had a name and a story and a grave or two attached. The Kestrel Way, where my father taught me to read rockfall. The Widow's Stair, where Tam had once talked a patrol into arresting a tree stump. The route we'd run the night of the blizzard, carrying a half-frozen child whose maps drawn in frost on the window had matched my own hidden charts stroke for stroke. Handing those over to public record felt like handing over letters from a dead lover. Keeping them felt like everything the compact stood against.

"When did you get wise?" I asked Maren.

"I've been wise for decades. Everyone was too busy thinking I was useful to notice." She smiled her dry smile. "Session's at dawn. Sleep on it. Or don't—your face will decide for you either way."

* * *

I slept on it badly, which is to say I slept on the porch with a blanket and a mug of cider going cold, watching the pass, which is what I did on nights when the adjustment got loud.

Near midnight, boots on gravel. Ferro, of course. He sat down on the other end of the bench without invitation, which was his version of knocking.

"Can't sleep either?" I said.

"Don't need much." He was quiet a moment. Down the valley, the checkpoint lanterns burned steady at Hollow Bridge, and beyond them the mountains stood black against the stars, holding their snow like a secret they'd finally agreed to share. "Thinking about the trails?" he asked.

"How'd you know?"

"Because you've got the face."

"The lemon face or the mourning face?"

"Neither. The stubborn one." He stretched his boots out. "For what it's worth, I'll vote however you vote. Always figured that was the arrangement."

"That's not an arrangement, Ferro, that's a liability."

"Mm." He was silent long enough that I thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up, which he could do. Then: "Tam would've voted to keep them secret. Not because he was wrong-hearted. Because secrets were the only wealth he ever trusted."

"Yeah," I said. The ford rose up in my throat again, and I let it sit there. "Yeah, he would have."

"So maybe we keep one trail unmapped," Ferro said. "The one where he died. Name it on the charter, mark it sacred, let nobody pave it. The rest goes in the books." He shrugged his enormous shrug. "Grief needs somewhere to live. Better a mountain trail than a bottle."

I looked at him. Two degrees of softness, maybe three tonight. "That's the wisest thing anyone's said to me since the war."

"Don't tell Maren. She'll write it down."

Too late; I'd already decided to tell Maren.

Dawn came up gold on the eastern peaks, and the session convened in the chapel-archive with all five seats filled—me for the wardens, a clan-mother named Ossa for the mountains, Magister Corvel for the crown, a young woman with ink-stained fingers speaking for the lowland guilds, and Maren as keeper of records, who technically held no vote and technically influenced every single one. The weavers' petition was read aloud. Ossa spoke for mapping everything. Corvel, to my genuine surprise, agreed—the crown had learned, slowly and expensively, that closed roads breed the very smugglers they fear. The ink-stained woman agreed. All eyes came to me.

I thought about my mother spitting on my boots. I thought about Tam's bottle. I thought about a boy drawing my own secret roads in frost on a window, a thousand-year-old knowledge arriving on my doorstep like it had been posted.

"All of it goes in the books," I said. "Except the Widow's Stair. That one we mark sacred. And I want the weavers to send someone to learn the Kestrel Way from me personally, because if my father's trail is going to be public property, it's going to be taught properly."

Maren wrote it down. The motion carried. Somewhere in Greyhollow a clerk would copy it into the compact rolls, and in fifty years some child would learn the Kestrel Way in a schoolroom and never know it had once been worth killing for, and that, I decided, was the finest epitaph a smuggler could ask for.

Afterward, Wren was waiting outside on the chapel steps, and he fell into step beside me as we walked down toward the high road, where the morning caravan was assembling—wool going down, iron tools coming up, the first truly lawful traffic these mountains had seen since before the crown was a lie.

"Did you do it?" he asked.

"Did what?"

"Give the trails away."

"Gave them to everybody. That's not giving away. That's putting them somewhere safe where everyone can reach them."

He considered this with his whole face, the way he considered everything. Then he said, "Like the songs."

"Like the—what songs?"

"The Thornic songs." He glanced up at me, brown eyes, ordinary, boyish, except for the thousand years behind them. "They only survived because grandmothers broke the law and sang them to babies. Secret roads, secret songs. Same crime." He shrugged, a small sharp shrug he'd learned from Isolde. "Now the roads are free. So Maren says the songs should be too. She wants to print them." He paused, and something flickered there—not amber, but close, a warmth behind the eyes like coals remembering fire. "She wants *me* to sing them. At the festival. Where everyone can hear."

The festival. The Compact Day festival, first of its name, three weeks out, the whole valley coming up the pass to celebrate the anniversary of the day a queen had knelt in the mud before history and gotten back up having traded a throne for a covenant. There would be speeches. There would be Ossa's clan drums. There was, according to a document I had successfully avoided reading, a program.

"Wren," I said carefully, "how do you feel about singing in front of several thousand people?"

"I practiced," he said. "Want to hear?"

And there on the high road, in broad daylight, lawful as anything, a boy opened his mouth and began to sing in a language older than stone—and the caravan mule-drivers stopped to listen, and the hearth-fire of the world seemed to lean toward him, and I understood that the guild I had mourned was well and truly dead, and that what we'd become instead was something no one had ever smuggled:

An open road.

What I didn't know—what none of us knew, as the first notes rolled down the pass and the mountains took them up—was that far to the south, in a palace built on a stolen crown, a young queen had just received a letter bearing no seal at all, only a single word in pre-kingdom Thornic, in handwriting a thousand years dead:

*Remember.*

And that word was signed with the name of someone sleeping.
