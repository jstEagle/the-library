## 39. Isolde's Sister

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were always the day the world remembered it owed Isolde Vane a debt and paid it in the smallest possible coin.

She was in the guildhall's old counting room—now, absurdly, an office, with a door that shut and everything—when the courier arrived. The compact had been three weeks old. Three weeks since the queen of all the mountains had knelt in the mud before a woman older than mud, and the world had not ended. Three weeks of caravans crossing Thornfell without paying anyone anything, which Isolde maintained was theft from herself personally.

The letter bore the seal of the Greyhollow Chancery. Her hands did not shake opening it. She wanted to note that for posterity: her hands did not shake.

*By order of the Mountain Compact and the Office of the Young Queen, the prisoner Sera Vane is released from custody at Coldbarrow. All charges are struck. She will be conveyed to the city of Greyhollow or any place her family designates, at the Crown's expense.*

At the Crown's expense. Isolde laughed at that, alone in the office, one short ugly bark. Ten years. Ten years of petitions, bribes, letters to magistrates who never wrote back, and now—at the Crown's expense—her sister would be handed back like a misdelivered parcel.

She read the letter four times. Then she put it face-down on the desk and pressed both palms flat against it, as if she could feel the words through the paper, warm and alive.

Sera was coming home.

---

They gave Isolde a horse from the compact stables—a real requisition, signed by Paloma Blackwood himself, whose signature still looked like a smuggler's mark crossed with a spider's death—and she rode down out of the passes toward Coldbarrow with snowmelt running loud in every ditch.

She had rehearsed this ride for a decade, in fragments. In the bad years she'd rehearsed arriving too late; in the worse years she'd rehearsed arriving and finding Sera changed beyond recognition, hollowed out, hating her. And in the very worst years—the ones after she'd written to Vael's contact, after the ford, after Tam died buying them time with his drunk brave useless heart—she'd rehearsed something else entirely. She'd rehearsed Sera learning what Isolde had done. Because Sera would learn. That was the thing about sisters. They always learned.

Coldbarrow prison was a squat grey fist of a building at the edge of a town that existed only to serve it. A clerk met her at the gate with the efficiency of a man who'd processed forty releases that month and expected no gratitude from any of them.

"Vane," he said. "You're the Vane?"

"I'm the Vane."

"Sign here. She's in the yard."

And there she was. Standing in the prison yard in a coat two sizes too big, blinking in the daylight like a cat carried somewhere unfamiliar, and she was smaller than Isolde remembered, greyer, but she was *standing*, and when she saw Isolde her whole face did something complicated—joy and fury and grief all trying to use the same muscles at once.

"You look terrible," Sera said.

"You've been in prison ten years."

"And you've clearly been smuggling. You have a scar now." Sera touched her own cheekbone, indicating where Isolde's ran white and thin. "You didn't have that when they took me. You were soft. I liked you soft."

Isolde meant to say something dignified. Something about how she'd fought for this, how every coin she'd earned and stolen had gone into the effort. What came out instead, in front of the bored clerk and two guards and God and everybody, was:

"I sold you out."

No. Wrong. That wasn't—it came out wrong, tangled, the confession she'd practiced for Paloma spilling sideways onto the wrong person.

Sera frowned. "What?"

"Nothing. Not you. It's—" Isolde dragged both hands down her face. "Can we do this outside? We're doing this outside. There's an inn."

"There's always an inn with you."

They walked out of Coldbarrow side by side, and neither of them took the other's arm, because some things you earn back inch by inch, and touching comes last.

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Lamb, which Isolde found grimly funny and said so, and Sera said, "You always did laugh at funerals," and then they sat across from each other with wine neither of them drank, because drinking would have made it easier, and easier was the enemy.

So Isolde told her. All of it. The child appearing at the tollhouse in the blizzard. Maren and the dead language. The hunters in grey. The flight through the passes. And then the part Isolde had told no one except Paloma, and him only under duress of her own confession, wrung out in the dark below the mountain with the bones of the first wardens watching:

"When Vael held me over that map table, he didn't threaten me. That's what I want you to understand. He didn't wave a knife. He put your file in front of me. Your transfer papers—to a deeper cell, colder, no visitors, reviewed never. And he said, 'People break in Coldbarrow, mistress Vane. I'd hate for your sister to become a story.' And I thought about you becoming a story, and I picked up the pen." Her voice went flat and steady, the voice she used for appraising stolen goods. "I wrote where we'd cross the ford. Tam died there. Ferro took a blade meant for the boy. I did it for love, which is the worst currency there is, because it never shows up counterfeit until you've spent it."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that the innkeeper refilled their untouched glasses and retreated again.

Sera looked at her hands. Then she said, "Tam. The old one? The one who talked too much?"

"He talked exactly as much as he always did. He just finally talked to the wrong person." Isolde swallowed. "He bought us time at the ford. Drunk off his arse and singing, and he stood in the road and made them chase him into the water so the rest of us could run. I think—I think he knew. About me. I think he figured it out and chose to die anyway so I'd get the chance to confess instead of rot."

"That sounds like him." Sera's jaw worked. "You told me stories about him. When we were girls. The great smuggler Tam who once stole a bishop's hat."

"He stole the bishop's *horse*. The hat was a bonus."

A ghost of a smile, there and gone, on both their faces. Then Sera set her glass down with a small precise click and said, "I'm not going to tell you it's forgiven."

"No."

"Because it's not mine to forgive, mostly. Tam's dead. Ferro bled. The boy—" she paused. "The boy they were hunting. What happened to him?"

"He's fine. He eats like a siege army and he's learning common speech and he calls Paloma 'Lo-ma' because Paloma's name has too many corners in it." Isolde heard her own voice soften and resented it. "He's fine. He's better than fine. He's the reason any of us are alive and free and employed, which reminds me—"

"Don't change the subject."

"I'm not changing the subject. I'm finishing the subject and starting a new one, which is a different skill entirely." Isolde leaned forward. "I need to say one more thing and then you can hate me at your leisure. I don't regret it."

Sera's eyes went flint-hard.

"Not the writing," Isolde said quickly. "God, no, I regret the writing every morning before breakfast. I mean—I don't regret that it was for you. If you'd become a story in Coldbarrow, if I'd kept my hands clean and let them bury you alive to protect my honor, then everything I am would be worth nothing anyway. So there it is. I betrayed everyone I loved to save the person I loved most, and I'd do it again, and that's the sin I'll carry, and I'm not asking you to make it light."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread.

"You know what I did in there?" Sera said finally. "Ten years, and do you know what kept me sane? I decided you'd forgotten me."

"Sera—"

"Let me finish. It was easier. Every year you didn't come, I thought, well, she's built a new life, I'm a weight she's put down. And it hurt, but it was *clean* hurt. It didn't have you in it." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "And then you come riding up with a scar and a requisitioned horse and a letter with the *queen's seal*, and I find out you didn't forget me. You spent ten years breaking yourself apart to get me out, and then you broke everyone else too, and Isolde, I don't know whether to hold you or hit you."

"Historically you've managed both."

"Historically yes." Sera wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, exactly the way she'd been at fourteen. "All right. Here's my ruling, since apparently someone in this family has to hand down judgments. I forgive you for what you did to save me. That part I understand down to the bone, because I'd have done the same and worse. But the rest—the guilt, the carrying of it—you don't get to hand that to me. That's yours. You'll work it off the way you work off everything. Slowly. Annoyingly. By being useful."

"That's the cruelest sentence ever pronounced."

"I learned law from prison walls." Sera finally drank her wine. "Now. Tell me why you mentioned employment, because you had a look. You get a look when you're about to ask for something you think you don't deserve."

* * *

The ride back up into the passes took four days, and Sera talked the entire time, which was how Isolde knew she was healing. Prison had taught her silence; the road was teaching her back her own voice. She interrogated Isolde about everything—the Wolf-King, the vault, the murals in the hollow chapel, the boy, the battle at Thornfell Gate where an unarmed woman older than kingdoms had stood between two armies and refused, simply refused, to let the killing start.

"And Paloma just... walked out and told the truth," Sera said, as they climbed the last switchback below the tollhouse. "To ten thousand soldiers."

"He's good at roads. Turns out truth is just a road nobody's maintaining. Somebody has to walk it first and show it's passable."

"That's the stupidest metaphor I've ever heard."

"It worked on the soldiers."

The tollhouse had grown. That was the other thing. What had been a dying smuggler's post was now a waystation of the compact—new roof beams, a stable yard full of honest horses, a bell by the door that travelers rang to report broken bridges and washed-out trails, because the wardens of the new compact were sworn to fix the roads rather than bleed them. Isolde still half-expected to be arrested every time she saw a uniform, and the uniformed people kept waving cheerfully at her.

Paloma was on the porch, mending a saddle strap, the boy cross-legged beside him sharpening a stick with tremendous concentration. At the sight of Sera, Paloma rose, and the boy rose, and Paloma said, "You must be the sister. She has your scowl."

"She has my everything. I'm the improved edition."

"Lo-ma!" the boy announced, pointing at Paloma with his sharpened stick, in case there was confusion.

"This is Wren," Paloma said, ruffling the boy's hair. "He's our newest warden-in-training. His current duties include sticks." Then, to Isolde, more quietly, with the look of a man who already knew why she'd brought her sister through his door: "Come inside when you're ready. Maren's in the chart room. She'll want to meet Sera. And Isolde—" he hesitated. "Whatever you came up the mountain to say, I'll hear it. No hurry."

There it was. He knew. Of course he knew. Paloma Blackwood had made his living reading trails and faces, and Isolde had been leaving tracks for weeks.

* * *

She waited until evening. The boy asleep, Sera installed by the fire getting fed stew by Ferro, who grumbled and hovered like a mother hen with a hangover, Maren listening to Sera's voice with her blind eyes turned toward it as though tasting it. The household hummed around its business.

Isolde found Paloma checking the lanterns on the porch rail, because even in the new lawful age he checked things twice.

"I want a place," she said. "In the guild. The new one. The wardens."

Paloma set down the lantern. "You have a place. You've had one since the ford."

"No." She shook her head. "You forgave me, and I've never decided whether that makes you the best man I know or a fool, possibly both. But forgiveness isn't a position. I don't want to stand next to you because you were gracious once. I want to stand next to you because I've earned it. Sworn it. Same oath as Ferro, same oath as the miners signing on at Greyhollow. I want the oath, Paloma. Out loud. Where it can be witnessed. Because I broke the old one, and oaths are like bones—they don't knit unless they're set properly."

For a while he said nothing. Down in the valley a caravan's lamps strung along the new road like a slow-moving constellation, lawful and lit and unafraid.

"You know what the oath says now?" he asked. "Maren rewrote it. The old one was all 'guard the door, keep the secret.' The new one starts different." He recited it slowly, from memory: "*We keep the roads open. We keep the truth spoken. We keep faith with those who keep faith with us.*" A pause. "Third line's the one that cost us Tam."

"Then I'll carry the third line heaviest."

"You understand I can't give it to you without conditions. That's not how setting a bone works. The condition is the bone." He turned to face her fully. "Here's mine: you tell the company yourself. Not me vouching for you. You stand in that kitchen tomorrow, in front of Ferro and Maren and Sera and Wren, and you speak the whole of it—what you did, what it cost, what you choose now. And then you take the oath, and if any of them says no, we wait a year and do it again. Those are my conditions."

Isolde exhaled. It was more than she'd braced for and less than she deserved, which she suspected was Paloma's definition of fair.

"All of it," she said. "Even Tam?"

"Especially Tam. He doesn't get edited out. None of them do. That's the whole point of the compact—we stopped editing." He picked the lantern back up, and his voice gentled. "For what it's worth, Isolde—there was never a version of this where you weren't in it. I voted to trust you the night at the table, before the ford, before everything. Trusting you isn't the hard part. It never was. The hard part is you trusting you."

"That's insufferable."

"I know. Maren says I get it from her."

* * *

Morning came grey and clean, with the smell of woodsmoke and porridge. Isolde stood in the kitchen doorway with her heart going like a smith's hammer and watched the company assemble—Ferro folding his huge hands, Maren turning her sightless eyes toward the sound of breathing, Sera beside her with her chin up, Wren perched on a stool gnawing his breakfast with the serene indifference of the very young.

"I broke my oath," Isolde said.

Her voice held. She noted that too, for posterity.

She told them all of it, the whole ledger, debts and payments both—the sister in Coldbarrow, the letter to Vael's man, the ford, Tam's singing in the water, Ferro's wound, the confession below the mountain among the bones of the first wardens. Nobody interrupted. When she finished, the silence had weight but not hostility, and it was Ferro, of all people, who broke it.

"Told you," he rumbled, to the room at large. "Weeks ago I said she'd stand up and say it plain. Tam knew too. Said, 'Ferro, that woman's guilt is heavier than her pack, watch her carry it.'" Ferro's eyes were wet and he pretended they weren't. "Tam also said you'd be useful. He was usually right about useful."

"The third line," Maren said softly. "Say it, child."

"*We keep faith with those who keep faith with us,*" Isolde said.

"And have you?"

"Every day since the ford. Badly, sometimes. But every day."

Maren smiled, that terrible gentle smile of hers. "That's all faith is. Doing it badly, daily."

Paloma brought out the new charter, ink still sharp, and Isolde spoke the oath with her hand flat on the page and her sister's shoulder pressed warm against her own, and when it was done nobody cheered, because it wasn't that kind of moment—it was the other kind, the quiet kind, the kind where something that had been broken quietly becomes something that holds.

Afterward, Sera caught her arm in the yard. Below them, the first caravan of the day was climbing toward the pass, bells ringing, drivers waving up at the tollhouse where no toll would be taken.

"So," Sera said. "Wardens."

"Wardens."

"Do they pay?"

"Compact wages. Modest. Honest." Isolde pulled a face. "It's horrifying."

"Good." Sera squinted down the road, and her expression shifted—something moving in it, quick and bright. "Because there's a rider coming up the switchback. Fast. Compact colors, but riding like the road's on fire behind him."

Isolde followed her gaze. The rider was still distant, but closing, and even at that distance there was something wrong about his pace, urgent and ragged, and Paloma had already stepped off the porch, and Wren had gone very still, his porridge spoon halfway to his mouth, his amber-flecked eyes fixed on the valley below.

"They're waking faster than we thought," the boy said clearly, in common speech, in his new careful voice. "Someone must tell them."

Isolde's blood went cold. "Who, Wren? Tell *who*?"

But the boy only pointed down the road, at the incoming rider, and waited.
