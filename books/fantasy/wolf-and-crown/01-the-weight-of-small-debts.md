## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The ledger did not want to balance, and Paloma Voss had learned over nine years that when a ledger refused to balance, it was rarely the ledger's fault.

She sat in the back room of the Guild of Impossible Things with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a stub of tallow candle burning down beside her, its light the color of weak tea. The guildhall above her was quiet now—Master Quill had gone to bed hours ago, muttering something about a client who wanted to purchase "a genuine miracle, none of your secondhand ones," and the apprentices had long since stumbled up the stairs in a haze of pipe smoke and complaints. Only Paloma remained, as she remained most nights, hunched over columns of figures that described things no honest accountant should ever have to price.

*One bottled thunderclap, slightly dented: 14 crowns.*
*Half a mile of rope that remembers being a serpent: 3 crowns, 6 shillings.*
*A debt owed by a man who has been dead for eleven years: pending.*

Pending. She wrote *pending* so often it had worn a groove into her favorite nib. There were debts in this book that would outlive the language they were written in. There was an entry from before she was born concerning a witch's bargain paid in firstborn laughter, and every year on the anniversary Master Quill made her check whether the interest had come due. It never had. The witch, apparently, was patient in the way that only the truly damned can be.

Paloma dipped her pen and worked through the column again, lips moving silently. Fourteen crowns for the thunderclap. Six shillings carried over. Minus the two crowns the ferryman still owed them for the fog he'd bought last spring—fog, honestly, you could get free off the river if you didn't mind it being secondhand—and plus the delivery fee for the mirrors that had arrived Tuesday, all seven of which had arrived *already broken*, which meant either terrible packing or exactly the sort of thing one expected from cursed merchandise.

Her total came out wrong again. Off by three shillings.

"Where are you hiding," she murmured to the three shillings, and the candle guttered—not from any draft, though she checked anyway, the way she always checked, the way she had trained herself to check the way other people trained themselves not to bite their nails.

Because here was the thing about Paloma Voss, keeper of ledgers, mender of impossible accounts, woman of thoroughly respectable dullness: there was a storm under her skin, and it had been brewing for nine years, and every day she woke up and chose, deliberately, with the discipline of a monk and the terror of a fugitive, not to let it out.

It started small, usually. A static prickle at the base of her skull when the barometric pressure shifted. A hum in her back teeth before rain. On bad days—the kind of days when Master Quill shouted at her about the mirrors, or when a client wept across her desk about a curse they couldn't afford to lift—the air around her went strange and metallic, and the hair on her arms lifted, and somewhere far off, thunder that had no business existing would clear its throat.

Nine years without a single uncontrolled discharge. Nine years of swallowing weather. She thought of herself sometimes as a dam with excellent bookkeeping habits.

The candle guttered again. Longer this time. And outside the shuttered window, faint but unmistakable, came a low roll of thunder—from a sky that had been cloudless at sundown. She had checked. She always checked.

"Not tonight," she told it quietly, the way one speaks to a dog begging at the table. "We are *balancing*."

The storm, whatever it was, whatever ancient Voss inheritance it drew from, subsided with the sulky reluctance of a thing that knew it would win eventually. Paloma pressed her palms flat against the ledger page until the paper stopped crackling with stray charge, and got back to work.

Three shillings. Three shillings missing. She ran the column a fourth time, and this time she found it—an entry she didn't remember making, tucked between the mirrors and the ferryman's fog in handwriting that was almost, but not quite, her own. Slightly older-fashioned. The letters leaned left where hers leaned right.

*Delivery received, one item, unpriced. See vault.*

She stared at it for a long moment. Then she did what she always did with entries that frightened her, which was to initial them, date them, and file the feeling away for later examination. Later examination was where she kept most of her life.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of river, and with it came Tamsin.

Tamsin was the guild's courier, a wiry girl of perhaps nineteen with a chipped front tooth, a coat with more pockets than coat, and the unshakable conviction that she was the fastest thing on two legs north of the capital. She arrived every Thursday with deliveries and news in roughly equal proportion, and she had a way of talking that suggested punctuation was a tax levied by people she intended to avoid.

"—and so I says to him, I says, that's not a haunted ring, mate, that's just a ring what bites, there's a difference, and he looks at me like *I'm* the unreasonable one—" Tamsin dumped a crate on Paloma's desk hard enough to make the ink bottle jump. "Morning, Miss Voss. You've got a face on you."

"I have a ledger on me."

"That too." Tamsin began unpacking the crate with the delicacy of someone who had once been bitten by a delivery. "Got your candles, got your paper, got the good ink, don't ask what I had to trade for the good ink. Oh—" she paused, and something in her pause made Paloma look up from the column she was auditing. Tamsin had gone still, which for Tamsin was roughly equivalent to another person screaming. "There's a proclamation up. Nailed to the customs house door. Wax seal and everything."

"The crown proclaims things weekly. Last month it was illegal to sell eels on a Sunday."

"This one's got names in it."

Paloma set down her pen. Carefully. The way one sets down a thing one does not want to hear rattling. "Names of whom?"

"Storm-callers." Tamsin said the word quickly, the way you'd spit out something hot. "All persons gifted with the calling of storms, winds, or weather unnatural, to present themselves for registration within thirty days." She recited it flatly, having clearly memorized it against her will on the ride in. "And them as doesn't register—" Her chipped tooth worried her lower lip. "You know how these ones end, Miss Voss. They don't end with fines."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; Master Quill, awake early, or the building settling, or the guild itself listening, because Paloma half-suspected the guildhall listened. Impossible things accumulated in old buildings like dust.

"Registration," Paloma said. Her voice came out level, which she counted as her finest professional achievement of the morning. "That sounds almost reasonable. A list. Names on a list."

"Yeah, well." Tamsin wouldn't meet her eyes. "My cousin's in the weather-trade down south, right? Little charms, rain for farmers, that line of work. Year before last there was a registration edict in her county too. You know what happened after everyone registered?" She finally looked up, and her eyes were wet and angry. "They had addresses, didn't they. That's all a list is, Miss Voss. It's addresses for later."

Paloma nodded slowly. Under the desk, hidden by wood and shadow, her fingers had begun to prickle. The pressure in the room shifted the way it did before weather, and she felt the storm under her skin turn over in its sleep, interested, attentive, *listening* to talk of lists and burnings the way a warhorse listens to drums.

Not tonight. Not ever, if she could manage it. Nine years of small, careful, invisible life. Nine years of being the woman who balanced books, nothing more. She had built that life plank by plank, entry by entry, and she would be hanged if a wax seal and a nailed-up proclamation were going to—

"Anyway." Tamsin hoisted her empty crate. "Thought you'd want to know. Being as you do the reading for Master Quill and all. He'll want to post notice, probably. Guild's neutral, he always says. Guild serves everybody." She snorted. "Reckon the crown counts 'everybody' different these days."

She let herself out, bell jangling, and Paloma sat alone with the proclamation echoing in her head and the ink drying on a column that still refused to balance.

Thirty days. She looked down at her hands—steady, ink-stained, ordinary hands—and then at the ledger, open to yesterday's page, to that entry in the not-quite-her handwriting.

*Delivery received, one item, unpriced. See vault.*

She had filed the feeling away for later examination. It occurred to her, sitting in the gray morning light with the word *burned* ringing in her ears like a struck bell, that later might have just arrived ahead of schedule.

Paloma picked up her pen, crossed out *pending* on the oldest debt in the book, and wrote instead, in her neatest hand:

*Urgent.*

Then she took the lamp and went down toward the vault, where the guild kept everything it could not afford to remember out loud.
