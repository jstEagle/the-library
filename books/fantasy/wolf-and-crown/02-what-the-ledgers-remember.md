## 2. What the Ledgers Remember

The stairs to the vault were the only stairs in the guildhall that creaked in a key of their own choosing. Paloma had catalogued them once—forty-one steps, thirty-eight complaints—and had long ago stopped trying to fix them. The guild's founding principle was that impossible things deserved a home, and the stairs, she supposed, had simply taken the philosophy personally.

She went down with the lamp held high and her ledger hugged against her ribs like a child who didn't want to be put down. The delivery note from yesterday was folded into her collar, where it had been all night because she had absolutely not been lying awake reading it by candlelight until the third bell.

*One item, unpriced. See vault.*

Unpriced. In nine years of keeping the Guild of Impossible Things' accounts, Paloma Voss had learned that *unpriced* meant one of three things: something priceless, something worthless, or something so dangerous that naming a figure would constitute an invitation.

Master Quill liked to say the vault was the guild's attic. This was a lie Quill told himself as much as anyone else, and Paloma let him keep it, the way one lets an old dog believe the thunder is very far away. The vault was not an attic. The vault was a throat, and they were all of them living comfortably inside it, pretending not to notice it swallowing.

* * *

The door recognized her before she finished the third knock—a shiver through the iron, a sound like a held breath being released. It did this for everyone, which was either magic or excellent craftsmanship, and Paloma had stopped asking which.

Inside, the lamps lit themselves in sequence, down the long aisles of shelving, past the crates marked in her own careful hand. *Confiscated, provenance disputed. Confiscated, provenance horrifying. Confiscated, do not open on Tuesdays.* That last one had been her joke, four years ago, when she'd still thought humor was a thing you could file alongside everything else. The crate had since begun humming on Tuesdays. She'd stopped making jokes about the inventory after that. Mostly.

The delivery sat on the receiving table where the porter said he'd left it: a plain wooden box, rope-tied, unmarked except for the guild's own receiving stamp—which was wrong. The stamp was hers. She hadn't stamped anything.

Paloma stood over it for a long moment, doing what she did best, which was arithmetic. One box, no sender, no price, her own stamp. Two possibilities: someone had access to her stamp, or the box had come from somewhere stamps didn't matter. Neither possibility was comforting, and she had learned that comfort was like the vault's Tuesday crate—you didn't open it just because it was there.

She untied the rope anyway. Curiosity, in Paloma's experience, was less a choice than a debt. You could defer it, but it collected interest.

Inside, under a layer of straw, lay books. Seven of them. Old ones—old the way mountains are old, old the way grief is old. Their covers were grey leather gone soft as cloth, and their spines bore no titles. When she lifted the first one out, the lamp nearest her flickered, though there was no draft in the vault. There was never a draft in the vault. That was rather the point of a vault.

"Inventory," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them ordinary, and ordinary things couldn't hurt you. "Seven volumes. Condition: suspicious. Provenance: none." She dipped her pen. "Value: pending."

The pen touched the page.

And the ledger—the guild's ledger, her ledger, the one she had ruled and balanced every day for nine years—*answered.*

Not in ink. In static. A prickling ran up her fingers, through her wrist, into the place behind her sternum where she kept the thing she never named, and for half a heartbeat the vault smelled of rain. Not vault-rain, not stone-and-dust pretending. Real rain, hot rain, the kind that comes off a summer sky with the smell of lightning already in its teeth.

Paloma snatched her hand back. The lamp steadied. The rain-smell faded like a guest remembering an appointment elsewhere.

She looked at her pen. She looked at the box. She looked at the seven grey books, sitting innocently in their straw like cats pretending they hadn't knocked anything off anything.

"Well," she said, in the voice she used for vendors who claimed a cursed mirror was merely *decorative*, "that's new."

* * *

She should have reported it. She knew exactly how the report would go, too—she'd drafted reports in her head her whole life, tidy little paragraphs with tidy little conclusions. *Found: seven volumes of unknown origin. Action taken: none, because I am a sensible woman who does not touch things that smell of storms.*

Instead she made tea.

This was procedure. It was not avoidance; it was *procedure*. Master Quill maintained that no audit should begin on an empty stomach, and Paloma had extended this principle to cover no audit beginning on a frightened stomach, which was close enough that her conscience signed off on it. She climbed back up the forty-one complaining steps, put the kettle on, and stood in the kitchen watching steam curl while she tried to remember her grandmother.

That was the strange part—that was the part she kept circling back to like a moth at glass. The rain-smell hadn't reminded her of storms. Storms she knew intimately, professionally, secretly. No: the static up her arm had reminded her of hands. Specific hands. Broad-knuckled, ink-stained, always slightly cold, smoothing her hair back in a house that had burned down before Paloma turned six.

Grandmother Voss. Dead thirty years, remembered in maybe six images, two of them borrowed from other people's stories. A voice like gravel wrapped in velvet. A way of looking at weather as though it owed her money.

Paloma carried the tea back down the stairs, and the stairs complained all the way, and she found she was grateful for the company.

* * *

Back in the vault, she did what any competent accountant would do with seven mysterious volumes of impossible provenance.

She opened the first one.

The pages were dense, small-handed, ruled with a care that made her own neatness look like a child's scrawl. Figures. Dates. Entries. It was a ledger—it was unmistakably a ledger, debits and credits, acquisitions and disbursements, the grammar of accounts—and yet every entry was wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name, the way a face can be wrong in a portrait. She read the opening line twice.

*Received into keeping: one storm, medium, unpriced. See binding.*

See binding. Her own phrase—or nearly her own. The phrasing of it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She turned pages faster now. Storms received. Storms spent. A drought bought off in the autumn of some year whose calendar she didn't recognize. A war paid for, partially, in thunder. And the handwriting—

Paloma set the book down. Picked it up again. Set it down.

"No," she said.

But the hand was steady, and the hand was familiar, and familiarity is a terrible liar. Broad-knuckled letters. Ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page, the habit of someone who distrusted paper and wanted the words to leave a scar. She had seen that hand once, on the inside cover of a recipe book that had survived the fire, in a single line: *For the girl, when she asks.*

Paloma had asked. Everyone had said there was no such book.

Her fingers hovered over the page without touching it, and the vault's lamps leaned toward her—all of them, all at once, like flowers finding sun—and the air filled again with that hot-weather rain, and somewhere far above her, faintly, impossibly, she heard thunder. Inside. Underground. Under three feet of guild stone.

She touched the page.

The world went white at the edges—not light, exactly, but the memory of light, the shape of it—and she was standing somewhere else. Somewhere high. Wind in her face that wasn't wind, carrying voices that weren't voices, and beneath her feet something vast turned over in its sleep, and a woman with broad knuckles and ink-stained fingers was writing by lightning-flash because she didn't trust lanterns, writing fast, writing—

*"If you're reading this, little storm, then the fire remembers us, and we have run out of clever ways to forget it—"*

Paloma came back to herself on her knees on the vault floor, both hands flat on the open book, breathing like she'd run from something. The lamps guttered and recovered. The thunder had stopped, or been stopped, or had never entirely started.

Static crawled over her skin and settled, reluctantly, like a cat deciding to stay.

She knelt there a while. She was, she noted distantly, crying a little, which was inefficient, and she filed that away for later examination along with everything else.

Because here was the thing—the thing that curdled dread slowly, over the next hour, into something warmer and far more dangerous:

She wasn't afraid of the books anymore. She had been afraid, right up until the moment the memory touched her, and then the fear had broken apart like ice in spring water, and underneath it was not relief but appetite. Six images of a dead woman. Six, across thirty years. And now one more—vivid enough to taste, real enough to ache—and it had cost her nothing but a touch.

There were seven books. There were hundreds of pages. There might be a hundred grandmothers in there, a thousand, a whole lineage of storms waiting patiently in grey leather to be known.

Paloma got up, fetched the good chair from the corner—hers, dragged down years ago against regulations—and sat, and poured her tea, gone cold, with ceremonial care.

"Right," she told the books. "Let's see what we owe."

She opened the second volume, and the lamps leaned in, and far overhead the guildhall settled around its secrets like an old man around a fireplace, and Paloma Voss began, with dread and delight in equal measure, to read her inheritance.

She was three pages deep into a storm that had been purchased with a kingdom when she noticed the marginalia—small, cramped, added later in different ink, in a hand she also almost recognized. Not her grandmother's.

A newer hand. A living hand.

The note beside the entry read: *The last key will find these. Keep the vault warm.—K.*

Paloma looked up at the dark end of the aisle, where the shelving gave way to a heavy iron door she had catalogued years ago as *storage, sealed, value: nil.*

Behind it, distinctly, something shifted its weight.
