## 3. Ash on the Doorstep

The knock came at dawn, which was insulting on two counts. First, nobody respectable knocked at dawn. Second, it meant whoever stood outside had ridden through the night to be precisely this punctual, and punctuality in an inquisitor was a very specific kind of bad news.

Paloma Voss knew this because she had spent nine years studying bad news the way other scholars studied poetry. She could read it in the tremor of a courier's hands, in the way Master Quill's pen scratched too fast across a bill of lading, in the quality of silence that settled over a guildhall when something had gone wrong upstairs and everyone was pretending to count inventory.

She was in the cellar when the knock came, elbow-deep in a crate of bottled fog from the Mistwaste, and for one absurd moment she considered simply staying down there. The fog bottles needed sorting. They would need sorting for approximately the rest of her life.

"Paloma." Master Quill's voice floated down the stairwell, pitched carefully light. He only used that voice when lying to someone within earshot of her, which meant he was about to lie *for* her, which meant she needed to be up there in four seconds looking like the most boring bookkeeper in the history of commerce.

She wiped her hands, tucked the anomalous pages — grandmother's pages, ledger-pages that hummed against her ribs like a second heartbeat — into the satchel she now carried everywhere, including, once memorably, into the privy, and climbed.

* * *

Inquisitor Serane did not look like fire. Paloma had expected fire, or at least embers — the edicts posted on every corner promised burning with such bureaucratic enthusiasm that she'd assumed the crown's agents would match. Instead Serane looked like winter. Grey coat, grey eyes, grey hair pinned back with the severe economy of a woman who had decided long ago that decoration was a tax paid by people who hadn't yet earned respect. She stood in the guildhall's entryway holding a warrant the way other people held a teacup: correctly, and without any warmth whatsoever.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," Serane read aloud. Her voice was dry as old vellum. "Registered under charter eleven-fourteen, licensed for the trade of relics of ambiguous provenance." She looked up. "Which is a lovely way of saying you sell things nobody can quite explain."

"We prefer 'things that resist explanation,'" Master Quill said. "It sounds more expensive."

He was a round little man built like a teapot, with ink-stained fingers and a face that had never successfully concealed anything in sixty years of life. Paloma watched him lie and felt something between horror and tenderness. His ears went red first. Then his left eyebrow began its traitorous twitch. He was telling Serane, "Of course we have no storm-callers on staff, madam, why, the very idea," while his entire body screamed otherwise in a language even a deaf woman could parse.

Serane looked at him the way one looks at a dog attempting long division.

"And your ledgers," she said. "I'll want them all. Every entry, every year. Trade in impossible goods attracts impossible debts, and impossible debts attract—" she paused delicately— "*people* of interest."

"They're terribly dull," Quill said. "Numbers mostly. Some receipts."

"I should hope so."

Paloma stepped forward before Quill could compound his perjury. "The ledgers are keyed, Inquisitor. Old guild enchantment — they only open for the keeper's bloodline. I'm the keeper. If you'll give me an hour, I'll prepare full copies of every entry for your inspection." She smiled the small, apologetic smile of a woman who lived in paperwork. "Copying takes time. We're meticulous here. It's our whole personality."

Something flickered behind Serane's grey eyes. Not suspicion exactly. Assessment. A fisherman deciding whether this particular river was worth the morning.

"One hour," Serane said. "And I'll inspect the vaults myself while you work. My men will accompany your master." She turned to Quill, whose eyebrow was now performing what could charitably be called a jig. "You may stop twitching, sir. Guilt is not evidence. Yet."

* * *

Here is what Paloma knew about cursed mirrors, having catalogued forty-one of them over the years: they were vain, temperamental, and above all *hungry*. A cursed mirror didn't show you your reflection; it showed you what you'd been avoiding, and then it kept showing you, and eventually you stopped coming to the mirror and started living inside whatever it showed you. The Guild kept theirs wrapped in black wool and stored in crates marked FRAGILE, MOSTLY EMOTIONALLY.

They were also, Paloma realized with the cold clarity of genuine desperation, the single most unpleasant place in the building to hide anything.

She worked fast. The anomalous pages — seven of them now, pulled from three different vaults, all in her grandmother's hand, all humming faintly like struck tuning forks — went between layers of wool-wrapped glass. She arranged them so no mirror faced another (mirrors facing mirrors was how the 1149 incident had happened, and the scorch mark still decorated the ceiling of Vault Six), and she whispered the small binding charm her grandmother had taught the ledgers themselves: *what is hidden remembers being hidden.*

The pages dimmed against her senses, going quiet as stones.

"You're doing it again," said a voice from the crate beside her.

Paloma did not jump. She had trained herself out of jumping years ago; jumping was a tell. But her heart performed something athletic nonetheless. "Doing what, Wren?"

Wren was the guild's youngest factor, sixteen, sharp-eyed, theoretically employed dusting the reliquary and actually employed narrating everyone else's business. She leaned against the crate of mirrors with the boneless confidence of youth. "That face. The one where you look calm but your jaw is doing arithmetic."

"My jaw does not do arithmetic."

"It's doing division right now. Something's wrong, and it involves the grey lady upstairs, and you've got wool fibers in your hair." Wren plucked one free and examined it. "Vault Six wool. The mirror room." Her eyes widened with the slow delight of a girl assembling gossip. "You hid something in the mirrors."

"Wren."

"I won't tell. Obviously. I tell people things, but only true things, and if I don't know anything—" she shrugged eloquently— "then I have nothing to tell, which is practically a virtue."

For a moment Paloma weighed it. Nine years of solitude pressed down on her, nine years of being the only person who knew what she was, and here was this ridiculous girl offering complicity like a sweet from her pocket. Complicity was dangerous. Complicity was how Master Quill had ended up lying to an inquisitor with both ears glowing like signal lamps.

But it was also warm. And Paloma was cold all the time lately.

"If anyone asks," Paloma said quietly, "the mirrors were inventoried Tuesday. By me. Alone. There were forty-one of them and now there are forty-one of them."

"There were always forty-one of them," Wren agreed solemnly. "I've counted twice myself. Forty-one. A very stable number." She paused at the stairwell door. "Paloma? Your jaw stopped. Whatever you're dividing, it came out even. That's good, right?"

Paloma thought of the pages humming against her ribs. Of the word her grandmother's handwriting had conjured in her sleep two nights running: *key*. Of the fact that the crown's witch-hunt had arrived at her doorstep exactly when the ledgers had started waking up.

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

Serane found her in the counting room at the fifty-minute mark, standing over ledgers laid open in neat rows, copying entries with a clerk's steady hand while her mind ran elsewhere entirely.

"You write quickly," Serane observed from the doorway.

"I write accurately. Quickly is just what accuracy looks like when it's frightened."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly: "Honesty. From a smuggler's clerk." Serane entered and walked the row of ledgers without touching them, the way one walks a fence line. "Do you know what I collect, Mistress Voss?"

"Guilt?" Paloma suggested. "It's the traditional inquisitorial hobby."

"Patterns." Serane lifted one of the older volumes — safe, clean, ordinary — and turned pages with a gloved finger. "Nine years ago, a storm-caller named Maren Voss burned her name out of every registry on the coast and vanished. No body. No trial. Just weather reports from three harbors that all describe the same impossible calm, as though someone had folded the storms away and put them somewhere." She closed the book. "Voss is not a common name."

"No," Paloma said. Her pen had not stopped moving. This was the trick she'd taught herself: let the hands stay honest so the mouth can survive. "It's a merchant family. Distant relations, probably. Everyone's related to someone embarrassing."

"Mm." Serane studied her for a long moment — and Paloma understood suddenly that the woman wasn't hunting. She was *harvesting information*, patiently, the way a farmer reads clouds. "Your copies will be ready soon?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Then I'll trouble you for tea while I wait. Black. No sugar." At the door she paused. "Master Quill tells me your ledgers are keyed to blood. That's an unusual security measure for a trade house. Very old magic. Very *specific* magic." Her grey eyes rested on Paloma a half-second too long. "I do wonder what they'd show, if the right blood opened them."

She left. Paloma sat very still among her neat rows of honest numbers, listening to her own heart count down like a dropped coin spinning toward stillness.

* * *

They were loading Serane's wagons at dusk when it happened.

Paloma had done everything correctly. The copies were flawless, the mirrors crated, the pages buried in wool and silence. Serane's men hauled the last inspection crates out — routine confiscations, relics pending review — and Paloma stood on the guildhall steps beside Quill, performing relief, feeling ash settle where her stomach used to be.

Then the wind shifted.

One wagon's canvas flap lifted — nothing dramatic, just the evening breeze off the harbor — and from inside came a sound Paloma felt in her teeth rather than heard. A low, resonant hum. The sound of pages remembering they were hidden, and choosing, briefly, not to be.

Serane turned her head.

It was nothing. Ten seconds of grey eyes moving across grey evening, a pause so brief Quill didn't even register it — he was busy waving goodbye with the desperate cheerfulness of a man whose ears had finally cooled. But Paloma saw the exact moment the Inquisitor's gaze snagged on wagon three, saw the small tilt of her head, the way a hunter marks a treeline where something moved.

"Master Quill," Serane said pleasantly, gathering her reins, "thank you for your cooperation. I'll return within the week to conclude the audit. There's no need to prepare anything special."

There was every need to prepare everything special, and they both knew it.

The wagons rolled away into the dusk, carrying seven pages of her grandmother's handwriting off toward the palace, toward whatever furnace the crown fed its secrets to. Paloma watched until they vanished, and felt the strangest sensation: not grief, not fear, but a kind of terrible *pulling*, like a hook set gently behind her sternum. The pages weren't lost. The pages were *calling* — and for the first time, she noticed they weren't calling to her at all.

They were calling to something underneath the city. Something that had been asleep for thirty years, two cellars down, directly beneath her feet.

Quill squeezed her shoulder. "We survived," he whispered. "Boring books. Boring guild. Nothing to see."

"Go inside, Master Quill," Paloma said softly. "Lock the cellar door."

"Why?" His face went pale in the way of a man who has just remembered he employs a bookkeeper who never asks for raises. "What's in my cellar, Paloma?"

"That," she said, already turning toward the stairs, "is an excellent question. And I think it's been waiting thirty years for someone to ask it."
