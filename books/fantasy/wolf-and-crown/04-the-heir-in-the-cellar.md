## 4. The Heir in the Cellar

Thirty years is a long time to learn the sound of a building.

Kael knew the guildhall the way a blind man knows his own hands: by creak and shudder and sigh. The third stair from the bottom groaned in E-flat when Master Quill took it too fast after his evening wine. The vault door two floors above hummed at dusk, a thin copper note, as though the ledgers inside were dreaming aloud. And every night, sometime between the last lamp going out and the first gull arguing with the dawn, someone walked the length of the cellar corridor above him—soft steps, unhurried, pausing once near the old coal chute before moving on.

He had catalogued them all over three decades. Quill's shuffle. The cook's decisive stomp. The apprentice who whistled off-key and always, *always* stopped to kick the loose brick by the wine racks, as if the brick had personally wronged him.

But tonight's footsteps were new.

They came down the corridor slower than any he'd heard before—hesitant, then certain, then hesitant again, like someone following a scent they didn't trust themselves to be following. They stopped above him. Not near the coal chute. Directly overhead, dead center, where no one ever stopped because there was nothing there to stop for except a door that hadn't opened in nine years.

Kael set down the chipped clay cup he'd been using as an ashtray, a candlestick, and once, memorably, a soup bowl, and pressed his ear to the cold stone.

The footsteps did not continue.

*Well,* he thought. *Either she's lost, or something down here has finally started calling.*

He had wondered, lately, whether it would happen this way. Thirty years of sitting in the dark with nothing but his own fire for company had given him a great deal of time to wonder. The ledgers upstairs were keyed to dragon-fire—he'd known that since he was seven years old, since the night they'd carried him down these stairs, small and furious and bound at the wrists with salt-iron, and told him he was collateral now, and that good collateral stayed quiet.

He'd been very good collateral. He'd also, in the privacy of his own skull, been insufferably smug about one thing: someday, someone would come looking. The pages would stir. The static would rise. Some Voss or other would feel the pull in their blood and come walking down a corridor at midnight, hesitating outside a door everyone else had forgotten existed.

He just hadn't expected her to hesitate quite so long.

"Hello?" The voice was a woman's, low, careful—the voice of someone who had learned that curiosity was a debt that collected interest. "Is someone down there?"

Kael considered his options. He could stay silent, and she might leave, and he could go back to thirty more years of counting bricks. There were four hundred and twelve, if anyone asked. He could answer honestly, which risked her bolting like a startled cat. Or he could do what he'd rehearsed, absurdly, alone, in the dark, for the better part of a decade: say something so disarming that fleeing would feel rude.

"There's tea," he called up, "but I should warn you it's been steeping since roughly the reign of the last queen."

A pause. Then, warily: "...That's not tea anymore. That's an archaeological find."

"Everything down here is," Kael said. "You get used to it. Or you don't, and you go quietly mad, and honestly the line between those is thinner than you'd think."

Another pause, longer. Then the door groaned open—a sound like an old man being asked to dance—and lamplight spilled down the stairs, and Paloma Voss stood on the top step with a ledger clutched to her chest like a shield and an expression on her face that Kael recognized instantly, because he'd worn it himself for thirty years.

It was the look of a prisoner who has just realized the cage next door has an occupant.

* * *

She descended slowly, lamp held high, and Kael made no sudden movements. He'd learned that lesson early too. Sudden movements got you struck with the salt-iron. Also, at five-and-thirty, with a beard he trimmed with a shard of mirror and a posture that had gone soft in all the places captivity goes soft, he was not exactly a figure that inspired terror. He looked, he suspected, less like a dragon-heir and more like a hermit who had lost an argument with a barber.

"You're real," she said, in the tone of someone confirming a suspicion they'd hoped was paranoia.

"I'm real." He spread his arms. "Disappointingly so. No wings, I'm afraid. The stories oversold us."

Her lamp swung toward him and caught his eyes, and he watched her flinch—not at him, but at the color. Amber shot through with red, the way coals look just before they catch. She'd seen that color before. He could tell by the way her hand tightened on the ledger until the cover creaked.

"You know what I am," he said.

"I know what your *eyes* are." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she probably used with smugglers and tax men. "I've read about it. In books the guild pretends not to have."

"And what do the books say?"

"That dragon-heirs were the crown's problem." A beat. "Before they became the guild's."

Kael laughed—a dry, rusted thing, a laugh that hadn't had much practice. "That's one way to put it. Here's another: thirty years ago, my grandfather burned half a province, and the guild bought me instead of justice. One heir, alive, in a box. Insurance against the rest of us." He tilted his head at the ceiling, at the vault humming its copper note somewhere above. "I'm not a prisoner, officially. Officially I'm an asset. It turns out those are the same thing with better paperwork."

Paloma stood very still. He was beginning to understand that stillness was her native language; she spoke it fluently, the way he spoke fire.

"Why are you here?" he asked. "Really. Nobody comes down here. Quill visits twice a year to confirm I'm breathing and leaves before I can finish a sentence. You came at midnight, following—" he nodded at the book in her arms "—that."

For a moment she said nothing. Then she opened the ledger, carefully, the way one opens a letter that might contain bad news, and turned it toward him. Pages written in faded brown ink, dense and slanting, annotated in margins with little storm-symbols that Kael had only ever seen in one place: branded into the doorframe of the house his family had lost.

"Whose hand is this?" she asked.

"I don't know whose hand it is," Kael said slowly, "but I know whose *fire* it is." He reached out—asked first, with a glance, and she nodded—and touched the page. Warmth bloomed under his fingertips, faint but unmistakable, the way a hearth stays warm hours after the logs have gone to ash. "There's a Voss key in your blood. That's why the pages wake for you. That's why you heard them humming through three floors of stone and followed the sound down here at an hour no sane accountant keeps."

"My grandmother," Paloma said. "I think it's her hand. I found entries tonight that only she could have made."

Something moved through Kael then, old and cold and complicated. He sat back down on his cot—the cot being a plank, some straw, and a blanket that had achieved texture through sheer persistence—and looked at her for a long moment.

"Sit down," he said. "This is going to take a while, and you look like a woman who hasn't slept, and I look like a man who has nothing but time."

"I shouldn't," she said, automatically, the way people say it when they mean *I want to but admitting it feels dangerous.*

"Noted for the record," Kael said gravely. "The prisoner formally objects to hospitality. Objection overruled. There's a crate."

She sat. The lamp between them threw their shadows up the wall, and for a while neither spoke, and Kael found the silence strangely easy. He was out of practice with conversation but not with company; he'd spent thirty years keeping company with a fire that lived under his ribs, and it had taught him patience the way drowning teaches swimming.

"The ledgers," he said finally. "Do you know what they actually are? Not what Quill tells the tax assessors."

"A record of debts," she said. "Impossible ones."

"That's the cover story, and it's a good one, because it's *also true.*" He leaned forward. "Every impossible thing the guild ever brokered—every miracle smuggled, every curse laundered, every favor owed by things with too many teeth—they wrote it down. And writing a thing down truly, with the right ink and the right key, doesn't just record it. It *keeps* it. The ledgers aren't a history, Paloma Voss. They're a cage. The biggest one ever built. And somebody in your family helped build it."

Her face did something complicated. He watched grief arrive and be immediately, professionally filed away behind competence, and thought: *ah. There it is. Another one of us.*

"My grandmother ended the last dragon war," she said quietly. "That's what the memories say. She caged the fire."

"She did," Kael said. "And the fire has been caged ever since—in the ledgers, in the vault, and—" he tapped his own chest, once, lightly, where something ancient smoldered beneath the softness "—in whatever's left of us. The heirs. We're not collateral, whatever the paperwork says. We're *sparks.* Kept alive so the flame never fully dies. Because a cage needs something on the inside worth keeping, or the lock forgets its purpose."

Paloma stared at him. "How long?"

"How long have I been down here?" He shrugged. "Thirty years. But that's the wrong question." He met her eyes, coal-red meeting storm-grey, and felt—for the first time in three decades—something in the dark cellar that wasn't resignation. "The right question is: how long does the queen intend to keep hunting people like you? Because I've felt it through the floor, these past weeks. The pages waking. The static rising. Something upstairs is stirring, and cages don't stir unless the thing inside them is getting ready to move."

Outside, far above, thunder rolled across the city—out of season, out of nowhere, the kind of thunder that had no cloud to blame. Paloma's jaw tightened. Kael saw her hands curl in her lap, saw the tiny arc of light jump between her knuckles and die, quick as a guilty secret.

And he smiled, slow and genuine, the first smile the cellar had seen in nine years.

"There she is," he said softly. "Hello, storm-caller. I was starting to think they'd all let the fire go out."

Paloma rose, clutching the ledger, already retreating into the shape of a woman with an alibi to build—but at the foot of the stairs she paused, and looked back at him, and said the thing that undid thirty years of careful quiet:

"They're coming for the gifted, Kael. An edict, this week. Register or burn." Her knuckles whitened on the book. "Which means either I find a way to get you out of this cage—or we both learn what's really locked in these pages. Soon."

Kael leaned back against the cold stone and felt, under his ribs, the old fire lean forward with her.

"Then I suggest," he said, "you start reading."
