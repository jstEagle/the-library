## 5. A Market of Impossible Things

The Saltglass Market opened at moonset and closed when the watch changed, which meant it existed for roughly two hours a night in a space that did not officially exist at all. It lived in the flooded undercroft of the old tannery district, where the canals ran green-black and the lanterns were all stolen from three different churches so that no one god could claim jurisdiction.

Paloma had been coming here for nine years, first as a runner for Master Quill, then as the guild's face. She knew which stalls sold truth and which sold hope, and she knew that hope cost more and broke faster.

Tonight she wore her second-best coat—the one with no guild buttons—and carried a purse of coin she'd counted four times, because anxiety always made her recount things, and a folded page torn from the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, because apparently she had lost her mind somewhere between the cellar stairs and now.

"You're doing the thing," said Maren, falling into step beside her.

Maren was a fence of indeterminate age who smelled of cloves and lye and could appraise a stolen crown jewel in the time it took most people to say good evening. She was also, in the loosest and most deniable sense, Paloma's friend.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're counting your breathing. You do it before every bad negotiation." Maren offered her a clove, and Paloma took it mostly to have something to do with her hands. "What are you buying?"

"Passage."

"Passage where?"

"Away."

"Mm." Maren chewed her own clove thoughtfully. "Away is expensive this season. The edict came down from the palace yesterday—all ports watching for gifted leaving without papers. Which means everyone with a boat has doubled their rates overnight, and half of them have also started taking Inquisitor's coin on the side." She glanced sideways. "Which means if you're buying passage *as* what I suspect you might be buying passage as, you want Ferro Vash, pier nine, and you want to be very polite, because Ferro's cousin is a register-clerk now and he gets nervous."

Paloma's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "How do you know about the edict? It's not public yet."

"Sweetheart. Nothing reaches the Saltglass after it reaches the palace. Sometimes before." Maren patted her arm with fingers like knotted rope. "Be careful tonight. And if anyone asks, I sold you pickled eels."

* * *

Pier nine smelled of tar and drowned wood. Ferro Vash turned out to be an enormous man built along the lines of a warehouse door, sitting on a crate beneath a lamp he'd shaded with his own hand so that only his customers could be seen and never himself.

"Voss," he said, before she'd given her name.

Her spine went very still. "You have me confused with someone."

"Probably." He didn't move. "But if I *did* know a storm-caller looking for quiet water out of the city, I'd tell her the price just went up. Not because of the edict." He leaned forward into the lamplight, and his eyes were flat and patient as a fisherman watching a line. "Because of what she's carrying under her coat."

The market noise seemed to recede, the way it does when your body decides, sensibly, that it would rather be elsewhere.

"Everyone's carrying something," Paloma said. "That's rather the point of a market."

"Not everyone carries pages that smell like lightning." He held up one huge hand before she could speak. "Relax. If I'd sold you already, we wouldn't be talking. But understand your position, little clerk: there are people asking after those pages. Serious people. People who pay in royal scrip." He smiled, and it was not a comforting arrangement of teeth. "So here's my offer. Passage for two—you and whoever's waiting in your cellar, don't bother denying him, the whole tannery district heard about the guild's pet heir years ago—out on the tide tomorrow night. Quiet water, clean papers. In exchange: you tell me what the ledgers open."

The world tilted slightly. Paloma gripped the edge of the crate and kept her voice level through what felt like sheer mechanical effort. "I don't know what you mean."

"Ferro," said a new voice behind her, dry as old paper, "you enormous sentimental fool. You're supposed to let them make the first mistake."

Paloma turned.

The woman leaning against a bollard was perhaps fifty, dressed like a prosperous spice merchant, with a ledger-satchel of her own slung across one shoulder—except Paloma knew that satchel. She'd seen its twin in the vault, and the year-stamp on its spine, and the small burned mark on the clasp shaped like a closed eye.

"That satchel," Paloma said slowly, "is Guild property."

"It's *guild* property," the woman agreed pleasantly, "in the way that I am a spice merchant. My name used to be Odile Ferrant. For thirty years I was keeper of the western annex. And then I made the same discovery you're making right now, dear—which is that the ledgers don't belong to the guild at all. They never did. We were janitors. Very well-paid janitors, keeping a firehouse we couldn't see the fire inside of." She pushed off the bollard and looked Paloma up and down with the frank assessment of one professional recognizing another. "And now the crown wants them. Which tells me the crown knows more than we ever did."

"How much does the crown know?" The question left her before she could weigh it.

Odile's eyebrows rose. "There it is. That's the face of a woman who's touched the pages." She sighed, and some of the merchant polish dropped away, and underneath was something tired and frightened that Paloma recognized from her own mirror. "Sit down, child. Both of you listen, because I'll only say this once, and then Ferro will name his real price, and then we all pretend this conversation was about eels."

Paloma sat on the crate. The harbor lapped against the pilings below, black and indifferent.

"The queen's hunt isn't a purge," Odile said. "I spent two years learning that, and it cost me—" she flexed her left hand, which ended at the wrist in an old, neat scar, "—more than I'd budgeted. The registrations aren't for burning the gifted. Oh, they burn a few, publicly, for appetite. But most of them go east. To the palace vaults. There's a reason the royal line hasn't produced a healthy heir in sixty years, and there's a reason the old wars stopped the moment the dragons were caged, and both reasons are written in books that answer to your blood." She tapped her own temple. "The crown doesn't want the gifted dead. It wants them *harvested*. And it wants the key that wakes the fire, because whatever's killing the royal bloodline, dragon-fire was holding it back—and now the fire's asleep, and the rot is winning."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere across the water, a church bell counted a hour Paloma didn't hear.

"They're coming for the ledgers," Paloma said.

"They've been coming for thirty years. Slowly. Because they need a Voss to read them, and they thought the line was dead." Odile smiled thinly. "And then, nine years ago, a girl with storm in her hands walked into the Guild of Impossible Things and asked Master Quill for work. And somebody in the palace keeps better records than either of us ever did."

* * *

Afterward, Paloma couldn't remember walking away from the pier. She remembered pieces: Ferro naming his price—not the ledgers' secret, but a favor owed, unnamed, uncapped, which was somehow worse; Maren materializing at her elbow with a basket of genuinely terrible eels; the green-black canals sliding past like something patient.

She remembered most clearly the moment she realized they were being followed.

It wasn't obvious. That was what made it excellent. Three separate shoppers who'd been near her at the pier were near her now, spaced too evenly through the crowd, each browsing with the particular stillness of people who had been trained to browse. Gray coats. Sensible boots. The middle one—a young man with a forgettable face—was carrying a lantern from the Cathedral of the Quiet Flame, and the flame inside it was burning white.

Witch-lanterns burned white near unregistered gifts.

Hers must be singing like a struck bell.

*Maren,* she thought desperately, and Maren, reading her face with the speed of thirty years' trade, steered them both toward the stall of a woman selling cursed mirrors by shouting cheerfully about discounts, and pressed the basket into Paloma's arms, and vanished into the crowd with a professionalism Paloma intended to repay for the rest of her life.

The mirrors surrounded her—dozens of them, tarnished silver, each showing her a slightly wrong version of herself. In one, her hair was wet. In another, she was older. In a third, she was smiling, which was frankly insulting under the circumstances.

In the mirror directly in front of her, she saw the young man stop at the edge of the stall and pretend to examine a hand-glass, and she saw, reflected over his shoulder, the gray coat of a second agent drifting to block the far exit.

They weren't arresting her. That was the detail that turned her blood to ice water. If Serane wanted her arrested, she'd be in chains. They were *following* her. Letting her run her errands. Watching who she met.

Which meant the Inquisitor already had everything she needed except one thing.

Except me, Paloma thought. Except the key itself. She's not hunting the ledgers. She's herding me.

The mirror-vendor caught her eye and, with the flawless instinct of the Saltglass, said nothing at all, merely turned up the price tags on the mirrors nearest the exits—a merchant's warning that this customer was about to leave in a hurry.

Paloma set down the eel basket, walked calmly out the unblocked side of the stall, and did not run until the third turning.

* * *

Kael was waiting at the cellar door, and one look at her face told him everything, the way fire knows smoke.

"Tell me," he said.

She told him. All of it—Ferro's price, Odile's ruined hand, the harvest, the dying royal line, the three gray coats, the white-burning lantern. Her voice stayed steady until the last part, and then it didn't quite.

"She let me walk the whole market, Kael. Every stall. She could have taken me at any moment and she *let me shop*." Paloma pressed her back against the cellar wall and slid down it until she was sitting among the dust and the old straw, the ledger heavy in her lap like a sleeping animal. "Do you know why? Because escape was never on the table. There is no table. She wanted to see if I'd try to run, because if I ran, I'd lead her to every other gifted person in this city trying to buy their way out. I almost did. I traded secrets to a smuggler tonight that could get Odile killed, that could get Maren killed—"

"Paloma."

"—and the worst part, the truly humiliating part, is that the passage was real. Ferro would have honored it. We could have been on quiet water by tomorrow night. Two fugitives and a book, gone—"

"*Paloma.*" Kael crouched in front of her, and his eyes had that banked glow she was beginning to recognize as the look of a man assembling a terrible sentence. "Odile said the crown needs a Voss to open them. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And the queen's line is dying without the fire."

"Yes."

"Then think about what happens next." He said it gently, which made it worse. "She doesn't need to chase you anymore. She only needs to take everything else away until the ledgers become the last door left standing. Your guildhall. Your friends. Me." A pause. "Especially me. I've been collateral for thirty years, Paloma. Collateral is what you collect *first*."

The silence that followed had weight and temperature. Above them, muffled through floorboards, the city went on with its ordinary midnight—and somewhere in it, Paloma knew, a woman in gray was writing a report that already knew how it ended.

Paloma looked down at the ledger in her lap. Under her palms, faint as a pulse, the pages were warm.

"Then we stop waiting for her to choose the ground," she said quietly. "Tomorrow I finish reading. All of it."

Kael nodded once, and under his ribs, the old fire leaned forward into the dark.
