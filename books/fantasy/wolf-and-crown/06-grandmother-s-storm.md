## 6. Grandmother's Storm

Kael insisted the lesson begin with tea.

"Reading the ledgers is like wrestling a river," he said, setting two chipped cups on an upturned crate in the cellar. "You don't do it by force. You do it by knowing where the current wants to go." He paused. "Also, you'll want something warm in your hands afterward. The first time, I cried into my soup for an hour."

"You had soup?"

"I had *broth*. It was a bad decade."

Paloma wrapped her hands around her cup and studied him across the candlelight. Thirty years in a cellar, and he still had the habit of arranging things—cups, crates, candles—with the fussy precision of someone who'd once been heir to a throne of fire and now owned four objects total.

"All right," she said. "Teach me."

He drew out one of the ledger-pages she'd smuggled from the vault, the paper gone soft as moth-wing with age. In the candlelight, the ink didn't look like ink at all. It looked like weather trapped under glass.

"The mistake everyone makes," Kael said, "is reading with your eyes. These weren't written for eyes. They were written for blood. A Voss wrote them so that only a Voss could carry them forward." He turned the page toward her. "Your grandmother didn't keep records, Paloma. She kept *witnesses*."

"What does that mean?"

"It means stop reading and start listening."

She touched the page.

The static came first—that familiar prickle crawling up her fingers, the taste of copper and ozone on her tongue. Then the cellar fell away.

---

*Maren Voss stood on a ridge above a burning valley, and the wind was screaming.*

*Not the wind. The dragons. Six of them wheeling over the lowlands, their fire turning the harvest fields to rivers of light, and Maren—twenty-three years old, hair whipping loose from its braid, ink-stained fingers gripping a ledger to her chest like a shield—was running toward them.*

*"MAREN!" someone shouted behind her. "The covenant says we record! We do not intervene!"*

*"The covenant," she shouted back, "can bite me."*

---

Paloma gasped and pulled her hand back. The cellar reassembled itself around her: candle, crate, Kael's steady face.

"There you are," he said gently. "Breathe. It gets easier. Well—it doesn't get easier. You just get better at breathing through it."

"That was—" Her voice cracked. She tried again. "That was my grandmother. That was *her*. She was young. She was—" Paloma laughed, a strange, wet sound. "She told the covenant to bite her."

"Maren Voss was famous for it. Among the maybe six people who remember she existed." Kael refilled her cup. "Again. Longer this time. Let the memory finish before you surface. If you come up mid-storm, you'll bring the storm with you—and I've only just gotten this cellar organized."

It took her three tries to find the page again, because her hands were shaking. But the fourth time—

---

*The war had been going for eleven years when Maren understood what no one else would say aloud: nobody remembered how it started.*

*She'd checked. She was the guild's youngest ledger-keeper, and checking was her whole soul. She'd read every account, every treaty, every drunken veteran's memoir. The dragons had burned the eastern provinces—or the eastern provinces had stolen the dragon eggs, depending on which ledger you opened. Both stories were written in identical, confident hands.*

*"Someone is lying," she told the council of keepers, "or everyone is lying, or—"*

*"Or there was never a truth to lose," said old Keeper Aldous, who was ninety and blind and could still out-argue anyone alive. "Child, wars don't start. They accrete. Like debt."*

*Maren hated that. She was twenty-four and believed debts could always be reconciled if you found the original entry.*

*She would spend forty years learning how wrong she was, and another ten learning why it didn't matter.*

---

"She was a keeper too," Paloma whispered. "A real one. Not—not hiding like me. She did it openly."

"She did everything openly. It was her most infuriating quality." Something moved through Kael's expression—grief wearing the mask of fondness. "I met her once. I was nine. She came to the eyries with her ledgers and interviewed me for three hours about hatchling feeding schedules. I thought she was the most terrifying person alive." He smiled at the candle. "She gave me a honey cake at the end and told me I had good penmanship. I've never recovered."

Paloma stared at him. "You knew her."

"I knew her for one afternoon, forty years ago. And then her ledgers kept me company for thirty years in this room, so between the two, I'd say she's the longest relationship of my life." He tapped the page. "Keep going. You're approaching the part that matters—the caging. But you have to earn it. Memory doesn't skip to the ending any more than storms do."

So she went deeper.

---

*Year nineteen of the war. Maren, thirty-two, standing in a granary that had become a field hospital, holding the hand of a dying boy who had been conscripted at fourteen and had forgotten his mother's face but not his regimental number.*

*"Write it down," he kept saying. "Somebody write it down."*

*"I'm writing," Maren promised. "Everything. Your name. Your number. The song you hummed when they marched you out."*

*"Don't write the song. Write that I was scared and I stayed anyway."*

*She wrote exactly that. Then she walked out into the ash-fall and made a decision that would end her career, her reputation, and nearly her life: the ledgers were done being witnesses. It was time they became weapons.*

---

Paloma surfaced crying, which annoyed her deeply.

"I'm fine," she said, before Kael could ask.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to make a *face*."

"I have a very expressive face for a man who spent thirty years talking to rats."

She wiped her eyes with her sleeve and reached for the page again. "The caging. Show me the caging."

Kael hesitated. For the first time all evening, he looked away from her. "This is the part where you learn what your family actually did, Paloma. Not the story the guild tells—the one where the brave keeper ended the war. The true one." His jaw tightened. "The one where she ended it by stealing the fire itself. By taking the dragons' flame and locking it inside pages, inside bloodlines, inside *you*. She didn't stop the war. She disarmed both sides and let the world pretend that was peace."

"And the dragons?"

"What do you think happened to dragons with no fire?"

The candle flickered. Neither of them spoke for a moment.

"Show me anyway," Paloma said.

---

*The last night of the war, Maren Voss climbed the mountain alone, because she had asked for volunteers and received none, because what she intended was either salvation or the greatest theft in history and no one sane would help her do it.*

*The great dragon Ashfall waited at the summit—old, wounded, half her scales burned away, the last of the great fires. She watched Maren approach without moving.*

***Little keeper,*** the dragon said, in a voice like continents shifting. ***You've come to take it.***

*"Yes."*

***And if I refuse?***

*"Then I die here, and the war goes on, and in fifty years there will be nothing left worth burning." Maren opened her ledger to the final blank page. "I'm not asking as a thief, Ashfall. I'm asking as a witness. Someone has to hold what happened. Someone has to keep it safe until the world deserves it back."*

*The dragon was silent a long time. Wind moved the ash between them like grey snow.*

***My fire is not mine alone,*** Ashfall said at last. ***It is every dragon who died carrying it. If you take it, you take their grief too. Every entry. Every name. You will feel what they felt.***

*"I know."*

***No, little keeper. You know the shape of it. You do not know the weight.***

*"Then tell me honestly," Maren said, and her voice finally shook, "if our positions were reversed—if it were your granddaughter standing here with a blank page—what would you do?"*

*Ashfall lowered her great head until her eye filled Maren's whole sky, an amber sun with a slit pupil.*

***I would burn the world down rather than give it up,*** the dragon said. ***Which is precisely why it must be you.***

*And she breathed her fire—not onto Maren, but into the open page, into the waiting bloodline, into the covenant older than either of them had understood. And Maren Voss held the weight of a thousand years of dragon-memory in her body and did not scream, though later accounts agree she wanted to.*

*When it was done, she knelt in the ash and wrote the final entry of the war ledger:*

*Held in trust. To be returned when the world remembers what it owes.*

---

Paloma came back to herself on the floor of the cellar. She didn't remember sitting down. Kael was beside her, one hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite touching.

"The ledgers," she rasped. "They're not records. They're *Ashfall*. They're the fire itself, banked down to embers, written in—"

"In Voss blood. Every generation, one keeper strong enough to hold the key without burning up. Your grandmother. Your mother, briefly, before—" He stopped. "Before they took her. Yes. I know that part too. I'm sorry."

Paloma lay staring at the ceiling beams, and the grief arrived—not sharp, but vast and slow, like weather. Grief for a woman she'd never met who had carried a continent's sorrow in her spine so the rest of the world could sleep. Rage, too, white-hot and quiet, at every master of the guild who had known what the ledgers were and let them gather dust in a vault while the gifted were registered and burned. At Quill, who had perhaps known. At herself, for nine years of balancing other people's impossible debts while the largest debt of all sat unclaimed three floors above her head.

"Held in trust," she said. "To be returned when the world remembers what it owes."

"That's the covenant. That's why the crown can't simply destroy the ledgers—they need a living key to open them safely. And why they need *you* dead only if you won't cooperate." Kael's voice hardened. "Serane isn't hunting you, Paloma. She's harvesting you."

Paloma sat up slowly. Above them, muffled by stone and floorboards, came a sound she knew too well: the guildhall's front bell, rung twice, hard. The signal for unscheduled visitors.

Then Master Quill's voice, pitched high and wrong: "Inquisitor! What an unexpected—of course, of course, come in, the vault is entirely—"

Kael was already on his feet, snuffing the candle. In the dark, his eyes caught the light like a banked fire.

"They're early," he said.
