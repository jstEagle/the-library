## 7. The Price of Staying Invisible

The morning the raid came, Paloma was arguing with a teapot.

Not metaphorically. The guildhall's kitchen possessed exactly one kettle that hadn't been confiscated, cursed, or eaten by a client's familiar, and it had developed opinions about heat. She was thumping its lid when Kael came down the cellar stairs two steps at a time, and she knew before he spoke—knew by the way the storm under her skin turned over in its sleep like a dog hearing a strange knock.

"They're coming," he said. "Serane. Now. Maybe twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes." Paloma set down the kettle. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. "That's not enough time to move the ledgers."

"It's enough time to burn them."

"No." The word came out flat and final, and even she heard how much of her grandmother was in it. "We've been through this."

"We have, and you keep winning on sentiment." Kael braced his hands on the table. He'd filled out since she'd pulled him from that cellar—thirty years underground had left him gray-faced and hollow, but three weeks of daylight and decent food had put color back in him, and something else too: a terrible, patient calm. "Paloma. If they take those pages, they don't just get a weapon. They get *her*. Every memory your grandmother fed into the fire, every name of every dragon still sleeping under this city. Serane wouldn't need to hunt the gifted anymore. She could just open the cage and let the crown harvest us all at once."

"I know what's at stake."

"Do you? Because I think you're protecting the book instead of the people in it."

"That's unfair," she said, and then, because he raised one eyebrow with the precision of a man who had spent decades learning to read faces through bars, "and possibly accurate. But burning them means losing her. The last of her. I only just found her, Kael. I'm not going to be the second person to bury my grandmother."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he sighed, the sigh of a man conceding an argument he intended to win by other means.

"Then we do it your way," he said. "Badly, probably, but your way."

* * *

Master Quill met them in the vault with the expression of a man who has already done the arithmetic and doesn't care for the total.

The old guildmaster had never struck Paloma as brave. He was a small, fussy, ink-stained creature who alphabetized his complaints and once made her rewrite an entire ledger because she'd used brown ink where black was specified. But he stood now in front of the iron cabinet holding the anomalous pages, keys in hand, and said, "You'll go out through the coal chute. It opens into the tanner's yard. Smells appalling, which is rather the point."

"You're coming with us," Paloma said.

"Don't be absurd. Someone has to answer the door." He patted the cabinet almost tenderly. "Besides, I've hidden things from inspectors for forty years. Inquisitors are merely inspectors with worse posture. Go on. And Voss—" he caught her sleeve as she passed, and his voice dropped to something she'd never heard him use, quiet and unarmored, "—the entry you wrote last month. The one about the boy in Salt Row whose debts I forgave and told no one. I read it. It was good work. You balance more than numbers, girl. Don't ever let anyone tell you different."

She wanted to argue. There was no time. Boots were already hammering up Copper Lane, and the front knocker went off like a war drum.

They went out through the coal chute, into the appalling smell, and were halfway across the tanner's yard when the sky over the guildhall changed color.

* * *

Paloma felt it before she understood it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of copper, the storm under her skin sitting up and *howling*. She spun. Over the rooftops, lightning crawled across the guildhall's weathervane like ivy growing in fast-forward.

"She's drawing it out of me," Paloma whispered. "Somehow she's—I'm not doing this—"

"No," Kael said grimly. "But whatever they're using to pull, it works both ways. Which means everyone within a mile can feel what you feel right now." He grabbed her wrist. "Run."

They ran. Through the tanner's yard, down the drainage alley, past a fishmonger who took one look at their faces and decided he hadn't seen them. Behind them the guildhall's windows lit blue-white, and the first thunder cracked so hard that pigeons fell off the roof of the chandler's shop like ripe fruit.

And then, cutting through the thunder, a sound Paloma would hear for the rest of her life: Master Quill's voice, pitched high and carrying, the voice of a man performing outrage for an audience.

"—*absolutely not*, madam, this is a licensed repository, you cannot simply—do you have a warrant? A *warrant*-warrant, not one of those dreadful printed ones—"

A crash. Glass. Then silence, and after the silence, worse than any sound, Serane's voice, level as a ledger line:

"Search the vault. Bring me everything written in a woman's hand."

Kael stopped so suddenly Paloma nearly lost him in the dark. She saw him turn back toward the guildhall, saw the decision assemble itself behind his eyes with terrible efficiency, and she grabbed his arm.

"Don't."

"He's dying in there."

"Yes."

"He bought us thirty seconds. Thirty seconds isn't enough to save anyone, including us."

"I'm not trying to save anyone." Kael's voice was very quiet. "I'm trying to make sure it costs them something. That's the whole point of being collateral, Paloma—you learn that your life is currency, and you learn to spend it well." He peeled her fingers off his sleeve, gently, one at a time, the way you'd handle something breakable. "Keep running. Keep the pages dry. When the dragons wake—and they will wake—tell them the heir remembers them."

"Kael—"

"Also," he added, already turning, and there it was, the ghost of the grin she'd coaxed out of him over three weeks of stolen meals and whispered histories, "you owe me for the kettle argument. I was right about the ledgers and you're going to live long enough to admit it."

Then he walked back up the alley toward the lightning, straightening his collar like a man going to a dinner party, and Paloma ran, because staying would have killed them both and saved nothing, and hating the mathematics did not change them.

* * *

She made it four streets before the storm caught her.

It wasn't Serane's pulling anymore—it was hers, answering, grief and terror and nine years of swallowed weather all rising at once, and she understood with awful clarity that if she didn't choose where it went, it would choose for her. So she chose. She climbed the chandler's stairs to the roof, spread her arms over the empty drainage canal, and let go.

The storm left her like a confession.

Rain came down in ropes. Lightning stitched the clouds together above the canal and nowhere else—she aimed it, actually aimed it, the way her grandmother must have aimed it once upon a time, funneling the whole shrieking weight of the sky into a column of water between her and the pursuit. Somewhere below, soldiers shouted and slipped and gave up the chase. Somewhere behind her, the guildhall burned with ordinary fire, the kind that takes books and furniture and small fussy men who alphabetize their kindnesses.

When it was over, Paloma knelt on wet shingles with her hair standing on end and her skin bare and silent, and listened for the storm under her skin.

Nothing.

For nine years she had carried it like a stone, cursing its weight, hiding it, feeding it lies about itself. Now the space where it lived was just—space. Quiet as a swept room. She pressed her palm flat against her own chest and felt only a heartbeat, mortal and unremarkable, and she began to laugh, or cry, or some ruinous combination of the two, alone on a rooftop in the rain with a satchel of her dead grandmother's memories strapped across her back.

She had wanted to stay invisible. Well. No one could overlook her now. Every gifted soul in the city had felt that storm pass through them like a bell through the body, and every one of them knew its shape, knew it the way you know a family face. *There*, they'd say for years afterward. *That was a Voss storm. That was the last one.*

* * *

She found the safehouse near midnight, soaked, hollowed, half-frozen. Mirren the fence let her in without questions, which frightened her more than questions would have—the woman always had questions, priced and itemized.

"There's a notice going up on the gates," Mirren said instead, handing her a towel and a cup of something hot and disgraceful. "Went to the printers this evening. They're calling it a proclamation." Her eyes flicked to the satchel, then away, professional courtesy. "Storm-caller sighted, district of the Tanners' Canal. Description follows. Reward follows that. It's a big reward, Voss. The kind that makes friends expensive."

Paloma dried her hair and drank the disgraceful cup and thought about Master Quill's keys, and Kael's collar, and the swept-room silence in her chest.

"And Kael?" she asked. "The heir. Is there anything about—"

Mirren hesitated. That was the worst part, later—remembering that Mirren had hesitated, had chosen her words with visible care, which meant the truth was bad and she was deciding how much of it Paloma could survive tonight.

"He's alive," Mirren said finally. "They took him to the palace, not the cells. That's either very good or very bad, and I honestly don't know which." She refilled the cup. "There's one more thing. The proclamation's got a seal on it I've never seen. Old one. Dragon-and-crown, interlocked. It's on the queen's private writs, they say. Has been for generations."

Paloma set down the cup. Outside, the rain kept falling, gentle now, ordinary, rain that belonged to no one.

Her grandmother's memories stirred in the satchel against her hip—pages keyed to dragon-fire, waiting for blood—and for the first time since the vault, Paloma felt something in that swept-out room respond. Not the storm. Something older. An inheritance, knocking.

The dragons' seal, on the queen's writ. The harvest wearing a hunting license.

She looked at the window, where the distant palace glowed on its hill like a banked fire, and understood that she had been running from the wrong building all along.

Tomorrow, she would stop running toward anything—and start walking straight at it.
