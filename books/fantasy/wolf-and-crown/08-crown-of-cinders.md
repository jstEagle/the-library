## 8. Crown of Cinders

Inquisitor Serane had burned forty-one people, and she remembered every one of their names.

This was not required by the Office of Holy Inquiry. The manuals said nothing about names. The manuals were concerned with procedure: the writ, the reading of charges, the three opportunities to recant, the oil, the pyre, the certification of ash. What a name was to any of that, the manuals did not say. But Serane had learned, in her first year, that if she stopped saying the names aloud before each burning—if she let them become cases, numbers, entries—she would stop being able to do the work at all.

So she kept them. Forty-one names, recited on cold mornings like a rosary, each one a small debt she intended never to repay because repayment was impossible and pretending otherwise was vanity.

She was reciting them now, walking the long colonnade toward the queen's private audience chamber, when a page caught up with her, breathless, holding out a sealed letter.

"From the guildhall district, Inquisitor. The watch says—the woman Voss walked into the garrison an hour ago. Voluntarily. She's asking for you."

Serane stopped. Somewhere below, the city went about its morning: carts, gulls, a knife-grinder's wheel singing against steel. None of it knew what had just happened.

"She's asking for me," Serane repeated.

"Yes, my lady. She also asked for tea. She said—" the page consulted his memory with visible anxiety, "—she said, 'the good kind, not whatever the crown pretends is tea.'"

Forty-two, thought Serane, and then corrected herself, because the woman had come willingly, which changed everything and nothing. She tucked the letter away unopened. Whatever was written there, she would hear it from Paloma Voss's own mouth or not at all.

* * *

They held her in the inquiry cells beneath the garrison, which were clean, dry, and designed by someone who believed cruelty should be administrative rather than theatrical. Paloma Voss sat on the cot as though it were a bench in a very dull waiting room, hands folded, satchel nowhere in evidence—surrendered at the door, along with her knives, her lockpicks, and a small tin of candied ginger that the sergeant had confiscated and then, Serane noticed later, eaten.

"You wanted tea," Serane said, setting down two cups. "The garrison has one kind. It tastes like boiled regret."

"Then we understand each other." Paloma took the cup anyway. "You came yourself. I half expected a subordinate."

"The writ for your capture has my seal on it. I don't delegate my own signature." Serane sat across from her, laid her ledger of proceedings on the table between them—a smaller ledger than the famous ones, but hers, kept in the same meticulous hand. "Before we begin: Master Kael of the dragon line, currently held at Torvask Tower. You'll want to know his condition."

"I know his condition. You want something from him, so he's alive. The question is how long 'alive' remains useful." Paloma drank her terrible tea without flinching, which Serane privately filed under *impressive*. "Here is my offer. Me for him. Full confession, full cooperation, no pyre. He walks out of the city by sundown tomorrow with a pardon under your seal."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I tell you nothing, and you spend a year pulling it out of me, and Kael dies of old age in a tower before you're done, and the queen burns, and you burn with her, and none of us get what we want." Paloma smiled—it was not a pleasant smile, but it was an honest one. "I've read your file, Inquisitor. You're thorough. Thorough people hate waste."

Serane looked at her for a long moment. Forty-one names, and this one might make forty-two, or might make something else entirely.

"You're negotiating for another person's life," Serane said, "with your own as currency. That's either bravery or arithmetic gone wrong."

"It's bookkeeping," said Paloma Voss, keeper of ledgers, and Serane felt, absurdly, a flicker of kinship.

* * *

The queen received them in the vault beneath the palace, because Her Majesty had stopped receiving anyone anywhere else.

Serane had been down here four times in nine years, and every visit it was worse. Not messier—nothing in the royal presence was ever messy—but *emptier*. The great iron cressets that lined the walls had once burned with a flame that cast no heat and needed no fuel. Old portraits showed them blazing white-gold. Now they guttered like ordinary fire, and two of the twelve were simply dark.

The queen stood among them, small and straight-backed, wrapped in furs in a room that should have been warm. She was fifty-three years old and looked eighty, and everyone at court called it *austerity*, because no one dared call it dying.

"Inquisitor," the queen said, without turning. "You brought her."

"I brought her, Majesty. Under protest regarding terms."

"Your protests are noted and boring." The queen turned then, and Serane watched Paloma Voss see her for the first time—and saw the smuggler's daughter, the ledger-keeper, the woman who had stared down pyres and inquisitors without blinking, go pale.

Not at the queen's face. At the queen's *shadow*. Or rather, at the place where the shadow frayed at the edges, thinning into something translucent, the way paper thins where too many hands have touched it.

"What is she?" Paloma whispered.

"Dying," said the queen, with the mildness of a woman discussing weather. "We all are, girl. Mine is merely more instructive. Come here. Look at the cressets. Tell me what you feel."

Paloma approached one of the guttering flames the way one approaches a sleeping animal. When she was close, her breath hitched.

"There are voices in it. Memories. Hundreds of years of—" she stopped. "These aren't lamps. They're *ledgers*."

"They are dragons," said the queen. "Or what remains of them. Nine flames, caged at the end of the last war, bound by a Voss hand—your grandmother's, I believe, though the family has been disappointingly slippery about its history. Each flame holds a life. A memory. A *fire*. And for two hundred years, that fire has fed the royal line." She gestured at herself, at the fraying shadow, at the dark cressets. "Longevity. Health. The vigor to rule. Every king and queen since the Accords has drunk, knowingly or not, from these wells. And the wells are running dry, because a caged fire consumes itself."

The silence in the vault was total. Even the guttering flames seemed to lean in.

"So when the gifted began appearing again—storm-callers, ember-witches, blood-readers—we did not hunt them out of fear," the queen continued. "Fear is for people who have something to lose. We hunted them because each one carries a spark of the old fire. Registered and brought to the palace, that spark can be drawn out, refined, added to the wells. A storm-caller yields perhaps five years of a sovereign's life. An ember-witch, seven." She looked at Paloma with eyes gone pale as ash. "A dragon-heir like your friend Kael could sustain the throne for a century. Which is why he has spent thirty years in a cellar, being kept fresh."

"A harvest," Paloma said flatly.

"A *stewardship*," snapped the queen, and for one moment the frayed edges of her blazed. "You think I enjoy this? Two hundred years ago your grandmother ended a war by caging the fire, and the price of her peace was paid by my line—slow death, generation by generation, so that yours could live safe and ignorant and *self-righteous*. I am spending human beings to keep a kingdom from tearing itself apart over succession, and I will spend them until there are no more, and history will call me a monster and be correct, and the kingdom will still be standing. That is what ruling *is*, girl. Now show me the pages."

* * *

Later—much later—Serane stood alone in the vault while the queen slept above, and did the thing she did after every difficult day. She recited names.

But tonight the ritual snagged, because a new question had wedged itself in among the forty-one, and it refused to be recited away:

*If the pyres feed the wells—then every name I burned went into those flames. Did any part of them live? Did anything of them remain?*

She had always told herself the burnings were mercy of a grim kind: quick fire instead of slow harvest, dignity instead of the cellar. She had built a whole architecture of duty on top of that belief, floor by careful floor, until she could stand on it.

And now the queen had shown her, with the offhand cruelty of the truly desperate, that the pyre and the cellar were the same building. That Serane had not been choosing mercy. She had been choosing *packaging*.

"I need air," she told the guard, who wisely did not point out that they were sixty feet underground.

In the corridor outside the vault, Paloma Voss waited under escort, satchel-less, free hands folded, looking for all the world like a woman waiting for a delayed coach. When Serane approached, she didn't ask what the queen had said. She'd heard most of it through the door.

"Well," Paloma said. "Now you know what you've been hunting for."

"I knew," Serane said quietly. "Some part of me has known since the twelfth burning. One chooses not to look directly at certain sums. It's bad practice, but it keeps the books balanced." She hesitated. Then, because thoroughness was the only virtue she had left: "Your offer stands. Kael for you. But understand what you're buying him into. Freedom in a kingdom that harvests the gifted is a longer sentence than a cell."

"He survived thirty years in a cellar," Paloma said. "He can survive a road. Besides—" and here she leaned closer, and her voice dropped to something that was not quite a whisper and not quite a promise, "—you didn't search the satchel properly, Inquisitor. Your people catalogued knives, papers, dried fruit. They did not catalogue what the papers *are*. My grandmother didn't just cage nine fires. She wrote down the key. And the key isn't in the palace vault. It's in my blood, and it's already been opened."

Serane went very still. "Opened. Where?"

"The final ledger was in the palace vault, wasn't it? Under guard. Waiting." Paloma smiled the honest, unpleasant smile again. "Ask your queen what woke beneath the capital last night. Ask her why the ground under the eastern district is warm. And then ask yourself, Inquisitor—which of us has been the instrument, and which of us has finally picked up the pen."

Far above them, muffled through sixty feet of stone, the palace bells began to ring—not the hour, not an alarm, but the old emergency pattern that hadn't sounded in living memory. The bells of a city discovering, all at once, that its foundations were breathing.

Serane closed her eyes. Forty-one names, and somewhere beneath her feet, forty-one flames—or things like flames—were waking up.

"Take me to the vault," she said to the guards. "Both of us. Now."

Paloma raised an eyebrow. "Changing sides already, Inquisitor?"

"No," said Serane, already moving. "Balancing mine."
