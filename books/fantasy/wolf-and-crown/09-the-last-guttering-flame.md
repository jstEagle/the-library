## 9. The Last Guttering Flame

The palace vault smelled of cold iron and old money, which was to say it smelled of everything Paloma had spent nine years despising.

They brought her down in chains that weren't necessary. She knew that the moment the first guard's hand trembled near her wrist. Whatever Serane had told them about the prisoner, the guards had heard the other stories too—the storm that had swallowed a city block, the witch who walked out of a burning guildhall without a singed hair. They kept their distance the way one keeps distance from a lit fuse, and Paloma, who had spent her whole life being small on purpose, found she did not hate it.

Serane walked beside her. The inquisitor's armor was immaculate, her face less so. Something had gone out of her since the guildhall—some belief, some load-bearing wall—and what remained stood only through stubborn architecture.

"You understand what you're agreeing to," Serane said. It wasn't a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere above ground, in the corridor where Paloma had said *take me instead* and meant it.

"I open the ledger. The queen gets her fire. Kael walks free." Paloma's chains clicked with each step. "You get to keep telling yourself you're not a butcher. Everyone profits."

Serane's jaw tightened. "The queen is dying, Voss. Whatever you think of the crown—"

"I don't think of the crown. I think of the ledgers." Paloma paused at the vault door, where two more guards stood with the particular blankness of men who had been ordered not to listen. "Your queen has been drinking dragon-fire through a straw for a hundred years and calling it a bloodline. My grandmother caged the last of it, and the crown has been starving ever since. That's not a dynasty. That's a debt."

She said it and felt the strangest thing: not fear, but *accounting*. Nine years of balancing impossible books, and here at last was the entry that balanced everything else.

The vault door swung open on a room that should not have existed beneath a palace.

* * *

The final ledger sat on a pedestal of black stone, and it was breathing.

Paloma had seen the guild's ledgers do small things—static under her fingertips, a page turning itself when a memory pressed too hard against its binding. This was different. The book was the size of a door, bound in scales that had gone grey as ash, and with each of Paloma's breaths, it breathed a half-second later, like an echo learning to be a voice.

Around it, the vault held everything the crown had harvested. Shelves of reliquaries. Jars of storm-light, dim and drowning. And along the far wall, behind glass that had been enchanted to look like nothing at all, the ledgers—*all* the ledgers, the guild's whole stolen memory, every impossible thing ever bought, sold, or buried.

"They brought them here after the guildhall fell," Serane said quietly. "Everything Quill died protecting. It's all in this room."

Paloma touched the glass. On the other side, a ledger with a scorched cover—she knew that scorch, had made that scorch, the night she'd hidden pages in a shipment of cursed mirrors—seemed to lean toward her hand.

*Hello, little keeper,* the vault said, in the voice of every entry she had ever read. *You're late.*

"I know," she said. "The traffic was terrible."

Serane gave her a look that said *this is not a joking matter*, which was, in Paloma's professional opinion, the exact moment a matter most required joking.

She unclasped the chains herself. The guards made no move to stop her; Serane made no move to order them. Perhaps the inquisitor had finally done the arithmetic Paloma had done in the wagon on the way down: that a woman about to feed her blood to a dragon-ledger was not meaningfully improved by restraints.

The blade Serane offered her was ceremonial, gilded, absurd. Paloma took it anyway.

"For the record," she said, "I want it noted in whatever records you keep that I am doing this freely. Not under edict. Not under threat. The queen gets her fire because I choose to give it—and the gifted get to live because I choose that too. Write it down exactly like that."

"I'll write it down," Serane said. And for one unguarded moment, the inquisitor looked at her the way one looks at a door one has been ordered to guard but never once thought to open.

Paloma drew the blade across her palm.

* * *

The blood hit the cover and the vault *woke*.

It began with her grandmother. Of course it did. The first page turned and Maera Voss was there—not a memory of a memory, not the secondhand grief Paloma had carried since chapter and verse, but *Maera*, young and furious, standing on a ridge while dragons blackened the sky, holding the last ledger against her chest like a shield and a wound at once.

*We cannot kill them,* her grandmother was saying, to someone Paloma couldn't see. *They are the memory of the world. But we cannot let them burn it either. So we do the coward's arithmetic. We cage the fire. We keep the accounts. And we pray someone braver comes along to undo it.*

"Hello, Grandmother," Paloma whispered. "Someone braver came along. She's me. She's also extremely tired."

The ledger drank her blood and gave back everything.

Every caged memory ignited at once—that was the only way to describe it, and Paloma was a ledger-keeper, she knew her vocabulary. The shelves of reliquaries lit from within. The jars of storm-light flared like drowned stars surfacing. The grey scales of the great book flushed color—red, gold, the deep banked orange of a fire that has been patient for a hundred years—and Paloma felt each entry as it woke, hundreds of them, thousands, every impossible thing the guild had ever catalogued rising through her blood like a tide remembering the moon.

And beneath the palace—beneath the city, beneath the whole patient weight of the capital—something enormous rolled over in its sleep and *opened its eyes*.

The floor shivered. Dust fell from the vault ceiling in golden threads. Far above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, came a sound Paloma had only ever heard in inherited memory: the first horn-note of a dragon's waking, so deep it wasn't heard so much as *remembered*.

"It's starting," Serane said. She had her hand on her sword, which was the funniest thing Paloma had ever seen, and she was laughing—she realized distantly that she was laughing—when the first crack split the vault wall.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers had never told her, because the ledgers were honest and the truth was unbearable: waking the fire was the easy part.

The dragons rose beneath the capital like a second city deciding to exist. Paloma felt them through the ledger's open throat—wings unfolding in the dark under the world, scales shedding a century of sleep, hunger waking first because hunger always wakes first. And the hunger had no address. It had no loyalty. It was fire, and fire, unaimed, burns the nearest thing, and the nearest thing was a city of two million souls who had never signed any ledger at all.

*They will rise through the streets,* her grandmother's memory said, calm as an entry in a margin. *Unless someone stands at the throat of the flame and gives it a direction. That is the last entry, little keeper. That is the one no one has ever had the courage to write.*

Paloma stood at the throat of the flame.

The ledger's light poured over her like a waterfall made of every debt ever owed, and she understood—finally, completely, with the terrible clarity of a balanced column—what the direction would cost. The fire needed a will to wrap itself around. A hand to aim it. And the only hand in the room that the ledgers recognized, the only bloodline in a hundred years, was hers.

It would take the gift. All of it. The storm under her skin that she had spent nine years strangling, that she had hated and hidden and secretly, shamefully, loved—the only thing that had ever made her *hers* and not merely a Voss, a keeper, a key.

She thought about arguing with it. She was, by nature, an arguer. She had argued with Master Quill about interest rates, with smugglers about tariffs, with the universe generally about the unfairness of being born interesting in an age that burned interesting things.

But the argument collapsed the moment she actually held it up to the light, because she had already made this choice. She had made it in the corridor above, trading herself for Kael. She had made it in the guildhall, unleashing a storm she couldn't take back. Every step of her ridiculous, ledger-balancing life had been leading to a column with only one entry left, and the entry was this:

*Paid in full: one gift, one storm, one Paloma Voss. Received: everyone else.*

"Serane," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "When the fire turns away from the city—when the dragons go east, to the peaks—tell them what I told you. That it was chosen. That the last storm-caller in the capital chose it, freely, and she'd like it noted that she was *extremely brave* and also that the queen still owes the guild nine years of back rent."

"Voss—"

"And tell Kael—" and here her voice did waver, just once, a single crooked line in an honest column, "—tell him he was the best impossible thing the guild ever kept in a cellar. And that he's free. Write it down exactly like that."

Then she put both hands on the ledger, and gave the fire everything she was.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the last cruelty and the last mercy of it. It felt like a storm deciding, after nine years of rattling at the windows, to finally go *outside*.

The gift poured out of her in a silver column and met the dragon-fire rising through the cracked floor, and the two things that had been at war for a century—the storm and the flame, her grandmother's cage and the crown's hunger—recognized each other, and *married*, and Paloma stood at the wedding holding the pen.

*East,* she told the fire, with the last of her gift, the way one writes a final instruction in a ledger that will outlive its keeper. *Away from the sleeping city. Away from the children and the bakers and the fools who never signed anything. East, to the high peaks, where the world is honest and nothing burns that didn't agree to it.*

The fire went.

She felt it go—felt the dragons turn beneath the city like a river given banks, felt them rise through the mountain's roots and burst into the dawn sky east of the capital, a hundred wings, a hundred voices, the ancient war reborn and immediately, mercifully, pointed *away*. The vault shook. The glass cases shattered. The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things sang in a chord that cracked stone, and Paloma Voss, kneeling at the black pedestal with her empty hands and her empty skin, began to laugh.

Because it was quiet.

For the first time in nine years, the storm under her skin was *quiet*.

She was still laughing—wet, cracked, entirely undignified—when the vault door burst open and Kael came through it like a landslide with opinions, singed, bleeding, dragging two unconscious guards behind him and wearing the expression of a man who has just learned his ransom has been paid and is *furious* about the exchange rate.

"Paloma." He dropped to his knees in front of her, hands hovering over her as if she might be a relic that would bite. "What did you do. What did you *do*—"

"Balanced the books," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her. Small. Ordinary. The voice of a woman with no storms left in her, which was, she was discovering, still a voice. Still hers. "You should see the final column. It's beautiful."

He gathered her up off the floor, and above them, through the broken ceiling, the dawn filled with dragons flying east—and far away, in a guildhall with a scorched door and a cellar that no longer held anyone, an empty ledger waited for a new hand, and a new first entry.

Paloma closed her eyes against the firelight, and started composing it.
