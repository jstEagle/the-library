## 10. Ledger of What Remains

The first frost of the season came on a Tuesday, which Paloma considered typical of it. Weather, in her experience, had never once respected a schedule, and now that she had no gift to argue with it about, she'd made her peace with the rudeness.

She was sweeping the guildhall steps when Kael came up the street carrying two loaves of bread and an expression she had learned to distrust over the past several months.

"You're smiling," she said, leaning the broom against the doorframe. "Either the bread is very good or something has gone catastrophically wrong."

"The bread is adequate." He handed her a loaf, warm through the paper. "A courier came this morning. From the capital."

Paloma's hands went still around the loaf. Couriers from the capital were rarely bearers of good news, though the news had improved considerably since the night the sky over the palace had caught fire and the queen's harvest had ended in cinders and negotiation.

"Truce business?"

"Truce business." Kael's smile widened, and she saw now that it was the careful, delighted smile of a man holding something he knew she'd want. "They've ratified the third article. The gifted are to be registered with the *Guild of Impossible Things*—ours, specifically—as custodians. Not the crown. Not the Inquisition. Us."

Paloma stood very still on her own doorstep, bread cooling in her hands, and felt something in her chest that she had no ledger-entry for.

"They want *us* to keep the registry," she said.

"They want *you* to keep the registry. My name appears in the document as 'the dragon-heir Kael, associate.' I'm an associate, Paloma. I've been promoted from collateral to associate. There's a whole line about it."

"There would be," she said, and had to laugh, because the alternative was crying on her own steps over a piece of bureaucratic parchment, and she had a reputation to maintain. "Come inside before the frost gets the bread. And you're making the tea. My hands are full of loaf and emotion."

* * *

The guildhall had never been grand, even in its smuggling days. Now it was less grand still—the vault emptied of confiscated relics, the mirrors sold off or returned, the cellar where Kael had spent thirty years as a living debt converted into something almost like a room. He'd insisted on keeping the door. "So I remember which way the wind blew," he'd said, and Paloma, who kept ledgers precisely so no one would forget which way the wind had blown, had not argued.

She'd hung new signage, though. Master Quill's old brass plate—*GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS: EST. BY NECESSITY*—had been polished and rehung, and beneath it she'd added a smaller one, painted by her own hand in careful letters:

*ALL DEBTS RECORDED. ALL RECORDS KEPT. NO ONE BURNED.*

The last part had been Kael's suggestion. The middle part was the whole point of her life, as far as she could tell.

The main room smelled of ink and woodsmoke and the particular dust of old paper that Paloma had come to think of as the smell of safety. Shelves lined the walls now—not smuggled relics but ledgers, hundreds of them, the guild's own accounts rescued from Serane's bonfires and the crown's vaults and one memorable drainage ditch. The ledgers of the impossible things. Debts owed to the gifted by the crown, debts owed by the gifted to each other, debts that had no currency except memory.

And on the great desk by the window, open to a fresh page, the new ledger. The one she was writing herself.

Kael filled the kettle and set it over the stove, moving around the kitchen with the unselfconscious ease of a man who had spent thirty years memorizing a single cellar and was now, greedily, memorizing a whole building. He still flinched at enclosed spaces sometimes. He still woke some nights convinced the door was locked. But he'd taken to sleeping with the shutters open, even in frost, because—as he'd explained with the gravity of a man announcing a scientific discovery—"I can hear the dragons better that way."

"They're a hundred miles away," Paloma said.

"I can *hear* them."

She didn't doubt it. On clear nights, when the wind came down from the eastern peaks, she could almost hear them too—a sound below sound, the great slow wingbeats of the seven who had risen from beneath the capital and then, at her cost, at her gift, turned away from the city and gone home to the high places. She felt them the way an amputee feels weather: as an absence with a shape.

Some mornings she woke reaching for the storm under her skin and found only quiet, and the quiet was like a missing tooth her tongue kept finding. Other mornings—the frosty ones, the bread-and-woodsmoke ones—it felt less like loss and more like having set down something very heavy after carrying it a very long way, and discovering her arms still worked.

Today was a heavy-set-down sort of morning. She sat at the great desk, dipped her pen, and wrote in her own hand—the hand that was also her grandmother's hand, though the ledgers no longer answered it, though nothing answered it now but the ordinary friction of ink on ordinary paper:

*Entry the first. On this day the third article was ratified, and the Guild of Impossible Things was made keeper of the registry of the gifted. Let it be recorded that the registry exists so that no one need hide again. Let it be recorded that Master Quill died for the vault and is owed a debt the guild cannot repay and will therefore never stop trying to. Let it be recorded that the tea in this establishment is made by Kael, who was a prisoner for thirty years and makes tea the way other men make war, and that this is the finest tea in the city.*

She heard him snort from the stove. She hadn't known she'd said that last part aloud. She wrote it anyway.

* * *

The first registrant arrived an hour after the kettle's second boiling, and she arrived the way most of the guild's clients did: sideways, apologetically, with one foot already out the door.

Her name was Wren. She was perhaps nineteen, with frost-white hair and the particular hunched posture of someone who had spent her whole life making herself small. She stood in the doorway of the guildhall and looked at the sign—*NO ONE BURNED*—for a long moment before speaking.

"They said—I heard, in the market—that this is where you go. If you're." She made a small, helpless gesture that encompassed her hair, her posture, whatever it was under her skin that the old edicts would have registered or burned.

"That's where you go," Paloma said. "Come in. Sit down. The chair by the window is the good one."

Wren sat in the chair by the window as if it might be a trap. Kael set a cup of tea in front of her without being asked, and Paloma opened the new ledger to a fresh page.

"Now," she said. "There's no test. There's no inspection. There's no Inquisitor behind that shelf, though I'll grant you he'd be very well disguised. I'm going to write your name in this book, and that's all the book does. It remembers. That's the whole of its magic, and I can promise you it's enough, because I've spent nine years learning what happens to things that aren't remembered."

Wren's hands were wrapped around the teacup like it was the last warm thing in the world. "And then what? I'm just—registered? And that's it?"

"And then you're one of ours. Which means if anyone gives you trouble, they're giving the guild trouble, and the guild has a dragon-heir, an excellent grudge, and—" she tapped the ledger "—a written record of everything."

The girl's mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile, but it was the raw material of one. "That's it? That's the impossible thing? A book?"

"A book," Paloma agreed, "is the most impossible thing there is. Everything else burns."

She wrote: *Wren. Storm-adjacent, she thinks—weather-sense, frost in the hair, came in scared and left less so. First of the new registry. May there be a great many more, and may every one of them walk in that door the way they please.*

Wren left an hour later with tea in her and her spine straighter by a degree, and paused at the door to say, "My mother said the Voss woman would burn the ledgers or be burned by them. She said no one could hold that much memory and stay a person."

Paloma looked at the great desk, at the hundreds of ledgers on their shelves, at the empty space where the vault door used to be and the new shelf where her grandmother's pages lived now, copied out in her own hand so that the originals could rest.

"Your mother," Paloma said, "was mostly right. It nearly worked out the way she said. But here's what she didn't know: you don't hold the memory. You hold the *pen*. It's a different job entirely, and it comes with tea."

* * *

Evening came down over the city in the ordinary way, which still felt like a miracle to Paloma. No bells for registration. No ash on the doorstep. Just lamplighters working their rounds and the smell of someone's dinner and, far to the east, faint as a heartbeat, the dragons turning on the wind above their peaks.

She and Kael ate at the great desk because the guild still didn't have a proper table, and because eating among the ledgers felt correct, like the accounts were guests at the meal. He told her about his day—the associate's duties, which so far consisted of arguing with a crown clerk about the wording of the third article. "He wanted 'custodianship' changed to 'supervision.' I explained, at length, the difference between keeping a thing and keeping a thing *down*."

"And?"

"He's drafting new language. I may have opened the window in his office first. It's very high up. He became agreeable."

"You terror."

"I'm an *associate*," Kael said with dignity, and stole the last of the bread.

Afterward, while he banked the stove, Paloma sat with the new ledger and turned back through its pages. Wren's entry. The ratification. The inventory of returned relics. The page where she'd recorded, in full and without flinching, what the opening of the final ledger had cost—her gift, surrendered to the flame, spent like a coin she'd been saving all her life for exactly that purchase. She'd written it plainly, because that was the rule she'd made for herself: the ledger held everything, especially the things that hurt. That was what made it a ledger instead of a lie.

At the back of the book, she found the page she'd been circling for weeks without landing on. The one headed, in her own hand: *Debts the Guild Owes.*

Most of the lines were crossed out—paid, resolved, transformed into tea and shelter and stubbornness. But one entry remained, the oldest one, the one she'd carried from the first chapter of all this:

*To the storm under my skin: owed a life. Paid in full.*

She looked at that line for a long time. Then she dipped her pen and, beneath it, wrote:

*Amended. Not paid. Transformed. The storm is in the peaks now, and the debt is mine to carry in a different coin: paper, ink, and the keeping of names. A fair trade. I would make it again.*

She was blotting the page when Kael said, from the stove, in the careful voice he used for the important things: "The courier brought something else. I was saving it."

"Kael."

"It's a letter. From the Inquisitor."

The word landed in the warm room like a stone in still water. Serane. Who had hunted her, who had knelt in the palace vault amid waking fire and learned she was only a harvest-tool of a dying crown, who had signed the truce with a hand that Paloma had never quite been able to read.

"She's resigned her post," Kael said. "The letter says she's—" he glanced at the page, and his eyebrows went up. "Huh. She's asking for a job."

Paloma set down her pen very slowly.

"She's *what?*"

"She says—" Kael read, with visible relish, "'I am told the Guild of Impossible Things keeps accurate records and does not burn its people. I have skills in the former and would like, very much, to learn the latter. I can start Monday.'"

Outside, the frost deepened, the lamplighters made their rounds, and somewhere east of everything, the dragons wheeled against the last light. Paloma looked at the letter, and at the ledger, and at the man who had been a prisoner and was now an associate, and at the empty chair by the window that would, come Monday, need filling.

"Well," she said, and picked up her pen. "She'd better be good with tea."

She opened a fresh page and wrote, in her own hand, the first line of whatever came next:

*Entry the second. On this day the guild took on its most impossible debt yet: an Inquisitor, seeking to be remembered as something other than what she was. Let it be recorded that we are, all of us, in the business of second entries now.*
