## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The merchant's name was Aldous Vey, and he smelled of tallow and fear, which was how most of them smelled by the time they reached the middle of Hollowmere.

Silas found him at the Drowned Lantern, three days into the valley, already halfway to forgetting why he'd come. The man sat hunched over a cup of small beer he kept lifting to lips that didn't remember wanting it, staring at the wall as if it might explain something to him. Around the tavern, other travelers did the same thing in their own ways—a carter counting coins over and over because the count never held; a pilgrim woman mouthing prayers whose endings slid away from her like fish. The valley ate memory the way sand drank rain. Everyone who crossed the border knew this, or had known it once, before they forgot knowing it.

Silas sat down across from Vey without asking.

"You're losing the coast," he said quietly.

Vey blinked. His eyes were the particular gray of a man whose thoughts had begun to pool rather than flow. "I'm sorry—do I—"

"Salt," Silas said. "You keep reaching for salt in your pocket that isn't there. Sailors do that. Or men who've lived close to the sea." He let the word sit between them. *Sea.* Vey's face cracked open like a door in wind.

"I can't—" The merchant's voice dropped to almost nothing. "There's water. There's a sound. I know there's a sound, and I can't find it anymore."

Silas knew exactly where it was. It was folded up neat as a letter in the warm dark behind Vey's eyes: gulls screaming over a fish market, spray off a harbor wall, his mother's kitchen smelling of brine and bread, the first time he'd seen open water at nineteen and felt the horizon pull at him like a hand on his collar. Twenty-two years of coastline, compressed and gleaming. Silas could feel it the way a blind man feels heat from a fire—not the shape of it, only that it burned.

He'd learned the trick of taking at fourteen, the way other boys learned snaring rabbits. You put your fingers to a man's temple, you thought of an empty room, and you *asked*. That was all anyone would ever tell him about it, because no one in Hollowmere knew anything else. Magic couldn't live here—the covenant saw to that—but memory wasn't quite magic. Memory was just the seam where magic leaked through, and Silas Yarrow had spent eleven years picking at that seam with his fingernails.

"Twenty marks," Silas said. "And you'll have your sea back before you cross the far border. Every wave of it."

"I don't have twenty marks."

"Ten, then, and the ring."

Vey looked down at his hand as though surprised to find it wearing one. This was the part Silas hated—or told himself he hated, in the flat way a man hates weather—and this was also the part he was best at: finding what people had left, when they had nothing else left to give. He took the ring. He pressed two fingers to the merchant's temple and opened the empty room in his mind.

The sea came out of Vey like water from a broken jug.

It poured through Silas—salt and shrieking birds, the groan of hulls, a woman singing off-key in a kitchen with blue shutters—and for one terrible, gorgeous moment Silas Yarrow stood ankle-deep in someone else's ocean, and it was so much *bigger* than anything he owned that it hurt. Then he closed the room. Folded it. Put it away in the vault behind his own eyes, next to a blacksmith's first love, a midwife's steady hands, a child's whole belief in dragons.

Aldous Vey blinked, and his face went smooth and mild, and he thanked Silas for the company of the conversation, having no idea any conversation had happened. He walked out into the gray morning lighter by ten marks' worth of past.

Silas ordered tea he didn't want and sat very still until his hands stopped shaking. They always shook after. Not guilt—he'd made his peace with guilt years ago, or buried it deep enough that its knocking sounded like weather. It was the *weight*. Other people's memories were heavy in a way his own life had never been. Eleven years in the valley, and what did Silas Yarrow remember of himself? A mother's silhouette against a doorway. The taste of snow. Learning the theft-trick from no one he could name. Everything else in his head belonged to strangers, and every year the strangers crowded closer, and every year there was less of him to stand among them.

*Rich as a king,* the widow Marta used to say of him, before she became his best customer instead. *Poorer than any beggar alive.* She'd said it laughing, once, before her husband's face faded from her. She didn't laugh about it now.

* * *

Her house stood at the crooked end of Lantern Row, the last one before the fields gave up and became marsh. Smoke rose from its chimney in the thin, defeated way smoke rises in a valley where even fire seems half-asleep. Silas knocked twice, the rhythm she knew.

She opened the door already holding coins. Fifty-three, counted that morning; she'd told him so last week, and last week she'd remembered telling him, and this week she wouldn't. That was how it went. Grief in Hollowmere was a race between mourning and forgetting, and the forgetting always won.

"He's going," she said. "Tomas. I can feel it going. Yesterday I lost his laugh. Today I woke up and I knew I'd loved someone but I couldn't—" Her jaw set. Marta was not a weeper. "You said you might find something."

"I found something." Silas stepped inside, out of habit more than manners. The house smelled of cold ash and dried lavender. On the mantel stood a carved boat, no bigger than his forearm, its paint flaked to ghosts of red and white. He looked at it too long.

"That's his," Marta said, watching him look. "From when we were young. He built it for our boy before—" She stopped, frowning, a woman arriving at the edge of a hole in the world. "Before something. Before."

"The sea," Silas said. "Would you like the sea?"

She stared at him. "I've never seen the sea. No one here has. That's the joke of this place, isn't it? We're the valley of the forgotten, and half of us were born forgetting." But her hands had gone still around the coins, and he watched her understand, slowly, what he was offering—not her memories, not Tomas's, but a stranger's. Borrowed years. A secondhand ocean.

"Is it wrong?" she asked. "To fill the hole with someone else's?"

Silas thought about lying. He was good at lying; it came with the trade. "It's wrong the way medicine is wrong," he said finally. "Bitter. And better than dying of it."

Marta laughed—a short, cracked sound, but real—and counted fifty-three coins onto the table, and then, after a pause, added the little brass key she wore at her throat, the one she'd stopped being able to explain years ago. Silas took the coins and left the key. Some things even he wouldn't lift.

"Close your eyes," he said, and pressed his fingers to her temple, and opened the room where the sea was kept.

He gave it to her gently, unspooled it slow so it wouldn't drown her: the harbor at dawn, the market noise, the blue-shuttered kitchen, the young man named Aldous Vey standing at a rail with the horizon pulling at him. He gave her all of it except the names. Those he kept back. The sea could be hers; the dead merchant's mother shouldn't be.

Marta's breath caught. Tears ran down her face without changing her expression at all, the way water runs off stone. "Oh," she said. "Oh, it's *loud*. I never knew it would be so loud." She opened her eyes, wet and bright and looking through him at something very far away. "Thank you. Whatever your name is."

That was the toll, then. She'd forget him by evening. They all did. In a week she'd remember loving the sea and not know where the loving came from, and maybe she'd come back to the crooked end of Lantern Row with fifty-three new coins, and maybe she wouldn't.

Silas walked out into the gray afternoon feeling like a man who'd eaten a feast and was starving anyway.

* * *

He counted the take that night in his rooms above the chandler's shop: fifty-three marks, plus what remained of the morning's other work—a farmer's memory of his wedding day, sold back to him at a markup, third time running. The farmer always bought it fresh, always paid full price, always wept the same tears. Silas had stopped feeling clever about that arrangement some time ago.

He should sleep. Instead he sat with his hoard.

That was what he called it, privately, though *hoard* was a dragon's word and he was no dragon—only a magpie, a picker-up of glittering things. Behind his eyes, in the vault, lay everything he'd ever taken, sorted into rooms he'd built for them in the dark: a corridor of childhoods, a gallery of griefs, a long hall of first times—first snow, first kiss, first kill (he avoided that hall). Hundreds of lives, filed and dusted and never once lived.

He walked the corridors now, as he did most nights, touching what wasn't his. The blacksmith's first love. The midwife's hands. Somewhere in the east wing, a noblewoman's memory of a mountain that breathed smoke and glowed at its crown like a banked coal—she'd called it a *forge* in her mind, over and over, *the forge remembers, the forge remembers*, a phrase she clearly hadn't understood herself, carried like a stone in her shoe—

Silas stopped walking.

*The forge remembers.*

He'd heard those words before. Recently. Today. A whisper, half-dissolved, at the valley's eastern edge where the mist thickened and the road went strange. A man's voice, cracking, urgent—pressing something paper-colored and scorched into another traveler's hands, or trying to. Silas had been checking his snares along the borderline, as he did each dusk, and he'd seen the shapes through the fog without understanding them: two figures, one falling. He'd told himself it was nothing. Border business. Not mine.

But the words were the same words. Exactly the same. And words didn't repeat themselves across strangers by accident, not in a valley where nothing was supposed to hold its shape overnight.

Silas stood in the dark of his rented room with fifty-three marks in his pocket and a hoard of stolen lives behind his eyes, and for the first time in eleven years, he was afraid of something he'd taken.

At dawn, he told himself, he'd go back to the borderline and see what the fog had left behind.

He didn't know yet that the fog had left a man dying, and a map, and the end of every quiet year he had left.
