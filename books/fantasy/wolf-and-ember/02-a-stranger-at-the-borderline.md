## 2. A Stranger at the Borderline

The border of Hollowmere was not a wall or a river or even a line anyone had drawn. It was a feeling. You walked the sheep track up through the gorse, and somewhere between one step and the next, the world went quiet—not silent, but quiet in the bones, like a house after everyone you loved had left it. Silas Yarrow knew that feeling better than he knew his own face, which, come to think of it, was saying something.

He was checking his snares when the man came through the wrong way.

Travelers crossed into Hollowmere from time to time—merchants, pilgrims, fools with more courage than sense—and Silas had learned to read them at a distance, the way a fisherman reads water. But this one came stumbling down from the northern ridge at a dead run, which meant he wasn't entering the valley so much as fleeing into it. He made it perhaps forty paces past the border before his legs decided they were done with him.

He went down hard among the gorse, and Silas heard the wet sound of it from twenty yards off.

For a long moment Silas did nothing. This was habit, not cowardice. The first rule of Hollowmere was that you waited. You let the valley do its work. Anything that happened near the borderline happened to memory first—a man might forget why he'd come, forget who he was running from, forget he could walk—and whatever fell out of him in those first confused minutes belonged, by the valley's own crooked law, to whoever picked it up.

Silas had picked up a great many things over the years.

But the man didn't fall out of himself the way travelers did. No slackening of the face, no soft confusion settling over his eyes like dust. He lay on his back in the gorse, breathing in short tearing pulls, and his eyes stayed fixed and furious and *present*, and that was wrong. Everything about that was wrong.

Silas approached the way he approached everything worth taking: slowly, hands visible, face arranged into something bland and harmless. "You're past the border," he said. "Whatever's chasing you can't follow."

The man laughed. It cost him; blood came up with it, bright against his teeth. "Can't it," he rasped. "That's what we thought too. Once."

He was young—thirty, maybe, though the gray at his temples argued otherwise—and dressed like no merchant Silas had ever robbed. Close-fitted traveling leathers, dark as old ink, cut for speed rather than show. A courier's kit, but no courier colors. The kind of clothes a man wore when he wanted to be mistaken for nobody.

And he was bleeding from everywhere. Not wounds, exactly—or not wounds Silas could find. Blood welled from under his fingernails. From the seams of his sleeves. As if the man were coming apart at every stitch.

"What did that to you?" Silas asked.

"The Court." The word came out with such loathing that Silas took an involuntary step back. "Ashen Court. They don't chase anymore, thief. They unmake." He coughed again, wetter. "I ran for the valley because it's the one place they can't reach me clean. Memoryless ground. They need my memories to kill me properly, and your valley eats memories." His mouth bent into something that had once been a smile. "Clever place. Ugly clever."

"I don't know anything about any Court."

"No," the man agreed. "Nobody in here knows anything about anything. That's the whole design, isn't it? A valley with no past. A people with no history." His hand moved—slowly, tremblingly—toward the satchel strapped across his chest. "They built your home as a vault, thief. Did you know that? Hollowmere isn't a refuge. It's a lockbox."

Silas said nothing, but something cold walked down his spine and stood there.

"Take it." The courier fumbled the satchel open one-handed. Inside: oilcloth, wrapped around something flat and stiff. "It won't stop them. Nothing stops them. But it buys time, if someone's stubborn enough to use it."

"I'm not stubborn," Silas said. "I'm practical. There's a difference, and it keeps me alive."

"Then be practical." The courier pressed the bundle toward him, and his fingers left red prints on the oilcloth. "Sell it. That's what you do, isn't it? Sell what doesn't belong to you." Another of those ruined smiles. "Wren told me about you."

Every hair on Silas's arms lifted. "Who's Wren?"

But the man's eyes had gone distant, tracking something Silas couldn't see, and Silas understood with a lurch that the conversation was already over—the man was spending his last coins elsewhere. "The Forge remembers," the courier whispered. "Tell it— tell it I paid. Tell it Yarrow's son paid—"

*Yarrow's son.* The words landed like a hook behind Silas's breastbone, and he would have demanded more, would have shaken the answers loose, but the light simply went out of the man's face. Not gradually. All at once, like a snuffed candle, like a stolen memory leaving its owner. One breath in. None out.

Silas crouched there a long while, listening to the gorse tick in the wind and his own heart bang against his ribs.

*Yarrow's son.* His father had died before Silas could hold onto him—that was the cruelty of Hollowmere; children forgot their parents the way other places' children lost teeth, naturally, painlessly, and grew replacements. But the name Yarrow had stayed, the way names sometimes did, worn smooth of everything around them. Nobody in Hollowmere had spoken it to him in years. And a dying stranger from beyond the world had known it.

Which meant somebody out there remembered Silas Yarrow. Remembered him specifically. Remembered enough to send a man to die on the border with his name in his mouth.

Silas sat down heavily in the gorse and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that weren't quite steady.

Inside was a map.

Not a map of Hollowmere—Hollowmere had no maps, being a place without a past to map. It was a map of everything else. Inked on hide, dense and fine as embroidery: roads braiding across mountains, rivers written in silver thread, cities named in a cramped, beautiful hand. And stitched across it all, seared into the skin itself rather than drawn, a single road that burned brighter than the rest, glowing faintly orange as if lit from beneath—

The Ember Road.

Silas had never seen it. Had never seen anything past the ridgeline, because seeing past the ridgeline required going past the ridgeline, and Silas Yarrow had sworn an oath about that at fifteen years old, alone, at night, with his father's name still fresh in the hole where his father should have been. Beyond the border was the world that had eaten his family. Beyond the border was magic, and grief, and everything too large to steal from. In Hollowmere he was a king of small things. He had told himself for twenty years that this was wisdom.

The map showed roads he had sworn never to take, laid out in loving detail, and along the Ember Road, in that cramped beautiful hand, small annotations:

*Waystation at the split birches — ask for the deaf ferryman.*
*Do not drink at Marrowford.*
*THE FORGE REMEMBERS.*

And beneath that final line, pressed deep enough to scar the hide, three words in different ink, newer, hurried:

*Find Wren.*

The wind moved through the gorse. Somewhere downslope, a snare bell rang—some rabbit, some ordinary death, the small honest economy of Silas's life going on without him.

He looked at the dead courier and felt fear and curiosity fighting for his throat, and hated them both, because both of them wanted him to move, and moving was how men like him died.

Fear said: burn it. Fear said: the Ashen Court unmakes people at their seams, and now you're holding the thing they killed for, and you are a thief in a valley of amnesiacs, alone, easy, invisible. Burn the map, bury the man, go sell the widow her sea tomorrow, live small and live long.

Curiosity said: *Yarrow's son.* Curiosity said: somebody out there knows what you've forgotten, and knowing is the only thing you have ever wanted that you couldn't steal.

And underneath both voices, quieter than either, something else—the same cold thing that had walked down his spine and never left. The courier had called Hollowmere a lockbox. A vault. Built, designed, *made*. Which meant someone had built it, and someone had locked it, and everything Silas had ever been—every hollow year, every borrowed memory, every morning waking into a life with no floor under it—wasn't misfortune.

It was architecture.

Silas Yarrow had spent his whole career learning what people would pay to get back. He knew, better than any living soul, the exact weight of a missing memory. He had just never once considered that the weight pressing on *him* might have been placed there deliberately. That he wasn't poor.

He was robbed.

He wrapped the map with care, took the courier's boots—he was practical, after all, and dead men's boots fit no one better than thieves—and dragged the body to the shallow sinkhole past the gorse where Hollowmere's soil asked no questions and kept no answers. Then he climbed the ridge to the borderline itself and stood there, one boot on valley earth, one boot on the world, looking north along a road he couldn't see but could suddenly, terribly, imagine.

*Find Wren.*

The wind came down off the mountains carrying a smell he had no name for—hot iron, maybe, or rain on stone, something older than either. For half a heartbeat the air above the ridgeline shimmered, faint and orange, like heat off a forge, and then it was gone, and Silas told himself he'd imagined it.

He turned back toward the valley, toward the widow waiting for her sea, toward the small safe kingdom of forgetting.

Behind him, on the map in his satchel, in the dark of the oilcloth, a fourth line of ink was slowly surfacing—as if the hide itself were remembering—writing itself in that cramped beautiful hand:

*SHE IS ALREADY COMING FOR YOU.*

* * *

Three days later, at dusk, a woman walked into Hollowmere from the north, and every snare bell on the ridge rang at once, though no wind blew.
