## 3. What the Stolen Memories Whisper

The hoard lived in the cellar, and the cellar kept its own kind of silence.

Silas went down the ladder with a lamp in one hand and the courier's map folded against his chest, and he told himself—again, for the third night running—that this was inventory. Stocktaking. A merchant checking his ledger after an unusual customer. That was all.

The shelves ran the length of the room, forty feet of them, carved into the valley stone by some hand older than Hollowmere itself. On each shelf sat jars of smoked glass, and in each jar curled something that had no business existing: memory, pressed and preserved like flowers between the pages of a book. He'd learned the trick of it from no one. It was simply there, the way breathing was there—a knack his body understood before his mind caught up. Touch a stranger's temple, whisper the little wordless asking, and the past came loose like fruit from a stem.

He'd stolen two hundred and eleven memories in nine years. He knew the number because numbers were the only thing about them that belonged to him.

He set the lamp on the workbench and unfolded the map beside it. The Ember Road crawled off the parchment's edge, out past the border, out past everything. *The Forge remembers.* The courier's blood had been warm on Silas's hands when he'd said it. Warm words from a cooling man.

"Inventory," Silas said aloud, because the cellar was the one place he could hear his own voice and not hate it.

He started at shelf one, as he always did when the nights got bad. Jar after jar, he lifted each to the lamplight—not to open them, never to open them, because opening meant *entering*, and entering other people's pasts too often was how you stopped having your own—but to check the seals. Amber wax, unbroken, unbroken, unbroken.

Jar sixty-three's seal was broken.

He stood very still. Then he remembered: three days ago, the widow Marta, before her grief had driven her back across the valley to buy her husband's last morning again. She'd held the jar while she wept. She hadn't resealed it properly. Her fingers had been shaking. Fine. Everything fine.

Except.

Except that when he picked it up—the memory of the sea, the one he'd sold her, the one he'd handled a hundred times without ever once looking inside—he felt it *leak*. Not spill. Leak. Like heat through a cracked window. And what leaked out wasn't the widow's husband or the grey water or the gulls. What leaked out was something else underneath, something that had been riding in the memory like a stowaway since the day he stole it, invisible until now, until the broken seal, until tonight.

A voice. A woman's voice. Reciting something.

Silas did not decide to open the jar. His hands decided for him.

* * *

Entering a memory felt like slipping beneath the surface of dark water. No splash, no cold—just pressure, then light, then *there*.

The sea came first, because it was the widow's sea: green-grey, enormous, smelling of salt and rot and distance. He stood on a dock he didn't recognize in a port town whose name he didn't know, and somewhere behind him a woman laughed—the widow's husband laughing, young, alive—and none of it was his, none of it would ever be his, and that ache was so familiar by now it barely registered.

What registered was the second layer. The one under the waterline of the memory, the way you can stand in a shallow stream and feel the current pulling at your ankles from somewhere deeper.

A forge. Not a picture of a forge—the *idea* of one, burning through the memory like a coal through paper. Heat that wasn't the sun's heat. Iron-song. And over it, that voice, a woman's voice, level as a plumb line:

*"By ember given, by ember bound. What the Forge holds, the world may not hold. What the world forgets, the Forge keeps."*

Silas surfaced, gasping, lamp guttering, heart slamming.

The jar sat innocently on the bench. Inside, the sea-memory rolled gently, undisturbed, as if nothing had happened. As if it hadn't just spoken to him in a dead woman's cadence—or a living one's; he couldn't tell if the voice belonged to someone who had once existed or someone who still did.

He should stop. He knew he should stop. Every instinct honed by nine years of survival in a valley where forgetting was mercy said: put down the jar, seal the wax, go up the ladder, sleep, forget by morning like everyone else in Hollowmere forgot.

But Silas Yarrow had never forgotten anything. That was his curse, the flip-side of his gift, the joke the valley played on its one thief: everyone around him leaked memories like sieves, and he kept every drop.

He reached for jar nineteen.

* * *

By the fourth hour, the cellar looked like a robbery had happened in reverse—jars everywhere, seals cracked, the lamp burning low, and Silas sitting cross-legged on the cold floor among them like a child amid broken toys, shaking.

It was in all of them.

Not whole. Never whole. Fragments, shards, splinters—buried in a shepherd's memory of his father's funeral, in a peddler's memory of a festival in some city called Vael Corren, in a soldier's memory of a battle he'd deserted, in a bride's memory of her wedding morning. Underneath every stolen life, woven through the fabric of the pasts themselves like thread through cloth, ran the same recurring images:

*A forge, always the same forge—vast, built into the throat of a mountain, its fire the color of dawn.*

*An oath, spoken in a circle of hooded figures, their faces deliberately blurred even inside the memories, as though remembering their faces was forbidden.*

*And the voice. Always the voice.*

*"By ember given, by ember bound,"* she said in the soldier's memory, and in the bride's: *"What the Forge holds, the world may not hold."* And in the peddler's: *"Let the covenant stand between realms, and let neither cross unbargained."*

Different travelers. Different years. Different roads, different lives, different reasons for crossing into Hollowmere—and yet every single one of them had brushed against this same thing. This same *ceremony*. As if they'd all walked past the same fire and carried away sparks without knowing.

Silas sat in the wreckage of his hoard and felt the vertigo open beneath him like a trapdoor.

He had thought himself a petty criminal. A scavenger. The lowest form of life in a valley already too low for gods to bother watching—stealing scraps of other people's joy and selling them back at a markup. He had built his whole self-image on smallness. Small thefts. Small sins. A small man in a forgotten place.

And all along, every jar on those shelves had been a page. Every victim a scribe who didn't know they were writing. He hadn't been building a business.

He'd been building an archive.

"No," he said to the empty cellar. "No, that's not—" But his voice died, because the evidence lay all around him, glinting in the lamplight, and evidence doesn't argue.

Two hundred and eleven fragments. If he laid them end to end—if he entered each memory in sequence and stitched the pieces together the way the courier's map stitched roads together—he might have something whole. An oath entire. A ritual entire. Whatever it was the dying man had spent his blood to deliver, Silas might have been carrying it home in pieces for nine years without knowing.

*The Forge remembers,* the courier had said.

Maybe the Forge didn't need to remember. Maybe Silas had remembered *for* it.

He got up on unsteady legs and crossed to the far shelf, the one he never touched—jar two hundred and eleven, the last theft, the courier's own memory. He hadn't dared enter it. A dying man's final moments were a country no one came back from unchanged. But his hand hovered over the wax seal now, and the seal seemed to hover back, patient as a door.

Behind him, at the top of the cellar stairs, the door to the shop creaked.

Silas went rigid. Nobody came at this hour. Nobody came to Hollowmere's memory-broker at midnight unless they were desperate, and desperate people came at noon like everyone else, hating themselves in daylight.

The creak came again. Footsteps on the floorboards above—light, deliberate, wrong. Not the shuffle of a grieving buyer. Someone who knew exactly where they were walking.

Then a voice drifted down through the floorboards, and it made the hair on his arms rise, because it was *her*. The woman from the memories. The voice that had recited the covenant-words in two hundred and eleven strangers' heads was speaking through his own ceiling, clear and calm as a plumb line:

"You can come up, thief. Or I can come down. I've read the Court's files on you—they're shorter than you'd hope."

Silas's hand closed around the nearest object—jar ninety, a farmer's memory of his daughter's birth—and he realized, distantly, absurdly, that he was holding it like a weapon.

"I know what you have," the voice said. "All of it. Every piece." A pause, and then, almost gently: "I'm the one they wrote it down for."
