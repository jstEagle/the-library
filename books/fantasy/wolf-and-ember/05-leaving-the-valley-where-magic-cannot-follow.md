## 5. Leaving the Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The map was warm in his coat pocket, the way a stolen memory was warm—alive with someone else's intention. Silas had spent two days telling himself he wouldn't go. Two nights lying awake in the loft above his shop, listening to the valley breathe its slow, forgetting breath, and every morning waking up certain he'd stay.

On the third morning he packed a bag instead.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. A man should know what waited at the edge of the world before the world came to him. The courier had died with blood on his lips and a name on them—the Forge remembers—and Silas did not intend to be found standing still when whatever had killed the courier came looking. That was all this was. Walking to the border. Looking over the edge. Coming home.

He left before dawn, when Hollowmere's streets were empty of everyone but the baker and the lamplighter, both of whom would forget him by noon anyway. He paused at the door of his shop and looked back at it once: the shelves of little glass vials, each one holding a sliver of somebody's life. Sea-light on water. A mother's voice reading aloud. The smell of bread in a city he'd never seen.

*Rich as a king,* the widow Maren always said. *And poor as a stone.*

He locked the door and didn't look back again.

* * *

The valley ended the way a sentence ends—not at any particular spot, but where meaning simply stops carrying. One moment Silas walked through grass he knew, gray-green and quiet, the air tasting of nothing much at all. Then his boot came down on a stone that was the same stone, and everything changed.

It began with color.

The grass went green so violently it hurt—a wet, shouting green, the green of something alive and unashamed about it. He staggered. His own hands were suddenly *hands*, pink and flushed and mapped with veins he'd never noticed, and above him the sky tore open into a blue so deep he made a sound like a wounded animal and sat down hard in the grass.

"Ah," he said. "Oh. *Ah.*"

Because it wasn't just the sky. It was everything. The wind had texture—he could feel it moving across his face in threads, each one distinct, cool and warm braided together. Somewhere downslope a bird sang, and the song wasn't a sound, it was a *shape*, spiraling and bright, and beneath it he could hear the bird itself, small and fierce-hearted, singing because the morning existed and it existed to hear it.

Silas Yarrow, who had spent thirty years trading in other people's memories, discovered in the space of ten heartbeats that he had been living his whole life inside a jar.

And then the grief arrived.

That was the part no one could have warned him about. The magic in the air wasn't gentle—it was *honest*. It pressed against his mind the way water presses against a diver, and everything he had ever buried floated up at once, vivid and unbearable. He saw his mother's face—and realized with a lurch that he had not thought of her face in years, that the valley had sanded it smooth without asking permission, and here it was again, sudden and complete: the crooked tooth, the flour dust on her sleeve, the way she hummed when she thought no one listened.

She was dead. Had been dead for twenty years. And he had forgotten her twice—once when she died, and once when the valley took what remained—and the second forgetting was the one that broke him open now, kneeling in impossible grass, weeping like a child while a bird sang shapes above him.

"That's what they feel," he said aloud, wiping his face with a shaking hand. "That's exactly what they feel."

Every traveler whose memory he'd lifted. Every widow and debtor and runaway who'd paid him to give pieces back. They hadn't lost a trinket. They'd lost *this*—the raw, screaming weight of a life—and he had wrapped it in glass and haggled over the price.

He laughed, wet and ugly. The magic soaked it up and gave it back to him brighter than he'd meant it, and for a moment he sat in the green loudness of the world feeling like the richest thief alive and the poorest man who'd ever lived, both at once.

* * *

He walked until noon, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant more of everything. The road beyond the border was real dirt, rutted and honest, and it climbed through hills where the magic lay thick enough to see if he let his eyes unfocus—threads of pale gold drifting like pollen, gathering around old stones and standing trees. Once, passing a shrine no bigger than a well-cover, he felt something brush his thoughts, curious and light as a moth, and he flinched away from it with his heart hammering.

In Hollowmere, magic couldn't follow you. That had always been the bargain, though nobody in the valley remembered making it. Out here, magic was in the ground and the rain and the minds of everyone he might meet, and it could *see* him.

By late afternoon he'd put enough distance between himself and the border that going home felt harder than going on. He stopped in the lee of a granite outcrop, built a small fire out of habit, and pulled the courier's map from his coat.

Unrolled across his knees, it looked the same as it had in the valley: the Ember Road running west like a vein of charcoal, marked with way-stations, a river, and at its far end a symbol like a flame closed inside a circle. But here, outside, the map seemed to *breathe*. The ink shifted when he wasn't looking directly at it. The mountains drew themselves in finer detail the longer he studied them, as though the paper were remembering terrain it had once known.

"The Forge remembers," he murmured. "What does that mean? Remember *what?*"

The fire popped. Somewhere behind him, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Then the map burned him.

He hissed and nearly dropped it—but he didn't drop it, some instinct deeper than sense held his grip, and he watched with his pulse roaring in his ears as a mark seared itself onto the parchment near the eastern edge, right where the border of Hollowmere ran. It bloomed like a brand: three lines crossing in a shape that hurt to look at, edges smoking faintly, the smell of scorched vellum rising off it sharp and chemical.

An eye. It took him a moment to understand it as an eye—an eye drawn by someone who hated eyes, all angles and accusation.

Silas knew that symbol. Everyone past the border knew it. He'd seen it branded into a smuggler's shoulder once, years ago, when the man passed through the valley fleeing something and forgot why he was running. The Ashen Court. The mark of their hunters—the ones they sent after covenant-breakers, after thieves of power, after people like—

Like whoever the courier had been. Like, apparently, him.

"No," he said quietly. "No, no—I haven't *done* anything."

But the mark pulsed gently, patiently, like a heartbeat, and Silas understood with cold clarity that it didn't care what he'd done. It cared where he was. He had crossed the line. He had stepped out of the place where magic could not follow, and the Court's long arm had reached across miles of hills to touch the thing he carried—or perhaps the thing he *was*, because the fragments in his head, the forge and the oath and the woman's reciting voice, were stirring now too, restless, as if they recognized the mark and feared it.

He looked at the burning sigil, and then west along the darkening road, toward the flame-in-circle at the map's far end.

Going home wouldn't save him. The mark knew where the border was; it would wait there, patient as winter, for the rest of his life. Staying put would only make him easier to find. Which left the road, and the Forge, and the ritual he carried in pieces inside his own skull—a hoard of stolen moments he'd never asked to mean anything.

Somewhere out in the dark, a horse snorted. Hooves on the road, unhurried, coming from the east.

From the direction of the mark.

Silas pinched out the fire with his bare fingers—it stung, everything stung out here, everything was *so much*—and pressed himself into the shadow of the rock, the map crushed against his chest, listening to the hoofbeats grow louder.

They slowed as they neared his dead campfire. Stopped.

A woman's voice, low and dry as old paper, said to the night:

"You can come out, thief. I know exactly what you're carrying."
