## 6. The Road of Small Betrayals

The Ember Road earned its name at dusk.

Silas watched it happen from the crest of the first hill beyond the river shrine: as the sun dropped, the packed clay of the highway caught fire with reflected light, every rut and hoofprint glowing like cooling iron, until the road itself seemed to run molten toward a horizon he still hadn't learned to look at without flinching. Three days out of Hollowmere and the sky was still too much sky. It went on forever in every direction, unbroken by valley walls, and some animal part of him kept waiting for it to close overhead like a hand.

"You're staring again," Wren said.

"I'm navigating."

"You're staring. Navigating is what I'm doing." She tapped the scorched map against her knee as she walked. "We make the toll-bridge at Gilders' Ford by tomorrow noon or we camp in the open, and I don't camp in the open on this stretch. Not since the Court posted hunters along the river."

Silas dragged his eyes from the burning road. "You said the mark on the map was weeks old."

"The mark was weeks old. Courts don't stop hunting because ink dries." Wren tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear—hair cropped short and uneven, scribe's discipline abandoned to travel—and lengthened her stride. "Keep up, memory-thief. The light won't wait for us and neither will the ford."

He kept up. He'd been keeping up for three days, and in those three days he had catalogued her the way he catalogued everything now: the way she counted steps under her breath when she was nervous (a scribe's habit, numbering things to keep them orderly); the way she never let the map out of arm's reach; the way she said *the Court* flatly, without heat, which took more discipline than shouting ever would. She had told him about her mentor's execution on the second night, in the dark, in a voice like someone reading a ledger. Silas had listened and offered nothing back, because there was nothing to offer. His own history fit in a thimble. That was the whole disease of him.

But she was good company, in the way a whetstone is good company—hard, useful, occasionally drawing blood.

They made the ridge above Gilders' Ford as the last ember-light drained out of the road, and Silas saw the problem before Wren said it aloud.

The bridge was guarded.

* * *

Wren saw the warden and felt the day tilt.

One man, alone, standing mid-span with a lantern and a long pole tipped in silver—a toll-warden, but not a hired one. She knew the cut of the coat. Ashen grey, court-issue, the collar stitched with a small flame sigil that only wardens sworn directly to the Nine wore. Not a toll collector. A checkpoint.

"They're screening travelers," she breathed. "That far out?"

"Which means they're looking for someone specific." Silas crouched beside her in the gorse, his voice carefully level. "Someone with a brand-new hunter's mark burned into their luggage."

"Don't be dramatic."

"I'm never dramatic. Drama requires an audience and feelings. I have neither."

Despite everything—the fear sitting in her chest like swallowed coal—Wren almost smiled. Three days of his dry, hollow voice had done something she hadn't authorized: it had made the road feel less like a corridor into a trap. She hated that. Trust was a debt, and she had seen what debts did to people at Court. Debts got people burned.

"We could go around," Silas said. "Upstream."

"And lose a day. And the ford is the only crossing shallow enough this season. And if they're watching the bridge, they're watching the banks." She chewed her lip. "No. We walk across. I can talk my way past a warden—I still know the countersigns, the old ones, the ones they haven't rotated."

"And if they've rotated them?"

"Then we improvise."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. In the dying light his face had the quality she'd noticed the first night: a face built for being forgotten. Ordinary features, arranged in no particular order, eyes the color of ditchwater. And yet when he looked at you—really looked—you had the unsettling sense of being *read*. Of pages turning somewhere behind that grey stare.

"Fine," he said. "But I go first. Wardens ignore porters and pilgrims. They remember well-dressed clerks with Court posture."

"I do not have Court posture."

"You're standing like a filing cabinet right now."

She glanced down. She was standing like a filing cabinet. She straightened into something looser and followed him down through the gorse, telling herself the tightness in her throat was just the cold.

* * *

The warden was younger than Silas expected, and more afraid.

That was the thing about men holding power they didn't trust: they gripped the lantern too hard, planted their feet like the bridge might buck them, scanned faces with a hunger that wasn't vigilance but terror of missing something. Silas had spent his whole life reading fear off strangers at the border of Hollowmere. It was the one fluency he owned.

"Halt." The warden raised his silver-tipped pole. "State your business on the Ember Road."

"Pilgrimage," Silas said mildly. "To the shrines east of Vail's Crossing. My sister accompanies me."

Wren, behind him, performed a passable imitation of a woman who wanted desperately to be anywhere else. Good instincts. He filed that away too.

The warden's lantern came up, its light crawling across Silas's face, then Wren's—and stopped there. Stuck. Something flickered behind the young man's eyes. Recognition, or the edge of it.

"Wren—" the warden started, and Silas heard the surname begin to form, half-swallowed, and understood in a single cold instant that this boy had sat in a lecture hall somewhere and memorized her name off a traitor's warrant.

*Not necessity,* whispered the old rule, the one he'd carried out of Hollowmere like a stone in his shoe. *Never outside necessity.*

This was necessity. Wasn't it? The word had edges. On the road, with no valley to fence his thefts in, *necessity* could stretch like taffy. He thought of the merchant whose sea-memory he'd sold, the widow who'd wept over borrowed water. Every one of them had been necessary too, once he'd decided it was.

His hand closed around the warden's wrist, two fingers finding the pulse point, and he *drew*.

It was different out here. In Hollowmere, memories came away dull, wrapped in fog, half-formed things. Beyond the border, the mind was loud. The warden's recollection tore free in full color and sound, and Silas staggered under the weight of it—

—a lecture hall, benches of black ashwood, a woman at the podium with cropped hair and a voice like a struck bell, teaching first-year scribes the grammar of binding-covenants. Younger Wren. Sharper. Her chalk striking the slate in strokes so precise they looked printed. *A covenant is a promise given form,* she was saying. *And form can be broken by anyone who learns where the seams are.* The students laughing. The warden-as-a-boy laughing, fifteen years old, certain he'd found the one teacher worth having—

—and then another memory, sliding beneath the first like a stone under water. The Forge. White heat. Wren standing at a doorway with a sealed writ in her hands, handing it to a man in Ashen grey, turning away, walking out while behind her someone screamed a name that was hers—

Silas released the warden's wrist so fast he nearly fell.

The young man blinked, swaying, the last three minutes gone soft and fogged. Silas stepped past him, pulling Wren by the sleeve, murmuring the counter-sign he'd just lifted from the stolen memory—the warden's own morning report phrase, *the ford holds, nothing moves*—and the boy nodded vaguely, already forgetting why his arm ached, why his lantern hand trembled.

They crossed the bridge. The river muttered below, black and quick. On the far bank the gorse swallowed them, and Silas walked until the bridge lights were gone and his lungs stopped trying to climb out of his chest.

Then he stopped, bent over, hands on knees.

"Silas?" Wren's voice, careful. "What did you take?"

"Nothing important. Signs and countersigns. Patrol rotations." Straightening. Lying. He was better at lying than he'd realized; it turned out all those years of selling other people's pasts had taught him exactly how a truth was built, and therefore how to fake one. "He recognized you. We're clear until the next checkpoint, but we should move at first light."

Wren studied him in the dark. He felt her study him, felt the questions lining up behind her eyes like type in a case, waiting to be set.

"You broke your rule," she said quietly. "You told me once you never steal unless you must."

"I must often, lately."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You steal when you *decide* you must. There's a difference, and I think you know it." But she didn't push. She unrolled her bedroll instead, and after a moment she said, almost too low to hear: "Thank you. Whatever it cost."

"It didn't cost anything."

"Stealing always costs. I was a scribe. I know the exchange rates of the soul better than anyone alive." She lay back and closed her eyes. "Sleep, thief. Dawn comes whether we deserve it or not."

* * *

Silas didn't sleep.

He sat with his back against a lightning-split oak and turned the stolen memory over in his mind like a coin he suspected was clipped.

Two memories. He'd only meant to take one—he'd have sworn it—but the warden's mind had been layered, and the second had come away stuck to the first like wet paper. The lecture hall was harmless. Charming, even. It showed him a Wren he could believe in: brilliant, principled, beloved by her students, teaching children that covenants were promises and promises had seams.

The Forge was the problem.

White heat. A sealed writ. Her hands passing it to a man in Ashen grey—the same grey the hunters wore, the same sigil—and behind her, through the doorway of the Forge itself, someone screaming her name while she walked away without turning around.

Betrayal. At the Forge, of all places—the place they were walking toward, day by day, trusting each other a little more with every mile. She had handed the Court the key to the one sanctuary in the world, and whatever had screamed behind that door had known her name.

Unless. Unless there was context he didn't have. Unless the screaming thing deserved it. Unless she'd been coerced, deceived, forced—unless the writ was blank, unless the man in grey was someone else, unless, unless. He knew better than most how thin a single memory was. He'd sold thousands of them. He knew a memory was a window, not a wall—one angle, one moment, one liar's worth of truth.

But he also knew what he'd felt coming off that memory, the way you feel heat off a stove: certainty. Hers. In the memory, in the white glare of the Forge, Wren had not hesitated. Her hands had been steady.

Across the dead fire, she slept with the map curled against her chest like a child with a doll, and her face in sleep was younger and less defended than he'd yet seen it, and Silas Yarrow—who had built a life on taking things from strangers and calling it commerce—discovered that keeping a secret was heavier than any theft he'd ever committed.

Tomorrow she would ask him something. Tomorrow the road would press them closer, because roads did that; they narrowed and funneled and forced two people into step. And every mile toward the Forge, the question would grow louder in him: *what did you do there, Wren? Who screamed?*

He could ask. He could watch her face when she answered, read her the way he'd read ten thousand travelers, and probably know.

Or he could carry it. Keep it folded in the dark with everything else he'd taken, and let it sit between them like a blade under a blanket—unseen, but sharp on both sides.

The stars wheeled, indifferent, too many of them, the sky still refusing to close. Somewhere behind them the warden would be waking with a hole in his evening and a story he couldn't quite reconstruct. Somewhere ahead, the Forge waited, remembering.

Silas pulled his coat tighter and began, silently, to rehearse the lie he'd tell if she asked what else he'd seen.

It was a good lie. He was almost proud of it.

That was how he knew he was in trouble.

In the grey hour before dawn, Wren opened her eyes without moving, and watched the thief watch the road, and wondered what he was carrying that made him sit so straight, like a man guarding a wound.

She'd been a scribe for nineteen years. She knew what secrets looked like from the outside.

She'd show him hers soon enough—when they were close enough to the Forge that the truth couldn't be used to turn him back. Until then, she let him keep his silence, and kept her own, and the road ran on ahead of them, glowing faintly even in the dark, like a seam of fire waiting to be torn open.
