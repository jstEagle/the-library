## 10. The Man Who Gave His Thefts Away

The valley took him back the way the sea takes a drowned man—gently, without judgment, closing over his head.

Silas knew the border when he crossed it because the silence came. After weeks on the Ember Road with magic humming through everything—the way fire spoke to Wren, the way rivers remembered their sources—Hollowmere's quiet felt like a hand laid flat against his chest. *Be still. Be empty. Be home.*

He was almost grateful.

Then he tried to remember why he'd left, and found the shelves bare.

Not all of them. He knew his name. He knew Wren's face, though her name arrived a heartbeat late, like a guest who'd forgotten which house. He knew that his hands were clever and that they had done something terrible for a very long time, the way you know a room has been painted even after the color's been stripped back to wood. But the memories themselves—the merchant's sea, the widow's grief, the courier's blood-warm map, the woman's voice reciting covenant-words in a language like cooling iron—all of it had poured out of him at the Forge and scattered into the world like sparks from an anvil, and what remained was the shape of a man where a man used to be.

"Say it again," he said.

They were walking the last mile into town, dusk coming down purple over the memoryless hills. Wren glanced at him. She had ash in her hair still, from a forge that no longer existed as it had existed, and she walked with the careful stride of someone whose body had learned every road might hide hunters and hadn't yet unlearned it.

"Your name is Silas Yarrow," she said. "You were born in Hollowmere, which almost nobody can claim. You have a scar on your left thumb from a lockpick, when you were fourteen. You hate the taste of goat cheese but eat it anyway because it keeps well on the road."

"And?"

"And you stole from everyone you ever met." She said it evenly, without cruelty. That was the arrangement they'd made at the Forge, the one she'd prepared in secret while he stood ready to be erased: *I will remember for you. All of it. Even the parts that shame you. Especially those.*

"Good people?" he asked.

"Some of them. Desperate ones, mostly. Travelers who crossed the border and lost things they couldn't afford to lose, and you sold the losses back to them at a markup." She watched his face as she said it, watching to see if the knowledge would break him fresh each time. It did. It broke him a little each time, and each time he stayed broken-open rather than shattered, which she seemed to consider progress.

"I don't feel like that man," Silas said.

"You aren't," Wren said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Hollowmere had not changed. Hollowmere could not change—that had always been its curse and its comfort—but the people inside it had begun, in small ways, to change themselves.

The covenant was gone. Whatever Silas had fed into the Ember Forge that last day—not surrender, never surrender, but release, the whole hoard of stolen years given freely back to the world—had torn the old law out of the valley like a rotten fencepost pulled from wet ground. Magic seeped in now the way water finds a cellar: slowly, everywhere, indifferent to borders. The Ashen Court's authority had been written in the covenant's ink, and when the ink dissolved, so did the warrants, the brands, the long arm that had chased them across two realms. Wren said the Court still existed, still had halls and robes and opinions, but it ruled nothing now except its own dwindling ceremonies.

Silas remembered none of this firsthand. He had Wren's account, delivered each morning like bread.

The widow with the sea-memory came to his door in the second week.

He didn't know her. That was the worst of it—he opened his door to a gray-haired woman holding a jar of honey, and she looked at him with an expression he had no context for, some old arithmetic of grief and gratitude and grievance—and she said, "You gave it back to me. I felt it go. All of it. The whole sea."

"I'm sorry," Silas said, because it seemed like the correct response to whatever crime sat behind her eyes.

She laughed, short and surprised. "Don't apologize. Do you know what the Court paid for memories? What *you* charged, before?" She set the honey on his table like a toll or a gift, he couldn't tell which. "It's strange. I have my sea again, but I also have the memory of losing it. Both now. They sit side by side." She looked at him then, really looked, and said, "You look hollowed out, thief. Like a house after the family's moved on."

"Yes," he said.

"Well." She patted the table once, decisively. "Houses fill again. Mine did." And she left him the honey and her footsteps fading down the lane, and Silas stood in his own doorway feeling like a man reading a letter addressed to someone with his handwriting.

Wren found him there later. "Who was that?"

"I don't know. Someone I robbed, apparently. She brought honey."

"Maren Cotter. Her husband drowned off the Amber Coast eleven years ago; you sold her his memory of the sea, and then it faded on her, the way stolen things fade outside the mind that grew them. She came back three times. You always charged her less." Wren recited it the way she recited everything now—precisely, completely, carrying his past the way a scribe carries a ledger, because that was what she had been trained for and what she had chosen to become instead: the keeper of one man's account.

"Why would I charge her less?"

Wren considered. "Because you watched her come back three times, and some part of you was keeping score of its own cruelty."

"That doesn't sound like mercy."

"It isn't. It's the seed of it." She said these things without softening them. He had asked her, in the first days, to never lie to him about himself, and she had taken the request with the gravity of an oath. "Mercy comes later. If you plant it."

* * *

The mornings became a ritual.

Each day began the same way: Wren at the table, tea going cold between them, and her voice laying out the previous chapters of his life like cards. His childhood in the eastern holler, the mother who forgot him by degrees until one day she smiled at him the way you smile at a polite stranger. The first theft—a traveling peddler's memory of a festival—and the vertigo of holding someone else's joy in his head like a bird with a broken wing. The years of competence and hunger. The courier dying at the border with a scorched map and four words: *the Forge remembers.*

Some mornings he wept at his own history. Some mornings he laughed. Once he got so angry—at the man he had been, at the ease of it, the sheer practiced efficiency of his thefts—that he walked out into the rain and stood there until Wren came and stood beside him without saying anything, because she understood that some entries in a ledger you have to read standing up.

"You could stop," he told her once, late autumn, frost ferning the window. "The Court's finished. Nobody's making you do this. You could go anywhere, be anything. You're free of them."

"I spent nine years writing down other people's lives for masters who used the records as weapons," Wren said. "This is the first archive I've kept that does no harm. I'm not giving it up because it's inconvenient." A pause. "Also, you burn porridge when unsupervised."

"That happened once."

"Twice."

* * *

Winter came, and with it the new thing—the thing nobody in either realm had words for yet, because it had never existed before.

Magic without owners.

It showed up strangely in Hollowmere. Old Maren Cotter found she could hold a tune in her kitchen and the bread would rise sweeter. Children born that spring arrived blinking with eyes that caught lamplight like a fox's. Nothing grand. No archmages striding out of the hills, no thrones of flame. Just small magics, unclaimed, growing in ordinary soil like mushrooms after rain—which was, Wren said, exactly what Silas had intended, though she'd had to explain his intention to him twice, patiently, the way you explain weather to someone who has never been outside.

"They'll want to study it," she warned him one night. "The remnants of the Court, the colleges, the ambitious ones. Something free looks like something unclaimed, to certain kinds of minds."

"Then they'll find out what you and I found out at the Forge," Silas said.

"Which is?"

He had to reach for it. That was the shape of his life now—reaching for things that had been handed to him once and given away. But it came, the way his own story came each morning, a little truer each retelling: "That a memory held in one fist is a weapon. The same memory, let go, is just—light. Everyone's. Nobody's."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "You've never put it that well before."

"Tell me tomorrow," he said. "So I can hear myself say it again."

* * *

On the first morning of the new year, Silas woke before dawn and lay in the dark testing himself, the way he did now. His name: present. Wren: present. The last year—the road, the river shrine, the white heat of the Forge—still smoke. But underneath the smoke, faint as footprints in frost, something new.

He got up and lit the lamp and found paper and the stub of a pencil, and he wrote, in his own uncertain hand:

*I gave away everything I stole, and it turns out that was the only thing in my life I did that was fully mine.*

He stared at it. It wasn't eloquent. It might not even be true. But it was *his*, grown from his own ground, needing no scribe and no archive and no keeper, and he read it three times and then folded it small and put it in his pocket like a coin.

Downstairs, Wren had the tea already steeping and the ledger open—his life, in her careful script, waiting for today's entry.

"Ready?" she asked.

"Not really," Silas said. "Go on anyway."

And she began, as she did each morning, telling him who he was—and outside the window, in the gray first light, snow was falling on Hollowmere, falling equally on every roof and every road and every remembering mind, belonging to no one, covering everything.
