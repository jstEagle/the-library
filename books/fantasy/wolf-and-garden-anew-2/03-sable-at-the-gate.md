## 3. Sable at the Gate

The city smelled of tallow and rain, and beneath both — faint as a thread pulled through cloth — of Wren Kavanagh.

I had tracked him since the counting-house doors spat him out at dusk: a thin man in a grey coat, walking the way debtors walk, spine bent toward the ground as though the cobbles might open and offer a kinder arithmetic. He carried nothing visible. But he carried *it* — I could taste it on the wind between us, that wrongness, that weight. The name. My brother's blood had bought its passage into his hands, and now it rode against his ribs like a second heart.

I kept to the rooftops where the tiles were slick with evening. Below me, the lantern-lighters worked their way down Candlemaker's Row, and the city did what cities do when something dies in them: it closed its shutters, drew its blankets, pretended the night was empty.

Ferran had died in a doorway. I knew because I had found the doorway, and the dark stain the rain had not quite managed to drink, and one wolf-tooth bead from his pendant scattered near the gutter like a dropped word. The Guild's enforcers wore their grey-and-silver well enough to pass for clerks, but they walked like butchers, and they had walked away from that doorway with clean hands and no body, which meant somewhere in this city my brother lay in a chalk circle while men with ledgers decided what his remains were worth.

I am not a patient creature. The wolf in me does not understand waiting; she understands hunger, territory, the long gray patience of winter only when winter is *hers*. But I had waited three days. Three days of watching this scribe carry what belonged to my family through streets that wanted to kill him for it, and learning the shape of him, and hating how small he looked.

Now he had come to the temple.

Of course he had. The map would have shown him nothing else — the old shrine of Saint Vessa-of-the-Thresholds, squatting in its hollow below the cliff road, walls furred with ivy gone rust-red in autumn. The place where the debt-orphans were raised. Where children whose parents owed the Guild were fed, taught their letters, and taught most carefully of all to sign their lives away. I knew the shape of such places. My mother had been born in one.

Wren stopped at the gate.

He didn't go in. That interested me. He stood there in the last light with his hand flat against the iron, the way you touch a door you're afraid has stopped being yours, and the wind came down off the cliffs and moved his hair, and I watched him breathe like a man deciding whether to drown himself or learn to swim.

The moon was rising behind the clouds. Not yet the thirteenth turn — six nights off, by my count, by Ferran's count before me — but fat enough tonight that I felt her in my teeth, in the backs of my hands, in the old scar along my jaw that ached whenever the world grew honest. She made me bold. Or foolish. The two feel identical from inside.

I dropped from the wall.

* * *

I let him hear me land. There was no point in subtlety; the name he carried would protect him better than shadows, and besides, I wanted to see his face when death arrived wearing mine.

He turned slowly. Credit to him — he didn't scream, didn't run. He went very still instead, the stillness of prey that has done this arithmetic before, and his eyes — brown, ordinary, tired — traveled from my boots to the knife at my hip to the teeth braided into my hair, and settled at last on my face, where the wolf shows if you know where to look. The amber eye. The too-wide set of the mouth. Most people flinched there. He didn't flinch. He *recognized*, which was worse, and then his hand went to his chest, to the inner pocket, an instinct so quick and so hopeless it nearly made me laugh.

"You're Sable," he said.

That I did not expect. "Ferran told you about me?"

"He said—" Wren's voice cracked and he started again, quieter. "He said if anything happened to him, I should find a woman called Sable who owes him nothing and hates the Guild more than breathing. He said you'd be angry."

"Was that all he said?"

"No." His throat worked. "He said you'd be right to be."

The moon slid free of the cloud then, and the courtyard filled up with silver, and I saw him whole for the first time: ink stains to the second knuckle, a ledger-man's pallor, and under his collarbone — just showing where his shirt gaped — the mark. A small grey sigil, stamped into the skin itself, like wax pressed into flesh. The Guild's accounting seal. The brand they put on assets, not people.

They had marked him already. Which meant they weren't hunting a thief anymore. They were hunting their property home.

My knife was out of its sheath before I'd decided anything, which is often how the wolf and I disagree. "Give me the map."

"I don't—"

"The map, scribe. The one my brother bled onto with his last breath. It was never his to give and it isn't yours to keep. Give it to me and I'll let you walk back to your numbers." The lie sat easily in my mouth; the wolf doesn't mind lies that are mercies. We both knew I couldn't let him walk anywhere. He'd seen my face. And the Guild's mark on him meant every door in this city was already closing, whether he understood it or not.

But Wren Kavanagh did not reach for the map.

He reached for the pocket anyway — and I tensed, knife turning — but what he drew out was not paper. It was a disc of black wax the size of a coin, unbroken, sealed with a sigil I had seen only once before: pressed into the dirt floor of our grandmother's hut, drawn in ash on Ferran's palm when we were children, sketched obsessively in the margins of every page he ever stole from the Guild. The true glyph. The god's own knot.

And Wren held it out to me flat on his palm like an offering, and said, "I haven't opened it. I don't know what it says. He pressed it into my hands while he was dying and I don't even know why *me*."

I should have taken it. Every instinct, every grief-sharpened plan I'd built over three sleepless days said take the seal, take the map, cut the witness loose and burn his name from the story. The wolf wanted it done quickly, cleanly, the way winters end.

Instead I looked at his palm. At the ink-stained fingers holding death like something fragile. At the Guild's brand riding his skin like a leash, and the terror under his exhaustion, and the strange stubborn gentleness of a man who, offered his life for a lie, had chosen to tell the truth to a stranger with a knife.

"You're marked," I said. "Do you know what that means?"

"They came to the scriptorium after—" He swallowed. "After the man died. They asked questions. I lied to them." Something moved across his face that was almost a smile and almost a wound. "I've never lied before. In thirty-one years of balancing accounts, I never once fudged a column. And then I lied to the Guild's enforcers about a dead man, and it felt—" He stopped, ashamed of whatever word was coming.

"Like freedom," I finished for him.

His breath left him in a rush, fogging in the cold. "Yes."

We stood there in the moonlight, the wolf and the scribe, and between us the little black seal caught the light like a drop of frozen midnight. Somewhere beyond the temple walls a dog began to howl — a real dog, a stupid happy city dog — and my own throat tightened around an answering sound I did not let out.

"Ferran was my brother," I said. It came out rougher than I meant. Grief always sounds like anger in my mouth; I've stopped apologizing for it. "Not by blood. By choice, by pack, by everything that matters more than blood. He spent four years getting inside the Guild's archives. Four years pretending to be less than he was, copying their seals, learning their roads, looking for the fragment they buried under this temple — the last piece of the name. And then he handed the result of four years to a stranger in a doorway instead of bringing it home to me."

"He wasn't a stranger," Wren said quietly. "He used to sit in the counting-house and watch me work. For months. I thought he was auditing me." A pause. "I think he was choosing me."

The wolf in me bristled, hackles high — *chose another, chose a stranger, chose a debtor over his own* — but underneath the bristle, some older and lonelier animal understood. Ferran had been careful all his life the way I had never been. Careful, patient, hidden. Of course he'd choose someone careful. Someone who could keep a secret in his bones. Someone whose whole life had been a locked room, waiting for one honest lie to break it open.

"He saw you carrying the same cage he escaped," I said. "That's why. Not mercy. Recognition."

"Maybe both." Wren's thumb brushed the seal's edge, reverent, terrified. "There's something else. The map — it doesn't lead out of the city. It leads *under* this temple. Under the orphanage. Under—" his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—under the dormitory where I slept until I was sixteen. Whatever they buried, they buried it beneath the children. Beneath *me*."

Cold walked up my spine on small feet. Because that was exactly what the Guild would do. What the Guild had always done: hide their treasures where no one dares dig, and hide their digging inside charity. Debt-orphanages were not shelters. They were vaults with lullabies.

"And there's more," Wren said. He was shaking now, though whether from fear or fury I couldn't tell, and I suspected he couldn't either. "When Mother Vess signed my bond — I found the record tonight, before I came here. The purchase price. It wasn't coin. It was *a portion of the god's name*. My debt was bought with a piece of a dead god's true name. I've been collateral my entire life and I never—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles to his mouth. When his hand came away his eyes were wet and blazing all at once. "They wrote me into their books as a payment. A *line item*."

The moon stood high and white above the temple roof, six nights from her thirteenth turn, and I felt her pull in the marrow of me like a tide going out. Six nights until the seal weakened. Six nights until the Guild would move to finish what their founders started — and now they wouldn't need to hunt for a key, because the key was standing right in front of me, branded and furious and free for the first time in his life.

They would come for him. Soon, and in numbers, and they would bring instruments for reading names out of living skin.

"Put the seal away," I said, and sheathed my knife, and heard myself say the rest before the wolf could object: "You're coming with me. Tonight. Because in six nights the moon turns, and the Guild means to complete the seal using *you* as the final key — and because my brother trusted you with the only thing that mattered to him, and I intend to find out why, even if it kills us both."

Wren looked past me, at the temple gate, at the dark windows of the dormitory where he'd been raised as a debt. Then he looked at the seal in his palm.

"Beneath the children's beds," he whispered. "All this time."

"Yes," I said. "So let's go dig up what they buried — before they bury you with it."

Beyond the wall, far off toward the Guild quarter, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. Not any bell that meant anything good.

The hunt had begun without us.
