## 4. What the Temple Buried

The map did not want to be read by daylight.

Wren discovered this in the grey hour before dawn, hunched over his desk in the rented room above the chandler's shop, with the stranger's blood still rusting the creases. By lamplight the ink had seemed ordinary enough — a tangle of streets, the river's crooked spine, the temple district rendered in careful miniature. But when he carried it to the window and let the first pale light fall across it, lines bloomed beneath the surface like veins under skin: a second map, older, drawn in something that shimmered faintly silver and sank away again when the light grew too strong.

*Below,* the hidden lines said, in the language of architecture rather than words. Stairs. A chamber. A door marked with a symbol Wren knew better than his own signature — the seven-pointed star of the Foundling Temple of Saint Verrine, where he had been raised from the age of four.

Where Mother Superior Vess still kept her candles burning for him every Duskday, or so she wrote in the letters he was too proud to answer more than twice a year.

He should have gone to Sable first. She was waiting — she'd made that plain enough at the temple gate three nights past, with her brother's grief worn like armor and her wolf-amber eyes measuring him for a coffin or an ally. *The map was mine by right,* she had said. *But you carry the name unknowing, and the Guild has marked you. That makes us temporary.*

Temporary. It was, he supposed, the most honest word anyone had ever offered him.

But Sable would have wanted to go in through the walls, or over them, teeth first. And this was the temple. This was *his* place, bought and paid for in a way he was only beginning to understand. If there were answers beneath Saint Verrine's flagstones, Wren wanted to walk in through the front gate, in daylight, as the debt-orphan who had come home — and see with his own eyes whether the people who raised him had sold him before they'd ever fed him.

So he went alone. He told himself it was prudence. He was learning to lie to himself now too; it came easier after the first one.

* * *

The temple smelled the same, and that was the cruelest part.

Beeswax and cold stone and the particular sweetness of the dried feverfew the younger children strung into garlands every autumn. Twenty years since Wren had last stood in the entry nave, and his body remembered everything: the exact depth of the dip worn into the third step by generations of small feet, the way the morning light fell in long amber blades through the eastern clerestory, the sound of the kitchen bell that meant porridge and that meant safety, that meant someone had counted you among the living and found you worth feeding.

"Brother Kavanagh."

Mother Superior Vess descended the nave steps with her ledger-clasped hands and her unhurried gait, white streaks in her dark braid, her eyes the same warm brown he remembered. Warm, and missing nothing. They had always been missing nothing. He had spent his childhood believing that was love. Now he wondered if it had simply been accounting.

"Mother Superior." His voice held. He was proud of that. "I've come home."

"For how long?" she asked softly, and there it was — not *welcome*, but a question of duration, a query of accounts. Or perhaps he was only seeing betrayal everywhere now, the way a man who finds one worm in the flour starts tasting worms in the bread.

"As long as I need," he said. "I've been having trouble sleeping. I thought... familiar walls."

She studied him a moment longer, then inclined her head. "You know where your old cell is. The novices are at lessons until midday bell. The undercroft is closed for repairs — the east stair is unsafe, so keep to the main corridors." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Do you understand me, Wren?"

Perfectly. He understood her perfectly, and it broke something in him even as he bowed and said, "Yes, Mother Superior."

The east stair. Of course. The map's silver lines ended at a chamber directly below the east cloister, reachable only by a stair the temple had supposedly bricked up decades ago.

They had never bricked anything up. They had just taught everyone which doors were walls.

* * *

He waited for noon, when the temple emptied its children into the yard and its elders into the refectory. Then he went down through the wash-house, whose back wall had always had a loose stone — he'd hidden stolen plums behind it at age nine, certain no adult alive could possibly know — and found the gap behind it exactly as wide as his shoulders and smelling of dust and old candle smoke.

Beyond lay a corridor that appeared on no plan of the temple he had ever seen, though he had copied the temple's own architectural records twice during his apprenticeship years, line for line. Which meant either the plans lied, or the ledgers did. Knowing the Guild of Impossible Things as he now did, he suspected both, in equal measure.

The stairs went down farther than the building above had height. He counted forty-one steps and felt the pressure change against his ears, felt the air grow cool and mineral, tasting of deep water and older things. His candle flame leaned steadily toward the wall, as if being pulled somewhere, and he followed it because following a flame was easier than thinking.

At the bottom: a door of black wood, iron-banded, bearing the Guild's mark burned into the grain — the impossible knot, the seal that has no beginning. Wren stood before it for a long time, listening to his own heart.

Then he took out the tools of his trade. Not weapons. A scribe's kit: wax, wire, fine picks. Twenty years of balancing accounts no honest arithmetic could balance had taught him that every lock is just a sum waiting for the right number.

The lock surrendered in eleven minutes. He noted the time out of habit. Somewhere in the dark, some part of him was already drafting the entry: *One secret, extracted. Cost: everything previously believed. Paid in full.*

* * *

The room beyond was a scriptorium, and Wren knew scriptoria the way sailors know weather.

But this one was wrong. The desks faced inward, toward a central pedestal instead of a superior's chair. The shelves held no books — only ledgers, hundreds of them, bound in the grey-green leather of the Guild's lesser houses, each spine stamped with a number and, beneath the number, a name.

Not the Guild's names.

Children's names.

Wren lifted the nearest volume with hands that had begun, very slightly, to tremble. *Kessaly Mirrin, entered Year of the Low Tide.* Inside, in a clerk's immaculate hand: the record of a bond. An orphan girl, aged six, indentured to the Guild's counting-house for a term of thirty years. Standard terms. Standard interest. Nothing remarkable — except the payment column.

The payment column read: *Consideration received: 1 fragment, name-class, minor. Verified.*

Name-class. The word hit him like cold water, because he had read about name-class fragments only once, three weeks ago, in the dying stranger's other papers — the ones Sable now carried. Fragments of the sealed god's true name, sundered at the war's ending, scattered into vessels that could hold them. The Guild had spent two centuries telling the city it *guarded* those fragments, sealed them away for everyone's protection.

Here was the protection: traded for orphan-debt. Bought and sold like any commodity, priced against a child's labor, amortized over thirty years.

Wren set down Kessaly Mirrin's ledger very carefully, the way one sets down something that might detonate. Then he found the shelf index — because of course there was an index; there was always an index; the Guild's one true faith was bookkeeping — and ran his finger down the entries until he reached the K's.

*Kavanagh, Wren. Entered Year of the Ash Wind. Age four.*

His ledger sat where it had sat for twenty years, waiting for him like a trap waits.

He opened it.

Age four: acquisition, standard terms, consideration received — *1 fragment, name-class, minor, designated Vessel-K.* Age five: first assessment of vessel stability, favorable. Age nine: note, subject shows unusual affinity for numeric work; recommend cultivation. Age twelve: recommendation implemented; transferred to Guild scriptorium training. Age sixteen: assessment, vessel integrity excellent; subject remains unaware; recommend continued unawareness. Every year thereafter, the same notation, patient as erosion: *Vessel intact. Subject unaware. Continue.*

Continue. Continue. Continue. All the way down to the final page, dated eleven days ago, in ink still faintly glossy:

*Subject located. Retrieval authorized upon completion of seal. Final key confirmed viable.*

Wren read it three times. The words did not change. He had spent his life among numbers and knew their honesty better than most men's — a number cannot be reasoned with, cannot be loved into a different value. Four plus four is eight, and a child purchased at four years old is a vessel, and a vessel is a key, and a key is used.

He had thought himself the Guild's clerk. He had been its inventory.

And Vess —

The thought arrived without permission and he tried to bar the door against it, but the door, like all doors tonight, was already open. Because Vess had signed the acquisition page. Her name was there at the bottom in the strong, slanted hand that had written *Well done, little wren* on his first copywork, that had signed his festival cards, that had held his face between her cool palms the night before he left for the Guild and said, *Whatever they ask of you there, remember that you were loved here first.*

Loved here first. Purchased here first, rather. Loved the way one loves a good investment.

He heard footsteps on the stair — measured, unhurried, known — and did not turn. There was no point. You cannot hide from someone who has watched you your whole life; she had simply done her watching from above while he did his from below, and neither of them had ever truly seen the other.

"You followed the east stair," Mother Superior Vess said quietly. "I told you it was unsafe."

"It was." His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "It led down to the truth."

Silence. Then the soft sweep of her habit on stone as she came to stand beside him, looking at the open ledger as though it were a wounded bird she might yet save.

"I was young when they brought you," she said. "Newer to my vows than you were to walking. The Guild came with papers, and I was told the alternative was the workhouse, and I signed." A breath. "I have signed my name to terrible mercies for thirty years, Wren. It does not make them less terrible. It makes them mine."

"You knew what I was carrying." Not a question. "All these years. You lit candles for a key."

"I lit candles for a boy who liked plums and hated thunder and cried when the kitchen cat died." Her voice frayed at last, just barely. "Both things are true. That is the cruelty of it — both things have always been true."

"The Guild is coming for me." He finally turned and looked at her, and saw her flinch at whatever she found in his face. "Eleven days ago they authorized retrieval. I'm not your ward anymore, Mother Superior. I'm your *stock*. So tell me the truth, all of it, because I am done being protected by people who profit from my ignorance. What happens when they complete the seal?"

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them, the warm brown had gone cold and clear, the eyes of a woman stepping off a ledge she'd been circling for decades.

"They will write your name into the binding," she said. "Your ledger-name — the one they built from the god's fragment inside you. And the seal will close forever, and everything you are will go into the dark with it, and the city will sleep safely, and no one will remember there was ever a boy called Wren Kavanagh at all."

Above them, muffled by forty-one steps and twenty years of lies, the temple bell rang for afternoon lessons — and beneath its familiar voice, Wren heard another sound, far off and rising: hooves. Many. Coming up the temple road.

Sable's warning rang in his skull like a struck bell: *When the hunters come, don't be where you said you'd be.*

"Back door," he said. "Tell me there's a back door."

"There's a back door to every room in this temple," Vess said, already moving, already pulling keys from her sleeve. "I helped build half of them. Run, little wren — and this time, don't look back for me."

He looked back anyway. He suspected he always would.

But he ran.
