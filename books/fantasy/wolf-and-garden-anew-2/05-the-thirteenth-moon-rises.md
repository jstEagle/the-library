## 5. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The city had two maps, Sable thought, and only one of them was honest.

The first hung in the Guildhall's counting-house, inked on vellum by men who believed streets were lines and buildings were boxes. The second was the one her brother Ashen had died carrying: the old roads, the under-roads, the paths the city had paved over and pretended to forget. She had learned them as a cub learns scent — not with the eyes but with the whole body, in the dark beneath the granaries where the wolf-souled were permitted to sleep.

Now she taught them to a scribe.

"Again," she said, and watched Wren Kavanagh press his palm against the damp stone of the tunnel wall, feeling for the seam he could not see.

"There." His fingers found it, a hairline crack where an older gate had been bricked shut and plastered smooth. "The mortar's newer here. Someone wanted this closed."

"Someone always does." She let him work the loose block free himself, though it took him three times longer than it would take her. That was the point. "The Guild sealed these passages after the last war. Not to keep people out." She nodded at the black mouth beyond the brick. "To keep something *in*."

Wren glanced back at her, and in the lamplight his face had that look she was learning to read — the ledger-look, the one that weighed every word before it entered his mouth. "You said your people used them."

"My people *were* them, once. Before the wolf-souled became convenient scapegoats and inconvenient witnesses." She ducked through the gap, shoulders brushing both walls, and heard him follow. "Ashen mapped every one of these roads when he was younger than you are now. He said a city forgets its bones before it forgets its debts."

They moved through the under-city for hours. Sable set the pace deliberately slow, marking each junction in chalk — not for herself, but so that Wren could draw them later, commit them to paper in that precise hand of his. He carried no map of his own yet; she wouldn't allow one until he'd walked every road twice. Paper could be stolen. Paper had already been stolen once, and a man had died for it, and the thief had been her own blood, and the grief of that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

But watching him learn — watching him run his fingers along the walls like a man reading braille, murmuring measurements under his breath, correcting his mental ledger of turns and distances — something in her unclenched, just slightly. Ashen had trusted this scribe enough to die pressing his secrets into the man's hands. The least she could do was make sure the trust hadn't been wasted.

At the third hour they surfaced in the ruins of the tannery district, where the roofs had fallen in and moonlight pooled in the flooded cellars like spilled milk. The thirteenth moon was rising — not full, not new, but swollen wrong, a bruised silver coin with a shadow at its heart. Sable felt it in her teeth. Wolf-souled always did.

"What is it?" Wren asked. He'd seen her stop. He noticed things now. It unsettled her how quickly he'd learned to notice her.

"The moon turns thirteen times in its great cycle," she said. "Twelve turns, it's an ordinary moon. On the thirteenth, the light comes from the other side." She looked up at it, hackles rising along her arms despite herself. "The night the seal weakens. The night whatever your Guild buried under your temple can be touched."

"Touched how?"

"I don't know. Neither do they, maybe. They've been maintaining that seal for nine hundred years, patching it with names and debts and stolen fragments, and I don't think any of them living remembers what's actually underneath." She turned to face him fully. In the cellar-light her reflection wavered in the water between them, and for a moment she saw herself as others saw her: yellow-eyed, sharp-toothed, a thing out of the old stories mothers told to frighten children into bed. "But I know what they want from *you*."

He went still. He was good at going still; it was the scribe's defense, the way small animals freeze. "My name?"

"Not the name you say out loud. The one in their ledgers. Your debt-bond isn't just money, Wren. Debt-bonds never were. Every bond is written with a true-name fragment folded into it — that's why they hold. Why you can't walk away. Why your signature binds you tighter than chains would." She watched the color leave his face by degrees, a slow tide going out. "Your ledger-name is a piece of the god. And on the thirteenth moon, they mean to use it as the final key."

"That's—" He stopped. Started again, and his voice came out smaller than she'd ever heard it. "Mother Vess signed my bond. She held the pen. She told me the debt was for my keep. For bread and a cot and lessons."

"It probably was. Debts that size are cheap. It's the *names* that cost." Sable crouched at the water's edge, needing to move, needing not to tower over him while the ground shifted beneath his feet. "Ask yourself why a counting-house keeps orphan-records in the same vault as its god-relics. Ask yourself why the seal needs renewing every generation, and why there's always another debt-orphan born that year, and why the Guild pays temples to raise children nobody claims."

"You're saying they've been farming us. Harvesting—" He laughed, one short ugly note with no humor in it, the sound of a man whose arithmetic had finally come out wrong. "I reconciled those accounts. I balanced the books myself. There was nothing—"

"Because you weren't meant to see it. Because the entries were written by people better at hiding than you are at finding." She softened, or tried to; her voice had always been more growl than comfort. "Ashen found it. One line in forty years of ledgers. A transfer of a name-fragment, dated the winter you were born, paid to the Temple of the Garden Gate. He followed it for three years. It killed him."

Wren sat down slowly on the cellar's flooded step, water soaking through his trousers, and didn't seem to notice or care. Above them the bruised moon climbed past a broken skylight, and its strange light turned the floodwater to hammered tin.

"They'll come for me," he said at last. "Before the turn. They'll need me alive, won't they? The key has to be given freely or spoken knowingly, something like that. So they'll take me gently. Offer me something." His eyes came up, and there was something new in them — not fear, exactly. Calculation. Her kind of calculation. "They'll offer to erase my debt."

Sable felt her ears prick forward. "Would you take it?"

"A month ago? I'd have wept with gratitude." He looked down at his hands, the ink-stained hands that had spent ten years balancing other people's impossible accounts. "Now I know what the debt is made of. Erasing it means giving them back the piece of the god in my name so they can nail it into the seal and start the whole cycle again with some other child." He shook his head slowly. "It's not mercy they're selling, Sable. It's maintenance."

Something loosened in her chest then, warm and alarming. She had spent her whole life measuring people for betrayal — it was the wolf-souled way, trust being a luxury her kind couldn't afford — and here was this soft-handed scribe doing the arithmetic of his own captivity and arriving, unprompted, at refusal.

"We need the temple orphans," she said. "If the Guild moves on the thirteenth turn, we'll need eyes inside, hands, runners. Children the Guild thinks are furniture."

"They're not children anymore. Most of them." A ghost of a smile crossed his face. "There's a girl, Pip, who can pick any lock in the temple. Two brothers who know the crypt tunnels because they slept in them during the fever winters. And Mother Vess—"

"Can she be trusted?"

The question landed hard; she saw it land. He was quiet a long time, watching the moonlight shiver on the water.

"She raised me," he said finally. "She also sold me. Both of those are true, and I don't know yet which one is bigger." He stood, wringing out his sleeve, and when he looked at her again his jaw was set in a way she recognized from mirrors — grief, worn as armor. "We go to them tomorrow. Tonight I want to walk the old road under the temple one more time. I want to know every stone of the place they think they own."

So they went back down, and Sable led him through the dark, calling out the turns before they came, letting him call some of them himself, and by the fourth junction he was leading and she was following, and neither of them remarked on it.

Near dawn they surfaced in the garden behind the Temple of the Garden Gate, among the dead rosebushes and the frost-killed herbs, and Wren stopped at the gate and put his hand flat against the wood.

"Sable." His voice was strange. "Thank you. For teaching me the roads. For—" He gestured vaguely at everything: the night, the tunnels, the truth sitting heavy in his chest. "Nobody's ever given me anything without putting it in the ledger first."

She shrugged, uncomfortable. "Wolf-souled don't keep accounts."

"No," he said. "I'm starting to understand that. You keep something else instead." He pushed the gate open onto the sleeping temple, and the bruised moon hung above the bell tower like an eye half-closed. "Three nights until the turn. Whatever the Guild is planning, they'll move soon — and I think I know which door they'll use."

Below them, deep beneath the flagstones, something ancient stirred in its long confinement and turned over, like a sleeper hearing its own name called from very far away.

* * *

They made camp that day in the tannery cellars, sleeping in shifts, and it was during Sable's watch that the hunters found their trail.

She smelled them before she heard them — wet wool and lamp-oil and the particular sourness of men wearing someone else's authority. Three of them, moving through the under-city with lanterns shuttered to slits, following chalk marks that should have been washed away by now.

She woke Wren with a hand over his mouth and a finger to her lips, and pressed her ear to the wall, listening.

"—found the chalk at the granary junction," a voice murmured, muffled by stone. "Master Corvane says bring the scribe breathing. The key must be willing, remember. Gentle handling. He's owed courtesy, apparently."

"And the wolf?"

A pause. Then, flatter: "The wolf is owed nothing."

Sable bared her teeth silently in the dark. Beside her, she felt Wren tense — felt him hear it too, the casual arithmetic of it, *the wolf is owed nothing*, as if she were a rounding error to be struck from the page.

They waited, barely breathing, while the lantern-glow slid across the seams of the wall inches from Wren's shoulder. Then the hunters moved on, deeper into the under-city, chasing chalk ghosts toward the temple.

When the glow had faded entirely, Sable released him. He didn't speak right away. Instead he took out his little book of figures — he'd started keeping it hidden inside his coat since the night the stranger died — and wrote three words in the margin, small and precise:

*They will come.*

"Yes," she said quietly. "And we'll be ready."

"No." He closed the book, and in the darkness his yellow-lit eyes met hers, and she saw the scribe was gone and something harder stood in his place. "We won't be ready. We'll be *ahead*. They think they're hunting me, Sable. Let them keep thinking it. Three nights until the turn — and tomorrow, we teach the orphans to read ledgers."

Above them, far away, the thirteenth moon crested the rooftops of the sleeping city, one turn closer to its breaking point, and somewhere beneath the Temple of the Garden Gate, the god who had been dying for nine hundred years felt its stolen name draw nearer, and nearer, and nearer home.
