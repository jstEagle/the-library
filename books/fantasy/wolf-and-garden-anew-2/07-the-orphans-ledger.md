## 7. The Orphans' Ledger

I smelled the Guild on him before I saw the blood.

They had taken Wren Kavanagh at dusk, in the crook of the Tanner's Stair where the old roads ran close to the new ones, and they had carried him down into the counting-house like a ledger being returned to its shelf. I had watched from the rooftops with my brother's map pressed flat against my ribs and my teeth aching with the need to follow. Wolf-patience, my mother had called it. The discipline of not lunging.

But patience has a smell too, and by the third watch of the night it had gone sour in me.

The counting-house squatted at the heart of the Guild quarter like a fat spider in a web of paper — I could hear it from three streets away, that endless dry whisper of pages turning, hundreds of clerks recording hundreds of debts, the whole city's hunger written down in copper and ink. Its windows were high and narrow. Its doors were iron-banded oak. Every lock in the place was made to keep people *in*.

Which meant, when I slipped through the coal-chute behind the boiler-rooms, that no one was watching for anything trying to get out.

He was in the master of accounts' private scriptorium, alone, which told me everything about how the Guild thought of him: dangerous enough to isolate, valuable enough not to chain. They'd left him a lamp and a chair and a blank sheet of paper, because their whole religion was the belief that everyone could be bought if you found the right price. He sat very straight in the chair, hands flat on the desk, staring at nothing.

When I dropped from the ventilation shaft into the room, he didn't scream. I'll grant him that. He went white as fresh vellum and knocked over the inkwell, but he didn't scream.

"Sable." My name came out cracked. "How—"

"Later." I put a claw-tip under his chin and turned his face to the light. Bruise-yellow blooming along his jaw. The particular gray around the eyes of someone who had spent hours being offered something he wanted badly. "What did they offer?"

"Erasure." His voice was flat. "My debt. Gone. All I had to do was give them the map and forget the stranger ever handed it to me."

"And?"

"And I said yes."

My hackles rose before my mind caught up. Then I saw his eyes — and understood. He said it the way a man says *I walked through fire and did not burn*, and there was a wildness under the exhaustion, a bright and terrified joy.

"They wrote it down," he whispered. "They wrote my surrender into their own ledger, Sable. Signed, witnessed, sealed. A binding contract of silence, dated tonight, witnessed by the master of accounts himself." He laughed — a small, broken, astonishing sound. "Do you understand? It's *in their book*. Which means it can be contradicted by a better book."

For a moment I just looked at him, this soft-handed scribe who had lied once in his life and escaped a Guild fortress by getting them to write down his treachery in triplicate. My brother would have liked him. Ash had loved exactly this kind of cleverness — the kind that looked like weakness until the moment it bit.

"Can you walk?" I asked.

"I can run if you growl at me."

So I growled at him, and we ran.

* * *

We surfaced in the Warrens an hour past midnight, in the shell of the old tannery where the Guild's writ thinned to nothing and the forgotten things of the city learned to live without being counted. I expected him to collapse. Instead he stood in the doorway of our hiding-place, dripping river water, shaking off cold like a dog, and said:

"The children. Tell me about the children."

I blinked at him. "What children?"

"The orphans." He wrung out his sleeve with precise, furious movements, as though the water were the Guild itself. "You said the temple raised debt-orphans. Plural. You said Vess kept records going back generations. If they bound me with a fragment of the god's name, they bound others. How many?"

I hadn't said those things — he had, in the days since we found what lay beneath his temple, saying them over and over like a man testing whether a floor would hold his weight. But he was right, and I hated how right he was, because I knew the answer.

"Hundreds," I said. "Maybe more. The temples take them in when their families can't pay. The Guild buys their bonds. Nobody asks what the bonds are purchased *with*."

"Then nobody knows." He was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a wolf-circuit even though he wasn't wolf. "Nobody knows because the records exist in one place, under one seal, in a language most of these children can't read. That's the whole trick, isn't it? Not cruelty — *bookkeeping*. You don't have to chain anyone if they never see the chain." He stopped. Turned to me. In the dark of the tannery his eyes had caught the last of the lamplight and held it. "So we show them the chain."

"You want to steal the ledgers."

"I want to *copy* them." He said it like a man correcting a small but important error. "Stealing proves nothing — stolen books can be dismissed as forgeries, and the Guild would burn us for thieves and move on. Copies are different. Copies mean witnesses. One true record in every orphan's hand, signed by every orphan who finds their own name in it. You can't call five hundred children liars. Well." A grim flicker. "You can try."

I sat back on my haunches and looked at this man I had cornered outside a temple wall intending to take his throat, and felt something turn over in my chest that I did not have a name for yet.

"Ash planned for years," I said quietly. "He mapped the old roads, learned the Guild's rhythms, waited for the thirteenth moon. And he died in a scriptorium doorway handing his life's work to a stranger because the stranger was kind to him for ten minutes." I let the silence sit between us. "If we do this, there's no waiting anymore. The moment the first copy leaves your pen, the hunt starts again, and this time they won't offer erasure. They'll offer the seal. Your name as the final key."

"Yes," Wren said simply.

Just that. Yes. As if I'd asked whether he wanted supper.

Wolf that I am, I know all the ways courage dresses itself. Roaring courage, bared-teeth courage, the courage that smells of blood and battle-fever. I had never seen this kind before: the courage of a man picking up a pen.

"All right, scribe," I said. "Teach me to make ink."

* * *

It took six days.

Six days of Wren hunched over salvaged paper in the tannery loft while I ran the old roads, carrying messages to the temple and back, learning the shape of the thing we were building. Mother Superior Vess turned out to be made of sterner stuff than her betrayal deserved — when Wren sent word asking for the orphan-rolls, she sent them wrapped in altar linens with a note in her careful hand: *I was wrong about what protection meant. Let me be wrong in the useful direction.* She could not leave the temple; her order still needed a traitor inside its walls. But every scrap of record she could smuggle out came to us, page by page, hidden in bread deliveries and hymnals.

And the children came.

That was the part I had not foreseen. I thought we would copy names and deliver them, a grim postal service for the deceived. I had not understood that the act of copying was itself a door.

The first was a girl named Tansy, nine years old, debt-bound to the chandlers' guild for a father's candle-debt she couldn't possibly have incurred. She came to the tannery following rumors of a wolf-woman, because the Warrens traded rumors like currency and mine were cheap that week. Wren sat her down at his worktable, pulled out the roll with her name in it, and read it aloud to her — slowly, line by line, translating the clerk's Latin-glassed jargon into plain speech.

"*Purchased against:* one fragment, minor grade, designation—" he paused, and I watched his jaw tighten as he read his god's stolen name in miniature, "—designation withheld. Value: sufficient."

"Sufficient for what?" Tansy asked.

"For buying you," Wren said gently.

She looked at the page for a long time. Then she looked up at him, and something passed between them that I recognized from my mother's pack, from the way wolves mark each other after a hard winter: *you are one of ours now.*

"I can write my letters," Tansy said. "Sister Maren taught me. Can I help?"

By the fourth day there were eleven of them in the loft. By the sixth there were nineteen, ranging from Tansy's nine years to a glower-jawed boy of sixteen named Ferro who had been bound at birth for a debt older than he was. Wren taught them the clerk's shorthand so they could read their own chains. He showed them how to make copies that would stand scrutiny — matching paper, matching iron-gall ink, margins ruled to Guild specification. "A forgery is a lie," he told them, pacing his three-step circuit while small hands scratched away at the truth. "This is the opposite. We are making the most honest documents in this city's history. They just happen to look official, because the Guild only believes things that look official."

They worked by day and slept in heaps by night, these discarded children, and somewhere in those six days the tannery loft stopped being a hiding place and became something else. I heard it change. Wolves hear such things. It began to sound like a den.

One evening I found Wren asleep at the worktable, cheek on a finished page, ink dried on his fingers, and Tansy had draped her threadbare shawl over his shoulders without waking either of them. I stood in the doorway a long moment, watching.

My brother had died believing the fragment-map was his burden alone, the last duty of a scattered pack. He had been wrong. The pack was here. It smelled of ink and candle-tallow and children who had never been counted, and its alpha was a scribe who fell asleep over his work.

Wren stirred. "Sable? Is something—"

"Nothing," I said. "Keep sleeping."

But sleep wouldn't come to me that night, and near dawn I was on the rooftop watching the Guild quarter when I saw why.

Fires. Not one — a ring of them, lit at the corners of the temple district, burning greenish-white in the pre-dawn dark. Seal-fire. The Guild wasn't waiting for the thirteenth moon to crest naturally.

They were starting the rite early, and they were doing it in the one place on earth where Wren's heart still lived.

I went down into the loft two steps at a time.

"Wren," I said, and my voice came out with the growl already in it. "Wake the children. We're going home."
