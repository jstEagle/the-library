## 8. Night of the Turning Moon

The thirteenth moon rose over Ashfall like a coin held up to a dying man's eye.

Wren crouched in the bell tower of the temple where he had been raised as a debt-orphan, and watched it climb — pale, swollen, wrong somehow, as though the sky itself had begun to sweat. Below him the temple courtyard lay empty, swept clean by order of Mother Superior Vess herself. The orphans had been sent to bed early. The novices dismissed. Even the beggars who usually slept against the eastern wall had been driven off with copper coins and cold words.

The Guild had bought the night entire.

"They've sealed every gate," whispered Pip, one of the youngest orphans, pressed against his side in the tower's shadow. She was eleven, sharp-eyed, quick-fingered, and she had volunteered for this without a flicker of fear that Wren wished he could borrow. "I checked them all twice. Wax seals with the master of accounts' own mark. He's down there already."

"Then we go down too."

"Through the crypt stair." She grinned at him in the dark, gap-toothed. "The one you fell down when you were seven and Vess told everyone you'd run away for a day so the other children wouldn't laugh at you."

He remembered. Of course he remembered. He remembered everything; it was both his gift and his affliction. The loose stone behind the reliquary shelf, the narrow stair the builders had bricked up and forgotten, descending past the old ossuary into whatever the temple had been built upon — and whatever the temple had been built *to hold*.

Sable appeared at the tower door without sound, which she managed even now, even favoring the arm the Guild's hunters had cut open two nights past. Her grey eyes caught the moonlight and gave nothing back. Around her neck, beneath her coat, hung her brother's wolf-tooth pendant; around Wren's, hidden against his skin, the wax-sealed scrap bearing the god's stolen name.

"The rite has started," she said. "I can hear it. Not with my ears." She touched her chest, where the wolf lived. "With this. It's like hearing someone call your whole pack home to be butchered."

* * *

They went down through the temple's bones.

The reliquary shelf swung aside on hinges oiled only that afternoon — Pip's work, done while the novices slept. The forgotten stair exhaled cold air scented with stone dust and something older: myrrh burned long ago, tallow, and beneath it all a faint sweet rot like fruit left too long in a locked room. Wren counted the steps out of habit — forty-one to the ossuary landing, then the crack in the far wall where the masons' work simply stopped, as if they had dug until they found what they were looking for and fled.

Beyond the crack, the air changed. Warmer. Humming. He felt it in his teeth before he heard it: a low sustained tone, the sound a struck bell makes after the ringing stops, except this ringing had never stopped. Nine hundred years, perhaps. However long the Guild had been holding its breath.

They emerged into a gallery above the seal-chamber, behind broken balustrades carved with wolves and gardens — the old faith, the one the ledgers never mentioned. And below, lit by a ring of white fire that cast no heat, the Guild performed its work.

There were perhaps thirty of them: account-masters in slate robes, enforcers with iron-shod staves, scribes at portable desks recording the rite line by line, because the Guild recorded *everything*, even its own blasphemies. At their center stood Master Corvane, the master of accounts himself, a spare man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a ledger closing. Before him, set into the chamber floor, was the seal.

Wren had expected chains. A cage. Something with teeth. Instead it was a garden — or the ghost of one. A circle of living green no wider than a wagon, grass and small white flowers growing in impossible defiance of the underground dark, and at its heart a single tree no taller than a child, its leaves silver, trembling without wind. The humming came from it. The god's fragment — the last piece, the piece the map had promised — slept in its roots.

And around the garden, driven into the earth at intervals, stood nine stone pillars, each engraved with a syllable of the true name. Eight glowed faintly, moon-white. The ninth was dark, and waiting.

"The final key," Sable breathed beside him. "Your ledger-name."

Below, Corvane raised his hands. "Bring the instrument."

Two enforcers dragged forward a figure bound at wrists and ankles — and Wren's heart stopped, stumbled, started again, because the figure was not him. It was Mother Superior Vess, stripped of her vestments, dressed in plain grey, her silver hair unbound for the first time he had ever seen. They had taken her quietly, then. Used her authority to clear the temple, and then used her.

"She refused to give up your name," Pip whispered, horrified. "I heard the sisters talking. They asked her for weeks and she said the ledger was lost."

Corvane circled her slowly, almost gentle. "Mother Vess. You raised the boy. You know his name better than anyone living — not the name his mother gave him. The name we *wrote*. The debt-name. Speak it, complete the ninth pillar, and your order survives the century. Withhold it, and I will take it from the records anyway, and your order will be buried beneath the scandal of what it did to its orphans."

"You would finish the seal either way," Vess said. Her voice carried up through the chamber, steady as it had been over twenty years of evening prayers. Wren knew that voice. It had told him stories. It had lied to him with love, and he understood now that those were different things than lying with greed.

"I would," Corvane agreed.

"Then hear me instead." She turned — deliberately, unmistakably — and looked up toward the broken balustrade, directly at the shadows where three children of her temple hid. "Wren Kavanagh. Whatever you carry, however you came to carry it — you are not a debt. You never were. No child is a debt. Run, if running saves you. Stay, if staying is yours to choose. But do not let them teach you that you were ever *purchased*."

The chamber went silent except for the hum of the silver-leaved tree.

Corvane sighed, the sound of a man balancing a column that refuses to sum. "Take her below the gallery. If the boy watches, he'll come. If he comes—"

"He comes," said Sable softly, rising beside Wren, and there was a smile in her voice that had teeth in it. "So let's not disappoint him."

* * *

What followed, Wren would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a storm — by its damage rather than its shape.

Pip went first, as planned, a small whirlwind of dropped marbles and scattered oil from a stolen lamp, and the nearest enforcers went down swearing in the dark. Sable came over the balustrade like something the old carvings had dreamed — wolf-quick, silent, her knife opening the strap of the first man's sword belt before he understood he was being robbed rather than killed. She left them alive. Every one of them alive. That mattered to her, Wren knew; her brother had died carrying mercy for a god, and she meant to honor it.

And Wren — Wren did the thing he was made for.

He ran along the gallery to the scribes' desks and began, with steady hands, to read aloud.

"The account of the Guild of Impossible Things," his voice rang off the stone, trained by ten thousand hours of recitation in the counting-house, pitched now to fill the chamber the way an organ fills a nave, "entry one: purchased, one fragment of the sealed god's name, price paid in orphan-bonds, forty-one children, temple of Saint Verity—" He tore pages free and flung them down among the enforcers, and the pages fluttered like white birds. "Entry nine: purchased, silence of Mother Superior, ongoing. Entry twelve: the seal requires a living debtor's name as key. Entry twelve, gentlemen. You wrote it down. You write *everything* down."

The scribes froze. The enforcers wavered, looking at their masters. Because here was the truth Wren had learned in the counting-house and nowhere else: institutions are held together by arithmetic, and arithmetic can be read aloud.

"The war ended centuries ago," he called, descending the gallery stair two steps at a time, pages streaming from his fists. "You're not holding back a flood. You're keeping a dying thing from dying, because if it dies, the ledgers close, and you have no idea who you are without them!"

"Seize him," Corvane said, but his voice had lost its closing-ledger calm, and four enforcers looked at one another and did not move, because they had heard entry twelve read in a clear boy's voice and understood exactly what they were.

Vess had not waited. Bound as she was, she had rolled toward the ninth pillar, and when an enforcer moved to stop her she said, "Aldous. I taught you your letters." And the enforcer — Aldous, a debt-orphan too, Wren realized, they were all debt-orphans, the whole order was built on purchased children — Aldous stepped aside.

Corvane reached the ninth pillar first. His hand was on it. From his sleeve he drew a strip of vellum — Wren's ledger-page, his debt-name in the Guild's precise hand — and pressed it to the dark stone.

"No," said Wren, and threw himself across the space between them.

He was not fast enough. He knew it even as he ran, the way he knew a column would not sum. Corvane spoke the name aloud — *his* name, the false one, the purchased one — and the pillar drank the word and began to glow, and the eight pillars answered, and the white fire roared up into a wall, and the humming became a scream.

But the scream was not the god's.

It was the tree's. The silver leaves shivered violently, browning at the edges even as Wren watched, because a completed seal did not preserve the fragment — it *consumed* it, fed the last living piece of the god into the lock forever. The Guild's rite was not protection. It had never been protection. It was a slow execution stretched across nine hundred years, and tonight they meant to finish it.

The garden began to die. Grass greyed. Flowers curled. The little tree bent as if under snow.

And through the fire-wall, untouched by flame because the flame was not for touching, walked Mother Superior Vess.

She knelt at the edge of the dying garden, in the place where she had knelt for decades over evening prayers, and she took Wren's hand — he had fallen short of Corvane, and Corvane had retreated behind his enforcers, and none of that mattered now — and she pressed his palm open, and looked at the wax-sealed name he had carried since a stranger bled out in a scriptorium doorway.

"I chose my order once," she said quietly, only for him. "Twenty years ago, when they brought me a ledger and a baby and told me it was the same thing. I choose differently now. Break the wax, child. Speak what's inside. Not to bind. Not to free." Her eyes were wet and absolutely certain. "To say goodbye. Everything that lives deserves someone to say its name at the end. Even gods. *Especially* gods, if no one else will."

Behind them the fire roared. Enforcers shouted. Somewhere above, through the broken ceiling-crack, the thirteenth moon reached its crest and poured silver light down into the earth, straight onto the garden, and the last leaves of the last tree turned toward it like faces turning toward a window.

Wren broke the wax.

Inside, in ink faded to the color of dried blood, was the true name — and it was not terrible, as he had feared. It was small. It was *tired*. It sounded like the end of an evening prayer, like a mother calling a child home at dusk, like a wolf lying down at last in deep snow.

He opened his mouth.

And Sable landed beside him out of the smoke, bleeding from a new cut along her jaw, her hand closing over his shoulder, her brother's tooth gleaming at her throat.

"Together," she said. "He'd have wanted the pack to say it."

Above them, the moon crested. Below them, the god's fragment gathered itself like a breath drawn to speak.

And Wren spoke.
