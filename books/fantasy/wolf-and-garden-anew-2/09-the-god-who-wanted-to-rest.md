## 9. The God Who Wanted to Rest

The stairs beneath the temple went down farther than the temple had ever gone up.

Wren counted them out of habit — four hundred and eleven steps, each worn to a shallow bowl by feet that had come before his — and then stopped counting, because the number meant nothing down here. Numbers meant nothing in the dark under the world, where the air tasted of cold stone and old incense and something else beneath both, something like the moment before a storm breaks over a wheat field. The lantern in Vess's trembling hand threw their shadows long against the walls, and the walls were not walls anymore. They were script.

Names. Thousands of names, carved into the living rock in spirals that wound downward like water circling a drain, and every one of them was a debt. He knew the hand that had cut them. He knew it the way he knew his own — the Guild's master of accounts had a slant to his letters, a certain cruelty in how he crossed his *t*s, and Wren had spent six years balancing ledgers written in that hand. The god's prison was an account book. Of course it was. Everything in his life had been.

"Wren." Sable's voice came low behind him, rough as bark. She moved like something born to darkness, her grey eyes catching the lantern light and giving none of it back. Her hand found his shoulder — not gripping, just resting there, the way she'd learned to touch him these past weeks, carefully, as if he might startle. "How much farther?"

The map said the heart lay below the last spiral of names. The map, drawn in his brother-in-all-but-blood's blood, said *the seal drinks at the bottom*. But it was Vess who answered, and her voice was older than Wren had ever heard it, older than the woman who had fed him and taught him his letters and told him, every night for ten years, that he belonged.

"Below us is the counting floor," she said. "Where they weighed the fragments. Where they weighed *him*."

"Him," Sable repeated. Not a question. She had been saying *him* since the thirteenth moon began to rise, and each time she said it, Wren heard the grief underneath — the grief of a pack that had lost one more member than anyone knew. Her brother had died carrying the god's name to safety, and she had followed the scent of that death across half the country to a scribe who smelled of ink and fear.

"The god," Wren said quietly. "Not *it*."

"No," Vess agreed. "Never *it*. That was the first lie. The first entry in the ledger." Her lantern dipped. "We called it a thing so we would not have to call it what it was. A prisoner. A patient. A creature that has been dying for three thousand years because we would not let it finish."

* * *

Sable smelled the seal before she saw it.

It did not smell like magic, or holiness, or any of the words the temples used. It smelled like a den where something had lain too long. Like fur gone stale and breath gone shallow and the particular sourness of a body that has given up hope of moving but has not yet given up the small animal stubbornness of continuing to breathe. Her hackles rose. Her lips wanted to pull back from her teeth. Every wolf-thing in her blood said *predator, danger, keep clear* — and every one of those instincts was wrong, and she knew they were wrong, because the smell was also the smell of her brother's sickroom in the last week, when Fenrir had stopped pretending he would heal and started simply enduring.

They came out onto the counting floor, and the seal was there.

It hung in the center of a chamber like the inside of a bell: a sphere of pale light the size of a chapel, and within it — coiled, folded, pressed impossibly small, the way a wolf curls smaller and smaller when the pain gets bad enough — *him*. Sable could not have said what shape he truly was. She saw scales and she saw fur and she saw feathers and she saw, for one vertiginous instant, a face, ancient beyond reckoning, with eyes that had been open so long they had forgotten how to close.

He was looking at them. He had always been looking at them. That was the horror of the seal, she understood suddenly — not the binding but the *witnessing*. Three thousand years of watching through a wall of light while the little clever creatures outside built their guilds and wrote their debts and named their children with pieces of him.

"Oh," Wren breathed beside her. "Oh, you poor—"

"Do not finish that sentence."

The voice came from everywhere, from the carved names on the walls, from the light itself. Master Corvane stepped out of the shadowed archway opposite, and behind him came a dozen enforcers in grey, and behind *them*, filling the stairwell, more. He looked tired. That was the terrible thing — he looked like a man who had been awake for three thousand years himself, keeping the books balanced, and Sable understood with cold clarity that this creature had once believed exactly what Wren had almost believed in the counting-house: that the ledger was mercy. That a debt recorded honestly was better than a debt denied.

"Boy," Corvane said. His eyes found Wren and stayed there, gentle as a snare. "You've seen what the seal holds. You've read the old records — you know what walks the world if this fails. The war that burned two continents to glass. It does not end tonight. It only ends *him*, and everything after him burns instead."

"He's dying," Wren said. "Corvane, look at him. Look at him *properly*. He's been begging you for centuries."

"I have looked." For a moment — one flickering moment — the master of accounts looked as though he might weep. "I looked for forty years before I took this office. And I concluded that a slow captive is kinder than a fast grave for everyone who draws breath above us. Mercy is arithmetic, boy. Someone must do the sum."

"And someone," Sable growled, stepping forward, putting herself between Wren and the crossbows rising along the wall, "has to tell him no."

* * *

Wren had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road — the confrontation, the speech, the righteous fury. Now that it was here he found he had no fury left. Only the strange calm of a man doing sums he had finally, fully checked.

"You need my ledger-name," he said. "The final key. My bond was bought with a fragment of his name, which means the fragment answers to me. That's why you let me live all these years. That's why the debt-orphan system exists at all, isn't it? Not charity. Husbandry. You were growing keys."

"We were growing *stability*," Corvane said. "Give me your name, Wren. Give me the map. Walk away, and tomorrow you own nothing and owe nothing, and every orphan in that temple goes on breathing. Or refuse, and I will take it from your blood, and I will be gentler about it than the alternative deserves." Behind him, the enforcers' crossbows tracked Sable. He hadn't ordered them to fire. He was still hoping. That, too, was a kind of mercy, and Wren hated how much it cost him to see it.

But Wren had done the sum too.

Three thousand years of captivity. A being who had begged — the records said so, in Vess's careful transcription, in the oldest stratum of names — *let me rest, let me rest, let me rest*, until the Guild had stopped recording the begging because it disturbed the apprentices. An ancient war ended not by victory but by exhaustion, its last ember sealed away alive rather than allowed to gutter out. And above them all, a city full of children whose names had been bought and sold with pieces of a dying god, taught to be grateful for the privilege.

"Vess," Wren said. "Step back, please."

She didn't step back. She stepped *beside* him. "My rank," she said to Corvane, "was forfeit the hour I showed him the records. Do what you came to do. But know that I am choosing my ward, and I am choosing him *freely*, and that is the one thing your ledgers never learned to price."

Wren opened the wax-sealed scroll. The dead stranger's blood had browned the edges; the name inside shimmered like heat over a road. He did not speak it yet. First he looked up — past the crossbows, past Corvane, into the folded light — and he spoke to the prisoner directly, the way you'd speak to any patient too long in bed.

"I'm going to say your name," he said. "Not to bind you. Not to free you into a war that ended before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath. To let you *finish*. Everyone else in this room is afraid of what comes after you. I'm asking you — what comes after doesn't matter to you anymore, does it? You just want to stop hurting."

The light shuddered. And in the shudder, unmistakable, unmistakably exhausted, was *yes*.

* * *

Sable heard the name leave Wren's mouth and felt it in her teeth.

It was not a word, quite. It was the sound a river makes under ice, the sound her brother made in dreams, the sound of a thing too large for any throat that carried it. The chamber rang. The carved names on the walls began to glow, all of them, every debt and fragment and stolen syllable lighting up like embers stirred — and Corvane screamed *"Take him!"* and the world broke open into motion.

She moved before thought. That was what her blood was for. The bolt meant for Wren's heart — the one fired not by an enforcer but by Corvane himself, the master of accounts doing the final arithmetic with a hand-crossbow he'd kept hidden in his sleeve — she saw it the way she saw everything at the last moment, slow and bright, and she turned into it.

It took her high in the chest, below the collarbone, and spun her, and the pain arrived a heartbeat later like a fire lit in a wet house, hissing and spreading. She hit the stone on her side and rolled and came up snarling because that was what her kind did, even dying, especially dying — but Wren had not faltered. Blood ran from his nose, his ears; the name was too big for him and he was speaking it anyway, kneeling now, both hands pressed flat to the stone floor, shouting it into the roots of the world.

*"Rest."*

The seal cracked like a bell struck once.

Light poured out — not the pale imprisoned glow but gold, warm, the color of late sun through wheat, and it touched everything and harmed nothing. It passed through Sable's wound and the pain went quiet, not healed, just *set aside*, deferred politely like a bill that would come due later. It passed through Corvane, who stood frozen amid his scattered bolts with his arms half-raised, and whatever it touched in him made him sit down slowly on the counting floor of his ancestors and put his face in his hands. It passed through Vess, who was laughing and crying at once. And at the center of it, unfolding at last from three thousand years of folding, the god stretched — wings, coils, fur, feather, the whole impossible architecture of him — and looked down at the small bleeding creatures who had finally listened.

His mouth moved. No sound came. But every soul in the chamber heard it anyway, in the marrow:

*Thank you. Thank you. Thank—*

And then, gently, the way a lamp goes out at dawn, he was gone. Not destroyed. Finished. The light settled to the floor like snow and faded, and the silence afterward was the kindest sound Sable had ever heard.

She tried to stand. Her legs disagreed. Strong arms caught her — Wren's arms, ink-stained and shaking, lowering her down against his chest while Vess tore strips from her own sleeves to pack the wound.

"You idiot," Wren whispered into her hair. His tears fell hot on her forehead. "You absolute — you took a *bolt* —"

"Fenrir would've taken worse," she managed, and grinned, and coughed, and held on to him. Above them, far above, the thirteenth moon crested the mouth of the shaft, and its light fell all the way down to the empty place where a god had been, and touched nothing at all.

From the stairwell came footsteps — many, small, quick. The orphans, coming down with their copied pages clutched to their chests, drawn by the light. The smallest of them stopped at the edge of the counting floor, staring at the fading gold dust, at the weeping master of accounts, at Sable bleeding in the scribe's arms.

"Mister Wren?" the girl piped up. "Is it over?"

Wren looked at Sable. Sable looked at Wren.

"No," he said, and his voice was steady, and new. "Now the real work starts. Bring me the ledgers. All of them. We're going to burn every debt in this city — starting with ours."
