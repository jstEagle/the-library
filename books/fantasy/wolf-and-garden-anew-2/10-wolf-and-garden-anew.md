## 10. Wolf and Garden Anew

The morning after the god died, the city smelled of rain and ash, and Wren Kavanagh woke without a debt for the first time in his life.

He lay a long while in the narrow bed the orphans had made up for him in the temple's east dormitory — the same bed he had slept in as a boy of six, when Mother Superior Vess had first written his name into her own careful book. The ceiling above him was cracked plaster gone gold with age. Somewhere below, water was being carried. Someone was laughing — one of the small ones, Pip perhaps, who laughed at everything now, as though she had been saving it up through all the gray years.

Wren pressed his palm flat against his chest, where the ledger-name had lived like a splinter of cold iron since the night the seal shattered. It was gone. Not torn out — *finished*, the way a sentence is finished, the way a long illness ends not with violence but with quiet. The god had taken its name back when it took its death, and what remained in Wren was only himself: a scribe with ink-stained fingers and no master.

He got up, dressed in the plain temple wool that still fit him after all these years, and went down to see what the world had decided to do with itself.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things fell the way a rotten tree falls — slowly at first, then all at once, with a groan that could be heard across the whole of Corvane.

The ledgers went out before dawn on copies of copies. Sable had run them through the city herself, wolf-swift through the pre-dawn streets, pressing bundles into the hands of bakers and lamplighters and ferry-women, anyone who had ever been counted, catalogued, bound. Wren had copied each page four times in his smallest, truest hand: every orphan-debt, every fragment purchased, every name bought and sold and sealed beneath the temple floor. He had left nothing out, least of all his own name, written there in a child's unsteady letters twenty years ago beside a sum he could never have understood:

*One (1) fragment, divine, recovered from the war-remnant. Applied against the bond of Wren Kavanagh, debt-orphan. Consideration received in full.*

By noon, the counting-house steps were crowded. By evening, the Guild's master of accounts had been taken by the city magistrates, still protesting in the language of arithmetic — *the balance must hold, the balance must hold* — as though mercy were an error in a column that would never reconcile.

And on the third morning, the debt-bonds burned.

They burned them in the temple courtyard, in the great iron basin that had once held coals for warming ink in winter. The orphans brought their bonds one by one — thin sheets of vellum, each inscribed with a name and a price — and Vess stood at the basin's edge with her silver hair loose for the first time anyone could remember, stripped of rank, wearing the same undyed wool as everyone else, and looking lighter than Wren had ever seen her.

"You needn't watch them burn," she told the children gently. "You needn't do anything at all."

But they watched. All of them. They watched until the vellum curled and blackened and the names inside it rose as smoke, and then they watched the smoke rise until it thinned into the ordinary sky, and only when the last scrap was ash did they turn away — and it seemed to Wren that they turned away taller, the way trees grow when something heavy has finally been lifted off their roots.

Pip came to him afterward with a scrap of half-burned parchment she'd fished from the basin, holding it out in her small sooty hand.

"Mine didn't all burn," she said. "The corner where my name was. See?"

Wren looked. The corner had survived, a little triangle of vellum bearing three letters in faded brown ink: *PIP*. He crouched to her level.

"What do you want to do with it?"

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she tucked it into her pocket. "Keep it," she said. "So I remember I was worth something."

His throat closed. He pulled her against his side, and she let him, smearing ash on his sleeve, and over the top of her head he saw Sable watching from the courtyard wall with an expression he was learning to read — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile begins, like dawn behind hills.

* * *

They found the seal-chamber open to the sky.

When the god's name had spoken itself through Wren's mouth — when the great binding had come apart not in fury but in something terribly like relief — the stone above the chamber had softened and sunk, folding down through the temple's foundations like snow settling, until what remained was a bowl of earth thirty feet across, walled in old foundation stones, open to the weather. Rain had already pooled in it. Moss had already begun its patient work along the cracks where the sigils had been.

Wren stood at the rim on the fourth morning and felt nothing underneath him but dirt. That was the miracle of it, he thought. Not power. Not wonder. Just *dirt* — honest, empty, waiting earth, with no god in it and no war in it and no name buried in it but the names they might choose to plant there themselves.

"We should fill it in," said Vess behind him. She had come out with a basket on her arm and soil darkening her knuckles. "Or build over it. Most sensible."

"Most sensible," Wren agreed.

Neither of them moved.

It was Sable who came down into the bowl first, dropping lightly from wall to wall, landing in the mud with her bare feet and her brother's wolf-tooth pendant catching the light. She crouched, pressed her palm flat against the earth the way Wren had pressed his to his chest that first morning, and held it there a long moment.

"She liked gardens," Sable said at last. Her voice was rough; grief for her brother had worn channels in it, but this was older grief, quieter. "The god. Before the war broke her into pieces, she was a growing thing. That's why the fragments sang when you planted things near them. Nobody ever noticed because nobody ever planted anything." She looked up at them both, and her yellow eyes were steady. "I think she'd rather be a garden than a grave."

So they made it one.

* * *

It took a season. Of course it did; real things take seasons.

Vess knew soil — she had kept the temple's herb-beds alive through decades of neglect — and she taught them all, her voice as patient as it had ever been over lessons in letters. The orphans hauled fill-dirt bucket by bucket until the bowl became a bed. Pip organized the smaller ones into a work-gang that was mostly an excuse to fall in the mud. Wren drew plans, because drawing plans was the only prayer he knew, and amended them nightly when reality disagreed, which it always did.

Sable brought seeds from everywhere. She went out along the old roads she had taught him in the hunted weeks — the wolf-roads, the paths between the world's attentions — and came back with seed-pods from hedgerows, cuttings from graveyards, a paper twist of saffron corms traded from a woman in the salt-market for three true stories about her brother. She planted with a strange ceremony, tamping each seed down with two fingers as though sealing a letter.

"What are you writing down there?" Wren asked her once, watching her kneel in the turned earth.

"Names," she said. "Hers. His. Ours. A garden's just a ledger where everything you put in comes back."

He thought of the counting-house, the high dim windows, the abacus clicks like insect wings. He thought of a life measured to the copper. And he knelt down beside her in the dirt and planted a row of white crocuses with his own hands, and if some of what got into the ground with them was tears, well — the soil didn't itemize.

* * *

Spring came up through the seal-chamber like an answer.

First the crocuses, white as unmarked pages. Then the herbs Vess had nursed — sage and rue, feverfew and sweet cicely — and then, absurdly, gloriously, the apple pip that Pip had stolen from the kitchen and planted in defiance of all horticultural advice, which sprouted anyway, one green fist raised from the center of the bed where a god had died. Around the rim, morning-glories climbed the old foundation stones and closed over the last faint scars of the sigils, so that by midsummer you had to know exactly where to look to find any mark of the binding at all.

People began coming. At first just the freed orphans, then their neighbors, then strangers — a lamplighter with a sick mother, a ferry-woman with grief she couldn't set down anywhere else, an old soldier who had fought in the last skirmishes of the war that ended the night the seal broke, who sat by the apple sapling for a whole afternoon and wept without shame. They came because word traveled, the way it does, that there was a garden in the old temple district where nothing was owed. Where you could sit among growing things and no one would ask your name for a ledger, only for friendship.

They called it a temple eventually. Nobody decided it; it simply stopped being anything else. There was no altar, unless you counted the flat stone where Vess taught letters in the mornings. No rite, unless you counted the planting. But people bowed their heads when they entered, and spoke more quietly inside it, and left offerings of a practical kind — a bag of nails, a spade, a jar of honey — because that was the liturgy the place asked for.

On the eve of the year's turning, Wren climbed down into the garden alone.

The others were above — Sable on the wall with the moon rising in her eyes, Vess asleep early with her mending unfinished, the orphans a rustle of breathing in the dormitory. The thirteenth moon stood full over the garden, the same moon under whose crest the Guild had meant to complete the seal, and it laid silver over the crocuses and the young apple tree and the dark rich earth that had been, for nine hundred years, the loneliest prison in the world.

Wren sat on the flat teaching-stone and opened the book on his knees. It was a new ledger, bound in undyed leather — the first page of it blank, every page of it blank. He had bought it with money earned honestly, copying documents for the magistrates' new records office, and it was the only account he intended to keep for the rest of his life.

He dipped his pen. He wrote, in his smallest, truest hand:

*Ledger of the Garden. Entries freely given.*

Then, beneath it:

*Sable Fairweather — sister. Debt: none. Ever.*

*Mother Superior Vess — family, rank retired. Debt: forgiven, and she knows it.*

*Pip, Bramble, Odji, Nell, the small ones — ours. Debt: paid forward daily.*

He paused over the next line, listening. The wind moved through the apple leaves with a sound almost like breathing, almost like singing, very far away and entirely at peace. Somewhere in the roots, he liked to believe, the last ember of an ancient war was warm soil now, feeding green things, asking for nothing.

He wrote:

*Wren Kavanagh — belonging earned. Balance: joy.*

And because a good scribe always leaves room for what comes next, he ruled one final line at the bottom of the page and left it open — a blank entry waiting, the way the earth had waited — and looked up at the moon, and smiled, and did not owe the smiling to anyone at all.
