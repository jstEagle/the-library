## 1. The Quiet Trade

The bone told Xanthe Whitlock things the living never said aloud.

She had learned this at nine years old, kneeling in her mother's workshop with a splinter of lamb's rib in her palm, and she had spent twelve years since learning to forget it — or to pretend she had. Forgetting was a discipline, like carving. You did it every day, and every day you did it a little better, until the lie wore smooth as river stone.

The bone on her workbench this morning did not want to be forgotten.

It was a femur, old and yellowed, bought honest from the knacker's yard off Tanner's Row for three coppers — two more than she could spare. Cow bone, dense and pale as winter milk, and when Xanthe drew her first gouge across its surface she felt it hum against her knuckles the way a plucked string hums after the note has died. Something lived in it still. Something remembered.

*Don't,* she told herself, and carved anyway.

The commission was simple enough: a mourning charm for a widow named Grelda Moss, whose grandmother had died eleven days ago of the lung-rot that came up the river every wet season. The charm would be worn at the throat, small enough to cup in one hand, and it would bear the old woman's name and the sign of the Quiet Garden — not because anyone believed in the Garden anymore, but because grief liked familiar shapes. Grief reached for whatever was nearest.

Vellharrow reached for informants.

Xanthe paused, gouge poised, and listened to the street outside her shuttered window. A cart rattled past. Two men argued about the price of tallow. Somewhere above, a gull screamed at the harbor fog. No footsteps that lingered. No knock.

In Vellharrow, magic was outlawed — had been for twelve years, since the Purification Edicts came down from the Inquisition with wax seals and fire behind them. The penalties were written plainly on placards nailed to every church door: *Possession of enchanted articles, death. Practice of sorcerous arts, death. harboring practitioners, death.* But the Inquisitor's men paid for names in bread, and bread was the currency that mattered in the lower city, where half the tenements went hungry by midwinter. Xanthe had seen neighbors sell each other for a loaf. She had seen mothers sell daughters. She had learned to be no one, to carve only dead things into shapes too dull to excite suspicion, to keep her hands clean and her gift buried deeper than any grave.

Three coppers for the femur. Four silver for the finished charm, if Widow Moss could pay — and she'd said she could, which meant someone in the Moss line still had savings hidden somewhere, because nobody in Saltcross quarter had four silver lying loose.

Xanthe set the gouge to the bone and began.

The work took her out of herself, as it always did. The femur yielded under her tools like conversation — first the rough shaping, the saw-work that turned a leg-bone into an oval the size of a dove's egg; then the rasps, coarse to fine, smoothing the surface until it held light like a candle's inner glow; then the fine blades, the ones she kept wrapped in oiled cloth beneath a loose floorboard, the ones that would hang her if a Warden ever saw them. She cut the border first: a running vine of garden-rows, each leaf a small green thought rendered in cream-colored bone. Then the figure of the old woman, seated in a chair beside a hearth rendered in negative space, the fire itself left uncut so the light showed through. Then the name.

*MAERWEN MOSS.*

And that was where the bone spoke.

It happened like cold water rising through a floorboard. One moment Xanthe was alone in her head, counting strokes, planning the depth of the letters. The next, there was a presence leaning over her shoulder — not hostile, not even fully aware of her, the way a sleeper is not aware of the room. An old woman's voice, thin and papery, speaking words that had been spoken once, at the end, and were now worn into the marrow like a groove worn into a stair.

*"Is the kettle on? Grelda likes it hot. She always takes it too hot, burns her tongue, silly girl. Silly, dear girl."*

Xanthe's hands went still.

Twelve years of discipline said: *stop. Set down the tool. Let it pass.* The gift didn't ask permission. It came through the carving like water through chalk, and the only defense was not to carve — but she couldn't not carve. Carving was what she was. It was the only trade she had, and hunger was a creditor that never slept.

So she did what she always did: she let the voice run through her like a draft through a shut house, and she kept cutting.

*"Grelda,"* the dead woman murmured, closer now, as though the letters themselves were doors opening. *"My Grelda. Tell her — I meant to tell her — the tin box. Behind the third brick by the stove. There's coin in it, enough for — don't cry over me, child, I've been dying slow for years, this is just the last inch of it—"*

A breath that wasn't breath. A pause that stretched.

*"Tell her I loved her better than quiet. That was my whole life's mistake, loving quiet better. Tell her —"*

The voice frayed. Old bones held only fragments; the dead spoke in scraps, the way cloth wears to thread. Xanthe exhaled slowly through her nose and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist before she registered that they were wet.

"The tin box," she whispered to the empty workshop. "Third brick."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. She never did.

* * *

Widow Moss came at dusk, hooded against the drizzle, smelling of tallow and wet wool and the particular staleness of a house where someone had died recently and the air hadn't turned over yet. She was younger than Xanthe expected — thirty perhaps, with chapped hands and red-rimmed eyes and the look of someone who had been crying in shifts, allowing herself ten minutes at a time because grief was a luxury a working woman rationed like sugar.

"You're the carver." Her voice was flat with exhaustion. "Tam said you'd do honest work. He vouched."

"Tam vouches for everyone," Xanthe said. "That's his whole religion now."

Something flickered at the corner of the widow's mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "He said you'd say something like that."

Xanthe unwrapped the charm from its square of linen and laid it in the woman's palms.

He watched her face do what faces always did — the sharp intake, the softening, the way grief rearranged itself around beauty like water around a stone. The vine-border caught the lamplight. The hearth glowed through its uncut heart. And Maerwen Moss sat in her chair forever, patient as bone.

"Oh," Widow Moss breathed. "Oh, it's *her*. It's — how did you —"

"I asked Tam what she looked like. People remember faces wrong, so I carve the way they're described, not the way they were. It comes out truer somehow."

It was a good lie. She'd had twelve years to polish it. But the widow wasn't listening anymore; she was turning the charm over, thumbing the letters of the name, and tears slid down her face without ceremony, the way rain comes down a wall.

"She used to sit just like that," the widow said. "By the fire. Complaining the kettle was never hot enough. I'd boil it twice." She laughed, wet and cracked. "I boiled it twice this morning. Out of habit. Isn't that stupid?"

"No," Xanthe said quietly. "That's love with nowhere to go."

The widow looked up at her then, really looked, and for a moment Xanthe feared she'd said too much — been too warm, given away something in her voice. But the widow only nodded, pressed the charm to her lips, and counted four silver pieces onto the table with the reverence of a woman paying a ransom.

Then she did the thing that poor people did in Vellharrow, the thing that broke Xanthe's heart every time because it was so human and so doomed: she prayed.

Not to anything specific. Not to the Church of the Nine, whose god was sanctioned and silent, whose priests collected tithes and delivered nothing. The widow prayed the way her grandmother must have taught her, the old way, the outlawed way — eyes closed, thumb moving over the charm, lips shaping words to a god who had been dead for twelve years, killed along with all the others by edict and erasure.

"Garden-Mother," Widow Moss whispered, "if you can hear me anywhere — take care of her. Take care of my Maerwen. She was afraid of the dark at the end, and I wasn't there, I was at the wash-house, I wasn't *there*, so please — wherever she is — don't leave her alone in it—"

The lamp guttered.

Xanthe felt it before she understood it — a pulling sensation behind her sternum, like a hook set in her breastbone and drawn taut. The workshop went very still, the kind of stillness that isn't silence but attention. The drizzle against the window seemed to hang suspended, each drop a small bright bead refusing to fall.

On the table between them, the mourning charm began to glow.

Not lamplight-glow. Bone-white, cold, sourceless — light coming from inside the material itself, from the marrow, from the deep grain where twelve years of careful nothing had kept all the old voices locked away. Xanthe watched the white bloom spread across the carved surface like frost climbing glass, filling the vine-border, filling the hearth, filling the seated figure of Maerwen Moss until the whole charm shone like a chip of moon fallen to earth.

And through the white light, faint as a voice heard across a frozen river, Xanthe heard — not the dead woman's scrap-worn fragment, not the papery echo of last words —

— but an answer.

*"She is not alone. I have her."*

The voice was neither male nor female, neither young nor old. It was the sound of a garden growing, if gardens could speak — patient, green, immense. It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then the light died, all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the charm lay on the table ordinary and cream-colored, except—

Except.

Where the glow had been, a mark remained. Thin as a hairline crack, white as scar tissue, it traced a shape across the bone that Xanthe had never carved: a circle enclosing a single sprouting seed. It sat in the center of the charm, over Maerwen's carved heart, delicate and unmistakable.

The room resumed. Rain fell. The lamp burned. Widow Moss opened her eyes.

"Oh," she said, wonder-struck, clutching the charm to her chest. "Oh, I *felt* that. Did you feel that? Like — like someone putting a hand on your shoulder." She was laughing, crying, laughing again. "Twelve years I've prayed and gotten nothing but the ceiling. And now — the Garden-Mother answered. She *answered*, carver. Do you understand? My grandmother is not alone. Someone told me so. Someone *told* me—"

"Yes," Xanthe said. Her own voice sounded far away, underwater. "Yes. That's... fortunate."

The widow paid, thanked her, blessed her in the old words — words that could get them both hanged if any passing ear belonged to the wrong person — and hurried out into the drizzle with her impossible charm held against her heart like a second pulse.

Xanthe stood in her empty workshop for a long time.

Then she picked up the charm-money, put it down again, picked it up again, and finally crossed to the loose floorboard where her finest tools slept. She knelt. She lifted the board. And she stared down at the collection wrapped in oilcloth below — the small carved things she had made over twelve years and never sold, never shown, never let out of her sight. Charms and tokens, buttons and beads, a little bone bird, a little bone boat. Keepsakes of her mother's trade, her mother's crime, her mother's execution.

Her hands were shaking. They never shook.

Because here was the thing she could not explain to Widow Moss, could barely explain to herself: the answer had not come *to* the widow. It had come *through* the charm. Through the bone. Through the carving — through *her*, the same way the dead grandmother's last words had come through her that morning, the way every voice had come through her for as long as she could remember.

Something had passed through Xanthe Whitlock tonight on its way to a grieving woman. Something vast and green and patient had used her hands' work as a door.

And it had left a mark behind, the way travelers leave footprints in snow.

Xanthe closed the floorboard. She banked the lamp. She sat in the dark with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, listening to the rain, and she thought about the word *fortunate* and how badly she wanted it to be true.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then a dozen, all across the sleeping city, as though something had passed overhead that only animals could see.

Xanthe pulled her mother's old shawl around her shoulders and did not sleep at all.
