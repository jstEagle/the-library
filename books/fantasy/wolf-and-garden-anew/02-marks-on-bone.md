## 2. Marks on Bone

The mark did not fade.

Xanthe had told herself it would, the way frost faded from a window when the hearth was lit, the way a bruise went green and then gone. She set the widow's charm on her workbench in the last of the afternoon light and waited for the bone to forget what had passed through it. Bone never forgot — that was the whole of her trade, and the whole of her curse — but marks of power, she reasoned, were another thing entirely. Power moved on. Power did not linger in the work of a poor woman on Cobbler's Row who sold mourning charms for six coppers and lied about how she made them.

By dusk the mark still gleamed like a splinter of moonlight pressed into the rib-bone.

She turned it under her lamp. The shape was simple, almost crude: a single line that branched once, twice, like a river seen from very high up, or a vein, or the track of some small animal crossing snow. It sat just beneath the surface of the bone, the way marrow sits beneath the wall of a hollow shaft. When she tilted the charm toward the flame, the lines threw no shadow at all. They were not *on* the bone. They were *in* it, the way her carvings held voices — except this thing held no voice she could hear. Only a kind of pressure, like the silence after a bell.

"Grandmother Ilsa," Xanthe said quietly, because talking to herself had become a habit twelve years deep and she saw no reason to break it now. "What did they ask you?"

The dead woman's residue was thin in the charm by now, worn smooth by the handing-over, the carrying home, the clutching through a long night of grief. But Xanthe pressed her thumbnail into the groove she'd carved along the rib's inner curve — the listening-groove, she called it, though never aloud, never where anyone could hear — and let the old habit take her.

*Warm bread,* said a voice like wind through wheat stubble. *I asked for warm bread on the sill, the way she used to leave it for me when she was small. And there was bread. Warm. On the sill.*

Xanthe took her thumb off the bone so fast she nearly knocked the lamp over.

Prayers to dead gods got nothing. Everyone knew it; the Inquisition had spent two centuries making certain everyone knew it. You could pray all you liked to Veyran of the Thresholds or Sereth of the Hunt or any of the thirteen whose names were chiseled out of every lintel in Vellharrow, and you would get silence, and if you prayed too loudly you would get Wardens instead. That was the order of things. That was the *price* of things, paid in blood a lifetime before Xanthe was born, so that children like her could grow up merely odd instead of burned.

And yet a widow named Maret Fenwick had prayed to her grandmother's god — some hearth-spirit whose name even Xanthe didn't know, one of the small country powers that had never mattered enough to chisel out — and something had answered. Something had left bread on a windowsill in Saltcote Lane, warm as a baker's morning, and come here afterward and left its signature in her carving like a merchant signing a ledger.

Like a creditor marking a debt paid.

"No," Xanthe said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the little workshop, too loud against the rows of bones hanging in their oilcloth bundles. "No, no. Coincidences happen. Kind neighbors happen."

But she was already pulling on her coat, because there was a second charm — there was always a second charm, a third, a dozen, she sold them faster than she could carve them in winter — and if the mark meant anything at all, it would not be alone.

* * *

She found the first one in a pawnshop on Tannery Street, three days later.

Old Bessel ran the shop the way a spider runs a web: patiently, half-blindly, with an instinct for what had fallen into his reach. He bought mourning charms off grieving families more often than he should have — people sold everything in a hard winter, even their dead — and resold them at a markup that would have scandalized a bishop. Xanthe went in on the pretext of selling him a bundle of antler tines, which was true as far as it went, and drifted along his tray of secondhand charms with her heart going like a fist against a door.

There. A knucklebone charm, one of hers from two winters past, the little spiral she'd cut into the joint worn soft by someone else's hands. And beneath the spiral, faint but unmistakable in the gray light from the window: a branching line, like a river. Like a vein.

Her mouth had gone dry. "That one," she heard herself say. "The knucklebone. How much?"

Bessel squinted at it, then at her. "That's a Whitlock piece. Good bone-work, that." He named a price that proved it. She paid it without haggling, which worried him more than any argument would have, and walked out into the cold with the charm closed in her fist like a stolen coal.

In the alley behind the tannery, out of sight of the street, she opened her hand.

The mark was identical. Not similar — *identical*, the same branching line, the same pressure beneath the surface, the same absence of shadow. Two prayers, answered months apart, and the same signature left behind both times. She stood in the alley breathing steam and trying to do arithmetic with her fear.

Who else had bought her work? Where had the rest of it gone?

It took her four days to trace the third.

* * *

The third charm belonged to a lamplighter named Corvo Dain, and his prayer had been answered in a way that made Xanthe's hands shake for an hour afterward.

She'd tracked him through Bessel's ledger — the pawnbroker remembered buyers the way other men remembered insults — and found him at dusk on Chandler's Hill, trimming wicks along the high walkway where the city's lamps strung out below like a netful of caught stars. He was a big man, slow-moving, with a face like a dropped loaf, and when she showed him the knucklebone and admitted — in the language of craft, carefully, as one artisan to another — that she'd made it and was curious about its history, he looked at her for a long time before he spoke.

"You'll think I'm mad," he said.

"I carve bone for a living," Xanthe said. "Madness is a professional courtesy between us."

So he told her. His daughter — four years old, fevered, the physicians done with her, the priests unwilling to pray for anything louder than a whisper lest the wrong ears catch it — and him, alone on the walkway at midnight, holding the little bone charm he'd bought off Bessel because his wife had wanted *something* of the dead near the child, and praying. Not to the Vigilant Light, the church's sanctioned god of watchfulness and law. Not properly. Just — talking. Begging. Into the dark, with no name attached, because naming a forbidden god was a flogging and naming none at all was only madness.

And the lamps, he said. All the lamps along the high walkway had lit at once. Every wick, untrimmed, half-spent, guttering in a wind off the harbor — lit, all together, steady as noon, and stayed lit until dawn. And his daughter's fever broke before the bells rang matins.

"The priest said it was the medicine finally working," Corvo finished. He turned the charm over in his enormous fingers, and Xanthe watched him find the mark with his thumb, the way she had, the way anyone would — a slight ridge beneath the surface, a texture like a scar. "He didn't see this. Or he saw it and said nothing. Which frightens me worse."

"What does it feel like," Xanthe asked, "when you touch it?"

Corvo considered. "Warm," he said. "Not hot. Like a hand that's been holding yours a while and lets go." He looked down at her, and his face, so blunt and ordinary, held something that cracked her open a little: hope. Terrible, dangerous, punishable hope. "You made this. Did you mean for it to do that?"

"No," Xanthe said. It came out flat and fast, and she had to make herself soften it. "I make vessels. What fills them isn't mine."

*Isn't mine.* The words hung in the cold air between them, and Corvo nodded slowly, satisfied, while somewhere behind Xanthe's ribs a much older and colder thought settled into place:

If the filling wasn't hers — then whose was it? And why, of all the bone-carvers in Vellharrow, of all the charms and relics and saint's-fingers peddled in a hundred shops, why was the signature appearing only on *hers*?

She climbed down from Chandler's Hill with her collar up and her mind running like meltwater.

Three marks. Three answered prayers. A grandmother's bread, a child's fever, and — she went back through her memory of Bessel's ledger again, the third entry, sold to a washerwoman off Grate Street — a missing son, drowned in the harbor two winters back, whose body the sea had given up last week, intact, identifiable, *delivered*, washed into the shallows by Grate Stair on the exact morning his mother had finally stopped praying for anything but his bones to bury.

Deliveries. All three of them deliveries. As though something had gone through the city answering the prayers attached to her work the way a servant goes through a house collecting laundry — methodical, thorough, and utterly indifferent to whether anyone had asked it to.

And leaving its mark each time. Not hiding. *Signing.*

Things that hid feared the Wardens. Things that signed wanted to be found — or wanted someone specific to know they were there.

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She laid all three charms on her workbench in a row, lit every candle she owned, and interrogated them the only way she knew how.

The dead spoke through her carvings — that was the gift, or the affliction, depending on the hour. Residue, echo, whatever the church chose to call it in its edicts: the lives people lived soaked into their bones the way wine soaks into oak, and a carver with her particular talent could read the grain. Twelve years she had kept it small. Kept it quiet. Carved grief-charms and heard last words, gave the dying's messages to the living in careful, deniable pieces — *he wanted you to have the blue shawl, madam, it came to me in a dream* — and never, never carved anything strong enough to draw attention.

Now she pressed her thumbs into the grooves of all three charms at once, closed her eyes, and listened past the dead.

The grandmother's residue: warmth, wheat-stubble wind, love worn smooth as a river stone. The lamplighter's daughter's charm — no, wait, that one wasn't the girl's; the charm had belonged to no one before Corvo bought it, it was blank bone when it left her bench — and yet when she listened to it, she heard something. Not a voice. A *direction*. Like standing in a doorway with wind at your back.

And through all three, underneath, threaded through them like wire through wax: the mark itself. She reached for it the way she reached for the voices, expecting nothing —

— and the workshop went away.

For one heartbeat — no longer, she was sure of it, only a heartbeat — Xanthe stood somewhere vast and cold and open, under a sky she did not recognize, full of stars arranged in patterns no astronomer of Vellharrow had ever charted. The ground beneath her was not ground. It was *threshold*: she understood that instantly and completely, the way you understand falling. She was standing on the idea of a doorway stretched across the whole of everything, and on the far side of the doorway something enormous had paused — not approaching, not retreating, simply *paused*, the way a wolf pauses at the edge of firelight — and it was looking at her.

Not at the city. Not at the charms.

At *her.*

She came back to herself with her palms bleeding — she had clenched her fists around the charms' edges without noticing — and found herself standing, though she had been sitting, three feet from the bench, though she had not moved. The candles had all guttered at once and relit. Outside her shuttered window, the thirteenth bell of the night-watch rang, and somewhere down on Cobbler's Row a dog began to howl, and then a second dog, and then every dog in hearing, a rising chorus of animal alarm that went on far longer than dogs usually bother.

Xanthe wrapped her bleeding hands in cloth and sat down very slowly on her stool.

"Something has noticed me," she said to the empty workshop. Her voice steadied as she said it, the way a lie will sometimes steady you better than truth. Saying it made it real, and real things, however terrible, could at least be *worked* — she was a carver, after all; her whole life was imposing form on stubborn material. "Something old. Something that answers prayers and signs its work. And it is using my hands to reach this world, and it has never once asked my leave."

The marks glowed faintly on the bench, three branching rivers, three veins, three tracks across snow, all pointing — she saw it now, had maybe always seen it, and hated herself for seeing it — pointing the same direction. Toward her. Toward the stool where she sat. Toward the door.

Toward whatever came next.

She thought of Maret Fenwick's face when she'd delivered the charm, the raw gratitude of the grieving. She thought of Corvo Dain's daughter, alive and eating porridge that morning, according to the note he'd pressed into her hand. Whatever this was, it was *kind*. So far. Answering the small desperate prayers of small desperate people, asking nothing, taking nothing.

But she had grown up in Vellharrow, where informants were paid in bread and magic was a word you whispered only to regret it, and she had learned early that nothing in this city gave gifts. Everything collected. Debts, favors, secrets — everything was a hook waiting for weight.

And she was suddenly, terribly certain that the thing signing its name in her bone-work considered itself very much owed.

A knock came at her door. Three slow raps, deliberate, unhurried — the knock of someone who knows the door will be opened, or doesn't care either way.

Through the wood, in a voice she did not recognize and would spend the rest of her life remembering, something spoke her name.

*Xanthe Whitlock.*

Not her shop-name. Not the careful alias on her tax papers. Her *true* name — the one her mother had whispered over her cradle and taken to the grave, the one that existed nowhere written, nowhere spoken, nowhere but in the memory of a woman twelve years dead.

Outside, all the dogs had gone silent at once.

The knocking did not come again. It didn't need to. The door latch, on its own, with a small click that sounded terribly like a lock turning, began to lift.
