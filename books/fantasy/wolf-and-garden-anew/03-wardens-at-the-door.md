## 3. Wardens at the Door

The knock came at dawn, three blows spaced like a hangman counting.

Xanthe knew the rhythm before she knew the reason for it. Wardens knocked that way — unhurried, certain, as though the door were already theirs and they were merely informing it. She set down her knife, looked at the half-finished charm on her bench, a knuckle-bone rosary for a sailor's wife, and made herself breathe.

Through the wall she heard Tam stir in the room he rented below her, floorboards creaking his particular warning: *stay quiet, stay small*. Odile's shutter across the alley did not open, but Xanthe imagined her there behind it, one eye to the slats, hand already on the dried nettle she kept for hexes.

Three more blows.

She wiped bone dust from her hands, checked that every carving in the shop bore nothing but honest work — no marks, no white bloom, nothing strange — and opened the door.

Inquisitor Halvard Reeve filled her doorway the way winter fills a window. Grey coat, grey eyes, the iron collar of his office polished dull rather than bright, because Wardens who wanted to be seen coming wore shine, and Reeve had never in his life wanted that. Behind him stood two juniors in black, young enough that their belts still creaked when they shifted.

"Inquisitor." Her voice came out level. Twelve years of practice.

"Bone-carver Whitlock." He did not smile. He never smiled, but there was something in his face this morning that was worse than unfriendly. It was apologetic. "You'll forgive the hour."

"I'll forgive whatever I'm paid to forgive. Bread prices being what they are."

One of the juniors bristled. Reeve lifted two fingers without looking, and the boy went still. "May we come in?"

"Is that a question or a formality?"

"It's courtesy," Reeve said. "I'd like to keep some, while I still have it."

* * *

The shop was too small for four people and the silence between them. Xanthe put water on to heat because her hands needed something to do, and watched Reeve move through her workspace with the slow, terrible patience of a man reading a confession written in shavings and dust. He touched nothing. That was the worst part. Wardens who suspected nothing picked things up; wardens who suspected everything let the objects accuse themselves.

"You've been here twelve years," he said.

"Thirteen in spring."

"Twelve since you came from the north road with nothing but a kit of knives and your grandmother's bones in a satchel." He turned. His grey eyes were not unkind, which was its own kind of violence. "I remember."

So did she. She had been nine years old, kneeling in the mud of the north road beside her mother's body, holding a carved antler whorl that still whispered with her mother's last breath, when the Warden patrol found her. The law said children who showed the gift were taken for assessment, and assessment in Vellharrow meant a cell and questions until the gift drowned or the child did. Halvard Reeve had picked up the antler whorl, turned it once in his gloved hands, set it back in the mud, and told his sergeant the child was in shock, that the carving was ordinary grief-work, nothing more.

He had known. She had seen him know. And he had lied anyway, and neither of them had ever spoken of it.

"Water's hot," she said.

"Thank you, no." He sat anyway, uninvited, on her single stool, which somehow made it worse than if he'd loomed. "Tell me about your trade."

"You know my trade. You've read my license twice a year for twelve years."

"Humor me."

"Mourning charms. Keepsakes. Bone work for the dead so the living have something to hold." She poured herself tea she didn't want. "It's not magic, Inquisitor. It's carpentry with better material."

"No," Reeve agreed mildly. "What you do isn't magic. What happened Tuesday is another matter."

The tea scalded her tongue and she welcomed it. "Tuesday?"

"A widow on Tanner's Row. Maren Ossel — no relation to the Corvanes, before you ask. Her husband died in the kiln collapse. She bought a mourning charm from you last month." He produced it from his coat: the little rib-bone bird Xanthe had carved, wings folded, head tucked, and along its breast ran a line of white like frost fern, like a vein of moonlight under skin. "Tuesday night the widow prayed at her hearth to Vessa-of-the-Last-Warmth. A dead god, Whitlock. Dead sixty years, struck from every calendar. And Tuesday night her dead husband spoke to her. Told her where he'd hidden the rent money. Told her he was sorry about the quarrel in March."

The shop felt colder than the morning justified.

"That's a story widows tell themselves," Xanthe said. "Grief does strange—"

"The money was where he said it would be." Reeve set the charm on her bench between them, white mark up, like an accusation laid out for sentencing. "Under the third hearthstone. Forty crowns. The widow told the parish recorder because she thought it was a blessing. The recorder told the Inquisition because that's what recorders are paid bread to do."

The junior by the door shifted. Outside, a cart rattled past, and someone laughed, and the world went on being ordinary, which felt obscene.

"So you see my problem," Reeve said quietly. "A god that has been dead for sixty years answered a prayer. And the only physical evidence — the only thing in the whole city that isn't a grieving woman's word against sense — is this mark. On your carving."

* * *

Halvard Reeve had been a Warden for twenty-two years, and in twenty-two years he had learned that the truth almost always arrived wearing the clothes of something smaller.

The report on his desk called it *anomalous thaumaturgic residue, source unknown, priority moderate*. The report was wrong. He had held the widow's charm under a lamplight for an hour the night before, and what he had felt through his gloves was not residue. It was attention. Something vast had brushed this small object the way a whale brushes a minnow's wake, and the minnow was sitting on a bone-carver's bench in Vellharrow, drinking tea it didn't want.

He had known Xanthe Whitlock carried the gift since she was nine years old kneeling in road-mud. He had told himself for twelve years that he'd spared a frightened child, that the gift would gutter out unused the way most gifts did when starved. But he'd read her work. You could read a carver the way you read handwriting, and hers had always been a half-tone too true, her mourning birds too much like sleeping things, her keepsakes warm in the hand long after bone should have gone cold.

And now the gods — the dead ones, the sealed ones, the ones the Church of the Nine had spent three generations assuring everyone were gone — were waking up. Three answered prayers in eight days, all within the walls. Beggar at the Gate shrine praying to a nameless old god for rain for his crops and getting a storm that spared his field alone. A fevered child whose grandmother prayed at a bricked-up altar and woke at dawn cool and whole. And now the widow Ossel.

Each time: a prayer to a god officially extinct. Each time: an answer. Each time, somewhere nearby, a piece of bone-carved work bearing the white mark.

Reeve did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what men called patterns they were afraid to name.

He looked at the woman across the bench from him — thin, watchful, tea going cold in her hands, lying to him with her stillness — and felt the old debt sit on his shoulders like wet wool. She had been nine. She had been holding her mother's voice in a lump of antler. And he had been thirty, ambitious enough to know that sparing her would cost him, and human enough to pay it.

"What do you know about the mark?" he asked her now.

"Nothing."

"Whitlock."

"It appeared after I sold the piece. I don't inspect my stock after it leaves my shop, Inquisitor; I'm a carver, not a nursemaid." A flicker of temper, quickly banked. Good. Temper was honest. Stillness was not. "How many are there? Marks, I mean."

He weighed the question. Lying to her would be easy and useless; she'd smell it the way she smelled everything. "Four that we've confirmed. Yours is the fifth." He watched her hands. They didn't move, but the knuckles went pale around the cup, and that was answer enough. "There may be more. We're searching."

"Searching how?" Too fast. Too sharp. She heard herself and pulled the words back, but he'd already caught them.

"With house-to-house inquiries," he said. "With confiscation of marked objects. With detention of persons of interest." He let each term land separately. "Persons of interest, Whitlock, currently includes every bone-worker in Vellharrow. There are eleven licensed carvers in the city. Four of them are over seventy and can barely hold a knife. Two work exclusively in ivory imported under seal, untouched by local hands. Which leaves a short list. You're on it. So is a journeyman named Petch, who is, I am reliably informed, too stupid to be guilty of anything but bad proportions."

"And you?" she asked. "Where are you on the list of people who think I'm guilty?"

Reeve considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty even if she'd recognize a lie. "I don't think you did anything," he said. "I think something did something near you. Or through you. Those aren't the same crime, but the Inquisition doesn't distinguish, and that should frighten you more than I do."

* * *

The juniors waited outside. Reeve dismissed them to walk the Row, and stood a moment in Xanthe's doorway with his hat in his hands, and for one moment he was not an Inquisitor but a tired man of fifty-one who had spent two decades enforcing laws he no longer understood the reasons for.

"Twelve years ago I owed you," he said, low, not looking at her. "I've paid debts badly my whole life. I'd like to pay this one properly."

"By arresting me slowly?"

"By buying you time." Now he looked at her, and the apology in his face hardened into something with edges. "I can slow the inquiry. Misfile reports. Keep your name off the detention order that's already drafted — yes, it exists, don't look surprised, this is Vellharrow. But I can't make the investigation go away, because I'm not the only one on it anymore. Word has reached the Cathedral. There's a prelate arriving within the fortnight, and prelates don't misfile."

Xanthe's throat was dry. "What do you want for it?"

"The price is this." He spoke each word like setting a stone in a wall. "Whatever you learn — whatever the marks mean, whoever or whatever is answering those prayers, whether it touches you or your friends or your dreams — you bring it to me first. Before anyone else. Before you act on it. Not because I'll use it against you. Because if I don't know what's moving in this city, I can't protect you from the ones who will use it."

"And if what I learn incriminates me?"

"Then I'll arrest you myself," Reeve said, "and do it kindly, which in Vellharrow is the only mercy left in stock."

They stood in the doorway, two liars bound into an alliance neither wanted, and the morning light came grey off the rooftops like dishwater.

"Thirteenth of the month," he said. "The Bell and Anchor, after compline. Come alone. If you're followed, I'll know, and the arrangement ends, and the draft detention order comes off my desk and onto the prelate's, and God help you then — although lately," and here his voice did something she'd never heard it do, something cracked and wondering and afraid, "lately I'm not sure anyone is listening."

He put on his hat and walked down the street without looking back, and Xanthe closed her door, and leaned against it, and looked at the widow's charm lying on her bench with its vein of white light.

It had grown. This morning the frost-fern reached the wing joint. Last night, when Reeve's juniors had carried it here, it had stopped at the shoulder.

Something was still writing on her work. Slowly. Patiently. Like a hand practicing letters.

* * *

She lasted until noon before she took the charm to the cellar.

Tam's smuggling den beneath the chandler's shop was dark and smelled of tallow and pipe smoke, and Tam himself sat amid ledgers he could barely read, doing sums with his fingers and swearing at them in three languages. He defrocked priest was a generous term — defrocked implied ceremony, and Tam had simply walked out of the seminary with a stolen psalter and a grudge — but he knew scripture the way sailors know weather, and he knew the forbidden texts better than any man alive who wasn't burned for it.

He looked at the charm once, and stopped joking.

"That's a receiving-mark," he said. "Old liturgy calls it a *vestigium*. When the Nine were alive, every altar-stone in every temple had one cut into it. It meant the god had heard." He turned it in the lamplight, careful not to touch the white line with bare skin. "This isn't cut, though. This is grown. Like frost. Like—" He stopped. His face did something complicated.

"Like what?"

"Like a signature being practiced." He set it down gently, as though it might wake. "Xanthe. Four prayers answered in eight days, and the proof lands on *your* work. Every time. Out of everything carved in this city — and there's a hundred workshops, ivory, wood, silver, stone — the gods' receipts are appearing on bone that passed through your hands."

"I know how arithmetic works, Tam."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do, not yet." He leaned forward, pipe forgotten, and his voice dropped to the register he saved for things that scared him, which was a very short list consisting mostly of his ex-wife and deep water. "When I was in the seminary, before I got sensible, I read a text the Church suppressed so hard there are maybe three copies left in the world. It was called the *Ledger of Thresholds*, and it was about Veyran."

The name went through her like cold wire. She hadn't spoken it aloud. Hadn't dreamed it. Had she?

"God of doors and crossings," Tam said. "God of debts. The ninth of the Nine, the youngest, the one the others trusted to carry the keys. The Ledger says that when the gods were sealed, Veyran was the one who shut the door — and the Ledger says, and I quote, because I quoted it at my examiners right before they expelled me: *the Doorkeeper keeps a tally, and the tally is kept in living bone.*"

The lamplight flickered. Neither of them moved.

"In living bone," Xanthe repeated.

"In carvers," Tam said. "Anchors, the old rite called them. Mortal hands that the seal used as... hinges. Fulcrums. Points of contact. The text was fragmentary — pages missing, pages burned — but it named a family line. A lineage of anchors. It gave their surname." He looked at her, and she saw in his face that he had known for years and had kept it the way he kept everything, buried under jokes and debt-ledgers, and that keeping it had cost him.

"Tam. What surname."

He didn't say it. Instead he reached into his shirt and drew out the thing he always wore and never explained: a small flat pendant of antler, worn smooth, carved with a whorled pattern she had seen a thousand times and never once examined closely.

Her mother's work. Her mother's pattern. The same whorl that ran through every piece Xanthe had ever carved without knowing why, the way a heartbeat runs through everything a body does.

On its face, faint as breath on glass, a line of white had begun to bloom.

Outside, somewhere above them, a church bell began to ring — and kept ringing, wrong, arrhythmic, thirteen strokes where there should have been ten — and in the pause after the thirteenth, every dog in the district began to howl at once.

Tam closed his fist around the pendant. "That," he said hoarsely, "is probably the debt collector."
