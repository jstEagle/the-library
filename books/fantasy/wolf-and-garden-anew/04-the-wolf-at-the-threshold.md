## 4. The Wolf at the Threshold

The mark had not faded.

Xanthe had told herself it would, of course. Marks on bone were nothing — she worked with the material every day, and bone remembered everything that touched it: knife, blood, oil, breath. A stain was a stain. She had scrubbed the mourning charm with lye and salt water until her knuckles cracked, and when the white sigil still gleamed beneath her thumb like frost on a windowpane, she had wrapped the charm in black cloth and buried it in the ash-bin behind her shop, under a layer of tallow scraps and failed carvings that no one would ever sift through.

It didn't help. She could feel it down there, in the way you can feel a splinter through a glove. Faint. Patient. Like something on the other side of a door, waiting to be acknowledged.

Three days she had managed not to acknowledge it.

On the third night, unable to sleep, she lit her lamp and went back to work — because work was the only prayer Xanthe Whitlock had ever trusted. The shop was small and cold and smelled of marrow-dust and linseed, shelves crowded with half-finished pieces: rosary beads of lamb's rib, a crib mobile of songbird bones for a merchant's wife who didn't know what her money actually bought, a saint's finger for the chapel of Saint Verity that was, ironically, carved from pig. The good work, the true work, stayed hidden in the back room behind a false wall — the pieces that sang, the pieces where the dead leaned close to her hands and spoke through the grain like wind through a keyhole.

She took up a femur from her stock — deer, clean, bought honestly from a butcher who asked no questions — and set it in her vice. And that was when the lamp guttered.

Not went out. *Guttered.* The flame bent sideways, as if someone had opened a door somewhere in the room, though every shutter was barred and the night outside was still. The flame stretched long and thin and turned, very slightly, the color of old silver.

Xanthe's hands went still on the bone.

She had spent twelve years learning every small language of her gift: the hum of a skull that held a talkative ghost, the chill in her fingertips when death was recent and raw. What she felt now was none of those things. This was larger. Older. It came up through the floorboards, through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her feet, which — she realized with a distant, clinical horror — had begun to ache in sympathy, all two hundred and six of them humming like struck tuning forks.

The white marks on her buried charm burned cold in her awareness, four points of light in the dark of the ash-bin, and she understood, in the wordless way one understands a sound in the next room, that something had noticed her noticing.

"You might as well come in," she said to the empty shop, because her grandmother had taught her that fear spoken aloud is fear halved, and because if she was going to die in her own workshop she refused to do it crouched behind a vice like a rabbit. "The door's barred, but I imagine that won't trouble you."

"It doesn't," said a voice from inside the room.

*Inside.* Not from the street, not through the shutters. From the space between the lamp and the shelf, the air itself folding open the way a page folds open, and out of that fold stepped —

A wolf.

Not a dog grown large, not the gaunt city wolves that slunk along Vellharrow's canals eating refuse and dead rats. This creature stood as high as her workbench, grey as winter river-ice, its coat moving like smoke given the memory of fur. Its eyes were the color of lamplight seen through amber, and they did not reflect the flame so much as contain a private version of it. When it moved across her floorboards there was no sound at all — no click of claw, no shift of weight — as if the room declined to notice it, or as if it declined to press upon the world more than courtesy required.

Xanthe did not scream. Later she would take an odd, stubborn pride in that. What she did was worse: she reached, without thinking, for the awl on her bench, because twelve years of hiding had taught her that the hand wants a tool even when the mind knows tools are useless.

The wolf watched the gesture with what could only be described as polite interest.

"Awls are for bone," it said. Its voice was not a growl. That was somehow the most terrible part — the voice came out of its mouth shaped like a man's voice, low and dry and unhurried, the voice of someone settling accounts across a ledger. "You are thinking of your own, perhaps. Yours are loud tonight."

"My bones are my business." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Who are you?"

"I am called many things. Few of them accurately." The wolf sat, folding its haunches with the ease of a creature that had been sitting in this exact spot for centuries and had merely allowed the rest of the world to catch up. "In the old tongue they named me Veyran. Keeper of thresholds. Warden of debts. The god of doors, if doors had opinions — and they do, little carver. Every door in this city knows my name. They simply haven't been permitted to say it aloud."

Magic. Outlawed magic, standing in her shop, wearing the shape of the one animal no one in Vellharrow would be surprised to find at their throat. Xanthe's mind raced through the arithmetic of survival: informants paid in bread, Wardens paid in fear, the gallows on Cornmarket Square paid in whatever the Inquisition used instead of coin. If anyone saw this — if anyone *heard*—

"No one will hear," Veyran said, reading her thought off her face as easily as she read the dead off a relic. "I have closed this room. We are between the walls now, you and I. In the seam of the world. Nothing said here leaves here unless I carry it out myself."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It is supposed to be true. Comfort is your own affair."

She gripped the edge of the workbench. The wood bit into her palms and she was grateful for the pain, for its plainness, its mortal honesty. "Then say why you're here. Gods don't visit bone-carvers. Gods don't visit anyone. The gods are—" she caught herself, because the catechism of the Inquisition lived in her mouth like a fishhook— "*dead*. Twelve generations dead. That's the whole point of the law."

Something moved in the wolf's amber eyes — not anger. Something wearier and more complicated. Pity, maybe, or its older cousin, regret.

"Yes," Veyran said softly. "That has been the arrangement. And arrangements, Mistress Whitlock, are currently being renegotiated."

***

He told her then — or began to tell her, pacing the narrow confines of her shop like a tide pacing a seawall, sometimes wolf, sometimes only a suggestion of wolf, the lamplight passing through him when he forgot to hold his shape together — what the marks on her carvings meant.

"The prayers are being answered," he said. "The old gods. My kin. For twelve generations they have lain beyond the seal — sleeping, starving, dreaming of a world they were cast out of. And now, one by one, they are waking. And when they wake, they reach. And when they reach..." He inclined his great head toward the ash-bin, toward the buried charm. "They leave fingerprints."

"On *my* work."

"On the only work they *can* reach." The wolf stopped before her, close enough that she could smell him — not musk, but cold stone and rain on a threshold, the smell of a doorway at midnight. "Do you truly believe you never wondered? Twelve years of hearing the dead through the bones you carve, and you never once asked yourself why you? Why bone? Why the dead speak *to you*, girl, and through you, and around you, as though you were not a listener but a doorway?"

"Because I'm cursed," she said flatly. "Or gifted. The hedge-priests argue about which. It doesn't make me a—"

"A door." His voice dropped, and the lamplight bent toward him as if leaning in to listen. "Every answered prayer since the first moon of this turning has passed *through you*. You are the seam in the seal, Xanthe Whitlock. You are the hinge. The gods are waking, and they are waking *toward you*, and every white mark on every carving of yours is a knock upon that door."

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She thought of the widow Maren — no, not Maren; the widow Aldsey, three days ago, weeping over her charm while her dead grandmother's voice poured through the bone in Xanthe's hands. *Tell her the loom needs oiling. Tell her I kept the blue wool for her.* And then the prayer — the widow's cracked whisper to a god twelve generations dead — and the answering warmth, and the mark blooming on the charm like frost forming in fast motion.

"I didn't consent to any of it," Xanthe whispered.

"No," Veyran agreed. "You didn't."

"Then *stop it*. You're a god — you're the god of *doors*. Close the door."

For a long moment the wolf was silent, and in that silence Xanthe saw something she had not expected to see in the face of divinity: helplessness. Carefully leashed, expensively concealed, but there — the way grief is there in a debtor who smiles at his creditors.

"Some doors I keep," Veyran said at last. "This one keeps itself. Or rather—" and here his gaze settled on her with a weight that made her feel flayed, mapped, *known* down to the marrow— "it is kept by what you are, and I have not yet your permission to speak of what you are."

"What I *am*?" A laugh tore out of her, ragged and disbelieving. "I'm a bone-carver. I owe eight months' rent to a landlord who waters his ale and cheats his widows. I eat twice a day on a good week. Whatever I am, it fits in this room."

"Nothing that matters fits in a room," said Veyran. "Ask the sky."

"Stop that." Her voice cracked sharp as a snapped blade. "Stop talking in riddles and portents. If the gods are coming back — if they're using *me*, without asking, without paying, without so much as a by-your-leave — then tell me plainly what they want, or get out of my shop and haunt someone richer."

The wolf tilted his head. And Xanthe had the sudden, vertiginous sense that behind those amber eyes, something vast was reevaluating her — revising a ledger entry, adjusting a figure upward.

"They want what they always wanted," he said quietly. "To return. To be worshiped. To walk the world again in the fullness of their power. Some of them mean it kindly. Some of them mean it the way fire means it." His lips peeled back from teeth longer than her fingers. "And some of them, little carver, remember exactly whose children locked them out — and intend to collect."

"And you? What do *you* want?"

The question seemed to cost him. The lamplight guttered again; for an instant the wolf's outline blurred, and beneath it she glimpsed — or dreamed she glimpsed — something else. A shape seated on a throne of doors. A figure bound in chains of its own forging, each link a promise, each promise a debt.

"I want," Veyran said slowly, "what I have wanted since before your city had a name. But wanting is not mine to act on tonight. Tonight I am only a messenger, and messengers are paid poorly and shot often." He rose, unfolding upward until his shoulders nearly brushed her ceiling beams. "So hear the message, and weigh it, and hate me for it if you must:

"The seal is failing. Moon by moon, turn by turn, it thins — and when it fails entirely, my kin come home whether the world is ready or not. Whether *you* are ready or not. There is a term to this, Mistress Whitlock. Thirteen moons. Count them. Pray to whatever saint the Inquisition permits you, count them anyway."

"Why tell *me*? Why not the priests, the kings, the—"

"Because," said the wolf, and now his voice was very gentle, which frightened her more than any threat had, "when the last moon turns, there will be exactly one soul in all the world who can decide what happens next. One door. One hinge. One pair of hands." His amber eyes closed briefly, as if in deference — or apology. "They are knocking, Xanthe Whitlock. They have been knocking for twelve years. And you are the door."

***

When she blinked, she was alone.

The lamp burned steady and upright. The shutters were barred. The floorboards showed no prints, no scuff, no sign that anything heavier than dust had crossed them. Outside, Vellharrow murmured its nightly murmur — cart wheels, a drunk singing off-key, the watch-bell tolling the hour of the Owl.

But in her right palm, faint as a scar seen underwater, a new mark had risen. White. Intricate. A circle bisected by a single vertical line — a door, drawn by someone who understood doors the way she understood bone.

And from the ash-bin, muffled by cloth and cinders, the buried charm pulsed once against her awareness — warm now, where before it had been cold — like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Like a knock.

Xanthe stood in the middle of her shop for a long time, holding her marked hand against her sternum, counting moons against her will. One. Two. Three.

Thirteen.

Then she snuffed the lamp, took her cloak and her satchel of finished charms, and slipped out into the dark streets of Vellharrow — toward the one people in the city she trusted with a secret this size, and the only ones alive who might know what a man does when a god tells him he is furniture for the divine.

Tam would know the name Veyran. Tam knew every forbidden name there was.

The question — the question that followed her down every alley like footsteps — was what Tam would say when she told him the door had a name too.

And that the door was her.
