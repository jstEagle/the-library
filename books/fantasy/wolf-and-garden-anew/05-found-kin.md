## 5. Found Kin

Tam Voss had smuggled a great many things through the salt-gates of Vellharrow: brandy in false-bottomed fish barrels, heretical pamphlets sewn into the linings of pilgrims' coats, once — and he still shuddered at the memory — a live cockatrice for a merchant with more money than sense. But he had never smuggled anything quite like the way Xanthe Whitlock came through his back door at dusk, soaked to the knees from the drainage culvert, with a look on her face like a woman who had seen the bottom of the world.

"Evening," she said, wringing riverwater out of her braid onto his floorboards.

"That's my floor," Tam said. "And that's not an evening greeting, that's a woman who's been swimming through shit."

"Culvert," she corrected. "Mostly rainwater." She dropped a heavy canvas satchel on his table, and Odile looked up from her mortar and pestle with the particular stillness she reserved for trouble. Xanthe's eyes went around the room — the low rafters hung with drying herbs, the shrine-niche where a saint's icon had been turned face-to-the-wall years ago, the kettle Odile was already moving toward because trouble meant tea, always tea — and something in her shoulders came down half an inch.

"I need to stay here tonight," Xanthe said. "Maybe two nights. There are Wardens asking questions on Copperlane, and I'd rather not be home when they ask them of me."

Tam set down the crate he'd been prying open. "What kind of questions?"

"The dangerous kind." She hesitated, and he watched her weigh it, watch the truth sit behind her teeth like a coin she couldn't decide whether to spend. "The kind where the answers get people burned."

Odile crossed the room without a word and took Xanthe's cold hands between her own warm ones, pressing them, reading them like she read pulses. Whatever she found there made her mouth go thin. "She's frozen through," Odile announced, in the tone she used for verdicts. "Tam, fire. Xanthe, sit. You'll tell us everything after you've had soup, and don't argue, you're terrible at lying when you're hungry."

"I'm terrible at lying all the time," Xanthe said, but she sat.

"That," said Tam, striking flint to the hearth, "is the single most inconvenient trait of anyone I have ever loved."

* * *

The soup was barley and smoked eel, because eel was what the river gave and Tam had long ago stopped apologizing for the river. They ate by firelight while rain ticked against the shutters, and Xanthe told them as much as she could bring herself to tell, which was, Tam noted, both more and less than the whole of it.

More, in that she described the wolf in detail — its size, the grey like stormcloud over slate, the amber eyes, the voice that came out of it like smoke given grammar. Less, in that when she reached the part where it spoke, she went carefully around whatever it had actually said, the way a tongue goes around a cracked tooth.

"It knew my name," she said, staring into the fire. "Not my trade-name. Not Whitlock. The name underneath. And it called itself—" she paused, and Tam saw her decide, again, what to give away— "it called itself Veyran. God of thresholds and debts."

The spoon stopped halfway to Tam's mouth.

He was aware, distantly, of Odile saying his name, and of Xanthe looking at him, and of the fire popping, and of none of it mattering at all beside the cold that had opened up under his breastbone, because he knew that name. He had spent three years of his life — the best and worst years, the seminary years — copying out texts the Church had burned so thoroughly that only smuggled fragments survived, and one of those fragments had been a litany of names, and Veyran had been on it, and next to the name, in the old marginal hand of some terrified copyist, had been written four words:

*He keeps what is owed.*

"Tam?" Odile's hand on his arm. "You've gone the color of candle tallow."

"Where did you hear that name?" His voice came out lower than he intended. "Exactly. Word for word. What did it say?"

Xanthe set down her bowl. "It said the others are waking. It said I'm—" and here the crack in her composure showed, fine as a hairline in glaze— "it said I'm the door they're knocking on. It wouldn't say why. It wouldn't say how. It just sat there in my workshop like it had every right to be there, which I suppose gods do, and it told me the thirteenth moon mattered, and then it left through a door that wasn't a door."

"And it asked nothing of you? Made no demand?"

"Not yet." Her jaw tightened. "It said we'd speak again. It said—" her hands curled in her lap— "it thanked me. For listening. That's the part I keep snagging on. A god thanking me. Like I'd done it a courtesy instead of surviving an ambush."

Tam got up. He didn't want to get up; his body wanted very much to stay by the fire and never think about this again. But there was a loose floorboard behind the grain chest, and beneath it an oilcloth bundle, and beneath the oilcloth the reason he was a smuggler instead of a priest, wrapped in sheepskin against damp: six books the Inquisition would pay a house's ransom to burn.

He brought back the thinnest one — a fragmentary psalter, its pages soft as cloth with age, its margins crowded with the annotations of men who had died badly. He laid it on the table between the soup bowls.

"This was copied before the Sealing," he said. "Or parts of it were. The Church calls the whole corpus *The Liar's Liturgy*, which tells you how scared they are of it. I transcribed this myself off a man in Greyharbor who was executed the following spring." He turned pages until he found what his fingers already remembered. "Here. The catalogue of names. This column is the gods of the threshold — doorways, crossroads, bargains, debts, all the in-between places. And this one—"

He turned the book toward her. The ink had gone brown, but the name stood clear enough, in the careful hand of a dead man:

*Veyran, who weighs the gate.*

"There's a gloss," Tam said. "See the margin note? Different hand, later, more frightened. Read it aloud. Names want to be spoken; it's the whole reason the Church burns books."

Xanthe bent close. "'He is the kindest of them,'" she read slowly, "'and therefore the most to be feared, for kindness is the shape his hunger wears.'"

The fire popped. Rain filled the silence.

"Well," said Odile at last, "that's cheerful."

"It gets better." Tam took the book back gently, the way you take back a knife. "There's a longer passage further in — a fragment of the old covenant-laws. Do you know what the word *debt* meant, before the Church repurposed it to mean sin? It didn't mean owing money. It meant owing *self*. A debt in the old law was a claim on your person — your labor, your years, your blood, your children's blood if the debt outlived you. And the threshold-gods held most of the paper." He met Xanthe's eyes. "Veyran doesn't lie. By every account, he can't — it's the one mercy in him. But he also doesn't volunteer. He answers exactly the question asked, and lets your own hope do the rest of the damage. Every text agrees on that. Kindness, patience, absolute honesty, and a ledger that never closes."

"He said he wouldn't lie to me," Xanthe said quietly.

"Of course he did. It's true." Tam closed the book. "That's what makes him dangerous. Sereth would hunt you and you could run from Sereth. The war-god would break you and you could hate the war-god. Veyran will help you, warn you, protect the people you love, tell you the truth in answer to any question you ask — and every hour of it will be accruing interest. Gods collect their debts in blood, Xanthe. Always. Not out of cruelty. Out of *nature*. You don't blame the tide for coming in."

"You sound like you've met one," she said.

"I've met their paperwork." He tried to make it a joke and heard it fail. "Three years in the seminary archives, before they defrocked me for asking what the burned books said. Everything I know about the old gods, I know from the ashes' shadows — what survived by accident, what heretics hid in walls. And every fragment, every scrap, every dying confession I've ever smuggled past a salt-gate says the same thing: the gods of the old pantheon were not monsters, mostly. That's the horror of it. They were *lords*. They expected tribute the way lords expect rent, and when humanity stopped paying—" he spread his hands— "well. You know how the Sealing is taught. The righteous Church casting down the demons."

"That's not what happened," Xanthe said. It wasn't quite a question.

"No. I don't think it is. I think it was a war, and wars have two sides, and the side that wrote the histories was the side that could still hold a pen afterward." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let himself say the thing he'd said to almost no one in fifteen years. "But here's what frightens me tonight, and I'll thank you not to repeat it within earshot of any wall with ears. If the seal is failing — if prayers are being answered again, if the old names are stirring — then either the seal was weaker than they believed, or someone has been working at it for a very long time. And either way, the thing knocking on your door isn't asking to come in, Xanthe. Things that knock are being polite. The question is what happens when it stops being polite."

* * *

Later, when the dishes were washed and Odile had dosed Xanthe with willow-bark and valerian despite her protests that she'd never sleep, Tam stood in the kitchen doorway and watched his oldest friend pretend to sleep on his good pallet anyway.

"She's going to say yes to it," Odile murmured, coming to stand beside him. Her shoulder fit under his arm the way it had for eleven years, since the fever winter when she'd patched his ribs and never quite left. "Whatever it offers. Whatever it asks. Have you seen her face?"

"I've seen her face."

"She spent twelve years carving alone in that drafty little shop, taking bread-payment and saying thank you to customers who shorted her. Twelve years being small on purpose." Odile's voice was soft but unsparing, the way truth usually sounded when she delivered it. "And now something vast has finally noticed her, and it told her she matters, and you want to know what terrifies me? Not the god. The god I can believe in; I patch wounds the gods ignore every day. What terrifies me is that she's been starving so long that attention looks like food."

Tam said nothing for a while. Down in the street a Warden's patrol passed, boots ringing on wet cobbles, lantern-light sliding yellow across the shutter seams, and the three of them stayed silent out of habit older than thought until the light moved on.

"We get her out of the city," he said finally. "South road, then east through the fen causeway — the Wardens don't ride the causeway since the bridge tolls went to the wrong cousin. We put distance between her and that workshop, and we buy time to think."

"Time to think about what?"

"About whether there's anywhere in this world a god can't follow." He rubbed his eyes. "I know the answer. There isn't. Thresholds are everywhere. Doors, gates, crossroads, dawn, dusk, the moment between asleep and waking. He's the god of *between*, Odile. Running from him is like running from Tuesday."

"Then why run at all?"

"Because she needs to see that running doesn't work before she stops doing it." He sighed. "Because she deserves one honest choice, made unhurried, and she won't get it here. And because—" he glanced at the pallet, at the shape of Xanthe breathing slow and deliberate under the wool blanket— "because whatever's coming for this city, I want her family around her when it arrives. Family's the only currency I've ever smuggled that was worth the risk."

Odile squeezed his waist, once, and went to bank the fire.

Tam sat up late with the psalter, reading the frightened margin-notes by candle-stub, and somewhere past midnight his eye caught a line he had copied a decade ago and never understood, a fragment of the old covenant-laws so worn that half its words were lost:

*...and the last of the Anchors shall be hidden among the carvers of bone, and the gate shall wear her face, and when the thirteenth moon—*

The rest was eaten by water-damage. It always had been.

He read it four times. Then he looked across the dark room at the sleeping woman who carved bone for bread and heard the dead, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath everything he believed about coincidence.

In the morning, he decided, they would talk about the east road.

In the morning, though, there came a knocking — three measured raps at the front door, patient as falling snow, at an hour when no honest soul was awake — and through the wood, mild as milk, a voice Tam had only ever read in dead men's handwriting said:

"Forgive the hour. I find I am owed a conversation."
