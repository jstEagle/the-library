## 6. What the Dead Remember

The bone market of Vellharrow kept its dead in a cellar beneath the tannery row, where the air was thick with lime and old salt and the particular silence of things that had been someone once. Xanthe had been coming here since she was nineteen, when Tam first showed her the loose grate behind the chandler's shop and told her that the tanner who owned the place was too drunk to count his stock.

She came at night now, always. She carried a lantern with a shuttered flame and a satchel lined with wool, and she moved between the shelves of catalogued bones like a woman moving through a library, reading spines.

The dead did not speak to everyone. They did not even speak to most carvers — bone held memory the way cloth held scent, faintly, for those patient enough to breathe it in. But Xanthe's hands were different. When her blade touched bone, the memory rose like heat off summer stone, and if she listened — truly listened, letting her own pulse slow until it matched the long stillness of the thing in her grip — she could hear the last hours of a life played out in whispers. Not words, exactly. More like the shape of words, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot.

Tonight she needed old bones. Older than the city. Older than the church that outlawed what she did.

She had told herself it was curiosity. She had almost convinced herself.

Three days since the wolf had stood in her workshop with eyes like winter stars and called her *the door they are knocking on*. Three days of not sleeping, of carving nothing, of watching Odile bar the door with charms that Xanthe knew were half superstition and half theater, because what barred a door against a god? Three days of the white marks on four of her charms gleaming in her memory every time she closed her eyes, each one a prayer answered through her without her leave, each one proof that something vast had put its weight against her life as casually as a traveler leaning on a gate.

*You are the door.* But doors were made. Doors had makers. And doors opened onto rooms, and rooms belonged to houses, and houses had histories.

If she wanted to know why the gods had chosen her — or worse, why they had *needed* her — she would have to ask the only witnesses who had been present at the beginning.

The dead remembered everything. That was the trouble with them.

* * *

She found what she wanted on the lowest shelf, in a crate marked in faded ink: *PRE-FOUNDATION, PROVENANCE UNKNOWN*. The bones inside were brown as tea-stained linen, dense and dark, cured by centuries rather than chemistry. A femur. Two vertebrae fused by calcium into a single knuckle-like mass. Part of a jaw, teeth worn nearly flat.

Someone had lived a very long time ago and died badly. She could feel it before she touched them — a wrongness in the air above the crate, like the hush before lightning.

Xanthe glanced toward the cellar stairs. The tanner's snores came through the floorboards, rhythmic and reassuring. She set the lantern on an upturned barrel, unrolled her leather tool-wrap on the floor beside her knees, and chose the smallest knife she owned: a paring blade with a crescent edge, good for fine work, good for listening.

Then she took the femur in her left hand and laid the blade against it, and stopped her heart's hurry, and listened.

The dead spoke all at once, the way they always did — no chronology, no courtesy. A flood of sensation: cold. The taste of ash. Running. Running so long that running became the whole of existence, legs and lungs and terror, and over it all a light in the sky that should not have been there, a second sun that burned without warmth and moved with intent, sweeping the horizon like a lamp held over a floor to find —

— to find *them* —

Xanthe gasped and pulled back. Her hand shook. The femur hummed against her palm, faint but unmistakable, like a struck tuning fork.

"Easy," she whispered to it, to herself. "Slowly. One hour. Show me one hour."

She breathed. Four counts in, four out, the way Odile had taught her for pain. She let her mind go narrow and quiet, a corridor with one open door at the end, and she stepped back into the bone.

This time she steered. This was the skill no one had taught her, the gift she'd hidden for twelve years — she could not make the dead speak, but she could choose which hour they showed her. She pushed past the terror of the running, deeper along the thread of memory, back toward its root, the way one wades upstream through a river of drowning people.

And there — near the oldest layer, where memory grew thick and strange as amber — she found a garden.

Not a garden of flowers. A garden of *antlers*. Trees whose branches were living horn, white and branching, hung with moss that glowed a soft blue-green. Rivers that ran uphill, laughing. And in the garden, beings.

They were hard to look at even in memory. Shapes that suggested wolves and stags and women and storms without settling on any of them, vast presences that walked through the antler-trees without bending them, that drank from the rivers and wept into them and sang songs that rearranged the stones. Gods. The old gods, alive, unsealed, walking openly in a world that had room enough for them.

And among them — this was the part that made Xanthe's breath go thin — *people*. Mortal men and women walking barefoot through the garden of antlers, unafraid, sitting at the gods' feet the way children sit at a fire. Being taught. Being loved. Being *counted*, the way a shepherd counts sheep, and there it was, the first hairline crack of wrongness in the beautiful memory: the counting. The gods counted their worshippers nightly, and any soul missing from the count drew their attention the way a missing tooth draws a tongue.

A voice, vast and warm and terrible, speaking over the heads of the kneeling mortals:

***"Belief is your breath. You would not withhold breath. Why do you withhold yourselves?"***

The memory lurched forward. Years, decades, compressed into sickening lurches. The garden thinning. The rivers running backward now, or drying. Mortals building walls — not against beasts, but against the sky. And always the counting, the counting, the gods' voices growing less warm and more hungry: ***"We made you. We shaped your marrow. Your love is our food, and you have grown stingy."***

Then war.

Xanthe had heard about the Godswar the way everyone in Vellharrow had — fragments, officially sanctioned ones. The church taught that sorcerers of the old age had bound the benevolent heavens out of envy, that magic was stolen divinity, that the Inquisition existed to keep thieves from stealing more. It was a clean story. It fit on a coin.

The dead told it differently.

She saw battlefields where the grass had grown white as bone from the blood in the soil — she understood, with a lurch, that the white marks on her charms were *this color*, the color of ground saturated with divine violence. She saw a god of harvests walk into a city that had stopped praying to him and stand in the marketplace, patiently, while everyone within sight of him starved — not cursed, just *forgotten how to grow*. She saw mortals fight back with weapons forged from their own surrendered years, burning lifespans like pitch. She saw children on both sides. She saw so many children.

And then she saw the seal being made, and everything she thought she knew turned over like a plowed field.

It was not sorcerers. It was not an army. It was a circle of *mothers*.

Mortal women — some old, some barely grown — standing in a ring around a stone that had fallen burning from the sky, singing a working older than any of them, taught to them by a defector. A god who had refused the counting. A wolf-shaped god who had gone among mortals and said, quietly, *you should not be required to feed us; I will show you how to close the door.*

Veyran.

Xanthe's hands went still on the bone. The lantern flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

The mothers' song was a bargain, not a binding. That was what the dead remembered, what the histories had burned away: the seal required consent. Each mother in the ring gave up something irreplaceable — a name, a face, a child's future memory of her — and in exchange, the stone took their willingness and wove it into a lock. The gods would sleep beyond the world until the lock was re-forged or willingly released. No god could break it from outside. No mortal could break it from within.

But the seal needed an anchor in the living world. A hinge. Something mortal enough to exist inside time, divine enough to touch the lock. The mothers had bred for it — no, not bred, that word was too clean — they had *made arrangements*, generations of quiet arrangements, families who passed down a duty none of their neighbors could ever know, until the line thinned and thinned across twelve centuries of hiding.

Until it came down to a bone-carver in Vellharrow who heard the dead.

*Xanthe Whitlock. Anchor. Hinge. Door.*

She pulled her hands away from the femur and sat back on her heels in the lime-smelling dark, and found that her cheeks were wet.

Because the story was not the church's story, and it was not the wolf's story either, quite. The truth was crueler and kinder than both: the seal had been an act of *mercy*. Desperate, bleeding mercy, paid for by women who gave up their names so their grandchildren wouldn't have to kneel. The gods hadn't been imprisoned by villains. They had been closed out by their own children, gently, the way you close a door on a beloved father who has begun to hit.

And someone was opening that door again. Unmaking it. Turn by turn, moon by moon, the lock was failing — and the mothers' bargain was being unwound by hands she couldn't see.

Unless.

A colder thought slid in, smooth as a blade going into water. *No god can break it from outside. No mortal can break it from within.* Then how? How were prayers getting answered? How were marks appearing on her work unless something was already reaching through?

Reaching through the hinge.

Through her.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked in the empty cellar. "I never consented. I never—" 

But the seal didn't need her consent, did it? It needed only her *existence*. She was the mechanism. If the lock was weakening, it might be weakening *through her*, the way a rusted bolt fails through itself. Every answered prayer, every white mark — not messages sent to her. Messages passing *through* her, wearing her thin, the way water wears stone.

Or the way a key wears a lock before it turns.

She wrapped the bones carefully, returned them to their crate, and shuttered her lantern, and stood a long moment in the dark listening to the tanner snore overhead and the dead murmur their endless hours below the floor.

* * *

Tam was awake when she got home — of course he was; he slept like a cat, in shallow laps, one ear always open. He took one look at her face in the doorway and put down the ledger he'd been falsifying and said nothing at all, which was his particular genius, and poured her a cup of the bitter chicory he pretended was coffee.

Odile appeared in her nightrobe, hair braided for bed, took one look too, and began heating water for tea without asking questions, which was hers.

Xanthe sat at their scarred table between them, holding the cup in both hands, and told them everything. The garden of antlers. The counting. The mothers and their song. The hinge. The word Maren Corvane would have used, though she didn't know yet that anyone else knew it: *Anchor*.

When she finished, the stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a Warden patrol rattled past, boots and iron, and all three of them went silent until it faded.

"The seal was mercy," Xanthe said finally. "That's the worst part. They weren't prison-builders. They were daughters closing a door." She stared into the chicory. "And I'm the lock. Or the key. I don't even know which, and either way I didn't agree to it."

Tam turned his cup slowly on the table. He had a smuggler's face, all weathered patience, and when he finally spoke his voice was gentle in the way he saved for bad news.

"There's another thing in the old texts, Xan. The ones I moved for the collector, the forbidden ones." He hesitated. "The seal has failed before. Twice. Small failures, localized — a village here, a valley there, a generation of miracles followed by a purge. Both times, the records say the same thing. The anchor of that era died. Suddenly. And the seal re-knit itself around... whatever was closest."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the lock doesn't fail on its own." His eyes met hers, steady and unhappy. "Something opens it. From the hinge side. And in both old accounts, there's a mention — just a phrase, a scrap — of a *third party*. Neither god nor mother. Something that wants the door open and has been picking at the lock for a very long time."

The stove ticked. Odile's hand found Xanthe's shoulder and stayed there, warm and grounding.

"So I'm not just the lock," Xanthe said quietly. "I'm the lock someone's already picking. And every mark on my work, every answered prayer — that's not the gods waking." She looked up at them, and the grief in her chest curdled into something harder, colder. "That's the sound of tumblers falling."

Above the table, hanging from its nail, her satchel sat where she'd dropped it. Inside, wrapped in wool, was the mourning charm she'd taken from her workshop shelf — the widow's charm, the first marked one. As they watched, in the dim kitchen light, a fifth white mark bloomed across its surface like frost forming on glass.

Fresh. New. Somewhere in Vellharrow, someone was praying, and something was answering, and the answer was passing through Xanthe Whitlock whether she willed it or not.

And beneath the mark, faint as watermark, something new had appeared — not frost this time, but a shape pressed into the bone from the other side. Small. Deliberate.

A paw print.
