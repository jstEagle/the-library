## 7. The Thirteenth Moon

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and Halvard Reeve still could not pray.

He knelt on the cold flagstones of the Warden chapel in the Vellharrow garrison, as he had every night for twenty-three years, hands folded over the small iron icon of the Unnamed — the church's answer to gods it refused to name. The ritual was muscle now, worn smooth as a stair: kneel, fold, recite. *Unnamed One, who is no god and therefore cannot fail us, keep this city from the old dark. Keep my hands clean. Keep my soul accounted for.*

He said the words.

Nothing answered. Nothing ever had. That was the doctrine's whole point — the Unnamed did not answer, because answering was what *gods* did, and gods were the disease the Inquisition existed to burn out of the world.

But lately, other people's prayers were being answered all over Vellharrow, and Reeve could not stop hearing about them.

* * *

It began, as most ruinous things in Vellharrow did, with a beggar.

Reeve had been walking the Saltmarket before dawn — a habit his superiors called diligence and he privately admitted was insomnia — when he passed the old woman who sold boiled nettles from a cart near the fish stalls. He knew her by file: Marget Sowle, sixty-one years, three citations for vagrancy, no family. She was kneeling beside her overturned cart that morning, weeping, and her hands — Reeve stopped. Her hands were pressed together in the shape of prayer, an act punishable by fine, and she didn't care, because the cart stood upright again. Not righted. *Restored.* The axle that had cracked two days past, the wheel he'd seen wobble for a season, both whole. New wood gleaming where rot had been.

"Who mended it?" he asked, because asking questions was the only prayer he trusted anymore.

She looked up at him with wet eyes and no fear at all, which unsettled him more than anything else that day. "I asked," she said simply. "First time in forty years I asked anyone for anything. And someone said yes."

"To which god?"

"I don't know." A laugh broke out of her, cracked and wondering. "Don't care. Someone said *yes*, Warden."

He should have cited her. Should have taken her in for questioning. Instead he gave her a coin for the nettles and walked away feeling like a man who has looked down a well and heard his own voice come back changed.

The reports multiplied through the month. A cooper's boy cured of the white lung. A fire in Tanner's Row that guttered out when a crowd prayed aloud — a fire the Wardens' own investigators confirmed had burned *against the wind*. A debt collector struck blind for an afternoon after cursing a widow's petition. Each report crossed Reeve's desk because he had quietly assigned himself to the docket, and each one he filed under *anomalous phenomena pending doctrinal review*, a category that had contained nothing for eleven years and now grew fat as a harvest mouse.

And underneath the reports, like a stone in his boot, walked the same thought, growing heavier with every mile:

*Marget Sowle asked and received. I have asked every night for twenty-three years, and the Unnamed One — who does not exist, who is not supposed to exist, whose silence is the entire architecture of my faith —*

The stone said: *why them?*

He hated the question. It was the question of a child. It was, worse, the question of a *believer*, and Halvard Reeve had buried that man so deep even he sometimes forgot the grave was occupied.

* * *

Inquisitor Halvard Reeve was not a cruel man, whatever the city whispered. Cruelty required appetite, and appetite was inefficient. What he was, was thorough. Thoroughness had carried him from a workhouse in the Tallow District to the white sash of the Inquisitor's office at thirty-four, the youngest appointment in Vellharrow's history. Thoroughness meant he read every file twice, interviewed every witness three times, and never, ever forgot a face.

Which was why the bone-carver troubled him.

Xanthe Whitlock. Age, by his estimation, twenty-eight or nine. Trade licensed since her sixteenth year, taxes paid, no citations, no associations of note beyond a defrocked priest named Tammas Corley and a hedge-witch healer whose file read like a list of misdemeanors. Whitlock herself: quiet, punctual, unremarkable except for the quality of her work, which was remarkable indeed. He owned one piece himself — a small carved hound, bought years ago at a winter market — and there was something in its grain that made a man want to hold it longer than necessary.

He had met her once before, though she likely didn't remember. No — she would remember; people always remembered. But she had never spoken of it, and neither had he, and between those two silences hung a debt he had never found a way to repay or dismiss.

Twelve years ago. A raid on the Gullwing tenements, following rumors of a bone-reader working the docks. Reeve had been a young Warden then, second rank, ordered to sweep the upper floor while his superior took the lower. He'd found a girl of sixteen crouched behind a water barrel, shavings and half-finished charms scattered around her like snow, and her eyes when they met his had held no plea and no terror — only a flat, waiting arithmetic, the look of someone tallying the cost of everything in advance.

Her hands had been stained with bone dust to the wrist. On the floor beside her, a charm lay half-carved, and even then, even without touching it, Reeve had felt something in the room listening.

His superior's boots were on the stairs.

Reeve had looked at the girl, and the girl had looked at him, and he had said — loudly enough to carry — "Upper floor clear," and turned his back while she went out the window into the rain.

He told himself for years it had been mercy. Then, for a few less comfortable years, he told himself it had been instinct — that some part of him had already known the rumor was wrong, that no bone-reader worked the Gullwing, that he'd merely declined to destroy a child over nothing. Both stories served. Neither quite covered the fact that he had known her name within the week and never entered it into any record.

Now the answered prayers had marks on them, and the marks were appearing on Whitlock's work.

* * *

"The analysis came back," said Warden Osk Brannic, laying the folder on the desk between them. Brannic was his second, a broad, patient man with a farmer's face and a torturer's certificate he'd never used and hoped to die without using. "The pigment. Or whatever you'd call it."

"And?"

"It's not a pigment." Brannic sat, heavily. He always sat like a man ending a long day even at the start of one. "We burned a shaving of marked bone in the assay crucible. Standard protocol. Sir — the mark didn't burn. The bone around it charred to ash and the mark just... stayed. Floating in the ash. Whole."

Reeve turned the folder open. Inside was a sketch of the mark — a small whorl, like a fingerprint made of frost, or a rose seen through ice. Three specimens now, recovered from three separate charms across three districts. Three prayers answered. The sketch was good; Brannic drew better than he talked. Still, it failed to capture what Reeve had felt holding the original: a coldness, faint and specific, like standing at the mouth of a tomb and realizing the air inside is moving.

"Conclusion?" he said.

"That it's not of mortal manufacture. That it's—" Brannic shifted. He was a devout man in the way of farmers, which was to say his faith lived in his spine rather than his mouth, and it pained him visibly to say what he said next. "That it's a signature, sir. Something signing its work."

"Or counting it," Reeve murmured.

Brannic frowned. "Counting?"

"Three marks. Three answers. If something is keeping accounts, it will keep making them until the account comes due." He closed the folder. "Find me every marked charm in the city. Quietly. And find out where they were sold — not bought, *sold*. I want the carver's stall, the market day, the hour."

"You think the carver's involved."

"I think," Reeve said, "that something vast is passing messages through this city, and whoever carves the paper it writes on is either its servant, its victim, or its door. All three warrant a visit." He paused. "I'll make it myself."

Brannic's eyebrows climbed a careful half-inch. "You, sir? Protocol says—"

"Protocol says the Inquisitor investigates anomalies personally when the anomaly may involve unregistered thaumaturgy. Read your manual, Warden; I wrote half of it." He rose, took up his white sash, and settled it across his shoulders with the ease of long practice. It weighed nothing. It had never weighed anything. Some nights he wished it would.

"One more thing," Brannic said at the door. "The Prelate's office sent a letter. They've heard the market rumors too. They're asking whether the Inquisition requires... augmentation."

Meaning: soldiers. Meaning: a purge. Meaning: burn first, count afterward.

"Not yet," Reeve said. "Tell them the investigation is progressing."

"And if they ask what's progressing toward?"

Reeve looked at the window, where the moon — one night off full, fat and pale as a peeled egg — hung over the rooftops of Vellharrow.

"Toward an accounting," he said.

* * *

The bone-carver's shop stood on Chandler's Lane, wedged between a tallow-renderer and a shuttered chandler — hence the lane's name and its smell. Reeve dismissed his escort at the corner. Two Wardens at a carver's door was a summons. One was a customer.

A bell above the door announced him — a small courtesy, or a small warning; he wasn't sure which she'd intended when she hung it.

Inside, the shop smelled of linseed oil and dust and something older underneath, something mineral and cool, like the inside of a cave. Carvings lined the walls on pegs: hounds and hares, hands and hearts, birds with folded wings, the common inventory of a mourning-charm trade. Afternoon light came slantwise through the front window and caught the bone dust suspended in it, so that the whole shop seemed to be breathing a slow golden breath.

Xanthe Whitlock stood at her workbench with her back to him, a carving knife moving in small, precise strokes. She did not turn around.

"We're closed," she said.

"For everyone, or for me?"

The knife stopped. When she turned, her expression was exactly what he remembered from twelve years ago and entirely different besides: the same level gaze, but older now, annealed, the flat arithmetic replaced by something harder to read. She had grown into a face that kept its secrets the way a river keeps its stones.

"Warden Reeve," she said. "Come to arrest me?"

"Should I be?"

"You tell me. You're the one with the sash."

He let that stand, and looked deliberately at the workbench, at the charm lying half-finished on the leather pad. A mourning piece. A grandmother, by the proportions. "I'm here about answered prayers, Mistress Whitlock. You'll have heard the talk. The nettle-seller's cart. The fire on Tanner's Row."

"People talk."

"People also buy charms. Yours, specifically. And some of those charms have recently developed a mark — a whorl, white as frost, that doesn't burn." He watched her hands. Hands betrayed what faces concealed; it was the first lesson the Inquisition taught and the truest. Her hands did not flinch, but they curled, slightly, protectively inward, the way hands curl around a wound.

"I've heard of the marks," she said. "Customers bring them back sometimes, wanting to know if I've cursed them."

"Do you curse them?"

"No." Something flickered behind her eyes — anger, maybe, or grief. "I carve what I carve. Whatever else happens to the bone afterward isn't mine."

"Isn't it?" He stepped closer, slowly, giving her room to object, and lifted the half-finished grandmother from the bench. The bone was warm from the lamplight — no. Warmer than lamplight explained. And beneath the warmth, that cold moving air again, faint as breath through a keyhole. He set it down carefully. "Mistress Whitlock. Twelve years ago I cleared an upper floor on Gullwing Street and saw nothing worth recording. I have never regretted it. I am telling you that now because I am about to ask you something I need you to answer honestly, and I want you to know the honest answer won't put you in irons today."

She was very still. Outside, a handcart rattled past; the bell over the door stirred without ringing.

"Ask," she said.

"What are the marks counting?"

For a long moment she said nothing, and he watched her decide — watched the scales move behind her eyes the way they'd moved on Gullwing Street, cost tallied against cost. Then, quietly, with the weariness of someone setting down something heavy:

"I don't know yet. But I know this much. The marks aren't random. They're not decay and they're not dye. They appear when a prayer gets answered, only on my work, and they're getting more frequent." She met his eyes. "Something noticed me, Warden. Something enormous. And I don't think it's finished noticing."

It was more confession than he'd expected and less than he needed, and the strange thing — the thing he would lie awake turning over that night — was that his first reaction, beneath the Inquisitor's discipline, beneath twenty-three years of doctrine, was not alarm.

It was envy. *Something enormous notices her,* thought the man who knelt nightly to a silence. *Something enormous has use for her.*

"Veyran," she said suddenly.

He blinked. "What?"

"The wolf-god. Of thresholds. Of debts." Her jaw tightened. "If your church remembers any names from before, start there. Because he came to my workshop last night, wearing the shape of a wolf, and he told me the others are waking." She picked up the half-carved grandmother and held it against her chest like a child, or a shield. "And he wouldn't say why. Only that there's a deadline."

"A deadline."

"He called it the thirteenth moon." Her voice dropped, and for the first time since he'd entered, he heard fear in it — real fear, sized correctly to the subject. "The seal that keeps the old gods out of the world, Warden. Your church built its whole house on top of it and called the foundation a miracle. It weakens every lunar cycle. It fails entirely at the thirteenth turn." She looked at the window, at the fat pale moon already climbing over the chandler's roof. "Unless it's re-forged."

Reeve stood very still in the golden dust-light, and felt the floor of his life tilt by a degree so slight that only a man who had spent twenty-three years checking his balance could feel it at all.

The Unnamed One. The doctrine of silence. The great refusal on which the Inquisition had raised its gallows and its cathedrals alike — and beneath it all, a seal, a mechanism, a *countdown*. His church hadn't abolished the gods. It had been sitting on their lid for two hundred years, calling the lid heaven, and now the lid was thinning and the things below were knocking and someone had given the knock a calendar.

"How many turns?" he heard himself ask. His voice sounded far away, a stranger's voice.

She counted silently, lips moving, and when she looked up her eyes were terrible and calm.

"This coming full moon makes eight," she said. "Five left after that."

Outside, the evening bell of the Prelate's cathedral began to ring vespers, and Halvard Reeve — Inquisitor of Vellharrow, keeper of the white sash, servant of the Unnamed — listened to it toll and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he no longer believed a single word it said.

Five moons. Five turns of the lock. And somewhere in this city, in this very room perhaps, the re-forging waited in the hands of a bone-carver who had just handed the Inquisition's own secrets back to it out of what looked disturbingly like trust.

"Mistress Whitlock," he said, and his voice was steady, which cost him more than the walk from the chapel had ever cost him. "When the eighth moon rises tomorrow night — I want to be there when it happens. Whatever happens. Show me what the mark does."

"Why?" she asked. "So you can report me?"

"So I can know," he said, "which side of that door I'm standing on."

She studied him for a long moment, the knife loose in her fingers, the little bell silent above them both. Somewhere across the rooftops the vespers bell finished, and the silence it left behind seemed to lean in and wait.

"All right, Warden," Xanthe Whitlock said at last. "But if you come to my door tomorrow, leave the sash at home. Tomorrow night, you're not the Inquisition."

"Then what am I?"

"Tomorrow night," she said, turning back to her bench, "you're just a man asking the dark a question. Pray it answers you kindly."
