## 8. A Debt in Silver

The wolf came back on the third night, and this time he brought the cold with him.

Xanthe knew it before she opened her eyes — the particular hush of a room where the air had stopped behaving, the way frost feathered across her window from the *inside*, climbing the glass in patterns like antler-crown, like branching rivers. Her breath hung silver above the blanket. Somewhere below, in the workshop that doubled as her bedchamber when the rent came due for warmth, the coals in her little stove had gone grey and still.

"You could knock," she said to the dark.

"I did." Veyran's voice was low thunder heard through a wall, patient as erosion. "You didn't answer."

"Because I was asleep."

"Because you were afraid." A pause, long enough that she could hear her own heartbeat counting through it. "Both are acceptable reasons."

She sat up, dragging the blanket around her shoulders like a cloak, and looked at him. He filled the space between workbench and door the way winter fills a valley — not moving, just present, his grey coat drinking what little light remained from the banked stove. His eyes were the color of old pewter, and they did not blink often enough for comfort.

"You're letting the heat out," she said.

"I am not letting anything out. I am keeping everything in. There is a difference, carver, though your kind has forgotten it." He lowered his great head, and frost crackled beneath his jaw where his breath touched the floorboards. "We need to speak of debts."

"I don't owe you anything."

"Not yet." The pewter eyes gleamed. "That is precisely what we must discuss."

* * *

She lit the stove first. It was stubbornness more than sense — lighting a fire in front of a god of thresholds felt like an argument she intended to win — but the coal caught, and the orange light pushed some of the winter out of the corners, and Xanthe found she could breathe again. She put the kettle on because her hands needed something ordinary to do. Carving was better, but carving in front of him felt like showing a card sharp your deck.

Veyran watched her the whole time. Gods, it turned out, had no shame about staring.

"Say what you came to say," she said at last, settling onto her stool with her arms crossed over the blanket. "And then leave, and let me get back to pretending my life is small."

"Your life was never small. That was the lie we built around you, and I apologize for it — poorly, and too late, and you will not accept the apology, which is also fair." He paced once, twice, the click of his claws precise as a clock. "Three nights ago I told you that you are the door. I told you the others are waking. I did not tell you everything, because everything is heavy, and I wished to see how you would carry the first weight before loading you further."

"And?"

"And you carried it by making soup for your smuggler and scolding your hedge-witch about her smoking, and by lying awake cataloguing every white mark on every charm you've ever sold." His muzzle twitched — the closest thing to a smile a wolf's face could manage. "You carry weight by continuing to live. It is more than most mortals manage. It is more than most *gods* managed, in the end."

The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling. Xanthe didn't move to take it off. "Get to the offer. You didn't come all this way to compliment me."

"No. I came to buy something."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped chisel. "I'm not for sale."

"Everyone is for sale. Most people are simply never offered the right currency." Veyran sat, folding his legs beneath him with impossible grace for something so large, and even sitting he nearly reached her workbench's height. "Let me tell you what is coming, carver, and then let me tell you what I can give you, and then you may hate me at your leisure.

"The seal weakens. You know this — you have felt it in the bone, in the marks, in the way the dead have grown louder these twelve years, whispering up through cracks we thought were sealed forever. Each moon that turns, the weakening quickens. At the thirteenth turn, the seal fails entirely. Not gently. Not gradually. All at once, like a dam going, like a tooth leaving the jaw. And every god behind it comes through in the same hour, awake and ravenous and *furious* after a thousand years of silence, with no understanding of what the world has become and no interest in learning."

"The war," she said quietly. "Comes back."

"The war never ended. It only paused, the way a held breath pauses." His voice dropped. "Sereth wakes first. Sereth always wakes first; she dreams of running even now, and her dreams leak through the cracks, and they are why the wolves in the Weald have gone strange this season, packing too large, hunting things they shouldn't. When she arrives, she will arrive already hunting. And the first thing she hunts will be the anchor holding the door — because if the anchor dies, the door dies, and there is nothing left to argue with."

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "Me."

"You. The last living anchor. The linchpin. The knot at the center of the rope." He said it plainly, without ceremony, and somehow that was worse. "Now. Here is what I offer."

He rose, and crossed to her workbench, and she flinched — gods did not touch mortal things, or so the stories said — but he touched nothing. Instead he laid his great head near the shelf where her finished charms hung on their leather cords, each one a small pale shape: hands, birds, doors, teeth. Her livelihood. Her secret made solid a hundred times over.

"There are powers waking besides the angry ones," Veyran said. "Older courtesies. Older *contracts*. I am the god of thresholds and debts — I hold the law of crossing, and while I walk free, certain protections attach to those under my... consideration. Your smuggler. Your healer. Your shop, your street, your city block, if I stretch." The pewter eyes lifted to hers. "No Warden will find them. No god will touch them. No plague will cross their threshold, no blade find their ribs, no lie be believed against them in any court, mortal or divine. While you carry my message, they are held. As safe as anything in this world can be made safe, which is not perfectly, but far better than what awaits them otherwise."

The kettle shrieked. Xanthe took it off the flame with hands that shook only a little, and poured two cups of the cheap barley tea she kept for customers who lingered too long, because her mother had raised her that way and her mother's ghost would haunt her otherwise. She set one cup down in front of the wolf. It was absurd. It was the most absurd thing she had ever done, and she had once carved a funeral mask for a man who'd faked his death to escape three marriages.

Veyran looked at the cup. Then at her. Then, deliberately, he lapped from it once — tea, steam, and all — like a guest honoring a host.

"And the message?" she asked.

"Four names. Four places. The other gods who wake clear-headed instead of furious — few, but not none. You carry word to each: that the thirteenth turn approaches, that the anchor lives and breathes and *chooses*, and that there may be another road than war, if they are willing to hear terms." His tail moved once, slowly, a metronome counting down something. "They will not all listen. Some will try to kill the messenger. That is the risk you carry, and I will not pretend it is small."

"And if I refuse?"

The question hung in the frosted air between them. Veyran did not look away.

"If you refuse," he said, "I will not harm you. Understand that — whatever else you believe of me, understand that I do not threaten you, because threats against you are wasted breath; you are already threatened from eight directions by things older and crueler than I. But if you refuse, then Tam of the River Gate remains a defrocked priest whose name sits in three Inquisitorial ledgers. Odile Marchand remains a hedge-witch practicing without license in a city that pays informants in bread. They remain exactly as unprotected as they have always been — which is to say, protected by nothing but luck, and luck, carver, is a debt that always gets called."

*There it is.*

Xanthe set down her cup harder than she meant to. Tea slopped over the rim and pooled in the grain of the workbench, among the bone dust and the shavings, and she stared at the mess because looking at the wolf was suddenly unbearable.

"That's not an offer," she said. "That's a hostage situation with better manners."

"Yes." No denial. No flinch. The honesty was almost gentle, which made it monstrous. "I could have dressed it differently. I chose not to, because you would have seen through the dressing within a day, and then you would hate me twice — once for the manipulation, once for thinking you wouldn't notice. This way you hate me once, cleanly, for the truth." He tilted his head. "Is it working?"

"No." Her laugh came out cracked and ugly. "I hate you in *layers*."

"Good. Layered hatred is durable. It will keep you alive longer than simple fear." He rose, shaking frost from his coat, and the temperature in the room seemed to gather itself around him like a garment being taken up. "You need not answer tonight. But answer soon. Sereth's dream grows sharper each night, and dreams, unlike gods, do not wait for permission to enter."

* * *

He left through the door. Of course he did. A god of thresholds would sooner die than use a window, and there was something almost funny in watching an enormous wolf sit back on his haunches, nose the latch with terrible delicacy, and ease the door open with one paw before slipping into the alley fog. The latch clicked shut behind him. The frost on the window melted in slow silver tears.

Xanthe sat alone with two cups of tea, one of them bearing the faintest dent of a divine tongue, and hated herself with great thoroughness.

Because here was the thing she could not stop seeing, turning it over in her mind like a flawed stone: she had known, from the moment he said *your smuggler, your healer*, that the answer was yes. Had known it the way you know a bone's grain before the knife ever touches it — not deciding, just reading what was already written there. Tam, who had given up his vestments rather than burn a family of unlicensed faith-healers, and who now fed half the tenement kids off his smuggling profits and called it inventory shrinkage. Odile, who treated plague-fever in the Sump for eggs and gossip, who had stitched Xanthe's hand last winter when a chisel slipped and never once asked why Xanthe sometimes talked to the bandages afterward.

Twelve years she had kept her head down. Twelve years of smallness, of the careful dull life, of hearing the dead whisper through her carvings and saying nothing, carving nothing true, selling grief-charms that were only grief-charms. She had done it all to stay invisible. And invisibility had lasted exactly until something vast looked at the world and saw her — and now the only question was whether the people she loved got eaten as collateral or shielded by a god's cold arithmetic.

Love makes you pliable. Her mother had said it once, laughing, kneading bread dough, and Xanthe had thought it tender then. Now it tasted like a warning. Every soft place in her was a hinge someone else could push. Veyran hadn't needed magic to find her leverage. He'd needed only five minutes and honest eyes.

She got up. She went to the shelf of charms. Her fingers found the newest one — a small door-shape, carved from elk antler two weeks ago, before the marks started — and she turned it in the lamplight, feeling the grain, listening to the faint cold hum the bone had carried ever since the world changed.

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the melting frost, to the god presumably still listening from wherever gods listened. "I'll carry your message. Four names, four places. But hear the terms from *my* side, wolf-god, since you're so fond of clean dealing: I'm not your instrument. I'm not Sereth's target, and I'm not the Inquisition's proof. I'm a carver, and carvers decide what a thing becomes. You want a messenger? Fine. But the message goes in my words, not yours. And when this is over—" her voice caught, steadied, "—when this is over, the debt runs both directions. You'll owe me. And I collect."

Silence. Long enough that she thought he'd gone.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere, warm as a banked coal: *"Well bargained, carver."*

And on the workbench, in the spilled tea and bone dust, frost bloomed briefly in a shape she recognized — a wolf's print, no larger than a coin — and faded.

* * *

Tam arrived an hour past midnight, smelling of river water and contraband candle-tallow, letting himself in with the knock that meant *it's me, don't stab anyone.* He found her at the bench, four cups of tea deep, charcoal sketching spread out before her: a map of Vellharrow's outskirts, lines radiating outward like cracks in ice, toward places she only knew from the dead men's memories — a barrow-field east of the salt marshes, a drowned chapel upriver, a standing stone in the Weald where hunters refused to camp.

"You look," Tam said, dropping onto her cot and immediately putting his boots on her good blanket, "like a woman who's made a deal with something."

"How can you tell?"

"Because you're doing the thing with your jaw. Odile says it's your tell. Says you clamp down like you're biting the decision shut so it can't run off." He peered at the map, and his grin faded by degrees, the way lamplight fades as oil runs low. "Xanthe. Those aren't trade routes."

"No."

"That's a line to the Weald." His voice had gone flat and careful, the smuggler's voice, the voice he used when the cargo was dangerous. "People don't go to the Weald. Things come *out* of the Weald." He looked up at her, and beneath the weathered cynicism she saw the young priest he'd been, the one who'd read the forbidden texts by tallow-light and paid for it with his collar. "Whoever you've been talking to — it's one of them, isn't it? One of the old ones. It gave you names."

"Four names. Four places. And protection for you and Odile while I deliver."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a drunk was singing the mourning hymn wrong, and neither of them laughed at it, which said everything about the state of the world lately.

"A god's protection," he said finally, "is a god's lien. You know that. Everything they shield, they consider theirs."

"I know."

"And you took the deal anyway. Because of us." It wasn't a question. His face did something complicated — gratitude and grief fighting over the same ground. "Gods, Xanthe. You should have let me be eaten. I'd have made a stringy meal."

"You'd have been insufferable about it, too."

"Right up to the end." He stood, and squeezed her shoulder once, hard, the way he did instead of saying things. "All right. If you're riding to the Weald, you're not riding alone, and you're not riding on my cousin's barge without paying the tariff, so we leave tomorrow after dark, and I'm bringing the good rope." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and glanced back. "The fourth name. The standing stone. Whose name is that one?"

Xanthe looked down at the charcoal map, at the line running north into the wild places, and felt the antler charm in her pocket pulse once, cold and white, like a second heartbeat.

"Hers," she said. "The huntress. The one who wakes first."
