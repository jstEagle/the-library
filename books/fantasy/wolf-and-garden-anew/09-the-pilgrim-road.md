## 9. The Pilgrim Road

Tam had smuggled contraband through nine provinces and out from under the noses of three different Inquisitors, but he'd never smuggled hope before. It was heavier than opium, more volatile than gunpowder, and it leaked through every seam of the wagon no matter how carefully he packed it.

"Slow down," he called to Xanthe on the bench beside him. "You're driving like the road owes you money."

"The road might," she said, not slowing down.

The mule plodded on regardless — a swaybacked creature named Bishop that Tam had acquired in trade for a bolt of Vellharrow linen and a promise never to return to the linen merchant's district. Behind them, lashed under canvas among bolts of cloth and casks of pickled herring, sat forty-one bone charms carved by Xanthe's hands over six sleepless nights. Every one of them bore the faint white traceries now: fine as frost ferns, wrong as teeth in a mouth that shouldn't have them. They were the reason they traveled. They were also, if Odile's grim arithmetic proved right, the reason half the waking gods in creation could find them like wolves finding blood on snow.

"You didn't have to come," Xanthe said. It was the fourth time she'd said it, and each time Tam answered the same way, because the answer was true and repetition is how truth becomes load-bearing.

"And leave you alone on a road full of pilgrims, miracle-mongers, and Wardens? What kind of brother would I be?"

"I'm not your sister."

"No," Tam agreed, flicking the reins across Bishop's grey rump. "You're worse. You're family."

The road to Aurelach ran northeast along the old pilgrim route, the one the Church had let fall into ruin once worship became a crime of state. Twelve years of neglect hadn't erased it, though. Roads like this remembered their purpose the way bones remembered their shape. And they were not alone on it. That was the thing that kept tightening something behind Tam's sternum, mile after mile: they were *not alone*.

They passed a farmer's cart piled with sleeping children and bundled grain, a crude wooden talisman of the Horned Mother swinging from the yoke where a tax seal should hang. They passed two old women walking barefoot, their shoes tied together by the laces and slung around one woman's neck, saving them for arrival. They passed a young man preaching to no one at all, standing in a ditch, arms spread, shouting that the Lady of the Wells had spoken to his sister, that she'd told her where the winter fish would run, and by the saints, she'd been *right*.

No one stopped him. No Wardens came. That was its own kind of dread. In Vellharrow, a man preaching in a ditch would be bread-and-copper informants' work inside an hour. Out here, past the city's shadow, the law felt thin as morning frost, and everyone on the road knew it, and everyone kept walking anyway, toward the answering altars, toward whatever was waking up in the world.

"They're not afraid," Xanthe said quietly. She'd gone still in a way Tam recognized from her workshop, the stillness of a carver reading grain in a piece of bone.

"No," Tam said. "That's what scares me."

He knew the texts. He'd spent seven years as a priest of the Concordant faith before the faith ate itself and spat him out defrocked and unrepentant, and in those years he'd read everything the archives allowed and a good deal they didn't. The old gods were not gentle. That wasn't slander; it was taxonomy. A god of thresholds collected tolls. A goddess of the hunt required running prey. Divinity, in the old cosmologies, was less a virtue than a hunger with excellent posture. The Concordant Church hadn't outlawed magic out of piety, whatever their catechisms claimed. They'd outlawed it because the last time the old powers walked openly, the world had learned what worship meant when it was compulsory: altars running red, harvests tithed to shrines while children starved, whole villages given to the hunt so Sereth would turn her antlers elsewhere.

And here came the pilgrims, barefoot and singing, walking straight back into the open mouth of it, calling it mercy because some of the miracles *were* merciful. That was the trap of it. That had always been the trap. The gods gave you exactly what you prayed for, and then they owned the prayer forever.

"Tam." Xanthe's hand closed over his wrist. "Look ahead."

* * *

The shrine of Saint Verren stood where the road crested the last hill before the river valley — or what remained of it did. Once it had been a waystation chapel, whitewashed, roofed in slate. Now the roof was gone and the walls were scorched black, and around it, in a rough crescent half a mile wide, a tent city had grown like mold on bread. Cookfires. Banners. Hawkers selling wax tablets and votive ribbons at prices that would have made Vellharrow's guildmasters weep with envy. And at the center, at the broken altar itself, a crowd pressed shoulder to shoulder around something Tam couldn't see, and the sound rising off them was not singing.

It was weeping. But it was the good kind, the kind Tam remembered from his vestment days, the kind that broke people open and left them cleaner. Hearing it made his chest hurt with nostalgia and revulsion in equal measure, which was, he supposed, the most honest feeling he'd had about the divine in fifteen years.

They left Bishop and the wagon with a toothless carter who charged them an extortionate fee and swore by every dead saint that no Warden would come within a mile of this place — too many eyes, too many witnesses, and besides, the Wardens were afraid. Tam paid and didn't believe a word of it except the fear.

Pushing through the crowd was like wading upstream through warm water. Bodies everywhere, breath and woodsmoke and crushed herbs, and under it all a smell Tam couldn't place at first, sweetish and mineral, until he realized: it was the smell of Xanthe's workshop. Of carved bone. Hundreds of charms, hundreds of relics, worn against skin by every soul in this crowd, and every single one of them humming, if you believed the pilgrims, with something new.

Then the crowd opened, and Tam saw the altar, and stopped pretending he was only here to protect Xanthe.

A child lay on the altar slab. A girl, maybe six, wasted thin in the particular way that spoke of long illness rather than short famine, her mother kneeling beside her with her forehead pressed to the stone. An old man stood over them both — a shrine-keeper, by his patched robes, or a man who'd decided to become one sometime in the last month, because his vestments were clearly cut-down bedclothes. He held the girl's small hand flat in both of his own, palm up, and on the palm lay a bone charm. A simple one. A knuckle-bone, polished smooth, strung on a leather cord.

Tam knew that knotting style. He'd watched Xanthe tie a hundred cords exactly that way, thumb-hooked, double-passed, the knot tucked under so it wouldn't chafe.

"Oh," he heard himself say. "Oh, no."

"What?" Xanthe whispered. But her voice said she already knew.

"That's yours. That's one of yours. Sold through the herring factor, probably, or—" He stopped. It didn't matter how. What mattered was that a piece of Xanthe's craft lay at the center of a crowd of five hundred desperate people, white-marked, and whatever happened next was going to happen *through* it.

The old shrine-keeper began to pray. His voice cracked on the first line — he was no priest, no orator, just a frightened old man improvising liturgy out of fragments his grandmother had taught him in secret — and it should have been nothing. It should have been a madman mumbling over a dying child.

The crowd went silent anyway. Five hundred people, silent as held breath.

And then the light came.

It didn't blaze. Tam would spend the rest of his life trying to describe it honestly, and the honest version was worse than any blazing: it simply *arrived*, the way warmth arrives in a cold room, the way a name arrives on your tongue. The air above the altar thickened and turned the color of early dawn, gold shot through with grey, and every bone charm in the crowd — every carved bead, every relic, every knuckle and rib and antler tip — lit with the same white tracery Tam had seen on Xanthe's workbench, a constellation of small cold stars spread through a sea of bodies. The girl's mother screamed once, softly, like a kettle taken off heat.

The wasting sickness left the child. There was no other way to put it. Tam watched color flood back into grey cheeks, watched the stick-thin limbs fill, watched the girl sit up on the altar slab — *sit up*, briskly, annoyed, the way children do when adults are making a fuss — and ask for bread. The crowd's silence held for one more impossible second.

Then it broke into a sound Tam had only ever heard described in scripture: the shout of a multitude with one throat.

He found himself gripping Xanthe's arm hard enough to bruise. She wasn't looking at the child. She was looking at her own hands, at the faint white shimmer crawling beneath the skin of her fingertips like veins of ore, pulsing in time with the light on the altar.

"It's coming through me," she said. Her voice was very level, which is how Tam knew she was terrified. "All of it. Every miracle on this road, it's — I'm the wire, Tam. They're lighting lamps and I'm the—"

"Don't." He turned her gently away from the crowd, shielding her with his body, an old smuggler's instinct — hide the valuable thing. "Don't finish that sentence out loud. Not here."

"But—"

"*Not here.*" He kept his voice low and pleasant, a market voice, a nothing-to-see-here voice, because he could feel the attention of the crowd starting to drift, the way it always drifted, seeking the next wonder, and he wanted them gone before it settled anywhere near the quiet woman with the carver's calluses. "We came to look. We've looked. Now we walk, slowly, like tourists, toward the food stalls, and then toward the wagon, and you tell me everything on the road."

It almost worked. That was the cruelty of it. They got thirty yards, nearly to the hawkers' row, close enough to smell frying onions and cheap tallow candles.

That was when the skeptic opened his mouth.

* * *

He was a big man, red-faced, dressed in the good wool of a grain merchant, and he'd clearly had wine with his courage. Tam marked him early — you learn to mark the volatile ones in crowds, the pressure points — standing on the base of a fallen pillar, gesturing at the healed child with a half-eaten sausage.

"Wonderful!" the man bellowed. "Wonderful! One sick brat saved, and five hundred fools ready to hand over their souls! Who paid for this miracle, eh? Who's collecting at the gate?" He jabbed the sausage toward the tent city's edge, where the hawkers worked their rows. "I'll tell you who. Same as always. And when your new gods come asking their price — and they will ask, my grandfather's grandmother saw the old days, and there's a reason the seals went up — don't come crying to me!"

Some in the crowd laughed. Some shouted him down. A few — Tam counted them, a habit, counting exits and threats — a few went quiet in a way he didn't like. Quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

"Friend," said a woman near the front, a pilgrim with road dust to her knees and devotion shining in her face like fever, "you should kneel while the Presence is still here. While it's generous."

"The Presence." The merchant spat. "It's a trick of light and a well-rehearsed cripple, is what it is. My coin stays in my purse, and my neck stays unbent, and if your gods don't like it—"

He never finished the sentence.

Later, trying to reconstruct it, Tam could only hold pieces: the sudden absolute stillness of the air, the way every fire in the camp leaned sideways at once as if inhaling; the white marks flaring on a hundred charms; the merchant's face going through surprise into something worse than fear, into recognition. Because that was the horror of it, the part that lived behind Tam's eyes afterward: the man *recognized* what had come for him. For one heartbeat he looked into the arriving presence and knew it by name, some ancestral memory older than the Church, older than language — and then the flame took him.

It didn't come from any torch. It rose out of the ground, out of the trampled grass, out of the air itself, a column of white-gold fire that burned without smoke and without heat — Tam felt no heat, he was twenty feet away, he felt nothing but a pressure behind his eyes like a struck bell — and it wrapped the merchant from boots to crown, and the man's scream lasted exactly as long as it takes to draw one full breath.

When the fire guttered out, there was ash on the pillar-base in the shape of a man, and the sausage, untouched, lying in the dust.

The crowd did not flee. That was the second horror. The crowd *knelt*. All around the smoking shape of the skeptic, five hundred people dropped to their knees as one, faces to the dirt, and the sound they made was not grief. It was relief. *Not me. Not me. See how it spares the faithful.* Tam stood upright in a forest of bowed backs, the only standing man for fifty feet except—

Except Xanthe. Who stood rigid, staring at the ash-shape, and whose lips were moving. Tam caught the words only because he was watching her mouth:

"Not me either. Why not me? Why am I *spared*?"

Because, he thought, ice sliding down his spine, because you're not a bystander, little sister. You're furniture. You're the house the fire lives in. Gods don't burn their own hearthstones.

He took her elbow and steered her through the kneeling crowd, fast now, done pretending. Behind them the shrine-keeper's voice had risen again, trembling, transfigured, already spinning the burning into doctrine: "The Lord of Altars receives all offerings! The proud he refines, the humble he keeps! Kneel! Kneel and be kept!"

Nobody questioned it. Nobody wept for the merchant. By nightfall, Tam knew, there would be a new hymn about it, and pilgrims would carry the story down every road like sparks on wind: *a miracle healed a child, and a miracle burned a scoffer, praise the returning ones.* Both halves true. Neither half the truth.

They didn't speak until the wagon and the mule and the toothless carter were behind them and the road bent north into deepening dusk. Then Xanthe finally said, in a small voice stripped of all its carver's steadiness:

"Veyran promised me protection. He promised the others would wake *gently.*"

"Gods lie kindly," Tam said. "It's practically their vocation." He glanced at the white shimmer still faintly visible under her skin whenever the moonlight caught it. "Question is whether our wolf lied, or whether he's the only honest one in a den of liars, and either way—" He exhaled. "Either way, we can't go home. Home is the first place they'll look, and the second place is wherever you stand still for longer than a day."

"So where do we go?"

Tam thought of the one place on earth where someone might actually know what the white marks meant — the one institution older than the Concordant Church, the shrine-city at the road's end, full of priests who'd spent twelve centuries secretly preserving everything the Inquisition burned. Dangerous. But ignorance was killing people on hillsides now, and he was done being ignorant.

"Aurelach," he said. "There's a high priestess there. Maren Corvane. If anyone alive knows what's writing on your bones, it's her."

He didn't see the rider until the mule's ears pricked — a lone figure on the far ridge against the last of the light, sitting very still, watching their wagon pass. Not a Warden's silhouette. Wrong hat. Wrong horse. But it didn't move on when they did, and it didn't follow either. It just sat there, patient as a threshold, and Tam found himself remembering the oldest warning in the forbidden texts, the one scrawled in a dead archivist's margin:

*A wolf at your door you may bargain with. A wolf on your road has already chosen you.*

By the time he turned fully around, the ridge was empty.
