## 10. Sister of the Seal

Aurelach smelled of incense and fear.

Xanthe had expected the first — every shrine-city reeked of it, sweet resin and old smoke baked into stone over centuries. The second she hadn't anticipated until they passed through the pilgrim gate at dusk, and she saw how the crowds moved: not with joy, though miracles were happening here daily, but with the careful, glancing movements of people who had learned too recently that the divine could also burn.

Tam walked close at her shoulder, hood up, his smuggler's face arranged into the bland expression of a man carrying nothing worth stealing. "Remember," he murmured, "we're pilgrims. Weeping, grateful pilgrims. You weep, I'll be grateful."

"I don't weep on command."

"You will when you see what a room costs in this town. Half of Vellharrow's smuggled silver is already here." He nodded toward the shrine district rising above them, terraced and white-walled, its spires crowned with lanterns that had been dark for twelve years and now burned again. "Everyone wants to touch a god before it touches them back."

The streets were thick with them — the lame leaning on crutches toward the healing altars, mothers with sick children wrapped against their chests, merchants selling prayer-ribbons at ten times their honest price. And everywhere the marks of the new age: chalked prayers on doorposts, offerings piled in alcoves where beggars had once slept, and painted over some older walls, the faded grey rectangles where images of the old gods had been scraped away by order of the Inquisition — scraped, Xanthe now understood, but never truly gone beneath the plaster.

Her satchel hung heavy against her hip. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, were the four marked charms — the widow's mourning piece, the soldier's knucklebone, the child's milk-tooth amulet, the fisherman's vertebra. Four answered prayers, four bone-white blooms like frost ferns spreading through the carved surfaces. She had stopped trying to convince herself they were nothing.

Veyran had told her to come to Aurelach. *If you want to know what you are,* the wolf had said from her workshop threshold, eyes like coins in the dark, *go where the gods are waking loudest. But go quickly, little carver. Doors that open for one may open for many.*

She hadn't told Tam about the wolf. Not all of it. She'd said she needed to see the answering altars for her trade — which was even true, in the way lies built on true stones always were.

* * *

They found lodging above a dyer's shop, in a room that smelled of urine and mordant, and at dawn Tam went out to work his contacts among the shrine-tenders while Xanthe did what she had crossed three hundred miles to do.

The great altar of the Undying Garden stood in the oldest terrace of the city, a slab of green-veined marble older than any kingdom, carved over with vines and sleeping faces. For twelve years it had been a place where the faithful came to remember that their gods were dead; now they came because flowers bloomed from the marble cracks overnight, real ones, dew-wet, impossible.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the crowd, watching an altar-priest lay hands on a fevered girl. Nothing happened for a long moment — long enough that Xanthe felt the crowd's hope curdle — and then the girl's mother screamed, and the fever broke like a snapped thread, and the crowd roared.

And across the whole terrace, Xanthe heard it: a sound like a great bell struck underwater, felt rather than heard, passing through her chest, through her bones, through the four charms wrapped against her ribs.

The charms grew warm.

No — not warm. *Aware.* Through the oilcloth she could feel them straining toward the altar like compass needles, and when she pressed her hand flat against her satchel she felt the white marks pulsing, faint as heartbeats, in time with the miracle.

*You are the conduit,* Veyran had said. *Every answer passes through you whether you consent or no.*

"Then stop me," she whispered. "If you're listening — any of you — just *stop me.*"

The bell-sound swelled once more and faded. On the marble altar, a new vine sprouted, pale and quick, and wound itself around the priest's wrist like something affectionate. The crowd wept. Xanthe turned and walked away before anyone could notice that the charms in her bag had begun to glow through the cloth, bone-white light leaking between her fingers like water.

She made it three streets before the woman found her.

* * *

"You carry answered prayers."

Xanthe spun. The speaker stood in the shadow of a shrine archway where candlelight couldn't quite reach — a tall woman in undyed wool, grey-streaked hair braided with iron rings, and eyes the colour of deep water over slate. She wore no vestments, but everything about her posture said authority worn so long it had become skin.

"I don't know what you mean," Xanthe said.

"No," the woman agreed. "But your bones do." She stepped forward, and Xanthe caught the smell of myrrh and cold stone. "I am Maren Corvane. I keep this city's oldest rites — the ones the Inquisition thinks died with the gods. I have spent eleven days watching pilgrims, and eleven nights reading the signs, and yesterday I dreamed of frost on bone. Then today I saw a woman by the Garden altar whose shadow flickered wrong, whose bag glowed like a lamp behind her ribs." Her gaze dropped to the satchel. "Show me."

Everything in Xanthe's body said run. Twelve years of hiding said run. But her feet stayed planted, because Maren Corvane looked at her the way no one ever had — not with suspicion or hunger or greed, but with recognition. The look of someone seeing a face from an old grief.

Slowly, Xanthe unwrapped the charms and held them out.

Maren didn't reach for them. She looked — really looked, the way Xanthe herself studied bone — and her breath went out of her slowly, and she made a small sound, half prayer and half wound.

"Frost-fern marks. All four." She lifted her eyes. "Where did you train?"

"Vellharrow. My father taught me carving. That's all this is — carving—"

"Your father," Maren said quietly, "was Sera Whitlock's husband."

The world tilted. Xanthe gripped the charm in her fist hard enough to bite. "How do you know my mother's name?"

"Because I knew your mother." Something ancient moved behind the priestess's composure — guilt, old and calcified. "Because I consecrated her. Because I helped hide her, and failed her, and I have spent twenty years wondering if the seal would hold, knowing what ran loose in the world wearing a carver's face." She extended one hand, palm up — not demanding, offering. "Come with me, daughter-of-Sera. There are things you must see before you can even begin to understand what those marks mean. And there is nowhere else in this city safe enough to say them aloud."

* * *

The safe house was beneath the shrine of a god with no name — a crypt chapel walled off from the terraces, accessible only through a tomb corridor that smelled of centuries. Candles burned at a bare stone altar, and on the wall behind it, hidden under whitewash someone had chipped away in patches, were murals: gods walking among mortals, gods burning fields, gods dying —

— and then a figure, repeated over and over across the mural's final panel, standing at the centre of a ring of kneeling divinities. A human figure, arms raised, light pouring from its chest like a fountain. Around its throat, rendered in gold leaf gone brown with age, hung a chain of small white charms.

Xanthe's mouth went dry.

"What is that?" she asked, though some part of her already knew.

"That," Maren said, lighting a fresh candle with steady hands that trembled only slightly, "is the Anchor. Or rather — that is how the first Anchor was depicted, nine hundred years ago, by people who still remembered why the world didn't end." She turned. "Tell me what you know of the sealing, child. What your parents told you. Anything."

"They told me the gods went to sleep. That the war ended and the church sealed heaven out of mercy, because the fighting—" She faltered. "That's what everyone's told. Everyone."

"And who do you imagine performed the sealing?" Maren asked softly. "Gods cannot bind gods. Mortals cannot bind gods — not with armies, not with prayers. It takes something between." She touched the mural, tracing the golden chain around the painted figure's throat. "It took Anchors. Living linchpins. Men and women in whom the seal was rooted — grown into their blood and bone like a tree grows into soil. While they lived and kept silent, the gods stayed out. The seal wasn't a wall, Xanthe. It was a person. Many persons, down the centuries, each one bred and trained and sworn to silence, because the binding lives partly in the keeping of the secret. Speak the truth of it aloud before your time turns, and the knot begins to slip."

Cold moved through Xanthe's veins, slower than fear, colder. "I'm not — I'm a bone-carver. From Vellharrow. I carve charms for widows and I can't afford heatstone in winter—"

"Yes," Maren said. "And the last Anchor was a bone-carver from Vellharrow who couldn't afford heatstone either. Your mother carried the seal the same way — quietly, in poverty, in a city that hated magic — because poverty and obscurity were armour. Because the Inquisition, if it had known, would have killed her not to end the seal but to control it. And because—" Maren's voice caught, the first crack in all that stone composure, "—because the Anchors' own order decided, generations ago, that the safest Anchor was an ignorant one. We stopped teaching them what they were. We hid them from themselves. We told ourselves it was protection."

"You knew her. You knew what she was, and you let her live blind?"

"We let her live *free*, for a while. There was a difference, we told ourselves." Maren's jaw tightened. "When your mother conceived you, the order despaired. An Anchor's child should have been nothing — the seal doesn't pass in blood. Except sometimes it does. Sometimes, when the line runs thin and the moons grow strange, the seal chooses its next vessel before the last one dies. We watched you be born, and we waited for signs, and the signs were quiet, and your parents begged us — *begged us* — to let you be ordinary. To let the seal pass elsewhere. So they fled. Took you to Vellharrow, buried the truth, lived small lives, died small deaths—"

"My mother drowned," Xanthe said. The words came out flat. "Crossing the Greywater. An accident."

"The ferry sank," Maren said, "the week the order learned where you'd gone. Both of them. Two good swimmers, in calm water." She let that sit in the candlelit silence. "I have never proven who arranged it. I have suspected everyone: the Inquisition, our own frightened elders, the seal itself, arranging its inheritance. Grief makes poor historians. But this I know: they died hiding what you are. They bought you twelve years of ignorance with their lives, and ignorance has just run out, because the gods are waking, and waking *through you*, and every white mark on your work is the seal announcing its linchpin to a world full of people who would use you, cage you, or kill you — some of them lovingly."

Xanthe sat down on the altar step without meaning to. Her hands, her carver's hands, lay in her lap, and for the first time since childhood she looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger.

"All those prayers," she said. "The widow's grandmother. The fevered girl just now. They weren't answered by gods deciding to be kind. They were answered *through me*. I'm the pipe. I'm the—" A laugh escaped her, ugly and small. "Twelve years I hid my gift like it was mine. Like it was a thing I chose. And the whole time I was furniture. A hinge. A *door*."

"A door," Maren repeated gently, "can be barred. Can be opened. Can be carved anew. That word — *carved* — is not incidental, Xanthe. The first Anchors weren't only vessels. They were makers. Bone-workers, stone-cutters, weavers of the binding. The seal is a crafted thing, maintained by craft, and craft can be remade." She crouched before her, taking Xanthe's cold hands in her warm, strong ones, and her deep-water eyes held no pity — only a terrible, level honesty. "I won't lie to you as your parents' order lied to them. I won't promise you safety, or a long life, or that love is possible for someone who is also a lock. What I will tell you is the truth I've guarded for twenty years: the thirteenth moon is coming. The seal fails at the thirteenth turn unless it is reforged. And the only hands that can reforge it are yours — or the only blood that can replace it is yours. Those are the two roads, and both begin the same way. You must learn what you are. All of it. Before anyone else decides for you."

Outside, muffled through tons of stone, a temple bell began to toll — not the hour, Xanthe realized dimly, but the signal that another prayer had been answered somewhere in the city. Somewhere a miracle was blooming, and here in the dark beneath it all, the frost-marks on four small carvings pulsed once in her satchel, soft as breathing.

"How long," Xanthe heard herself ask, "have I got?"

Maren's hands tightened around hers.

"Two turns of the moon," she said. "And child — the wolf that sent you here already knows. Whatever bargain he offered you, whatever name he gave it — ask yourself what a god of debts wants with a door he cannot open himself."

The bell tolled again. And far above, through stone and earth and the weight of a city drunk on returning miracles, Xanthe felt something vast turn its attention toward the crypt — patient, familiar, and smiling.
