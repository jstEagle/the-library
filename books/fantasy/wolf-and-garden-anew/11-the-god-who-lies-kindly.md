## 11. The God Who Lies Kindly

The shrine-city of Aurelach did not sleep so much as it smoldered. Even past midnight, incense drifted through the broken colonnades, and pilgrims murmured at altars that had been cold for twelve years and were suddenly, terribly warm. Xanthe sat in the guest cell Maren Corvane had given her — a narrow room of pale stone with a window that framed a sliver of moon — and tried to carve.

Her knife would not steady. The piece of antler bone in her palm kept catching the light wrong, throwing back the white of the marks that had begun to spread across everything she touched: the mourning charms, the prayer beads, the little household gods she'd sold for coppers in Vellharrow. *Anchor*, Maren had called her. Linchpin. Living hinge between the world and whatever waited behind it.

She set down the knife.

"You're thinking too loudly," said the wolf.

He was already there — no door opening, no shadow lengthening into shape, simply Veyran where there had been empty air, grey as river fog, his eyes the color of a debt about to come due. He lay across the stone floor with his chin on his paws, the way he had lain on her workshop floor in Vellharrow a lifetime ago, which was in fact eleven days ago, which felt like another incarnation entirely.

"You don't knock," Xanthe said. "You're the god of thresholds. You could at least respect one."

"Thresholds are for crossing." His voice was warm gravel, patient as erosion. "I've already crossed. That's rather the point of me."

"Then say why you're here."

He was quiet long enough that she heard the distant sound of a pilgrim weeping at an altar two courtyards away — gratitude or terror, impossible to tell. When he spoke again, the warmth had gone out of his voice, replaced by something older and more careful.

"I woke them early."

Xanthe turned. The words took a moment to find their meaning, and when they did, they landed like a dropped anvil.

"The others," Veyran said. "Sereth. Ondrys of the deep water. Ilyane who keeps the orchards of the dead. All of them, sleeping under the seal as they have slept since your ancestors' children drove the knife of mercy through their backs — I woke them. Not the seal weakening. Not the thirteenth moon turning on its own. Me."

The room seemed to tilt. Xanthe gripped the edge of the cot and made herself breathe. "No. No — the marks started on their own. The prayers started getting answered on their own. I felt it happen—"

"You felt what I did." The wolf rose, unfolding to his full height, which was too much height for any honest animal. "Twelve years ago, you carved your first relic that sang with the dead, and something behind the seal stirred and noticed. Do you know how rare that is, Xanthe Whitlock? A living Anchor who can *hear*? The last ones were deaf hinges. They held the door and never knew there was a door. But you — you heard us knocking before you even knew what you were. And I thought—" He stopped himself, jaw working around the sentence like a bone he couldn't crack.

"*Thought what.*"

"That if I answered just a few prayers through your hands, you might begin to understand what you are in time to matter."

*In time to matter.* She heard it again, replaying backward through every moment since the widow's charm had bloomed white in her workshop. The marks appearing only on her work. The Wardens arriving precisely when the Inquisition's attention needed redirecting. Reeve's debt, invoked at exactly the right hour. Tam's forbidden texts naming Veyran as the god who collects. Every kindness, every warning, every coincidence — all of it fitted together now with the horrible smoothness of joints in a well-made skeleton.

"You used me," she said. Her voice came out very flat, very quiet, the way it did when she was angriest. "From the first mark. From the first answered prayer. You wrote yourself onto my carvings so the whole city would look at my work, so the Wardens would hunt me, so I'd run straight into the arms of the one god offering protection. You built the maze and then walked me through it calling it a road."

"Yes," said Veyran.

Not *you misunderstand*. Not *it wasn't like that*. Just *yes*, offered up plainly, the way a debtor lays coins on a table. Somehow that was worse than any lie.

"Do you know what they do to people like me here?" She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "Do you know what the Inquisition does to Anchor-blood? They burn the body and salt the ash and grind the bones into church foundations so the magic dies *in the mortar*. My parents died hiding what I am. Twelve years I starved politely in Vellharrow, carving dead grandmothers' last words into bone and telling no one, because telling anyone meant exactly this — gods and Wardens both deciding what I'm *for*. And you knew. You knew from the beginning, and instead of telling me, you—"

"Instead of telling you, I gave you proof," Veyran said, and now his voice had edges. "Because I have told the truth before, Xanthe. To Anchors. To priests. To mortal kings with good hearts and short spines. And do you know what truth does to a person carrying a secret that heavy? It breaks them. They flee, or they confess, or they kneel to whoever promises to carry the weight for them — and the seal fails anyway, only messier, with more graves. So this time I chose differently. I let the world reveal itself to you mark by mark, prayer by prayer, until you couldn't unsee it."

"You gambled with my life."

"I gamble with everything." For a moment the wolf-shape flickered, and she saw beneath it — not a man, but something vast and weary, a figure standing at a door that had been shut against him for twelve hundred years, his hand pressed flat against wood that would not open. Then it was gone and he was only a wolf again, enormous and tired. "That is what gods do. It is also, if you're being honest, what mortals do. Your smuggler friend risks his neck on every run because he gambles the Wardens are slower than he is. Your healer wagers her hedge-remedies against death itself every time she sets a splint. The difference is that my stakes are larger, so you call it betrayal."

"The difference," Xanthe said, "is that Tam and Odile get to choose their gambles. Nobody stacked the deck before they were born."

Veyran's ears flattened — the closest thing to flinching a god permitted himself.

"And what," he said softly, "would you have chosen? If I had come to you twelve years ago, or twelve days ago, and said: *you are the last anchor binding nine sleeping gods out of the world, and in thirteen moons the lock fails unless someone reforges it, and reforging it means dying willingly on a stone while your friends watch — oh, and the alternative is war.* Would you have believed me? Would you have done anything but run, and been caught, and been killed by men like Halvard Reeve following orders?"

Xanthe opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the terrible, humiliating answer was that he was right — she would have run. She had spent twelve years running from a gift smaller than this truth by a factor of ten thousand.

"That doesn't make it kind," she managed. "Lying kindly is still lying."

"No," Veyran agreed. "It makes it necessary. Those are not the same thing, and I have never pretended otherwise. I am not a kind god, Xanthe. I am a god of debts and thresholds, and I pay what I owe and collect what I'm owed, and right now what I owe you is the truth entire, whether it unmakes your trust in me or not. So here it is, without ornament."

He stepped closer. This close, she could see that his eyes were not simply grey but layered, like ice over deep water, like a city seen through fog.

"The seal was not merely a prison. It was a bargain gone rotten. The old terms require a willing death to renew — your death, willingly given, on the thirteenth turn. Sereth knows this, and Sereth has decided that if the seal must be renewed by blood, better to spend yours spectacularly and take the world back in the same stroke. The Inquisition has reached its own conclusion and will arrive at its own violence. And I—" his voice roughened, "—I woke them early because I believe there is a third term. A new bargain. But bargains require two willing parties, and I could not offer the old gods a willing party I did not have. So I went looking for one. I went looking for *you*."

"And if I refuse? If I refuse to die, refuse to fight, refuse your third way — what happens to the maze then, wolf?"

"Then the thirteenth moon turns, the seal fails, and Sereth gets her war, and I lose." He said it without drama, without plea. "And everyone you love loses with me. I won't pretend otherwise. I have done enough pretending."

Silence filled the cell like rising water. Outside, the moon had moved; the sliver of light had crawled from the floor to the foot of the cot. Somewhere below, a bell tolled for morning prayers that wouldn't come for hours — some novice unable to sleep, ringing faith into the dark.

Xanthe looked down at her hands. Carver's hands, scarred and nicked, hands that had fed her honestly for twelve years by listening to the dead. Hands that, according to a high priestess and a wolf-god, had been bred for one purpose across a thousand years of careful marriages and quiet deaths. Even her refusal, she realized, was a move on someone's board. Sereth wanted her martyrdom. The Inquisition wanted her execution. Maren wanted her sacrifice. Veyran wanted her consent. Everyone wanted something from the same pair of hands, and none of them had asked first.

Except—

"You could have forced it," she said slowly. "Couldn't you? An Anchor who hears. You could have driven me mad with voices, worn me down, hollowed me into whatever vessel you needed. Gods have done less to mortals for less."

Something moved behind Veyran's eyes — pain, or its divine equivalent.

"Yes," he said. "I could have. And I want you to hold onto that fact, Xanthe Whitlock. Hold onto it harder than you hold your anger at me. Because when Sereth comes to you — and she will come — she will offer you truths dressed in honesty, and she will tell you that at least she doesn't manipulate, at least she shows you the knife. And she will be right, and it will still be a knife. I lied to you kindly because the alternative I refused was to treat you as material. Remember that distinction. It may be the only thing that saves anyone."

"You still lied."

"I still lied." He lowered his great head, and for a heartbeat he looked less like a predator than like a dog waiting to be struck. "Hate me for it if hate keeps you upright. Use the anger. Anger is honest fuel. Only—" and here his voice dropped to nearly nothing, "—don't let it make your choices for you. Everyone else in this story will try to do your choosing for you. Don't surrender the last vote."

He was fading even as he finished speaking, grey dissolving into the pre-dawn grey of the stone walls, until only his eyes remained, two coins of ice-water light.

"Wolf," Xanthe said, before he could go entirely.

The eyes sharpened.

"When you woke them — Sereth, the others — did you know what she'd do? Did you know she'd start burning skeptics at altars, that she'd come for my throat the moment she learned my name?" Her hands were shaking. "Answer me honestly. You owe me that much, and you know I'll collect."

A long pause. Long enough for the bell to finish tolling.

"I knew she would be dangerous," Veyran said at last. "I did not know she would find you so soon. That part was not my design. That part frightens me, if it helps you to know that gods can be frightened."

"It doesn't help," Xanthe said.

"No," he agreed, almost gently. "I didn't think it would."

And he was gone, leaving only the smell of cold iron and pine, and the antler blank on the table, and the small white mark that had appeared along its grain sometime during his visit — a mark shaped, she saw with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, like nothing so much as an open door.

Xanthe picked up her knife. She did not carve the widow's charm or the prayer beads or anything Maren's church might want. She carved, instead, a single figure into the antler: a woman standing at a threshold, one hand raised, neither inviting nor barring — *deciding*. And as the last line of the cut came clean, she understood three things with perfect, terrible simplicity.

First: Sereth was coming for her, and soon.

Second: everyone she loved was now a piece on someone's board, and the only way to protect them was to stop being a piece herself.

Third: she needed to learn everything the seal could do — every rite, every clause, every hidden term — before the thirteenth moon turned. Which meant going back to the one person in Aurelach who knew them all.

She wrapped the antler in cloth, tucked it under her arm, and went to find Maren Corvane — knowing, even as she knocked, that the priestess's door would open before her fist reached the wood, because somewhere in this city, a goddess with amber eyes was already counting the days.
