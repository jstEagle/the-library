## 12. Wardens Descend

The order came at dawn, written in the Inquisitor's own hand, and Halvard Reeve read it three times before his eyes would accept it.

*By authority of the Grand Inquisitor of Vellharrow: all heretical assemblies in Aurelach to be purged. The Anchor, if identified and not contained by nightfall of the twelfth turn, is to be executed where she stands. The seal must fail clean or not at all.*

Clean. That was the word they used. As if a woman's death were a wound that needed cauterizing, as if murder could be tidy if you gave it the right name. Reeve folded the parchment along its creases until it was a small hard square in his fist, and stood on the wall of the Warden garrison at Aurelach watching the shrine-city wake below him.

It was beautiful, which made it worse. Morning light came off the pilgrim road in a long gold ribbon — thousands of them now, more every day, farmers and fishwives and children with candles cupped in their palms like caught moths. They came because the altars answered. Because for the first time in twelve years, when a mother knelt at a wayside stone and begged for her fevered son, something *listened*. Reeve had watched it happen two days past: an old man at the fountain-shrine of the Threshold, praying not for himself but for rain, and the sky had opened within the hour, gentle and gray and merciful. The crowd had wept. The old man had wept. And Reeve, standing in his white-and-iron uniform among them, had felt nothing answer him at all. He had prayed every night since he was six years old. He had given the church his body, his vows, his whole obedient life. The silence had never once broken for him.

Now the Inquisition wanted to kill the reason it was breaking for everyone else.

"Commander." Sister Vess at his shoulder, young, earnest, her warden's blade polished to a shine that hurt the eye. "The cordon's set. South gate, east quarter, the shrine district. We move on your word."

"On whose word do we move," Reeve said quietly, "yours or mine?"

She blinked. "Yours, Commander."

"Then remember I said this." He turned to face her, and the square of parchment sat between them like a dead thing on the table of his open palm. "Today we are going into a city full of people who have done nothing but pray. Some of them will run. Some of them will fight us. Most of them will kneel, because kneeling is what they know." He met her eyes. "When they kneel, you will not strike them. Whatever you see today, whatever you're ordered to do — the moment you stop seeing a person and start seeing a target, come find me. Understood?"

"Yes, Commander." A pause. Her jaw tightened. "And the Anchor?"

Reeve looked out over the city again. Somewhere down there, in a tangle of burning streets or a smuggler's safehouse or a priestess's crypt, Xanthe Whitlock was hiding with bone-dust on her hands. He had known her since she was nine years old, small and furious behind her father's workbench, the night he'd been ordered to arrest her family and instead had looked away just long enough for a child to vanish into the dark. Twenty years of looking away. Twenty years of a debt compounding like interest, and today the note had come due with the Inquisitor's seal stamped across it.

"If she can be contained," he said, "she'll be contained."

He did not say what happened if she couldn't. Vess read it anyway, in the flatness of his voice, and said nothing more.

* * *

They came through the south gate at midmorning, boots striking cobbles in unison, and Aurelach learned what was happening to it the way cities always learn — one scream at a time.

The purge was methodical, because Reeve made it methodical. Wardens in columns of six. No looting. No torches except for the forbidden texts, and even those burned reluctantly in his presence, because he found himself checking each title first, finding excuses to set half of them aside. Altars were toppled. Shrines were chalked with the sigil of suppression. Pilgrims were driven from the temple squares with shield-buttocks and shouted orders rather than steel — and mostly, in the early hours, it worked. Mostly they ran, and mostly no one died, and Reeve moved through the smoke and shouting feeling like a man walking on river ice, listening to it crack beneath each step.

But the gods were awake now, and awake things defend themselves.

It began at the fountain-shrine of the Threshold. A warden squad moved to topple it and the water rose against them — not violently, not with malice, but rising in a slow silver column, curving like a question, and where it touched the men they staggered back weeping, hands pressed to their faces, whispering names of their dead. Reeve watched a hardened killer named Doss drop his mace and sit down in the flooded square, sobbing his brother's name, a brother dead eleven years, and the water held him like a hand on the back of the neck, gentle as a mother's.

"Pull back from the shrines," Reeve ordered. "Chalk them. Don't touch the water. Don't touch anything that answers."

"But Commander—" Vess started.

"*Don't.*"

The word cracked out of him harder than he intended, and around them the wardens went still, looking at their commander standing ankle-deep in holy water with a grief that wasn't his soaking through his boots. Reeve straightened. Somewhere in his chest, under the armor, under twenty years of doctrine, something had begun to lean — slowly, inevitably, like a tree whose roots the flood has undermined.

Because here was the fracture, and it was this: the church taught that magic was theft. That every answered prayer was a coin stolen from the natural order, a debt taken without consent, and the Wardens existed to collect. Reeve had believed it the way stone believes in gravity. But he had watched the fountain give an old man rain, and there had been no theft in the old man's face — only the naked, unbearable gratitude of someone who had stopped expecting to be heard. And Reeve knew, with a certainty that felt like falling, that if the water had risen for *him*, if one prayer in thirty years had been answered, he would have knelt in that square and never gotten up again.

The Inquisition wasn't fighting theft. It was fighting envy, armed and armored and marching in columns of six.

* * *

By late afternoon the city was burning after all — not by his order, but fires start easily when frightened men with blades meet frightened crowds with faith, and somewhere in the east quarter a warden panicked and a pilgrim died and then the whole day tilted into ruin. Smoke stood up from the shrine district in black pillars. Bells rang from the great basilica until the wardens silenced them. Reeve walked the streets with ash settling on his shoulders like gray snow, giving orders, countermanding orders, saving what he could, and all the while part of his mind worked the other problem, coldly, patiently:

Where would she be?

Not the temple district — too obvious, too guarded even now. Not fleeing; she wouldn't flee while others stayed behind to burn. She'd go toward the danger, toward whoever needed her. Bone-carver. He remembered her hands, small and precise, turning scraps of antler into small mercies. He remembered her father, too — quiet man, careful man, who had looked at Reeve across a workbench the night of the raid and said only, *You were a boy once. Remember what that cost.* And then had let himself be taken so his daughter could run.

The Maren Corvane connection. The high priestess had gone silent since the purge began; her acolytes scattered; but the old records Reeve had pulled before departure listed a reliquary under the basilica's north cloister, deconsecrated forty years ago, sealed, forgotten — exactly the kind of place a woman raised on secrets would choose.

He went alone. He told himself it was command prudence, that a commander didn't bring witnesses to what might need doing quietly. He told himself a great many things, walking through the burning city, and none of them were true, and he knew it, and kept walking.

* * *

The reliquary door was oak and iron, and it was already open.

Inside: dust, dark, the smell of cold stone and older incense. Racks of empty relic-shelves climbing into shadow. And at the far end, lit by a single shuttered lantern, four figures around a table strewn with bones — the priestess Corvane, tall and gray-eyed and utterly unafraid; a broad-shouldered smuggler with a knife already half-drawn; a hedge-witch with wild hair and a healer's satchel clutched like a shield; and Xanthe.

She looked up at the sound of the door, and the lantern light caught the bone-white marks on the carving in her hands — and on her wrists, faint as old scars, marks that hadn't been there when she was nine years old.

"Wardens," the smuggler said. "Tam, get behind me— no, Odile—"

"Everyone stay calm," Reeve said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. His hand rested on his sword hilt. Protocol lived in his muscles: announce, demand surrender, contain or execute. The order was in his breast pocket. The Anchor, if not contained by nightfall, executed where she stands. Nightfall was perhaps two hours off. Containment required a warded cell the Inquisition had built in Vellharrow and never transported here, because they had never intended to transport her anywhere at all.

Xanthe rose slowly from the table. Up close he could see how exhausted she was, how stretched-thin, a wire drawn to singing pitch. But her voice, when it came, was almost amused.

"Halvard Reeve. You've gotten taller."

"You haven't changed at all."

"Liar. I've changed completely. Ask anyone." She set the carving down on the table with deliberate care, showing him both empty hands, and something in the gesture — the practiced surrender of it, the ease — told him she had done this arithmetic already, days ago maybe. She had known the Wardens would come. She had known what the order would say. And she had decided, apparently, to meet it with jokes.

"The Inquisitor knows what you are," Reeve said. "The word is *Anchor*. There are documents. There's a theory of the seal, and a plan, and the plan ends with you dying at the twelfth turn so that magic dies with you. Permanently. They think it's mercy." His throat tightened. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from someone who isn't lying about why."

"I know," Xanthe said simply.

"You—"

"Maren told me. Days ago." She glanced at the priestess, who inclined her head slightly, watchful. "I've had time to get used to the idea. That's the trick of being useful to powerful people — you spend a lot of time getting used to ideas."

Behind her, the smuggler — Tam — shifted forward. "So that's it? You tracked her down to deliver the eulogy?" His knife was fully drawn now. The hedge-witch had produced something from her satchel that steamed faintly. Reeve noted all of it the way he'd been trained to, threat-assessment running automatic, and found that he genuinely did not care.

"No," Reeve said. "That's not it."

The silence stretched. Dust drifted through the lantern light. Somewhere above them the city groaned and burned, and here in the reliquary four people waited to learn whether the fifth was their death or something else.

Reeve drew his sword.

Xanthe didn't flinch, but the hedge-witch gasped, and Tam stepped bodily in front of her — and then Reeve reversed the blade and drove it point-down into the stone floor between them, where it stood humming, quivering, a white line of light along the seam it had cracked in the ancient flagstones.

"That's my oath," he said. "What's left of it. I was ordered to contain or execute you by nightfall. I'm doing neither. Which means as of this moment I am no longer a Warden of the Inquisition. I'm a traitor with a sword, and everything I do from tonight onward is treason." He looked at Xanthe, and twenty years of debt and doubt and unanswered prayer came up his throat and came out level. "You gave my mercy a purpose once, whether you meant to or not. I'm repaying it badly and too late, but I'm repaying it."

"Halvard," Xanthe said softly. "Oh, you idiot. You brave, stupid, wonderful idiot."

"They'll send for me. By morning there will be warrants, and by tomorrow night there will be hunters, and some of them will be very good." He pulled a folded map from inside his coat and laid it on the table among the bones. "There's a drainage tunnel under the west cloister, disused, not on the purge maps because I took it off them myself this morning. It comes out in the olive terraces beyond the walls. After that I can't help you — I have to stay visible, keep commanding, keep slowing them down from inside. Every hour I hold this uniform is an hour they don't know to look for you."

"And you?" Odile asked quietly, first words she'd spoken. "What happens to you?"

"What happens to traitors," Reeve said. "Eventually." He shrugged, and found that the shrug was almost light. Thirty years of carrying an institution, and setting it down — even broken, even betraying him — felt like putting down a weight he'd forgotten he was holding. "Listen to me. All of you. The Inquisitor believes killing you cleanly ends magic forever, and he has an army that believes him. The wolf-god's kind believe breaking the seal starts a war, and they have miracles that believe *them*. Everyone wants to spend you like coin, Xanthe. Don't let them. Find a third door. I don't know what it looks like. But you carved your way out of Vellharrow when you were nine years old, and you'll carve your way out of this."

For a moment she just looked at him. Then she crossed the room and did something no one expected: she took his gauntleted hand in both of hers, bone-dust and blood and all, and pressed it briefly, like sealing a bargain or a wound.

"Twelfth turn," she said. "Two turns left. If I find a third door, I'll leave it open for you."

"Don't wait for traitors," he said. "Go. Now, before the next patrol sweeps the cloister."

They went — Maren dousing the lantern, Tam gathering the bones into a sack with strange tenderness, Xanthe pausing at the door to look back at him once, a look he would carry through everything that followed, through everything he had left to carry. Then the reliquary door closed and Reeve stood alone in the dark with his sword in the floor and his order unsigned in his pocket.

He left the sword standing there. Let them find it. Let them understand.

Then he climbed the stairs into the smoke and the bells and the burning gold of evening, put his commander's face back on, and began — calmly, loudly, in full view of his officers — to order a search of the temple district. The wrong district. The first lie of his new life slid out of him smooth as oiled steel.

Above the burning city, low on the horizon, the moon was rising.

It was nearly full. One turn from the end, and counting.
