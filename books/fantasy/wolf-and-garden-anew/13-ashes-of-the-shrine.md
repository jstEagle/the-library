## 13. Ashes of the Shrine

The shrine-city burned like a second dawn.

Xanthe ran through streets she had walked only three days before, when they had been full of pilgrims and candle-smoke and the low murmur of answered prayers. Now the murmur had become screaming. Embers rode the wind in spirals, settling on her shoulders and in her hair like fat orange snowflakes, and every breath tasted of charred cedar and something worse beneath it — burnt wool, burnt hair, burnt offerings that had once been people kneeling at altars.

Behind her, Tam's hand was fisted in the back of her coat, hauling her along as much as guiding her. Ahead, Odile moved through the smoke with the sure-footedness of a woman who had spent her life finding her way to the sick and the hurt, head turning at every cry from the burning buildings.

"We can't just leave them," Odile said, for the third time.

"We can't help them either." Tam's voice was raw. "There are two hundred Wardens in this city and four of us. Do the arithmetic, Odi."

"I'm a healer."

"You're a dead healer if you stop walking."

They cut down a side street toward the tanners' quarter, where the walls were low and the watch was thin — Tam's plan, shouted over the roar of the fire back at the safehouse: out through the south, into the olive terraces, gone before the Wardens sealed the gates. It was a good plan. It might even have worked.

Then they heard the child crying.

It came from a collapsed colonnade near the fountain square, a thin, hitching wail under the crackle of flame. Xanthe felt Odile change direction before Xanthe herself did — felt it the way she felt the pull of bone under her carving knife, an inevitability, a thing already decided somewhere deeper than thought.

"Odile—" Tam began.

"Three seconds," Odile said. "I hear where it is. Three seconds and I'm back."

She ducked under a fallen lintel and was gone, swallowed by smoke.

Tam swore with a fluency that would have made his old seminary instructors weep, and pulled Xanthe toward the gap after her. But the smoke was thick as wool in there, and when they stumbled into the courtyard beyond, there was no child.

There were six Wardens.

And Odile, standing very still between them, one Warden's gauntleted hand closed around her upper arm, her satchel of herbs spilled across the scorched flagstones like the guts of a small green animal.

The lead Warden lifted his visor. He was young, Xanthe saw. Young enough that the soot on his face sat strangely against eyes that hadn't yet learned what they were looking at. He looked at Odile, then past her — at Tam, at Xanthe — with the particular hunger of a man who has been given an order he doesn't entirely understand but intends to carry out anyway.

"Xanthe Whitlock," he said.

The name dropped into the burning courtyard like a stone into a well. Xanthe heard it fall all the way down.

"The carver," the young Warden said. "By order of the Inquisitor's Office of Vellharrow, you will—"

"Let her go first," Xanthe said.

Her own voice surprised her. Steady. Almost calm. She stepped forward, away from Tam's grip, and held up her empty hands.

"You want me. Not them. Let her go and I'll walk wherever you want."

"No." Tam's hand clamped her shoulder, hard enough to bruise. "No, absolutely not, don't you dare—"

"She was only helping a child," Xanthe said, not taking her eyes off the young Warden. "That's all she was doing. You saw that yourself, didn't you? You saw her run *toward* the fire."

The young Warden's jaw worked. For one heartbeat — one — Xanthe thought she'd reached him. Then a horn sounded from the gate quarter, three long notes, and something behind his eyes shuttered closed like a shop door at curfew.

"The hedge-witch stays," he said. "Inquisitor Reeve's orders. The Anchor comes freely or the witch goes to the pyres with the rest."

*Anchor.* The word again. It followed her now like her own shadow, whispered by Maren Corvane in the ruined crypt of Aurelach's great shrine, written in Maren's careful hand on stolen pages, and now spoken aloud by a boy in armor who couldn't possibly know what it meant and yet had been ordered to use it.

Reeve. Even now. Even here. Giving orders with her name inside them.

"Xanthe." Tam's voice had gone very quiet, which was how she knew he was terrified. Quiet was Tam's register for terror; loud was his register for everything else. "Look at me. Don't do this."

"They'll burn her, Tam."

"They'll burn her whether you stand here or not! That's the whole point of leverage — it only works if you let it!" His grip tightened. "You go with them, they take all three of us, and then there is no one left who can do what you can do. No one left to—"

He stopped himself. The word hung unspoken between them: *no one left to choose.* No one left to carve the seal. No one left whose invitation could open or close the door the gods were hammering on.

The young Warden shifted. Behind him, the soldier holding Odile adjusted his grip, and Odile made a small sound — not pain, exactly. Something more controlled than pain. Xanthe knew that sound. It was the sound Odile made when she was deciding how much of her fear to show other people.

"It's all right," Odile said.

It was not all right. Her face was gray with ash except for two bright tracks where tears had cut through, but her voice was level, almost gentle, the voice she used with dying patients. "It's all right, love. Go. Both of you. I've treated half the garrison's wives in Vellharrow for things they don't want their husbands knowing about — nobody's going to burn me. I'm too useful alive."

"Odi—"

"*Go,* Tam." And then, softer, her eyes finding Xanthe's through the smoke and the falling embers: "Whatever they told you about the seal, whatever the wolf-god told you, whatever the priestess told you — none of it works if you're dead or caged. So be neither. That's your job now. That's what I need from you."

A beam collapsed somewhere behind the colonnade. The fire was coming, eating its way toward them street by street, patient as winter.

The young Warden made his choice too, pulling Odile backward toward the gate lane, his soldiers forming around her. "You have until the fire reaches this square," he called. "Then we're gone, and the witch goes with us."

And they were moving, dragging her into the smoke, and the last thing Xanthe saw of her was Odile turning once in the soldiers' grip — turning not toward Xanthe but toward Tam, and mouthing two words with terrible clarity:

*Keep going.*

* * *

They went north, because south was gates and gates were death. They went through the olive terraces in the dark, scrambling up switchback paths slick with ash-rain, and by the time the sky went gray they were high enough to see the whole shrine-city burning below them like a coal in a black dish.

Xanthe stood on the ridge and looked at it and could not move.

Three days. They had been here three days. In three days she had watched a miracle heal a blind child in the temple square while ten thousand pilgrims wept, and watched another miracle — Sereth's miracle, the huntress-god's answer to a man who dared doubt her — burn that same kind of doubter alive on the steps of a minor altar. In three days she had learned she was bred like a hound for a single purpose, that her parents had died running from the truth of her, that a god had spent twelve years grooming her life like a gardener training a vine against a wall.

And now Odile was in a cage because of it.

"She ran toward a child's cry," Xanthe said. The words came out flat, strange, like someone else's voice heard through a wall. "That's all. That's the entire crime. She heard a baby and she went."

Tam didn't answer. He was crouched at the edge of the terrace path, packing their single bag with hands that shook only slightly, and Xanthe understood suddenly that the shaking wasn't fear. It was fury being kept on a very short leash.

"If I give myself up," she said, "they trade her back. That's how it works, isn't it? Leverage. You release the hostage when you get what you—"

"Stop." Tam stood. He didn't shout. Somehow that was worse. "Stop talking like that. Stop talking like my family is a debt you can pay with your body."

"That's exactly what she is, Tam. She's a debt. *My* debt. They took her because of me, and every hour she's in their hands is—"

"Every hour she's in their hands, she's alive, because you're still free." He crossed the space between them and took Xanthe's face in both his hands, the way he did when she woke screaming from the dreams of the twelfth year, the way he did when the grief got too big for words. His palms smelled of woodsmoke and sweat. "Listen to me. I have buried everyone, Xanthe. My congregation. My god — whatever he was. My whole life, once. I know what surrender looks like from the inside. It looks like mercy and it is a trap. If you walk into that camp, Odile dies anyway — because they don't need hostages once they have the Anchor. They kill her and they kill you and the seal dies with you and everything Sereth wants comes true on Inquisition steel instead of a goddess's claw. Do you understand? Your guilt does not get to make this decision. Your guilt is the least honest part of you right now."

Xanthe's breath came ragged. Below them, a section of the shrine-city's outer wall gave way in a bloom of sparks, and even from this distance she heard it, a soft industrial sigh, like the city exhaling.

"She mouthed it," Xanthe whispered. "*Keep going.* She knew. She knew what I was going to do before I did."

"Of course she knew. She's known you for nine years." Tam's thumb traced a soot-streak on her cheekbone, wiping it away, though the ash was everywhere and the gesture accomplished nothing. "We chose each other, remember? All of us. Nobody was assigned. Nobody was bred for it. Out of everything the gods and the Inquisition built — that's the one thing they didn't build. We did."

"And I'm going to lose it."

"Maybe." He said it simply, without flinching, and somehow the honesty of it steadied her more than any comfort would have. "Maybe we lose her for a while. Maybe we lose more. But we don't get to spend each other, Xanthe. Not for gods, not for Wardens, not for guilt. That's the rule. That's the whole rule."

Above the ridge, the moon was setting — waxing, fat, eleven days short of full. Eleven days short of the thirteenth turn. Xanthe looked at it and felt the white marks on her charms like brands. Somewhere in the burning city behind them, or in a Warden camp on the road to Vellharrow, Odile was sitting in the dark with soldiers, counting the same moon.

"There's a smuggler route," Tam said at last, turning back down the ridge path. "Old salt road, north through the hills. Two days to the crossroads country. There are people who owe me favors in every village between here and the border, and some who owe me money, which makes them friendlier still." He shouldered the bag and held out his hand. "Come on. Grief walks faster than it stands."

She took his hand. They descended together into the gray morning, the burning city shrinking behind them step by step, until it was only a smudge on the horizon, then a memory, then nothing at all.

But that night, in the lee of a dry-stone wall with the stars sharp overhead, Xanthe took out the last charm she had carved in Aurelach — a small antler pendant, unfinished, meant for Odile's birthday — and held it in her palm until her fingers ached.

The bone was warm.

Not ember-warm. Not sun-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and along its surface, fine as frost-fern, a white mark was spreading — not the answering-mark of a prayer granted, but something new. A pattern she had never carved, never seen, a lattice of lines that resolved, slowly, unmistakably, into the shape of a wolf's print.

And beneath her fingers, faint as breath, she heard Veyran's voice come up through the bone like water through a well:

*They have taken what is yours. Now I will tell you what I never wanted you to learn this way — what the Inquisition truly plans for the twelfth turn. Come to the crossroads, little carver. Come quickly. And bring your knife.*
