## 14. The Court of Antlers

The crossroads found her before she found it.

Xanthe had walked out of Vellharrow three days past, following a map Tam had scratched into wax from the memory of routes no honest man should know. The road forked at a place called Hallowmere on old survey charts and nothing at all on new ones — a name scraped off maps the way names get scraped off gravestones when the family dies out. She knew it when she saw it: two roads crossing in a field of frost-burned grass, a leaning waystone at their heart carved with antlers so worn they looked like branches, like fingers, like something reaching up through the earth to scratch at the sky.

The moon stood full above it. Not the thirteenth moon — that was still two turns away — but full enough to pour silver over everything until the frost glowed like scattered bone dust.

She stopped at the edge of the field and made herself breathe.

*You can turn back,* said the coward's voice she'd carried for twelve years, the one that had kept her alive by keeping her small. *Carve your little charms. Take your bread. Let the gods knock on someone else's door.*

But there was no one else's door. That was the whole rot of it. Maren Corvane had named her Anchor with the certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant, and every white mark that bloomed on Xanthe's carvings was another prayer answered through her flesh, another thread pulled tight between her and whatever waited at this crossroads. If she didn't come to them, they would come to her. They were already coming. The marks proved it nightly.

And Tam was asleep in the hedgerow behind her, snoring softly against their shared pack, because he'd refused to let her walk into a god's court alone and then, being Tam, had fallen asleep an hour into the vigil. She'd let him sleep. Some things you had to do standing up, or not at all.

"Right," she whispered to the frost, to herself, to whatever listened. "I'm here. Someone had better be too."

She crossed the field.

The cold deepened with each step — not the ordinary cold of a Vellharrow winter but something older, a cold like the inside of a sealed tomb, like air that hadn't been breathed in a thousand years. Her breath came out in plumes that did not dissipate but hung, shaping themselves, she realized with a crawling scalp, into faint silhouettes. Figures of mist standing in rows along both roads.

The court had arrived before her after all. Or perhaps it had always been here, waiting under the frost the way seeds wait under snow.

At the waystone, the shadows gathered.

They came out of the ground itself — she watched it happen and her mind flinched from it — rising like ink through water, taking shapes that were almost shapes: a tall form crowned in birch leaves though no birch grew within twenty miles; a broad shape whose outline shimmered like heat-haze and smelled, impossibly, of bread; a small hunched thing whose presence made her teeth ache like biting foil. Seven of them. Eight. She lost count because counting required looking directly at them and looking directly at them felt like pressing her eye against a knife's edge.

And at their center, where the roads crossed, stood the wolf.

Not Veyran. She knew Veyran's grey shape the way she knew her own hands, and this was not it. This creature was white as the marks on her bone, white as winter bone itself, and it wore antlers — great branching crowns of them, sweeping back from its skull like a dead forest given legs and hunger. Its eyes were the yellow of lamplight through ice.

When it spoke, the voice was a woman's voice, and it was beautiful, and that was the most frightening thing about it.

"You are smaller than I expected."

Xanthe's knees wanted to fold. She locked them. "You're taller than I expected," she managed. "I was told gods came as wolves."

"Wolves are my brother's habit. He hides in them. I do not hide." The white wolf stepped forward, and the mist-court bowed as one, a wave of silhouettes folding like wheat. "I am Sereth. Goddess of the hunt, of the red chase, of the moment the arrow leaves the string. You have heard my name even in your outlaw city, if only in the songs your church forbade."

"I've heard it." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "They burned the songs."

"Yes." Something moved beneath the beauty of Sereth's expression — not quite a smile. "Your church is thorough. It has had eleven centuries of practice."

Eleven centuries. The number landed in Xanthe's chest like a stone dropped down a well. In Odile's cottage kitchen, Tam had read to her from his forbidden texts by candle stub light, and she remembered the dates now, remembered how old the silence was. *The Quiet Years,* the smuggled books called them. Eleven hundred years of prayers falling on nothing.

"They weren't falling on nothing anymore, were they," she said quietly. "The prayers. Lately."

"No." Sereth circled her, slow, the antlers carving arcs against the moon. The mist-court turned their heads to follow, dozens of faceless faces tracking her like spectators at an execution. "For eleven hundred years your ancestors' seal held us beneath the world like stones over a fire, smothering us to embers. And then—" the wolf paused, directly in front of her, close enough that Xanthe could see the moonlight moving *inside* the creature's pale fur, like light through milk— "something began to crack."

"The seal weakens. I know. Thirteen turns."

"You know what the wolf-god has told you. He tells truths the way a moneylender gives credit — always less than the full sum, always expecting interest." Sereth's lip curled from teeth longer than Xanthe's fingers. "Did he tell you what the seal is made of?"

"Stone. Bone. Rites." She hesitated. "A willing death."

"A death, yes." The word fell between them like an axe head. "Every generation of your line has died to feed it. Do you understand what you are yet, little carver? Or does Veyran still keep you fed on crumbs?"

"I'm the Anchor." The word tasted strange in her mouth, a word she'd known for six days and had been living inside for twelve years without knowing. "Maren Corvane said—"

"Maren Corvane knows the church's version. The church's version is that Anchors are chosen. Blessed. Called." Sereth laughed, and the sound was the sound of ice cracking on a river, beautiful and ominous all at once. "Shall I tell you what you are? Truly?"

The mist pressed closer. Xanthe became aware that her heartbeat was audible in the silence — loud, mortal, absurdly human in this company. But she thought of Tam sleeping in the hedgerow. Of Odile waiting in Vellharrow, stitching poultices and pretending not to worry. Of Reeve's face the day he'd spared her, twelve years ago, a boy-Warden looking down at a child covered in bone dust who should have been arrested and wasn't, and never explaining why.

Her people had taught her one thing well: how to stand still when standing still was the bravest option.

"Tell me," she said.

"You were bred."

The words went through her like a blade through soft wood — clean, fast, and then the awful delayed splitting.

"Not chosen. Bred. As one breeds cattle, or hounds, or — forgive me — hunting birds. The first Anchors were made in the last years of the war, when your mortal ancestors understood they could not kill us but might imprison us. A god cannot be bound by stone. A god cannot be bound by rite. But a god can be bound by *blood* — by a living line of flesh carrying our essence thinned and threaded through generations, each life a knot in the net." Sereth's eyes held hers, unblinking. Lamplight through ice. "Your mother was such a knot. As was hers, backward through forty generations. Each Anchor born, each Anchor grown, each Anchor dying willingly at the seal-stone to weave her years into the binding. That is the mercy your history books will never record: the seal is not stone. The seal is a graveyard of women."

The field tilted. Xanthe reached for the waystone and her hand met cold antler-carved granite, and she gripped it because otherwise she would fall in front of a goddess and she would rather die.

Her mother. Quiet Marta Whitlock, who'd sung off-key and burned the stew and taught her daughter to carve by guiding her small hands around a knife. Who had died — how had she died? Fever, they'd said. A fever in autumn, quick and strange.

*A fever,* Xanthe thought, *or a rite.*

"She didn't die of sickness." Her voice cracked on it.

"She died at a stone much like this one, two nights before the Wardens burned your village. She wove her thirty-one years into the seal as every Anchor before her had done, and she bought the world another generation of quiet." Sereth's tone did not soften — it couldn't, Xanthe sensed; pity was a tool the hunt-goddess had never needed to sharpen. "And then she hid you. Broke pattern. Refused to surrender her daughter to the next rite. Do you know why?"

"She loved me."

"She feared for you. Love, fear — from outside they look alike." The white wolf paced a slow half-circle. "And she had help hiding you. A god who chose mortality, who put aside his divinity to walk in flesh, who lay with an Anchor knowing what her daughter would be — the last knot in the net, the strongest, because divine blood makes the binding iron. Your father gave up heaven to keep you from the altar. And then the Wardens found him anyway, and he burned for it, and you were left with a gift you could not explain and a city that would kill you for it."

The frost. The singing off-key. The burned stew. *My father was a god.* The sentence refused to assemble itself into anything real. She stood at a moonlit crossroads being told her blood was iron in a divine prison, and somewhere underneath the vertigo, a small vicious thought surfaced:

*Veyran knew.*

Had known since the workshop. Had spoken to her of doors and thresholds while sitting on the knowledge that she was the lock itself. Every careful omission, every crumb withheld — she could hear Sereth's mockery of him now, *he tells truths the way a moneylender gives credit.*

"Why tell me this?" she asked. Her voice sounded scraped raw, distant, like it belonged to someone else. "What do you want from me?"

"What any hunter wants." Sereth stopped before her, and for a moment the goddess's shape flickered — the wolf-form thinning like smoke, showing something vast and crowned beneath, something with too many eyes and a beauty that hurt — before the white fur closed over it again. "The seal fails at the thirteenth turn whether you act or not. Veyran will have told you that too, and that at least is true. Two turns remain. You may reforge the binding — die at the stone as your mother died, as forty mothers died, buying the world another quiet generation. Or—"

"Or."

"Or you may let it break. And we return." The mist-court stirred, a ripple running through the silhouetted ranks, and Xanthe heard it now — heard what she'd taken for wind. Voices. Hundreds of voices, whispering from the mist like a congregation murmuring through a wall. Hungry voices. "We return, and we take back what was stolen. Eleven hundred years, little carver. Eleven hundred years of your species growing fat in a garden built on our graves. You call it peace. We call it theft."

"It wasn't theft." The anger arrived before the fear did, which was how Xanthe's anger usually worked. "I asked the dead. I spent a night listening to the bones of people who lived through the sealing — farmers, soldiers, children. Your war burned the world for a century. Rivers ran black. There were places where the sky didn't clear for a generation. Your own worshippers sealed you, Sereth. Their descendants built the church. The seal wasn't a prison. It was a *mercy*, and everyone agreed on it except the ones it imprisoned."

Something moved in Sereth's eyes. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe — the look of a hunter seeing prey show teeth.

"You have been listening to corpses," the goddess said. "How fitting. Corpses are honest, aren't they? They have no future to lie about." She stepped closer, and the cold rolled off her in waves, frosting the breath in Xanthe's lungs. "Yes. The war was terrible. Wars are. Your kind started it — did your corpses mention that? We demanded worship, and your ancestors decided worship was tribute, and they rose up against their own creators. We fought because we were *owed*. And when we lost, they did not kill us — they could not — so they buried us and called it mercy and sang hymns about their own virtue for eleven centuries while we starved in the dark."

"And the answer is to burn the garden again."

"The answer is to take back what is ours." Sereth lowered her great head until the antlered skull was level with Xanthe's face, close enough that she could see her own terrified reflection in the goddess's yellow eye, tiny and upright and refusing to step back. "Here is the truth your Veyran will never give you plainly, so I will: you are the door, Anchor. Your death relocks it. Your *willingness* to break it opens it wide. The choice has always been yours — that is what makes your line so valuable. The seal answers consent, not force. Which means everything now depends on what you want."

"And if I want neither?" Xanthe's voice shook. She let it shake. "If I refuse the rite and refuse the war?"

Sereth smiled. It was the worst thing Xanthe had ever seen.

"Then the seal breaks anyway, at the thirteenth turn, unshaped by anyone's hand. And we rise ragged and starving, and we take what we need, and the world learns why its ancestors preferred the mercy." The goddess straightened. Around her, the mist-court began to thin, dissolving back into the frost, the whispering fading like a tide going out. "Think on it, daughter of the gate. You have two turns. Spend them wisely — and know that I am patient. Patience is the longest hunt of all."

The white wolf turned. The antlers caught the moon one last time.

"One more thing." Sereth glanced back, and for a single instant the ancient fury in her face flickered into something almost gentler, almost sad. "Your father's name was Aldous. He played the fiddle badly and loved your mother hopelessly and chose to burn rather than give you up. Whatever else you decide, little carver — decide knowing you were loved by something that gave up eternity for it."

Then she was gone, and the frost was only frost, and the crossroads was only a crossroads, and the moon hung empty over the field.

Xanthe stood gripping the waystone for a long time. Long enough for her hands to go numb. Long enough for dawn to grey the eastern sky and the mist-silhouettes to be revealed, in the honest light, as nothing but fence posts and leaning stones worn smooth by forgotten travelers.

Behind her, in the hedgerow, Tam stirred and sat up, blinking, hair wild.

"Well?" he called softly. "Did they show?"

"They showed." She walked toward him on legs that felt borrowed. Her mind kept circling one fact like a tongue finding a broken tooth: *bred, not chosen. Forty mothers in a graveyard. A father who played the fiddle badly.*

Tam's smile faded as he read her face. He got to his feet slowly. "Xanthe. What happened?"

"They told me what I am." She took the pack from him and slung it over her shoulder because her hands needed work, any work, or they'd start shaking. "And Tam — they told me who my father was."

His eyebrows went up. She opened her mouth to say it — and stopped, because across the frosted field, at the very edge of hearing, she caught a sound that did not belong to morning birdsong or waking wind.

Howling. Far off to the north. Grey-voiced. Familiar.

Veyran was coming, and after what she'd learned tonight, she no longer knew whether she wanted to run to him or from him.

"Walk," she said, and set her face south, toward home, toward whatever came next. "Fast."
