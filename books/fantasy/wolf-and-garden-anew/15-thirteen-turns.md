## 15. Thirteen Turns

Maren Corvane had stolen many things in her sixty-one years — candles, secrets, a silver reliquary once, when she was young and certain the church had no right to it. But she had never stolen herself out of her own cathedral before.

She did it at dawn, when the bells of Aurelach were still ringing the hour and every eye in the sanctuary was turned toward the east window, where the light came through the new glass like honey poured over glass. The pilgrims had tripled in a fortnight. They knelt three deep at altars that had been cold for twelve generations, weeping, singing, leaving offerings of bread and buttons and baby teeth because they had nothing else to give. The gods were answering. Maren could feel them under her feet like a second heartbeat, vast and half-asleep, pressing up against the floor of the world.

And she knew — she had known since the night the white marks bloomed on Xanthe Whitlock's carvings like frost on a window — that everything the church had taught her about this moment was a lie told by frightened men who never expected to be alive when the truth came due.

She wore a pilgrim's grey cloak over her vestments. She carried one bag: the Anchor codex, wrapped in oilcloth; a knife her grandmother had used for gutting fish; and nothing else. Not even her ring of office. She had left it on the high altar, set precisely in the center of the sun-disc, where the Provost would find it and understand exactly what she intended.

The gate guards let her pass without a glance. That was the great joke of holiness: everyone looked at your face and saw what they expected. A tired old woman going out to pray among the ruins. No one looked twice at the hands that had held the rite of severance for thirty years.

Outside the walls, the world smelled of rain and woodsmoke, and for the first time since she was nine years old, Maren Corvane breathed without counting the cost.

* * *

She found them in an abandoned mill two miles down the Pilgrim Road, where the river bent away from the highway into alder country. Tam had chosen it well — one door, one window, sightlines down both banks of the stream. The smuggler's instincts were good. It was his theology that worried her.

She knocked the way Xanthe had told her to knock, three quick and one slow, and stood with her hood back so they could see her face in the grey light. When the door cracked open, Odile's hedge-witch eyes went from her face to the oilcloth bundle in her arms and back again.

"You actually came," Odile said. "Tam owes me a copper."

"I said you'd come," Tam called from inside. "I said no high priestess alive would walk out on her own cathedral. I stand corrected. I hate being corrected before breakfast."

Maren stepped inside. The mill was dim and dry, dust hanging in the shafts of light from the boarded windows, and in the far corner — where the old grinding stones had been dragged together into something like an altar by someone who missed having altars — sat Xanthe Whitlock, whittling a piece of antler into nothing in particular, watching Maren with the level gray gaze of a woman who had already decided not to trust her and was willing to be proven wrong only slowly.

"High Priestess," Xanthe said.

"Not anymore." Maren set the bundle down on the grinding stone and unwrapped the oilcloth. "As of this morning, I'm just an old woman with a book nobody's supposed to read."

The codex was small — smaller than it should have been, given what it held. Black leather gone soft as cloth with handling, its spine stitched with sinew, its pages made of vellum so thin the words showed through from both sides like veins through skin. Maren had been its keeper for three decades. She had read it in secret more times than she had taken communion, which was itself a heresy she'd stopped bothering to feel guilty about.

"What is it?" Xanthe asked. She hadn't moved from her corner, but she had stopped carving. Her whole body had gone still the way prey goes still — or the way a carver goes still before the first cut.

"The Rite of the Anchor," Maren said. "The true one. Not the version in the seminary libraries, not the sanitized fragment the Inquisition permits. The whole of it." She turned the book so the title page faced the room. The letters were old Vellish, pressed deep by a scribe who had wanted them to survive fire, flood, and forgetting. *Of Her Who Holds the Door.* "It was written four hundred years after the sealing, by the third Anchor's confessor. He watched her die, and he wrote down everything she told him, and then he spent the rest of his life hiding the book from people who wanted it burned."

"Why?" Odile asked quietly. "Why hide it?"

"Because of what it asks." Maren looked at Xanthe, and found she could not soften it, and decided that softening it would be the crueler mercy anyway. "Because of what it requires."

* * *

They read it together through the morning, while rain began on the roof and Tam kept watch at the window and pretended not to listen and listened to every word.

The rite was simple. That was the horror of it. Four hundred years of dread had built the thing up in Maren's imagination into some baroque engine of sacrifice — blood on stone, names spoken backward, a death staged with theatrical cruelty. The truth was almost gentle. On the thirteenth turn, at the seal-stone, the Anchor spoke her lineage — mother to grandmother to the first of the line, all the way back down the root of the family tree. Then she named what she held: every god, every door, every debt. And then, with a bone knife carved by her own hand, bearing her own name, she opened her wrists over the stone and gave the seal her life as mortar.

That was all. That was everything. The willing death of one woman, freely offered, renewing the lock for another age.

"It doesn't have to be the wrists," Maren said. Her voice sounded strange to her, lecturing calm laid over something shaking. "The text is specific that the manner doesn't matter. What matters is the willingness. The seal reads intent, not anatomy." She paused. "I'm sorry. I've spent thirty years reading this in silence. Speaking it aloud is harder than I thought."

Xanthe sat across the grinding stone from her, the codex open between them, her fingers resting on the page beside a diagram of the seal-stone rendered in faded iron-gall ink. She hadn't touched the diagram. She seemed afraid to.

"And if the Anchor isn't willing?" she asked. "If she refuses?"

"Then the seal holds until the thirteenth moon turns, and fails." Maren closed her eyes, reciting. "*'And the doors shall fall as rotten timber falls, and what was shut shall walk again, and the debt of the old war shall come due against the children of the debtors.'*" She opened her eyes. "Unless someone breaks it deliberately first. Sereth's way. Kill the Anchor publicly, spill the blood unwillingly, and the shatter is — worse. Louder. The gods come through all at once, in their war-shapes, with twelve centuries of hunger behind them."

"That's what she wants," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"That's what she wants. Martyrdom as a crowbar." Maren leaned forward. "Child, listen to me. I have served the church my whole life, and the church has lied to me, and I am done defending liars. But this book is not a lie. This book is the oldest honest thing I have ever held. The women who wrote these rites — the Anchors who died by them — they weren't fools and they weren't victims. Read the marginalia. Read what Ilsette wrote, the fourth Anchor. She had children. She carved them each a charm the week before she died, and she wrote in the margin that she was not giving up her life but spending it, the way you spend anything you love, on the people it buys safety for."

Xanthe's jaw tightened. "You believe I should do it."

"I believe you're the only one who can," Maren said. "Which is different. Belief is cheap. I'm telling you what the rite requires, and what happens without it, and then —" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through — "and then I'm going to do something no priestess has ever done in the history of this office. I'm going to stop talking, and let you choose."

* * *

The rain thickened toward evening. Odile cooked something with river greens and smoked fish over a brazier, and the smell filled the mill with an ordinary human warmth that felt almost obscene against the words lying open on the stone. Tam took his turn at watch without being asked. Twice Maren caught him looking at Xanthe with an expression he probably thought was hidden — the look of a man doing arithmetic with someone else's life and hating every number.

When the dishes were cleared, Xanthe spoke.

"My parents," she said. "Did they know? About me?"

Maren folded her hands. "Your mother wrote to us once. A letter, sent through three intermediaries, unsigned. She asked whether the line could end. Whether an Anchor who never had children broke the succession cleanly." She watched Xanthe absorb that — the image of a woman carrying that question alone through the years, asking the institution that had bred her line like livestock whether she might be permitted simply to stop it. "We never answered. The Provost ruled that contact with an unregistered Anchor was grounds for excommunication. By the time cooler heads prevailed, the letter was eleven years old and your parents were dead."

"I know how they died," Xanthe said flatly. "Everyone in Vellharrow knows. Fever."

"Fever," Maren repeated. She reached into her cloak and drew out a folded paper, travel-worn, sealed with a broken stamp. "This is the Provost's own correspondence, which I stole on my way out along with everything else. Read it."

Xanthe unfolded it. For a long moment she read nothing, because her eyes had blurred, and Maren waited with the patience of a woman who had spent decades watching people receive unbearable news. Then Xanthe read it aloud, because she was the kind of woman who needed to hear a thing in her own voice to believe it.

"*'Confirm: the Whitlock woman is of the line. If she has borne a daughter, retrieve the child quietly. If she resists retrieval, arrange an accident. The twelfth Anchor's line must not lapse into unknown hands. — C.'"*

"They didn't die of fever," Xanthe said.

"No. They died running, with you hidden in a root cellar outside the city, and they bought enough time that the Wardens lost the trail." Maren's voice was steady. It cost her. "You were seven years old. You were raised by strangers who never knew what you were, which means the church failed to retrieve you, which means — child, forgive me, I need you to see the shape of this — the entire history of the last twenty-five years happened because two dead people loved you more than they feared us."

Xanthe put the letter down very carefully, the way she handled finished carvings, as if her hands might otherwise betray her. When she looked up, her eyes were dry and terrible.

"So the church killed my parents to control me. The gods are using me as a door without my consent. The wolf-god woke the others early and gambled my life on it. Sereth wants me dead in front of a crowd. The Inquisition wants me dead in private. And now you arrive with a holy book that says the only way to save the world is for me to cut my own wrists at the next full moon." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all. "Have I missed anyone who wants a piece of me?"

"The smuggler wants you alive," Tam said from the doorway, not turning around. "For what it's worth."

"It's worth a great deal," Maren said. "More than you know. Willingness, Xanthe. The seal reads willingness. Which means — and here is the part I stole myself out of my cathedral to tell you, the part I have turned over every night since I saw those marks on your charms —" she leaned forward, gripping the edge of the grinding stone, "— the rite does not say what the willingness is *for*. Only that it be true. Ilsette died willingly for her children. The first Anchor died willingly for peace. Every woman in that book chose what her death purchased. The purchase is yours to name. The text has never once dared to tell an Anchor what to buy."

Xanthe stared at her. "That's sophistry."

"It's the only crack in the wall that I have found in thirty years of looking," Maren said. "It may be nothing. It may be everything. But you asked why I came, and this is why. Not to deliver you to the knife. To tell you that the knife has never been the point. The point is the choosing."

* * *

Late that night, when the others slept and the rain had thinned to a whisper in the alders, Maren sat awake by the banked coals and found Xanthe already there, cross-legged on the mill floor, the codex open in her lap, turning the vellum pages by coal-light.

"You should sleep," Maren said.

"Two turns left." Xanthe didn't look up. "I'll sleep when I'm dead, apparently. One way or the other." She smoothed a page flat. "Read this part to me again. The lineage-speaking. Why does the seal want the names?"

Maren settled beside her, shoulder to shoulder, old bones against young ones. "Because the seal was made by mothers," she said. "Every generation of it. It isn't a lock built by kings or priests. It's a chain of women saying *mine*, naming what belongs to them, holding the door shut with the weight of everything they love. The names aren't magic. The love is. The names are just the shape the love takes."

Xanthe was quiet for a long time. Outside, the moon was rising — the eleventh moon of the count, fat and pale over the black alders, one full sister closer to the thirteenth.

"Maren," she said at last, and her voice had changed, gone quiet and precise, the voice she used when carving the fine work. "If I refuse the rite entirely — if I go to the seal-stone and do nothing, offer nothing, refuse everyone — what happens? Exactly. No metaphors."

Maren looked at her, at the firelight moving in her gray eyes, and understood suddenly, completely, that whatever this woman had already decided, it was not any of the options Maren had brought her.

"The seal fails at moonrise," Maren said slowly. "The gods come through. All of them, all at once, in whatever shapes they choose. There will be no war unless they make one — but there will be no terms either. No invitation, no consent, no anchor. Just a door falling off its hinges."

"And if I accept your rite as written?"

"The door holds for another age. And you are dead."

Xanthe nodded once, as if confirming a measurement. Then she picked up the antler she'd been whittling earlier, turned it in the coal-light — and Maren saw, with a chill that started in her spine and spread outward, that it was no longer a nothing-particular. It was a knife-blank. Unfinished. A handle waiting for a blade, and a space at the base of the handle, sanded smooth and empty, exactly the size of a name.

"And if," Xanthe said, "someone were to write a new rite?"

Far off in the dark, down where the river met the road, a wolf began to sing — low, long, and patient, like something keeping time with a clock only it could hear.
