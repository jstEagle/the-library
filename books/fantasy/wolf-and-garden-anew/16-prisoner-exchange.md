## 16. Prisoner Exchange

The cells beneath the Bastion of the Pale Flame had no windows, and Halvard Reeve had always thought that was deliberate. A man who could not see the sky could not count the days, and a man who could not count the days would eventually believe whatever he was told about how many remained.

He stood now before the door of cell nine, keys in his hand, and counted.

Twelve days since Odile Marchand had been taken from the burning shrine-city. Twelve days of interrogations conducted with a courtesy that made them worse — tea offered, questions asked gently, answers written down in a fair hand. Twelve days since he had signed, as senior Warden present, the order that authorized her detention. His signature. Still wet when he'd pressed it to the page.

The key turned with a sound like a small bone breaking.

The woman inside sat on the cot with her hands folded in her lap, as if she had been expecting him for some time. She was older than Xanthe by fifteen years, grey threading her dark hair, and she had the particular stillness of hedge-witches who had spent their lives being careful around other people's fear. Her wrists were bare. They had taken her charms on the first day — every knot, every pouch of dried herbs, every bone button that might have held a whisper of working. Without them, Reeve knew, she was simply a tired woman in a stone room.

"Warden," she said. "You've been standing out there for six minutes. I heard your boots stop."

"I know."

"You're deciding something."

"I am." He opened the door fully. Lamplight from the corridor fell across her cot, across the wall where she had scratched nothing at all — no tallies, no prayers, no marks. She hadn't even allowed herself that. "How are they treating you?"

"Politely." Her mouth curved without warmth. "Which is how I know they mean to kill me. Kind men don't bother with manners before they need something. Manners are what you give people you intend to use up."

Reeve glanced down the corridor. Empty. The night shift was thin — he had arranged it to be thin, three rotations ago, in the slow patient way a man arranges things when he is not yet ready to admit what he is arranging them for.

"Stand up," he said.

Odile did not move. "Why?"

"Because in ninety seconds I am going to walk you out of this building, and if you're sitting down when I say it, we'll lose time we don't have."

She studied him then, the way she must have studied patients — looking past the surface complaint to the thing underneath. Whatever she saw in his face made her rise, unhurried, smoothing her ruined skirt.

"They'll hang you for this," she said.

"Yes."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway."

* * *

The corridors of the Bastion were built to be walked in pairs, Warden and shadow, each man watching the other watch him. Reeve had spent twenty-three years inside that architecture of mutual suspicion and had believed it kept him honest. Walking it alone now, with a prisoner at his side and no warrant in his hand, he understood that it had only ever kept him obedient.

They passed the scriptorium, where novice Wardens copied the catechisms in their careful hands. *Magic is a debt. The Flame is the payment.* He had copied those same lines at eleven years old, tongue between his teeth, certain he was transcribing truth. The Inquisition had found him an orphan of the border wars, half-starved, and had given him bread, doctrine, and a purpose, and he had loved them for it the way children love the hand that feeds them: absolutely, and without asking what the bread cost anyone else.

He had spared a bone-carver child twelve years ago because something in her face had reminded him of himself — someone small, hiding something large, surviving in a city that paid informants in bread. He had told himself it was mercy. It had been recognition.

Now the order bearing his own countersignature sat in First Inquisitor Drevane's strongbox: *On the twelfth turn of the moon, the Anchor is to be terminated by sanctioned means, publicly, at the crossroads. The resulting collapse of the seal will be permanent. Magic will end.*

He had read it four times before he understood it. Not contain the Anchor. Not bind her, not use her, not negotiate. Kill her — not to save the seal but to break it deliberately, to let the old gods die in the dark forever, and to let every answered prayer in Vellharrow curdle into silence. The Church had stopped believing in its own god generations ago; Reeve had begun to suspect that during his novitiate, when the High Flame altar had felt cold as riverstone under his palms. But this was different. This was the institution choosing, finally and openly, to murder the last person standing between the world and either apocalypse or awakening, because both terrified it more than murder did.

And they had put his name beside the order. His signature, already on record, already implicating him. They had never intended to give him a choice. That was the moment he had understood they had made him long ago, and that the making had been a leash, and that tonight he would test whether the leash was real.

"The side gate," Odile murmured. "Not the postern. The gate wardens drink on the third watch — I can hear it from here, even without my charms. There's a bottle in the guardhouse that's been open since dusk."

"You can hear—"

"I'm a hedge-witch, Warden. My ears are the least of it." She paused at the junction ahead, listening. "Two men, east stair. We wait."

They waited in the shadow of an alcove where a statue of Saint Alveric stared blindly into the middle distance, sword raised against enemies the sculptor had never specified. Reeve found his heart was steady. That surprised him most of all. He had expected terror, or at least the sick lurch of transgression. Instead there was only a terrible clarity, like the air after lightning — the feeling of finally standing in the right place after years of standing slightly to one side of it.

"Halvard Reeve," Odile said quietly, watching the stair. "You were at the shrine purge in Aurelach. You gave the carver a head start. Don't look startled — Tam told me, and Tam hears everything the smugglers hear, and the smugglers hear everything. You've been coming apart for weeks. Why tonight?"

"Because I read the order." He said it flatly, because anything softer would be a plea. "Because I watched a beggar's prayer get answered outside the Grainmarket shrine while my own went up like smoke and came back as nothing. Because the Church I served taught me the Flame was justice, and justice doesn't execute a woman in a public square to settle its accounts." He breathed out. "Because I'm tired of being a good Warden, which turns out to be a different thing from being a good man."

The footsteps faded above them. Odile touched his sleeve — light, brief, the gesture of a healer confirming a pulse.

"Well," she said. "Better late than kneeling."

* * *

The side gate was exactly as she'd promised: unbolted, unguarded, breathing cold night air into the corridor like an opened lung. Beyond it lay the Warden training yard, then the drainage channel, then the crooked lanes of Saltrow where a smuggler with a cart and a bad leg was waiting with the patience of a man who had done illegal things in the dark so often they had become a craft.

Reeve stopped at the threshold.

Odile looked back. "This is where you leave us, isn't it. You're not coming."

"I can't. If I vanish tonight they'll tear the city apart looking for me, and they'll start with everyone I've ever been seen speaking to." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, sealed with plain wax rather than the Warden's flame. "Give this to Xanthe. Not to Tam — he'll read it first, which is fine, but she has to read it herself. All of it."

"What does it say?"

"It says the Inquisition knows what she is. They've known since Aurelach — a survivor described the white marks, and Drevane had scholars match them against sealed records from the founding of the order. Records about the Anchors. What they were bred for. How the seal works." His jaw tightened. "It says they intend to kill her at the twelfth turn. Publicly. At the crossroads where the gods are gathering. They believe a public martyrdom of the Anchor will shatter the seal faster than any rite — that the collapse will kill the waking gods mid-stride and end magic permanently. Every charm, every healing, every answered prayer. Gone. Forever. And they think the world will thank them for it."

Odile had gone very still. "They'll kill her and call it salvation."

"They'll call it the Pale Flame's final victory." He almost laughed, and it came out wrong. "I helped write the doctrine they'll cite. I was proud of it once."

"And you?" She searched his face. "What happens to you after I walk through that gate?"

"After?" He considered it honestly, the way he had been considering it all night, all week, all twenty-three years. "There's a tribunal chamber under the Bastion where traitors are questioned. I've sat on that bench eleven times. I know exactly how the chair feels from the other side, and tomorrow I'm going to find out how it feels from this one." He straightened. "Drevane will discover the escape within the hour — I'm leaving the cell door unlocked and my keys in it. He'll know immediately who did it. There won't be a trial worth the name; there'll be a formality and then a sentence, and the sentence will be fire. It always is, for our own."

"You could run. With us. Tam would take you in a heartbeat — he owes you, and more than that, he trusts you, and Tam doesn't trust anyone."

For one naked moment, Reeve wanted it so badly his teeth ached. A cart, a bad leg, a life in Saltrow with smugglers and hedge-witches and a bone-carver who heard the dead. A small warm life, hidden well, ending in bed instead of in flame.

"No," he said, and meant it. "If I run, everything I know dies with me and they win by default. But if I stay—" He exhaled. "If I stay, I stand in front of the tribunal and tell the truth. All of it. The order, the Anchor, the plan to murder a woman in a public square. Some of the younger Wardens still have consciences. I trained half of them myself. When they hear what Drevane intends, some of them will break, and broken men talk, and talked-about plans fail. I can do more damage to this from inside their fire than I ever could from a smuggler's cart."

"That's a martyr's arithmetic."

"It's a soldier's. I've been a soldier my whole life. Let me die like one — for something true, instead of something convenient."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she took his hand in both of hers, turned it palm-up, and pressed something into it: a small knot of braided cord, worn soft, smelling faintly of rosemary and ash.

"My last charm. They missed it — sewed it into my hem and I talked my way past the search, which tells you what my week's been like. It's nothing grand. A quieting. For pain, for fear." Her eyes were bright and dry. "For the chair, if it comes to that."

He closed his fist around it. It was the first magic he had ever willingly accepted, and it weighed less than a coin and more than a cathedral.

"Tell Xanthe," he said, and his voice finally cracked, just once, "that I owed her a debt from when she was nine years old, and I've been paying it wrong my entire life. Tell her—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Tell her the twelfth turn. The crossroads. They're bringing two hundred Wardens and Drevane himself, and they will not hesitate. She should not come to save me. Promise me she won't try to save me."

"I promise nothing," Odile said, "except that she'll know. Everything." She stepped backward through the gate, into the dark of the yard, and the shadows took her the way water takes a dropped stone. From somewhere in the black came the creak of cart springs and Tam's low voice saying her name with a relief he didn't bother to disguise.

Then the yard was empty, and Halvard Reeve stood alone at the threshold of the life he had chosen, holding a dead woman's charm in his fist.

He turned back toward the Bastion. Above it, rising over the rooftops of Vellharrow, hung the moon — swollen, strange, one night short of full, the twelfth turn already pulling at the tides of the world. He could feel it the way sailors feel weather. Something vast was leaning against the doors of reality, and the Inquisition meant to answer it with an executioner's blade, and there were eleven days left.

He walked back into the light of the corridor, left his keys in the lock of an empty cell, and went to find First Inquisitor Drevane.

There was a confession to make. He intended to make it loudly.

* * *

They came for him before dawn, as he'd known they would.

Four Wardens, young ones, faces he recognized — Corvin, who still prayed nightly; Asche, who'd wept at his mother's funeral; the twins Pell and Marta, who finished each other's sentences and each other's drills. They stood in his quarters while he dressed with deliberate calm, and none of them would meet his eyes except Corvin, whose jaw was set hard enough to crack.

"First Inquisitor's orders," Corvin said. "You're to be taken to the tribunal chamber. Sir."

*Sir.* Even now. Even with the irons in Pell's hands. Reeve fastened the last buckle of his coat and felt the little knotted charm shift against his chest, rosemary and ash, a hedge-witch's blessing riding over a Warden's heart.

"At ease," he said, and smiled, and meant it. "All of you. You're doing your duty. I'm about to go do mine."

They marched him down through the sleeping Bastion, past the scriptorium where the catechisms waited in their careful ink, past the statue of blind Saint Alveric with his sword raised against nothing. In the tribunal chamber the chair waited, and the brazier waited, and Drevane waited behind the high desk with the order spread before him, Reeve's own signature glowing pale in the lamplight.

"Halvard Reeve," Drevane said, without looking up. "Warden, First Class. Do you confess to freeing the prisoner Odile Marchand?"

"I confess to it," Reeve said. "And to something larger. Ask me what."

Drevane's eyes lifted at last, cold and patient as the space between stars. "What?"

Reeve set his palm flat on the desk, over the order, over the plan to murder an innocent woman at the twelfth turn, and spoke clearly enough for the young Wardens at the door to hear every word.

Outside the high narrow window, the false moon leaned closer to the earth, and somewhere across the sleeping city a bone-white mark bloomed on carved antler, and eleven days began to fall away like petals from a cut flower.
