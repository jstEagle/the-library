## 17. The Wolf's Confession

The safehouse Tam had chosen was an old tannery on the river's wrong side, all stone and salt-stink and rafters black with a century of smoke. Odile slept in the loft above, her wrists still ringed with the bruises the Wardens' irons had left, and Tam sat by the door with a whetstone and a knife he had no intention of using, because the door was guarded by a god.

Xanthe sat across from Veyran and did not sleep either.

The wolf lay where he always lay now — between her and the wall, between her and any exit — his grey bulk filling the space like a shadow that had decided to have weight. He had not spoken since they fled Aurelach. He had not spoken since Reeve's message, since she learned what the Inquisition intended to do to her at the twelfth turn. He simply watched her carve, watched her eat, watched her fail to sleep, with those pale eyes that held more patience than anything alive should hold.

Tonight, finally, she was too tired to be afraid of him.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out raw from disuse. "In my workshop, the first night. You knew I was the Anchor. You knew what Maren would tell me in Aurelach. You knew everything."

"I did."

"You could have told me."

"Yes," said Veyran. And then, after a silence long enough for the river to slap the stones twice: "And you would not have believed me. Or worse — you would have."

Xanthe set down her knife. The half-carved sparrow bone rolled against her palm, and through it she felt nothing but its own small death, the ordinary silence of ordinary bone. Even that felt like a rebuke.

"Then tell me now," she said. "Not the parts you've rationed out like bread at a siege. All of it. Why you woke them early. Why me. What you've actually been doing for twelve years while I carved charms for widows who couldn't pay." She leaned forward. "You owe me that much, wolf. You taught me about debts. So let's settle this one."

Tam's knife went still against the whetstone. Above them, the loft creaked — Odile awake, listening.

Veyran rose. It was strange how large he became when he stood, as if the room had been built around his absence and now had to apologize for it. He crossed the floor and stopped before her, close enough that she could smell cold air and old pine on his coat, and when he spoke again it was quieter than she had ever heard him.

"Sit by the fire," he said. "This is a story for sitting."

* * *

"The first thing you must understand," Veyran said, "is that we were never what your priests say we were. Not distant. Not unknowable. In the beginning we were *present* — thick in the world the way honey is thick in the comb. You could not plant a field without Sereth noticing. Could not cross a threshold without me feeling your footfall. The dead spoke freely because I kept the doors open. It was not worship, then. It was neighborliness."

The fire cracked. Xanthe found she had drawn her knees up like a child at a hearth-telling, and could not make herself care.

"And neighbors quarrel," he went on. "Over fences, over water, over pride. But a quarrel is not a war. The war began because we grew hungry in a way neighbors should never grow hungry." His lips peeled back from his teeth — not quite a snarl, not quite grief. "We demanded worship. Not love. Not gratitude. *Tribute.* We told your ancestors that the sun would rise only if fed, that rivers would stay in their beds only if praised, that their children's souls required our permission to pass the thresholds into death. And because some of it was true — because we *were* the doors, whether we liked it or not — they believed us."

"You're saying you held the world hostage."

"I am saying we convinced ourselves the world owed us rent." He turned his head toward the fire, and the light moved strangely in his eyes. "There were gods who never wanted it. Gods who wept when the first altar smoked. But the hunger spread among us the way blight spreads through wheat, and the ones who resisted were... outnumbered. Outshouted. I opposed the demand. Formally, at the Convocation of Antlers, before every throne and stump and running water in creation, I stood and called it theft." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh, if laughter could rust. "Sereth threw me down a mountain for it. I got up. That is the whole of my legend, carver: the god who got up."

"Then why does everyone fear you?"

"Because getting up is not the same as stopping it." His voice dropped. "I lacked the strength to break the hunger alone, so I did something smaller and meaner. I opened my doors wide and refused to charge. Every soul that died passed through my thresholds free, unweighed, untaxed. I became the leak in their treasury. For three hundred years I bled them quietly, one freed soul at a time, waiting for the mortals below to notice that the sky's threats rang hollow."

Xanthe thought of the dead grandmother's voice through the mourning charm, the last words given freely, no toll asked. Twelve years she had thought her gift a curse. Now she heard the shape beneath it — a god's old habit, worn down to a whisper.

"What happened?"

"What always happens when thieves quarrel over stolen goods." The firelight shuddered. "The mortals noticed. But they noticed the wrong thing. They saw the gods' power flickering — my leaking, the famine of tribute — and instead of walking away from us entirely, they decided to end the argument with knives. Your histories call it the Godswar. Ours has no name, because none of us survived it with enough dignity to name it. Cities burned. Rivers changed course. Sereth hunted whole bloodlines to extinction because one ancestor had slighted her. And the mortals — your mortal ancestors, Xanthe, the ones whose bones sit in every grave-mound from here to the sea — did the unthinkable. They stopped fighting *for* the gods or *against* them, and began building the Seal."

"The seal wasn't mercy," she said slowly. "Maren said—"

"Maren Corvane knows the version written by victors who needed to forgive themselves." Veyran's gaze came back to her, steady and terrible. "It was both things at once. That is the truth no one will give you cleanly. Yes, it ended the war. Yes, it saved the world from being fought over like a carcass. And yes, it was also a cage, and we were locked in it screaming, because mortals do not build cages for things they respect. They built it because we had proven we could not be trusted with the door."

He stepped closer, and lowered himself down until his great head was level with hers, close enough that she could see the individual silver hairs threaded through the grey.

"They needed an anchor. Something living, something bound to the world by flesh and breath, to hold the lock shut from the outside. They made the first Anchors the way you make a tool — deliberately, patiently, breeding divinity into mortal blood until it thinned to almost nothing. Almost." His nostrils flared. "Your mother was the ninth. Your father was one of us who chose mortality rather than watch another Anchor be made and spent. They thought hiding you in a city that hated magic would keep you from ever learning what you were. They were wrong, but they were not foolish. There is a difference."

Xanthe's hands had gone cold in her lap. She had known — Maren had told her, Sereth had confirmed it with that hunting-smile — but hearing it from Veyran was different. Hearing it from Veyran was like having the wound cleaned.

"So when the seal weakens," she said, "and the gods wake—"

"We wake starving," he said simply. "That is what you saw in Aurelach. The child healed, the skeptic burned. Gods waking from a thousand years of hunger do not arrive gently. Sereth wants the seal broken outright — war renewed, tribute restored, humanity put back in its place as livestock that prays. The others waver between her fury and their own exhaustion. And I—" He exhaled, a long plume of breath stirring the fire. "I woke them. Early. Deliberately. Before the seal failed on its own and they woke ravening with no voice but hers."

The words landed like a dropped stone. Xanthe sat forward. "You *what?*"

"You heard me."

"All of it. The answered prayers. The marks on my carvings. The pilgrims, the Wardens, the purge, Odile—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Odile in irons. Reeve—" She couldn't finish that one. "All of it, because you decided to gamble?"

"Because the alternative was worse." For the first time, something moved behind the god's composure — something fast and desperate, the thing underneath the patience. "The seal was failing, carver. Not in thirteen turns — that is merely when it fails *entirely*. It had been failing for years, a little more each moon, and every god it touched woke a little more starved. Left alone, Sereth breaks it within the decade, and the war that follows makes Aurelach look like a kitchen fire. But there was one variable the seal's makers never accounted for." He inclined his head, very slightly, toward her. "An Anchor with a will. Every Anchor before you was raised to obey the rite — to maintain, to sustain, and at the end, to die willingly into the lock. You were hidden. Unraised. Ungroomed. The first Anchor in history who might look at the choice and refuse both offered answers."

"Both offered—"

"Sacrifice or surrender," Veyran said. "Die into the seal and buy the world another age of imprisonment, or break it and unleash Sereth's war. Those are the only doors the old powers left standing. I have spent four thousand years trying to carve a third."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, Odile shifted, and the loft boards groaned.

"A third door," Xanthe whispered.

"A seal rewritten rather than merely maintained. A covenant rather than a cage. Worship freely given or freely withheld — faith as a gift, which is the only kind that was ever worth anything." His voice roughened. "But I cannot carve it. I am a god; the seal does not answer to my hands. It answers to yours. To an Anchor who chooses. Which is why I woke them early — to force the question while there was still an Anchor alive who might answer differently. Which is why I marked your work, so you would feel the pull and come looking. Which is why I protected Tam and Odile, why I sent you to Aurelach, why I let Maren find you." He paused. "Which is why I lied to you, and rationed the truth, and used you. I will not pretend otherwise. I am four thousand years old and I have done monstrous things in service of one idea, and I no longer fully know where duty ends and obsession begins."

Xanthe looked at him for a long time.

She thought of the widow in chapter one of her own life — the first prayer she'd ever overheard through bone, a woman begging a dead god for rain, and the rain that had come anyway, and the terror that had taught her to hide. She thought of Reeve's face when he'd handed her the head start, of Tam's knife going still against the whetstone, of Odile's bruised wrists. Everyone in her life had been moved across a board by someone larger. The Inquisition wanted her dead to end magic. Sereth wanted her dead to start a war. Maren wanted her dead to save the world. And Veyran —

Veyran wanted her alive, and had still spent her like coin.

"That's the confession?" she said. "You used me, but for a good cause?"

"No," said Veyran. "The confession is this." And he settled down onto his belly before the fire, folding his paws under him, and for the first time since he had walked out of the dark into her workshop, the god of thresholds and debts looked *small*. "When they sealed us, I could have fought. Some of us did. The ones who fought are the ones mortals remember as monsters, and they were right to remember them so. But I saw the cage coming, and I walked into it. Willingly. Because I judged that a world without us was worth more than a world ruled by us — even though I was one of the us." His ears flattened. "Four thousand years in the dark, carver, holding the door shut from the inside. Do you know what that is like? To be imprisoned beside the very gods whose crimes you tried to stop, blamed equally with them, forgotten equally with them? To know you were right and have rightness count for nothing?"

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. She had hidden her gift for twelve years in a city that paid informants in bread — twelve years of swallowing truth, of carving silence into bone, of being right about what she was and having it count for nothing.

"I know exactly what it's like," she said quietly.

Veyran's pale eyes closed. When he opened them again, something in them had changed — or perhaps something in her had.

"I stopped seeing you as my jailer a long time ago," she said. "Somewhere on the pilgrim road, maybe. I saw you as my captor. My creditor. The thing that had hooked me and was reeling." She reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a strange dog — and laid her palm flat on his brow, between the ears, where the fur grew coarse and cold. "I don't see that anymore. What I see is two prisoners arguing over who holds the key. And I'm tired, wolf. I'm tired of everyone deciding what the key is *for*."

"Then take it from all of us," he murmured, his voice a rumble under her hand. "Take the key and decide yourself. That was always the wager. That was always the hope I dared not speak aloud, because hopes spoken aloud become demands, and I have seen what gods do with demands."

Above them the loft creaked, and Odile's voice drifted down, hoarse but dry as ever: "If the two of you are done having your moment, there's a problem at the north window, and it has torches."

Tam was already moving, knife sheathed, body low. Through the tannery's grimy glass, past the river's black ribbon, lights were assembling on the far bank — not the scattered torches of a patrol but a lattice of them, orderly, deliberate, spreading along the shore like a lit fuse.

Veyran lifted his head, nostrils working, and the sound he made was almost a growl and almost a sigh.

"Wardens," he said. "A full company. And at their center—" his ears turned, reading the wind, "—someone carrying the Inquisition's own seal. They're not searching the tanneries tonight, carver."

Xanthe rose, her heart hammering the old familiar fear, and found, strangely, that her hands were steady.

"They're looking for me."
