## 18. Twelfth Turn

### Xanthe

The crossroads had no name until the moon rose, and then it had too many.

Xanthe stood at the center of four roads that met in a field of frost-burned grass outside the walls of Aurelach's ruin, and she felt them the way a bone-carver feels grain beneath the knife: north toward the mountains where Sereth's wild places thickened into forest; south along the pilgrim road, now black with torchlight; east toward the sea; west toward Vellharrow and everything she had buried there. The twelfth moon hung overhead, swollen and bone-pale, one turn shy of fullness. One turn shy of the end.

Around her, the field was filling with people who should not have been allies.

Tam moved through the smuggler camps with his defrocked priest's cadence intact, all warmth and negotiation, pressing cups of mulled wine into hands that gripped knives. Odile worked beside him, her hedge-witch satchel open, binding wounds before they existed—charms against smoke, against panic, against the specific terror of facing an army that had been taught since childhood that what it faced was sin. The pilgrims of Aurelach had come down from the burning shrine-city by the hundreds, then the thousands, drawn by rumor of a gathering at the crossroads, and they knelt in the frost singing hymns that had been illegal for twelve years, their voices rising like heat from a forge.

And above them all, at the edges of the firelight, the gods were arriving.

Xanthe felt each one land like a needle finding a nerve. A presence like river-water pooling in her lungs. A presence like iron left out in rain. A small, sharp thing like a splinter of green wood. They came cautiously, diminished, testing the air of a world that had forgotten them—and they came because of her, because the white marks on her carvings were lanterns and every waking god was a moth.

"You're shaking." Tam appeared at her elbow, wine cup thrust into her cold hands. He didn't look at her when he spoke; he watched the southern road, where the torches had begun to organize themselves into columns. "Drink it anyway."

"I'm not afraid for myself."

"No," he said. "You're afraid you'll be made into an argument." His jaw tightened. "That's what they're all doing, love. The Inquisition, Sereth, Maren's old church, half these pilgrims—you're not a woman tonight, you're a thesis. *Kill her and magic dies. Kill her and magic lives.*" He finally turned, and his eyes were wet and furious. "I've spent my whole life watching institutions spend people like coin. I'll be damned if I watch it happen to you."

"Tam." She took his hand. His fingers were cracked and warm and utterly mortal. "Whatever happens—"

"Don't." He laughed, broken. "Don't do farewells. We've got a whole turn left. Thirteen moons, you said. We have time."

She didn't tell him what Maren had told her three nights ago, kneeling in the ruins with the Anchor rites open between them like a wound: that seals weakened unevenly, that the thirteenth turn might arrive early if enough blood touched the stone. That Sereth knew this. That Sereth had always known this.

Across the field, on a low rise crowned with dead hawthorn, Sereth waited.

The goddess of the hunt had taken a form meant to be seen by armies: tall as a banner, crowned in antlers that caught the moonlight and threw it back in shards, her cloak moving like a flock of starlings deciding. She did not look at the pilgrims. She did not look at the smugglers. She looked only at Xanthe, and her gaze had the patience of a winter that intended to outlast you.

*Come*, that gaze said. *Come and die where everyone can see.*

* * *

### Reeve

Inquisitor Halvard Reeve rode at the head of four hundred Wardens, and for the first time in thirty years of service he could not make himself look like what he was.

His white-and-grey cloak sat wrong on his shoulders. His seal of office—the twin axes of the Inquisition stamped in lead—felt heavier than lead had any right to be. Behind him rode men and women he had trained, had wept over, had once believed shared his conviction that law was the wall between humanity and the flood. Ahead of him lay a crossroads full of kneeling pilgrims and waiting gods and, somewhere in the middle of it, a bone-carver from Vellharrow whom he had spared as a child and betrayed with every kindness since.

The order sat in his breast pocket, written in High Prefect Casserly's own hand, and Reeve had read it so many times the ink was wearing thin:

*If the Anchor cannot be contained, she is to be put to death before the thirteenth turn, publicly, that her martyrdom may shatter the seal and end the age of magic entire. God's peace on the instrument of His final victory.*

*God's peace*. Reeve had recited the words of office that morning and heard nothing in them but wind through a dead man's ribs. When had his prayers gone quiet? Not all at once—he knew that much. Quietly, the way a hearth goes cold while you sleep, until you wake in a room you don't recognize. And all the while, beggars' prayers had been answered in bread and healing and small impossible mercies, while his own petitions rose like smoke into a ceiling that did not exist.

Beside him, Warden-Captain Ilse Varga reined closer. Young, devout, sharp-eyed. She had been watching him for days; he'd have had to be blind not to see it.

"The pilgrims are armed," she said. "Smugglers, too. The ones in leather with the red scarves—they fight for coin, usually. Why here?"

"Because someone gave them something better than coin," Reeve said.

"Which is?"

"A reason." He let the word sit. Varga frowned at the field, at the fires, at the antlered silhouette on the hill that made half his Wardens cross themselves with the old sign, the one the church had forbidden and never quite managed to kill. "Captain. If the order changes tonight—if what we're asked to do stops being law and becomes something else—where do you stand?"

Varga looked at him for a long moment. "Where the law stands, Inquisitor. Where else?"

*Yes*, Reeve thought grimly. *That's exactly what I would have said. That's exactly what I said, twelve years ago, standing in a doorway in Vellharrow with a child's carving-knife at my feet and orders to burn the workshop.* He had spared her. He had told himself it was mercy. It had been the last honest act of his career, and he had spent every year since paying interest on it.

The column crested the last ridge, and the crossroads opened below them: thousands of candle flames, the hymn rolling up like surf, and at the center, small and straight-backed, a woman with bone-white dust on her hands standing alone in the trampled grass.

Reeve's chest hurt. He realized he was praying again—for the first time in months—not to any god, but to her. *Walk away, Xanthe Whitlock. Take your family and run, and damn us all.*

But she didn't walk away. She lifted her chin, and even at this distance he saw her raise one hand—not in defiance, not in blessing, but the way a carver signals a pause before the cut.

And on the hill, Sereth began to descend.

* * *

### Xanthe

Sereth crossed the field the way frost crosses a window: everywhere at once, and suddenly simply *there*, ten paces away, close enough that Xanthe could smell pine sap and old blood.

"Little anchor." The goddess's voice was many voices braided—hunt-horn, wolf-call, the creak of a bow drawn and held. Around them, the field went silent in widening rings, as if sound itself had been given the choice of fleeing. "They've come to kill you. Your Inquisition. Four hundred axes and a moon still shy of turning. Shall I tell you what happens if their blades find you here, under my sky, before the thirteenth night?"

"I know what happens," Xanthe said. Her voice carried further than it should have; she felt the field listening through her, the way the dead listened through bone. "You've told me. Martyrdom shatters the seal faster than patience. You've wanted me dead since the day we met."

"I have wanted you *free*." Sereth's starling-cloak rippled. "There is a difference. You were bred for chains, child—your mother died in hers, and hers before her, generation upon generation nailed to a stone so that mortals could pretend the world had no teeth. Every year you hold the seal, you hold the leash on your own throat. I offer you the only death that has ever meant anything: one that ends the chaining forever."

"And gives you your war."

"And gives the world its *gods*." For an instant, something moved behind Sereth's eyes—grief, maybe, old as the seal itself. "You think I want slaughter for its sweetness? I remember the war, little anchor. I remember what we were before it, and after. But your kind sealed us away rather than kneel, and mine swore oaths in blood, and now both oaths are coming due, and you stand in the middle holding the ledger." She extended one hand, long-fingered, tipped in moonlight. "Or—there is a third road. Kneel to me now. Renounce the stone. Open it willingly, and no blood need be spilled tonight, and I will call off what comes up that southern road."

Xanthe looked at the offered hand. She thought of her mother, whose face she knew only from a locket-bone she'd carved herself. Of Maren saying, *the rite requires a willing death, and I believe yours is owed.* Of Veyran, somewhere at the field's edge in his grey wolf-shape, watching, always watching, having gambled everything on her without ever once asking her leave.

She thought of Tam's words: *you're not a woman tonight, you're a thesis.*

"No," Xanthe said.

The silence deepened.

"I won't kneel to you, and I won't die for you, and I won't die for them either." She raised her voice, and it rang strangely, doubled, as though something vast spoke through her whether she willed it or not. "Everyone on this field wants to spend me. My death to end magic. My death to start a war. My sacrifice to keep a dying world tidy. I am *not coin*." She turned, slowly, addressing the whole crossroads—the pilgrims, the smugglers, the line of white cloaks on the ridge, the gods gathered at the borders of the firelight. "I am a carver. And before anyone spends another life tonight, they will hear what I intend to carve."

Sereth's antlers lowered, and the temperature fell. "Then speak quickly, little anchor. Because your Inquisition did not march four hundred strong to listen."

Down on the southern road, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. The Wardens began to advance.

### Reeve

The order to advance came not from Reeve but from Warden-Commander Doss, who had arrived at midnight with reinforcements and Casserly's authority, and who now sat his horse like a man attending a coronation.

"Whitecloak line, forward at walk. Archers on the flanks. No engagement until they break rank—we give the mob every chance to disperse." Doss smiled thinly. "And when the Anchor shows herself, Inquisitor, *you* will be the one to take her. It's fitting. You have history."

Reeve looked at the advancing line—four hundred torches walking downhill toward a field of kneeling faithful—and understood with perfect clarity that he was about to choose, finally and irrevocably, which side of the wall he had spent his whole life maintaining he actually stood on.

Twelve years ago, he had spared a child and called it mercy and changed nothing. Tonight mercy would cost everything.

"Captain Varga," he said quietly.

"Inquisitor?"

"When the line reaches the fires—look at what we're marching into. Really look. Hymns, not hexes. Old people. Children asleep against their mothers' backs." He drew a slow breath. "Tell me this is law."

Varga's hand rested on her sword. Her face, in the torchlight, was a battlefield of its own. "It's orders," she said at last, and her voice was not steady. "Orders aren't law, Halvard. I learned that distinction from you."

"Then learn the rest of it now."

He wheeled his horse out of the command formation, deliberately, visibly, letting Doss and every officer on the ridge see it. Then he spurred downslope, alone, white cloak streaming, riding straight through the gap between two armies toward a woman standing in the frost with bone-dust on her hands.

Behind him, he heard Doss shout his name, then the sharper sound—the one he'd known was coming—the command to loose.

The first arrows rose against the twelfth moon like a flight of migrating birds, beautiful and doomed and aimed at everything he had decided to love.

Reeve bent low over his horse's neck and rode into the dark between them, and his last coherent thought before the screaming started was a prayer, finally answered, addressed to no god at all:

*Hold the line, Tam. Hold the line. I'll buy her the road to the stone if it's the last thing I carve into this world.*

* * *

### Xanthe

The first arrow took a pilgrim boy through the shoulder ten feet from where Xanthe stood, and the field broke like a dropped plate.

Hymns became screams. Smugglers surged up from the ditches where Tam had planted them all afternoon, red scarves bright in the firelight, shields locking into a ragged crescent around the crossroads. Odile's voice rose above the din in a hedge-witch's working cry, and a wall of tangled bramble heaved itself out of the frozen earth along the southern approach, snarling the Warden vanguard like flies in amber. Tam was everywhere at once—hauling the wounded boy behind a cart, roaring the smugglers into formation, laughing in the terrible way he laughed when the world demanded he refuse despair.

And through the smoke, a single rider on a single horse, white cloak torn and streaming, crashing through the bramble line toward her with arrows feathering the ground behind him.

"Halvard—" Xanthe started.

"Listen to me!" Reeve hauled his horse to a stop, bleeding from a graze along the scalp, his eyes blazing with a certainty she had never seen in them. "Doss has a second force circling from the west—they'll be here within the hour. Casserly wants the seal collapsed, Xanthe. Not maintained. *Collapsed*. They'll kill you at the stone and call it holy." He tore the folded order from his breast pocket and threw it at her feet. "I'm done. Do you understand? I'm done being their instrument. Get to the seal-stone. Whatever you mean to carve—carve it tonight, because there is no thirteenth turn. There's only this one."

Beyond him, on the hill, Sereth threw back her antlered head and howled, and every god at the edge of the firelight turned as one toward the north—toward the old road, the deep forest, the direction of the seal-stone—and Xanthe felt the whole night tilt, like a table losing its last leg.

The twelfth moon reached its zenith overhead.

And in her pocket, uncarved, untouched, the piece of antler bone she had carried since Vellharrow began to glow bone-white, humming against her palm like a struck bell, as if it already knew her name.
