## 19. War's Old Face

Halvard Reeve had marched to war three times in his life, and every time he had told himself the same lie: *this will be quick.*

The first time he was nineteen and believed it because he knew nothing. The second time he was thirty-four and believed it because he wanted to. Now, standing at the edge of the Widow's Crossroads with two hundred Wardens at his back and an army of pilgrims, smugglers, and waking gods gathering across the frost-burned fields, he did not believe it at all.

The moon above them was swollen past fullness — the twelfth turn, one shy of the last. It hung low and wrong-colored, a coin of tarnished silver, as though the sky itself were being pulled out of true by what gathered beneath it.

"Lord Inquisitor." Captain Vess stood at his shoulder, her breath steaming. She was young, competent, and loyal in the way that frightened him most: she had never once asked whether the order she'd been given deserved to exist. "The forward companies are in position along the hedgerows. The pilgrims are not dispersing."

"They're not going to disperse," Reeve said. "They've come to watch their gods be born."

Vess's jaw tightened. "Then we clear them."

"With steel? Two thousand unarmed faithful?"

"With whatever is necessary. Your own orders, my lord. The Anchor is to be contained or executed before moonset. Anyone obstructing—"

"I wrote the orders," Reeve said quietly. "I know what they say."

That was the wound of it, the thing he could not stop turning over like a bone-carver's knife: he had written them. He had sat at his desk in Vellharrow with candle-grease pooling on the parchment and written, in his precise Warden's hand, that the woman who had once been a barefoot child hiding under a workbench should die so that magic could die with her. He had told himself it was mercy of a kind — that a world without gods was safer than a world at war with them, that Xanthe Whitlock would understand if she could see the whole board.

He no longer believed she could not see the whole board. He believed she saw it more clearly than anyone, and had still chosen to walk onto it.

He had given her a head start instead of chains. He had freed Odile Fenn from a cell in Aurelach with forged papers and a prayer he did not believe in. Every hour since, he had been waiting for the moment when those choices stopped being deniable. When the moment came, he told himself, he would know what to do.

It came sooner than he expected.

* * *

The first sign was silence.

Not quiet — silence, the way a held breath is silent. The wind died across the crossroads all at once, and every torch flame on both sides of the field leaned toward the center, toward the old seal-stone that rose from the frozen earth like a broken tooth. Reeve felt the hair on his arms lift. Beside him, Vess made a small involuntary sound.

Then the antlers came up out of the ground.

They erupted from the soil in ranks, pale as peeled birch, taller than the hedgerows, and between them walked shapes that Reeve's eyes refused to resolve properly — tall figures robed in shadow and starlight, faces like weather seen from very far away. Sereth came first, goddess of the hunt, and where she stepped the frost bloomed into white flowers that opened and closed like breathing mouths. Behind her moved others: a drowned king trailing river water that never touched the earth, a smith whose hammer-strokes rang inside Reeve's skull rather than his ears, a dozen more, half-formed, hungry, *waking*.

Across the field, the pilgrims fell to their knees in waves, like wheat before wind. Some wept. Some sang. A few, Reeve noted, did neither — they simply stood, arms crossed, watching with the flat expressions of people who had already decided these things were not gods but something wearing godhood like stolen clothes.

"Hold formation," Reeve said. His voice carried down the line the way it always did, steady as a metronome. Inside, everything was screaming. "Archers to the second rank. No one looses without my word."

"And if they loose first?" Vess whispered. "My lord — if *those* things attack—"

"Then I'll give you your word quickly."

Sereth raised her hand, and the silence deepened until Reeve could hear his own heartbeat. Her voice, when it came, needed no volume; it arrived inside every ear on the field simultaneously, warm and terrible, like blood reaching a numb limb.

*"Children of the seal. You have lived long in a house with the windows nailed shut. Tonight the nails come out. Stand aside, or stand against us — but know that we remember which you chose."*

Reeve stepped forward before he could think better of it. Later he would understand this was the moment the war truly began — not with arrows, but with one man walking alone into the space between armies because someone had to answer.

"Inquisitor Halvard Reeve," he called, "of the Holy Office of Vellharrow. On behalf of the Concord of Men—" the old treaty name sounded absurd now, a child's word shouted into a storm "—I tell you the seal stands. It stands tonight, and it stands tomorrow, and no army of yours crosses while I draw breath."

Sereth turned. Her face was a stag's and a woman's and neither, and her eyes were the color of winter dawn.

*"Little warden,"* she said, almost gently. *"You smell of ash and doubt. Whose breath do you think holds the seal? Not yours. Hers. And we have only to wait for the thirteenth moon, or hasten it. Hastening is faster."*

She looked past him, across the field, to where the smugglers' wagons formed a rough barricade around a figure in a patched grey coat. Even at this distance Reeve could see the antler-handled carving knives at her belt, could feel the shape of her attention turn toward the seal-stone like a compass needle finding north.

*"Bring me the Anchor,"* Sereth said, *"and I will let every mortal here walk home. Refuse, and I begin with the ones you love, and I will spend them one by one until you understand that love is leverage, and leverage exists to be used."*

Reeve heard it then — the thing that crystallized twelve years of doubt into a single hard diamond of certainty. Not the threat. The *accounting*. The casual arithmetic of lives as currency, the same arithmetic the Church used when it burned hedge-witches to protect the faith, the same arithmetic he had used when he signed the execution order for a carver of mourning charms. Gods and men, throne and altar — everyone spent other people like coin.

"No," Reeve said.

Sereth tilted her great head.

"You don't get to spend them," he said. "None of you. Not you, not the Church, not me. That ends tonight."

For a moment the whole field hung motionless. Then Sereth smiled, and the smile was the oldest face of war, and she drew a bow of white bone from her back.

*"So be it,"* she said. *"Warden. Show your children how you choose."*

* * *

The arrow that killed the first man on either side was not aimed at Reeve. It took a pilgrim boy through the chest — sixteen perhaps, kneeling with his hands open, singing — and the sound of it broke the field like ice on a pond.

After that, everything happened in pieces.

Reeve remembered shouting the withdrawal — *fall back, fall back to the wagons, do not engage the manifestations* — and remembered Vess's voice cracking as she relayed orders no Warden had ever been trained to give, because no Warden manual contained a chapter titled *What To Do When Gods Walk Out of the Ground*. He remembered the archers loosing anyway, panic doing what discipline couldn't, and the arrows passing through the tall shadow-shapes like wind through smoke while finding flesh among the kneeling pilgrims with horrible ease.

He remembered the drowned king raising one webbed hand, and the irrigation ditch beside the road rising up in a slow black wave that swallowed an entire company of Wardens — not drowning them, worse, *turning them*, sending them back up the slope with river-water eyes, moving to Sereth's silent command. Turned soldiers. His own people, wearing his own colors, now weapons.

And he remembered running.

Not away. Across. Through the screaming, through the burning hedges — someone had fired the dry hedgerow and the flames ran along it like a fuse — through the press of bodies, toward the barricade where Tam Vosk stood on a wagon bed with a smuggler's cutlass in each hand roaring orders in three languages, and Odile Fenn moved through the wounded with her healer's satchel leaving a trail of green light, and Xanthe—

Xanthe was climbing down from the wagon with her knives drawn and her face set in an expression Reeve recognized from a workbench twelve years ago: the look of a person about to do the difficult, necessary, unforgivable thing.

"You can't reach the stone," he gasped, catching her arm. Between breaths: "Sereth wants you there. The moment you touch it, she kills you in front of everyone. Martyrdom shatters the seal faster than patience — Maren Corvane sent word—"

"Maren's word got us this far." Xanthe's eyes were bright and furious. "If I don't re-forge it tonight, at the thirteenth turn it fails anyway, and we fight this war forever instead of once. Someone has to carve it, Halvard. That's the whole point. It has to be *me*."

"Not through that field. Look at it." He gestured, and she looked, and he watched her take in what he had already counted: the burning lines, the turned soldiers closing in a crescent, the shadow-shapes of the waking gods drifting toward the stone like icebergs, and between Xanthe and the seal-stone, four hundred yards of killing ground.

"There's no path," he said.

"There has to be—"

"I'll make one."

She understood immediately. He saw it land in her face — saw her start to refuse, saw her mouth form his name, saw the exact moment she realized that refusal cost more lives than acceptance. That was the cruelty of it. That was always the cruelty of it. Everyone he loved learned the same lesson tonight: that other people's deaths could be spent on their behalf whether they consented or not.

"No," Xanthe said. "No. Find another way. We find another way together—"

"The Wardens are mine," Reeve said. "My order, my command, my fifty-one years. If anyone clears that field, it's me." He gripped her shoulder, hard, the way he had never dared grip anything he intended to keep. "Twelve years ago I spared a child because I was afraid. Tonight I'm not afraid. Let me have this. It's the only honest thing I'll ever do."

"Halvard—"

"Tell Veyran—" He stopped. Started again. There was nothing to tell Veyran. There was only one message left in him, and it wasn't for the wolf. "When it's done. When the world after comes. Carve something ordinary. A door hinge. A spoon. Something no god will ever want."

Her hands were shaking. "You come back and I'll carve you anything you want."

"That," Reeve said, smiling, feeling the smile crack something in his chest, "is the first debt I've ever taken on gladly."

Then he turned to the field, drew his sword — plain steel, church-issued, the only god he had ever faithfully served — and began to shout commands in the voice that had carried across thirty years of battlefields.

* * *

Later — in whatever place later existed for him — Reeve would piece the battle together the way survivors always did, in fragments that refused to make a single picture.

He remembered forming the loyal remnant of the Third Company into a wedge, two hundred shields locked, and telling them the truth for the first time in his career: that they were going to buy a stranger ninety yards with their bodies, and that they could refuse, and that he would go alone if they did. He remembered Vess stepping into the front rank without a word, and then the whole line stepping forward as one.

He remembered Tam Vosk's smugglers hitting the turned soldiers from the flank, screaming their strange dockside war-cries, Odile's green fire arcing overhead to bloom where it landed and turn river-water eyes back to human grief. He remembered the drowned king shrieking — an actual shriek, fury and surprise — as his stolen puppets were reclaimed one by one.

He remembered Sereth's arrows falling like hail, each one seeking Xanthe through the smoke, and the shield-wall tilting and bucking to catch them, men dropping with white shafts through their throats, the wall closing over every gap without being told because they all understood the geometry of sacrifice: *she reaches the stone, or none of this meant anything.*

He remembered the smith-god descending on the left flank with its hammer, and the sound — the *sound* of it, each blow a bell tolling inside the marrow — and Vess going down beneath a stroke he was too far away to block, and the rage that followed, clean and clarifying as cold water.

And he remembered the last eighty yards.

The shield-wall was gone by then. What remained was a scattered tide of Wardens, smugglers, and bleeding pilgrims advancing in ragged surges, and Reeve at the point of it, older than he had ever been, sword arm leaden, lungs full of fire, watching the seal-stone grow larger through the smoke — a grey monolith carved over with names worn nearly smooth, and beside it, impossibly, a wolf the size of a horse tearing through Sereth's hunters with white fangs and a roar like a landslide.

*Xanthe was running.* Twenty yards behind him, guarded by Tam and a knot of pilgrims who had linked arms around her like a living wall, she was running for the stone with a carving knife in her fist, and Sereth had seen her, and the goddess was drawing back her bow one final time, and the arrow on the string burned white as the marks on bone-white charms.

Reeve did not decide anything. Twelve years of decisions — the sparing, the suspicion, the orders, the betrayal of his order, the freeing of prisoners — all of it funneled down into a single instant of motion, and the decision had already been made somewhere deeper than thought, made the day a frightened child hid under a workbench and he had looked at her and, for one unguarded moment, seen the world she came from and chosen not to burn it.

He crossed the last ten yards between himself and the goddess's aim.

He spread his arms.

He thought, absurdly, of a spoon. Of a door hinge. Of a world where a carver could make small, useless, beautiful things for no one's purpose but her own.

*"Out of the way, little warden,"* said Sereth, and loosed.

The white arrow took him through the chest, and Halvard Reeve looked down at it with something like relief — the debt collected, the account finally balanced — and then he did the last thing his body would ever do.

He smiled at the goddess of the hunt, wrapped both hands around the arrow's white shaft, and *held it*, holding the death inside himself, holding the line one final time, as behind him Xanthe Whitlock reached the seal-stone, pressed her palm against the ancient carved names, and began —
