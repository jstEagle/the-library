## 20. The Carver's Name

The crossroads had become a battlefield of waiting.

Xanthe stood at its heart with the seal-stone before her, a slab of grey granite older than the city that had grown up around it and forgotten it. The moon overhead was swollen past fullness — the thirteenth turn, the moon that had no name in any calendar the church allowed to survive. Its light fell on the stone like milk poured over slate, and in that light she could see what no one else could: the old carving beneath centuries of lichen and soil, lines so fine they might have been cut by insect legs, spiraling inward toward a single word at the center.

A word worn nearly smooth.

*Her name,* Maren had said, weeks ago, in the burning dark of Aurelach's crypts. *Every Anchor has carved her name into it. That is how the seal knows who holds the door.*

Around her, the night held its breath.

To the west, where the pilgrim road climbed out of the lowlands, Sereth waited. Xanthe could not see the goddess directly — no mortal eye could hold her whole — but she saw her the way you see wind moving through wheat: a pressure in the air, a silvering of every blade of grass, the trees along the road bowing as though before a queen. The hunt had come to the crossroads. Somewhere in that silvered dark, antlers turned like a crown catching light.

To the east, torches. Hundreds of them, strung along the ridge like a constellation brought low to earth — the Inquisition's army, come to kill her at the moment of greatest power, when her death would tear the seal open and let the Inquisition burn magic out of the world forever. Reeve had warned her with his last free breath. *They don't want the seal maintained, Xanthe. They want it broken on their terms.* And then he had gone back to the men who would execute him for treason, because someone had to slow them down, and he had decided, in his quiet stubborn way, that someone was him.

Behind her, closer than either army, stood Tam and Odile and whatever remained of the smugglers and pilgrims who had answered Tam's call. She did not look at them. If she looked at them, she would think about Odile's burned hands from the shrine-city, about Tam's voice cracking when he'd said *you stay free or none of us survives*, about Reeve holding a bridge against men he'd trained. And if she thought about those things, her hands would shake, and tonight of all nights her hands could not shake.

"Xanthe." Veyran's voice came from beside her, low, the voice of a wolf speaking human words around the shape of them. He stood at her shoulder in his great grey body, fur silvered by the thirteenth moon, his eyes the color of deep water over stone. "Sereth approaches. Whatever you mean to do — decide now."

"I know what I mean to do."

"You do not." There was no cruelty in it. That was the strange grace of him, the thing she had learned across all those terrible weeks: Veyran never lied to her, not even kindly, not anymore. "You believe you do. But belief and decision are different animals, carver. One is a feeling. The other is a debt."

"Everything is a debt with you."

"Yes," he said simply. "It is the only language I have ever spoken fluently. Which is why I am telling you this one final time, so that you cannot say afterward that the god of thresholds did not warn you." He lowered his great head until his muzzle nearly touched her ear. "The rite will work. Maren taught it truly. Your willing death will reforge the seal for another age, and your family lives, and the world sleeps another few hundred years before the question comes again. It is the safe choice. It is the choice every Anchor before you made, and their names are worn smooth in that stone because they gave everything and asked nothing, not even remembrance."

"And Sereth's way?"

"Is also true. Kill you publicly, unwillingly, at the height of the thirteenth moon, and the seal shatters like dropped glass. The gods return in full glory. There is war, and fire, and the world remade in blood — but the world, she would argue, was always going to end one way or another, and better it end honest." His ears flicked backward, toward the torchlit ridge. "And there is a third truth neither of them offered you, because offering it costs them both too much."

"That I can choose something else entirely."

"You can choose something else entirely," Veyran said. "You always could. That is what an Anchor *is*, Xanthe. Not a lock. Not a key. A hand on the door — and a hand may open, close, or build a new door altogether. The rites were written by people who assumed obedience. You have never once been obedient in your life. It has been the great frustration and the great hope of everyone who has watched you since the first white mark appeared."

Xanthe laughed — a small, cracked sound, absurd in the hush of the crossroads. "You planned this. From the beginning. The marks on my carvings, the widow's prayer, all of it — you woke them early because you wanted an Anchor who wouldn't follow the rite."

"I wanted an Anchor who would follow *her own* rite," Veyran said. "Whether that meant dying, or warring, or something no god had imagined. I gambled twelve years of careful silence on a bone-carver in Vellharrow who talked to dead grandmothers through dove bones and never once told anyone." His eyes closed briefly. "I gambled everything. If you fail tonight, I lose more than divinity. I lose the argument. Forever."

"Then I suppose," Xanthe said, drawing her carving knife from her belt, "we'd both better not fail."

She stepped forward to the seal-stone.

Up close, in the nameless moonlight, she could feel it humming — not sound, but pressure, the way a struck tuning fork hums in the bones of the hand. Twelve generations of Anchors lived in that stone. She laid her palm flat against the granite and felt them: women and men who had died willingly, one after another, each one certain they were doing the only thing that could be done, each one's love and grief and terror pressed into mineral like flowers pressed between pages. They had been brave. They had been kind. They had been spent like coin by a world that never learned any other way to spend its holy things.

*I refuse,* she thought, not to the stone, not to the gods, but to all of them — the dead Anchors, Sereth in her silvered distance, the Inquisitor's army on its ridge, the church that had bred her mother's mother's mother for this purpose. *I refuse all of it. Not out of pride. Out of arithmetic. Someone has to stop spending people.*

She set her knife's edge against the granite.

The first stroke of the new carving felt like cutting into living flesh.

Not her flesh — though she would learn, soon enough, that there was no longer any difference. The stone screamed without sound. Light poured from the groove of the knife, white as the marks that had appeared on her charms, white as bone bleached in desert sun, and through that light Xanthe felt the seal wake around her like a vast animal turning over in sleep. Twelve generations of dead Anchors stirred. The old word at the center — worn smooth, half-erased, the name of some woman three thousand years gone whose sacrifice had bought the world its long uneasy peace — began to glow, and began to resist.

*Who are you?* the stone asked, in a voice like continents settling. *What gives you the right?*

"My name," Xanthe whispered, and began to carve it.

*X-A-N-T-H-E.*

Each letter cost her something she could not name and could not get back. With the *X*, she felt her childhood go — the smell of her father's workshop, sawdust and linseed oil, his big hands guiding hers over scrap bone, saying *always carve with the grain, little bird; fight the material and it fights you*. With the *A*, she lost the memory of her mother's face, which she had hoarded carefully for twenty years and which dissolved now like sugar in rain, leaving only the fact of having once known it. With the *N*, the cold of Vellharrow winters, the hunger years, the taste of bread paid for informing — all of it pouring out of her into the stone, spent, spent, spent, the price of a word written in granite.

She kept carving. Her hands did not shake. Later, she would understand why: they belonged to the work now, the way a carpenter's arm belongs to the saw's rhythm, and the work was larger than fear.

The letter *T* went deepest. At its stroke, the world tilted.

Light everywhere. She stood outside herself, outside the crossroads, outside time's narrow gutter, and saw what the stone saw: the web of the seal spreading out from this granite heart through every living thing, anchoring in roots and rivers and the small blind prayers of ordinary people, holding the old gods pressed flat against the underside of the world like moths pinned in a collector's case. She saw Sereth's fury, vast and cold and old, straining against pins. She saw the waking gods scattered through the dark like embers refusing to die. And she saw — closest of all, wrapped around her like a second skin — Veyran, diminished, patient, the god of thresholds who had opposed the demand for worship four thousand years ago and had been paying for that opposition ever since, alone among his kind, alone among humanity, belonging fully to neither.

*This is the cost,* the stone said. *Rewrite me and be rewritten. Name yourself and be named. There is no carving without loss, carver. What remains of you when I am done?*

"Enough," Xanthe said. Her voice came from very far away, and very close. "Enough of me to invite whom I choose. Enough to say no. That's all an anchor needs to be — enough."

She carved the *H*.

Something tore.

She felt it happen — felt the boundary between herself and the seal thin to nothing, felt the stone's vast impersonal attention flow *into* her like water into a cracked vessel, filling her, replacing her, the way flood replaces river. Her memories didn't leave anymore; they drowned. She was the seal. She was twelve generations of willing deaths. She was a granite heart beating under a nameless moon, and somewhere at the bottom of all that enormity a small voice that had once been a girl in Vellharrow was screaming *my name, remember my name, my name is Xanthe Whitlock and I carve bone* —

— and then warmth.

A pressure at her back, steady as a hand between the shoulder blades. A heartbeat that was not hers, slower, deeper, older, keeping time against the stone's vast rhythm like a drum against a bell. Through the drowning light she heard Veyran's voice — not in her ears, but in the place where she still existed, wherever that was.

"Here," the wolf-god said. "Hold onto here."

*You'll lose what's left of you,* she managed. *You're already diminished. This will take the rest.*

"I am the god of thresholds," Veyran said, and she felt him smile, felt the smile in the warmth at her back. "Do you know what a threshold is, carver? It is not the door. It is not the room on either side. It is the place where two things meet without destroying each other. I have held that place alone for four thousand years. Let me hold it for you tonight. It is, at long last, a debt I *want* to pay."

He poured himself into the gap.

She felt his divinity go — what remained of it, the dregs of godhood, the last coin of a being who had spent millennia paying down other people's debts — flowing into her like candlelight into a dark room, not replacing what she was but *marking* it, tracing her outline the way a carver traces a pattern before cutting, so that the flood of the seal could fill the space around her shape without erasing her shape itself. Around the outline of Xanthe Whitlock — daughter of a god who chose mortality, daughter of the last Anchor, bone-carver of Vellharrow, friend of smugglers and hedge-witches — the seal's enormity broke like surf around a standing stone.

She carved the final letter.

*E.*

The world went silent. Then white. Then —

Dawn-colored stillness. The crossroads. Grass under her knees. The seal-stone before her, transformed: the old smooth-worn name gone entirely, and in its place, cut clean and bright and permanent, a single name — hers — ringed by words she did not remember carving but knew in her marrow were hers, written in the old tongue that predated the church and would outlive it:

*THE DOOR OPENS TO INVITATION ONLY. ONE HEART AT A TIME. NO GOD ENTERS UNASKED. NO BELIEVER PRAYS ALONE.*

The power drained out of her slowly, like water finding its level, and left her kneeling in wet grass, hollowed and shaking and — impossibly, miraculously — *whole*. Not untouched. She would discover in the coming days the rooms that stood empty inside her now, the childhood scents and faces that had gone into the letters. But the walls stood. The name remained. She was Xanthe Whitlock, and she had carved herself into the foundation of the world, and the world had accepted the terms.

Across the crossroads, the silvered pressure of Sereth recoiled like a wave hitting a seawall. Xanthe heard her then — properly heard her, for the first time, a voice like winter wind through a forest of spears, furious and ancient and, underneath it all, *tired*.

"*What have you done?*"

"What should have been done four thousand years ago," Xanthe said. Her voice was raw gravel. She got one knee under herself, then the other, and stood. "You wanted war. The rite wanted another obedient death. I'm done letting other people write the terms, Sereth. Even you. Even the dead." She put her hand on the stone, over her own name, and felt it hum — gently now, a hearth-hum rather than a battle-drum. "You want to walk in the world again? Fine. Be invited. Find one person who looks at you — at *all* of what you are, hunt and hunger and majesty — and says yes. Convince them. Serve them. Earn it, the way everything worth having is earned. No more worship as tribute. No more martyrs. No more armies."

For a long moment the goddess said nothing. The silvered grass stilled. Somewhere far off, on the eastern ridge, the Inquisition's torches were in chaos — she could hear faint shouting, the crackle of something burning that wasn't a pyre, and she sent a prayer after Reeve, wherever he was, whatever was happening, that his gamble had bought him more than a grave.

Then Sereth laughed. It was not a kind sound, but it was not entirely unkind either. It was the sound of a predator recognizing, with grudging respect, a larger predator.

"One heart at a time," the goddess said. "Do you know how long that will take us, little carver? Centuries. Millennia. We may go hungry in the dark places for a thousand years before we are welcomed anywhere."

"Then you'll finally understand what it's been like for us," Xanthe said.

Another silence. Then, softer — almost wondering: "*Your father said something like that once.*"

And she was gone — the pressure lifted, the silvered grass bowed once and straightened, and the wild dark to the west was just dark again, emptied of majesty, keeping its retreating goddess somewhere in its deep folds.

Xanthe's legs gave out. She sat down hard in the grass, and Veyran was there — smaller now, she realized dimly; the wolf who knelt beside her was barely larger than an ordinary hound, his coat dulled, his eyes still deep water but shallower water, a pond where an ocean had been. He had given nearly the last of himself to hold her shape against the flood. He lay down beside her with the heavy grace of the exhausted, and rested his head on her knee like a dog who has finally come home.

"You fool," she said, and her hand found the ruff of his neck. "You magnificent, impossible fool. You've got almost nothing left."

"I have exactly what I need," Veyran said. His voice was thinner too, frayed at the edges, but warm. "A doorstep. A household. Time." His tail moved once, sweeping the grass. "Four thousand years, carver, I have been the god of thresholds, and I have never once been invited *in*. Every door I guarded, I guarded from outside. Tonight you wrote a world with a thousand thousand doors." His eyes closed. "Someday, perhaps, someone will ask me across one. Until then—" a sigh, deep and contented and utterly mortal-sounding, "—until then, this knee will do."

Above them, the thirteenth moon was setting, and behind it, faint as breath on glass, the first pale edge of dawn.

From the east, across the churned fields below the ridge, came the sound Xanthe had been listening for all night and dreading: hoofbeats. A single rider, fast, weaving through the abandoned siege lines. For one terrible heartbeat she thought — Warden, executioner, the Inquisition's answer arriving too late to matter and in time to break her heart all over again.

But the rider wore no Warden's grey. The horse was a smuggler's nag, lathered and stumbling, and the figure on its back wore a patched coat she knew, and even at two hundred paces she could hear the voice carrying across the grass, ragged with exhaustion and disbelief and something that sounded almost like joy:

"XANTHE! *Xanthe!* You'll want to hear this — the Wardens broke — Reeve's alive — he's *alive*, do you hear me — and half the army's thrown down their arms and the other half is asking the priests what happens now that nobody can tell them what to pray!"

Tam hauled the horse to a stop, slid off, caught himself on the animal's flank, and stood there grinning like a man who has walked out of his own funeral — mud-spattered, singed, missing an eyebrow, alive.

"And Xanthe?" He was breathing hard. His grin wavered at the edges, softened into something else. "There's folk coming down off that ridge right now. Ordinary folk. Soldiers and pilgrims both. Walking toward that stone of yours." He jerked his chin toward the seal-stone, toward her name shining faintly in the dawn. "And they're asking — they keep asking — *how does it work? How do we ask?*"

Veyran's tail swept the grass again. Against her knee, the wolf-god began, very quietly, to laugh.

Xanthe looked at the dawn, and at the stone, and at the small crowd already picking its way across the fields toward her — the first believers of a world remade — and found, hollowed and grieving and spent down to the marrow as she was, that she was smiling too.

"Well then," she said, rising on unsteady legs, one hand on her knife, one on the wolf's neck. "Best teach them how to knock."
