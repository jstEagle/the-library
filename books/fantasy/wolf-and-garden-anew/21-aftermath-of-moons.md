## 21. Aftermath of Moons

The thirteenth moon did not set so much as let go.

Xanthe watched it happen from the crossroads hill, her hands still crusted with bone-dust and her own dried blood, the seal-stone warm behind her like a hearth that had finally been banked. The moon hung low and enormous over Vellharrow's rooftops, and then—without ceremony, without thunder—it simply loosened from the sky the way a knot loosens when the right thread is pulled. Light spilled down like water finding its level, ran through the streets and the gutters and the bell-towers, and went out.

Where it had passed, small things remained.

She saw them in the days after. A gutter-candle in Candlemaker's Row that burned without being lit whenever someone prayed beside it. An old dog at the fish market who turned his head to listen to a child's whispered worries, ears pricked toward something no one else could hear. Frost that bloomed in the shape of antlers on a widow's window, and melted by noon. Not gods. Not anymore. Remnants. Embers. Each one waiting, patient as a banked coal, for someone to open a door and say *you may come in.*

Sereth had gone first.

The goddess of the hunt had stood before the rewritten seal-stone for a long time after Xanthe finished carving—a long, terrible stillness, like a drawn bow deciding whether to loose—and then she had laughed. It was not a kind sound, but neither was it cruel. It was the laugh of a hunter acknowledging a worthy quarry that had escaped her.

"*Invitation,*" Sereth had said, tasting the word. Her antlers caught the moonlight and threw it back in splinters. "*You have made us beggars, little carver.*"

"No," Xanthe had answered, her voice raw, her knees threatening to fold beneath her. "I've made you guests. There's a difference. Beggars take. Guests wait to be asked."

The goddess had looked at her for a long moment with eyes the color of winter stars, and something in them—not warmth, never warmth, but perhaps respect, or its wild cousin—had flickered. Then she had stepped backward into the treeline at the edge of the crossroads, and the trees had closed around her like a hand closing around a coin, and she was gone. Bound now, as they all were bound, to the terms carved in Xanthe Whitlock's own hand: *return only where invited, only by name, only freely.*

The wild places were hers now. Xanthe felt that truth settle over the hills beyond the city like snowfall—every forest, every fen, every stretch of unplowed dark. Sereth would endure there, hunting whatever hunted, answering no prayers but those spoken aloud under open sky by someone who meant them. It was, Xanthe thought, more mercy than the goddess would ever have shown humanity. But mercy had always been the strangest thing humans gave away for free.

* * *

They buried Halvard Reeve on the fourth day, in the ground behind Odile's cottage, because he had no family left to claim him and because Tam said—gruffly, digging with a smuggler's efficient brutality—"Wardens get marble. He earned dirt."

There had not been much of him left to bury. That was the truth of it, and Xanthe refused, this once, to look away from a truth. She had spent twelve years carving lies into comfort—*he died peacefully*, *she is watching over you*, all the gentle fictions bone could hold. She would not carve one for Reeve.

He had died holding the line at the eastern ditch while pilgrims and smugglers and two dozen former Wardens who'd chosen him over their order fought to buy her thirty yards of open ground. He had died with his sword broken and a god-shaped wound across his chest, shouting orders in a voice that didn't shake until the very end. He had died before she could tell him—

Before she could tell him what? That she forgave him? He'd never asked forgiveness. That she understood why a boy Warden had once spared a frightened child with bone-dust on her fingers, and why a man Warden had finally spared her again, at the cost of everything? That his unanswered prayers—the ones he'd whispered into empty chapels while beggars' prayers bloomed white across her carvings—had hurt worse than any wound, and that she was sorry the heavens had been so busy dying that no one heard him?

She knelt at the grave's edge and pressed her palm flat against the turned earth.

"His hands," she said quietly, to Tam and Odile standing behind her, "were steady. Even at the end. I heard it in the bones of everyone he carried off the field. Steady hands, all the way down."

Odile wept openly, the way hedge-witches do, without shame. Tam took off his hat and held it against his chest like a man holding shut a door. And Xanthe mourned honestly, which was harder than magic, harder than the seal, harder than facing down Sereth with nothing but a chisel and her own name. She mourned the debt she could never repay. She mourned the report he'd demanded of her in her workshop, all those months ago, and understood at last that it had never been blackmail—it had been a rope thrown to a drowning man, a reason to keep talking to the person he suspected, because suspicion was the only company the Inquisition had ever allowed him.

"He believed the church could be saved," she said. "Right up until he learned it couldn't. And then he believed *we* could."

"That's a better epitaph than marble," Tam said.

They buried him without rites, because rites belonged to the church that had betrayed him, and without prayers, because prayers belonged to gods who now waited, politely, to be invited. Instead Xanthe laid a single carving on the mound: a wolf's skull worked from deer antler, small enough to fit in a palm, its eye-sockets hollow and open skyward. Unmarked. White-free. No dead man's voice in it, because Reeve was simply, plainly, finally done.

But she left it unfinished in one deliberate way—one facet rough, unpolished, waiting for a hand. Someday, if some grieving soul wanted to finish it, they could. Invitation. It was going to be her answer to everything now, she suspected, for the rest of her life.

* * *

Vellharrow changed faster than anyone expected, the way ice changes—slowly, slowly, then all at once.

The Provisional Council repealed the Edict of Silence eleven days after the battle. Magic, the new proclamation read, posted on every door and shouted by criers in every square, is lawful within the city bounds. There were riots anyway—there are always riots when fear loses its legal armor—but they sputtered out within a week, because the thing people had feared turned out to look like Odile setting a broken arm with a whispered word, and a lamplighter coaxing stubborn flames alight on windy nights, and old women leaving offerings of bread and honey at doorsteps for whatever small presence might be listening.

That was the part Xanthe had carved into the world and was still learning to live inside: faith was legal now, but so was its absence. The gods could not demand worship as tribute—that old extortion had been the seed of the original war, Veyran had confessed, and she had carved its prohibition into the stone itself. No altar could compel. No miracle could threaten. Every blessing began with a knock, and every knock could go unanswered.

Some people hated it. Maren Corvane wrote to her from Aurelach—letters that arrived smelling of ash and ink—reporting that hardline congregations called the new terms "the Beggar's Faith" and prayed loudly to gods who could no longer hear them unless they truly meant it, and grew furious when heaven stayed silent. Others, thousands of others, discovered something quieter: that a prayer spoken freely, to a presence that might decline, meant infinitely more than a prayer extracted by terror. Small shrines appeared in window boxes and alley niches. People left doors literally ajar at dusk—an old custom revived, an invitation made physical.

And informants, Xanthe noted with grim satisfaction, no longer got paid in bread for reporting bone-carvers. The bread had gotten better, at least. She'd bought a whole loaf that morning, white flour, real butter, and eaten half of it standing in the street like a woman celebrating a coronation.

Her shop reopened in the third week. THE WHITLOCK BONEWORKS, repainted over the scorch marks, the sign carved by her own hand and—this was the joke only she and Tam understood—deliberately left full of tiny flaws, wobbles, asymmetries, so that no charm that left her bench could ever again carry a mark she hadn't chosen to put there. She kept her gift. Of course she kept it; it was not a curse, it had never been a curse, it was a hearing, and hearing is not consent. But now, when the dead spoke through her carvings, she told the living the truth about it up front, priced it fairly, and let them choose.

Business was strange and good. Grief-stricken families came from three towns over. One gray-haired woman brought her the femur of a husband dead forty years and asked, trembling, "Will you tell me if he regretted it?" and Xanthe had listened, and the answer was no, and the woman had walked home lighter than she'd walked in four decades.

And every few evenings, someone knocked on her door who wasn't there—or rather, something knocked that wore the shape of almost-being-there. A pressure against the wood. A coldness shaped like curiosity. Once, faintly, a smell of rain on hot stone.

Each time, Xanthe set down her tools, crossed the room, and opened the door herself.

Sometimes she said, "Not tonight." The presence would withdraw, courteous as a guest reading the hour. Sometimes she said, "Come in, if you'll wipe your feet." And something small and bright and nameless would enter her workshop for an evening—warm the tea, mend a cracked beam, sit in the corner like a cat that had once been enormous—and leave before dawn without taking anything, because taking was forbidden now, and giving was the only currency left to things that had once demanded tribute.

Veyran had not yet knocked.

* * *

She found him—she always found him—on the flat rooftop above Tam's warehouse, where the city's chimney-smoke rose thin into the autumn dark. He lay stretched along the parapet in his wolf-shape, grey as weathered slate, and when she climbed the last ladder he did not rise or speak, only shifted to make room, which for Veyran was practically a sonnet.

"You're thinner," she said, sitting beside him. His flank was warm against her leg, warmer than any animal had a right to be.

"I am *less*," he corrected. "Thinner implies I might eat my way back. What I spent at the seal-stone does not return. Anchoring you cost me—" a pause, the wolf-equivalent of a wince, "—most of what remained. This shape is not a punishment. It is simply what fits now."

"Do you regret it?"

The great head turned. His eyes were amber and ancient and tired in a way she recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors for twelve years—the exhaustion of carrying duty like a stone in the belly.

"I regret," Veyran said, "that it took me four thousand years to find a mortal willing to tell the gods *no* gently. I do not regret the cost." His gaze slid toward the distant hills, where the wild places began. "Sereth watches me, you know. From the treelines. She thinks I've been tamed."

"Have you?"

A low sound, not quite a growl, not quite laughter. "I have been *invited*. Which she correctly observes is worse."

Xanthe pulled her coat tighter and looked out over Vellharrow—its thousand chimneys, its relit lamps, its doorways left ajar against the coming cold. Somewhere below, Tam was arguing with a smuggler-turned-legitimate-freighter about tariffs. Somewhere below, Odile was teaching three apprentice hedge-witches the difference between healing and meddling. Somewhere below, a widow slept with a finished wolf-skull carving on her sill, and the grandmother inside it was, Xanthe was fairly sure, telling her to stop crying and eat something.

"The other gods," she said. "Do you hear from them?"

"Whispers. Sereth hunts. The river-god Ondrell has taken up residence in a well in the Weavers' Quarter—the family there leaves him milk and asks about the weather, and he answers, because he has always loved weather more than worship. Two or three others drift. The rest..." He sighed, a sound like wind moving through a cathedral. "The rest sleep, and will sleep until named. You unmade empire, Xanthe Whitlock. You should feel the weight of that."

"I feel the weight of the rent," she said. "It's due next week."

This time the sound he made was definitely laughter.

They sat in silence awhile, the comfortable kind, the kind that had taken a war to earn. Then the wolf spoke again, quieter, and she heard in his voice the thing he'd carried longest:

"There will come a night—I don't know when, a decade or a century—when someone stands at their door and speaks your name instead of mine. When mortals realize the seal itself has a maker, and that the maker is a bone-carver in Vellharrow who charges fair prices and feeds strays. They will knock on *your* threshold, Anchor, and ask for miracles." His amber eyes found hers. "What will you tell them?"

Xanthe thought about it honestly, the way she'd promised herself she would think about everything now. No doctrine. No duty. Just the question, held up to the light.

"I'll tell them what I told Sereth," she said. "Knock first. Wait to be asked. And wipe your feet."

Far off, at the edge of the dark hills, something with antlers lifted its head and listened—and, bound by the terms carved in her hand, did nothing else. But closer, much closer, at the foot of the ladder, something else had arrived without either of them noticing: a second wolf-shape, smaller, leaner, fur the color of moonlit snow, sitting patiently at the bottom rung with its head tipped up toward them, waiting.

Not Veyran. Veyran was beside her, going rigid, his hackles rising with recognition and something very like alarm.

The stranger-wolf looked up at Xanthe, and its eyes were the exact grey-green of a woman she had buried twelve years ago, and it spoke with a voice she had last heard through a mourning charm, on the day all of this began.

"Little carver," said the wolf at the bottom of the ladder, in the voice of her mother, "I believe I was told I may knock."
