## 22. Wolf and Garden Anew

The morning Xanthe Whitlock reopened her shop, the bell above the door was new.

She had carved it herself — rowan wood, shaped like a moth with its wings half-folded — and when the first customer pushed through, it rang a note so clean and small that Tam, who was stacking charcoal behind the counter, stopped mid-motion and looked at her as if to say *there, that's the sound of a life beginning again.*

"Good morning," said the customer. She was a young woman with flour on her sleeves and a baby strapped to her chest, and she did not flinch at the sight of bone knives and antler blanks in the window. That alone would have been unthinkable a year ago. "I'd like a charm made. For my mother's grave."

Xanthe set down the awl she had been cleaning. "Tell me about her."

And that was all. No whispered bargain, no glance over the shoulder toward the street, no fear of the Wardens who no longer existed. The Inquisition's tower on Corvane Hill still stood — they hadn't torn it down; instead they had turned it into a school where children learned letters and, if they wished and only if they wished, learned the old prayers too. The white marks on bone were studied openly now. Maren Corvane taught there, three mornings a week, and she had stopped wearing the high collar of her office because, she said, it made her neck itch.

The widow's charm took Xanthe two days. When she finished it, she held it to the light and waited for the grandmother's voice to come up through the grain the way voices always had — thin as smoke, sweet as an old song.

*Tell my girl,* the dead woman said, *the bread rises better if you sing to it.*

Xanthe laughed until she cried, and then she sang the message into the carving, and gave it to the baker's daughter without charge, because some debts were meant to be paid forward and never called due.

That was the year, mostly. Small things. Bread and singing. A city learning to breathe.

* * *

Tam ran the front of the house now, which suited him, because Tam had never met a stranger he couldn't turn into a paying customer or a friend, usually both. Odile kept the back room, where the herbs hung from the rafters in bundles that smelled of sage and feverfew and something greener underneath — the hedge-magic coming back into the soil of Vellharrow like water returning to a dry well, slow and shy and everywhere.

"You're brooding," Odile said one evening, appearing at Xanthe's elbow with two cups of willow-bark tea.

"I'm thinking."

"You're brooding *while thinking*, which is worse." She pressed a cup into Xanthe's hands. Her fingers were ink-stained from the ledgers she now kept — every answered prayer in the city logged by invitation, name, and consent, a book Maren called the Ledger of Doors. "Out with it."

Xanthe looked past her, through the open workshop door, to the doorstep.

He was lying there in the last of the light. Grey as river-fog, enormous even diminished, his head resting on his paws and his eyes half-closed in the way of a creature who has decided, after long negotiation with himself, that this is home. Children dared each other to touch him. He let them. Once, he had been Veyran, god of thresholds and debts, keeper of the way between worlds; now he was a wolf who could not cross a door uninvited, who weighed less than an ox, whose divinity fit inside a body like sunlight inside a jar.

He had spent the last of himself anchoring her at the seal-stone, holding her name steady while it rewrote the oldest law in the world. What remained of him had come back with her, across the pilgrim road, through the gates of Vellharrow, up three steps to her shop — and stopped.

*I'll wait here,* he had said, and his voice was smaller now, rougher, but still carrying that old wry music beneath it. *Thresholds are my whole nature, little carver. It seems fitting I should become one.*

"He doesn't eat enough," Xanthe said. "Odile. He barely eats."

"He eats what wolves eat." Odile followed her gaze, and her voice gentled. "And what he can't eat anymore is worship, and he's learning to live without it. So are we all." She touched Xanthe's wrist. "You know he chose this."

"I know he says he chose it."

"And you've asked him?"

"Every week since midwinter. He gives me the same answer every time." She drank the tea. It was bitter and good. "*A debt honestly paid needs no second payment.* He thinks in debts. He always did. I'm trying to teach him that some things are gifts."

"Gods are terrible students," Odile said. "But you were a terrible student once too, and look at you. You opened a shop."

* * *

On the first true day of spring, Xanthe carried a bowl of marrow broth out to the step and sat down beside him without asking permission, because that was their arrangement: neither invited nor uninvited, simply present.

"You're staring," Veyran said.

"I'm looking. There's a difference."

"Mm. Carvers and their distinctions." One amber eye slid open, regarding her. His fur had gone silver at the muzzle over the winter, which was either age or something else — gods did not age, but perhaps ex-gods learned to. "You have sawdust in your hair."

"You have a leaf on your ear."

They regarded each other. Somewhere down the lane, Tam was arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the moral character of eels.

"The baker's daughter came yesterday," Xanthe said. "For a charm for her mother. The grandmother spoke through the bone."

"And?"

"She told her to sing to the bread." Xanthe smiled despite herself. "Veyran. A year ago, prayer was a weapon. Answered prayers left marks like scars on everything I made. Now a dead woman spends her one message on bread."

"That," said the wolf, "is the entire war won, right there, in one sentence, and most people will never notice." He shifted, laying his great head against her knee — a liberty he had begun taking in autumn and she had never corrected. "Sereth sent word, did I tell you? Through the deep woods. The wild places are loud again. Not with war. With *hunting* — the honest kind, deer and hunger and moonlight. She says—" his muzzle wrinkled, "—she says the terms suit her better than she expected to admit."

"What terms?" Xanthe teased. "You mean my terms."

"I mean your terms. Which are now the world's terms. Which is why I am a wolf on a doorstep instead of a god on a throne." He said it lightly, but she heard the seam of something else under it, the way she heard seams under everything — twelve years of listening to bone had trained her ear. "You never regret it? The invitation clause. Every soul must ask. Every soul may refuse. It makes us small, Xanthe. All of us. Gods and mortals both. Small enough to be ignored."

"Small enough to be *chosen*," she corrected. "There's a difference."

His eye closed again. But his tail moved once, slowly, against the stone — the wolf equivalent of a man swallowing a speech he wasn't ready to give.

* * *

Summer came, and with it the anniversary, and the city did not hold a parade, which everyone agreed was correct. Instead, on the night of the thirteenth moon's turning — the moon that had once meant deadline, seal-collapse, war — Vellharrow lit candles in windows. Just candles. Anyone might light one; anyone might not. Tam put theirs on the sill and stood beside it with his arm around Odile, and Xanthe noticed he was crying quietly and pretended not to, because that was also love.

Reeve's grave was on the hillside behind the old shrine-city, facing east, because he had spent his whole life watching for threats from the east and Xanthe thought he should get to watch sunrises instead. They had buried his sword with him. Maren had spoken the rites — the new ones, the ones that required nothing but memory — and Tam had gotten drunk afterward and told stories until dawn, and Odile had planted rosemary on the mound, because rosemary is for remembrance and Odile never wasted a good herb.

Xanthe visited alone in summer, bringing a small carved bird she set among the rosemary.

"You'd hate all this peace," she told the grave. "You'd find something to be vigilant about. Probably the pigeons." She sat a while in the grass. "I forgave you, you know. For the purge, for the orders, for making yourself the wall between them and me. I forgave you before the end, but I don't think I understood it until after. You spent yourself like coin — the thing I swore no one would ever do again. And it was still the bravest thing I ever saw." She touched the carved bird. "So I keep you here. Invited. That's how it works now. Even the dead get to choose."

The wind came up the hill smelling of cut hay and distant rain, and for a moment — just a moment — she thought she heard something in it that was almost a voice saying *good work, carver*, and almost Reeve's dry baritone, and entirely her own heart. She let it be ambiguous. Ambiguity was a kind of mercy too.

* * *

Autumn. The shop was busy now — busier than it had ever been in the hidden years. People came from three towns over for charms, and not because they feared the dead, but because they loved them. That was the difference, Xane thought, planing a rib bone smooth: the whole difference, the entire revolution, contained in one small preposition. Not prayer dragged out of terror. Prayer offered out of love, and refusable, and therefore real.

One evening a boy of perhaps ten came in, gap-toothed, solemn, clutching a coin.

"I want to learn," he said. "Carving. My gran says you hear the dead in bone and I want to hear my da. He died in the flood year."

Xanthe crouched to his height. "It's a gift," she said carefully. "Not everyone has it. And those who do — it costs something. You hear griefs you didn't ask for. Are you sure?"

He nodded so hard his hair flopped. Then he hesitated. "Do I have to decide now? Can I come back?"

Something in her chest went soft and wide as sky. "You can come back whenever you want," she said. "As many times as you want. That's the rule now. Everything's the rule now."

He left with a scrap of practice bone and instructions, and Tam watched him go and then looked at Xanthe with his eyebrows raised.

"You just founded a school."

"I did not."

"You absolutely did. By spring there'll be six of them and Odile will be teaching them herb-lore and I'll be selling their parents charcoal." He grinned, defrocked priest turned smuggler turned shopkeeper turned whatever-he-was-now, and threw an arm around her shoulders. "Wolf and garden, eh? We built the garden."

"We're still building it."

* * *

Winter again. One year, nearly to the day, since the thirteenth moon.

Snow lay thick on Vellharrow, and the shop was warm, and Xanthe sat at her bench late into the evening working on a commission she had given no one else: a garden gate, wrought in antler bone, tall as her forearm and fine as lace.

She had been planning it since summer. Every element meant something. The bars were crossed like growing stems, because a gate is a living thing's edge, not a wall's. At the top, a wolf's head looked outward — not snarling, not guarding. *Watching*. Waiting at a threshold. Below it, flowers she had chosen one by one: rosemary for Reeve. Sage for Odile's healing. Rowan for protection freely given. And threaded through the latticework, so fine you had to tilt it to the light to see them, thirteen moons, full circle, complete — and past the thirteenth, a fourteenth space, left empty.

Room to grow. Room for whatever came next, unwritten.

When the last curl of shavings fell away, she held the gate up to the lamplight and breathed on it, the way she used to breathe on charms in the hidden years, the old instinct of a woman hiding evidence. But there was nothing to hide. The bone stayed plain and beautiful and silent, waiting — because silence now was not suppression. Silence was patience. The gate would speak when someone asked it to.

She carried it outside, snow squeaking under her boots, breath smoking in the blue dark.

He was awake, of course. He was always awake on the step, though he slept more than he used to, dreamed more, she suspected, dreamed the dreams of an ordinary animal and found them strange and precious. Snow dusted his back and he hadn't bothered to shake it off.

"For you," Xanthe said.

One eye opened. Found the gate. Stayed on it a long moment.

"It's a door," he said at last, and his voice was rough with something he didn't try to hide. "You carved me a door."

"A gate. Doors shut. Gates you choose to walk through." She knelt in the snow, setting it before him. "Here's what it means. Read it however you like — you're better at reading carvings than anyone living. The wolf at the top isn't a prisoner at a threshold. He's a companion at one. The flowers aren't tribute. They're gifts. And the empty space after the thirteen moons—" she tapped it gently, "—that's yours. Whatever comes next, nobody's written yet. Not Sereth, not the church, not fate. Not even you."

"Little carver." His breath fogged. "You know what I was going to ask you. Tonight. On this anniversary. I have been composing the question for a month like a courting bird."

"I know. You pace. Wolves shouldn't pace, it's undignified."

"I was going to ask—" he stopped. Started again, humbler: "Whether there might be a place inside. Not as creditor. Not as god. As—" the word cost him, and he paid it anyway, because that was what he had learned, "—as family."

Xanthe smiled, and the snow fell softly, and somewhere inside the shop Tam was laughing at one of Odile's terrible jokes, and the candle in the window burned for anyone who needed it.

"Veyran," she said, "you've been family since the night you wouldn't tell me why the gods were knocking. You just owed a debt first." She stood, and pushed open the door — her door, her shop, her life — wide and warm and gold-lit against the snow. "Now it's a gift. Come in."

The wolf rose, shook the winter from his coat, and stepped across the threshold — invited, choosing, free.

Behind them on the bench, in the lamplight, the antler garden gate caught the fire glow and seemed, for just a moment, to stand open onto a world where every faith was a door someone had chosen to knock on — and every knock could be answered, or not, in love.
