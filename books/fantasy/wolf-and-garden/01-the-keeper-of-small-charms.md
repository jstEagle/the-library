## 1. The Keeper of Small Charms

The luck-knots sold best on rainy nights, which was a small irony Quentin Eastwick had long since stopped finding funny.

Rain made people think about luck. It made them think about the thinness of the rope between one day and the next — the cart wheel that might slip, the fever that might find a child in the night, the Ministry wagon that might turn down your street for no reason at all. So they came to his stall in Vell's night market with their hoods dripping and their coins counted twice, and they bought knots woven from river-reed and three strands of red thread, and they walked home believing, a little, that tomorrow would be kind to them.

Quentin let them believe it. That was most of the trade, really. The other part was that every knot he tied *did* hold a whisper of working in it — not enough to light a candle, not enough to interest anyone with a badge and a warrant, but enough that a luck-knot from the Eastwick stall genuinely did tip fortune's shoulder now and then. He charged fairly. He under-promised. He never, ever smiled too widely while doing it, because mages in stories always smiled too widely, and in Vell the stories were law.

"Fever-charm," said a woman, shaking rain off her shawl like a wet dog. "For my mother. She's got the cough again."

"Charm helps the sleeping," Quentin said, taking down one of the little tin medallions stamped with a leaf pattern so worn it was nearly abstract. "It doesn't cure anything. If she's coughing blood by Thursday, you take her to a proper physician and you don't waste money on more of these."

"You're supposed to say it cures everything," the woman said. "That's what the man on Cordwainer Street says."

"The man on Cordwainer Street is going to be arrested within the month, and I'd rather you spent your coin here while he's still open." He wrapped the charm in brown paper. "Two coppers. Tell her to sleep with the window cracked. Half of medicine is air."

She paid, and smiled despite herself, and went out into the rain, and Quentin watched her go with the particular ache he felt after every such transaction — because the woman thought she'd met a charm-seller, and what she'd actually met was the last warden of the Hollow Gate, standing in the wet dark selling two-copper trinkets to fund his real work, which was keeping a door shut.

Not a door like the ones in houses. A door like the ones at the end of the world.

* * *

His stall stood at the mouth of Tanners' Row, where the night market spilled out of the covered arcade and into the street proper, lanterns strung between awnings on ropes gone gray with age. Around him the market breathed: the fishmongers shouting their last prices, the spice-seller whose cinnamon had once been smuggled through Thorn before the borders closed and who still priced it like contraband, the old men playing tiles under the arcade's dry arches and cheating each other with the serene dishonesty of fifty years' practice.

Three years ago, Quentin had inherited all of this — the stall, the shop above it, the cellar below *that*, and the thing beneath the cellar — from a dying woman who had held his hand and given him instructions instead of goodbye.

*"You'll want to run," his mother had said, her voice already half somewhere else, traded-away thin. "Everyone wants to run. Don't. Runners die tired; keepers just die. It's better work."*

She had taught him the wards in eleven days. She had been given those eleven days by something that lived near the Gate and ate names, and she had paid for them with hers, and Quentin had never learned what her name had been before — only that afterward she'd called herself nothing, answered to everything, and looked at him sometimes as if trying to remember why his face mattered.

On the twelfth day she was gone, and Quentin had come downstairs into the cellar and stood in front of the Hollow Gate and understood, finally and completely, what he was.

The Gate was woven thorn and iron, older than the city above it, older than any city. It hummed when you put your hand flat against it — a low note like a struck bell heard through water. Through its seams, on certain nights, light leaked: not lamplight, not moonlight, a green-gold glow like sun through leaves in a country that had never existed. Behind it lay Thorn, the wild realm, the exiled magic, everything Vell had burned its mages to forget. The covenant had sealed it centuries ago. The covenant had frayed. And someone had to sit in a shop selling luck-knots and make sure the fraying stayed slow.

He checked the wards twice a day, morning and midnight, walking the cellar's perimeter with a candle stub and a length of knotted cord, counting the knots the way a sailor counts bells. Forty-one knots meant quiet. Fewer than forty-one meant the Gate had been chewing at the bindings in the night, and he'd have to re-tie, sweating, hands shaking, while the humming climbed up through the floorboards like something singing to itself in its sleep.

In three years, the count had dropped below forty-one four times.

He told himself that was a good record. Most nights, he almost believed it.

* * *

"Eastwick! You alive down there or do I get your awning?"

Wren. Of course it was Wren, appearing out of the crowd like a coin out of an ear, a wiry fence in a coat with seventeen pockets and rain beaded on her cropped gray hair. She was sixty if she was a day and could outrun a constable, which she proved annually.

"I'm alive," Quentin said. "Barely. Business is drowned rats and nothing else."

"Drowned rats buy charms." She leaned on his counter, produced a paper twist of salted plums, and offered him one. He took it. This was their whole friendship, more or less: salted plums, insults, and the unspoken understanding that Wren moved his rarer stock through channels he preferred not to examine. "Your fever-charms are wanted in Harrow Lane. Three households. Also—" she dropped her voice, and the humor went out of her face like a snuffed lamp, "—there were gray coats at the fountain an hour ago. Asking questions. Not the usual questions."

Quentin's hands kept tying the knot he was working on. He'd learned that much, at least: whatever happened, keep your hands doing something ordinary.

"What sort of questions?"

"Not 'have you seen a mage.' The usual questions are 'have you seen a mage.'" She popped a plum into her mouth. "These were 'does anyone here deal in charms,' and 'who supplies them,' and — this is the one I didn't like — 'are any of the shops built on older foundations.'"

The knot under Quentin's fingers pulled tight. He made himself loosen it.

"Foundations," he repeated.

"Mm." Wren gazed out at the rainy market with the mild expression of a woman discussing weather. "Now, I know a certain shop on Tanners' Row that's older than every building touching it. I know its cellar goes down further than cellars ought. And I know the young man who runs it gets a look on his face when I mention the word *Ministry*, like a cat hearing the word *bath*. So." She turned back to him. "Is there anything I should be knowing, Quentin, that would help me avoid being hanged next to you?"

"No," Quentin said pleasantly. "I sell charms. My cellar has pickles in it."

"Pickles."

"Aged in brine. Very traditional."

"Three years I've known you," Wren said, "and you've never once offered me a pickle."

"They're for emergencies."

She snorted, took her plums, and melted into the crowd, and Quentin stood alone at his stall in the rain-light feeling the way he always felt after conversations with Wren — which was grateful, and seen-through, and lonelier than before she'd come. Because the truth was there was no one in Vell he could tell. Not the fishmonger, not the tile-players, not the woman buying fever-charms for her mother. Three years of checking knots at midnight and re-tieing wards with shaking hands and lying, cheerfully, constantly, about pickles. Three years, and his closest confidant was a sixty-year-old criminal who thought he was hiding smuggling ledgers.

Some nights, after the market closed, he sat on the cellar steps and talked to the Gate. Just talk — the weather, the price of reed, a joke he'd heard. The Gate hummed back at him, toneless, patient, indifferent. It was the longest conversation he'd had with anything in months, and it wasn't a conversation at all, and he was aware of how pathetic that was, and he did it anyway.

* * *

The Ministry came at the worst possible hour, which was presumably policy.

Midnight-minus-a-quarter. Quentin was in the cellar, halfway through the ward-check, candle stub guttering, counting knots — thirty-nine, forty, forty-*one*, all quiet, all well — when the knock came at the shop door above. Three knocks. Evenly spaced. The knock of a person who has never in their life knocked urgently, because urgency is for people who can be made to wait.

Quentin blew out the candle and stood in the dark, listening.

The knock came again. Then a voice, muffled by floorboards: "Ministry of Unnatural Affairs. Open the door."

There was no rule for this. His mother had left rules for the Gate, rules for the wards, rules for what to do if the seams wept light or the humming changed pitch or something knocked *from the other side* — but she had died believing the Ministry would never look twice at a charm-stall. Everyone believed that. That was the whole architecture of his life: be small, be dull, be a man who sells knots to nervous people, and the fire that burns mages would pass over your roof.

He climbed the stairs slowly, giving himself time to arrange his face into pleasant, dim concern. In the mirror by the door — a habit — he checked: no soot on his hands (ward-ash), no thorn-scratch visible at his collar, the iron key to the cellar lifted from its nail and slipped into his sleeve where it pressed cold against his wrist like a warning.

He opened the door on two gray coats and a lantern.

"Evening," Quentin said. Rain blew in past them. "Lost?"

The taller of the two held up a document case, wax-sealed, official-looking enough to make a bishop nervous. "Quentin Eastwick. Proprietor?"

"That's me."

"We're conducting inspections of premises in the night market district. You'll admit us for a search."

"Search of what?" Quentin scratched his head, aiming for baffled and hoping he'd landed somewhere near it. "I've got charms, some thread, and a kettle."

"You'll admit us," the inspector repeated, in the tone of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice, "for a search."

Behind them, down the row, Quentin could see other lanterns. Other doors. The gray coats had spread along Tanners' Row like frost — six stalls, eight, ten — and at each one, merchants were being politely, terribly awake at midnight. Wren's empty stall. The spice-seller standing with his arms folded, furious and helpless.

They weren't looking for him specifically. That was worse, somehow. They were looking at *foundations*.

"Of course," Quentin said, and stepped aside. "Mind the puddle. Roof leaks."

They searched the way Ministry men searched: thoroughly, joylessly, without apology. The tall one went through his stock drawers, holding luck-knots up to the lantern with two fingers as though they might bite. The short one — younger, with the over-careful movements of a new recruit — examined the kettle, the ledger, the jar of reed-soak.

"Where's your cellar?" the tall one asked.

Quentin had prepared for this. He had prepared for this for three years; he had a speech about flooding, about the stairs being unsafe, about the pickles.

"Down there," he said, "but I warn you, it flooded last spring. Everything smells of brine. You may not thank me for the experience."

The tall one gave him a look that suggested thanks was not among the Ministry's offerings, and started toward the cellar stair — and that was when the Gate, forty feet below, chose to *hum*.

It wasn't loud. It was never loud. But it traveled up through stone and timber the way cold travels up through boot-soles, and the younger inspector stopped mid-step and cocked his head like a dog hearing a whistle no one else can hear.

"Do you hear that?"

"Hear what?" Quentin said.

The humming swelled, faint and green-gold and utterly wrong for a pickle cellar, and Quentin felt sweat start cold between his shoulder blades, and his hand found the iron key in his sleeve and gripped it, though gripping it accomplished absolutely nothing—

"It's pipes," said the tall inspector flatly. "Old quarter. Everything groans. Move."

The young one hesitated a moment longer, head tilted, listening to a note that Quentin knew — knew with sick certainty — had risen to greet the touch of magic coming down the stairs. The Gate recognized wardens. The Gate apparently also recognized *inspectors of unnatural affairs*, and had opinions.

"Pipes," the young one said finally, unconvinced, and followed his senior down.

What followed were the nine longest minutes of Quentin's life. He stood at the top of the stairs holding the lantern, watching two gray coats move through the amber light below — past the pickle barrels (real; he'd actually brined them, out of spite), past the shelves of dried herbs, toward the back wall where a rack of old sacking hung. Behind the sacking: the inner door. Behind the inner door: the stair down to the Gate itself, and the threshold-wards that would, if crossed by unauthorized feet, do things Quentin had never witnessed and prayed nightly never to see.

The tall inspector lifted a corner of the sacking. Looked at the wall behind it. Looked at the inner door — plain oak, iron-banded, locked, and above all *locked* — and said:

"What's through there?"

"Root cellar," Quentin said. "Older section. Flooded worse."

The inspector rattled the handle once. The lock held, because the lock was not entirely a lock, and Quentin kept his breathing even through sheer professional discipline. Then the inspector grunted, dropped the sacking, and turned around.

"You're clean," he said, in the voice of a man disappointed to say so. "For now. We'll be back, Eastwick. There's a list, and you're on it."

"There's a list?"

"Everyone's on a list." He climbed the stairs past Quentin and paused at the top, close enough that Quentin could smell wet wool and lamp oil. "Funny thing," he added quietly. "My grandmother used to buy her fever-charms from a stall on this row. Before the burnings. Said the charm-man's knots never failed." His eyes flicked, once, to Quentin's hands. "Never failed."

Then he was gone into the rain, and the short one went after him, glancing back exactly once — at the cellar door, not at Quentin — and the night swallowed them both.

Quentin bolted the shop door. Stood with his forehead against the wood until his heart remembered its ordinary rhythm.

Then he went down, past the pickles, past the sacking, through the inner door and down the deep stair, to stand before the Hollow Gate with his palm flat against woven thorn and iron, feeling it hum against his skin like a vast animal purring in its sleep.

"Don't," he told it softly. "Whatever that was — whatever you're waking up for — *don't*. We stay quiet. Quiet is how we live."

The Gate hummed. It did not agree. It never agreed; it only endured, the way mountains endure weather.

But that night, for the first time in three years, the knot-count came up *forty-two* — one more binding than his mother had ever managed — as if the Gate itself had decided, inexplicably, to hold on tighter. As if something on the far side, or the near side, or the side that hadn't happened yet, had reached through and helped.

Quentin stood in the green-gold leak-light for a long time, counting the impossible extra knot, and could not shake the feeling that his three years of quiet had just ended — that somewhere out in the rainy city, a page was turning, and his name and his door were written on it.

He just couldn't yet read the date.
