## 2. A Date Written in Ink

The book smelled of drowned things.

Isolde Vane had restored four hundred volumes in her eleven years at the Bindery of Saint Corvin, and she had a private taxonomy for the ways water ruined them. There was flood-water, which left silt in the gutters and turned pages to felt. There was rain-through-a-broken-roof, which made tide-marks like rings on a tree. And then there was this — the rarest kind, the kind her master called "weeping water," which soaked into vellum slowly, over years, as though the book itself had been crying.

She set it on her bench beneath the good lamp and peeled back its covers with a bone folder and infinite patience.

It was a fat thing, bound in green leather gone black with damp, its spine stitched twice — once by an original binder, once by someone later, clumsier, in thread that still held a faint smell of iron. The title page was illegible except for one word: *Thirteen*. No author. No date. Just a bookseller's pencil ghost on the flyleaf: *Bought Vell, winter market, three coppers.*

"Three coppers," Isolde murmured to it. "You were underpriced."

The Bindery closed at dusk, but Master Ollivan had gone home early with his gout, and the junior apprentices had scattered at the first bell, and so Isolde stayed alone with the lamp and the drowned book and the particular happiness she never told anyone about — that feeling of being the first person in decades, maybe centuries, to read a page.

Her work was slow by nature and slower by temperament. She relaxed the swollen leaves with steam from a kettle, pressed them between felts, eased apart the ones that had fused into bricks. She mended tears with Japanese tissue thinner than breath. She did not read ahead. That was the rule she'd made for herself years ago, when she'd caught herself skimming a private diary instead of repairing it: *restore first, read after.* A book deserved to be whole before it was known.

But some pages refused to wait.

The twelfth leaf came free of its fusing with a soft sigh, and there, in ink that had faded to the color of weak tea, she saw a list. Dates, actually — thirteen of them, written down a column in a hand that trembled only slightly, each beside a small annotation. Most of the annotations had washed away entirely. Two survived.

Beside a date one hundred and six years past: *the witness is born.*

And beside tomorrow's date — tomorrow, the ninth of Thawmonth, the day after the spring equinox, a date she knew because she'd been complaining about the mud on her walk to work all week — in a steadier, more deliberate hand:

*Isolde Vane comes to the gate.*

The kettle went on steaming. The lamp hummed. Somewhere out in the dark streets of Vell, a drunk sang something about a fishmonger's wife.

Isolde did not move for what she would later estimate was four full minutes.

Then she said, aloud, to no one, "That's not possible," in the same tone she used on apprentices who claimed a client's dog had eaten a folio. Firm. Dismissive. Slightly too loud.

Because names could be coincidence, couldn't they? Isolde wasn't even a rare name; there were two other Isoldes within walking distance of the Bindery, an apothecary and a woman who sold buttons. And *Vane* — well. But *comes to the gate*? What gate? Vell had gates, seven of them, all heavily taxed and none of them the sort of thing you *came* to, exactly, unless you were a cart of onions.

Unless it meant something else. Unless it meant—

She turned the page.

There was a drawing.

It was small, tucked into the lower corner of the leaf as if the artist hadn't wanted it noticed, done in the same tea-colored ink with a few strokes of white chalk heightening that had somehow survived the drowning. It showed a man from the chest up, standing behind what looked like a market stall — a canopy of striped cloth, strings of small knotted charms hanging from the frame. He was youngish, maybe thirty, with dark hair pushed back off a high forehead and a jaw that suggested he'd recently decided to stop shaving and then changed his mind halfway through. His eyes were the most detailed part of the sketch, which was unusual; artists usually lavished their attention on hands or drapery. These eyes were drawn tired. Kind, but tired, the way a lighthouse must be tired.

Underneath, in that deliberate hand: *He will not want her. She will come anyway.*

"Oh, no," Isolde said. "No, thank you."

She had been raised by a practical mother and a superstitious grandmother, and had inherited exactly half of each: she believed in nothing she couldn't mend, but she also believed you should always know where your salt was. Prophecy fell firmly into the category of things other people needed. She mended books. Books contained prophecies the way houses contained rats — a fact of the trade, dealt with professionally, never invited inside.

And yet.

She looked at the sketch again. At the tired kind eyes. At the stall with its little knots of string.

*He will not want her. She will come anyway.*

"I'm not coming anywhere," she informed the book, with dignity. "I have work. I have a commission due on a psalter and a kettle that whistles in B-flat and a landlady who notices if I'm home late." She smoothed the page flat with both palms, gently, the way she touched everything, because everything she touched was older than she was and deserved gentleness. "Whatever you think happens tomorrow, I am going to spend it right here, being sensible."

The lamp flickered. Drafts, certainly. Old buildings breathed.

But when she packed up for the night, she wrapped the prophecy book in dry wool instead of leaving it on the rack, and carried it home through the mud-slick lanes of Vell with her coat buttoned wrong and her heart doing something she refused to name.

* * *

She slept badly and dreamed of doors.

In the morning she was late, which never happened. In the morning she argued with herself in front of the mirror while braiding her hair, one voice saying *you are a professional woman with a psalter commission*, the other saying *but what if*, and the first voice winning every round until the last, when the second voice pointed out — quietly, reasonably — that if she didn't go and look, just look, from across the square, she would spend the entire day with her hands shaking over the tissue paper and ruin a page she could never forgive herself for ruining.

So she went. To look. From across the square.

The night market in Vell ran along the canal behind the tanneries, where rents were cheap and the Ministry of Unnatural Affairs patrolled rarely, because nothing worth burning ever happened among the fish-smokers and the rag-sellers. By day it was mostly shuttered. But a few stalls traded at all hours — and there, at the mouth of Cooper's Row, exactly where a person might stand if they wanted to be seen by everyone and known by no one, stood a striped canvas canopy with strings of little knotted charms swaying in the canal wind.

Behind it sat a man with dark hair pushed off a high forehead, unshaved in a way that suggested deliberation rather than neglect, reading a book he was holding upside down.

He wasn't reading it at all. He was watching the street over the top of it, and pretending otherwise, and doing it badly — badly enough that Isolde, who had spent eleven years noticing how things were put together, noticed immediately. He had the look of a man keeping watch without admitting it. A sentry dressed as a shopkeeper. The lighthouse, wearing a fisherman's coat.

*He will not want her,* said the tea-colored memory of the ink.

*I haven't decided I want him either,* Isolde replied internally, with great maturity.

She meant to circle the square. She meant to buy a bun from the baker's cart, glance over once, confirm that reality was duller than ink, and go home to her kettle. That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had the word *sensible* written all over it.

Instead her feet took her directly across the cobbles to the stall, and she heard herself say, in a voice that came from somewhere above and slightly behind her actual body:

"Do you have anything for bad decisions?"

The man lowered his upside-down book. Up close he was taller than the sketch implied, and younger, and the tiredness around his eyes resolved into something sharper — wariness, quick and total, like a door closing. He looked at her the way she imagined people looked at letters from the tax office: braced, polite, already composing a refusal.

"That depends," he said. His voice was low and careful. "Whose decision?"

"Mine. Made about an hour ago. Possibly several years ago, depending on how you count." She was aware that she was babbling and found she couldn't stop. "I restore books. Yesterday I opened one that had been water-damaged for — I don't know, a century, maybe longer. And it had my name in it. My full name. Beside today's date. And—" she stopped herself one syllable before *your face*, and course-corrected, badly, "—and it mentioned a gate."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise — recognition, which was worse. Surprise would have let her laugh this off. Recognition meant the book had been right, and he'd known it would be.

"We sell luck-knots," he said. "Fever-charms. Dampeners for nightmares, though those are just lavender and confidence. Nothing for decisions. Decisions are outside my jurisdiction."

"Everything's outside your jurisdiction," Isolde said. "That's rather the point of a stall like this, isn't it? Harmless little charms, harmless little man, nothing here worth a Ministry raid." She winced inwardly — that had come out crueler than intended — but she watched the words land and watched him not flinch, and understood that flinching was a luxury he'd trained out of himself long ago. "I'm sorry. That was rude. I've been rude since yesterday, mostly to a book."

To her astonishment, the corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. More like a smile considering an application. "Mostly to a book," he repeated. "What did the book do?"

"It lied."

"Books don't lie." He set the upside-down volume down — she caught the title, a treatise on canal tariffs, chosen precisely because no living soul would ask about it — and began tidying his charms with unnecessary busyness, his fingers quick and brown-knuckled. "They get misread. They get mistranslated. They get copied by idiots. But lying takes intent, and paper has never once in history managed it."

"This one wrote my name next to today's date a hundred years ago."

His hands went still. One charm — a knot of red thread, the cheapest thing on the table — swung gently on its string between them, ticking like a slow clock.

"You should take the fever-charm," he said quietly. "You're warm. You're talking fast. Fever does that."

"I'm not feverish."

"Then take the luck-knot. Red thread, three loops. It's two coppers and it does nothing, which makes it the most honest item I stock." He held it out, and she noticed his fingers weren't entirely steady either, and that small shared tremor did more to convince her than any argument could have. He was afraid. He had been afraid since she walked up, and he was afraid *of her arrival*, which meant he knew what she was arriving *for*.

She didn't take the knot. Instead she leaned across the counter, dropped her voice to match his, and asked the question she'd been carrying since dawn like a stone in her shoe:

"What happens tonight?"

The market noise seemed to lean in around them — the fish-smoke, the gulls arguing over the canal, a cart wheel squeaking somewhere. For a moment Quentin Eastwick — though she didn't yet know his name, wouldn't learn it until much later, under worse circumstances — looked at her with those drawn-tired, chalk-heightened eyes, and Isolde saw him weigh every possible answer and find every one of them a kind of trap.

"Nothing," he said finally. "Tonight nothing happens. That's the whole job. Nothing, forever, if I do it right."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best answer I have." He withdrew the luck-knot, wound its string neatly around his finger, and looked at her with something that was almost — almost — kindness. "Go home, mistress bookbinder. Mend your psalter. Whatever your drowned book says about tonight, I promise you the truth is smaller and drier."

"The book also says," Isolde said, "that you won't want me here. And that I'll come anyway."

For the second time, his hands stopped moving. This time they didn't start again.

"And the third thing it says," she went on, because now that she'd started she couldn't seem to stop, the words pouring out with the momentum of a year of sensible behavior finally cracking, "is underneath your portrait, in the margin, in handwriting so steady it must have been written by someone who'd already seen the ending. It says—"

"Don't," he said. Very softly.

"*The moon begins counting tonight.*"

Above Cooper's Row, past the striped canopy and the strings of harmless knots, the afternoon sun was sliding westward, dragging the evening behind it like a hem. And on the inside of Isolde Vane's left wrist, in a place she had scratched that morning without thinking and now thought of with a new and crawling attention, something faint stirred beneath the skin — not pain, not yet, but a warmth, like a page held too close to a candle.

Quentin Eastwick stared at her wrist as if he could see through to it. Perhaps he could. Then he reached beneath his stall, blew out the little oil lamp that had no business being lit at midday anyway, and said, in a voice stripped of all its careful shopkeeper's calm:

"Close the canopy. We need to talk — and not here."
