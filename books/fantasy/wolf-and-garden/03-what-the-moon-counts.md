## 3. What the Moon Counts

The prophecy book lay open between them on Quentin's workbench like a patient animal, and neither of them wanted to be the first to touch it.

Isolde had been the one to bring it, of course — she'd carried it down the hill from her bindery in a satchel wrapped in oilcloth, as though it might bite. Which, Quentin was beginning to suspect, it might.

"Read it again," he said. "The part about the moons."

She sighed the sigh of a woman who had read that part eleven times already and knew exactly what it said. But she put on her spectacles anyway, which Quentin found unfairly charming, and bent over the vellum by lamplight.

"*When thirteen turns of the moon have counted,*" she read, her voice taking on the careful flatness people use for things that frighten them, "*the Hollow shall stand at its dividing. One hand shall seal, and one hand shall sever, and between those hands the realms shall hold their breath.*"

"Seal or sever," Quentin said. "Wonderful. The cosmos gives us a menu and both options are terrible."

"It's not a menu, it's a—"

"A choice between bricking up the door forever and knocking the whole wall down. Yes. I've read it too." He turned his mug of chicory in a slow circle on the bench. "There used to be a third option. My mother used to say there were always three doors, but the book only remembers two because nobody wrote down the third."

"What happened to your mother?"

Quentin looked at the ceiling beam, where a charm against damp hung in its little cage of copper wire, swaying slightly, though there was no draft.

"She sold something she couldn't afford," he said. "We'll get to that. Probably around moon ten, if the book has any dramatic sense. It does. Books always do."

* * *

The problem with Isolde Vane — one of the problems, and Quentin kept a list, because lists made him feel organized about catastrophe — was that she did not scare properly.

He'd tried everything. He'd shown her the Ministry notices pasted to the lampposts, the ones with the woodcut flames and the cheerful slogan BURN THE UNNATURAL, KEEP VELL NATURAL, printed by the same press that did the fishmongers' guild circulars, which explained why the flames looked suspiciously like herrings. He'd told her what happened to mages when they were caught: the trial lasted an afternoon, the pyre lasted somewhat longer, and the crowd brought snacks. He'd even, in a moment of desperation, opened the trapdoor in his back room just wide enough for her to feel the cold breath rising from beneath the floor, the breath of a door that should never be breathed on.

Isolde had peered into the dark, adjusted her spectacles, and said, "It hums."

"That's not humming. That's hunger. There's a difference, and the difference is that humming is pleasant."

"Well, I thought it was rather like a hive." She'd straightened up, dusting her knees. "My grandmother kept bees."

And now here she was, three days later, still in his shop at closing time, reorganizing his shelf of luck-knots by color instead of by strength, because — she claimed — customers bought with their eyes. She had moved in the way people move into a place they intend to stay: a spare shawl draped over the chair, a tin of tea leaves on the mantel, her mending basket tucked beside the hearth as though it had grown there.

"You can't stay," Quentin told her, for the fourth time that evening.

"Mm," said Isolde, who was holding a luck-knot up to the light.

"I mean it. You have a life. A bindery. A trade. People who know you as a respectable woman who repairs books instead of a fugitive-in-waiting who consorts with—" he gestured at himself, all of himself, the charms and the cellar and the three years of lies folded small inside his coat "—this."

She set down the knot. She took off her spectacles, which meant business; she only removed them to clean them or to deliver verdicts.

"Quentin Eastwick. Read me the next verse."

"I don't think—"

"The next verse. Please."

Because she said please like that, he picked up the book. The vellum was soft as mothwing under his thumb. He found the place.

"*The witness of the first blood shall stand at the threshold,*" he read slowly, "*and the mark of turning shall come upon her wrist, and the gate shall know its own.*" He stopped. "I hate this verse."

"You hate it because it's you reading it and me standing here."

"I hate it because 'witness of the first blood' sounds like a title someone gets right before they're killed."

"It sounds like a title someone gets," Isolde said, "right before they're needed. Those aren't the same thing." She rolled up her sleeve — briskly, the way she did everything, as though her own arm were a stubborn page refusing to lie flat. "Look."

On the inside of her wrist, pale as a scar made of moonlight, sat a thin crescent. It hadn't been there yesterday. It hadn't been there an hour ago. It sat just below the skin like the moon behind cloud, and when Quentin touched it — gently, with one fingertip, the way you touch a sleeping animal — it was cool, then warm, then cool again, breathing on some slower clock than hers.

"The first turn," he said. His mouth had gone dry. "That means the count has started. Twelve more moons, Isolde. Twelve more marks. And then the thirteenth night, and the choice, and—" He made himself stop, because he could hear his own voice climbing toward the register his mother had called his hen-with-a-fox voice, and hens, in his experience, survived foxes mainly by being somewhere else. "You should go to your sister's. In Corrow. It's far. The far is the point."

"No."

"It wasn't a real question."

"No," Isolde agreed pleasantly, rolling her sleeve back down over the crescent. "It wasn't."

* * *

They argued until the lamp burned low, which in Quentin's shop was a genuine unit of time, since the lamp was charmed to burn low whenever conversations got serious — a mercy from his mother, or possibly a cruelty, hard to say.

"You don't understand what the Ministry does," Quentin said. "You've seen the posters. Posters are the polite version. Wren — my fence, the woman who moves half my stock — she got searched last month, and they found a fever-charm, one fever-charm, the kind grandmothers sew into collars, and they held her for two days and broke three of her fingers asking who taught her the stitch. She didn't even know. Her grandmother taught her. Dead twenty years, and they broke her fingers over it anyway, for practice."

Isolde had gone very quiet. Good, Quentin thought. Finally. Scared is sensible.

Then she said, "Show me how to make one. A fever-charm."

"That's — no. Absolutely not. Do you know what happens to people who learn? Knowing is the crime. The knot is just evidence."

"So teach me nothing," she said, "and let me watch nothing, and I'll learn nothings until I can do them myself." She folded her arms. Under the folded sleeves, he knew, the crescent was keeping its slow time. "Quentin. I found my name in a hundred-year-old book. I found your face sketched in the margin — and it is your face, I'd know it anywhere, including the way your hair refuses to lie down on the left side. Tomorrow's date was written under my name before I was born. Whatever this is, it isn't something I can walk away from by going to Corrow. The book would just follow me. Books do. I repair them for a living; I know how they travel."

"They don't follow people."

"This one does. It followed me to your stall the exact evening it promised it would. I walked past forty other stalls and my feet decided for me." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not brave, Quentin. I want to be clear about that. I'm curious, and curiosity is braver than courage and twice as stupid, and I've been cursed with it since I was six years old. You cannot send me away. You can only decide whether I do this with you or without you, and I promise you the second version will involve me breaking into your cellar within the week."

Quentin stared at her. The lamp guttered obligingly.

"My hair doesn't refuse to lie down."

"It absolutely does. It's the first thing I noticed about you. Well. The second."

He laughed before he could stop himself — a short, rusty sound, like a door opening after years shut. Three years, he realized. Three years of selling luck-knots to people who needed them and lying to everyone who asked and checking the trapdoor every single night before bed, alone, and here was this woman with ink stains on her fingers and a moon growing under her skin, threatening to break into his cellar if he didn't invite her.

His mother would have adored her. That was the worst part. That was the truly unforgivable part.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. But we do this my way. Rules."

"Rules," Isolde agreed, in the tone of someone accepting terms they fully intend to renegotiate.

"One: you don't touch the gate. Not the door itself. Ever. It knows you already; we don't need it getting ideas."

"Two?"

"Two: you learn the small charms and only the small charms. Luck, fever, finding-things. Nothing with a price attached. Small magic is honest work. Big magic is debt."

"Three?"

He hesitated. Rule three had been going to be *you keep your name*, which sounded mad out loud, so instead he said, "Three: when I tell you to run, you run. No arguments, no heroics. You run, and you let me handle whatever's behind us."

Isolde considered him across the workbench, lamplight catching the ink on her knuckles and the crescent just visible at her cuff.

"All right," she said. "One through three. And my rule: you stop lying to me by omission. If something is coming — the Architect, the Ministry, your mother's bargain, whatever it is you keep almost saying and then swallowing — you tell me before it arrives, not after. I can't witness anything if everyone keeps shielding the witness."

"That's—" He ran a hand through the hair that refused, on principle, to lie down. "That's a reasonable rule, which makes me suspicious of it."

"I'm a bookbinder. We're terrifyingly reasonable. Ask anyone."

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the mark woke up.

Isolde felt it first as warmth — a coin of heat at her wrist while she sat mending the spine of a hymnal by the banked fire, Quentin asleep in his chair across the room with the prophecy book still open on his chest, rising and falling with his breath. She set down the needle. She pushed back her sleeve.

The crescent had brightened. Not glowing, exactly — more that the rest of the world had dimmed around it, as though her wrist were the only thing in Vell remembering the moon tonight. And beneath the fire's crackle and Quentin's breathing, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she'd heard through the trapdoor: the hum. The hive-hum. Rising up through the floorboards, through the soles of her feet, through her bones, and meeting the crescent on her wrist like two notes finding each other.

*The gate shall know its own.*

"Oh," Isolde said softly, to no one, to the fire, to the moon doing its slow arithmetic somewhere above the rooftops. "Oh, that's what that means."

Across the room, Quentin woke all at once, the way keepers must learn to — no drifting, no drowsy blinking, just suddenly entirely awake, the book sliding from his chest as he sat up.

"What time is it," he said. Not *what's wrong*. *What time*. As though time itself were the thing most likely to betray them.

"Nearly midnight. Quentin — the mark. It's—"

But he was already crossing the room, kneeling, taking her wrist in both hands with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him — the wary shoulders, the merchant's smile, the three years of secrets worn smooth as river stones. He held her wrist up to the light. The crescent shone. Below them, through the floor, the Hollow Gate sang its low, patient note, and the crescent answered, and for one strange suspended moment Isolde felt less like a woman in a shop and more like a word being spoken in a very old sentence.

"It started early," Quentin murmured. "The count. It shouldn't start until—" He stopped himself. Swallowed whatever came next, per tradition.

"Rule three of yours," Isolde reminded him quietly. "No. Sorry. Your rule three was running. My rule was the telling. Tell me before it arrives, remember. So tell me: until when?"

He didn't let go of her wrist. She noticed that. She filed it away, the way she filed everything — margins, dates, names.

"Until the moon was further along," he said finally, which was true and incomplete, and they both knew it. "Twelve marks, Isolde. Thirteen nights. And on the thirteenth, somebody stands at that door downstairs and chooses. Seal, or sever." His thumb moved once, absently, over the crescent, the way you'd touch a wound you caused. "The book doesn't say which one we choose. It just says we'll be the ones choosing."

"Then we have twelve moons to find out what the book isn't telling us." Isolde flexed her fingers. The warmth faded; the hum sank back beneath the floor, patient as ever. "Starting tomorrow. Because I found something today, in the gathering — a signature. In the margin of the third quire. Someone repaired this book before me. A hundred years ago. Same stitching style as mine."

Quentin went very still.

"Not just the same style," he said slowly. "Same hand?"

"I didn't say that."

"You were going to."

Isolde looked down at her ink-stained fingers, at the crescent fading beneath her sleeve like a moon setting, and admitted it: "It looked like my handwriting, Quentin. A hundred years old, water-damaged, half burned — and it looked exactly like mine."

Outside, high over the crooked roofs of Vell, the waning moon slid free of a cloud, one turn older than yesterday, counting.
