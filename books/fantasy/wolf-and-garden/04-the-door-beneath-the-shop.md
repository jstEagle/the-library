## 4. The Door Beneath the Shop

The trouble with secrets, Quentin Eastwick had learned, was that they were like cats. You could feed them and shelter them and arrange your whole life around them, but the moment you tried to show one to somebody else, it arched its back and hissed and made you look like a fool for ever having loved it.

He stood at the top of the cellar stairs with a candle in one hand and Isolde Vane's name caught somewhere behind his teeth, and he did not say it.

"You're stalling," Isolde said.

"I'm deliberating."

"Deliberately." She had a way of smiling that made even corrections feel like gifts. She'd been in his shop for an hour now, ostensibly examining his stock of luck-knots with a bookbinder's critical eye — she'd turned over a fever-charm and said, "Your stitching is better than half the binderies on Cooper's Row," which was, he suspected, the kindest thing anyone had said to him since his mother died — and all the while her eyes kept drifting to the floorboards behind the counter, as if she could hear what slept beneath them.

Which, of course, she could. That was rather the problem.

"The cellar's damp," Quentin said. "And there are spiders. Large ones. Philosophically opposed to visitors."

"I've restored books that were underwater for forty years," Isolde said. "I once pulled a beetle out of a psalter so old it predated the Ministry. A spider won't end me, Quentin."

She said his name easily, the way she'd said it the first night at the stall, as though they were already old friends negotiating the terms of a long acquaintance. He found this both wonderful and deeply inconvenient.

"It isn't the spiders," he admitted.

"I know it isn't the spiders."

* * *

Here is what Quentin knew about the Hollow Gate, gathered across three years of sleepless vigils and one childhood spent pretending not to notice his mother's midnight absences:

It was old. Older than Vell, older than the Ministry, older — if his mother's stories held any water — than the idea of doors at all. It was made of thorn woven through iron, or possibly iron grown through thorn; no one who examined it closely had ever agreed on which came first, and after a while most examiners stopped examining and started praying. It hummed. Not constantly — only when something on either side wanted through, and then only low, like a struck bell heard through three walls of stone. And it was sealed, properly sealed, by a covenant that had cost three wardens their lives and one witness everything she had, back when the world was younger and more inclined toward dramatic gestures.

Quentin's job was not to open it. His job was to make sure nobody else did.

For three years he had tended it alone: renewing the wards each dark of the moon, feeding small charms into the door's hunger so it wouldn't go looking for larger meals, listening to it hum in his dreams until the humming felt less like sound and more like a second heartbeat, one he couldn't live without and couldn't quite forgive.

And now here was Isolde Vane, standing in his shop, whose name had appeared in a century-old prophecy beside tomorrow's date, and the gate beneath his feet had started humming the moment she crossed his threshold and had not, in two days, entirely stopped.

"There's something under my shop," he said finally. "And I think it knows you."

Isolde set down the luck-knot she'd been holding. "Then let's go be introduced properly," she said. "I hate being talked about behind my back. Especially by furniture."

* * *

The cellar stairs were narrow and smelled of tallow, old iron, and — faintly, only when you knew to notice — green things. Living things. Bramble-sweet, like hedgerows in June, though there hadn't been a living plant down there in Quentin's lifetime.

Halfway down, Isolde paused. "Do you hear that?"

"Hear what?" But he already knew. The hum had risen to meet her, gentle as a cat greeting someone home late.

"That." Her hand found his sleeve in the dark — not gripping, just resting, the way you touch something to confirm it's real. "It sounds like... a choir warming up. Very far away. Underwater."

"That's it noticing you," Quentin said. "Try not to take it personally. It notices everyone. Usually it just grumbles and goes back to sleep."

"And with me?"

He hesitated on the last step. "With you it's been singing for two days straight. I haven't slept properly since you walked into the market. I'd like it noted, for the record, that I am showing you this despite considerable exhaustion and against every instinct I possess."

"Noted," Isolde said solemnly. "You're very brave. Practically heroic. Now open the door before I die of curiosity."

The cellar was small, low-ceilinged, and looked, at first glance, like the cellar of any struggling charm-shop: crates of unsold stock, a workbench scarred by decades of knives, jars of dried herbs going quietly to dust. Quentin had arranged it carefully for exactly this first impression. He lit three lamps, watched Isolde's gaze travel the ordinary room, and saw the exact moment she noticed what he'd hoped she wouldn't see so quickly — the way the crates were arranged in a ring around the far wall, the way the herb-jars formed a pattern too deliberate to be clutter, the way the whole room, once you really looked, resolved into something less like storage and more like a fortress.

Or a cage.

"Oh," Isolde said softly. "Oh, Quentin."

He crossed to the far wall, where a shelf of preserves stood in front of what appeared to be solid stone. He took down the jar of pickled walnuts — Isolde made a small wounded noise; she'd had plans for those — set it aside, and pressed his palm flat against the stones.

"Wardens' word," he said, feeling slightly ridiculous, as he always did, saying it aloud. "Eastwick. Open for tending."

The wall sighed. There was no better word for it — a long exhalation of dust and old air, and then the stones slid apart like curtains drawn by a careful hand, revealing a short passage lined with the same dark stone, sloping gently downward, breathing cold.

Isolde had gone very still. "How long has that been there?"

"Since before the shop. Since before the street. Possibly since before the city." He picked up a lamp. "My mother used to say the shop wasn't built over the gate. The gate grew the shop, the way a tree grows bark. We're just the outer layer."

They went down together, single file, the passage too narrow for anything else. Quentin heard Isolde's breath change — quickening, steadying, quickening again — and found, to his surprise, that his own had gone calm. This part he knew. This part was his. Whatever waited below had been his responsibility since a woman with his eyes and a stranger's voice had put his hand on the door and told him, *keep it shut, love, whatever else you do in this life, keep it shut.*

The passage ended at a second doorway — no wall this time, just a frame of black iron, and hanging in the frame, filling it completely—

Well.

Quentin had never managed to describe it well, not even to himself. His mother had called it a door of woven thorn and iron, and technically she was right, but that was like calling the sea technically wet. Thorn and iron had grown together here, or argued together here, for so long that neither remained distinct: black iron branches twined with briars that gleamed like polished bronze, leaves of hammered metal that rustled without wind, blossoms of dull silver closed tight as fists. It was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and it filled the little chamber with that green, growing smell, impossibly, impossibly alive.

And it was humming.

Not the distant choir-warmup from the stairs. This was close, rich, layered — a chord held by many voices, some low as roots and some high as frost, and underneath it all, unmistakable now that he listened, a thread of melody that rose and fell like a welcome.

Isolde stepped past him.

"Wait—" Quentin said, because his mother's voice in his memory said *wait*, always wait, never let anyone touch it, never let anyone near it —

But Isolde was already reaching out, and her fingers, when they brushed the lowest branch, did not flinch from thorns that had drawn blood from Quentin more than once.

The hum changed.

It didn't stop. It *resolved*. All those layered voices suddenly agreed on a single note, clear and glad and enormous, and the chamber filled with light — soft, green-gold, rising from the door itself, from the silver buds along its surface, which were opening. Unfurling. Not wide, not dangerously, just enough: enough to show petals of pale light within, enough to spill warmth across the cold stone floor like the first sun of spring through a window.

Isolde laughed. It was an involuntary sound, startled out of her, bright as struck glass. "It's *warm*—"

"Step back." Quentin's voice came out rougher than he intended. "Isolde. Step back. Slowly."

She did, and the light followed her like a dog following its person, dimming only when the distance grew too great, and the buds closed again, and the great chord softened into that welcoming hum, patient, attentive, waiting for her to come back.

Silence, of a sort. The lamp flames crackling. Isolde's breath. Quentin's heart doing something complicated and undignified in his chest.

"So," Isolde said at last. Her hand was still extended toward the door, as if it might reach for her on its own. "When you said it knows me—"

"It doesn't just know you." Quentin set the lamp down because his hands had begun to shake and he refused to let them do it where she could see. "Do you know what a witness is? In the covenant? Not a bystander — the covenant has a formal meaning. When the gate was first sealed, three wardens gave their oaths, but oaths need someone to hold them. Someone to carry the terms. To remember them, and be bound by them, and — when the time comes — to release them or renew them. The first witness was the one who accepted the wardens' oaths on behalf of both realms. Without a witness, the oaths are just words. With one, they're law."

Isolde turned to look at him. In the lamplight, with the green glow fading from her face, she looked very young and very steady, both at once, which was rapidly becoming his favorite combination in all the world.

"The prophecy book," she said. "The one I was restoring. The marginalia — I assumed it was a later owner's hand. But some of the notes were written *around* the text, like the writer was answering it. Like the book was talking to them."

"The first witness kept a record," Quentin said. "Everyone knows that much. What happened to it—" he shrugged, helplessly. "Lost. Scattered. My mother thought parts of it survived in fragments. A page here, a binding there. Books get broken up. Rebound. Sold."

"Water damage," Isolde whispered. "The book came to my workshop with water damage. From a flood. Quentin, the flyleaf — there was an inscription I couldn't read, the ink had bloomed, but tonight, after I touched your charm, after the mark—" She pressed her wrist against her chest, where the thin crescent-shaped line had appeared two nights ago, the first of thirteen. "I read it. On my walk here, I remembered it clearly, like someone had wiped the glass. It says *to the witness who comes after.*"

The hum swelled, gently, like a listener leaning in.

"My grandmother's family came upriver during the Vell floods," Isolde said, and her voice had gone strange and wondering, a scholar watching a theory become a fact become a life. "My mother always said we had old blood, but everyone says that. Everyone thinks their grandmother was secretly somebody." She laughed, unsteadily. "I restore old books, Quentin. I spend my life handling other people's inheritances. I never thought—"

"I know," Quentin said. "Believe me. Nobody ever thinks it's them. That's practically the first rule."

* * *

They sat on the cold steps afterward, side by side, the door humming its patient chord behind them. Quentin had fetched the good tea from upstairs — the tin marked FEVER HERBS, DO NOT USE, which contained actual tea, hoarded against emergencies, and this qualified — and they drank it in silence for a while.

"It wants something from me," Isolde said eventually. Not a question.

"Yes."

"What?"

"I don't know. Truly." He wrapped both hands around his cup. "My mother knew more. She died before she finished telling me — or before she was willing to finish, which amounted to the same thing. She said the covenant was breaking down, slowly, like a knot working loose, and that someday it would need..." He searched for the word. "*Renewing,* she said. And that renewal required a witness of the first witness's line. And then she looked at me and said, thank all the merciful powers, that's not us. Our family keeps the door. We don't hold the covenant. Different duty, smaller burden. Keep it shut, Quentin, and you'll live a quiet life."

The tea had gone bitter in his mouth, or perhaps that was memory.

"She bought me time," he said. "That's the other thing. She made a bargain, before she died, to buy me three safe years. Three years past the point where the gate would have started calling for a keeper's full strength. Those three years ended—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the shop, at the night market beyond it, at the whole precarious arrangement of his existence. "Recently."

"How recently?"

"The night you walked into my stall," Quentin said. "Right on schedule. Right on the date in your book."

Isolde was quiet for a long moment. Above them, muffled through floors of stone and timber, Vell went about its evening: cart wheels, a drunk singing, the distant clang of the Ministry watch-bell that everyone pretended not to hear.

"When I found my name in that book," she said at last, "my first thought was that I'd finally lost my mind. My second thought — and I'm not proud of the order — was *at last.*" She turned her cup in her hands. "Twenty-nine years of repairing other people's stories, and I have wanted, so badly, to be inside one. Even a frightening one. *Especially* a frightening one. And now the door under your shop lights up like midsummer when I touch it, and I should be terrified, and I am, I promise you I am terrified, but underneath the terror there is this—" she pressed her fist briefly against her sternum, "—this appalling *rightness*, like coming home to a house I've never seen."

"I know that feeling too," Quentin said quietly. "That's how the keeping feels. Like a coat I inherited that fits perfectly and weighs a thousand pounds."

They looked at each other. Something passed between them there on the cold steps — recognition, maybe, two people discovering they were dressed in the same impossible coat.

"Whatever it wants," Isolde said, nodding toward the humming door, "we should find out. Properly. Before the moon turns again." She flexed her right wrist; beneath the skin, faint as an old scar, the crescent mark seemed to shift, like something turning over in its sleep. "Thirteen turns, your prophecy said. Twelve left after tonight. And Quentin — the Ministry burned a hedge-witch on Coalmarket Row last month for selling charms a tenth as strong as yours. If the law finds out what's under this shop, we won't get warnings or trials. So whatever we do next, we do it fast, and we do it quietly."

There was a scrape, then — soft, deliberate, from the direction of the door.

Both of them turned.

One of the silver buds had opened again. Just one, at the height of a reader's eye, and resting in the curve of its metal petals, gleaming like a coin dropped in a collection plate, lay a single blackthorn leaf — fresh, green-veined, wet with dew that had no business existing in a cellar under Vell.

On its surface, written in a script that shimmered and would not hold still, were four words:

*THE RIVER REMEMBERS FIRST.*

Isolde reached for it. Quentin caught her hand — gently, but he caught it — and their eyes met in the lamplight, and the hum beneath the earth rose around them like a tide beginning to turn.
