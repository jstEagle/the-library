## 5. Ministry of Ash

The trouble with living above your workshop, Isolde decided on the third morning after the moon-turn mark appeared on her wrist, was that you could hear everything the city did to itself.

She heard the bells first — not the usual quarter-hour chimes from Saint Aldery's, but a flatter, harder ringing, the kind that meant someone official wanted everyone's attention and intended to keep it. Then boots. Many boots, in the rhythm of men who had been told to walk loudly because walking loudly *was* the message.

She was at her bench before she fully understood why, glue pot steaming at her elbow, the prophecy book open under a linen cloth where she'd been working on its water-warped spine. Through the window she watched four gray coaches draw up outside the shop across the lane — Quentin's shop, though it sold nothing so honest as books. Luck-knots hung in its window like little knotted suns. Fever-charms turned slowly on their strings.

And now eight officers of the Ministry of Unnatural Affairs were filing into it with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the morning.

Isolde's hand went to her wrist without her permission. The mark beneath her sleeve — a thin silver crescent, fine as a hairline crack in glaze — gave one small, cold pulse, as if it too had looked out the window.

"No," she told it quietly. "Absolutely not."

The mark pulsed again, which she chose to interpret as disagreement.

* * *

She did the sensible thing. She stayed at her bench and worked, or made a convincing show of it, re-sewing a hymnal's broken cords while her ears strained through the floorboards. The sensible thing lasted eleven minutes.

It ended when two officers crossed the lane to *her* door.

She opened it before they could knock, mostly so they wouldn't touch anything. Bookbinders developed strong feelings about strangers touching things.

"Madam Vane." The taller officer had a face like a folded document — all creases and officialdom, with eyes that had been stamped flat long ago. "Investigator Hobb. Ministry of Unnatural Affairs."

"I don't have any unnatural affairs," Isolde said. "I have glue, thread, and a cat who is arguably too large."

"We're not here for you." He produced a paper, but didn't offer it; he simply held it up like a lantern. "We're conducting interviews regarding one Wren Calloway, known dealer in contraband charms. You've been seen entering the Eastwick establishment. You've been seen speaking with Calloway there."

"Everyone speaks with Wren there. Wren speaks the way other people breathe."

Hobb's flat eyes didn't change. "You restored books for Calloway. We found three of your repairs in his stock. Your stitching is distinctive, madam. Very tight. Very tidy." Something almost like approval moved through his voice and died there. "We'd like you to come answer some questions."

"And if I decline?"

"Then we ask again tomorrow," said the shorter officer, who until then had been studying her window shutters with professional interest, "with more paperwork."

There it was, then: the gentle machinery of the state, which never broke down your door when it could simply arrive every day forever, patient as rust.

Isolde got her coat.

* * *

They did not take her to the cells. They took her to a room with good light, which frightened her more than a dark one would have. A dark cell said *we are cruel.* This room — oak table, comfortable chair, a carafe of water and two clean glasses, morning sun falling in a civilized gold bar across the floor — said *we would prefer you to feel at home while we take you apart.*

Hobb sat opposite her. The shorter officer, whose nameplate read VESS, stood by the wall with a leather ledger and the posture of a man pretending not to listen.

"You bind books," Hobb began.

"When I'm allowed to finish them, yes."

"You repaired a psalter last autumn for the widow Anselm. A book of hours for a merchant family off Tanner's Row. And—" he turned a page in his own ledger, unhurried, "—a grimoire. Fragmentary. Water damage, thorn-and-iron clasps, provenance unknown. Sold to you by Wren Calloway, four months ago."

The floor of Isolde's stomach became politely interested in the ceiling.

"That book was blank," she said.

"Was it?" Hobb asked, with terrible mildness.

"Its pages were damaged past legibility. That's why it came to me. If it has since become *legible*, I can only assume the Ministry's reading standards have declined."

For the first time, something flickered behind the flat stamp of his eyes — not amusement. Recognition. The look of a man meeting another person who also knew how to lie professionally.

"Madam Vane," he said, "let me describe my morning, since yours has evidently been duller. At dawn we entered the Eastwick premises and seized two hundred and eleven items of trafficked charm-work. Luck-knots. Fever-laces. A salt-ring kit. Nothing dangerous, mind — nothing that *burns*, nothing that *opens*. Small mercies, small charms. Mr. Eastwick was cooperative to the point of tedium. But Mr. Calloway, when we reached his premises on Cordwainer Street, had already begun burning his ledgers. He burned three before we arrived, which tells you what sort of man he is, and he burned a fourth *while we were arresting him*, holding it in the fire with his bare hands, which tells you what sort of loyalty he keeps." Hobb folded his hands. "Mr. Calloway is currently in a room much less pleasant than this one, and he is being asked who supplied him, who he supplied, and what he knows about a certain door."

"A door," Isolde repeated.

"A door," Hobb agreed.

She drank some water. It gave her hands something to do besides shake. Outside the high window, a gull argued with the bell-tower, ordinary and oblivious.

"Ministry work is mostly boredom," Hobb went on, conversational now, a man settling into the part of the conversation he'd actually come for. "Ninety percent of it is confiscating love-charms from teenagers and fining hedge-wives who hang charms over their doors against fever. The city burned its mages a generation ago and what's left to police is mostly superstition wearing ribbons." He paused. "But ten percent, madam. Ten percent is interesting. And in eleven years, the most interesting thing I have ever encountered is a door."

"Most houses have several."

"This one is beneath a shop. It is made of thorn and iron. It hums." He said the word *hums* the way other men said *bleeds.* "Our records go back further than the city likes to remember. There was a covenant once. There were wardens once — three of them, river, stone, and sky. There was a realm on the other side that our grandparents' grandparents sealed away, and everyone agreed this was a triumph, and everyone stopped talking about it, which is how triumphs are usually finished." He unfolded his hands and refolded them. "Three weeks ago, the instruments in our lower archive began registering resonance from a location in the Lamplighters' Quarter. Faint. Rhythmic. Increasing."

"Increasing," Isolde said. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"Something is counting," Hobb said. "Or something is being counted. Either way, madam Vane, the Ministry's position on this door may surprise you. We do not want it destroyed. We do not even particularly want it *sealed*. Sealed things decay. Sealed things rot until they burst. No — the Ministry wants the door *opened*. Under supervision. Under control. Ours."

The word *ours* sat in the golden sunlight between them like something dead and beautifully preserved.

"Why?" she asked.

"Because magic is a resource, madam. Because the realm beyond that door is full of it, and this city has spent a generation pretending it doesn't miss what it burned. Because there are men on the Council who remember the fires fondly and men on the Council who remember the fires *usefully*, and the useful ones have been listening to me." He leaned forward slightly, and the creases of his face rearranged themselves into something almost gentle. "You've touched that door. Our instruments say the resonance answers to a bloodline — an old witness-line. Yours, unless I am very much mistaken, and I am paid not to be."

The crescent under her sleeve went cold enough to burn.

"I think," Isolde said carefully, "that you have confused me with someone whose family kept better records."

"Perhaps." Hobb rose, smoothing his coat, and slid a card across the table — heavy stock, gray, the Ministry seal embossed in ash-colored ink. "Consider this a standing invitation rather than a summons. People find, in my experience, that the truth comes to them gradually, the way water finds a basement. When it arrives, madam, do write ahead. I'd hate for us to meet again in a room with worse light."

At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"One more thing, since you'll hear it within the hour anyway. Mr. Calloway has requested counsel, which will take days he does not have. He has also, in the interval, asked whether anyone has checked on the shop. On the knots in the window. Whether anyone waters the little fern by the till." Now he did turn, and his flat eyes held something that in a better man might have been called regret. "Loyalty like that is rare, madam. It is also exactly the kind of thing that gets people hurt. Do give Mr. Eastwick my regards. Tell him Investigator Hobb says the door sounds *lovely* this time of year."

Then he was gone, and Vess closed his ledger with a soft, final snap, and Isolde sat alone in the good light with two untouched glasses of water and the growing, sickening understanding that she had walked into this room believing the Ministry was a wall.

Walls kept things out. Walls you could trust, or at least lean against.

This wasn't a wall. It had never been a wall. It was a mouth — vast, gray, patient — and it had just smiled at her with excellent manners and told her, in the politest possible language, that it was hungry, and that it knew precisely which door it wanted opened, and that it was perfectly willing to wait for the world to hand it the key.

* * *

Quentin met her halfway back, which meant he'd been running, which meant the composed shopkeeper was somewhere under the dust of the Lamplighters' Quarter, breathing hard.

"They took Wren," he said, before she could speak. His eyes went over her shoulder, counting the street, the windows, the gulls. "They took Wren at dawn and then they sat in my shop for two hours drinking my tea and asking me charming questions about my suppliers, and I have spent the whole day trying to decide which of those was worse. Are you all right? They interviewed you. Of course they interviewed you, they interviewed everyone, Vess has a list longer than my arm—"

"Quentin."

"—and Wren won't talk, Wren would sooner eat the ledgers, Wren *did* eat—"

"*Quentin.*" She caught his sleeve. Under her palm, his pulse was going like a trapped bird. "They know about the gate."

He stopped. All the motion went out of him at once, the way a fire goes out when the door is shut — suddenly, completely, leaving only heat and no light.

"How much?" he asked quietly.

"The door. Where it is. What it's made of. That it hums." She swallowed. "That it's getting louder. And Quentin — they don't want to destroy it. Their man said—" she heard Hobb's mild voice repeat itself in her head, and hated how clearly it had stuck, "—sealed things rot until they burst. They want to *open* it. Under their control. They think it's a resource."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — one short, unamused note with no humor anywhere in it.

"Of course they do," he said. "Three years I've kept that door shut and quiet and boring, and the whole time the law was standing behind me with a crowbar, waiting for me to die or slip or fall in love." He caught himself on the last word a half-second too late, and they both heard it, and neither of them picked it up. "Wren knew. That's why Wren burned the ledgers. Not the charm sales — the *dates*. Every delivery Wren ever made to my shop, logged against every night the gate hummed. Wren was keeping my alibi and my evidence in the same book, and now the Ministry has the ashes and Wren has a cell."

"So we get Wren out."

"We?" He blinked at her.

"You have a fence in a Ministry cell who protected you with his bare hands in a fire," Isolde said. "I have a mark on my wrist that counts moons, a name in a century-old book, and a fresh invitation to visit a room with worse light. Neither of us is walking away from this, Quentin. The only question left is whether we do it stupidly together or stupidly apart, and I have read enough tragedies to have opinions about which chapters survive."

Something moved through his exhausted face — relief, mostly, and underneath it the startled look of a man who has been alone with a secret so long he'd forgotten company was possible.

"The shrine first," he said finally. "The Riverkeeper. The prophecy gives us the order, and the oaths won't wait for the Ministry's paperwork. But Wren—" his jaw tightened. "Wren I am not leaving in there. Give me tonight to think of a plan that isn't suicide."

"Wonderful," Isolde said. "Between us we'll manage half of one."

They walked back toward the Quarter together, two fugitives-in-waiting moving at the speed of the merely suspicious, and neither of them noticed the gray coach that followed at a polite distance, nor the man inside it writing in a ledger with a very tight, very tidy hand:

*Subject confirms attachment to keeper. Attachment is leverage. Leverage opens doors.*

Behind them, in the empty interview room, the sun had moved on, and the two glasses of water stood untouched, catching the light like small, clear promises no one had made.
