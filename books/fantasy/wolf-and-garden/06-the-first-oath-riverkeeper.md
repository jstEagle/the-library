## 6. The First Oath: Riverkeeper

The river that ran beneath Vell had no name anymore. The Ministry had scrubbed the maps a generation ago, renaming it the Municipal Drainage Channel, which was the sort of thing Quentin thought should be illegal on grounds of sheer ugliness. Before that, the old books called it the Liss, and the older books — the ones Isolde now carried everywhere in her satchel like a sleeping cat she refused to put down — called it something else entirely: *the Riverkeeper's spine.*

"The shrine is where the spine bends," Isolde said for the third time that morning, walking beside him along the embankment with her hood up and her nose in the prophecy book. "Look. There's an illustration."

"I know there's an illustration. You've shown me eleven times."

"Twelve," she said, and showed him again anyway.

It was a small woodcut, water-stained at the edges: a bend of dark river, and rising out of it what looked like a crown of drowned stones, each one carved with an open eye. Beneath it, in ink gone brown as old blood, ran the line Quentin had been trying not to read all week:

*Where the first warden kept her word until the water took it, there the first oath sleeps, and wakes for witness-blood alone.*

"Witness-blood," he said. "Which means you go in. Which means I stand on dry land and hold the door, and we both pretend I'm fine with that arrangement."

"You're not fine with it?"

"I'm *thrilled* with it. I'm the picture of a man who is fine." He adjusted the strap of his pack, which contained, among other things, two breathing-charms, three light-knots, a coil of waxed cord, and a flask of tea he'd charmed to stay hot, because if he was going to drown himself he intended to do it warm. "I'm just noting, for the record, that every plan so far has ended with me standing somewhere unpleasant while you walk into danger."

"That's not true." Isolde closed the book and gave him a look — the level, patient look of a woman who mended books for a living and had opinions about people who fell apart. "The night we met, your plan was to sell me a luck-knot and never speak to me again."

"And how did that work out?"

"You tell me. You're the one who's been carrying my satchel for half a mile."

He glanced down. She was right. He'd taken it without thinking when they'd climbed the embankment stairs, and hadn't asked for it back, and she'd let him carry it, which by Isolde's standards was practically a marriage proposal. He handed it over with as much dignity as he could salvage.

* * *

The bend in the river was easy to find and hard to approach. The Ministry had fenced the whole stretch years ago — DANGER, STRUCTURALLY UNSOUND, BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF UNNATURAL AFFAIRS — but fences meant nothing to two people who could unpick a warding-charm between them in under a minute, and the fence here had been neglected so long its warning sigils had faded into decorative squiggles. Beyond it, the ground sloped down into a hollow of black willows, and the river moved slow and green and silent, deeper than any municipal drainage channel had a right to be.

"There," Isolde whispered.

The stones rose out of the water exactly as the woodcut promised — a broken ring of them, maybe ten paces across, their tops just breaking the surface, each carved with an eye whose pupil had been worn smooth by decades of current. In the center of the ring the water sank away into darkness, and from that darkness came a sound. Not a sound, exactly. A *hum*, low and patient, felt in the teeth more than the ears. Quentin knew that hum. He'd lived above a version of it for three years.

"It's like the gate," he said.

"It's part of the gate." Isolde knelt at the water's edge, careless of the mud soaking through her skirt. Her fingers hovered over the nearest stone without touching. "Or it was. This is a hinge. One of the places the covenant anchored itself before it was — before whatever happened happened." She looked up at him, and in the green shade of the willows her eyes seemed lit from below. "Quentin. It's still humming. After a hundred years of no one keeping it."

"Dead things hum sometimes," he said, because he'd inherited his mother's gift for comfort, which was to say none whatsoever. "Kettles. Bells. Dead wardens' shrines, apparently."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and the sound did something inconvenient to his chest, and he decided firmly to ignore it.

The prophecy's instructions were specific, because prophecies are written by people who enjoy making things difficult. At moonrise, the witness must enter the ring and descend to the shrine door. The keeper must remain at the threshold and hold the way open, for the shrine, like all doors of the old covenant, would close behind her unless held. And the holding, the text noted helpfully, *will ask more of the keeper than the witness knows.*

"'More than the witness knows,'" Isolde read aloud, frowning. "That's ominous. What does it mean?"

"It means the person writing this wanted whoever read it secondhand to worry," Quentin said. He unpacked the breathing-charms — two small knots of braided rush, greasy with beeswax, that would pull air from water the way a wick pulls oil. He pressed one into her palm and closed her fingers around it. "You hold the door open from inside. I hold it from out. We meet back here when you've got the oath. Simple."

"Simple," she repeated, in a tone that suggested she'd heard him leave out several important words.

"Isolde." He made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plain. "Whatever the shrine shows you in there — dead wardens see things, show things, it's how they talk — don't take anything it offers you. Don't promise anything. And don't stay past the count. Two hundred heartbeats, then you come up whether you've finished or not."

"And if I need two hundred and one?"

"Then the shrine will still be there tomorrow. You won't be." He said it lightly, and hated how unlight it came out.

Isolde studied him for a long moment. Then she did something he didn't expect: she reached out and tucked a loose strand of hair behind his ear, briskly, the way one straightens a crooked collar. "Two hundred heartbeats," she agreed. "And Quentin? Whatever the holding asks of you — *more than the witness knows* — you come up too. That's the deal. Both of us or neither."

"That's not how deals work in prophecies—"

"Then the prophecy can learn," said Isolde Vane, bookbinder, witness, and apparently a woman who renegotiated century-old covenants over breakfast, "because I don't sign lopsided contracts. I mend them."

* * *

Moonrise came thin and white through the willow branches, and the eyes on the stones opened.

There was no dramatic flare of light. They simply — woke. Pupils that had been worn blank for a hundred years found their focus all at once, and turned, all twelve of them, toward Isolde as she waded into the ring. The water parted around her knees with a sound like a held breath released. The hum deepened until Quentin felt it in his sternum.

He took his place at the ring's edge, where the largest stone broke the surface, and laid both palms flat against it. Cold. Old. Underneath the cold, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the hum answered him — recognized him, *keeper* — and the door in the center of the ring began to open.

Not a door of wood or iron. The water itself folded downward, peeling back like pages turning, revealing a stair of slick stone descending into a chamber of green-gold gloom. Isolde looked back once — just once, a quick check, the way travelers check for each other on crowded streets — and then she was descending, the breathing-charm glowing faintly at her throat, her silhouette shrinking into the shrine's drowned light.

And Quentin held the door.

For the first minute it was nothing. He'd held the Hollow Gate shut for three years; holding a door open was practically a holiday. He counted Isolde's heartbeats in his head, a trick his mother had taught him for tracking wards — *you can't count another person's heartbeat, silly boy, only your own, but fear keeps good time* — and reached forty, sixty, eighty.

At a hundred, the water noticed him.

That was the only way he could describe it. The current, sluggish and sleepy all evening, gathered against his legs like a crowd pressing toward a stage. The cold climbed. His boots were soaked; then his trousers; then a finger of water slid up under his coat and laid itself, curious as a cat's paw, against his ribs. The hum changed pitch. Lower. Hungrier. The shrine had been sealed a hundred years, and here was warmth, here was breath, here was a living man standing in its doorway, and the river had forgotten its manners.

*Hold,* Quentin told himself, and held.

A hundred and twenty. His arms shook. The stone under his palms had begun to pull, gently, steadily, like the tug of a sleeping child rolling toward the edge of a bed. If he let go, the door would close — and Isolde was a hundred feet down in a room that had been waiting a hundred years for company, and Quentin had a sudden, vivid image of what the shrine would make of a guest who arrived just as the door shut.

A hundred and fifty. Water to his waist now. He couldn't feel his hands. Somewhere far below, a green-gold light flared — Isolde reaching the oath, or being reached by it — and the whole river shuddered, delighted, and pulled.

He went under.

The world became green roar. He got one arm hooked over the stone's shoulder, wedged his boot into a crack, and hung on while the current tried to peel him off like bark. The breathing-charm at his own throat burned cold and gave him thin, metallic sips of air. Below him, through the churned water, he could see the shrine door straining to close — folding upward, page by page — and he could see, absurdly, distantly, Isolde standing in the green light with her hand raised and her mouth open in a shape he couldn't hear but understood perfectly, because it was his name.

*A hundred and seventy.*

His grip slipped. He caught the next stone lower, and the water slammed him against it, and something in his shoulder went with a crack he felt more than heard. His vision spotted. The charm was giving out — he could taste the magic souring, going thin, his mother's knots were better than this, he should have re-braided them, he should have—

*A hundred and ninety.*

And then hands closed on his collar.

Isolde came up out of the door like something born of the river — soaked, blazing-eyed, the oath wrapped around her wrist in a band of pale light — and she did not waste one heartbeat on horror. She planted her knee against the stone, hooked her arms under his, and hauled, this small fierce woman with ink-stained fingers and no magic at all except the kind that refuses, and together — mostly her, if he was honest, mostly her — they tore free of the water and collapsed onto the bank among the willow roots.

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. There was coughing. There was Isolde wringing out her braid with the grim efficiency of a laundress, and Quentin lying on his back watching the moon swim in and out of focus through the willow leaves, and the slow discovery that his left arm worked if he didn't ask too much of it.

"Your shoulder's dislocated," Isolde diagnosed, kneeling over him.

"It's *relocated*," Quentin said. "Aggressively. It'll settle."

"It won't. Sit up." She sat him up, braced her foot against his ribs the way a farrier handles a horse, took his arm, and popped the joint back in with a competence that made him yelp and then laugh and then yelp again. "There. Now breathe."

He breathed. The pain settled into a deep, honest ache. Around them, the drowned shrine had gone quiet — the eyes on the stones closing one by one, contented, the hum fading to something almost like a lullaby. On Isolde's wrist, where the first moon-turn mark had appeared weeks ago, a new mark had joined it: a thin silver ring, cool to the touch, like a band of river-water that had decided to stay.

"The Riverkeeper's oath," she said quietly. She turned her wrist so the moonlight caught it. "It accepted me. It said—" she paused, and her voice caught on something, "—it said it had waited so long for someone to keep a promise that it had nearly stopped believing in them. And then it said I smelled like a bookbinder. Like glue and mended things." She laughed wetly. "I think that was a compliment."

"It was," Quentin said. "Wardens have terrible manners and excellent judgment." He looked at her — really looked, at this woman who had walked into a drowned grave for a stranger's war and dragged him out of a river without hesitating — and said, before his caution could catch him: "You didn't let go of me. The door was closing. Your oath was in hand. By every sensible measure, you should have swum."

"Sensible measures." She snorted, wringing out her sleeve. "You held a door open with your body for a hundred and ninety heartbeats. We're neither of us sensible, Quentin Eastwick. That's rather the point of us."

*The point of us.* He turned the phrase over carefully, the way he turned charms, checking for flaws, finding none.

They packed up slowly, stiffly, sharing the last of the hot tea from the same flask. It was only as they climbed the embankment stairs, Isolde ahead of him with the prophecy book under her arm, that Quentin noticed the change. New ink on the old page — not the brown of a century, but fresh, dark, wet-looking, as though the book had been writing while they weren't watching. Beneath the woodcut of the shrine, in a hand that was not Isolde's and not his, a new line had appeared:

*One oath reforged. Two remain. But the Architect has tasted the opening of the first door — and he is coming to see who holds the hinges.*

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought, in handwriting Quentin knew with a sick, cold certainty he would recognize anywhere:

His mother's.

*"Tell my son the debt collector reads his mail."*

Isolde turned back at the top of the stairs. "What is it?" she said. "Quentin — what does it say?"

He closed the book gently, the way you close a door on something listening.

"We need to get off the river," he said. "Now."
