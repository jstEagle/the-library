## 7. Thornlight

The drowned shrine did not so much end as exhale.

One moment Isolde was still waist-deep in black water, her fingers hooked into the Riverkeeper's stone basin while Quentin's grip bruised her wrist; the next, the water simply stopped being water. It became a threshold. She felt it the way a bookbinder feels a spine give way — that soft surrender of one thing becoming another — and then they were through, gasping on a bank of moss that glowed like a lamp behind frosted glass.

Isolde lay on her back and stared upward and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

There was no sky. Not as Vell understood skies. Above her arched a canopy of briars grown vast as cathedral rafters, their thorns longer than her forearm, and along every curling stem ran veins of light — blue-green at the heart, shading to gold at the tips — pulsing slowly, patiently, in a rhythm she realized after a moment matched her own heartbeat. The whole realm above them breathed when she did.

"Thorn," Quentin said quietly. He was sitting up, wringing river water from his coat with the resigned efficiency of a man who had done this before. "Welcome to the other side of every story Vell tells its children to keep them indoors."

"It's beautiful," Isolde said, and then, because the beauty deserved honesty: "It's terrifying."

"Yes." He smiled — tired, crooked, real. "That's generally the review."

She sat up. The moss beneath her palms was warm, faintly yielding, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and where her fingers pressed, small flowers opened — pale things with petals like folded paper. She snatched her hand back. The flowers stayed open anyway, patient as bookmarks.

"Did I do that?"

"You did that." Quentin's tone had shifted, gone careful in the way he got whenever her bloodline did something inconvenient. "First witness's line. Thorn remembers you. Or rather—" he stood, offering his hand, "—it's introducing itself. Politely. For now."

* * *

They walked because there was nothing else to do and nowhere else to go, and because the path — a ribbon of pale stone winding between the briars — seemed to have opinions about their direction. Every time Isolde drifted toward a side passage, the light along the nearest stems dimmed by a degree, subtle as a librarian clearing her throat.

"The shrine is behind us," Quentin said, for the fourth time. "The second oath is ahead of us. The prophecy is very insistent about order."

"I noticed the path is insistent too. I'm beginning to suspect this whole realm is one long passive-aggressive footnote."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, as though laughter were something he kept locked up with everything else valuable in his shop. Isolde had begun cataloguing those small escapes: the laugh, the way his shoulders dropped half an inch when she made him eat, the private satisfaction on his face when a charm sold well. Three years of solitary keeping, Wren had told her once, over cheap wine. Three years and no one to be human at.

The briars thickened overhead until the light came down in shafts, and in those shafts moved things Isolde had no shelf in her mind for. Moths with wings like stained glass, each pane a different memory-colored hue. A stream running uphill without hurry, as though gravity were a suggestion it had politely declined. And once — only once — a stag made entirely of shadow and starlight standing at the treeline, watching them pass with eyes like two full moons, before dissolving into the dark as if embarrassed to be seen.

"Don't stare," Quentin murmured. "It's rude. Everything here can tell when you're staring."

"Everything here is staring at *us*."

"Yes, but we're guests."

* * *

They made camp — or rather, the realm made camp for them — in a hollow where the briars curled into something like a room, walls woven tight, a floor of soft grey moss, even an alcove that suggested, with tremendous optimism, a hearth. When Isolde sat down, warmth bloomed out of the stone at her back. She decided not to question it. Questioning hospitality seemed unwise in a place where the furniture might have feelings.

Quentin laid out their provisions with the grim precision of a quartermaster: hard bread, dried plums, a flask of the tea he brewed from herbs gathered on this side — herbs that tasted, Isolde had discovered, like honey and thunderstorms.

"Eat," he said. "Then sleep. Tomorrow the path turns north, and north in Thorn is not a direction, it's a negotiation."

"With whom?"

"Everything." He tore his bread in half and handed her the larger piece without appearing to notice he'd done it. Isolde noticed. She filed it away in the growing drawer of small Quentin observations that she suspected would one day require a much larger piece of furniture.

She ate. The bread tasted of rosemary and, faintly, of ink — which should have been alarming but instead felt like the realm reading over her shoulder.

"Tell me about the Architect," she said.

Quentin went still in that particular way of his, the way a shopkeeper goes still when a customer picks up the one item priced to frighten. "You've asked me that three times since the shrine."

"And you've answered me three times with 'later.' It's later."

"It's never later," he said, but gently. He looked up at the luminous ceiling, at the slow pulse of gold along the thorns. "My mother traded with him. That's most of what I know, and what I don't know is worse. But the fox will tell you more than I can, if the fox decides you're worth talking to."

"The fox?"

"Fox-spirit. Lives near the border paths. Older than the covenant, younger than the briars — foxes are cagey about their ages, it's impolite to ask." He paused. "It warned my mother, once. About the debt."

"And you trust it?"

"I trust that it hates the Architect," Quentin said. "In Thorn, that's nearly the same thing as trust."

* * *

The fox found them at dusk, or what passed for dusk — the great briar-canopy dimming its veins to ember-red, the whole realm drawing its blanket up to its chin.

Isolde woke to a weight on her feet. Her first thought, honed by three years of handling other people's fragile treasures, was: *something has climbed onto me.* Her second thought, upon opening her eyes, was: *something has climbed onto me and it has nine tails and it is judging me.*

The fox sat on her ankles with the serene entitlement of a cat in a sunbeam. It was red-gold, or possibly gold-red; its fur shifted between the two the way candlelight shifts on gilt. Its eyes were amber coins, ancient and amused, and its nine tails lay around it in a fan of perfect, deliberate elegance.

"Hello," Isolde said, because what else does one say.

"*Hello,*" the fox repeated, in a voice like dry leaves learning to speak. Its mouth did not move quite in time with the words. "*You're the new witness. You smell like glue and old vellum.*"

"That's — accurate, actually."

"*Good. Liars smell like rain.*" The fox turned its head. "*Keeper. Your mother smelled like rain.*"

Quentin had come awake without seeming to move — one moment asleep, the next upright and wary, a man whose body remembered raids. "Maren," he said carefully. "It's been a while."

"*Four years. You've gotten taller. Sadder, too, but taller.*" The fox — Maren — swiveled back to Isolde. "*You crossed the shrine. You bled on the basin. The Riverkeeper's oath rides in your pocket now like a stolen egg. Bold. Stupid. Bold-stupid. My favorite flavor.*"

"We didn't have much choice," Isolde began.

"*Everyone has choice. Most people just prefer the ones that don't cost anything.*" Maren licked one paw with tremendous condescension. "*But you're right, the times are narrow. So listen, bookbinder, because I'll say this once and then I'll be insultingly cryptic, as is traditional.*

"*The Architect walks. He walks tonight, he walked yesterday, he has been walking since the covenant first cracked. He is old as the first promise anyone ever broke, and he eats promises the way your city eats coal — endlessly, hungrily, leaving ash behind. Oaths, vows, wedding-words, deathbed words — all of it feeds him. And broken ones—" the fox's eyes flared, "*—broken ones are his vintage.*"

Isolde felt the cold arrive before she understood it. "And our oaths? The three we're gathering?"

"*Are the richest meal in either realm.*" Maren stood, stretching fore and aft with theatrical leisure. "*Three dead wardens' oaths, reforged by living hands, carried by the last witness's blood. If he takes them from you, the gate doesn't just open, bookbinder. It opens *hungry*. Both realms pour through, and neither survives the pouring.*"

"Then we won't let him take them," Quentin said flatly.

"*Oh, Keeper.*" The fox looked at him with something almost tender under the mockery. "*Your mother said something similar. Words are wind until they're paid for. Remember that. Remember it especially when someone offers you something free.*"

"What do you mean?"

"*I mean,*" Maren said, and for one instant the amusement drained out of the ancient amber eyes and left something old and frightened looking through them, "*that he has already spoken to one of you. And that one of you already listened.*"

The silence that followed had texture. Isolde felt Quentin go rigid beside her — felt it in the moss, in the dimming of the briar-lights overhead, in the way the whole hollow seemed to hold its breath along with them.

"Maren," Quentin said. His voice was very quiet. "What exactly did you see?"

"*I see tracks, Keeper. Not faces. He wears borrowed ones.*" The fox stepped off Isolde's feet and shook itself, tails settling into immaculate order. "*Tracks leading away from this camp. Made sometime while you slept. He came close enough to count your breaths and left again unsatisfied — you're both too poor a meal while your oaths are few. But he'll come back richer-hungry. And Keeper—" the amber eyes flicked to Quentin, held there a beat too long, "*—when he comes wearing a face you love, check the eyes. Rain-smell. Always check for the rain.*"

And then, between one blink and the next, there was only an impression of nine tails vanishing into the briars and a voice drifting back like smoke:

"*North, children. The Stonefather waits in the deep places. Mind the third turn of the path — it lies.*"

* * *

Isolde did not sleep again. Neither, she judged by his breathing, did Quentin.

She lay on the warm moss and stared up at the breathing canopy of light and turned the fox's words over like a page she couldn't stop rereading. *He has already spoken to one of you. And that one of you already listened.*

She thought about the dream she hadn't mentioned — the one from two nights past, the woman with her mother's voice and her mother's hands, standing at the edge of a garden that wasn't a garden, saying, *you're doing so well, dear, keep going north.* She had woken comforted. She had woken comforted and told herself it was grief, or exhaustion, or the strange food, anything except the obvious.

Her mother had been dead eleven years.

Beside her, Quentin's breathing hitched — once, twice — the rhythm of a man rehearsing a confession and losing his nerve at the same line. She could feel the shape of what he wasn't saying pressing against the dark between them like a hand against a window.

"Quentin," she said softly. "When the Architect spoke to you. What face did he wear?"

A long silence. Long enough that she heard the briars creak as they grew, patient and enormous, closing the night in around them like covers drawn up to the chin.

"My mother's," he said at last, barely louder than the growing things. "He wore my mother's face. And Isolde—" his breath caught on her name, and somewhere far to the north, something in the luminous dark turned over, listening, "—I think I told him where we're going."
