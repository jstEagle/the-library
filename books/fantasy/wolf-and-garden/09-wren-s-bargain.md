## 9. Wren's Bargain

The Ministry of Unnatural Affairs kept its prisoners the way a miser keeps coins: underground, counted twice nightly, and never spent. Isolde knew this because Wren had told her so herself, months ago, over tea in Quentin's back room, laughing into her cup.

"They put me in a cell once when I was nineteen," Wren had said. "For selling a singing candle. Just one candle! It only sang *Lament for the Drowned*, which frankly nobody should be allowed to sell anyway. Three days underground. You know what the worst part was?"

"The lack of candles?" Isolde guessed.

"The paperwork. They make you sign your own confession four times. Four! And each copy has to be countersigned by an officer who pretends his pen is dry so you have to lend him yours, and then he confiscates it." She'd shaken her head with genuine grievance. "The Ministry doesn't fear magic, love. It fears inefficiency."

Isolde thought of that conversation now as she stood across the street from the Ministry annex in the gray hour before dawn, watching frost crawl up the barred windows and trying very hard not to be sick.

The charm sat in her left pocket like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn.

Quentin had forbidden it. Not suggested against — forbidden, in that quiet way of his where the gentleness was the whole warning. He'd laid it on the counter between them two nights ago, a small disc of pressed briar-glass no bigger than a coin, wrapped in wax paper, and said: "This one you don't use. Whatever happens. Promise me."

"What does it do?"

"It opens things. Locks, doors, wards — anything built to keep someone in or out. It's the only charm I make that works on Ministry iron." He'd turned it over in his fingers without offering it. "But it isn't powered by thornlight or moonlight or any of the polite energies. It runs on memory. Yours. The charm drinks one, whole, and it chooses which."

"Chooses how?"

"That's the cruelty of it. It takes the memory you're holding most tightly at the moment you break it." His eyes had been very steady. "So if you're thinking of someone you love —"

"I understand," she'd said, too quickly, and he'd known she didn't, not really, and she'd known *he* knew, and neither of them had said anything else about it.

She'd stolen it from his stockroom that same night. Which meant that technically she had now lied to him twice: once with her mouth, promising, and once with her hands.

The frost on the windows was thickening. In perhaps twenty minutes the night guard would change, and the changing of the guard at the annex was, according to Wren's old gossip, a leisurely affair involving a heated argument about whose turn it was to fetch breakfast rolls.

*You are doing this,* Isolde told herself, and the sentence felt less like courage than like reading her own name in a prophecy book: true, inevitable, and terrifying precisely because it was written down.

She crossed the street.

* * *

The annex's back door was exactly as unimpressive as Wren had promised. "They spend all their budget on the interrogation rooms," Wren had said. "The cells are an afterthought. The Ministry believes suffering should be austere."

Isolde crouched by the door, took out the wax paper, unwrapped the briar-glass charm, and held it in her cupped palms like something newly hatched.

Here was the problem she had turned over all night and could not solve: the charm took the memory she held most tightly. So she needed to hold something loosely. Something disposable. She needed to stand at a locked door, in mortal danger, with her friend rotting three floors below her, and think of something *unimportant*.

She tried. She summoned up the taste of bad coffee from the shop on Candlewick Row. The color of the ribbon on a hat she'd owned at fourteen. The name of a boy who had once — no, that one had edges; that one pulled.

Her mind kept sliding, treacherous and loyal, toward the faces she loved. Her mother, dead six years, hands dusted with flour. And —

And Mara.

Mara, who was eleven years older and had taught her to bind books before she could write her own name properly. Mara, who laughed from the belly and swore like a stevedore and had gone north two winters ago to take a commission binding ledgers for some northern merchant house and had written exactly nine letters since, each shorter than the last. Mara's face — round, freckled, one eyebrow permanently skeptical — floated up in Isolde's mind with the awful vividness of things remembered by people who don't yet know they should be memorizing.

"No," Isolde whispered to it. "Not you. Go away. Think of hats. Think of coffee."

The door's lock gleamed at her, patient and Ministry-gray.

Somewhere below, Wren was sitting in an austere cell, and Wren had been arrested for trafficking charms that were, as far as Isolde knew, entirely harmless — luck-knots and fever-charms and one scandalous singing candle — but Wren knew things. Wren had heard things in the interrogation, if the smuggled note tucked in Isolde's sleeve was truthful: *they asked about the gate. they asked about Q by name. they want it open, dove. i'll die politely before i tell them anything but please get me out before i run out of politeness.*

Wren had also drawn a small rude picture of the chief interrogator, which Isolde chose not to dwell on.

She looked down at the charm.

And here was the thing she understood only then, standing in the cold with her breath making little ghosts in the air: there was no clever trick. There was no way to hold Mara lightly, because Mara was not light. Nobody you love is light. The charm would choose, and the charm would choose well, and the only real question was whether Wren's life was worth one face.

It was. That wasn't even hard. That was the easy arithmetic.

What was hard was everything after.

"Forgive me," Isolde said — to Mara, to Quentin, to the charm, to whoever governed the exchange rate between memories and doors.

She broke it.

* * *

The sound the charm made was small and private, like a knuckle cracking in another room. The lock sighed. The door drifted open an inch, exhaling warm air that smelled of ink and floor wax and, underneath, faintly, of ash.

Isolde stood very still.

Something was missing.

That's the strange truth no one warns you about: forgetting doesn't feel like absence. It feels like a smooth place where a stone used to be. She reached for her sister — the reflex of a lifetime, the mind's habit of touching its treasures — and found a shape with no features. A beloved silhouette. She knew Mara existed. She knew she loved her. She knew, distantly, that there had been a face, the way you know there was weather yesterday.

She could not see it. She would never see it again. Somewhere inside her, in a place she couldn't follow, a door had opened and something had walked out carrying her sister's smile, and it was not coming back.

Isolde discovered she was kneeling on the frost-slick step. She did not remember getting there. She was crying without drama, the way you bleed from a wound you haven't found yet, and some distant practical part of her noted that she had perhaps four minutes before the guards finished their argument about breakfast rolls, and that grief, however legitimate, was a poor use of them.

"Later," she told herself out loud, and her voice came out cracked and strange. "Grieve later. Move now."

She moved.

* * *

The stairs down were narrow, lit every third flight by a lamp that burned greenish and gave off the smell of pickled something. The Ministry believed in austerity, all right. The corridors had the personality of a ledger.

Two guards passed her on the second landing. This was the part Isolde had rehearsed until her throat was sore: she did not hide. Hiding made you suspicious. Instead she walked with the brisk misery of a junior clerk, clutching a folder she'd forged that morning (her bookbinding skills finally useful for crime), and muttered, "Third confession copy, countersigned, don't ask," and both guards stepped aside with the instinctive courtesy of men who wanted no part of anyone else's paperwork.

One of them even held the door.

The cells were behind a gate of black iron that hummed faintly when she touched it — the same note, she realized with a chill, as the Hollow Gate, though thinner, like a bell compared to a cathedral. The lock here was ordinary. Ordinary locks, at least, still respected picks, and Wren had taught her picks over three patient evenings ("Everyone should know how to commit minor crimes, dove, it builds character").

The gate swung open. The corridor beyond smelled of damp straw and despair administered in regulated doses.

"Wren," Isolde whispered. "Wren Alderby. Which cell?"

A pause. Then, from somewhere to her left, a voice like dry leaves having opinions: "If you're a hallucination, you're underdressed. If you're real, you owe me a new set of picks, because they confiscated mine along with my dignity and my left boot."

Isolde followed the voice. Wren sat on a cot behind bars, small and wiry and gray-faced, wearing a prisoner's smock and an expression of profound administrative disgust.

"You look terrible," Isolde said.

"Three days of Ministry porridge will do that. You look worse. What happened to your face?"

"Nothing. Later. Can you get out? The bar mechanism —"

"Is a joke. They think the humiliation does the work." Wren was already up, working the latch with fingers that shook only slightly. "Which is lucky, because my hands aren't what they were. Right. Before we go anywhere—" she stopped, gripping the bars, looking at Isolde with sudden terrible clarity, "—you used it. The forbidden charm. Quentin's memory-charm. I can smell it on you. Thorn-glass has a scent after it breaks, like burnt honey."

Isolde said nothing.

"Oh, you *idiot*," Wren said softly, and it was the softness that undid her more than any shout could have. "What did it take?"

"I don't — I can't —" Isolde's voice failed. She pressed her hand flat against her own sternum, over the smooth place. "I know I lost something. I know it was hers. I can't even tell you whose face it was. Isn't that the worst part? I've lost the ability to mourn her properly. I'm grieving a stranger I apparently loved more than anything."

Wren came through the opened gate and took Isolde's face in both grimy hands.

"Listen to me. Listen. It was your sister. Mara." She said the name gently, like setting down something fragile. "Round face. Freckles. One eyebrow always up like she's waiting for the world to justify itself. Laughed like thunder being charming about it. Taught you to bind books. Went north two winters ago. Writes short letters."

Isolde made a sound that was not words.

"She'll write again," Wren went on, relentless and kind. "And when she does, you'll learn her face again, letter by letter, the way blind people learn the sun. It won't be the same face. But it will be hers, because she'll still be in it. Faces are just where people keep themselves, dove. The keeping goes deeper than the shelf."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I trade in charms, and every charm I ever sold was just a shelf." Wren released her, wiped her eyes roughly with her wrist, and became brisk. "Right. We're leaving through the coal chute, because the front desk logs exits and I refuse to be a line item. And while we walk, you're going to tell me everything, because I have information that's going to ruin your week."

* * *

The coal chute was exactly as dignified as it sounded. They emerged through a grate into an alley smelling of cinders, both of them streaked gray, Wren limping on her bootless foot and refusing to discuss it.

"The interrogators," Wren said, low and fast, as they wound toward the river district. "They're not fools, whatever their pens suggest. Third day, they stopped asking about the charms. Started asking about the Hollow Gate. Asked if I'd seen it open. Asked who held the key. Asked —" she glanced sideways at Isolde, "— about a woman named Vane. Your name, dove. They have your name. And then the chief interrogator said a thing I haven't stopped hearing since. He said, 'The Architect requires the door functional by the thirteenth turn.' Said it like a man reporting weather. Like the Ministry reports to someone."

The alley narrowed. Isolde's heart was doing something arrhythmic.

"The Architect," she repeated. "Quentin mentioned — there's something in the prophecy. An original architect of the Gate. Quentin thinks it's still—"

"Alive. Or whatever passes for it. And the Ministry answers to it, or serves it, or fears it — pick your poison. Either way, the law itself is on the other side, and it wants the door open." Wren hopped a gutter with surprising grace for a woman missing a boot. "Now. Second piece of ruinous news. They mentioned the oaths. Riverkeeper, Stonefather, Skywarden — said the names like items on a delivery manifest. They know you're gathering them. Which means they know where the next one sleeps."

"The Stonefather," Isolde said slowly. "The quarries below Vell. The prophecy gives a verse — 'where the mountain learned to kneel.'"

"Mm. And the Ministry owns those quarries. Leased half of them out for the new aqueduct works." Wren stopped at the alley's mouth, where gray daylight was starting, and turned to Isolde with her skeptical eyebrow — the borrowed memory of a different eyebrow entirely, but the gesture landed all the same. "So. You broke a forbidden charm, cost yourself heaven knows what, committed approximately four crimes before breakfast, and rescued a fence who is about to become a fugitive. How does it feel, this covenant business?"

Isolde thought about the smooth place inside her. About the letter she would someday receive from a beloved stranger with her sister's handwriting. About Quentin, who had forbidden the charm because he'd known exactly this would happen, and who would look at her when she told him — because she would tell him, she owed him that much — with that terrible gentle grief of his.

"Like a debt," she said. "Everything worth having comes due."

"Then we'd better go collect the rest of it before the collectors do." Wren pulled the hood of Isolde's cloak up over her coal-dusted hair and grinned, fierce and exhausted. "Come on, dove. The quarries wait for no woman, and neither does the moon."

Far above the rooftops, pale in the brightening sky, the waning moon hung like a mark on a wrist — five turns down, eight to go — and somewhere beneath the city, in the dark where mountains had learned to kneel, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and waited to be woken.
