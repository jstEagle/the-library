## 10. The Second Oath: Stonefather

Quentin had always believed that the worst thing about being a fugitive would be the running.

He was wrong. The worst thing was the packing. Specifically, it was Isolde Vane packing, which she did the way a magpie furnishes a nest — enthusiastically, indiscriminately, and with a great deal of commentary.

"Do we need the good luck-knots?" she asked, holding up an armful of them. They were traveling light, which in Isolde's translation meant one satchel for him and three for her.

"Those are for hangnails," Quentin said. "Leave them."

"Hangnails are serious. You told me that yourself."

"I tell customers that so they buy two."

She set them down. She picked them up again when he wasn't looking. He saw. He said nothing, because three moons into this strange partnership he had learned to choose his battles, and also because — though he'd have chewed off his own tongue before admitting it — the knots were useful. Hangnails happened on the road. So did worse.

They went down into the quarries before dawn, taking the old cart road that switchbacked beneath the city's southern wall. Vell was built upward, story upon story of pale stone and ambition, but everything above was quarried from below, and below was where the city kept its secrets. The Ministry watched the gates and the rooftops. It did not watch the holes in the earth, because the holes were ugly, and power has never liked looking at what it came from.

The prophecy's verse sat folded against Quentin's heart, copied out in Isolde's careful bookbinder's hand:

*Where the mountain gave its bones away,
the father sleeps in borrowed clay.
Wake him not with sword or fire,
but with the word of true desire.*

"True desire," Isolde read aloud as they walked, her breath fogging in the cold. "That's wonderfully specific. Not vague at all."

"It's more specific than the Riverkeeper's verse. That one just said *beneath the water, under will*. We spent two days working out that *will* meant willow."

"And then I nearly drowned, and you actually drowned a little, and we agreed never to speak of it."

"I died briefly," Quentin agreed. "It's been a very educational season."

But he was smiling as he said it, and he caught her catching him, and neither of them looked away quite fast enough. Three moon-turns since the shrine. Six marks now on her wrist, small silver crescents that appeared each night like the moon keeping tally. Six turns gone, seven remaining. Somewhere behind them, Wren sat in a Ministry cell; somewhere ahead, the Stonefather slept in stone; and somewhere in between, walking beside him with three overfull satchels and ink-stained fingers, was the person Quentin Eastwick had begun, quietly and against all his better training, to love.

He was not going to say anything about it. Saying things got people hurt. He'd learned that from his mother, who had loved him enough to trade her name for his safety, and whose love had therefore become a debt coming due.

The quarry opened around them like a cathedral designed by someone who hated cathedrals. Terraces of cut stone descended in gray steps, each terrace littered with the leavings of a century: chipped blocks, rusted chains, the skeletal cranes that had once lifted mountains into walls. Frost furred everything. In the lowest basin, half-drowned in shadow, stood the shape they had come for.

It was a giant, or had been. Now it was a statue taller than any tower in Vell, seated on a throne of unquarried rock, its knees drawn up like a tired shepherd resting on a hillside. Moss grew in the seams of its shoulders. Its face had weathered smooth, features worn to suggestion — but there was no mistaking the posture, the immense patience of it. It looked like something that had sat down intending to rest a moment and had simply continued.

"The Stonefather," Isolde breathed.

"He quarried the first stones of Vell himself," Quentin said. "Before the covenant, before the gate. The stories say he loved the city so much he let them take pieces of him to build it. Then the breaking came, and he sat down here where his heart used to be, and turned to stone rather than watch." He paused. "That's probably embellishment. Prophecies encourage embellishment."

"You're a romantic wearing a cynic's coat," Isolde said. "I've known that since the fever-charms."

"My fever-charms are extremely cynical."

"They cure fevers, Quentin. There's nothing cynical about that."

They climbed down the terraces toward the giant, their footsteps ringing oddly in the cold air, and Quentin felt the old familiar hum rising through the soles of his boots — the same hum the Hollow Gate made, the same note that lived under his shop, under the whole city if you knew how to listen. Magic didn't die in Vell. It just went underground and waited. Like everything else down here.

They reached the basin floor. Up close, the giant was less a statue than a landscape: lichen seas on his arms, a waterfall of frost down his chest, small pale flowers growing from the crook of his elbow as if the stone itself had decided, after all these years, to grow something gentle.

Isolde stepped forward, opened her book, and began — as they had planned, as the Riverkeeper's oath had taught them — to speak the waking words.

She got four syllables in.

Then the quarry lit up with lantern-light, and the world ended politely, from above.

* * *

Isolde heard the click of boot-heels on the upper terrace a heartbeat before the lanterns flared — thirty of them at least, arranged in a crescent along the rim of the quarry like a smile made of fire. Figures in the grey-lacquered armor of the Ministry of Unnatural Affairs stood between each flame, and at the center of the crescent, leaning on the crane-rigging with the relaxed posture of someone attending the theater, stood a woman in a coat the color of dried blood.

Isolde knew that coat. Everyone in the book-trade knew that coat. Deputy Inquisitor Halloway had burned eleven mages last year and attended every burning personally, because paperwork, she reportedly said, should be witnessed by the hand that signed it.

"Well," Halloway called down, her voice carrying with unnatural clarity across the basin. "The bookbinder and the charm-seller. Sitting in a quarry. About to wake a giant. If I'd written it as satire they'd have called it heavy-handed."

Quentin's hand found Isolde's wrist — the marked one — and pressed once. *Stay calm.* His other hand slid, casually, terribly, into his pocket, where the forbidden charms lived: the ones that cost things. Memory. Years. Names.

"Don't," she whispered.

"We can run."

"Thirty of them, Quentin. On the high ground. With crossbows." She looked up at the lantern-crescent, at the little silhouettes of the bolts already nocked. "We can't run. But they don't want us dead. If they wanted us dead we'd already be pincushions. They want the oaths. They want the gate." Her mind, trained by years of restoring damaged texts, was already reading the scene like a page: the ambush too neat, the timing too perfect, the Inquisitor too pleased. "They've known about the Stonefather. They've been waiting for *us* to find him, because they can't wake him themselves."

Below them, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the giant hummed.

Halloway began to descend the terraces with her soldiers, unhurried, a woman collecting a debt. "Here is what happens next," she announced conversationally. "You both come quietly. The girl shows us how the oaths work. The charm-seller shows us how the gate works. And in exchange, you get trials instead of pyres. I'm told the cells beneath the Ministry are quite comfortable. Dry, even."

"She's lying," Isolde said quietly. "About the dry part."

"I know," Quentin said. And then he did the thing Isolde would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would later write into the margins of the prophecy book in her smallest, most careful script, because some events deserve documentation: he kissed her knuckles, once, quickly, like a man signing his name to something — and stepped out in front of her, onto open ground, and took his hands out of his pockets empty.

"Deputy Inquisitor," he called up, clear and steady. "You should know something about me before your people shoot."

"Enlighten me."

"I'm the mage you've been looking for."

The word dropped into the quarry like a stone into deep water. Isolde saw the soldiers stiffen. She saw Halloway's theatrical ease falter into genuine hunger. And she saw — this was the worst of it, the part that made her chest ache even in the moment — that Quentin was smiling. Gently. Almost apologetically. As if he'd just admitted to tracking mud across her clean floor.

"Three years I've sold luck-knots under your noses," he went on, and the hum in the ground began to rise through him, up through his boots and spine and voice, until the frost on the nearest stones started to glow with a soft gold light, thornlight, gate-light, *his* light. "Three years I've hidden. And you want to know the funny thing? It worked. Right up until the day a bookbinder walked into my stall and ruined absolutely everything."

"Quentin—" Isolde started.

"So here's my counteroffer," he said, and raised one hand, and the quarry answered.

Light erupted from the terraces — not fire, not lightning, but growing things, golden briars bursting up through the seams of the quarried stone, fast and green-gold and utterly alive, tangling crossbows, snuffing lanterns, wrapping soldiers in cages of bloom and bramble that scratched without cutting. The men shouted and stumbled. Halloway's coat whipped in a sudden wind that smelled, impossibly, of spring.

Isolde stood frozen in the middle of it, in the eye of the blooming storm, watching Quentin hold thirty soldiers at bay with the magic he had spent three years burying — spending it all at once, openly, gloriously, the way a miser spends a fortune, the way a man spends his own name.

Because that was the truth of it, and she understood it in one cold, clarifying flash: the moment the Ministry saw his face doing this, he was dead in Vell. No trial, no cell, no deal. Pyre. He was not buying their escape. He was buying hers, and paying with everything.

"No," she said aloud, to him, to the quarry, to the whole stupid arrangement of a world that asked this of people. "No, you absolute *idiot*—"

And she turned to the giant, tore her book open to the waking verse, and shouted it at the top of her lungs.

*Where the mountain gave its bones away!*

The hum became a roar.

*The father sleeps in borrowed clay!*

Cracks spidered up the giant's chest, glowing gold. Lichen fell away like shed clothing. Somewhere deep in the stone, something that had been holding its breath for a hundred years began — slowly, tectonically — to inhale.

"Wake him not with sword or fire," Isolde screamed over the cracking, over the shouting, over Quentin's briars roaring skyward, "*but with the word of true desire*—" and she ran to Quentin, grabbed his face in both ink-stained hands, and finished the verse against his mouth:

"I desire *you*, you stubborn, self-burying fool — live —"

The Stonefather opened his eyes.

* * *

There was no describing it, so Quentin never tried. Later, he would say only that the quarry filled with the smell of rain on dust and the sound of a hammer striking an anvil somewhere far underground, and that a voice like a mountain clearing its throat said:

***WHO WAKES ME?***

"Isolde Vane," Isolde shouted back, still holding Quentin's face, both of them lit gold by the giant's opening eyes. "Witness of the covenant! I've come for the second oath!"

The great weathered face turned toward them. Where features had been worn to nothing, light now carved them fresh: brow, cheek, a mouth old beyond language. The eyes were the color of granite shot through with quartz, and they looked at Isolde first, and softened.

***THE WITNESS'S BLOOD.*** A pause, vast as a season. ***AND THE KEEPER. BURNING HIMSELF TO KEEP HER. YES. THAT IS THE WORD OF TRUE DESIRE. IT ALWAYS WAS.***

His hand moved — a movement like a cliff deciding to be elsewhere — and pressed one enormous finger gently, gently, to the ground before them. From the fingertip, a sliver of stone rose, small as a coin, gold-veined, humming the gate's own note. It drifted to Isolde and settled in her palm, warm as a held hand.

***THE SECOND OATH. CARRY IT TO THE DOOR. AND KEEPER—***

The stone eyes shifted to Quentin.

***STOP SPENDING YOURSELF LIKE A MAN WHO EXPECTS TO DIE YOUNG. IT IS CONTAGIOUS.***

Then the light folded back into the giant like a wave returning to the sea, and he was stone again — but different, somehow. Rested. The flowers in the crook of his elbow had spread another inch.

Around them, the quarry was chaos and quiet at once. The Ministry soldiers lay tangled in briars that were already, mercifully, going dormant and dropping their captives into bewildered heaps. Lanterns guttered. And Quentin swayed, caught Isolde's shoulder, and discovered that revealing yourself as the most wanted mage in Vell costs rather a lot out of a purse you'd kept hidden for three years.

"That," he managed, "was a mistake."

"Which part?"

"All of it. Every part. Especially—" he gestured vaguely at his own face, at the kiss, at the entire catastrophic sequence, "—that."

"Especially that," Isolde repeated. She put his arm over her shoulders, tucked the stone-oath into her satchel next to the Riverkeeper's relic, and looked up at him with an expression he couldn't read and would spend chapters failing to forget. "Quentin Eastwick. You just showed the entire Ministry of Unnatural Affairs your face while casting illegal magic to save me. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I'm a terrible fugitive?"

"That you're *mine* now," she said. "Fugitive mine. Both of us. There's no Vell left for either of us after tonight, so we'd better make certain there's a *us* instead." She hauled him toward the far tunnel — the one that ran, according to the maps she'd memorized, east and down, toward the borderlands where Thorn's briarlight leaked into the world. "Come on. We have seven moon-turns, two oaths, one third warden, and a lifetime of nowhere to live."

Behind them, untangling herself from a briar cage with tremendous dignity, Deputy Inquisitor Halloway picked up her fallen hat, put it on, and smiled at their retreating backs — the smile of a woman who had gotten exactly what she came for, though not at all in the way she'd planned.

"Note it down," she told her scribbling clerk. "Confirmed: living keeper, active gate, witness-bloodline. And send a bird to the Architect's address."

The clerk hesitated. "Ma'am, the Architect doesn't have an address. The Architect has a *door*."

"Then knock," Halloway said pleasantly, "and tell it dinner's coming early."
