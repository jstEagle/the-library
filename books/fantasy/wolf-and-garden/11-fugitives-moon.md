## 11. Fugitives' Moon

The cottage had no door.

That was the first thing Quentin noticed about it, standing in the briar-light of Thorn's borderlands with a bruised rib and Isolde's hand locked around his wrist like she intended to keep it. The structure was stone at its bones and living wood everywhere else — walls that had been hedges once, roof of woven branches still putting out new leaves, a chimney made from the hollow trunk of something that had probably objected. Where a front door should have been, the wall simply parted into an archway hung with flowering vines, as if the house had decided doors were for people who expected visitors.

"It's watching us," Isolde said.

"Everything in Thorn is watching us."

"No, I mean the house specifically." She narrowed her eyes at the vine archway. "It grew shut when we walked past it the first time. Then it opened again when you said you were tired. It's *listening* to you."

Quentin considered this. Three years of keeping secrets had made him fluent in the language of things listening: wards that murmured, locks that remembered, a gate beneath his shop that hummed when certain bloodlines came near. A house that eavesdropped was, frankly, one of the politer entities he'd encountered since crossing over.

"Good," he said. "Then it heard me say we're staying. Let's see if it makes up a bed."

The house did. By morning there was a mattress of moss inside that smelled faintly of rain and mint, two blankets spun from — he chose not to investigate too closely — thistledown, and a shelf that hadn't existed the night before, holding three clay jars and a wooden spoon, as though the house had guessed they'd be the sort of fugitives who cooked.

"I've lived above a magical gate my whole adult life," Quentin told the shelf, "and this is the first time magic has ever done something *helpful* without being asked or paid."

From the garden — which was less a garden than a small riot that tolerated them — Isolde called back, "Maybe it likes us."

"Maybe," Quentin said, "it's fattening us up."

* * *

They fell into days the way water finds a channel: without deciding to.

Mornings belonged to herbs. Thorn's borderlands grew things that Vell's Ministry had spent a century burning out of the human world — feverleaf that glowed faintly blue at dawn, woundmoss that stitched itself into cuts if you apologized to it first, a bitter little flower called moonspite that Quentin remembered his mother using in charms against nightmares. He taught Isolde which stems to cut and which to ask. She learned faster than any apprentice he'd ever had, mostly because she treated the plants like authors whose books she was repairing: gently, respectfully, with an eye for where the damage was.

"You're talking to the nettle," he observed on the fourth day, watching her negotiate with a stand of it.

"I'm being polite."

"The nettle doesn't have ears."

"Neither does the prophecy book, and it wrote your name down a hundred years ago." She stripped the leaves into her basket. "Everything here is listening. You said so yourself."

He had no answer to that, so he picked up the basket and carried it, which was becoming his primary role in their partnership: the one who held things. Her basket, her tools, her temper when the woundmoss sulked, his own heart, which he'd stopped trying to take back somewhere around the sixth day and had not mentioned aloud, because some inventory you don't declare at the border.

Afternoons were charms. His kit had survived the flight from Vell better than most of his other possessions, including his dignity, and there was always work: luck-knots gone slack, fever-charms cracked from the cold nights, the little tin star Wren had given him years ago that needed re-soldering and, if he was honest, forgiving. Isolde sat beside him with her bookbinding tools and repaired what the Ministry's ambush had torn — a satchel strap, the spine of the prophecy book, a page corner that had charred when Quentin's magic went off like a struck match in the quarry.

She worked on that page a long time.

"It's the first witness's handwriting," she said finally. "Underneath the prophecy. You can barely see it unless the light comes through the paper sideways." She tilted the leaf against the afternoon sun, and for a moment the hidden text ghosted up through the vellum like fish under ice. "There's more in here than a prophecy, Quentin. There's a testimony. Someone wanted this found by whoever came next."

"After the thirteenth turn?"

"After everything." She smoothed the page flat but didn't press it closed. "I'll need proper light. And quiet. And possibly a miracle." She glanced at him sidelong. "Two of those are available."

He laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising him on its way out. It had been a long time since laughing cost nothing.

* * *

The evenings were the dangerous part.

Not because of Thorn. Thorn's dangers announced themselves — the briars that shifted when the moon moved, the distant bell-like calls of things he chose not to identify, the fox-spirit who had warned them about the Architect and who now appeared every few nights at the edge of the garden to watch them eat, tail curled around its feet, radiating the specific judgment of a cat that has seen you do something foolish and intends to remember it.

No, the dangerous part was how easily the evenings came.

They cooked together because there was only one pot and the house kept moving it between them, apparently under the impression that this was helpful. They ate at a table grown from a stump, knees nearly touching because the stump was not large. Isolde told him about her sister — the real stories, not the guarded ones — about Maren who laughed at funerals and cried at weddings and had once, aged nine, tried to bind a book using honey and their mother's good thread. Quentin told her about his mother in return, carefully at first, then less so: the woman who had sold her name to buy him three safe years, the debt ticking underneath his life like water under ice.

"She never told me what she traded away," he said one night, turning the tin star over in his fingers. "Her name. Whether it was the word itself or the memory of it or—" He shook his head. "I just know that near the end, when people spoke to her, she'd flinch. Like every sentence was addressed to someone else."

Isolde reached across the stump-table and put her hand over his.

She didn't say anything. That was the thing about her he kept failing to predict: everyone else would have filled the silence. She let it hold weight.

* * *

On the ninth night, the mark appeared.

He saw it first because he was mending her sleeve — she'd caught it on a briar that, in his professional opinion, had reached for her on purpose — and her wrist turned in the firelight, and there it was: a thin crescent of silvered skin, fine as a scar, curving along the inside of her forearm like a moon setting into her flesh.

Six marks had come before it, each on the waning of the moon, each one a turn of the count the prophecy kept. This was the seventh.

Isolde looked at it the way she looked at damaged pages: steady, cataloguing, refusing to flinch. But her other hand, below the table, had gone white-knuckled around her knee.

"Seven," she said.

"Seven."

"Thirteen total." She flexed her fingers. The crescent caught the light. "Six turns left. Six turns to find the third oath and figure out what the first witness was trying to tell me before someone decides the story ends differently."

Quentin set down her sleeve. Then, because his hands needed employment and lying was a craft he practiced daily, he picked it back up and finished the stitch.

"When it hurts," he said quietly, "you could tell me. When the marks burn. You don't have to perform composure for me, Isolde. I've seen you cry over a water-stained flyleaf. Your reputation for stoicism is already ruined."

That earned him half a smile, which he counted as a victory and hoarded accordingly.

"It doesn't hurt yet," she said. "It hums. Like—" She paused, tilting her head, and he watched her arrive at the same place he already had. "Like the gate."

"Like the gate," he agreed. "You're becoming a kind of keeper yourself. The covenant isn't just counting down. It's counting *onto* you." He tied off the last stitch and bit the thread. "I keep thinking I should be terrified of that."

"And instead?"

Instead he looked at her — firelit, ink-stained, seven moons marked into her skin and walking toward a death the prophecy might or might not name, and the truest thing he could say was the simplest one.

"Instead I'm grateful," he said. "Whatever's coming, it gets both of us. That's more than I had three weeks ago, which was a shop full of harmless charms and a secret I couldn't put down."

The house creaked — warmly, he thought. Even the walls approved. In the garden, the fox-spirit flicked an ear and vanished, unimpressed.

* * *

But the moon kept waning. Moons do.

On the twelfth night the temperature dropped wrong — not the clean cold of autumn but a thin, greedy chill, like breath drawn through teeth. The briars at the borderland's edge went dark, their light dimming all at once, and the fox-spirit appeared at the garden wall earlier than usual, hackles lifted, staring east toward the deeps of Thorn.

"What is it?" Isolde asked, coming out with her shawl clutched closed.

The fox looked at her, then at Quentin, and spoke in its dry rust-and-paper voice: "Something has been asking after you. Not hunting. *Asking.* It goes among the exiles wearing borrowed faces, and it asks, politely, whether anyone knows where the keeper and the witness have gone." Its ears flattened. "Tonight it stopped asking."

The chill deepened. Down the slope, in the dark where the briar-light failed, Quentin heard — very faintly, very far off, and unmistakably patient — footsteps. Not approaching. Just walking. As if something had all the time in the world and wanted them to know it.

Isolde's hand found his in the dark. Seven crescents glowed faintly along her arm.

"Quentin," she whispered, "it knows exactly where we are."

The footsteps paused — directly, deliberately, at the edge of the briar-light — and waited, courteous as a knock.
