## 12. The Architect's Invitation

The herbs needed drying before the moon turned again, and Isolde had decided — in the particular way she decided things, which was to say stubbornly and with a great deal of muttering — that she would be the one to hang them.

Quentin had argued otherwise. Quentin argued most mornings, gently, the way he did everything: *stay inside the wards, stay below the ridge, stay where I can hear you if something moves.* But the borderland cottage they'd claimed sat at the seam between realms, where Vell's gray light bled into Thorn's green, and the seam grew the best feverfew either of them had ever seen. It grew it defiantly, as if daring someone to pick it.

So she picked it. She filled two baskets, hummed a tune Wren used to whistle while fencing stolen luck, and told herself firmly that the prickling feeling at the back of her neck was only the weather.

It was not the weather.

"You have his hands," said a voice behind her, warm as bread and wrong as a mirror. "Did you know that? Bookbinder's hands. He has his father's shoulders and his mother's hands."

Isolde did not turn around immediately. This was partly tactics and partly because her knees had opinions of their own, and their opinion was *no.*

When she did turn, she found a woman standing among the feverfew where no woman had been a heartbeat before. Middle-aged, silver-streaked hair pinned carelessly, ink stains on her fingers — actual ink stains, the kind a person earns over decades of letters ledgers and lists. A face like a kitchen on a cold morning. A face that had clearly laughed often and meant it.

Isolde had never seen her before in her life. She knew exactly who she was looking at anyway.

"Quentin's mother," she said.

"Margaret Eastwick." The woman smiled and spread her stained hands, an apologetic little gesture, as though embarrassed to be caught existing. "Or the shape of her. I borrowed it from his memory, not mine. I hope you don't mind. Faces are so much easier to talk to when they belong to someone you love. You'll listen longer. People always do."

"That's a horrible way to conduct a conversation."

"It is," Margaret's face agreed pleasantly. "I find honesty disarms people far better than courtesy. Shall we sit? Your basket looks heavy, and I promise I won't eat anything until dessert."

*She eats promises,* the fox-spirit had warned them, months ago, from its perch on a luminous briar. *The Architect walks and eats promises. Careful what you swear near it, little witness.*

Isolde set down the basket. She did not sit. Sitting felt like agreeing.

"You're not her," she said. "You can't be. Quentin watched her die three years ago."

"I'm wearing her the way you'd wear a coat," the thing said, unbothered, settling itself onto a flat stone with the ease of long practice. "A very well-loved coat. He remembers every stitch. Do you know what he remembers most? That she burned the porridge every single morning and pretended she hadn't. He's never told anyone that. He thinks if he says it aloud, he loses it." The smile softened, and that was somehow worse. "I keep it safe for him. I keep everything safe for him. That's rather the whole trouble."

Wind moved through the briars behind the cottage. The thornlight caught in the woman's silver hair and made it glow faintly, the way everything glowed in Thorn, gently and without permission.

"You've come about the oaths," Isolde said.

"Direct. Good. Margaret hated dithering too — another stitch I kept." The Architect folded its borrowed hands. "Two oaths. Riverkeeper's, pulled dripping from a drowned shrine. Stonefather's, cracked out of a petrified giant while the Ministry shot arrows at you both — which, by the way, was magnificent. I do enjoy watching people surprise themselves. You especially. You were never supposed to be brave. Prophecies make cowards of most witnesses."

"And the third one's still up a very tall tower," Isolde said, "behind your new pet."

Something flickered across Margaret's face — not hurt, nothing so human, but a recalculation, quick as a ledger being balanced. "The hollowed mage is not my doing. The Ministry hollows its own. I merely... appreciate the craftsmanship."

"You eat broken promises," Isolde said. "He hasn't broken one yet. His mother bought him three years, and those three years end at the thirteenth turn, and until then he owes you nothing you can collect. So why are you here?"

For a moment — just a moment — the warm kitchen face went still in a way no living face could go still. All the laughter drained out of it at once, evenly, like water finding its level, and underneath was something old and patient and terribly hungry, wearing patience the way it wore skin.

Then the warmth poured back in, seamless.

"Because I would rather not collect," it said. "Believe it or not, keeper-debts are thin fare. A name here, a death there. Dry eating. What I love — what I *feast* on — is a broken vow between two people who meant it. A wedding promise abandoned. A friend betrayed. A lover who swears *I will come back* and doesn't." Its eyes — Margaret's eyes, brown and crinkled at the corners — fixed on Isolde with dreadful kindness. "And you two have been swearing things to each other all season. Little vows. *We'll get through this. I won't let them take you. Whatever happens at the gate—*" It clicked its tongue fondly. "Do you know how rich you're making me? Every night by that fire, I dine."

Isolde's stomach turned over slowly, like a page being leafed backward. All those quiet evenings in the borderland cottage. The stolen domesticity she'd hoarded like pressed flowers. Had some part of it been feeding the thing in the dark?

"You lie," she said. "That's what you do. You twist."

"I never lie," the Architect said, and there was real pride in it. "Lying breaks promises. Lying is for amateurs and ministries. I simply hold people to what they actually said, in the words they actually used, and let them discover the gap between that and what they meant. It's remarkable how wide that gap runs." It tilted its head. "But I digress. I came with an offer, and Margaret would scold me for wasting daylight."

It rose. Brushed nonexistent dirt from its skirts. And held out one ink-stained hand, palm up, in the universal gesture of merchants everywhere — the gesture Quentin himself used at his stall, selling luck-knots to people who didn't know what luck cost.

"The debt," the Architect said. "Your beloved keeper's debt. Three years purchased by a dying woman's name, coming due on the thirteenth turn, payable in full. I can release it. Entirely. Cleanly. He lives, unfettered, owing me nothing — free to grow old and burn porridge for his own children someday." The smile deepened. "In exchange, you bring me the two oaths you carry. Riverkeeper's and Stonefather's. Tonight. And the third, when you fetch it, which you will, because you are thorough and he is doomed and you love him, which makes you predictable in the most beautiful way."

The wind stopped.

Isolde noticed that the way one notices a held breath. The whole borderland seam had gone quiet — no birds, no briar-rustle, no distant bells from Vell below. As if the world were leaning in to hear her answer.

"And if I say no?" she asked.

"Then nothing today," the Architect said mildly. "Nothing tomorrow. I am very old; I do not pout. The debt comes due on its own schedule. He dies on the thirteenth turn unless the covenant is reforged, and the covenant cannot be reforged without three oaths, and you will have given me none, and I will simply wait here, at this lovely seam, and watch the two of you spend twelve more nights making vows you may or may not keep. Either way, I eat." It shrugged Margaret's small shrug. "The offer isn't a trap, child. It's a menu."

Isolde looked at the hand. Ink-stained. Warm-looking. A dead woman's hand offered in friendship by the thing that had eaten her name.

She thought about Quentin — really thought about him, the way she tried not to during the day because it made the herb-work slow. Quentin measuring out fever-charms with absurd precision. Quentin laughing at his own jokes half a beat before finishing them. Quentin asleep with his mouth slightly open and one hand curled around a warding-chalk stub even in dreams, as if duty had grown into his bones. Quentin writing letters last week in a careful hand and burning them when he thought she wasn't looking, because he was already practicing leaving her.

Three years he'd lived under that debt. Three years of keeping a door shut beneath his shop, alone, telling no one, because telling anyone would put them in the path of the Ministry and the Ministry burned mages. He had paid for his mother's bargain in loneliness, daily, compounding interest.

And here was the thing offering to wipe the slate clean. All he had to lose was everything else.

"You're wondering whether I'm telling the truth," the Architect observed. "About releasing the debt. Fair. Here is my word, freely given: deliver the two oaths tonight, and the debt is dissolved before dawn. You may verify it any way you like. Ask him how his chest feels. Keepers feel the debt like a stone behind the sternum — he'll know the moment it lifts. He's carried it since he was nineteen years old." The voice gentled further, insufferably. "Imagine giving him that. Three years of dread, gone in one evening. No one else in either realm can offer him that. Only me."

*Only me.* Yes. That was the whole architecture of it, wasn't it? Build a cage, then sell the key.

Isolde picked up her basket. The weight of it steadied her — feverfew and valerian and yarrow, ordinary plants, ordinary work, the small honest economy of staying alive.

"You want an answer now," she said.

"I want an answer eventually. Tonight would be elegant. I'm told elegance matters to bookbinders." The Architect clasped its hands behind its back, the picture of a patient shopkeeper. "Take the evening. Talk to him. Tell him, tell him nothing — I confess I'm curious which you'll choose. Though I should mention—" and here Margaret's face did something terrible and tender, a mother's expression laid over a predator's intent like tissue paper over a knife, "—that if you refuse, and he learns you refused, he will spend his remaining turns trying to trade himself to me instead. He's his mother's son. They both think love means paying alone. I've eaten that particular vow before, from her. It tasted like iron and lilies. I imagine his will taste similar."

The world resumed its sounds all at once — birdsong, wind, the far bells of Vell striking the quarter hour, cheerful and oblivious.

"I'll consider it," Isolde said.

"Of course you will." The Architect began to fade — not dramatically, not in smoke and thunder, but the way morning fog goes, so gradually she couldn't name the moment it stopped being there. Margaret's face lingered longest, smiling at her with borrowed love. "One gift before I go, since we're being diplomatic. A hint, freely given, no charge: you've had the answer in your possession since the river shrine. You've simply been reading it wrong. Or rather — reading only half of it." A wink. "Prophecy books are like onions, little witness. There's always another layer, and everyone cries eventually."

Gone.

The feverfew stood undisturbed. The baskets sat where she'd left them. The borderland seam breathed around her, innocent again, and somewhere below, Vell's bells finished their quarter hour and began, absurdly, to ring a wedding peal — some couple in the city, vowing things in front of witnesses, meaning every word, having no idea what vows weighed or who was hungry.

Isolde stood in the quiet for a long moment.

Then she did the sensible thing. She sat down hard on the flat stone the Architect had warmed with its borrowed body, set the basket aside, and let her hands shake for exactly sixty seconds, because Wren had taught her that fear was like a fever-charm: useless unless you gave it its allotted time, and dangerous if you let it run past.

Sixty seconds. Then she stood, wiped her palms on her apron, and went inside.

* * *

Quentin was mending charms at the table, as he did every afternoon — a row of small failures waiting to become small mercies. Fever-knots with frayed tails. Luck-sigils whose ink had run. He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did, the softening, the relief he probably thought he hid.

"You're back early. Did the feverfew fight you?"

"Worse. I had a visitor."

His hands stilled over the knot he was tying. He knew. Of course he knew; he'd grown up knowing what visitors meant. "Describe it."

"A woman. Silver hair. Ink on her fingers. Said you burn the porridge every morning and pretend you didn't."

The color left his face in stages, like a tide going out. "That's—" He swallowed. "Nobody knows that. Nobody *living* knows that."

"She called herself Margaret Eastwick. She offered to erase your debt." Isolde crossed the room, took his face in her hands — bookbinder's hands, glue-and-thread hands, steady now that it mattered — and made herself look at him while she said it. "The price is the two oaths we gathered. Delivered to her tonight. And the third one after."

Quentin closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "You said no."

"I said I'd consider it."

"*Isolde.*"

"I'm considering it right now," she said. "I considered it the entire walk home. And I've concluded that I need to reread a certain book, because our guest got talkative at the end, and things that smug usually leave with more than they came for. She said I've had the answer since the river shrine and I've been reading only half of it." Her thumbs traced his cheekbones. "Quentin. How many pages does the prophecy book have?"

"What? I don't — however many. Two hundred? Why?"

"How many have I restored?"

He frowned. "Maybe ninety. The water damage—"

"Ninety pages of prophecy," Isolde said slowly, feeling the shape of it arrive the way the fox's warning had arrived, the way her own name had arrived in century-old ink, "and a hundred and ten I haven't touched. Ninety pages telling us to seal or sever. And a hundred and ten pages of *something else*, sitting in the back, waiting for someone with my hands and my patience and my infuriating habit of finishing books." She smiled, and it felt fierce. "She came to stop me reading, Quentin. Predators don't visit the prey they've already caught."

He stared at her. Behind him, through the window, the seam-light shifted — Vell's gray and Thorn's green braiding together over the ridgeline, two realms holding each other up.

"She said tonight would be elegant," Isolde added. "So I suggest we disappoint her elegantly. Get me good light, keeper. Tonight we read the other half of the book — and whatever's written in it, we decide *our* answer before she decides it for us."

From the direction of the feverfew patch, faint and far off, came a sound like a laugh with no mouth to make it.

The book, waiting on its shelf, seemed almost to lean toward them.
