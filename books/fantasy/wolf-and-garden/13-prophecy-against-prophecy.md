## 13. Prophecy Against Prophecy

### Isolde

The book had been fighting her for three days.

Isolde Vane had restored water-damaged volumes for eleven years — psalters swollen like drowned men, ledgers furred with mold, one unfortunate cookbook that had spent a winter in a pigsty and smelled of it forever. She knew how parchment sulked and how vellum forgave. She knew that a book was not an object but an argument, and that the restorer's job was to listen to the argument without taking sides.

This book took sides. This book cheated.

Every time she relaxed the binding enough to reach the inner quires, the pages near the spine went soft as wet leaves, ink bleeding into gray ghosts. She'd separated them with silk thread and patience. She'd hummed to them, because Quentin swore his mother had hummed to stubborn charms and it couldn't hurt. She'd even, on the second night, apologized aloud, which was ridiculous, except that the book had behaved marginally better afterward and she refused to examine what that meant.

Now, in the lamplit back room above the shop, she laid the final quire flat and held her breath.

The last leaf of the prophecy came free of its neighbors cleanly, the way a key turns when the lock has finally decided to trust you. The text resolved: *and at the thirteenth turn the keeper shall give what he was given, and the door shall be shut, or the door shall be severed, and between these two there is no third.*

She read it four times. It didn't improve.

"No third," she muttered. "How very tidy of you."

The prophecy had been annoyingly certain about everything else. Thirteen moon-turns. Her name, written a century before her birth, beside tonight's date — the night she'd walked into a charm-seller's stall and ruined her orderly life forever. A sketch of Quentin, younger-looking, tired around the eyes in a way she now recognized from three feet away rather than from a century-old page.

But this final line bothered her the way a badly stitched headband bothers a binder: not because it was damaged, but because it was *wrong*. Not factually wrong. Structurally wrong. The hand that wrote it pressed too hard, the letters crowded toward the gutter as though the scribe were writing quickly, or fearfully, or both.

Books lie by omission more often than by invention. Isolde had built a career on catching them at it.

She tilted the leaf toward the lamp. There — along the spine-side edge, where the paper had been folded back on itself for a hundred years — a faint ridge. Not a watermark. Not foxing. A fold so old it had become part of the page, hiding whatever lay beneath it the way a scar hides a wound.

Her heart did something unprofessional.

She worked for another hour, relaxing the fibers with warm air and unhurried fingers, teasing the fold open the way you'd coax a frightened animal from under a porch. When it finally gave, the hidden surface caught the lamplight, and Isolde Vane forgot to breathe entirely.

There was writing beneath the prophecy. Older writing. Smaller, cramped, slanting left as though the writer had been left-handed and hurried and cold all at once. And at the top, in letters that trembled only slightly:

*I am Maren Ostreva, first witness to the covenant, and I set down here what they will not let me say aloud.*

Downstairs, the shop bell chimed. Quentin was home.

### Quentin

Quentin Eastwick climbed the stairs with a bag of oranges, two meat pies, and the specific lightness he'd come to distrust whenever it arrived. Three years of keeping the Hollow Gate had taught him that happiness was usually the sound of something about to change.

He found Isolde bent over the worktable, lit like a saint in a bad fresco, holding the prophecy book open with both hands and looking at him with an expression he could only describe as *found something* mixed with *please don't be alarmed*, which historically meant he should be extremely alarmed.

"You've made a face," he said, setting down the pies. "I know your faces now. That's the one you made when you discovered Wren waters down the glue."

"Wren waters down the glue?"

"Don't change the subject. You've found something."

She turned the book toward him. He looked. He read the trembling headline. He read the first paragraph. He put down the orange he'd been absently polishing on his sleeve, which was the closest thing to dropping it that his composure allowed.

"That's not possible," he said. "Mother went through this book twice. I went through it once, badly, before I knew what I was looking at. There's nothing under the prophecy."

"There's everything under the prophecy." Isolde's voice had the bright, dangerous steadiness she got when she was excited and pretending not to be. "It's a palimpsest fold — no, don't glaze over, I'll explain simply. Whoever bound the original testimony into this volume folded the page back against itself before sewing the quires, so the prophecy was written on top and the older text lived inside the fold. Hidden. Deliberately."

"By whom?"

"By Maren Ostreva herself, I'd guess. Or by whoever loved her enough to hide what she wrote instead of burning it." Isolde smoothed the page with one finger, gentle as touching a sleeping cat. "Quentin. Read it. Really read it. Because if I'm understanding this correctly—"

"Then say it correctly, because my understanding is currently screaming."

She read aloud:

*"They tell the story now as though sealing was always the plan. It was not. I stood at the Hollow Gate when it was young and green, and I watched the three wardens swear their oaths into it, and I heard the Architect — we did not yet call it that; then it was only the Builder, and we trusted it — ask for one thing more. Not a seal. A renewal. The covenant was written to be reforged each generation by a keeper who chose it freely and a witness who chose to stand beside them. That is why the gate recognizes bloodlines. Not ownership. Invitation."*

Quentin sat down slowly on the stool, which creaked, which was somehow comforting.

*"The sealing was our cowardice, not the covenant's design. When the thirteenth turn came, the heir who should have renewed it refused — frightened, or bribed, or both; the histories disagree and the histories are wrong anyway — and the wardens, already dead, could not argue. So the Builder's hunger turned inward. A promise made to be kept and left unkept is the sweetest meal there is. We built it a feast and called it safety."*

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a charm ticked on its shelf, doing whatever small harmless magic Quentin sold to people who would burn him if they saw the rest.

"Renewal," Quentin said at last. His mouth felt strange. "A willing heir."

"A willing keeper and a willing witness." Isolde's eyes were shining in a way that terrified him more than the Ministry ever had. "Quentin — the choice was never seal *or* sever. That line, the one that's been hanging over us like a headsman's blade — someone *added* it. Someone rewrote the ending."

"Who rewrites a prophecy?"

"Someone who wanted it to end badly." She tapped the crowding, over-pressed handwriting of the false final line. "Look at the pressure. Look at the haste. This isn't vision, it's forgery. Prophecies are seen; this was *written*. And there's exactly one creature we know of who eats broken promises and profits enormously from a prophecy that can only end in a broken one."

"The Architect." He said it carefully, the way you'd handle a wire that might still be hot.

"It offered my mother-in-law—" Isolde stopped, corrected, offered him a small apologetic look for the word, which he pretended not to find devastating, "—it offered your mother a bargain. It stole Wren. It wants the oaths. Every move it makes only makes sense if the true covenant stays buried and the false one runs out the clock." She closed the book softly, reverently, hands flat on the cover. "We've been trying to survive the prophecy. We should have been trying to correct it."

Quentin stared at the table. Under it, through the floor, through the cellar, behind iron and woven thorn, the Hollow Gate hummed its low patient note. For three years he had thought of that hum as a lock turning. Tonight, hearing Maren Ostreva's words in his head, it sounded different. Less like a lock. More like something holding its breath, waiting to be asked properly.

"My mother traded her name for three safe years," he said quietly. "Three years of *sealing working*. Of the debt deferred and the door shut and nobody asking anything of me. If the covenant was meant to be renewed — if refusing is what fed the thing in the first place — then she didn't buy me safety." His voice cracked on the arithmetic of it. "She bought me the exact shape of the mistake that created the Architect. She bought me the trap."

"No." Isolde came around the table and took his face in both hands, ink-stained thumbs against his cheekbones, and he let her, because there was nowhere else he wanted to be. "She bought you *time*, and time is what let us find this. That's not a trap, Quentin. That's a relay. She couldn't run the leg, so she ran as far as she could and passed it to you."

"I'm not a runner."

"You're a keeper. Different job, similar cardio." And he laughed — actually laughed, a rough surprised sound — because gentle humor was her way of prying panic's fingers loose, and it worked, it always worked, God help him.

Then her expression shifted, and he braced, because he knew that shift too. That was the *but* face.

"But," Isolde said, "there's more, and it's less comfortable."

"Naturally."

"Maren doesn't stop at the revelation. Keep reading with me. There's a method described — how a renewal is performed. What the witness writes. What it's written *in*." She hesitated, and he watched her decide to be brave, the same way she decided everything, completely and slightly too fast. "It's blood, Quentin. The covenant is rewritten in the witness's blood, willingly given, freely chosen. That's the price. Not a life. A *keeping*. The witness becomes part of the door."

The warmth in the room seemed to lean away from them both.

"Part of the door," Quentin repeated.

"Keeper and witness. Two sides of one threshold." She said it lightly, but her hands weren't quite steady on the book anymore. "Maren writes that it's not a prison. She writes that it's — hold on — 'the only home I have ever had that was also mine to give.'"

Neither of them spoke for a moment. Above the worktable, the lamp flickered in a draft from the stairwell, and Quentin thought absurdly of the first night she'd walked into his stall, exactly on schedule, looking terrified and curious in equal measure, and how he'd thought: *oh no, not this, anything but this,* and had been wrong in every particular since.

"We're not deciding anything tonight," he said finally.

"We are absolutely deciding things tonight, just not the big one."

"Agreed." He slid a pie toward her. "First we eat. Then we copy Maren's testimony — every word, in two hands, in case the book objects again. Then tomorrow we figure out how to show the world an original document proving its most cherished law was a century-old act of cowardice, while evading a Ministry that burns mages and a monster that eats promises."

"When you phrase it like that it sounds hard."

"When I phrase it like *that* it sounds Tuesday."

She smiled at him across the pies and the lamp and the century of hidden truth, and Quentin thought: *this is what hope feels like. It feels like being thoroughly outmaneuvered by someone who loves you.*

* * *

### Isolde

They copied until the candles burned to stubs.

Isolde wrote the fair copy — her binder's hand, precise and clean. Quentin wrote the second, and she discovered he had the careful, self-conscious script of someone taught to write wards, where a shaky letter wasn't ugly but dangerous. Between them, Maren Ostreva's voice came off the page twice over and into the world.

Near midnight, Quentin dozed sitting up, chin on chest, one hand still resting near the book as if standing guard even asleep. Isolde used the quiet to reread the testimony alone, and it was then she found the last lines of Maren's hidden page — lines she'd skimmed past in the excitement, small and slanted at the very bottom, almost as if the first witness had been embarrassed to include them:

*To whichever heir finds this: I hid the truth because they would have burned it, and I hid myself because I was a coward twice over. Do better than me. It is not a high bar.*

Isolde huffed a laugh, quiet so as not to wake him, and pressed her palm flat against the old ink.

"I'll do better than you, Maren," she whispered. "I've got excellent references."

Below them, through floorboards and stone and iron, the Hollow Gate hummed — and Isolde would have sworn, then and ever after, that the note changed. Lifted. Like a held breath released. Like a door, long shut, hearing footsteps it recognized coming down the hall.

In the corner of the room, unnoticed by either of them, the thirteenth blank page at the back of the prophecy book darkened by a single degree — the color of dawn deciding whether to bother — and waited.

And three streets away, in a house with no lights in its windows, something wearing a borrowed face stopped mid-stride, turned its head toward the shop as though scenting smoke, and began, softly, to laugh.

*Because a promise freely rewritten,* it murmured to the empty room, *is the one meal I have never learned to swallow — and now they mean to serve it.*
