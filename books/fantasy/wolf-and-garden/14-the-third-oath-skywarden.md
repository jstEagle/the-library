## 14. The Third Oath: Skywarden

The Spire of Bells had no bells.

That was the first thing Quentin noticed when they came within sight of it at dusk, and the wrongness of it settled in his stomach like a swallowed stone. He'd grown up in Vell. He remembered the Spire as a child remembered everything — larger than life, louder than sense — its crown of bronze bells ringing out the hours so enthusiastically that three streets' worth of dogs howled along in ragged chorus. His mother used to put her hands over his ears and say, *Someday you'll thank me for this,* though he never had.

Now the crown was bare. The bells were gone — not removed, he realized as they drew closer, but *drained*. They hung in their frames still, each one gray as old ash, silent as grave-markers. Whatever the Ministry's people had done up there, they hadn't taken the bells. They'd taken what the bells *were*.

"They're drinking it," Isolde said quietly beside him. She stood with her hood back despite the cold, because hooded figures drew more attention in Vell than bareheaded ones did, and because — she claimed — she refused to skulk in her own city, even as a fugitive. Her wrist, where eleven moon-turn marks now circled like a dark bracelet, was tucked against her ribs. "The bells. Magic pools where things repeat. Every hour struck for two hundred years — that's a well, if you know how to tap it."

"You've been reading my grandmother's marginalia again."

"Your grandmother," Isolde said, "annotated everything. Including a recipe for plum wine that I intend to try once we're not wanted criminals." A pause. "If we're not wanted criminals."

Quentin didn't answer that. He'd learned, these twelve turns, which silences were kind and which were cruel, and that one sat somewhere in between.

* * *

They made their approach through the tannery district, where the stink kept the Ministry's patrol-walkers scarce and the shadows thick enough to hide two fugitives with a satchel full of reforged oaths between them. Two oaths now. The Riverkeeper's oath rode against Quentin's chest in a vial of shrine-water that never stilled; the Stonefather's slept in a chip of quarry-stone wrapped in Isolde's own handkerchief, which she'd embroidered — absurdly, wonderfully — with a tiny stone face wearing an expression of profound disapproval. "He earned it," she'd said. "He head-butted a Ministry captain into a drainage ditch."

Now the third. The Skywarden. And above them, the drained bells ticked faintly against one another in the wind, making a sound like teeth chattering.

"There'll be guards," Quentin murmured.

"There are always guards."

"And something worse than guards." He turned the prophecy page in his mind, the way he'd read it a hundred times by candlelight while Isolde slept. *The last oath sings where the sky came down to listen, and it is kept by one who has forgotten his own song.* He'd thought it meant a guardian construct. A warding-automaton, maybe, or a bound spirit. Something impersonal. Something he could out-think.

He should have known better. The Hollow Gate had never once given him a problem he could solve without also bleeding.

* * *

The Spire's lower door was guarded, as predicted — four Ministry men in gray coats, bored, playing cards by lantern-light. Getting past them was almost insultingly easy; Wren had taught Quentin a charm that made lamplight lean, and he bent the shadows around them like a man parting curtains. It cost him a nosebleed and a ringing ear. He was getting stronger at this. That frightened him more than the weakness ever had, because strength invited use, and use invited attention, and attention, in Vell, invited ash.

Inside, the Spire's stair wound upward through a shell of scaffolding. The Ministry had gutted the belfry floors, replacing wood with gray iron lattice, and run cables up through the heart of the tower — thick black cables that hummed as they passed, carrying something downward toward the Ministry's vaults. Toward the gate-knowledge they'd been hoarding since before Quentin was born.

Isolde touched one cable and snatched her fingers back. "It's *hungry*," she whispered. "Whatever's up there, it isn't guarding the oath. It's being *milked*."

They climbed.

Ninety steps. A hundred and forty. The air grew colder and thinner and stranger, tasting faintly of ozone and old incense, and somewhere around the two-hundredth step Quentin began to hear it: a sound beneath the silence. Not ringing. Not quite breathing. A slow, rhythmic *hollow* sound, like wind through an empty jug, keeping time with nothing.

His hands started shaking on the railing.

He told himself it was the climb.

* * *

The belfry opened around them like the inside of a bell itself — curved iron walls, ribbed struts, and a floor of open grating through which all of Vell glittered, ten thousand lamps laid out like spilled jewelry. The missing bells hung overhead in their great wooden yokes, gray and dead. And beneath them, in the exact center of the floor, sat a chair.

An ordinary chair. Kitchen-made, oak, one arm mended with wire.

A man sat in it.

He wore no restraints. There were no guards up here, no Ministry technicians, no apparatus — just the cables running down from the empty bell-yokes and terminating, Quentin saw with a sickness rising in his throat, in thin silver threads that entered the man's wrists, his throat, his temples. The threads pulsed faintly, in that same hollow rhythm. Draining him upward into the bells, or the bells down into him. It was impossible to tell which direction the river ran.

The man was old. Thin as winter branches, white-haired, in robes that had once been deep blue and were now faded to the color of a sky after rain. His eyes were open. They were fixed on the middle distance, and they were empty — not blind, not asleep, but *vacated*, like a house with the furniture gone and the windows left open to weather.

"No," Quentin said. Out loud. To no one. To the universe in general, which he felt deserved it.

"What is it?" Isolde's hand found his sleeve. "Quentin—"

"That's Master Alderyn."

The name came out of him cracked down the middle. Because it wasn't possible, and it was. Because Alderyn Voss had been burned to death seven years ago — Quentin had been seventeen, hiding in the crowd with his mother's hand crushing his shoulder, watching the pyre on Unnatural Affairs' scaffold, forbidden to cry, forbidden even to look like he cared. Everyone knew Alderyn Voss was dead. Alderyn Voss was the reason the burnings had gotten worse; he'd been the most famous mage in Vell, the teacher who'd dared to take students openly, who'd written letters to the Ministry arguing that magic was a civic good. They'd made an example of him. Quentin had carried the smoke-smell home in his hair for days.

Except they hadn't burned him. Or they'd burned something else. Or —

"Hollowed," Quentin whispered. "This is what hollowing means. They don't kill the strong ones. They *empty* them and use the shell as a vessel." His voice sounded far away to himself. "I thought it was a rumor. Wren said it was a rumor."

"The prophecy said the guardian has forgotten his own song." Isolde's face was pale in the lampless light. "Quentin. Does he know you? Can he — is there anything left?"

Quentin stepped forward, and the hollow rhythm stuttered.

The old man's head turned. Slowly, joint by joint, like a door on rusted hinges. The empty eyes swung across Quentin and stopped.

For a moment — nothing. The cables hummed. Vell glittered below. Somewhere far off, a night-bird complained about the state of the world.

Then Alderyn Voss's mouth moved, and what came out was a voice with nothing behind it, flat as paper: *"Small charms. Luck-knots. Fever-thread. You smell of the shop on Candlemaker's Row."*

Quentin's whole body went cold. "Master."

*"Students forget the shop. Students remember the lessons."* The head tilted. Something flickered behind those vacant eyes — not surfacing, just pressing upward from underneath, like a drowned man's hand against ice. *"Lesson four. A ward is only as honest as its keeper."*

"I remember." Quentin's voice broke on it. He knelt — actually knelt, on the iron grating, two hundred feet above the city where saying that word aloud could get him burned. "Master, I remember all of them. Lesson nine. *Never bargain with something that eats.* You told us that the week before they took you. You *knew*, didn't you? You knew what they were becoming."

Something happened to the old man's face then. It might have been a smile, in the way that a crack in a dam might be called a trickle. *"Eastwick,"* he said, and the name had weight, had warmth, had a ghost of the man inside it, and then the cables flared silver and the warmth was gone, scraped away, and the flat voice returned: *"The oath must not pass. The covenant must not be renewed. The Architect has promised the Ministry order. Order is safety. Safety is—"*

"He's warded," Quentin breathed. He could see it now, with keeper's eyes: threads of compulsion woven through the silver cables, threaded down into whatever remained of Alderyn's will. Not a guard. A *puppet*. The Ministry hadn't posted a weapon at the top of the Spire. They'd posted a message: *this is what happens to mages we catch. Even your heroes serve us, in the end.*

"Can you break it?" Isolde crouched beside him. Her hand hovered near the vial at his chest, near the Stonefather's chip in her pocket. "The Riverkeeper's oath nearly took your lungs. The Stonefather's took three days off your life, by my count, and don't argue, I keep count. What does this one take?"

Quentin looked at his teacher — at the wire-mended chair, the silver threads, the eyes that had once crinkled when he laughed at his own terrible puns about wards and wizards.

"To take the oath," he said slowly, "the old holder has to let go of it. That's how the other two worked. The shrine-keeper gave hers willingly. The Stonefather's giant had held so long it had become the mountain, and the mountain let go." He swallowed. "But Alderyn can't give anything willingly. There's no *him* left to want things. So I have to take it. And taking an oath from someone who hasn't released it means..."

He couldn't finish the sentence. Isolde finished it for him, very quietly.

"It means unmaking what remains of him."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour. Later, Quentin would remember almost none of what either of them said — only that Isolde argued with her whole body, pacing the grating, gesturing at the city below, at the moon, at him; and that he argued sitting still, because some griefs make you heavy.

"We find another way," she said. "There's always another way. We come back with Wren's contacts, with the first witness's testimony, with *something*—"

"With what?" Quentin asked gently. "Isolde. Look at him. There's no coming back *from* this for him. There's no rescue. There's no cell to break him out of. What's in that chair is a lamp with the flame taken out of it, and the Ministry keeps it lit because a hollowed Alderyn Voss terrifies every mage in Vell more than a dead one would." His voice steadied, which surprised him. "The kindest thing anyone will ever do for my teacher is tonight. By me. And I have to be the one, because oaths pass along keeper-lines, and I'm the keeper, and — and because lesson eleven was *finish what you start*, and I was always his worst student about that."

Isolde knelt in front of him and took his face in both hands. Her palms were ink-stained and cold and steady.

"When it's done," she said, "you're going to fall apart, and I'm going to be there for it, and you're not going to apologize for it. Those are my terms."

"That's extortion."

"Yes," said Isolde Vane, bookbinder, witness, fugitive, the woman whose name was written in a century-old prophecy beside a sketch of the man currently crying on a belfry floor. "Welcome to the covenant."

* * *

He did it with a bell-hammer.

There was one left, hanging on a peg by the yoke — a small ceremonial mallet, padded with worn leather, the kind used to strike a bell softly on solemn occasions. It felt right in his hand. It felt like the only honest tool in the building.

He stood before his teacher and pressed his palm to the old man's forehead, and spoke the opening words of the Rite of Release — words he'd learned at fourteen, doodling them in a notebook during lessons, never dreaming. The silver cables flared. The compulsion-wards bit at him like dogs on chains, and he felt them clearly for the first time: Ministry work, competent and joyless, knots tied by people who had learned magic only as a way to cage it.

"Lesson six, Master," Quentin whispered. "*Every knot can be untied by someone patient enough.*"

He untied them.

It took everything. It took the reserves he'd rebuilt over twelve turns and the strength the Stonefather's chip had lent him and something extra besides, something he suspected he'd miss later, some bright coin of himself spent without receipt. The wards fought. Then they frayed. Then, with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go, they came apart entirely — and the cables went dark, and the silver threads slipped from Alderyn's wrists and temples and throat and lay coiled on the grating like shed skins.

And the hollow rhythm stopped.

In the sudden true silence, Alderyn Voss breathed in — really breathed, chest rising like a man surfacing — and his eyes found Quentin's, and for three seconds, maybe four, they were *full*. Full of the man. Full of recognition, and sorrow, and something that looked unbearably like pride.

"*There you are,*" Alderyn said, in his own voice, warm and dry and fond. "*Worst student I ever had. Best keeper Vell ever got. Don't tell either of them I said so.*"

"Master—"

"*The Skywarden's oath lives in the fourth bell. The little cracked one, second from the left. It always liked you — you used to sneak up here and talk to it, don't think I didn't know.*" His voice was speeding up, spending itself. "*Take it, boy. Take it and finish it. And Eastwick — the girl. The witness. Keep her close. Prophecies are doors, and she is* — "

The light in his eyes guttered.

"*— the best argument for renewal I ever heard.*"

And then he smiled — the real smile, the crinkle-eyed one — and went out. Not died. Went out, the way a lamp goes out when its oil is finally, mercifully finished. What remained was a slight old man at rest in a mended chair, and Quentin made sure of it, checked twice with every art he had, that what remained was *at rest*, truly and completely, before he picked up the hammer.

The fourth bell, the little cracked one, rang once under his hand.

It didn't make a sound. But the Skywarden's oath rose out of it like heat-shimmer, like a hawk leaving a fist, and came to him — and this time, instead of pain, there was only a vast, clean openness, as if a window in his chest had been opened onto a sky he'd forgotten was there. Three oaths. All three, gathered. The vials and the chip warmed together against his chest like eggs in a nest.

He didn't fall apart, exactly. Isolde held to her terms anyway. She held him on the belfry floor while Vell glittered below, ignorant and sleeping and burning its mages, and she didn't ask him to stop shaking until he stopped on his own.

* * *

They buried him at dawn — or the closest thing to burial the Spire allowed. Quentin tipped the little cracked bell from its yoke and laid it over Alderyn's folded hands, and Isolde stitched his name into the hem of his robe with thread from her kit, neat as a spine-label: *ALDERYN VOSS, TEACHER.* Below it, smaller, she added: *HE FINISHED WHAT HE STARTED.*

They were halfway down the stair, gray morning light rising up to meet them, when Quentin felt it — all three oaths flaring hot against his chest at once, singing a chord that had no business existing outside the Gate itself.

And somewhere far below, at the bottom of the tower, a door that should have been locked swung open on the morning street, and a voice Quentin knew — silk over hunger, patient as rot — called up the stairwell, sweet and delighted:

"Oh, *wonderful*. You've saved me the trouble of collecting them one at a time."
