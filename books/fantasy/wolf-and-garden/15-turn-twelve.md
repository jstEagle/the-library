## 15. Turn Twelve

The marks woke her before dawn.

Isolde had learned to sleep through a great many indignities since the third moon-turn — Quentin's snoring, which he denied having; the thorn-light that crept under doors and pooled like spilled honey; the way Wren hummed sea shanties in his sleep, badly, in a key that did not exist anywhere else in creation. But she could not sleep through this. The three oaths on her wrist — river, stone, and sky — lit up beneath her skin like lanterns behind parchment, and they *burned*.

She sat up in the dark of the safehouse above the old tannery and pressed her palm flat over them, breathing hard.

"Quentin," she whispered. "Quentin, wake up."

He was asleep in the chair by the window because Quentin Eastwick had not slept properly in a bed since his mother died, a fact Isolde had catalogued among the small griefs of him, filed away for later, for when there was time. He came awake the way he always did — all at once, no grogginess, hand already finding the charm-bag at his hip — and then his eyes found her wrist and went very still.

"Turn twelve," he said.

"Turn twelve."

"That's tomorrow night. That's — " He stood, crossed the room in three strides, and knelt beside her cot to look. In the moonlight coming through the slats, the marks were beautiful, which felt like an obscenity. Three spirals braided into one line: the Riverkeeper's water-silver, the Stonefather's deep grey, the Skywarden's pale gold. They pulsed together now instead of separately, one heartbeat, and Isolde understood with terrible clarity what that meant.

"They're finished," she said. "They're done waiting."

"Yes." Quentin's voice was very quiet. He took her hand — the unmarked one — and held it the way he held fragile things he'd spent his life repairing, as if it might break and as if it were worth breaking over. "We have until moonrise tomorrow. Then we go to the Gate, and we renew the covenant, and it's over."

"It's over," Isolde repeated. She tried to make it sound like relief. She mostly succeeded. There was a crack in the middle of the sentence, though, and she knew he heard it, because his thumb traced a slow circle on the back of her hand, once, twice, like he was binding something invisible.

"You should sleep," he said.

"I was asleep. My wrist set itself on fire."

"Ah." A ghost of his dry smile. "In my professional experience, wrists do that when you've been carrying the hopes of two realms around without a proper splint."

"I'll requisition one from the Ministry."

"Their splints are made of ash and bad intentions."

She laughed — actually laughed, at four in the morning, with fire in her arm and one turn left — and the sound surprised both of them. For a moment they just sat there in the dark, her hand in his, and the marks pulsed their patient, dreadful pulse, and outside the tannery the city of Vell slept on, unaware that its outlawed magic was counting down the hours to come home.

Wren appeared in the doorway holding a candle and wearing the expression of a man who has been eavesdropping out of love rather than habit.

"If we're doing speeches," he said, "I've got one prepared. It's short. It's mostly about biscuits."

"Wren," Isolde said, "go back to bed."

"Biscuits," he said with dignity, "are load-bearing," and he withdrew.

* * *

They spent the last day the way soldiers spend the night before — badly, and together.

Quentin wanted to go over the ritual again. Isolde wanted to go over it again too, which surprised her; she had thought herself past the point of needing rehearsal, but her hands kept wanting tasks, so she gave them tasks. They laid out the three oath-vessels on the tannery table: the Riverkeeper's bell, green-crusted and still faintly dripping after all these years, as if it had never entirely accepted being taken out of its shrine. The Stonefather's heartstone, heavy as guilt, warm as a sleeping animal. And the Skywarden's feather, grey and long and impossibly light, which kept drifting off the table edge whenever no one watched it, as if it remembered flying.

"Stop that," Quentin told it, catching it for the fourth time.

"It's excited," said Wren, who was sharpening a knife nobody had asked him to sharpen.

"It's a feather."

"All the more reason. If I were a feather I'd be insufferable about it."

Isolde watched them bicker and felt the ache underneath it, the one she couldn't name yet. It was the ache of things being almost finished. For eleven turns she had been running — from the Ministry, from the Architect, from the prophecy that had written her name before her grandmother was born — and running leaves no time for looking at what you're running *with*. Now the road ended tomorrow at a door, and she found herself memorizing Quentin Eastwick in lamplight: the ink stain on his left knuckle that never washed out, the way he tucked the feather into a linen wrap with the tenderness most men reserved for infants, the little frown he wore when arithmetic refused to cooperate.

"What?" he said, catching her staring.

"Nothing. Your face is crooked today."

"My face has always been crooked."

"I know. I'm practicing noticing."

He looked at her a moment longer than the joke required, and whatever he saw made him glance away first, which was rare, and say, "Moonrise is at half past nine," which was deflection, and let him deflect, because her wrist was burning again and some part of her already knew the day would not go the way the plan said.

It went wrong at dusk.

* * *

Later, Isolde would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened, and fail, and hate herself for failing, and be forgiven for it by everyone except herself. This is how she remembered it:

The light went strange first. Not dark — Thorn-dark, briar-dark, the luminous underwater gold that leaked through the Hollow Gate on bad nights — but standing in the middle of the tannery loft, in the middle of Vell, where no gate had ever been. The shadows grew roots. The floorboards sweated sap.

And the Architect stepped out of the corner where the shadows were deepest, wearing the face of Quentin's mother.

Isolde had seen that face only once before, in the Architect's previous visitation, and she had hated it then too — not the woman, who by every account had been fierce and tired and kind in the specific way of people who make terrible bargains for their children, but the *wearing* of her, the theft of it. The Architect smiled with Mirabel Eastwick's mouth and said:

"You've gathered them beautifully. All three. I confess I had doubts about the Skywarden, but grief is such a reliable crowbar."

Quentin moved in front of Isolde before she even registered rising. "You're not welcome here."

"I'm rarely welcome anywhere. It's why I stopped knocking." The Architect's gaze slid to the table, to the three vessels lying in their wrappings, and something in the room *leaned* toward them — the shadows, the air, the burning in Isolde's wrist, everything leaning like grass toward wind. "Thirteen turns I have waited. Thirteen turns since your mother promised me a debt and thought distance would dilute it. Promises don't dilute, keeper's son. They ferment."

"You can't take them," Isolde said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "They're bound to a witness now. Bound to me. You'd have to break me first, and the covenant doesn't recognize broken witnesses."

"No," the Architect agreed pleasantly. "It recognizes *dead* ones. Or dying ones. Or—" and here the borrowed face turned, slowly, with terrible theatrical patience, to Quentin, "—keepers who die keeping."

The silence afterward lasted one full second and cost Isolde a year of her life.

"What," she said, "did you say."

"Oh." The Architect pressed Mirabel's fingers to borrowed lips, the picture of apology. "Hasn't he told you? Hasn't dear Wren mentioned? No one ever reads to the end anymore." And the Architect raised one hand, and the air above the tannery table filled with light, and in that light hung pages — pages Isolde knew better than her own hands, pages she had restored fiber by fiber, water-stain by water-stain, in a bookbinder's shop that felt like several lifetimes ago.

The prophecy book opened itself to the final page. To the final line. The line she had read a hundred times, or thought she had — *and at the thirteenth turn the choice shall be made, seal or sever, and the covenant shall live or the realms shall part* — except now, in the Architect's cruel illumination, the ink shifted, rearranged, showed her what restoration had hidden and what the Architect had always known:

*and at the thirteenth turn the choice shall be paid,*

*in the keeper's blood, at the keeper's door.*

The words burned themselves into her memory in a single glance. She would never unread them. She would carry them the way she carried the sister's face she no longer had — permanently, in the place where important things are kept against their will.

"No," Isolde said.

"It's quite elegant," the Architect continued, warming, the way predators warm, the way lecturers warm, the way all creatures do when feeding approaches. "Your mother bought you three years with her name, Quentin. I have enjoyed watching you spend them. But debts mature. And tomorrow at moonrise, the Hollow Gate opens, and it wants a keeper's blood to open *properly* — and there you'll be, standing in front of it, being noble. You were going to offer anyway. Don't bother denying it. I've eaten ten thousand promises and yours is the most transparent kind."

"Get out," Quentin said. His voice didn't shake. Isolde would think about that later, how it didn't shake, and understand it wasn't courage. It was a man who had known since he was seventeen how this ended, walking toward it with his coat buttoned.

The Architect gathered the light — and the oaths with it. The bell rose from the table. The heartstone rose. The feather tore free of its wrapping, and Wren lunged for it and caught only air, and then all three vessels hung in the golden dark like ornaments on an invisible tree, spinning slowly, singing a chord that made Isolde's teeth ache and her marks blaze white-hot.

"These belong to the witness now," she said, and her voice did shake, and she let it. "Take them and they're worthless to you."

"Mm." The Architect considered her with Mirabel's tired, fond eyes, which was the cruelest thing yet. "You're right. Which is why I'm also taking the boy who carries the other half of the binding. Debts travel, Miss Vane. You of all people should know — you paid one in memories." A pause. "How is your sister these days? Do tell her I said hello. Or don't. You couldn't pick her out of a crowd."

Isolde lunged. Wren caught her around the arms, and it was lucky he did, because the Architect's shadow was full of thorns and she would have gone into it like a hand into a grinder.

Then the light contracted. The vessels vanished. The shadows folded up like a letter being closed, and the last thing the Architect left behind — hanging in the air where the pages had been, in ink that was already drying into permanence — was a single line, freshly written, in handwriting Isolde recognized as her own:

*the witness arrives at the thirteenth turn.*

*the keeper does not leave it.*

And then the tannery loft was just a tannery loft, smelling of old hides and lamp oil, with a knife dropped on the floor and three people breathing too fast and nothing at all missing except everything.

* * *

Wren spoke first, because Wren always spoke first, because silence was a room he couldn't stand to be locked in.

"Right," he said, in a voice with no humor left in it at all, which frightened Isolde more than the Architect had. "Right. So. Options."

"There are no options." Quentin was standing very straight, looking at the place where the prophecy had been, and his face had gone into the calm Isolde had learned to dread — the shopkeeper's calm, the face he wore for customers and raids and dying teachers, the face that meant the feeling underneath had been locked in the cellar. "There was never an option. I've known since the Spire. Since before the Spire. My mother traded her name for three years, and the price of the loan was always going to be collected at the same door."

"You let me gather the oaths," Isolde said. "You let me bleed on shrines. You let me forget my own—" She stopped, because her voice had climbed somewhere she didn't want it to go. She brought it back down. "You knew."

"I hoped." He finally turned to look at her, and his calm cracked down the middle, and what was behind it was so much worse than fear. It was tenderness. It was a man looking at the one thing he'd gotten right in a life of careful wrongness. "I hoped the first witness was right. I hoped renewal meant something gentler. But hope isn't a plan, Isolde, and the prophecy — the real one, the whole one —"

"The prophecy is a *draft*." She was across the room now, gripping his shirtfront, and neither of them had decided she should move. "I'm a bookbinder, Quentin. I know drafts. I know what ink does and what it doesn't, and I know a text is not a verdict. The Architect showed us that page because it wants you to walk in there pre-defeated. It eats broken promises — well, it doesn't get to eat this one, because I haven't *made* it yet. Nothing is broken until someone breaks it."

"And if the only way to keep it whole is your life instead of mine?"

"Then we'll negotiate."

"With *that thing*?"

"I've negotiated with publishers."

Despite everything — despite the burning in her wrist and the empty table and the line drying into permanence somewhere behind her eyes — a laugh escaped him, short and wrecked and real. "You're impossible."

"I'm thorough." Her grip tightened. "Tomorrow at moonrise, we go to the Gate. Together. Both of us walk through that door, and we rewrite the ending before it writes us. That's the plan. That's the only plan. And if you try to go alone, I swear on every oath in my arm, I will follow you into Thorn itself, and I am *far* more annoying than the Architect."

Behind them, quietly, Wren picked up his knife and began, once more, to sharpen it.

"Half past nine," he said. "Moonrise. I'll bring the biscuits."

Outside, the twelfth moon was already climbing, one night from full, and Isolde's marks burned like a fuse lit at both ends.
