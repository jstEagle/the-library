## 16. The Empty Shop

Quentin had always believed that a man's last night should be tidy. His mother had died in a tangle of unwashed cups and half-mended wards, and he had spent three years quietly forgiving her for it. He intended to do better.

So on the morning after the twelfth turn — after the Architect had torn the oaths from Isolde's hands like a child stripping petals from a flower, after Wren had vanished into the dark between realms, after the final line of the prophecy had been read aloud in a voice like his mother's wearing someone else's grief — Quentin Eastwick swept his floor.

He swept it properly, corners included, because the broom was charmed to reach corners anyway and it seemed rude not to let it. Then he polished the counter until it shone like river ice, restacked the luck-knots in their little pyramid (six at the base, three above, one crowning — a shape that meant *may your road be short and kind*), and put the kettle on.

The shop smelled of beeswax and dried feverfew and the faint green hum of the Hollow Gate beneath the floorboards, still humming even now, even with its oaths stolen, like a heart that hadn't yet heard the bad news.

"Right," Quentin said to the empty room. "Letters."

---

The first letter was the hardest, so he wrote it first, while his hand was still steady enough to be honest.

*Dear Wren,* he wrote, then crossed it out, because Wren was gone and might never read anything again, and starting a letter to a missing person felt like shouting into a well and calling it conversation. He tried again.

*To whoever inherits this shop:*

*There is a door under the floor. You will have noticed the humming. Do not pry up the boards out of curiosity; I did that at fourteen and my mother made me re-lay every single one by hand as penance, which I now recognize was less punishment than lesson.*

*The door is called the Hollow Gate. It is not yours to open. It was never mine either — that was rather the point of keeping it. If you are reading this, the covenant has failed or been remade, and either way the wisest thing you can do is make tea, sit down, and wait for instructions from a woman named Isolde Vane, who has better judgment than I ever did and considerably worse handwriting.*

He paused. His eyes had gone hot somewhere around *remade*. He blew his nose with dignity, like a man with a head cold and nothing more, and continued.

*I leave you the stock, the ledger, and the kettle, which sticks but forgives. The luck-knots are harmless. The blue bottles on the second shelf are also harmless. The jar behind them, labeled FLOUR, is not flour. Do not open the jar. Sell the jar to no one. When you have finished grieving me — and you will grieve briefly, I hope, I was a modest sort of man — throw the jar into the river and do not watch where it lands.*

He signed it, sealed it, and set it in the center of the counter where no one could miss it, weighted down with the brass bell that had announced customers for forty years.

Then he took out fresh paper and began the letter he had been avoiding since dawn.

*Isolde.*

Just her name, sitting alone at the top of the page like a lit candle. He looked at it for a long time.

Here was the trouble: everything true sounded like begging, and everything practical sounded like a lie. *Don't trade yourself for me* was both. He had watched her face when the Architect spoke the final line — *the keeper dies so the door may live* — and he had seen her do the arithmetic she thought she was hiding. One life weighed against a gate, a city, a realm of exiled magic, and a prophecy already two-thirds fulfilled. She was a bookbinder. She understood structure. She could see exactly where the story wanted to go, and she hated it, and she was going to try to rewrite it with herself as the cost, because that was what Isolde did: she repaired broken things by taking the damage into her own hands.

He knew this the way he knew the kettle stuck. Not a theory. A fact of the household.

*By the time you find this,* he wrote, *I will have taken certain precautions. Please don't be angry. Or do be angry, but be angry at me alive about something else instead.*

*You cannot trade yourself for me. This isn't stubbornness, though I grant it looks like stubbornness wearing a nice coat. It's arithmetic, which you'll appreciate. The Architect eats broken promises. What is my death, if you buy it with a vow? A promise freely made and freely broken — yours, broken against itself. We would be handing it a banquet and calling it rescue. It would take you, take the oaths' worth of you, take the gate, and still keep the debt, because debts are the one thing it never spends.*

*I know you've found the first witness's testimony. I know what it says about willing heirs and renewed covenants. But the testimony doesn't name who pays, Isolde. Books like that always leave the price blank and let love fill it in. I'm filling it in myself, in advance, so you don't have to. Consider it bookbinding. I'm simply sewing the pages shut before you can write in the margin.*

He stopped. Read that back. Winced.

"That's cruel," he told the page. "That's exactly the kind of clever-cruel she'll see straight through." He almost crossed it out. He left it. Better she saw the cruelty and recognized it for what it was — fear, dressed in wit — than that she missed the argument underneath.

*The wards come down tonight,* he wrote. *All of them. The ones on the door, the windows, the cellar, and the ones woven into your coat pocket that you think are embroidery. Without them there is nothing here to bargain with. No gate under guard, no keeper under contract, just an empty shop with a humming floor and a very good recipe for fever tea. Let the Architect come find an absence. Absence is the one thing it cannot digest.*

*Go to Thorn. The fox-spirit owes us — it said so twice, and spirits hate owing. Wait out the turn among the briars and come back when the moon is new and I am, regrettably, a story you tell.*

*I loved you in a shop full of small charms, which means I loved you in the language I actually speak. I never learned the grand one. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for all of it, except none of it, which I believe is how love works.*

*Yours in ink and thorn,*
*Q.*

He sealed it and set it beside the first.

Then, because his hands needed doing and his heart needed distracting, he went downstairs and began dismantling the wards.

* * *

It took him until well past dusk, and it hurt more than he'd expected.

Wards don't come undone like knots; they come undone like scabs. Each one had been laid down in a moment of fear and each one remembered it. The threshold ward — three years old, laid the week his mother died — peeled back through his palms with a cold ache and a whisper of her voice saying *careful, careful, careful*, which had been her entire philosophy of parenthood. The window wards let go one pane at a time, and with each release the shop grew slightly more ordinary, slightly more visible, slightly less his. By the time he reached the cellar, the Hollow Gate's hum had risen to meet him, anxious as a dog hearing keys.

"No," Quentin told it gently, kneeling on the cold stone. "Not you. Never you. You stay shut. You're not a ward, you're a promise, and I don't break those."

The door of woven thorn and iron pulsed once, slow and sullen, like something deciding to trust him.

"I know," he said. "I know what tomorrow wants. I've read the same book you have." He pressed his palm flat against the iron band across its middle. It was warm. It had always been warm. "If there's any mercy stitched into you anywhere, spend it on her. Not me. I'm the expendable paragraph. She's the whole chapter."

The gate hummed a note he'd never heard before — low, mournful, almost human — and he chose to believe it was agreement, because a man preparing to die is permitted a few generous interpretations.

Upstairs again, past midnight, he wrote the third letter.

*Mother.*

One word. He sat looking at it for the length of an entire pot of tea.

What was there to say to a woman who had traded her own name to a monster to buy him three safe years, whose borrowed face the Architect wore now like a stolen coat? Three years. She'd bought three, and he'd stretched them into five through parsimony and paranoia, and tomorrow the account came due regardless, because the Architect's ledgers, unlike his mother's, were perfectly kept.

*I used to be angry at you,* he wrote finally. *For the secret. For the debt. For dying before you could explain any of it properly. I've decided to stop, partly because anger is heavy luggage for a short trip, and mostly because I understand now. You didn't buy three years because three was enough. You bought them because they were what you could afford, and you spent every coin of yourself getting even that. That's not abandonment, Mum. That's the same terrible arithmetic I'm doing tonight, and I'd like to formally apologize to you for ever thinking I'd have done it differently.*

*Watch over her, if watching is something the dead still do. And if the thing wearing your face comes near her, I hope there's some part of you left, some splinter of the real name it ate, that remembers how to bite.*

He sealed it. Set it with the others. Three letters, standing in a row like small headstones.

And then — because the shop was quiet, and the moon was a thin nail-paring in the window, and sleep was a country he had no papers for — he wrote a fourth letter that he did not seal and did not address, because it was not really a letter at all. It was just everything, poured out:

*Things I never told you, in no particular order:*

*I practiced asking you to stay for supper eleven times before the first time I managed it aloud.*

*When you repaired the prophecy book, you held the spine the way other people hold sleeping children, and I fell in love with your hands before I finished falling in love with your face.*

*The luck-knots don't work. They never worked. Mother made them work, and after her they were just pretty rope, and I sold them anyway because people need to buy a little hope with their bread, and I couldn't bear to be the one who said otherwise. You should know that about me. I deal in gentle frauds.*

*Except one. The knot in your coat pocket, under the embroidery I pretended was embroidery. That one works. I paid for it with a week of headaches and half the sight in my left eye at dusk, and it does only one thing: it keeps you findable. Wherever you are, whatever realm, some thread of you tugs toward this shop. If you go to Thorn, it will tug across the dark, thin as spider silk and strong as sinew. Don't cut it. Not out of loyalty to me. Cut nothing that holds you to the living world, whatever happens tomorrow. Promise—*

His pen stopped.

He looked at what he'd written and laughed, one dry note, alone in the lamplight. *Promise me.* Asking Isolde Vane for promises, the night before facing a creature that dined on nothing else. He might as well have salted her and set her on a plate.

He left the sentence unfinished. Some arguments were better lost.

* * *

He must have dozed at the counter, because he woke to grey light and the sound of the shop bell, which he had forgotten to muffle, because of course he had; sentiment got him every time.

Isolde stood in the doorway with rain in her hair and the fourth letter open in her hands.

She had read it. Of course she had read it — the others too, he realized, seeing their seals broken and folded neatly atop the counter, arranged in reading order like a scholar's evidence. She had picked the lock of his shop — his lock, which he had left warded until yesterday and naked today, and oh, that was the wound in all this, wasn't it, that he had disarmed himself precisely so she couldn't be locked out of anything ever again, and she had walked straight through the gap like water finding its level.

"You unfinished sentence," she said. Her voice was rough. She'd been crying, or walking in weather built for it, or both. "You absolute, deliberate, self-immolating man. You left it unfinished so I couldn't refuse it."

"I left it unfinished because asking you for promises the night before a promise-eater arrives struck me as poor strategy."

"And the wards?" She held up her coat, turned the pocket out. The thread that wasn't embroidery glimmered faintly, stubbornly, still there. "You dismantled everything but the one charm that would lead me straight back to your corpse. Explain that logic to me slowly, Quentin, because I've read your letters twice and the arithmetic doesn't balance."

"The wards kept things out," he said. "That one keeps you found. Different animal entirely."

"It keeps me *tethered*." She came forward into the lamplight, and he saw that under the rain and the fury her eyes were red-rimmed and magnificent. "It keeps me dragging a hook through my chest that leads back to this floor, to this exact spot where you plan to stand tomorrow and die tidily with the floors swept. Did you sweep?"

"Corners included."

"Oh, that's the part that breaks my heart, that you swept." She laughed, wet and furious, and dropped the letters on the counter one by one, tapping each like a judge striking down charges. "Letter one: you've left the shop to a stranger with instructions. Refused — the shop is ours or it's nobody's. Letter two: go to Thorn, wait out the turn, tell stories about you. Refused — I don't tell stories about people I can still save. Letter three—" she hesitated on it, gentled, "letter three was beautiful and none of my business, and I'm leaving it exactly as you wrote it, because some things shouldn't be edited. But letter four. Letter four you addressed to no one, which means it was addressed to me, which means it's fair game."

"Isolde—"

"You're right about one thing." She planted both hands on the counter, leaning across the luck-knot pyramid, scattering it without ceremony — six, three, one, rolling everywhere, the road suddenly neither short nor kind. "The Architect eats broken promises. Freely made and freely broken, that's the meal. So here's what I'm not going to do. I'm not going to promise you I won't trade myself. I'm not going to promise I'll run to Thorn. I'm not going to make you a single vow tonight, Quentin Eastwick, because every vow I make you tonight is food on its table tomorrow, and I have *read the testimony*, and I know what the first witness knew — that the covenant dies of kept bargains and lives on given gifts. There's a difference. A bargain is a cage. A gift walks in the front door with its hands open."

"So your grand plan is to make no plans."

"My grand plan," she said, "is that tomorrow, when that thing wearing your mother's face stands at our gate and asks for a death, it will find two people holding nothing it can eat. No vows between us. Only—" she reached across the counter and took his ink-stained hand, turning it over, studying the calluses like a binder studying a spine, "—this. Whatever this is. Unguarded. Unwritten. Yours, if you want it, and I'm not promising to keep it safe, and I'm not promising to give it back, and that is the only honest offer anyone has made in this whole cursed prophecy."

The gate below them hummed. Not sullen now. Something closer to attention.

Quentin looked at her hand around his, at the scattered luck-knots, at his four letters lying broken-open like eggs, and felt the strange loosening in his chest that he supposed other men called surrender and he could only call relief.

"The jar behind the flour," he said hoarsely. "In letter one. You should know it's not flour."

"I assumed as much. We'll deal with the jar after." She smiled, tired and terrible and bright. "Now sit down. I'm making tea, and then you're going to help me finish restoring the last pages of the testimony, because if we're going to defy a prophecy at midnight, we're going to do it with the text properly collated. I refuse to be martyred over a misprint."

Outside, the nail-paring moon was setting, and somewhere beyond the edge of the city, something ancient and hungry turned its face toward a small shop with an unlocked door and no wards left at all — and found, to its surprise, nothing there it knew how to swallow.
