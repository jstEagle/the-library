## 17. The Thirteenth Turn

**Quentin**

The moon did not rise so much as arrive, the way a creditor arrives — announced by no one, expected by everyone, and impossible to keep waiting at the door.

Quentin stood in the cellar beneath his shop and watched it come through the arrow-slit window he'd never gotten around to bricking up. Three years of sweeping that floor. Three years of oiling the hinges of a door that must never open, of humming to the thorn-and-iron lattice so it would stay asleep, of telling himself each night: *one more night, and then another one.* He had thought, in his more honest moments, that he would die of it eventually. Not dramatically. Just — quietly, the way a candle goes out when someone forgets it.

He had not planned on Isolde Vane reading every letter he wrote her and refusing, point blank, to let him be forgotten.

"You're sulking," she said from the top of the stairs.

"I'm *preparing*."

"Preparing to sulk." She came down with two cups of chicory and the particular set of her shoulders she got when she had decided an argument was already won. She wore her bookbinder's apron over her coat, because she said if she was going to face down an immortal horror she wanted to be dressed for work. "I brought you something to drink and something to argue about. Choose your poison."

"The Architect is coming tonight," Quentin said. "The moon's full. The debt comes due. My mother sold my name's safety for three years and the three years are spent, and the thing wearing her face is going to walk through that door and collect. The only variable left is whether it takes me standing up or kneeling down." He took the cup anyway. It was good chicory. She'd put honey in it, which felt like cheating in an argument. "There's nothing to argue about."

"Mm," said Isolde, which was what she said when there was *everything* to argue about and she intended to enjoy all of it. "You wrote me eleven letters. I counted."

"Twelve."

"The twelfth was just instructions for feeding the cat."

"That's still a letter."

She sat down on Wren's old stool — Wren, who was somewhere in Thorn's borderlands with a fragment of Isolde's memory tucked safe in her coat pocket, and gods willing would stay there until this was done — and looked at him with the expression she used on water-damaged books. Patient. Appraising. Certain that underneath the ruin there was something worth restoring.

"Quentin Eastwick," she said. "You have spent three years being a doorstop. Tonight you want to be a doormat. I decline to allow either."

"That's not how debts work—"

"No, but it's how *people* work, and people are the whole point. I read the first witness's testimony. I've been reading it for a week. You know what she says? She doesn't say the keeper dies. She says the keeper *gives*, and she says — Quentin, listen — she says a gift given under compulsion feeds the Architect, but a promise freely rewritten starves it. That's not poetry. That's a recipe."

"It's a recipe that requires someone standing at the door who isn't me," he said, "because everything in that testimony assumes the keeper pays."

"Everything in the prophecy assumed I'd watch," Isolde said. "Prophecies are written by pessimists."

The gate hummed.

It was a sound Quentin knew better than his own heartbeat — the low, patient thrum of woven thorn and old iron, like a hive dreaming. But tonight it changed key. Tonight it climbed. The honey in his chicory trembled in rings. Along the lattice, the iron filaments glowed faintly gold, and the thorns between them stirred like a field of grass deciding, all at once, to stand up.

Isolde set down her cup and stood beside him, and her hand found his without either of them looking, the way it always did now, the way hands learn a doorframe they intend to lean on forever.

"Whatever happens," Quentin began.

"Don't you dare say 'let go of my hand.'"

"I was going to say 'thank you for the chicory.'"

"Liar." But she squeezed his fingers, and above them, at the top of the cellar stairs, the shop bell rang.

Nobody had come through that door in three days. Quentin had closed the shop, turned the sign, salted the threshold. The bell rang again — a bright, cheerful little sound, utterly wrong for the hour, utterly wrong for the cold that came pouring down the stairs behind it like water finding a crack.

And then she descended.

She wore his mother's face. Of course she did. She always did. Grey-streaked hair pinned carelessly, flour on one cheek as though she'd just been baking, the exact smile that had once meant *supper's ready* and *you're safe* and nothing else in the world. The Architect had found that face in Quentin's grief the way a crow finds a shiny button, and it had worn it ever since, and it never seemed to understand why the wearing hurt more than any blade.

"Hello, darling," it said, in his mother's voice. "You've kept the place tidy."

Quentin's hand shook. Isolde's didn't. He took strength from hers like a man taking warmth off a fire he hadn't built.

"It's time," the Architect said. "Three years, bought and paid. Your mother's promise has ripened. Come to the door, Quentin. Give me what's owed, and I'll leave your city your books and your girl intact. I'm reasonable. Ask anyone whose promises I've eaten."

"The last person who asked you anything," Isolde said pleasantly, "was Wren, and you stole her."

"A business expense."

The gate roared.

Not hummed — *roared*, a sound like a forest falling and rising simultaneously, and the thorn-and-iron lattice split along seams Quentin had never seen, and light poured out. Not lamplight. Green-gold briarlight, thick as honey, smelling of rain on hot stone and crushed herbs and something older underneath, something like the inside of the world before anyone drew lines on it. The Hollow Gate swung wide for the first time in a century, and Thorn came through it like floodwater through a broken weir.

* * *

**Isolde**

Later — much later, when she had time to be afraid retroactively — Isolde would try to describe what Thorn looked like spilling into Vell's cellar, and she would fail, and she would settle for drawing it instead, because some things were bookwork and not words.

Vines of luminous briar poured through the gate and climbed the cellar walls, blooming as they went, flowers opening in colors that Vell had no names for — a green that sang, a purple that tasted of copper at the back of the throat. They threaded between the floorboards and up the stairwell, and where they touched the mortar of old Vell, the mortar *remembered*. Isolde watched the cellar walls soften, watched old stones recall being riverbeds, watched the outlawed magic of a hundred years of burned mages rise through the floor of the city like groundwater finding its level. Somewhere overhead, a church bell began ringing on its own. Somewhere overhead, someone laughed — really laughed, the startled laugh of a person whose hands had just lit with light for the first time since childhood.

"They'll panic," Quentin whispered.

"Some will," Isolde said. "Some have been waiting their whole lives to panic *joyfully*."

The Architect stepped aside to admire its own flood, wearing his mother's face with an expression his mother had never once worn: satisfaction. Hunger dressed as satisfaction.

"You see?" it said. "This is what breaking does. This is what I *am*. Every promise Vell ever made to its mages and broke — every oath, every vow, every 'we would never' — it's all come home to roost, and I am simply the roost. One small death balances it. Yours, darling. Then I seal the door behind you and everyone gets to keep their new toys until they break those promises too. Everyone wins." Its eyes — his mother's warm brown eyes — settled on Isolde. "Well. Almost everyone."

"You're offering terms," Isolde said.

"I'm offering mercy."

"You're offering a contract." She had spent ten years restoring damaged books; she knew predation when she saw it, and predation always arrived dressed as paperwork. "A death for a sealing. A life for a lock. And you get fed either way — you feed on the keeping of cruel bargains as much as the breaking of kind ones. That's the trick, isn't it? That's why you eat promises. It doesn't matter whether we honor them or break them. As long as they're *yours*, you're fed."

Something flickered behind his mother's eyes. Something with too many joints.

"Clever bookbinder."

"Restorer," Isolde corrected. "There's a difference. A restorer knows that a text isn't finished when it's written. It's finished when it's *read*." She reached into her apron and drew out the prophecy book — the real one, the first witness's own, its hidden testimony open to the final page. "And this text has one line left. The line you've been counting on. '*On the thirteenth turn the keeper pays, and the door is sealed in blood.*' That's your meal ticket. That's the promise you've been fattening on for a hundred years."

"Yes," the Architect said, softly, hungrily.

"So here's my problem with it." Isolde turned the book around. "It's unsigned."

* * *

**Quentin**

For a heartbeat — one long, ringing heartbeat — the cellar went silent except for the singing of the briars and the distant, liberated bells of Vell.

Then the Architect laughed, and it was the worst sound yet, because his mother had laughed exactly like that, head tipped back, helpless with delight, and hearing it come out of that stolen mouth while the thing's shadow grew claws on the wall —

"Ohh," the Architect breathed. "Oh, you *little*—"

"It's unsigned," Isolde repeated, steady as a press screw. "The first witness wrote the covenant and left the final line open, because a covenant renewed by a willing heir can't be coerced. You've spent a century trying to force someone to sign it in blood — first her daughter, then the wardens, then Quentin's mother, now Quentin. Every generation you've cornered someone at this door and demanded they pay. And every payment made under compulsion just *fed* you and left the line unsigned for the next poor soul. You haven't been collecting a debt, monster. You've been starving in a pantry, breaking your own teeth on jars."

Quentin stared at her. He had known, these past weeks, that Isolde was brave. He had watched her break Wren out of the Ministry cells at the cost of her sister's face and call it *fair trade*. He had watched her lie to the Architect's face over tea. But watching her now — small, ink-stained, absolutely unmovable at the threshold of a hungry god — he understood that he had mistaken courage for a virtue she possessed. It wasn't a possession. It was a practice. It was bookbinding. You did it page by page, stitch by stitch, and you did it even when your hands bled.

Especially then.

"Move," the Architect said to her, quietly, and the cellar temperature dropped, and the briars nearest it bowed away as if from frost. "The debt is his. Stand aside and live, bookbinder. I'll even let you keep your memories. Most of them."

"He offered me chicory," Isolde said. "Honey in it. We're bound past your jurisdiction."

"ISOLDE." Quentin grabbed her arm. "Whatever you're doing — I love you, and whatever you're doing, do it fast, because it's losing us the room."

She looked at him then, and smiled, and it was the smile from the night market, from the very first evening, dread braided with curiosity, except the dread had burned away and left only the other thing. "Quentin. Do you trust me?"

"With the gate. With the city. With the cat."

"Good." She pulled the penknife from her apron pocket — the plain bone-handled one she used for trimming boards — and opened her own palm over the prophecy's final page. "Because the first witness left instructions, and I've been doing my homework. A covenant isn't a bargain. A bargain is a trap with two doors. A covenant is a *promise freely rewritten* — and rewriting is bookwork."

She cut. Blood welled, dark in the briarlight. And she wrote.

* * *

**Quentin**

He heard the words as she wrote them, because the gate heard them first and repeated them in its iron voice, and every word fell on the Architect like a struck bell:

*Not sealed. Not severed. Shared.*

*A keeper on one side. A witness on the other. Two hands, one door, opened willingly each dusk and closed willingly each dawn.*

*No debt. No purchase. No feast.*

*Let the hungry thing find nothing here to eat — for a promise given freely cannot be broken, and a promise that cannot be broken cannot be devoured.*

The Architect screamed.

It was not his mother's voice anymore. It was not any voice. It was the sound of a hundred years of cornered heirs and stolen oaths and swallowed names all leaving at once, and the thing wearing Quentin's grief clawed at its own borrowed face as though the face were burning — which, Quentin realized, watching with a fierce and terrible joy, it was. There was nothing left to feed it. The covenant stood rewritten, signed in witness's blood, witnessed by a keeper who was still, against all odds and eleven letters and his own best arrangements, alive.

"You can't—" the Architect snarled, shrinking, guttering, its shadow collapsing joint by joint. "The debt! Her debt! The three years—"

"Bought with a compelled promise," Isolde said coldly. "Voided with a free one. Read your own law, monster. Or don't. Starve either way."

It lunged anyway — hunger's last argument is always the teeth — and Quentin moved without deciding to, stepping between it and Isolde with his palms out and the gate's iron song running through him like a second spine. For one instant the Architect's claws met the flat of his hands, and he felt it straining against him, felt all its century of appetite pressing on his bones—

—and felt, beneath it, nothing. No grip. No hook. Nothing in him left to hold. His mother's debt had dissolved the moment the covenant was rewritten, and the creature's claws slid off him like rain off oiled canvas.

"*Nothing*," Quentin said wonderingly. "You've got nothing on me. You never did. You just had a promise she couldn't take back — and I just watched someone show me how to take it back for her."

The Architect made a sound like a library burning. And then, at the last, it did the cruelest thing left in its repertoire: it looked at Quentin with his mother's eyes, and his mother's mouth shaped the words "*darling*" one final time — and came apart. Unraveled into motes of grey light that the briars drank greedily, flower after flower flaring white as it fed, until the cellar held nothing but bloom-light, bellsong, and two people breathing hard in the ruins of a hundred-year siege.

* * *

**Isolde**

The quiet afterward was enormous.

Isolde became aware of things in order: the blood drying on her palm, sticky as binding glue. The chicory, gone cold, still somehow steaming faintly — no, not steam; briarlight rising off it, blessing it. The gate, swinging slowly shut of its own accord, not slamming, not sealing — *closing*, the way a house door closes at dusk, expecting to be opened again tomorrow.

And Quentin, staring at his own empty hands.

"She's gone," he said. "The — the face. Whatever it was using." His voice wobbled in the middle. "I keep waiting to feel free and mostly I just feel —"

"Robbed," Isolde said. "Of the chance to say goodbye properly. To the real her."

"Yeah." He scrubbed his face with both hands. "Yeah."

Isolde crossed the cellar, took his chin in her uninjured hand, and tilted his head down to hers. "Then say it now. To the real her. Wherever promises go when they're finally paid off — I'd bet my whole shop she's there, and I'd bet she heard every word you said to that thing, including the ones you muttered about the cat."

Quentin laughed — wetly, helplessly — and pressed his forehead to hers. "Hi, Mum," he whispered. "Debt's cleared. Sorry about the mess. Your son's keeper of the Hollow Gate, and the woman I love rewrote the ending, and there's honey in the chicory, and I think — I think we won."

Above them, through the floorboards, all of Vell was waking up alight.

Bells everywhere now, dozens of them, none of them rung by hands. Shouting — but half of it joyful. The smell of bread baking itself somewhere near Tanner's Row. And beneath it all, threading up through the cellar stones, the green-gold pulse of the briars, patient as a heartbeat, spreading through the cracks of a city that had spent a century pretending it had no magic in it and discovering, tonight, that the magic had been waiting in the walls the whole time.

"Ministry's going to have opinions," Quentin murmured into her hair.

"Ministry's going to have *meetings*," Isolde corrected. "Half of them just watched their own headquarters grow roses. You don't burn that. You negotiate with it."

"And us?"

She pulled back far enough to look at him, and lifted the prophecy book between them. The final page had gone blank — clean, smooth, waiting, like all the pages behind it. On the cover, faint as a watermark, new script was surfacing, letter by letter, in a hand neither of theirs:

*A keeper keeps. A witness witnesses. The door opens at dusk.*

"Us," Isolde said, "is a doorway."

Through the gate's narrowing gap, deep in Thorn, something moved — quick and tawny, a fox-shape pausing at the border of the briarlight, regarding them with eyes like coins. It dipped its head, almost politely. Then it turned and bounded away into the luminous dark, and Isolde could have sworn it was carrying a message.

Or delivering a warning.

Because far off across Thorn's flowering miles, where the briars grew tallest and the light grew strange, something else was stirring — something the fox-spirit had named long ago and the Architect had merely served. The unravelling of a hungry god leaves leavings. And leavings, Isolde knew — restorers always know — are never truly lost.

They are only waiting to be found.

Quentin laced his bloodied fingers through hers. "Tomorrow?"

"Tonight," Isolde said. "Dusk's already come. The door's supposed to open." She smiled, tired and fierce and entirely unafraid. "Come and keep it with me."
