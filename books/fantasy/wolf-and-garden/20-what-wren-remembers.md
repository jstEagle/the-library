## 20. What Wren Remembers

The trouble with miracles, Isolde had learned, was the paperwork.

The Ministry's collapse had left half a city of forms unfiled and half a city of prisoners unaccounted for, and Wren Ashgrave — fence, smuggler, occasional liar, and full-time survivor — came back to them on a gray Tuesday in the third week of spring, walking through the door of Quentin's shop with a Ministry escort who looked embarrassed to be there, as though he'd been assigned to return a library book that could bite.

"Your man," the escort said, thrusting a paper at Quentin. "Cleared of all charges. Compensated for unlawful detention." He paused. "There was an incident with the holding cells."

"What kind of incident?" Quentin asked.

"The kind we've agreed not to write down," the escort said, and left at a pace just short of running.

Wren stood in the middle of the shop, thinner than Isolde remembered, grayer at the temples, wearing a coat two sizes too big that she clearly intended never to acknowledge. She looked around at the shelves of charms, the luck-knots, the fever-wax, the little brass bells that Quentin still sold to people who thought he was nothing more than a charm-monger, and her face did something complicated.

"Well," she said. "It's uglier than I remembered."

"You remembered it correctly," Quentin said. "You're remembering it fondly."

"I'm home," Wren said, "is what I'm doing," and then her knees went, and it was Isolde who caught her, and for a long moment the three of them stood there in the doorway like the world's worst-kept secret, holding each other up.

* * *

They fed her soup because that was what one did with returning prisoners, and tea because Wren refused soup on principle, and brandy because Wren refused to be reasonable about anything before noon ever again, she said, having earned it.

She talked the way she always talked — fast, sideways, funny in the places where other people would grieve — about the cells, about the interrogator with the kind voice and the terrible questions, about the six weeks she'd spent pretending to know less than a turnip. But Isolde noticed, the way you notice a missing tooth in a familiar smile, that Wren kept pausing. Little hesitations. A word held in the mouth and set down again. Her eyes going to the middle distance as if something were written there that she couldn't quite read.

It was on the third day, when Wren was reorganizing the shop's stock by a system known only to herself, that Isolde found her standing very still in front of the shelf of memory-charms — the ones Quentin forbade, the costly ones, the ones that took instead of gave.

"I remember doing this," Wren said, without turning around. "Buying one. Before they took me. I paid myself, mind, no one forced me. I needed the space in my head, and I knew exactly which memory would fetch the best price against what I needed to forget." She turned. Her face was pale under its gray. "I don't remember what I bought."

Isolde's hands went cold on the counter. "Wren—"

"It's you, isn't it," Wren said. Not cruelly. Carefully, the way you handle a bird with a broken wing. "The night you broke me out. You used one of these. Quentin forbade it. You used it anyway, and it cost—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "There's a hole in me, Isolde. A clean hole, the way these things cut. And everything around the hole is shaped like you."

* * *

Quentin confirmed it that evening in the back room, with the lamp turned low and the Hollow Gate humming beneath their feet like a sleeping animal. He told it plainly, because he'd learned from Isolde that plainness was a kindness: the charm she'd used, the price it demanded, the memory it had taken.

"Your sister," he said. "Her face. The charm took the memory of her face."

Isolde had known this for months now. She had lived beside the absence the way you live beside a staircase you can no longer climb — aware of every step, unable to take them. She had a name, Maren. She had a voice remembered in fragments. She had the shape of a girl who used to braid flowers into Isolde's hair when they were small, and the certainty of being loved, and none of it came attached to a face. When she tried to picture Maren, she got a candlelit blur, a warm smudge where a person should be. It was like loving someone through frosted glass.

"She has it," Isolde said quietly. "Wren has Maren's face in her head. She's been carrying it around for weeks without knowing whose it was."

"That's how the old charms work," Quentin said. "Nothing is destroyed. It's only moved. Somewhere in her, there's a stranger's face that feels like home." He reached across the table and took her hand. "We can get it back. The Architect's hoard scattered when it unraveled — the Ministry's been collecting the leavings for weeks. Broken promises, stolen memories, all the debris of a thing that ate them. If your memory is in there, intact, we can reclaim it. There will be forms," he added, wry despite himself, "because apparently even miracles require forms now. But it can be done."

"And it wouldn't cost anything?" Isolde asked. "This time?"

"This time the taking is honest. It's yours. It was always yours."

* * *

The Ministry's recovery vaults sat beneath what had been the Office of Unnatural Affairs, in rooms that smelled of ash and old paper, and they were stranger than Isolde had imagined. The Architect had been a creature of bargains, and its remains reflected that: shelves of bottled oaths gone cloudy; drawers of names folded into paper cranes that twitched when you walked past; a whole cabinet of memories sealed in wax, each one labeled in a handwriting that made Isolde's skin crawl because it was almost, but not quite, her own.

A young clerk named Pem — earnest, overwhelmed, visibly delighted to have visitors who weren't auditors — led them through the stacks with a lantern.

"We don't fully understand the taxonomy yet," Pem admitted. "Some of these are debts the Architect collected. Some are... payments people made voluntarily. We've been trying to return things where we can identify the owners." He lowered his voice. "It's slow work. Some of the memories are homesick. They get into the walls."

"They get into the *walls*?" Quentin said.

"You get used to it," Pem said, in the tone of someone who absolutely did not.

He found the ledger entry within the hour. *One (1) memory, visual, personal. Tendered willingly by subject V., I., in exchange for operational capacity.* The wax seal on the little jar was broken — the Architect's death had cracked every seal in the vault — but the memory itself sat inside like a drop of colored light, and when Isolde leaned close, the frosted glass between her and her sister grew suddenly, achingly thin.

She could feel it wanting to come home.

"There's a restoration rite," Pem said. "Takes about ten minutes. You'll want to sit down afterward. Reunions are heavier than people expect." He smiled kindly and stepped away to fetch the book, leaving her alone with the jar.

Isolde stood there a long time.

Through the glass she could almost see it — not the memory itself but the edges of it, the frame of it. A kitchen? A garden? Light on hair. She thought of Maren's name and felt the jar hum faintly in answer, like a struck bell recognizing its note.

And underneath the longing, quiet and clear as water, another thought rose:

*What did it buy?*

She closed her eyes and let herself remember the night properly. The Ministry cells, the iron, Wren's face gray as dishwater through the bars. The charm in her palm, warm as a living thing. Quentin's voice saying *don't*, and meaning *I can't lose you too*. And the choice — not a grand choice, not a prophecy-shaped choice, just a small human arithmetic done in the dark: *one memory, weighed against one life.* Wren had information they needed. Wren had three children and a shop and a laugh like a cart rattling downhill. Wren had built half her life on knowing Isolde's secrets, and Isolde had built half of hers on being known.

The memory had bought that. All of it. Every day since.

Pem returned with the restoration book and found her still standing there, and he must have read something in her face, because he stopped a respectful few feet away and waited.

"Can I ask you something strange?" Isolde said.

"People usually do, down here."

"If I don't take it back — does the memory suffer? In there. With Wren."

Pem considered this with the seriousness of a man who had learned that strange questions were the only honest ones. "Memories aren't lonely," he said finally. "That's the thing nobody expects. They're more like seeds. They keep whether they're planted or not. Yours has been keeping in her for months now. It'll have rooted a little." He smiled apologetically. "If you reclaimed it, you'd be pulling up something that had started to grow in borrowed ground. It would live. It might miss the soil."

*It might miss the soil.*

Isolde looked at the jar a moment longer. Then she set it down on the shelf, gently, the way you set down something that belongs to someone else.

"I'd like to speak with her first," she said. "Before anything's decided."

* * *

She didn't tell Wren about the vault. Not directly. Instead, a week later, she invited her to dinner — real dinner, cooked, at the little flat above the shop that Quentin had finally admitted was theirs now, plural, the way the gate was theirs, plural, everything theirs in duplicate halves — and after the plates were cleared and the second pot of tea brewed, Isolde said:

"Tell me about the face."

Wren went still with the teapot in both hands. "What face?"

"The one that lives in your head and feels like home. The one you've been trying not to ask me about for a month."

Wren set the pot down. For once in her life she didn't reach for a joke, and Isolde loved her a little for that.

"She's young," Wren said slowly. "Younger than us. Dark hair — no, wait." She frowned, feeling along the edges of it. "Light hair? It changes when I'm not looking. But the *look* of her doesn't change. The way she looks at you when you talk. Like whatever you're saying is the most interesting thing anyone's ever said, even when it's obviously rubbish. She laughs before the joke lands — she laughs at the *shape* of jokes coming, like she can see them over the horizon." Wren's voice had gone soft and wondering. "I've caught myself smiling at strangers who look nothing like her. I dream about her sometimes. I wake up happy and I don't know why, and then I remember I don't know why, and it's—" She stopped. Looked up. "Whose face is it, Isolde?"

"My sister's," Isolde said. "Maren's."

The name landed in the room and stayed there. Wren put her hand over her mouth. When she spoke again her eyes were wet. "I've been carrying your sister around in my head for half a year."

"You have. And here's the hard part, so let me say it all before either of us cries." Isolde took her hand across the table. "It can come back to me. The Ministry has it. There's a rite, ten minutes, no cost. I could have Maren's face tonight. I could *see* her."

Wren's grip tightened. "Then why are you telling me first?"

"Because if I take it back, you lose her. You'd wake up tomorrow with a hole where the happiness comes from, and you wouldn't even know what you'd lost, and I would — I'd know. Every time I saw your face, I'd know I'd taken it." Isolde laughed, wet-eyed herself. "Do you know what I've learned this year? Everything costs. Everything, Wren. Magic, love, survival — all of it costs, and the only question worth asking is whether the price buys something worthy. That memory bought your life. It bought the information that saved the covenant. It bought every morning you've had since. And it's been keeping well in you — better than well. It's *happy* in you. You smile at strangers because my sister's face taught you to."

"But she's *yours*."

"She's mine," Isolde agreed. "And she's also, right now, the only part of her I still have — because I can't see her face, but I know exactly where it is. I know it's laughing at the shape of jokes over the horizon. I know it's making you wake up glad." She squeezed Wren's hand. "Let me keep it there a while longer. Let it keep buying things."

Wren cried, of course. Wren cried and swore at herself for crying and then cried harder, and somewhere in the middle of it she said, "Your sister must be extraordinary," and Isolde said, "She is, and now you're the only person alive besides me who knows it," and somehow that made everything worse and better at once.

Later, much later, when the lamps were low and Wren had gone home to her own bed and her own dreams full of borrowed light, Quentin found Isolde sitting at the window with her tea gone cold.

"You could still change your mind," he said. "The vault isn't going anywhere."

"I know."

"He'd have understood. Your mother. Anyone. No one would think less of you."

"I know that too." She leaned her head against his shoulder. Below them, through the floor, the Hollow Gate hummed its patient note — the sound of a door that had learned, at last, to stay open. "It's strange. Everyone keeps telling me grief gets lighter if you set it down. And maybe it does. But some things you carry because carrying them is how you honor them. I lost her face so Wren could live. If I take it back now, the loss stops meaning anything. It becomes just — a theft I undid. And if I leave it—" She shrugged, helpless and certain at once. "Then every morning Wren wakes up glad, my sister's still in the world, doing good. Working. Being useful. Maren would have hated being useless above all things."

Quentin was quiet a moment. Then he said, "You realize that's the most keeper-ish thing you've ever said. You're doing wardenship with a *feeling*."

"Somebody has to," Isolde said. "You do doors. I do the rest."

* * *

Spring deepened. The shop did brisk and gentle business — luck-knots for nervous brides, fever-charms for anxious mothers, and, new that season, small brass tokens stamped with a briar-bloom, sold openly, legally, under the covenant's broad and battered shield. The Ministry sent polite letters now instead of raids, which everyone agreed was an improvement, even if the letters required forms.

And on an evening in late spring, with the light going gold over Vell's rooftops, Isolde came down to close the shop and found Wren behind the counter, sorting charms, humming, a stranger's smile on her face that wasn't a stranger's smile at all.

"You'll never guess what happened today," Wren said. "Somebody came in asking after a prophecy book. Said it had her *name* in it." She shook her head, laughing. "A child, Isolde. Couldn't have been more than nine years old. Said the book wrote itself, and it knew her, and it wanted—" She stopped. Frowned at the door, at the gold light, at something just past the edge of seeing. "Funny. I almost feel like I should know her."

Isolde looked at the empty doorstep, where the evening breeze stirred dust into little dancing shapes, and felt the covenant shift its weight, patient as a seed.

"No," she said softly. "But I think you're about to."
