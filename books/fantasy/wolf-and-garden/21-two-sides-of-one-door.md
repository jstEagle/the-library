## 21. Two Sides of One Door

The trouble with living on the wrong side of a door, Quentin Eastwick had decided by the second week of spring, was the laundry.

Thorn's air was clean and sweet and smelled of warm briars and river stones, and it ruined soap. He'd hung a line of washing between two luminous hawthorn saplings on Isolde's side of things — *her* side, though the word had gone soft at the edges — and by evening every shirt smelled of green growing things and faintly of fox. He didn't mind the fox. It was the folding that defeated him. Thorn cloth refused to lie flat, as if it still remembered being wild.

"You're fighting it," Isolde said through the Hollow Gate, her chin propped on her folded arms in the doorway of Vell, watching him wrestle a pillowcase that kept curling into the shape of a sleeping animal.

"I am *folding* it."

"You're negotiating with it. There's a difference. Here—" She reached across the threshold, took the pillowcase from him, and shook it out with the brisk authority of someone who had spent years breaking book spines back to health. "You have to tell it what it's going to be first. Same as paper."

"And does it listen?"

"Everything listens," she said, "if you speak its name properly." She folded it into a crisp square, set it down, and the square stayed a square, smugly. Then she looked up at him, and there was that expression she got — the one that meant she'd noticed something he hadn't said yet. "You could just live here, you know. In Vell. The Ministry signed the truce in *ink*, Quentin. Your name's not on any list anymore. Wren checks weekly."

He knew. He knew all of it. He also knew that when he slept on the Vell side of the gate, he dreamed of thornlight, and when he slept on the Thorn side, he woke homesick for the smell of his old shop — dust and beeswax and luck-knots drying on strings. Three years of keeping a secret door had done something permanent to his heart: it had split it neatly down the middle, one half on each side of the hinge.

"Maybe I like visiting," he said.

"You like *me*."

"That too," he admitted, and she laughed, and somewhere above them the gate hummed — a low, contented note, iron and thorn agreeing about something. It did that now whenever they were both near it. Quentin had stopped pretending he wasn't flattered.

* * *

Spring came differently to each city, and they made a study of it, the way Isolde made studies of everything — carefully, in ink, with marginalia.

In Vell, spring arrived as a rumor and then as a riot: crocuses splitting the cobbles along Chandler's Row, the Ministry's new Garden Corps planting feverfew where the ash-barrels used to stand. Children who'd been taught to cross the street from mages now followed them openly, asking to see small magics. Quentin still sold charms at the night market — luck-knots mostly, harmless as ever, though now he stamped them with a tiny open-door mark instead of hiding them under plain twine. His stall sat where it always had. Half his customers came for the charms. The other half came to hear how the war had ended, told in his dry way, leaving out the parts that still made his hands shake.

In Thorn, spring was less an arrival than an awakening. The briars that ringed Isolde's cottage — the cottage she'd been given by the realm itself, grown up around her in a single astonished week after the covenant was reforged — put out blossoms that glowed faintly at dusk, amber-green, like lamps someone had forgotten to blow out. Fox-spirits left gifts on her step: a perfect spiral of shed antler, a feather that hummed, once, memorably, a dead mouse arranged with what could only be called optimism.

"The realm likes you," Quentin said, examining the mouse.

"The realm has terrible taste in courtship gifts."

"It gave you a house."

"It grew me a house," she corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that the chimney smokes sideways when it disapproves of my reading choices." She paused. "Which is often. It did not care for the Architect's ledger. Very judgmental architecture."

They had divided the days without ever discussing it, the way water finds a level. Mornings belonged to Vell — to the shop, to Wren's deliveries, to the slow bureaucratic miracle of magic becoming legal again, one permit at a time. Evenings belonged to Thorn — to the garden Isolde was coaxing back from a century of neglect, to supper eaten on either side of the threshold because the food tasted better crossed over, and to the hour at dusk when they met, always, at the gate.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole arrangement, and people found it baffling.

Wren had been the first to say it, in their blunt fence-of-contraband way: "So you're married to a doorway."

"We're not married," Quentin had said.

"To a *doorway*, Eastwick. You share a house with no one and a threshold with everything. I've smuggled stranger arrangements, but not many."

And Isolde, when her sister — faceless in memory, beloved anyway — had asked her gently whether she wasn't lonely, had answered with the truth: "I'm alone exactly half the time, which is more company than most people manage."

But here is what neither of them said aloud, in those early weeks: that the arrangement was not a compromise but a discovery. That love, they were finding, did not require a shared roof. It required a shared direction. And every dusk, walking toward the same door from opposite sides, they were pointed at each other like compass needles, and there was nothing in either city — not the Ministry's new order, not the realm's old hunger, not the blank pages of prophecy — that could make them miss the meeting.

* * *

The book worried her, though. She would admit that much, even to herself, only at night, with the sideways-smoking chimney for company.

The prophecy book sat on her workbench in Thorn, restored at last — every water-warped page flattened, every torn leaf mended with tissue so fine it was nearly prayer. The text of the thirteen turns remained, faded but legible, the record of a story finished. But the pages after it, the ones that should have held whatever came next, were blank. Not empty-blank. *Waiting*-blank. She had tested them with every method she knew. Ink sat on the surface and slid off, beading like rain on wax. Her pen touched a page and the page seemed to hold its breath.

On the twentieth of April, she brought the book through the gate at dusk and set it on the counter between them.

"It won't take ink anymore," she said. "Not mine, not anyone's. I've asked the Garden Corps' archivist. I've asked a fox-spirit, who laughed at me, which was informative in its way."

Quentin turned the book toward himself. The blank pages caught the last light coming through the shop's front windows and threw it back pale gold. "It wrote your name once," he said. "Your date. My face. Maybe it's finished doing that kind of thing."

"Or maybe," Isolde said quietly, "it's waiting to see what we do before it decides what we are."

He looked at her across the counter — across the threshold, which was the same thing now; the counter straddled it, built that way on purpose during one long happy afternoon of carpentry and argument — and thought about the word *waiting*. Waiting was a word he knew intimately. He had waited three years behind this counter, keeping a door shut, keeping himself small. Waiting had been the shape of his whole life until a bookbinder walked into his stall precisely on schedule.

"I don't want to be written again," he said. "No offense to the book."

"None taken on its behalf. I don't want to be written either." She traced the edge of a blank page with one fingertip, and for a moment — just a moment — the paper warmed under her touch, the way the gate warmed, the way everything in both realms had learned to lean toward her since the night she'd rewritten the final line in blood. "I want to write. There's a difference, and I intend to spend the rest of my life insisting on it."

"Then write something else. Write recipes. Write bad poetry about the fox-spirits. Write an inventory of my charms; gods know I need one."

She smiled. But she didn't close the book, and neither did he, and it lay there between them, open, patient, a door of its own.

* * *

The dusk meetings developed rituals, as all good things do.

First, the crossing. One of them stepped through — whose turn it was depended on a system involving a copper coin, a thorn-blossom, and a dispute neither of them had any intention of resolving. Then the report: news of whichever city the other hadn't seen all day. Isolde delivered hers in full sentences, organized by importance. Quentin delivered his in fragments, buried in charm-talk, requiring excavation.

"Wren says the Ministry's new warden is fair," he said, tying off a luck-knot. "Also that the permit queue for hedge-witches is down to six weeks. Also there's a cat in the alley who can apparently predict rain, and Wren wants to charge admission, and I told Wren that's exactly how trafficking charges start."

"Fair warden?"

"Frightened, but fair. Frightened-fair is the best we're going to get for a decade. Fair comes later, if we water it."

Then, the touching. This was not romantic in origin, though it had become so. On the night after the truce, Isolde had pressed her palm flat against the gate's iron bands and felt it hum back, and Quentin had watched her do it and understood — really understood, for the first time — that the covenant lived in her now, that she was the keeper's other half the way a book is the other half of its binding. So each dusk they touched the door together, one hand each, iron and woven thorn under their palms, and the gate sang its low note, and somewhere in both cities, flowers opened slightly ahead of schedule.

Finally, the parting — never a goodbye, because goodbyes implied an ending, and they had both developed strong opinions about endings. Instead Quentin would say, "Same door tomorrow," and Isolde would say, "Bring the good honey," or some variation, and they would turn away from each other toward their separate lamps, and the gate would hum behind them like a struck bell fading.

It was, Wren observed, watching them one evening from the market with the expression of someone auditing a very strange account, the least efficient romance in the history of either realm.

It was also, both of them knew, unbreakable. You could burn a roof over lovers' heads. You could outlaw a shared bed, seize a shared name. But a threshold? A threshold was a thing you couldn't confiscate, because it existed only in the act of crossing, and they performed that act daily, faithfully, like prayer.

* * *

On the last evening of April, Isolde came through the gate carrying the book, and stopped halfway.

She stood in the doorway itself — one foot in Vell, one foot in Thorn, the exact center of the threshold where the two airs mixed, woodsmoke and briar-light — and she went very still, in the particular way she went still when the world showed her a sentence she hadn't expected.

"What is it?" Quentin asked, setting down his needle.

She turned the book toward him. Open, of course. To a blank page, of course. But on the blank page, in the top corner, in handwriting that was hers and was not hers — the same hand that had once written her name beside tomorrow's date — a single line had appeared:

*A keeper and a witness keep a door between them. What grows in the space between two faithful hands?*

Below it, the rest of the page waited, blank as new snow.

Isolde looked up at him. Quentin looked at the words. Above them, the Hollow Gate hummed — louder than usual, brighter, the thorn-light in its weave pulsing like a heartbeat that had noticed something.

"Well," Quentin said finally, his voice not entirely steady, "at least it's asking questions instead of assigning homework."

Isolde laughed — that startled, bright laugh he'd crossed realms to hear — and then she picked up her pen, dipped it in the little pot of ink she now carried everywhere, and held it over the page.

"Not tonight," she said, to him, to the book, to the listening dark on both sides of the door. "Tonight we have supper. Tomorrow—" the nib hovered, trembling with possibility, "—tomorrow we answer it ourselves."

Far away in Thorn, a fox-spirit sat up suddenly among the briars, ears pricked, and began, very softly, to laugh.
