## 22. The Garden Grows Back

The garden came back first in the cracks.

That was how Isolde thought of it, a year on — not as some grand flowering, though there was plenty of that too, but as small green insistences. Moss in the mortar of the shop's front step. A stubborn thread of moonwort climbing the drainpipe that Quentin swore he'd never planted and could never quite bring himself to pull. The whole city of Vell had gone soft at its edges since the covenant was rewritten, like a book left open in the rain, its pages swelling with things growing where pages shouldn't.

She'd gotten used to sweeping petals off the threshold every morning. She'd even gotten fond of it, though she complained about it daily on principle. Quentin said complaining was good for her; he claimed it kept her from turning into one of the briars entirely.

"Isolde," he called from the back room, "did you move my soldering iron?"

"No."

"Did Wren move my soldering iron?"

"Wren moved everything you own last Tuesday out of spite. It's probably wherever Wren put it."

A pause. Then, resigned: "It's in the teapot, isn't it?"

"It might be in the teapot."

He appeared in the doorway with the iron in one hand and a look of long-suffering affection that Isolde had come to treasure more than any of the old charms they sold. His hair had gone silver at the temples — early for a man his age, but then, keeper-ship had never been kind to anyone's hairline. He wore it well anyway. She'd told him so exactly once, because telling him twice would have been insufferable, and he still brought it up at least weekly, insufferably.

"Customer," he said, nodding toward the front.

"I heard the bell."

"You were ignoring it."

"I was *savoring* the moment before the bell," she said. "It's a luxury I've earned."

The bell over the door — a gift from Wren, salvaged from a drowned church and blessed by no one in particular, which these days meant nothing at all or possibly everything — rang again, and Isolde went through into the shop proper.

The morning light fell in long gold bars across the counters. The shelves had been reorganized four times since spring: once by Quentin, who believed in alphabetical order; once by Wren, who believed in *vibes*, a word Isolde had learned against her will; and twice by Isolde herself, who believed in whatever system made the luck-knots easiest to reach when the market crowd got pushy. The current arrangement was hers. It looked like chaos. It worked like clockwork.

The customer was a child.

She stood just inside the door, small enough that the counter came up past her chin, maybe eight years old, with mud on her boots and a smudge of something — charcoal? ash? — on one cheek. Her hair was dark and wildly uncombed, and she clutched a book to her chest the way children clutch things they've been told are precious and have decided independently are *theirs*.

"We're open," Isolde said, coming around the counter. "Mostly. If you're here about the fever-charms your mother ordered, we've been waiting on rivermint from Thorn all week. The ferryman keeps stopping to argue with a heron."

"It's not about charms." The girl's voice was steady in the way that certain children's voices are steady — not calm, exactly, but certain. Certain the way weather is certain. "I found a book. Nobody else can read it. My nan says take it to the charm-seller under the hill, the one with the gate."

Isolde's hands went very still on the counter.

Under the hill. The Hollow Gate had kept that name even now, even after the Ministry's fall and the truce and the slow, strange peace — partly because the name was accurate, partly because Vell loved a name it could whisper. The shop sat at the top of Gate Hill, and beneath it, behind three doors and a curtain of woven thorn that no longer needed guarding but guarded itself anyway, the Hollow Gate hummed its low patient note, day and night. Open now. Watched always.

"And what's your name?" Isolde asked, though she already felt she knew, the way you know a chord is going to resolve before it does.

"Maren," the girl said.

Not Vane. Not Eastwick. No bloodline Isolde could trace on any of the family trees she'd drawn and redrawn across the margins of the prophecy book. Which meant —

Which meant the covenant had gone looking on its own.

"Let me see the book, Maren."

The girl hesitated. Isolde recognized the hesitation intimately. She crouched down to the child's level, forearms on the counter, and waited, and after a moment Maren held the book up with both hands like an offering.

It was bound in grey-green leather gone soft with age, stitched with thread that caught the light wrong — thorn-thread, Isolde realized, actual thorn-thread, the kind Quentin's mother had used on the gate itself. The cover bore no title. But when Isolde's fingers brushed the spine, the book warmed against her palm like a sleeping bird, and somewhere below the floorboards, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the Hollow Gate *hummed*.

Quentin had come to stand in the back-room doorway without her noticing. She didn't look at him yet. She opened the book.

The pages were blank.

Of course they were blank. Their own book — the prophecy book, the one Isolde had restored line by water-damaged line, the one whose final page still bore her blood-written covenant in letters that glowed faintly at dusk — their book had gone blank too, a year ago, the morning after the Architect unraveled. Blank and warm and waiting, like a held breath. They'd kept it on the shelf behind the counter and told themselves it was resting. Told themselves books rest, after finishing.

But this wasn't their book.

This was smaller, older, the leather worn at the corners by other hands. And on its first page, in ink that shimmered between brown and green depending on how the light hit it, in handwriting that made Isolde's chest tighten with recognition she couldn't explain — someone had written a single line:

*Maren, witness.*

Just that. A name and a word.

"She won't say where she found it," Maren reported, jerking her chin at the book. "I asked it loads of times. It just hums."

"It does that," Isolde said. "They're rude like that."

From the doorway, Quentin made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a prayer. Isolde finally looked up at him.

He had gone pale in the particular way he went pale — not frightened, not anymore; those days were mostly behind them — but *awed*, the way he'd looked the night Isolde first touched the Hollow Gate and it sang. He crossed the shop slowly, wiping his hands on his apron out of habit, and knelt beside her so that the three of them formed a strange little triangle over the counter's edge: keeper, witness, and whatever Maren was becoming.

"May I?" he asked the girl.

Maren studied him with frank, unchildlike appraisal. "You're the man from the picture," she said.

The words landed in the shop like a dropped glass.

"What picture?" Quentin asked carefully.

"The one in my dream." Maren shrugged, matter-of-fact. "There's a door made of brambles, and a man stands in front of it holding it shut, and a woman comes up the hill with ink on her fingers. Every night. Nan says I talk in my sleep. Nan says I should stop."

Isolde looked at her own hands. Ink on the fingers — always ink, these days, since she'd taken up keeping the book alongside keeping the shop, recording the small covenants of ordinary life: the ferryman's promise to the heron (settled, terms undisclosed), the neighbor boy's oath to stop feeding the moonwort (broken, consequences pending). The book wrote itself sometimes. Other times it waited for her.

And Quentin — Quentin who had stood in front of a door made of woven thorn and held it shut for three lonely years, whose vigilance had been the only thing between two worlds, whose loneliness she had walked into exactly as foretold.

"Your nan," Isolde said gently, "sounds like a sensible woman."

"Nan says magic is like weather. You don't argue with it, you just bring a coat."

Quentin did laugh then, a real laugh, startled out of him, and Isolde watched the last of the pale awe in his face soften into something warmer. He reached out and touched the book's spine with one finger, the same gesture he used on the gate — respectful, asking permission rather than taking it.

The book hummed louder. Below them, the Hollow Gate answered, two notes finding each other like tuning forks.

"Well," Quentin said. "It likes her."

"It would," Isolde said. "It has excellent taste. Look who it chose last time."

"You chose yourself last time. With blood. Very dramatic. I nearly fainted."

"You *did* faint."

"I sat down quickly," he corrected with dignity, "for tactical reasons."

Maren watched them bicker with the expression of a child witnessing something both baffling and deeply reassuring, and Isolde caught herself doing it — catching the look, the exchange over the counter, the silent conversation that took half a second and covered a whole history. *Here we go again*, the look said. *Are we ready?* the eyes asked. And the answer, in the set of his shoulders and the curve of her own mouth: *We were never ready the first time either. Ready is a myth. Willing is enough.*

Because that was the thing the first witness's hidden testimony had taught them, the thing Isolde had written into the covenant in her own blood while the Architect starved and screamed: the covenant was never meant to be sealed, and never meant to be severed, and never meant to be *carried* like a debt. It was meant to be offered. Renewed by a willing heir, again and again, generation after generation — a gift passed hand to hand, not a burden bolted to anyone's back.

Her mother had died when she was young. Isolde had inherited grief instead of duty. Perhaps that was why it had taken her a full year to understand what she was feeling now, watching Maren hug the humming book: not dread. Not the cold weight of the prophecy's old arithmetic, where every gift was priced and every price was paid in flesh.

Just — gladness. Startled, unarmored gladness.

"Can you read it?" Maren asked. "Nan said nobody can read it."

"Not yet," Isolde said. "Books like this write themselves slowly. One line at a time. Usually when nobody's watching." She smiled. "Though I'll warn you — it watches back."

"Does it hurt?" The question came quick and quiet, the way children ask the questions adults spend decades learning to ask aloud. "The writing part. In your story — the lady who wrote the last bit with her blood. Did it hurt?"

Isolde was aware of Quentin going still beside her. Of the gate's hum beneath the floor, patient as ever. Of the ink drying on her fingertips from this morning's entries.

"Yes," she said honestly. "It hurt. Some true things do." She turned the book gently toward Maren, to the single shining line. "*Maren, witness.* That's not a job, little one. It's not a chain. It means the world saw you and liked what it saw. It means when there's a door that needs opening — or holding, or mending, or singing to — you'll be someone it trusts. That's all. That's everything."

Maren considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then: "Do I get paid?"

"*There* it is," Quentin said delightedly. "Oh, she's perfect. Isolde, we're keeping her."

"We're not *keeping* her, she has a nan—"

"Room and board, lessons in charm-work, unlimited access to the good tea," Quentin continued, counting on his fingers, "and a cut of the luck-knot profits, which frankly are criminal—"

"The luck-knot profits are *not* criminal, I set those prices—"

"You set them at market rate, and market rate for luck is extortion and you know it."

Maren looked between them, and something in her small serious face eased — the particular easing of a child who has walked a long way carrying something heavy and has finally found people who argue about teapots and prices and clearly, obviously, know exactly what this book is. She set it down on the counter with a careful thunk, as if laying down a sword after a war.

"Nan said you'd feed me," she said. "She said if the book hummed, feed me and send word."

"Your nan," Isolde said, "is wiser than most ministries I've burned down. Tea it is. And toast. And then—" she glanced at Quentin, and he nodded, and together they finished the sentence the way they finished most sentences now, seamlessly, without deciding to—

"—then we'll teach you how to listen to it."

* * *

The afternoon went golden and unhurried. Maren ate four pieces of toast and interrogated Wren, who arrived mid-morning smelling of river water and triumph ("Don't ask," Wren said, dripping, "unless you want a forty-minute story," and Maren, predictably, wanted the forty-minute story). Quentin dug the good tea out from behind the soldering iron — which was, indeed, in the teapot — and showed Maren how to hold a luck-knot without squeezing the luck out of it. Isolde sat at the big table by the window with the new book open in front of her and copied its single line into their own blank prophecy book, and when she finished, both books hummed in harmony, and the moonwort on the drainpipe put out a flower out of season, just because it could.

At dusk, as always, Isolde went down to the gate.

The thorn-curtain parted for her the way it always did now — eagerly, like a dog hearing its name. Beyond it, Thorn glowed in its evening colors, luminous briars and drifting spores of light, the exiled magic that was exile no longer but simply *neighbor*. Somewhere in the deep green, the fox-spirit was probably watching. It always watched. It had stopped eating promises years ago and had developed, instead, a taste for gossip, which Isolde supplied generously and Quentin pretended to disapprove of.

She pressed her palm flat to the door's iron-and-thorn surface and felt the answering pulse. On the far side, right on time, Quentin's hand met hers through the gap — keeper on one side, witness on the other, meeting nightly at the threshold as they had every dusk since spring. Two sides of one door. Neither willing to give up their side, neither needing to.

"A witness," Quentin said quietly, through the weave. His thumb traced the faded scar on her wrist where thirteen marks had bloomed and faded, leaving only smooth skin and memory. "Chosen early. Do you think we're doing something right, or the universe is doing something impatient?"

"Both. Probably both." Isolde leaned her forehead against the thorns, which parted softly around her, courteous as ever. "It feels different this time, doesn't it. Than it felt for us."

"It does."

"Lighter."

"Because it is." His voice was warm and certain, the voice of a man who had prepared himself to die and had been handed a life instead and had spent every day since treating it as the extravagant gift it was. "No debts. No dates written in blood. Just a book that found a girl who talks in her sleep. That's not a burden arriving, love. That's a garden growing back."

Through the weave of the gate, their fingers laced together — impossible, ordinary, theirs.

Behind them both, faintly, from the shop above, came a sound that made them both smile: a child's voice asking Wren a follow-up question, Wren's voice launching happily into another forty-minute answer, and the small clear hum of a book beginning — very softly, one word at a time — to write.

Isolde listened to the first word form, and laughed, and squeezed Quentin's hand.

"Oh no," she said. "It starts with a riddle."
