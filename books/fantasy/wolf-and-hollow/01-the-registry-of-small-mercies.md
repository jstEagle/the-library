## 1. The Registry of Small Mercies

I had copied scripture for ten years, and I will tell you what nobody believes about that work: it is not holy. It is *carpentry*. You cut each letter to fit the one before it, you sand the joins smooth, and at the end of the day you have built something that stands whether or not God is home. I was good at it. Careful. The kind of careful that gets mistaken for piety.

Then the witch-hunters came to Thornfell on a Tuesday, which felt about right. Catastrophes respect no liturgy.

They arrived at the temple of Saint Aldhelm-the-Deaf — yes, deaf; the mountain folk appreciate a saint who minds his own business — six riders in slate-grey, with the crown's sunburst stamped on their breastplates and the particular patience of men who have been paid in advance. Their captain was a woman named Serreck, all cheekbone and economy, who looked at our abbot the way an auditor looks at a ledger she already knows is cooked.

"By decree," she said, unrolling a proclamation as if it were a tablecloth, "all persons gifted with uncanny hearing are to be entered into registry."

Uncanny hearing. In Thornfell. Where the mountains talk, and half the valley has learned to listen without admitting it. It was like ordering a registry of people who breathe.

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, including the abbot, including the God whose letters I cut so neatly: I hear the mountains. Not metaphor. Not the wind making shapes in my head. The stone itself has a voice — low, mineral, older than language, and it has been muttering under my skull since I was seven years old. My mother called it a gift before she understood that gifts, in this province, are inventory for confiscation. After that we called it nothing at all.

So when Captain Serreck's clerk produced the master rolls of the parish and said, "Scribe, you'll make fair copies for the crown," I did what careful men do. I said yes.

My debt bound me to the temple — ten years' service for my schooling, four years remaining — but debts are negotiable things when compared to heads. I sat down with the parish rolls and began copying names into the registry: the miller's son who hears the river change its mind; old Maren who knows storms three days out because the peaks gossip about weather like washerwomen; Tam Wicker, whose gift is so small he mostly hears his own chickens disapproving of him.

Forty-one names. Forty-two, counting mine, which I did not write. A careful man omits himself from lists. A careful man keeps his letters uniform, his margins true, his face arranged into the mild blankness of a man thinking only about ink.

For nine days, I was very careful.

* * *

The trouble was a child named Pell Ashdown, age eleven, listed between his grandmother and a goatherd, his talent noted in my own fair hand: *hears where lost things are.* On the tenth morning, Pell did not come to the well, or the chapel, or anywhere else. His grandmother beat on the temple doors until Abbot Coss relented, and I stood in the cloister and watched two grey riders walk her back down the hill, and I understood with a clarity that felt almost physical: the registry was not a census. It was a harvest schedule.

That night I could hear the mountain more clearly than usual. It does this when something is wrong — the way a house goes quiet when someone dies in it. It kept saying a word I didn't have a translation for, something like *underneath*, something like *soon*. I pressed my palms flat against the chapel floor and listened until my ears rang, and the mountain told me, in its slow geological way, that there were new holes in the world. Small ones. Child-shaped.

* * *

A careful man would have done nothing. A careful man would have reasoned that forty-one names were beyond saving, that falsifying one line endangered all of them, that Pell was perhaps already past any help a scribe with an inkpot could offer. I lay awake in my cell running through every argument for caution, and they were all excellent arguments, and I want it recorded that I heard every single one.

Then I thought about Pell's line in the registry. *Hears where lost things are.* Eleven years old, and the crown had found a use for him.

At dawn I mixed two inks — a trick of the trade, one that dries to match aged parchment — and I turned to the Ashdown entry, and above it I wrote a death notice in the margin, dated eight days prior, with a cause of fever, witnessed by no one, attested by the parish priest who happened to be me. Then I struck the name itself with a single clean line, the way clerks do, and beneath it wrote: *deceased — entry void.*

It took perhaps four minutes. Four minutes to end a life I had spent thirty years building. Because here is what I knew, lying the ink onto the page with hands that did not shake until afterward: Serreck audits everything. She would check the parish burial roll against my margin note, find no record, and then she would look up from the ledger at the scribe who made it, and she would know. Maybe not prove. But know.

There is a version of Edmund Ishikawa who sands the join smooth, omits the death notice, lets the child be harvested, and lives out his four years in careful silence until the mountain finally talks loud enough to crack his skull open with guilt. He is a good man, that version. Efficient. Dead inside in the way ledgers are dead.

I am apparently not him.

* * *

Serreck found the discrepancy herself, of course. She came to the scriptorium at dusk with the registry under her arm and set it down in front of me with terrible gentleness.

"Scribe. The Ashdown boy. Your note says he died of fever."

"The winter fevers run early in the high parishes, captain." My voice came out level. Ten years of copying scripture teaches you to read aloud in a voice that means nothing.

"Mm." She turned pages. "No burial entry."

"We bury quick here. Ground's hard by midwinter."

"And the priest who attended him?"

"Me. We're short-handed."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I looked back with the mild blank face, and somewhere behind my ribs my heart was doing the work of a blacksmith. Then she closed the book.

"I'll want to see the body."

Of course she would. Of course.

"He's buried," I said. "Up the slope. Frozen ground, captain — I'd need a day and two men and a pick."

"You'll have them tomorrow." She gathered the registry, and paused at the door, and said, without turning around: "You have steady hands, scribe. Steadier than most. It's a thing I notice."

She left. I stood in the scriptorium while the light died, listening to the mountain underneath us grind out its word again — *soon, soon* — and I did the arithmetic of a careless man. One day. One frozen grave I'd have to dig and fill with something, or flee before dawn and confirm everything by absence.

There was a third option, though I hadn't let myself think it yet. There was a smuggler's road over the passes that the maps pretended wasn't there, and a door at the end of it that everyone in Thornfell swore was a story, and a province full of names on lists who would need, fairly soon, someone who knew how to erase a line.

I packed that night. Ink, flint, the false-bottomed strongbox where I'd hidden my mother's hearing-charms, and one last thing: the original parish roll, torn from its binding, because a careful man takes evidence.

At the gate, in the blue hour before dawn, I looked back once at the temple where I owed four more years. The debt, I decided, would have to be paid in another currency.

The mountain, beneath my boots, said: *run.*
