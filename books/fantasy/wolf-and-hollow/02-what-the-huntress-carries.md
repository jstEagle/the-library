## 2. What the Huntress Carries

The order came down on the third morning, written in a hand so neat it looked apologetic: *One scribe, Edmund Ishikawa, fled the temple at dusk. Track and return. Alive, if convenient.*

*If convenient.* I'd been carrying that phrase around like a stone in my boot for two days before I understood what bothered me about it. Not "alive." Not "unharmed." Convenient. The crown had stopped pretending, somewhere in the last year, that we were anything but a convenience service. We fetched things. We buried things. And when the fetching and burying were the same thing, nobody wrote that part down.

I knew how to read between lines. I'd been raised on it.

So I rode out of Thornfell's grey streets with six days' provisions and no intention of using more than three, because I already knew where he was going. Everyone who'd served the suppression knew about the Hollow Door — not as a place, but as a rumor we were ordered to mock. *A myth,* Commander Hale said, with the particular flatness she reserved for things that were true. *Tell your patrols it's a smuggler's tale. Smugglers love a door that isn't there.*

She'd said it while looking directly at me, which was her way of saying: *you know better, and I know you know better, and neither of us will say so out loud.*

That was four years ago. I was twenty-two and very good at not saying things out loud.

* * *

I found him on the second day, on the old salt road where the pines close in and the snow lies blue in the shadows. He wasn't running anymore. Running requires calories, and he'd clearly run out of those some distance back. He was sitting against a boulder with his hands in his lap like a man waiting for an audience with a king, except his lips were the color of tallow and his eyes kept sliding sideways to track me when he thought I wasn't looking.

Which was its own kind of impressive. Most people can't tell when I'm not looking.

"Vess Arden," he said, when I dismounted. His voice came out cracked, like a door in a house nobody lived in. "You're the one they sent."

"I'm the one they sent," I agreed.

"You wrote the order yourself." He held up a scrap of paper — my own dispatch, torn from the relay post. "Your hand. I copied beside it for ten years. I know everyone's hand in that temple. Yours is the only one that presses harder on the left side of the page. Like you're angry at the margin."

I should have arrested him then. That was the job. That was the whole architecture of my life: there are orders, and you follow them, and the following is the living. Instead I crouched down, unwrapped my bread, and broke it in half.

"Ten years copying registries," I said. "And you spent them studying handwriting."

"Studying everything." He took the bread with both hands, the way starving men do, ashamed of the hunger and hungrier for it. "You learn things, transcribing. Who writes small because they're hiding something. Who crosses their t's like knife cuts. Who adds a name to a list and then sits very still afterward, hoping no one counts twice."

My mouth went dry. Because I had counted twice. The night the registry went missing from the archive — the night before the witch-hunters arrived — I was the one who reported the gap in the seal. My report is why the hunters came early. My report is why a child on that list had to vanish, and why a careful man decided to be careless, and why I was now sitting on a frozen road feeding him my rations instead of dragging him back in chains.

He watched me work all that out. I could see him watching. Scribes do that — they watch you arrive at the truth the way other people watch weather come over a ridge.

"You falsified a line," I said.

"And you reported a broken seal." He shrugged, thin shoulders under a temple robe gone stiff with frost. "We've both been busy."

* * *

Here is what I carried into Thornfell, the first time, eight years ago. A writ, a company of forty, and a firm belief that I was going to help people.

They told us the gifted were a danger to themselves. That the registry was mercy — that naming them meant protecting them, schooling them, keeping them from being lynched by frightened villages. I believed it the way you believe a floor will hold you. You don't examine it. You just keep walking.

Then came the winter of the cullings. I stood watch outside a farmhouse in the Vellows while they took a woman named Sera Brant away for hearing her dead husband in the walls of her own kitchen. She went quietly. She thanked the soldiers. She asked if someone would feed her goats, and I said yes, I would, and I did, for three days, until the order came down to burn the farm as unsanitary. The goats first. Then the farm.

I fed her goats. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever ledger the dead keep. I fed her goats, and then I burned her home, and I told myself both acts were duty, and the telling worked for about six years.

Commander Hale promoted me twice. She said I had a talent for clean operations. What she meant was that I had a talent for not asking what happened to the people after the paperwork closed. It's a real skill. It takes practice. Some nights I still feel it atrophying in me like a limb I cut off myself.

* * *

"So the Door," he said, when he could walk again. We'd made camp in a shepherd's hut with half a roof, and he'd gotten enough heat into himself to be dangerous. "You're not taking me back."

"No."

"Why?"

I fed the stove another stick and considered lying to him. It would have been easy. He was weak, I was armed, and liars lie best to people who can't check the story. But there was something in the way he waited — patient, attentive, like the answer mattered more than his survival — that made lying feel like shouting in a chapel.

"Because I burned a farm once," I said, "with the livestock inside. Because I've written reports that turned women into entries. Because I'm thirty years old and I have never once done the thing I actually believed in, and I'm tired, scribe. I am so tired."

He nodded slowly. "Sera Brant," he said.

The cold went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. "How do you—"

"She heard her husband in the walls. The registry listed her gift as 'auditory haunting, class two.' I copied that line eleven times. Eleven registries, eleven provinces, one woman reduced to a classification I invented a footnote for because the format didn't have room for grief." He looked at the fire. "I never met her. But I carried her for years too. Isn't that strange? You burned her house and I filed her away, and here we are, both of us still holding pieces of her."

"That's not strange," I said. "That's just what the crown does. It makes sure everyone carries something."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, exhausted and crooked, but it was the first honest thing either of us had produced since the road began.

"The Hollow Door," he said again. "You know where it is."

"I know where the maps stop being maps." I pulled out the patrol chart, the one with the western passes marked in the cartographer's euphemism — *unreliable terrain* — which is what the crown writes on places it has decided to forget. "And I know what Hale told us. That it's a myth. That anyone who goes looking dies stupid in the snow." I looked up at him. "But I also know that Hale lies the way other people breathe, and that she only calls things myths when she's posted guards near them."

His eyes sharpened. "You think she knows it's real."

"I think the crown erased Thornfell from every map in the kingdom after the rebellion, and you don't erase a province unless there's something in it worth erasing." I rolled the chart closed. "Rest. Tomorrow we go west, into the unreliable terrain."

"And if the Door is truly a myth?"

"Then we die stupid in the snow," I said, "but at least we'll be liars to the end. Consistent, at least."

* * *

We walked for three days through country that got less real the further west we went. The roads gave up. The trees grew wrong — leaning east, all of them, like a crowd flinching from something. And Edmund changed. I don't know how else to put it. The closer we came to whatever he was fleeing toward, the more he listened, and I mean listened with his whole body, head tilted, stopping mid-stride to stand in the snow like a man hearing music from a house that shouldn't have any.

"What is it?" I asked him, on the third evening, when he stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his back.

"The mountain," he said simply.

"The mountain."

"It's unhappy." He said it without embarrassment, without performance, the way you'd report a limp. "There's weight above us. Old snow on new ice. It wants to come down and we're standing where it wants to land." He glanced at me, and for the first time since the road, he looked afraid — not of me, not of the hunters I could hear behind us now, faint bugle-calls threading through the pass. Of this. Of the thing he'd hidden for ten years, finally useful, finally fatal. "This is what they hunt us for, Vess. Not fireballs. Not curses. This. Hearing."

Behind us, the bugles sounded again, closer. Ahead, the pass narrowed toward a wall of black stone that was not on any map I'd ever been given — and carved into its face, half-drifted with snow, was a shape I had been told my whole career did not exist.

A door.

Edmund touched my arm. "Hunters behind," he said quietly. "Door ahead. And Vess — the mountain knows we're here."

From the shadow beneath the stone, something else knew it too. Figures. Still as grave-markers, watching us from the drifts — and among them, stepping forward with her sword drawn and her pardon already in her voice, Commander Hale herself.

"Sergeant Arden," she called across the snow. "Bring me his head, and come home."
