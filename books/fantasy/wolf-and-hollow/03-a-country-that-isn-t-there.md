## 3. A Country That Isn't There

Commander Hale looked exactly as I remembered her: grey at the temples, iron everywhere else. Ten years since she'd pinned my sergeant's badge on me and told me the law was a blade and I was lucky to be its edge. She'd never once lied to me that I knew of. She'd never needed to.

"Sergeant Arden." The word *sergeant* was a kindness and a knife both — she was reminding me of what I'd been before Thornfell took my name off the rolls. "Bring me his head, and come home."

Behind her, six hunters in crown-grey, breath steaming, crossbows levelled. Behind them, the pass we'd climbed for three days, and behind *that*, somewhere below the snowline, the men who'd burned the registry office with the scribes still inside it.

I laughed. It came out wrong — thin, high, the laugh of a woman who has just understood the shape of her own life. Hale's eyes flicked to Edmund, then back to me, cataloguing. She'd always been good at reading people. She read me now like a confession half-written.

"You found him," she said. "On a smuggler's road, in a dead province, walking toward a door that doesn't exist. And you didn't arrest him."

"I was tracking him," I said. "Cautiously."

"Cautiously," she repeated, tasting it. "For nine days?"

Edmund shifted beside me. He had his hands visible, palms out, the posture of a man who'd spent ten years being harmless on purpose. But he wasn't looking at Hale. He was looking past her, at the figures standing in the drifts — still as grave-markers, frost on their shoulders, no tracks leading to or from them.

"Hale," he said, and his voice did something strange — went soft, went *listening*. "You should tell your people not to shoot the ghosts."

"They aren't ghosts," Hale said flatly. "They're rebels. Deserters. The last of the Hollow Company, holed up in these passes for twenty years like rats."

One of the figures moved. Just slightly. Tilted its head.

"Ma'am," I said carefully, "with respect — those are corpses. Or they were. They've got snow *through* them."

And they did. Where the wind lifted their cloaks you could see the rockface behind, sharp where it should have been blurred. Twenty years dead and standing watch anyway. The rebellion hadn't ended at Thornfell. It had just stopped bothering with bodies.

Hale didn't look at them. That was the thing about my commander — she'd decided long ago what the world contained, and anything outside the list simply wasn't there. A country erased from the maps was a country that couldn't be seen. Ghosts were weather. I used to admire that in her.

"The pardon is real, Vess," she said, quieter now, the voice she used for soldiers worth saving. "Full restoration. Your name back on the rolls. Your mother's pension reinstated — yes, I know about the pension, don't look surprised, I signed off on the file myself when they cut you loose. Come home. Bring me his head, and none of this ever happened to you."

My mother's pension. Eleven crowns a month, paid out of guilt for a war she'd cooked in for the quartermasters until the fever took her hearing and then nearly everything else. Hale had done her homework. Hale always did.

"That's the offer?" I said. "A head and eleven crowns?"

"It's an *amnesty*."

"It's a price tag."

"Everything is," Hale said, without a flicker. "The only question is whether you're the one selling or the one being sold. You're standing in a dead country next to a debt-slave scribe, Sergeant. You've already been sold. This is your one chance to buy yourself back."

And here is what I will say about myself, since no one else is left to say it: I am a liar by trade, but I have never once lied to myself about arithmetic. Six crossbows against two people and a handful of the unburied dead. There was no version of this where we walked away whole.

But there was a version where *he* did. Maybe.

So I did the only clever thing left in me. I turned to Edmund, loud enough for Hale to hear every word, and said: "Tell her what you told me. About what the crown did to the gifted here. Tell her what's under the temple at Hollow's Reach."

"Vess—" Edmund started.

"Or don't," said Hale, "because it won't matter. Whatever fairy story he's told you—"

"He hears mountains, Commander." I watched her face. Nothing. Not even a blink. "People whose blood remembers things. The gifted. You hunted them out of Thornfell in '61 and you wrote the reports saying there weren't any. I carried some of those reports. I *wrote* some of them."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes then. Small. Buried deep, like everything in that woman. But it moved.

"Stand aside, Sergeant. Last order I'll give gently."

"No," I said.

It was such a small word for how enormous it felt leaving me. Ten years of service, a badge, a pension, my mother's name cleared — all of it balanced on one syllable, and I spent it like it was nothing.

Hale sighed. Genuinely sad, I think, in whatever way she was capable of sadness. "Crossbows," she said.

* * *

EDMUND

I have heard stone my whole life. Most people think that means rumbling, grinding, the great slow voice of the earth. It doesn't. Mostly it means listening to something that thinks in centuries and considers a landslide a passing mood. Stone is patient. Stone forgives almost nothing.

When the crossbows came up, I stopped listening to the hunters entirely and put my whole self into the mountain.

And the mountain said: *they are standing where the snow does not want them.*

I want to explain what that's like. Imagine someone whispering a secret in a language you love, and knowing you have perhaps forty heartbeats to act on it, and knowing that acting means opening a door that has been shut for twenty years and holding it open with your own hands while winter tries to close it over your fingers.

The Door wasn't a door, exactly. It was a place where the mountain had been *persuaded* — old work, rebellion work, done by gifted hands that were ash now. The ghosts stood around it because guarding it was the last promise they'd made. One of them — a woman with a spear through her, the wound gone silver with time — caught my eye and inclined her head. Like a colleague. Like someone clocking in for the same shift.

"Edmund," Vess said, low. "Whatever you're doing, do it faster."

"I'm asking," I said. "Stone takes convincing."

The first bolt flew. Vess moved — I'll give her this, she moved like the stories they tell about huntresses, all economy and fury, dragging me down behind a rib of granite while the shaft buried itself in snow. The second volley came ragged, and the ghost-company stepped between us and it, and the bolts went through them and struck nothing, and the hunters began, quite reasonably, to scream.

Then Hale shouted the word that changed everything. "Loose at will! Burn the threshold!"

They had fire-oil. Of course they did. The crown always brings fire to doors.

It hit the stone and the Door *woke*. Not wide — a crack, a seam of light the width of a hand, spilling warmth into air cold enough to crack teeth. Through it I felt the other side: hollow land, green memory, the smell of a country that maps refused. People were moving beyond it. I could hear them the way I hear everything now — the hunted, the hiding, dozens of hearts pressed up against a door they'd prayed toward for twenty years.

Not wide enough. A child couldn't fit through a hand's width. Nothing living could fit through a hand's width.

"It needs more," I gasped. "It needs — someone to hold it—"

"Then hold it," Vess snarled, shoving me toward the seam, "and I'll handle the rest."

Above us — I heard it before anyone saw it, because of course I did — the mountain cleared its throat.

*Snow,* it said, in the tone of a landlord mentioning rent. *So much snow. So many warm bodies making noise beneath it.*

"Vess!" My voice cracked across the battlefield like a whip. "Avalanche. The whole face of it. Two minutes, maybe less. Everyone under the cornice dies — everyone."

She looked at me. At the Door. At Hale, already shouting retreat orders to soldiers who couldn't outrun falling winter.

"Can you hold it open that long?"

"With what I spend? No." I put my hands on the seam and pushed. "But I'm going to anyway."

* * *

VES

He held it. Gods help me, he held it.

The seam widened under his hands like a wound deciding to heal in reverse — a shoulder's width, then a body's, light pouring out, and through it the faces of the hidden ones, pressing forward, and Edmund screaming at them in a voice I'd never heard him use: "*Through! Now! All of you, through!*"

They came. Old men carrying children. Women with frostbitten feet running on them anyway. The ghosts stood aside and saluted each one with weapons twenty years rusted, and I stood with my sword drawn between Edmund and the hunters, and for the first time since I was sixteen years old I was exactly where I belonged.

Hale didn't run. That was the last thing she taught me, though she never meant to. When the mountain roared, when her soldiers broke and fled downslope, she stood her ground in the rising white and looked at me across the snow with her pardon still folded in her hand.

"Sergeant," she said. Almost gently.

"Wrong name," I said, and tore the badge off my collar, and dropped it in the snow between us.

Then the cornice let go, and the world came down white and roaring, and I turned my back on the law that raised me, and pulled the man who hears mountains through the last armful of falling sky—

—except I didn't. Because he wasn't beside me anymore.

He was still at the Door. Still holding it. Still pushing the final stragglers through — a limping boy, a blind old woman — while the avalanche ate the distance between him and eternity.

"EDMUND!"

He looked at me over his shoulder, snow filling the air between us, and smiled like a man settling an account.

"Debt's paid," he said. "Go on through, Vess. Somebody has to witness it."

The white closed over him like a page turning, and the Door began to close, and I did the worst and best thing I have ever done in my life.

I turned around and went back in after him.
