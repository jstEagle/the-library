## 4. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The Door did not open the way doors open. It opened the way a wound opens — reluctantly, with a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it in my ears, a groan of stone remembering what it had been asked to forget.

I put my hands against it and listened.

That was all my gift had ever been, in the end. Not magic as the witch-hunters understood it, not fire or flight or any of the pretty horrors from their registry. Just listening. The mountain spoke constantly, a low litany of stone and water and old cold, and for thirty years I had been the only one in Thornfell who heard it. The temple called it a small talent. A merciful one. They were wrong on both counts, but they would be proven wrong on the second count tonight.

*Hold,* the mountain said, or something that was not words but meant them. *Hold, and I will remember how to be open.*

So I held.

The first of them came through at dusk. An old woman with a loom-shuttle still tucked into her apron, as if she might finish the weaving she'd abandoned on the far side of the world. Then a family of stonemasons whose names I had copied into the registry myself, my own handwriting turned against them. Then a boy of perhaps twelve who looked at me with the particular terror of someone who has learned that being noticed is fatal.

"You're the scribe," he whispered.

"I was," I said. "Go through."

He hesitated. Children always hesitate at the edge of large decisions; adults have usually already made theirs and are simply waiting for permission to admit it. "Will it hurt?"

I considered lying. I was, by then, quite practiced at it. "The crossing? No. What comes after? I can't say. But it won't be here."

He went through. Behind him came more — the gifted, the suspected, the merely related-to-the-suspected, a whole province's worth of people the crown had decided were problems rather than persons. And every one of them passed through a Door that stayed open only because I kept listening to a mountain that was trying, with the last of its strength and mine, to remember an older shape.

Here is what spending a gift feels like: it does not burn. It thins. Each hour the mountain's voice grew fainter, the way a song grows fainter when you walk away from it, until I was straining toward sounds I could no longer be certain weren't my own blood moving in my own ears. I catalogued what I was losing the way I once catalogued names — methodically, in the neat hand the temple had praised. The deep hum of granite under load. Water finding its way through limestone. The particular creak a mountainside makes three days before it decides to shrug.

That last one mattered. Because the mountain, thinning even as it held itself open, managed one more sentence:

*They are coming up the valley. Many boots. And the snow above you is tired.*

* * *

Vess found me kneeling at the threshold, blood in my nose, both palms flat against stone gone warm as fever.

"How many?" she said. She didn't ask what was happening. She had stood at this Door her whole career pretending it was a myth, and now that it was real she wasted no time on astonishment. That was Vess. Astonishment was a luxury; triage was a discipline.

"Forty hunters. Maybe more. Commander Hale's banner." I wiped my face and left red on my sleeve. "The mountain says the slope above the pass is ready to fall. It says it will fall if provoked."

"Can you provoke it?"

"It's not a dog, Vess. It's a country. And besides—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Besides, provoking things is nearly finished for me. Can't you hear it? The quiet?"

She went very still. Huntresses notice sound the way priests notice sin. "The wind's stopped."

"The wind hasn't stopped. I've stopped hearing the wind." I said it plainly because there was no kind version. "Whatever I am, I'm spending it. Every minute this Door stands open costs me a piece. When the last of them is through, there won't be anything left to spend."

Vess looked at the line of refugees still winding down the smuggler's road — torches strung out like a rosary, families carrying what little the crown hadn't confiscated, which was each other. Then she looked at me. Then she did the thing I would think about for the rest of my life, in whatever years I was granted.

She reached into her coat and took out her pardon.

Hale had written it herself. I had seen it once, in chapter three of our misfortunes, when she offered Vess everything back — rank, name, the law that raised her — in exchange for my head. Heavy paper, crown seal, the works. Vess had carried it since. I had assumed sentiment. Or temptation. With Vess it was hard to tell the difference, and she preferred it that way.

"What are you doing?" I said.

"What the document deserves." She struck flint to the torch she'd taken off a fleeing mason. "It's a lie with a seal on it. I've been carrying lies with seals on them for eleven years. I'm retiring."

"Pardon is worth more than my—"

"Your gift?" She fed the corner of the parchment to the flame and watched it catch. "It was never mine to trade. That's the whole point of a pardon, Edmund. It makes you believe your life belongs to whoever wrote it." The fire climbed. Hale's careful script curled and blackened, the crown seal going last, wax bubbling like something wounded. "It doesn't. It never did. I learned that too late and in bad company."

Wind came down the pass and took the ashes. All of them. Not one fleck drifted back toward Thornfell, and if you want an omen, huntress, take that one.

"You could still go through," I said. "Clean slate on the other side. New country. No debt."

"I served a country that erased another one from its maps." She crouched beside me at the threshold, shoulder against mine, facing out toward the coming hunters the way soldiers face things — which is to say without any expression whatsoever. "Somebody should stay behind and remember it existed."

* * *

The last refugee crossed at moonrise — a deaf old man who couldn't hear my instructions and read them instead off my lips, which felt like a benediction of some kind. Then the road was empty, and the boots on it were the wrong boots.

Hale came up the pass with forty men and a lantern held high, and she saw us — the disgraced huntress and the debt-bound scribe — sitting at the foot of a Door that glowed like a held breath.

"Ishikawa," she called. "Step away from the Door. Vess, arrest him. Your pardon still stands."

"It's ash," Vess said. "You're welcome to check the wind."

For a moment — just one — something moved behind Hale's face. Not doubt. Doubt would have required imagination. But something adjacent to it, the flicker of a woman realizing her offer had been refused not out of fear but out of arithmetic. Vess had done the sums and found the crown wanting.

Then Hale drew steel, because that is what the law does when it runs out of arguments, and the mountain, having waited politely through the entire negotiation, ran out of patience.

I didn't provoke it. I want that recorded somewhere, in some registry of small mercies. I only stopped holding the slope together — the way you might stop holding shut a door that was always trying to open. The snowpack above the pass had been asking to come down for three days. I let it answer.

The sound was not loud. That was the terrible part. It was the softest thing I have ever failed to hear — a white hush rolling down the valley like a bedsheet pulled over a body, and where Hale's forty lanterns had been there was suddenly nothing but smooth moonlit snow, unmarked, unbothered, already beginning to look eternal.

The Door closed behind us with a click so small it might have been a lock turning in an empty house.

And the silence where the mountain's voice used to be settled over me like snow of its own.

* * *

Spring came late that year, and quietly, which suited us.

We built on the ruins of a shrine two valleys over — one the maps forgot, though for different reasons than Thornfell. There was stone enough standing to build around, timber enough downhill to haul, and water enough singing in the melt to make even a deaf man feel accompanied. Because that's what I am now. Deaf to the world beneath the world. The mountain speaks constantly and I hear nothing at all, and some mornings I grieve it like a dead sibling, and other mornings I think: *every temple begins with someone who finally stopped listening to the wrong voice.*

Vess raised the walls. I copied nothing — my scribbling days are done, or rather repurposed. Now I write letters the refugees send back through the passes, carried by traders who pretend not to know what they carry. Names, mostly. Births. The old weaver finished her cloth on the far side and sent half of it back, addressed to "the scribe who stayed." Vess pretends not to be moved by these things. She is very bad at pretending, for a professional.

Yesterday a girl arrived at our half-built door — twelve or thirteen, mud to the knees, alone, with the look of someone who has learned that being noticed is fatal. She wouldn't give her name. She said, instead, in a whisper, that she could hear the river talking, and did we know anyone who'd believe her?

Vess looked at me. I looked at Vess.

"Come in out of the cold," I said. "We'll start a new registry. Of small mercies. You'll be the first name in it — and this time, the ink protects you."

She stepped inside. Behind her, far off, the mountain said something I could not hear.

But I felt it smile.
