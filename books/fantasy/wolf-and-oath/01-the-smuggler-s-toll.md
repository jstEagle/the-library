## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

There are three rules to the Thornfell mountains, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way this range teaches anything.

One: the mountain does not hate you. Hatred requires attention, and the Thornfell cannot be bothered.

Two: every path you can see is a path someone wants you to take, which means it's a path someone will be watching.

Three: when the wind starts singing in a register that makes your back teeth ache, stop walking and start praying, because a storm is being born somewhere above you, and it will want a mother.

I was thinking about rule three on the morning we crossed the snowline, because the wind had started humming two days ago and hadn't stopped since. A long, low note, like a struck bell underwater. Most people would call it weather. I called it an invitation — one I had spent six years refusing.

"Yara." Doss fell into step beside me, his boots crunching the frost crust in that deliberate way of his, as if the mountain owed him money and he wanted it to know he'd come collecting. "You've been staring at the sky like it insulted your mother."

"It hummed at me."

"The sky."

"The wind."

He squinted upward at a sky the color of old pewter, featureless from ridge to ridge. "Sounds like wind to me."

"That," I said, "is exactly what worries me."

Doss was my employer, if you could call a man who paid in suspicion and dried mutton an employer. He ran goods through the Thornfell for people who preferred their commerce unrecorded — salt, iron, medicine, occasionally people, though I made him swear off that trade before I signed on. Six years I'd been guiding caravans through passes that didn't exist on any map worth stealing, and in six years Doss had never once asked why a woman who could read stone and cloud and the mood of the wind like other folk read letters had ended up doing this work for coin barely worth the cold.

He didn't ask now, either. That was the arrangement. Competence in exchange for incuriosity. It was, I reflected, the closest thing to intimacy I'd managed since the exile, and there are worse marriages.

Behind us, eleven mules picked their way up the switchback with the resigned patience of creatures who had learned that complaining achieved nothing. Behind the mules came eight men and a boy named Pip who was fifteen and lied about it constantly. The caravan carried dye-stuffs and surgical steel and, hidden in the false bottom of the third wagon, forty pounds of something Doss refused to name and I refused to ask about. Rule of my own: I guide the road, not the cargo. The day I start caring what's under the floorboards is the day someone pays me to care, and then I'm not a smuggler anymore. I'm a weapon with a price tag.

The pass I'd chosen — the Hollowman's Stair, though the name is mine; no one else living remembers it — threaded between two faces of granite so close together that in places the mules had to turn sideways. Snow lay in the creases like flour sifted into a baker's knuckles. Above us, the ridgeline ran jagged against the pewter sky, and above *that*, nothing visible, which was precisely where the humming lived.

"You feel it too," Doss said. Not a question this time.

"I told you. The wind's humming."

"And that means?"

"It means the storm's still deciding." I kept my voice flat, bored, the way you talk about a wolf seen at a distance. "Could break tonight. Could wander off east and drown some poor valley in Vessia instead. Storms are like cats."

"You're lying."

"I'm a smuggler, Doss. Lying is most of the job description."

But he was right, which annoyed me. The truth was that the storm wasn't deciding anything. Storms don't decide. They gather, they hunger, they wait — and this one was gathering around *me* specifically, the way iron filings find lodestone, because six years ago I had stood on a hill in the country of my birth and called down lightning until the sky itself seemed to tear, and the sky, apparently, holds grudges longer than kings do.

Exile teaches you things. It taught me that home is a word that stops meaning a place and starts meaning a wound. It taught me that a talent is just a debt you were born owing, and someone always comes to collect. And it taught me that the safest distance between yourself and your own power is measured in years of careful, grinding, daily refusal — and that the distance is never quite far enough.

We made the high shelf by late afternoon: a bowl of bare stone ringed by walls, with a spring frozen into a sculpture of blue ice and just enough shelter from the wind to keep the tents honest. Good ground. I'd slept on it four times before, and four times the mountain had let me.

That should have been my first warning. The Thornfell has a memory like a spurned lover. It lets you in twice, maybe three times, and then it wants something new.

Pip built the fire while the men watered the mules, and I walked the perimeter out of habit, reading the rock the way you'd read a page — the grain of the stone, the seams, the places where meltwater had gone and therefore would go again. Everything orderly. Everything quiet. The hum overhead had deepened by a half-tone, the way a singer leans into a chorus, but I'd heard deeper. We'd be fine.

Which is when I saw the smoke.

Not ours. Ours was a small, disciplined fire, fed dry scrub, burning low behind a windbreak. This smoke rose from the notch at the eastern lip of the shelf — thin, black, greasy. Tar-smoke. Signal smoke.

I stood very still and did arithmetic. There was no settlement within a day's walk. No shepherd burned tar; shepherds burn wood and dung and wish they had tar. Tar-smoke meant soldiers, or hunters, or the particular species of bandit who liked to dress as both. And it meant they already knew our position, because nobody burns signal smoke at a checkpoint that hasn't found its quarry.

"Doss," I said, when I got back to camp. "Kill the fire."

He looked at the flames, then at me, then at the flames again. In eight seconds flat our little disciplined blaze was dirt and steam, and eight men and a boy were looking at me with the particular expression of people who have just remembered that their guide is also the most dangerous thing on this mountain.

"Checkpoint?" Doss asked quietly.

"Worse. Ambush wearing a checkpoint's clothes." I crouched and drew in the ash with a stick: the shelf, the eastern notch, the western wall, the narrow throat of the Stair below. "They'll have the notch held — that's their exit if we bolt downhill, and they'll want us funneled. Crossbows, probably. Maybe twelve men. Maybe twenty."

"How do you know they're not just toll-keepers?"

"Because toll-keepers don't smoke tar at dusk in freezing wind, and because I chose this pass *because* it doesn't have a checkpoint." I tapped the ash-map. "Someone told them where we'd be."

Doss went carefully blank, which among smugglers is a confession. Someone had sold us. Fine. That was a problem for whoever survived till morning.

"So we fight," said Bren, the big one, the one who thought volume equaled courage.

"We die," I corrected. "Eight of us, one boy, open ground, crossbows above us. Do you see the word *fight* anywhere in that sentence? Because I'm having trouble finding it."

"Then what?"

I looked at the notch. I looked at the sky. The hum had become a chord now, three notes braided tight, and my fingertips had started prickling in that way I hated, the way a missing limb itches, the way a door you nailed shut rattles on its hinges.

"There's a crack in the west wall," I said. "Big enough for mules if we unload them and pass the packs by hand. It comes out on the scree slope above the river gorge. Two hours, in the dark, on ice, with animals that hate us."

"And the men in the notch?"

"Are going to be very confused about where we went."

Bren snorted. "And how do you propose we manage that miracle?"

I should have said: noise, diversion, a decoy fire on the far side, luck. All true. All insufficient. What I actually said was nothing, because in that moment the wind dropped dead — total silence, the whole shelf holding its breath — and every man in camp heard what I heard underneath the silence: the chord resolving toward a single, patient, waiting note.

Directly above us.

"Oh," I said softly. "It followed me here."

"Yara." Doss's voice had gone careful. "What followed you?"

"A storm that's been humming my name for two days." I stood. My hands were shaking, which was inconvenient, because I was about to need them. "Get the packs moving. Now. Everyone in the crack inside ten minutes or I leave without you, and I mean that with love."

They moved. To their credit — and smugglers' credit is a debased currency, but still — they moved. Mules unloaded, packs passed hand over hand into the dark seam of the western wall, Pip cursing in three registers as he shoved the last stubborn animal through. I stayed outside, alone, facing the notch and the sky, doing the thing I was worst at in all the world.

Deciding.

Because here is the truth about exile that no decree says out loud: they don't banish your body. They banish the part of you that mattered. Six years ago, the Council of my order had looked at what I could do — what I *had* done, one terrified night of thunder and grief — and pronounced it unnatural, unbound, a corruption. The storm I'd called had killed three men. The storm had also, though no one weighed this in the scales, put out a wildfire that would have taken a village. The scales of exile do not balance. They simply fall the way the powerful push them.

Since then, I'd touched the power maybe five times. Flickers. A gust to fill a sail. A chill to keep meat sweet. Once, a fog so thick that a patrol rode past us close enough to count the buttons on their coats. Each time, afterward, I'd felt it — the sky leaning closer, interested, remembering me. Like feeding a stray. You feed it once, and it owns your doorstep forever.

Above me, the clouds began to turn. Slowly, majestically, a hole opened in the pewter deck, and beneath it the air went glassy and green-black, and I felt the charge crawl down my spine and lift the small hairs on my arms, and somewhere below in the notch, men started shouting, because they felt it too.

Ten minutes. The caravan needed ten minutes, and I had a sky full of borrowed lightning sitting over my head like a debt collector who knows where I sleep.

"All right," I whispered, to the storm, to myself, to whatever part of me I'd nailed shut six years ago. "A flicker. Just a flicker."

I raised my hands.

I won't pretend it was graceful. Calling weather isn't casting a spell; it's an argument, and I'd gotten rusty at arguing. I reached up into that green-black pressure the way you'd reach into a river, and I took hold of the charge coiled there — just a thread of it, just enough — and I pulled it *sideways*, away from the shelf, away from the mules and the men and the boy, out over the empty gorge to the north, and I said, in the old tongue of my order, the single word that means *fall*.

Lightning hit the scree slope a quarter mile north with a crack that I felt in my molars. Then another strike. Then a third, walking downslope, herding thunder and echo ahead of it, and the sound rolled around the gorge walls and came back doubled, trebled, a war-drum played by a giant, and in the notch above the eastern lip I heard shouting turn to panic — men scrambling, crossbows abandoned or dropped, because no soldier alive holds formation under a lightning strike he can't explain.

The storm wanted more. Of course it did. It pressed down against my grip, vast and delighted, whispering in the register that makes teeth ache: *more, more, you remember how, you remember what you are.* And I remembered. That was the horror of it. Six years of refusal, and the knowledge sat in my hands like it had never left — the exact angle of the pull, the taste of ozone, the word for *fall* and the word for *again*. It wasn't a skill I'd lost. It was a hunger I'd starved, and it had been waiting, patient as winter, for one bad evening on a frozen shelf.

I let go.

The silence afterward rang louder than the thunder. The green-black hole in the clouds sealed itself, grudgingly, like a mouth closing on a withheld remark. My knees buckled and I caught myself on a boulder, gasping, my palms printed red where I'd dug my nails in — I always did that after; it was how I checked I was still only me.

"Yara!" Pip's voice, shrill with terror, from the crack in the wall. "Yara, come *on*!"

I came on. I crawled, mostly, then walked, then climbed, and the caravan pulled me through the last of the seam by the arms, and then we were on the scree slope in the dark with the river roaring far below, and Doss was looking at me the way men look at a fire that has jumped the hearth.

"That was no flicker," he said.

"That was three flickers in a trench coat."

"Yara."

"Three *large* flickers." I wiped my bleeding palms on my coat. "Move. They'll regroup when the thunder dies, and I'd rather be two valleys away before anyone works out that the lightning walked north instead of down."

We moved. Down the scree, along the gorge path, mules slipping and swearing in their silent mule way, men silent in the way of men who have just watched something they'll argue about for years. Pip fell in beside me and didn't say anything for a long time, which for Pip was a eulogy.

Finally: "Was that the storm you got exiled for?"

"No," I said. "The one I got exiled for was bigger."

He swallowed audibly. "How much bigger?"

I looked up. The clouds had closed, ordinary again, pewter and indifferent. But underneath the wind I could still hear it, faint, retreating, unmistakable — that same long note, the one that made my back teeth ache, except now it sounded less like a bell and less like a cat and more like something following.

"Bigger," I said, "than anyone should ever have to answer for."

We walked on into the dark, and behind us, far up the mountain, I heard the faint sounds of soldiers finding an empty camp — and one other sound, small and wrong, that I wouldn't understand until morning: footsteps on the scree, light and steady, keeping pace with us from a half-mile back.

Someone had seen the lightning walk north. Someone had decided to follow the woman who called it.
