## 2. Blood on the Snowline

The fire was a mistake, and I knew it was a mistake, and I built it anyway.

That's the thing about the Thornfell in late autumn: it doesn't kill you with drama. No avalanches, no wolves, nothing so honest. It kills you slowly, by degrees, with cold that seeps through wool and leather and stubbornness alike until one morning you simply don't get up, and the mountain keeps your bones as rent. A small fire, tucked into a hollow behind a shelf of granite, smoke swallowed by pine — that wasn't recklessness. That was bookkeeping. I'd spent three days hauling a smuggler's caravan over the high passes, taken my cut at the snowline, and sent them down toward the lowlands while I turned back alone. My cut included a night of warmth. I intended to collect.

So when I heard footsteps crunching through the scree above my camp, I didn't panic. I did arithmetic.

One set of feet. Uneven. Dragging on the left side, which meant injured or exhausted or both. Coming downhill fast enough to be falling more than walking. Anyone hunting me would come quiet, in pairs, from two directions, because Brannic's crew had talked about me enough over the years that even strangers knew to fear the storm-witch of Thornfell — a reputation I had cultivated with great care and very little truth.

"Whoever you are," I called, not turning from the fire, "you're about to fall off that ledge, and I'm not catching you."

He fell off the ledge anyway.

Not all the way. He caught himself on a juniper root, slid the last few yards in a rattle of stone, and came to rest against a boulder ten feet from my fire like a parcel someone had dropped. Grey cloak, road-filthy. Younger than his posture. And across his ribs, soaking through the grey until it looked black in the firelight, a wound that had no business belonging to a man still upright.

I should mention that I'm not a kind person. Kindness is a luxury for people whose faces aren't known in three provinces. But I've also dragged enough dying men out of gullies to know the sound a body makes when it's done arguing, and this man hadn't made it yet. There's a difference. The done-arguing ones you cover with stones and move on. This one was still spending everything he had left on something, and curiosity has always been my second-worst vice.

"Were you followed?" I asked.

His eyes found me. Good eyes, sharp despite the blood loss, the kind that take inventory before they ask favors. They inventoried me — the scarred staff leaning against my pack, the mountain-worn gear, the face I keep deliberately forgettable — and whatever sum they arrived at, he didn't like it, but he couldn't afford better terms.

"Hunters." The word cost him. "Oathbound. King's."

"Then you've had a bad week," I said, "and it's about to get worse, because I'm leaving."

"Please." He laughed, wet and brief. "You think I'm begging for my life? I gave that up at the ford." His hand went inside his cloak, slow, deliberate, the way you move when you want the armed woman to see exactly what you're reaching for. What he drew out was a scroll case, oilskin-wrapped, sealed at both ends with wax stamped by a signet I didn't recognize — a crown split by a vertical line, which is either very old heraldry or a very pointed joke.

"I need it to go north," he said. "To someone who can read what's in it. The order is gone. Everyone who could—" He coughed, and the cough had blood in it, and I took an involuntary step back that I hated myself for. "You're her. Okonkwo. The smugglers talk. Storm-born, they say. Exiled for a storm."

"They say a lot of things about me. Half of them are flattering and all of them are exaggerated."

"The map knows," he said, and pressed the case into my hands before I could decide whether to let him.

Here's what nobody tells you about moments that change your life: they feel like nothing. No thunderclap, no shiver of destiny. Just a stranger's blood cooling on your fingers while he watches you with the desperate focus of a man trying to finish a sentence before the sentence finishes him. The scroll case was lighter than it should have been, the way important things always are.

"I don't want it," I told him. "Whatever this is — royal business, courier business, any business with hunters attached — I have spent six years being no one's problem, and I intend to die of old age in that condition. Give it to someone else."

"There's no one else." Flat. Not self-pitying. Arithmetic again, the same kind I'd been doing since the footsteps started. "They killed the archivist. They killed Mother Anselm. They killed—" Another cough. "Thirteenth moon," he said instead. "When the thirteenth moon turns, it's done. Sealed. You have to be under it. Under the citadel. Before—"

Boots. Above us, maybe four hundred yards up the switchback, more than one set, moving in the disciplined cadence of people who had trained together and hunted together and never once wondered if they were the villains of somebody else's story.

The courier heard them too. Something in him settled, the way a house settles before it comes down.

"Listen to me." He grabbed my wrist with the last of his grip strength, and it still wasn't much. "The Hollow wears the crown. It eats kings. It ate this one. Everything Aldric decrees now, he decrees with its mouth. The map shows the way under — under, do you understand, beneath the throne there's a door older than the kingdom, and only storm-script opens it, and you're the last one who reads it because they made sure you would be—"

"You're not making sense."

"No," he agreed. "Dying rarely does." He pushed himself up against the boulder just enough to face uphill, toward the boots. "Go. Down the east chute, there's scree, loose all the way to the river. Don't fight them. Whatever you are, don't fight them tonight — there's a captain among them, Vess, she carries an oath-blade, and it will find your storm like a hook finds a fish."

"And what will you do?"

"Buy you ninety seconds." He smiled, and it was a good smile, wasted on its timing. "It's the only thing I've got left to spend. Ninety seconds for you to get the map somewhere they can't follow. Promise me you'll read it. Promise me you won't burn it."

"I promise nothing," I said. "I'm not promising anything to a dead man."

"Not to a dead man," he said. "To the map."

And then he stood up — actually stood, swaying, hand pressed to the ruin of his ribs — turned to face uphill, and shouted into the dark with everything his lungs had left:

"HERE! IT'S HERE! THE COURIER'S HERE!"

* * *

Ninety seconds is longer than you think, if you spend it well.

I spent the first ten standing there like a fool with a scroll case in my hands, because some part of me — the part I keep locked in the cellar where I put the storm and everything else about myself I don't look at directly — was screaming that this was exactly how it starts. Six years ago it started with weather reports and worried elders, and it ended with my name struck from the rolls and a boat I wasn't allowed to return on. Trouble finds people who carry strange things. The only defense is to never carry anything strange. I had built my whole life on that principle, and here I was holding the strangest thing I'd ever touched while a dying stranger sold his last breaths to buy me time I hadn't asked for.

Twenty seconds. I got the case into my coat, against my chest, where it sat like a second heartbeat.

Thirty seconds. Boots on the switchback now, close, torchlight bleeding through the pines in ugly orange smears. I kicked dirt over my fire — a courtesy to the mountain and a denial of light — and moved for the east chute with my staff in hand.

Forty seconds. From above, the courier's voice, ragged and triumphant, doing its job: taunting them, leading them, making himself loud so they'd believe he was cornered prey rather than bait. I didn't look back. Looking back is how you join him.

Fifty seconds. The east chute opened below me like a throat — fifty yards of loose shale stacked at the angle of least stability, running down toward the black ribbon of the river. Perfectly navigable by a careful climber going slow. Utterly fatal for anyone trying to descend it fast, and equally fatal for anyone trying to climb up it in pursuit, which meant it was a barrier but not an escape, and the hunters would simply circle around and pick up my trail on the far bank.

Unless the chute stopped being a chute.

I want to be clear about what I did next, because I've had six years and a great deal of wine to lie to myself about it, and I'm tired of lying. I didn't slip. I didn't lose control. I chose.

Sixty seconds. I planted my staff, closed my eyes, and reached down into the cellar.

People think storm-calling is about rage. The elders taught it as grief, which is closer, and wrong in a way that ruined me. It's neither. It's listening — listening to the argument the mountain is always having with itself, stone against stone, water against ice, everything straining toward the path of least resistance and never quite taking it. All a caller does is agree with the mountain. Say yes. Say *go ahead.*

Seventy seconds. The mountain said yes.

The chute didn't roar; that's the terrible part. It whispered — a long, granular sigh, thousands of stones deciding at once — and then the whole slope let go of itself and poured downhill like water finding a drain. I rode it on the staff, half-surfing, half-praying, shale hissing around my boots, and above me I heard the hunters' shouts turn from pursuit to alarm to the particular silence of professionals watching their route bury itself.

Eighty seconds. I hit the bottom, rolled, came up on my knees at the river's edge with grit in my teeth and my heart slamming and the taste of ozone on my tongue like a confession.

And the worst thing — the thing I want you to understand — is how good it felt. One flicker. One yes. Six years of careful, cramped, apologetic living, and the moment I opened the door a crack, the power came through me like it had never left, warm and vast and *mine*, and every cell in my body said *more.*

That's why they exiled me. Not for the damage. For how much I liked it.

Ninety seconds. Behind me, up the buried chute, faint through the settling dust, I heard steel leave a scabbard, and then I heard nothing at all, and I understood exactly what those ninety seconds had purchased and who had paid.

* * *

I walked the riverbank until dawn because stopping felt like agreeing with him.

The courier. I never learned his name. He'd shouted himself hoarse directing the hunters to his own position, and when the rockslide cut me off from the sound of it, the sound of it cut me off from the sound of him, and I have constructed several versions since of how those last thirty seconds went — versions where he ran, versions where they took him alive, versions where it was quick. I don't believe any of them. I'm not a liar by nature, whatever else I am.

By full dark I was five miles downstream and shaking, and not from cold. The storm was still awake in me, purring along my nerves like a cat that had gotten a taste of cream, whispering *again, again,* in a voice that sounded suspiciously like my own. I built a fire — a real one this time, defiance and necessity in equal measure — and sat with my hands around it and made myself small until the purring faded.

Then I took out the map.

The oilskin was stiff with old handling. Inside the scroll case lay not a parchment but a sheet of something finer, silk-treated hide, covered in lines that were not ink. At first glance they might have been contour marks, the shorthand cartographers use for elevation — but they moved. Not visibly. Not when I watched. But every time I glanced away and back, the lines had shifted, subtly, the way clouds shift, and I understood with a lurch that the thing in my hands was less a drawing than a creature, or a piece of one, and that it was looking back.

And threaded through the contours, dense and deliberate, was writing I could read.

Storm-script. Actual storm-script, the flowing pressure-marks my grandmother had traced in spilled flour when she thought no one saw, the script the elders burned every copy of after the exile edicts, the script I had spent six years pretending I'd forgotten. I hadn't forgotten. Of course I hadn't. You don't forget your mother tongue; you just stop speaking it out loud and call the silence peace.

*Under the thrones of men lies the door of the sky,* the margin note said, in a hand tight with hurry. *Only the called may open. Only before the thirteenth turn.*

I sat back. Across the fire, the river muttered its endless one-word conversation with the rocks, and somewhere south of me a man was dead or captured, and somewhere north of me a king I'd never met was wearing something on his head that wore *him*, and I — Yara Okonkwo, exile, guide, professional no-one's-problem — was holding the only key.

No. No, no, no. I held a scroll. Scrolls burn.

I fed a stick into the fire and considered it. It would be easy. That was the seductive part — it would be *so easy.* Toss the map in, walk away clean, meet Brannic's caravan at the winter camp, spend the season hauling brandy over passes and telling stories I almost believed. The hunters wanted the map, not me. Burn it and I became boring again. Boring was survival. Boring had kept me alive for six years.

My hand didn't move.

Because underneath the sensible voice, another voice was doing its own arithmetic, and it kept arriving at the same sum: *thirteenth moon.* The courier had died saying it. Hunters had chased him across half a kingdom for a scroll worth killing for, and whatever the Hollow was, it feared this map enough to feed people to mountains to stop it. And I was the last reader. He'd said it like a diagnosis. *They made sure you would be.*

I thought about my order. Two hundred souls scattered across the coast provinces, and after the edicts, scattered further — some fled overseas, some changed their names, and the ones who didn't run fast enough simply stopped sending letters, and eventually we all stopped asking why, because asking questions about the king's decrees is a good way to join the letters-that-stopped.

The fire crackled. The map shifted when I wasn't looking. And the storm purred in its cellar, patient as winter.

"One look," I told the fire, the way you negotiate with yourself when you've already lost. "I'll read it once. Then I decide."

The map, I swear, seemed satisfied.

Far upstream, back along the river toward the buried chute, a single birdcall sounded — twice, paused, twice again. Too regular for a bird. Too far for comfort. I put out the fire with my boot, wrapped the map against my heart where it beat its borrowed second pulse, and stood up in the dark with my staff in my hand.

Someone was following me down the river.

I told myself it was probably just an animal. I was lying, and the map knew it, and the lines shifted anyway.
