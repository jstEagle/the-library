## 4. The Knight Without a Banner

I had been a knight for nineteen years, and in all that time I had learned exactly two things worth teaching: how to hold a shield, and how to lie to myself about why.

The courier's name was Dain Fell. He was twenty-three years old, he chewed mint leaves the way other men chewed tobacco, and he had trusted me with his life because that was the whole of my profession — I was sworn to him the way a dog is sworn to its master, which is to say completely, and without anyone asking the dog's opinion. Three days ago he died on a snowline above the Thornfell passes with a hunter's arrow through his chest and a map pressed into the hands of a stranger, and I was not there, because I had stopped at a shrine to pray, of all the useless, human things.

So now I tracked the stranger. And I told myself, as I climbed, that this was justice. That this was duty. That when I found her, I would take the map back and deliver it as Dain would have wanted, and that the words *by force* were simply an unfortunate logistical detail and not the entire point.

A knight without a banner is just a man with a sword and a grudge. I had both, and I was lying to myself about the third thing, which was grief.

The trail was easy to read if you knew what to look for, and I did. She traveled with smugglers — six of them, maybe seven, moving light and fast through terrain no honest caravan would attempt. But she moved apart from them. Her fire was always built a little downslope from the others, her bedroll always a little farther out. I have watched soldiers do that. It is not caution. It is the posture of someone who has decided the group will be better off if she is the first thing between it and whatever comes in the night.

That told me something about her before I ever saw her face. People who put themselves on the outside of the circle are either protecting it or planning to leave it. Sometimes both.

On the fourth day I caught up to them at a place where the pass narrowed to a throat of grey stone, and I got my first look at Yara Okonkwo.

She was smaller than the stories. The stories — and there were stories, even among men like me, whispered over garrison fires about the storm-caller exiled from the southern coast, the one whose temper cracked the sky — the stories gave her lightning in her eyes and thunder in her voice. What I saw instead was a woman crouched at the edge of a stream, filling waterskins, with wind-chapped cheeks and hair braided back in the practical way of someone who has no one to braid it for. She moved like water finding its level. Every step placed before it was committed.

And she was talking to herself. Or rather — I realized after a moment — to the pack mule, telling it in a low, exasperated voice that if it ate one more thornbush she would personally introduce it to winter early.

I had come four days and thirty miles to kill or capture a legend, and the legend was negotiating with a mule.

*Later*, I told myself. *Watch first. Take her alone, away from the smugglers, so no one else bleeds for your failure.* That was the second lie of the day, and it wasn't even noon.

* * *

The chance came sooner than I expected, and it came wrong.

It was near dusk when the hunters found them — three of the king's oathbound, moving down the scree in that silent, terrible coordination that marks men bound by spell and oath alike. I knew their captain by reputation: Vess, iron-grey, incorruptible, the kind of soldier I might have been if I had ever been good enough. They had the smugglers pinned against the rock wall before anyone heard so much as a bootlace shift. Clean work. Textbook.

Yara Okonkwo was below me at the stream's edge, cut off from her companions, and the youngest hunter — a boy, really, barely old enough to shave the oaths off his tongue — came down the slope toward her with his blade drawn and his form perfect and every intention, I could see, of doing his duty.

Here is what I should have done: nothing. Let the king's law take its course. Then follow the hunters, recover the map from wherever they stored it, and complete my dead charge's errand with clean hands. That was the plan. That was the whole architecture of the last four days.

Instead, when the boy raised his blade, Yara Okonkwo raised her hand — and the sky answered.

Not much of it. A flicker, a stutter of white across the darkening clouds, like a lamp guttering in a draft. Enough light to blind. The boy flinched, shield coming up a half-second too slow, and went down hard on the wet stones, and Yara was on him with a knife at his throat before his helmet stopped ringing.

And then — and I want to be precise here, because precision is the only honesty I have left — she looked at him. Really looked. At the fear in his eyes, at the pulse jumping under the edge of her blade. I was close enough to see her knuckles whiten on the knife's grip. Close enough to see the choice move through her like weather.

She took his blade. She broke it against the rock. She shoved him backward into the shallows and said something too low for me to catch, and then she ran — up the slope, away from the fight, drawing the other two hunters after her and away from the smugglers, taking the danger onto herself exactly as her camp placement had promised she would.

The boy in the stream lay there gasping, alive, disarmed, weeping with what I suspected was relief.

I stood in the rocks above with my hand on my sword hilt and my plan in pieces around me, feeling like a man who has rehearsed a speech and walked into the wrong room.

Dain died carrying that map. Dain died running from these same hunters, and the least I owed him was to finish the run. That was the arithmetic I had been doing for four days, and it still balanced. But arithmetic does not account for a woman who carries a storm in her blood and uses it, at knife's-point, to spare the life of a boy sent to kill her.

I had served the crown for nineteen years. In that time I had executed two deserters, burned a village on written order, and once held a gate closed while families hammered on the other side of it. Each time I told myself the same story: *the law is the law, and the law is the king, and the king is the realm.* It is a comfortable story. It fits like armor, and like armor, its purpose is to keep you from feeling what is happening underneath.

The woman below me had every reason to let that boy bleed. Exile, bounty, the hunters who had chased her since the snowline — I'd read the reports; I'd been reading them since Dain's death, piecing together who she was. She spared him anyway, at cost, at risk, and then she ran uphill into two swords to pull them off her friends.

Knights are supposed to recognize virtue. That is supposedly what the training is for, beneath the drills and the heraldry: to know honor when you see it, even dressed in rags and exile and storm-magic.

I sat down heavily on a cold rock, out of sight, and admitted to the empty mountainside that I had no idea what I was doing anymore. My charge was dead. My oath had died with him — or worse, it hadn't, and I was betraying it right now by sitting still. And somewhere down that slope a woman with lightning under her skin was leading killers away from smugglers, and I had a sword, and no banner, and no earthly idea which direction honor pointed.

The sun went down. The cold came up out of the stones like a tide. Somewhere below, faintly, I heard the mule complaining again.

I decided to follow her. Not to take anything. Not yet. Just to see. To find out what Dain had died carrying, and whether the woman who carried it now was the danger the reports described or something else entirely — and what I would do, God help me, if she turned out to be something else.

It is remarkable, the lies a man can live inside, if he builds them roomy enough. This one I built carefully: *I'm only observing.*

I almost believed it.

* * *

Three nights I followed her, and in three nights she never once knew it, which stung more than I care to admit. Nineteen years of soldiering, and a smuggler's cook nearly caught me twice with nothing but a ladle and suspicious eyes.

I learned things in those three nights. I learned that she kept first watch every night and woke last, and that she checked the horizon before she checked her own gear. I learned that the smugglers called her "stormwitch" without malice — the way you call someone by an old wound they've stopped bleeding from — and that she answered to it with a ghost of a smile that never reached anywhere important. I learned that she slept with a scrap of wax-sealed parchment wrapped oilcloth-tight against her ribs, and that sometimes, thinking herself unwatched, she would take it out and stare at it the way a person stares at a letter they haven't decided to open.

And on the third night, I learned something about myself, which is the discovery a man dreads most on any journey.

We had made camp in a hollow below a ridge, and I had taken my position on the ridge out of habit, and from there I could hear the low murmur of voices — hers and an older smuggler's, a leathery man they called Brannoc. Most of it was lost to the wind. But the wind carries what it wants, and what it brought me was a fragment:

"—sell it," Brannoc was saying. "Whatever it is, someone paid good blood for it already. Let it be their problem."

And her answer, quiet, tired, in a voice I was beginning to know too well: "If I sell it, whoever buys it walks into whatever killed Dain Fell. And if I burn it—" A long pause. "I don't know what happens if I burn it."

"You don't owe the dead man anything."

"No," she said. "But he chose me. Out of everyone on that mountain, he crawled to me." Another pause, longer, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Brannoc, everyone who ever chose me ended up paying for it."

I sat on that ridge in the dark with frost forming on my shoulders, and I thought about oaths.

Mine had been simple once: protect the courier, carry the message, serve the crown that issued both. Dain was dead. The message was in the wrong hands. The crown — and here was the thing I had been circling for four days like a bird over open water — the crown had sent the hunters who put the arrow in him. I had seen the fletching. I had pulled it from his chest myself, and I had known royal fletching the way you know your own handwriting.

So either Dain had betrayed his king, or his king had betrayed Dain, and I had spent nineteen years learning to tell the difference between treason and truth, and lately the line had begun to move under my feet like a riverbank in flood.

There were decrees coming out of the capital that made no sense. There were villages struck from the rolls. There were orders signed in the king's seal for things the Aldric I had served in my banner-years — brief, distant, but real — would never have sanctioned. Men said the king was ill. Men said the king was changed. Men said it quietly, in the tone men use for things they've decided not to think about.

And a dying courier had run thirty miles through hostile country, past a dozen safer hands, to press a sealed map into the arms of a storm-caller and gasp about a thirteenth moon.

None of it fit. All of it fit. Both things were true, and the space between them was where I now lived.

Below me, Yara Okonkwo folded the map back into its oilcloth, tied it against her ribs, and took first watch, facing outward, alone, the way she always did.

I looked at her — this exiled woman carrying a dead man's secret through the mountains, sparing the lives of the men sent to kill her, refusing to sell, afraid to burn — and I felt something crack loose in my chest that had been set wrong since the snowline. It was not quite grief. Grief I knew. This was adjacent to it. Closer, maybe, to shame, or to hope, or to whatever name you give the moment you realize the story you've been living in has been the wrong one, and the true story is going to cost you everything you used to be.

I am not a good man. I want that on the record, carved somewhere, in case anyone reads this who is tempted to make me better than I was. I burned a village once because the paper said to. I have lied to myself with professional skill for two decades.

But I know an oath when I see one being kept. And watching that woman keep watch over people who weren't hers, guarding a burden that wasn't hers, for a dead stranger who chose her — I recognized the shape of it. It was the shape of the thing I had sworn and fumbled and half-betrayed my whole life, walking around in exile's rags and talking to a mule.

Dain, I thought, you absolute fool. You didn't just pick a carrier. You picked better than me. You picked better than the whole rotten chivalry of us.

I settled back against the rock, drew my cloak over my knees, and made the decision I had been avoiding for three nights, saying it aloud to no one, because some vows only count if you waste breath on them:

"I'm with you, then. Whether you like it or not."

Below me, the fire burned low. Above me, clouds were gathering in the west — real clouds, ordinary ones, though looking at them I found I couldn't help wondering what she felt, this woman who had once cracked the sky, when the weather turned. Whether the storm in her itched toward it. Whether she was afraid.

Whether she had any idea what was coming for her out of the capital, or what the seal on that map meant, or why the king's own hunters had shot a royal courier on a snowline.

I didn't know either. But I was done pretending I wasn't going to find out.

In the morning they would break camp and move east, toward the high passes, toward whatever the thirteenth moon meant. And I would be behind them, unseen, unbannersd, unsworn — a knight following a smuggler into whatever waited at the end of a dead man's map.

It was, I reflected, pulling my collar against the cold, the first time in nineteen years I had chosen my road myself.

I only wished it didn't feel so much like falling.
