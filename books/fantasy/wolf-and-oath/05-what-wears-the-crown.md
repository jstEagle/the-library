## 5. What Wears the Crown

There are certain things a king should not have to learn from his own fingernails.

I discovered this on a Tuesday — or what I believe was a Tuesday, though I confess my relationship with the calendar has grown somewhat loose of late, the way an old man's grip loosens on a rope he once climbed easily. I woke in my own bed, in my own citadel, beneath my own canopy embroidered with my own crest, and there was blood under my fingernails. Not mine. Mine would have been simpler. Mine I could explain away: a shaving cut, a nosebleed, some ordinary failure of the body that kings are permitted along with everyone else.

This was someone else's blood, dried brown in the crescents beneath my nails like tiny rusted moons.

I lay very still and conducted an inventory, as one does. Head: present, aching at the left temple, which had become its habitual address for pain. Hands: present, filthy. Memory of yesterday: a corridor. A long corridor, grey stone, torchlight, and then — nothing. Nothing where the day's second half should have been. It was as if a page had been torn cleanly out of a ledger, and worse, as if the clerk who tore it had been careful enough to leave no ragged edge. The absence was smooth. Polished. Almost courteous.

"Your Grace." My chamberlain, Pell, stood at the foot of the bed with the particular stiffness of a man delivering news he'd rather have posted on a door in another city entirely. "You signed the decree last night. The one concerning Thornfell."

"I did?"

"You did, Your Grace."

"What did it concern?" I sat up. The room performed a slow, dignified revolution around me, the way rooms do when they have decided you haven't earned your balance yet.

Pell's throat moved. "The village of Hallowmere, sire. It is to be struck from the rolls. Its records burned. Its name removed from all maps and ledgers of the realm."

"That seems," I said carefully, "an unusual decree."

"Yes, Your Grace."

"For a Tuesday."

"Thursday, Your Grace."

Ah. So it was Thursday. That accounted for two missing days, then, not one. I found this information did not comfort me so much as reorganize my discomfort into a more efficient shape.

"And why," I asked, "does the crown wish a village forgotten?"

Pell looked at me the way men look at their king when they are trying to decide whether the king is testing them or whether the king has genuinely gone somewhere none of them can follow. "You said it was harboring sedition, sire. You said—" He stopped. Swallowed again. "You said quite a lot, Your Grace. Most of it was... not in your usual voice."

* * *

Let me tell you how a king gets ready in the morning, because I suspect most people imagine it involves trumpets and velvet and perhaps a small choir. In truth it involves a great many hands — mine least among them. Valets wash me, dress me, comb me, button me, and by the time I reach the great hall I am less a man than a finished product, an argument for monarchy assembled fresh each dawn. There is something obscene about it, if you think about it too long, so mostly I don't think about it too long.

That morning I thought about it too long.

Because while Tammas shaved me — a nervous boy with steady hands, which is the correct ratio — I watched my own face in the mirror and tried to remember signing anything. And my face did something I did not instruct it to do. It smiled. Just briefly. Just at the corner of the mouth, the way a man smiles at a private joke.

I had not told my face to smile.

"Gently, sire," Tammas murmured, mistaking the tremor in my jaw for impatience.

"Of course," I said, in the voice of a man whose jaw trembles from impatience. What else can you say? *My face is doing things without my permission, boy; fetch me a priest and possibly a mason, as I intend to have this skull opened and inspected.* One does not say such things. Kings especially do not say such things, because a king who says such things stops being a king and becomes a problem, and problems get solved with quiet rooms and kindly physicians and eventually a younger brother with excellent posture and a ready smile.

So I said nothing. And beneath my breastbone, in a place I could not point to on any anatomy chart, something said nothing back. But it was a different quality of nothing. A listening nothing.

You will want to know when it began, and I will disappoint you, because I cannot say. There was no night when a cold wind came through the shutters carrying something old and hungry. No fever, no wound, no bargain made at a crossroads — I checked my memory for all of these the way a man checks his pockets after a pickpocket brushes past him. What I found instead were gaps. Small ones at first. A conversation with the Master of Coin I couldn't recall. An afternoon in the archives that existed only as a smear of candle-grease and dust. Then larger ones. A week, once. I told myself it was grief — my queen had died the winter before, and grief does eat a man hollow, everyone says so, everyone agrees, it is practically a proverb.

It was convenient, having a dead wife to blame. She would have hated that. Marta could spot an excuse at forty paces and had once made a visiting duke cry simply by raising one eyebrow at his.

"Marta," I said aloud, alone in the council chamber before the others arrived, just to hear whether saying her name still hurt properly.

It hurt. Thank all the patient gods. It hurt exactly right, sharp and specific and hers.

And underneath the hurt, faintly, came that other thing — the listening nothing — and it seemed almost to flinch from her name. As though it disliked the taste.

I have thought about that often since. It may be the single most useful thing I learned in those months: it could not bear what I loved. Remember that. Whatever else happened, however far it went into me, it could not bear Marta's name, or the smell of rain on the training yard, or the sound of my granddaughter laughing at her own jokes, which she does even before the punchline, which is either a character flaw or a form of genius.

It could take my days. It could not take my love. It could only make me ashamed of the gaps where love used to keep regular hours.

* * *

The council convened at midmorning. I will describe it honestly, because honesty is the one luxury left to me now, writing this in whatever light I can steal.

Lord Chancellor Bren sat to my right, a good man shaped like a wine cask, loyal the way oak is loyal — slowly, over decades, without anyone noticing. Marshal Duvane stood by the map table, arms crossed, radiating disapproval at geography. And Captain Vess of the King's Hunters stood at the far end, straight as a drawn blade, because Vess is always standing somewhere straight. I have never seen the woman sit. I suspect chairs file complaints about her.

"The decree regarding Hallowmere," Bren began, shuffling papers, "has already caused... comment, sire. The village pays its taxes. It has produced three soldiers and one rather mediocre poet. There is no sedition on record—"

"There is sedition," I heard myself say.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, because everything afterward depends on it: I did not decide to say that. I did not weigh it, shape it, choose it. It came out of my mouth the way water comes out of a pipe — with pressure behind it that was not mine. Somewhere below my voice, in the cellar of me, something leaned against my throat and spoke.

Bren blinked. "Sire, the garrison report—"

"The reports lie. Villages lie. Ledgers lie." My hand — my hand, moving without consultation — flattened itself on the map table, fingers spread, and I watched it the way one watches a dog do something clever and slightly wrong. "Hallowmere forgets its duty. Let it forget its name as well."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Duvane studied me with the flat professional gaze of a soldier deciding whether a bridge will hold. Vess looked at nothing, which was its own kind of study.

Then the pressure lifted, like a hand withdrawing from the back of my neck, and I was alone in my own mouth again, tasting the words I'd just said, and they were ash and oil and not mine, and every face in the room was waiting for me to continue, and the worst part — write this down, whoever finds this, the worst part — the worst part was that no one objected. Because I am the king. Because the words had come out of the king's mouth in the king's voice. A crown is a machine for converting one man's speech into a hundred men's obedience, and I felt it turning, turning, taking my stolen words and stamping them into law like coins.

"Draft the writs," I said — I said, myself, choosing — "and bring me every document concerning Hallowmere before anything is burned. I wish to review the evidence personally."

A pause. Bren's eyebrows performed a small diplomatic negotiation with each other.

"Sire, the decree was sealed last night. The burnings begin at dusk."

"Then postpone the burnings."

"It was sealed under the privy seal, Your Grace. By your own instruction, changes require—" He hesitated, delicately, like a man stepping around a corpse in a parlor. "They require your clear and repeated affirmation, witnessed. Forgive me, sire, but you were... emphatic on that point."

Was I. How thoughtful of me. How very thorough, the me who signs decrees in the dark hours with blood under his nails. He builds locks and then swallows the keys, and I wake each morning a trespasser in my own authority.

"Very well," I said. "Witnessed affirmation. Witness it now."

And the pressure rose again — I felt it come this time, felt it like floodwater climbing the stairs of me — and I set my jaw against it and said, clearly, in front of six witnesses: "The decree stands as sealed. Burn the records. Strike the name."

Two sentences. One mine. One not. Both in the same voice, from the same mouth, an hour apart. And the council heard no difference at all.

That is the horror I want recorded. Not the blood, not the gaps, not even the thing wearing me. The horror is that it needs no disguise. It borrows my face, my seal, my voice, and the world sees only the king, and the world obeys the king, and so the world becomes its hands. I am not its prisoner. I am its instrument. A prisoner at least knows where the walls are.

* * *

That evening I dismissed my attendants early — a small victory; they grumble, but a king's insomnia is traditional and cannot be argued with — and I sat at my desk with a candle and did what I should have done months ago. I wrote down everything. Every gap. Every hour unaccounted for. Every morning blood or mud or wine appeared on me without provenance. The night I apparently rode out with twenty riders and returned with nineteen and no memory of either direction. The decree dissolving the storm-caller sanctuaries, three years ago, which I signed — I checked the hand, it was my hand — in a script slightly too perfect, the way a forger writes when he is being careful, because real handwriting is never entirely sure of itself.

I filled four pages. Then I took a fifth and wrote at the top, in large letters, so that future-me — hungover-me, gap-me, waking-with-blood-under-his-nails me — could not miss it:

*If you are reading this and you do not remember writing it: YOU ARE NOT WELL. TRUST THE PAGES. DO NOT TRUST THE COMFORTABLE THOUGHTS.*

Because here is what I have noticed, in the small hours, in the territory I still hold: it argues. When I resolve to fight it, a warm reasonable voice rises in me — *you are tired, you are grieving, kings are strange to themselves, look how much you still remember, look how fine you feel right now* — and it is so gentle, so flattering, so precisely tuned to the man I wish I were. Grief explains the gaps. Power explains the cruelty. Age explains the rest. It offers me a complete account of my own decay in which I am blameless and whole and merely sad, and every night I nearly sign it, and some nights I fear I already have.

Tonight, though, the pages won. Four pages of my own hand telling me what my mornings confirm. I know how madmen are made, and it is not with chains. It is with explanations.

So I sat with the candle and interrogated the dark.

"All right," I said quietly. "I know you're there."

The candle flame leaned, though the shutters were closed.

"I don't know what you are. Old, I think. Older than the crown — you were in it before me, weren't you? Before my father. How many kings have you worn?" My voice stayed level. I was rather proud of that. "How many of them woke with blood under their nails and told themselves stories?"

And it answered.

Not in words. Words would have been a mercy — a voice I could hate, an intruder I could name and curse. No. It answered in *certainty*. It poured a single thought into me the way you'd pour warm honey into cold milk, and the thought was:

*I am your strength. Everything you have built, I held upright. Every hard decree, every necessary cruelty, I carried the weight so your hands could stay clean. You were drowning before me. I am why you float.*

And the truly vile part, the part I set down so that some future reader understands the shape of the enemy: it wasn't lying. Or rather — it was lying, but the lie was built from true timber. I *had* been drowning. After Marta died I had sat in this citadel like a man under ice, watching the bright world move above me, unable to care about grain tariffs or border raids, wishing only to stop feeling. And something had come — had been waiting, I think, coiled in the crown itself, patient as rot in a beam — and offered to carry it all. The decisions. The weight. The guilt.

I said yes. I must have said yes. Perhaps I said yes the way drowning men say yes to water filling their lungs, without ever meaning to, without noticing the moment of consent.

"No," I said aloud, to the candle, to the dark, to the thing in the marrow of me. "Strength doesn't hide from its own hand. Whatever you are, you are not mine."

The certainty came again, softer now, almost tender, and this is the sentence I want carved somewhere, anywhere, in case I lose the pages and the war both:

*Then why do you sleep so well, little king, on the nights I hold the reins?*

And gods forgive me — it was true. On the nights I lost, I slept like the dead. On the nights I fought, I lay awake in the wreck of my own mind, counting my missing hours like coins.

It let me feel how much I wanted the sleep. That was its answer, and its threat, and its promise, all in one.

* * *

Near dawn — I had not slept; I had won, barely, and winning looks a great deal like losing from the inside — a knock came at the antechamber door. Pell again, gray-faced, holding a slate.

"A rider, Your Grace. From the mountain road. The hunters' captain requests immediate audience." His eyes flicked to my hands, folded on the desk, and I realized too late I had not hidden them. "She brings word of a courier, sire. Found dying on the snowline. Carrying — she says it matters greatly, Your Grace — carrying a map."

Something in the cellar of me stood up.

I felt it happen. Felt the pressure shift, the way a house shifts when something enormous turns over in a room below. It wanted that map. It *feared* that map — no, worse, it feared whoever could read it, and its fear came up through me as hunger, sudden and enormous and not mine, a hunger with teeth and a very specific address.

"Your Grace?" Pell prompted. "Shall I admit her?"

I looked at my hands. Steady. Clean, since the washing. Under the nails, nothing at all.

"Send Captain Vess in," I said, in my own voice, with my own breath, holding the door of myself shut with both hands. "And Pell — whatever I order in the next hour, write it down. Write down everything I say."

He stared at me. "Sire?"

"Consider it," I said, smiling the way kings smile, "a test of your penmanship."

He went. And beneath my ribs, the thing settled back down — patient, amused, hungry — already reaching for the captain's news like a man reaching across a table for the last letter that could hang him.

One night remained, I would learn later, before the thirteenth turn of the moon. Neither of us knew yet what was coming down out of Thornfell toward us: a smuggler with a storm in her blood, and a knight with no banner, and a map written in a language only the dead of my own massacres could read.

But the Hollow knew something was coming.

And for the first time since it had taken up residence in my head —

it was afraid.
