## 6. Road of Thorns

The mountain road to Vell's Crossing was the kind of road that made you question every decision that had brought you to it. Narrow as a liar's conscience, switchbacking up the shoulder of Mount Halloway with a drop on the left side deep enough that the birds circled below you. I'd walked it eleven times in my years guiding smugglers through the Thornfell, and I had a theory about it: the mountain didn't want us here, and it was polite enough not to say so directly. It just kept offering little reminders. A loose stone here. A gust there. Once, memorably, an entire goat.

I was thinking about the map again.

It sat against my ribs inside my coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and I could feel it the way you feel a splinter — not painful exactly, just *present*, insisting on its own existence. Three days since the courier died at my fire with blood on his lips and "thirteenth moon" on his breath. Three days since I'd triggered a rockslide onto four of the king's hunters and run like a woman with sense. The smugglers had scattered to their winter routes, which meant for the first time in years I was alone on a road, and alone was when the map did its talking.

*Burn me,* it seemed to say. *Or follow me. But stop pretending those are the same weight.*

"Shut up," I told it quietly. A marmot near the trail's edge gave me a look of profound judgment and disappeared into the rocks. Fair enough. Talking to maps. That was how it started, according to Mother Ije's old stories — first you talked to them, then they talked back, then one day you woke up exiled in a country where nobody said your name right.

I crested the bend above the Crossing and stopped.

Something was wrong with the light. Not the quality of it — late afternoon, amber and long-shadowed, ordinary — but the *arrangement* of it. There was a way sunlight fell on men crouched behind granite, a particular interruption in the glitter of frost on scree, and once you'd spent enough years reading mountains the way other people read letters, you couldn't unsee it.

Four interruptions. Maybe five. Spread along the switchbacks below, positioned exactly where a traveler would have no room to maneuver and no room to run. The king's hunters didn't set ambushes like bandits did; bandits wanted your goods and preferred you alive enough to be robbed. Hunters wanted *you*, specifically, and they were patient about it.

I eased back off the ridgeline, heart doing arithmetic against my ribs.

Options. One: go around via the Ashen Steps, two days lost, exposed sky the whole way, and if they had a second team watching the far approach — and they would, because Captain Vess struck me as a woman who checked her work twice — then I'd simply be trading one killbox for another. Two: go down into the river gorge and swim it, in thaw-season water that could stop a strong man's heart in twenty strokes. Three: walk straight down the road, hands open, and talk.

I'd tried three once before, back when the exile was fresh and I still believed institutions contained reasonable people somewhere beneath the uniforms. The reasonable person I found had put iron cuffs on me and called me *storm-witch* like it was a diagnosis.

So. The gorge, then, or—

"There," said a voice behind me, low and male and entirely too close. "Below the third switchback. You see them?"

I had a knife out and turned before the sentence finished, which is the only reason I'm alive to narrate this. He didn't flinch. He stood maybe eight feet away among the pines, dressed in travel-gray with no crest, no colors, nothing but a sword worn honest at his hip and a face that looked like it had been quarreling with winters for a decade and losing narrowly. Gray at the temples. A scar through one eyebrow. Eyes the color of a sky deciding whether to snow.

"You," I said. Because I knew him. Not his name — I'd never had that — but the shape of him. He'd been at the checkpoint ambush in the pass, weeks ago now, standing apart from the king's men with his hand never quite reaching his sword. And he'd been at the edge of the rockslide aftermath, I'd sworn, a figure on the ridge I'd told myself I imagined.

"The courier," I said. "You were his."

"I was his shield." Something moved through his face when he said it, something old and heavy. "And now I'm yours, apparently, whether either of us likes it."

"I don't."

"Noted." He nodded down the slope. "We can discuss our mutual disappointment later. Right now there are five hunters on that road and they've been waiting since dawn, and the sun is going to move off that ridge in about ten minutes, and when it does, the man on the far end will see your silhouette against the snowfield and this conversation becomes very short."

"How do you know there are five?"

"Because I counted them last night while you slept badly and talked in your sleep about goats."

"That was—" I decided not to litigate the goat. "Who are you?"

"Cassel." He said it like a man handing over something he wasn't sure he still owned. "Late of the king's oathguard. Very late. Now — are we fighting together or are you going to make this complicated?"

Here is what I should have done: run. Every instinct bred into me by seven years of exile said *never stand beside a stranger, never let anyone learn your patterns, never owe anyone anything.* And here is what I actually did, which was look down at that road — the road I needed, the only road — and do the arithmetic I hated.

Five hunters. One of me. One of him, and he carried his sword like the sword had opinions he agreed with.

"Together," I said. "But if you're leading me into a cage—"

"If I wanted you in a cage, witch, I'd have done it while you were dreaming about livestock." He drew his sword without flourish. "Now listen. They'll expect you to come down the road or bolt uphill. So don't. The scree field on the east side — start a slide, small one, just enough noise to pull their eyes. Then come down through the pines on their left flank while they're looking wrong. Can you do that without—" he hesitated, delicately, the way people do when they're trying not to say *without using the thing that got you exiled* — "without making weather?"

I smiled, and I felt it come out crooked. "Ser Cassel. I am extremely capable of making noise without making weather. It's one of my finest skills."

"Then go be fine at it."

* * *

Scree is honest work. You find the right shelf of stone, you lean your shoulder into it, and you ask the mountain — politely, in the old grammar, the grammar of *nudge* rather than *command* — and the mountain, being contrary, usually agrees. The slide went down the east slope with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and five heads turned toward it, exactly as promised.

I came through the pines low and quiet, and the first hunter never knew I was there until my boot found the back of his knee and my knife found the junction of his neck and shoulder — muscle, not artery; I'd learned to cut where a man drops but doesn't die, partly from mercy and mostly because corpses raise more questions than sleeping ones. He folded. I took his crossbow off his back before he finished falling.

The second one heard me. He turned fast, good training, sword already moving — and met Cassel coming out of the trees like winter itself arriving early. I'll say this for the disgraced knight: he fought the way water fights, no wasted motion, everything flowing to the lowest point, which happened to be the gaps in a man's guard. The hunter went down under three precise strokes and stayed down, breathing shallowly, disarmed and concussed.

Which left three, and the three who remained were better than the first two, because of course they were. The ambush's spine was a big woman with a shaved scalp and a sergeant's knots on her bracer, and she'd already abandoned the road and come uphill, reading the terrain faster than I liked. She saw Cassel and her face did something complicated.

"Vane," she said. "They said you were dead."

"They say a lot of things." 

"They also say you're hunting the king's courier-killers." Her eyes flicked to me. "And traveling with a storm-caller. That's a hanging offense times two, Vane. Walk away. Last offer."

"Captain Vess sent you?" Cassel asked.

"Captain Vess executes the king's decrees. Same as she always has." The woman's hand flexed on her sword. "Same as you used to."

What happened next happened quickly, and I've replayed it enough times since to know it mattered, though I couldn't tell you why at the time. The third hunter — young, nervous, positioned on the flank — raised his crossbow at me, and his hands were shaking, and I had a clear shot with the dead man's crossbow and no reason whatsoever to take mercy on a man paid to drag me back to a capital that burned my order out of history.

I shot the crossbow out of his hands instead. Bolt through the stock, splintering the tiller, close enough to his knuckles that he'd find splinters in them for a week. He stared at his ruined weapon. I stared at him. Neither of us moved.

The sergeant saw it. I watched her see it — watched her recalculate whatever ledger she kept about me, storm-caller, exile, quarry — and something in her jaw shifted. "Fall back," she said to the young one. "Both of you. We're done here."

"We can still take—"

"*Done here,* Tam." She backed down the slope without turning, sword up, eyes on Cassel the whole way, and the last I saw of the king's hunters that day was her retreating back and the expression on her face, which was not hatred. It was doubt. Doubt is a crack, and cracks are how storms get in. I filed that away.

Then it was just me and Cassel and five groaning men on a mountainside going blue-gold with evening.

"You spared him," Cassel said. It came out strange, almost accusatory.

"He was shaking too hard to aim. Killing a man who can't aim is bad manners."

"That's not why."

"No," I admitted. "It's not." I slung the captured crossbow over my shoulder and looked at him properly for the first time since the fight started. "Now. You're going to tell me who you are, what you want, and why a dead man's shield has been following me across half the Thornfell. And Cassel — that's your name? — you're going to tell it to me walking, because this ridge is going to be crawling with Vess's people by midnight and I know a hollow under the old watchtower where the wind doesn't reach."

He fell into step beside me. "You're trusting me."

"I'm *using* you. There's a difference, and it's measured in knives."

* * *

The hollow under the watchtower was a place I'd found my first winter in the Thornfell, when I was nineteen and stupid and nearly froze to death learning that mountains keep secrets even from people who can speak to them. The tower itself was a ruin from some older kingdom, its stones furred with lichen, and beneath its collapsed stair was a chamber the wind forgot. I built a fire small enough to hide and put water on for tea, because if I was going to interrogate a knight I intended to do it warm.

Cassel sat across the flames with his sword across his knees and his hands flat on his thighs, and he looked, in the firelight, like a man being audited by his own conscience.

"Talk," I said.

"You know most of it. I was sworn to the courier — Master Odell, royal cartographer's office. He found something. A route into the citadel that isn't supposed to exist, and papers to go with it, and he wouldn't tell me what they proved. Then the hunters found us at Sarn Ford." His jaw worked. "Odell told me to run the other way and draw them off. I did. I was supposed to be his shield and I ran, and he died alone on a snowline with a stranger for a witness."

"And now you want the map back. To finish his work. Or to bury it. Which?"

"I don't know what I want." He said it plainly, no performance in it, and somehow that was worse than a lie. "I know what I *don't* want. I don't want another decree signed in the king's name that empties a village for no reason. I don't want to stand in a throne room and watch a man I swore to give orders that smell wrong. Odell's map leads to the citadel. Whatever's in that citadel killed him for knowing about it. I want to see its face."

I turned my cup in my hands. Outside, the wind worked at the tower stones with patient fingers, and I thought about the map against my ribs, shifting when I lied to myself, written in script only my order could read. Script my order had been murdered for. If Cassel was telling the truth, then the thread running through all of it — the courier, the hunters, the king's strange cruelty — led somewhere I'd sworn never to go again.

"Why spare the boy?" Cassel asked suddenly. "On the road. You had him."

"Did you ever kill someone who didn't deserve it?"

The silence stretched long enough to have its own weather. "Yes," he said finally.

"So did I. Once. Before the exile, during — it doesn't matter. Afterward I decided that if I ever had a choice between the easy kill and the hard mercy, I'd try the mercy, and if it got me killed someday, well. Exile's a slow death anyway. At least the mercy dies clean." I shrugged, embarrassed by my own honesty. "Your turn. Why follow me instead of taking the map? You had chances. I counted at least two."

"Three," he said. "You sleep badly."

"Goat-related."

"Mostly." He fed a stick to the fire. "At the ford, after Odell died, I read his papers. One line stuck in me. He'd written: *the crown is not the crown.* I thought it was cartographer's poetry. Then I started hearing what the king's decrees actually said, and I watched good soldiers enforce them with dead eyes, and I thought — maybe Odell meant it exactly as written." He looked up at me across the fire. "You're going to the citadel. I can see it in how you hold that map, like it's a debt. I know the citadel — its gates, its garrison, which walls have eyes. You know these mountains better than any living soul. Alone, each of us dies on the way. Together—"

"Together we might die slightly later," I said. "Thrilling."

"Wry doesn't suit you, by the way. It fits like borrowed clothes."

"Everything I wear is borrowed. I was exiled with one bag." I stared into the fire and let the truth arrive on its own schedule, the way it does when you stop chasing it. "Fine. Truce. You know the citadel, I know the passes. We go together as far as it suits both of us, and the moment it stops suiting, we split clean, no debts, no graves owed. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He extended his hand across the fire, and I took it, and his grip was careful, like he was afraid I'd spark.

"One condition," I added.

"Name it."

"When we get wherever this ends — if the map's truth is what Odell thought it was — nobody hangs for it. Nobody burns. I've seen what happens when justice gets organized. It starts wearing uniforms." I held his eyes. "Can you live under that?"

Cassel was quiet for a moment, and in the firelight I watched him set something down that he'd been carrying a long time. "I broke my oath once already," he said. "Apparently the second time's easier."

Outside, the wind changed. It came around the mountain from the north, and it carried a sound I knew too well — the far-off belling of hounds, thin with distance but unmistakable, working the slope below the watchtower. Cassel's head came up. My hand found my knife.

"Hounds mean Vess," he said. "Hounds mean she's not sending patrols anymore. She's coming herself."

I looked at the fire, at the map's weight against my ribs, at the stranger-oathbreaker-maybe-ally across the flames, and I felt the old pressure building behind my sternum, the storm that lived in me asking, as it always asked, *let me out.*

"Not yet," I whispered to it. Then, to Cassel: "Get your sword. We're about to learn whether this truce holds under pressure."
