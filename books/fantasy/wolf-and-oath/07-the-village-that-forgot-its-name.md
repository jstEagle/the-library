## 7. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The hounds never caught us. That should have been a relief. Instead it felt like being spared by something that intended to collect later.

We ran east through the night, off the smugglers' roads entirely, because Cassel — who claimed to know Vess the way you know an old scar — said she'd mapped every route the Thornfell smugglers used and simply hadn't told anyone. "She doesn't need to tell anyone," he'd said, breathing hard as we climbed. "She just needs to wait."

So we went where waiting wouldn't help her: down into a fold of the mountains that wasn't on any map I'd ever seen, including mine, and mine were better than most. The trail dropped through a seam in the rock that I only found because my grandmother's storm-script hummed faintly against my skin when I passed it — a tickle like a word half-remembered. Old paths, old wards. My order had threaded this country with them once, back when we still had a country.

"Where are we going?" Cassel asked for the third time.

"Down," I said, because the honest answer was *I don't know, but the mountain seems to*, and I wasn't ready to say that out loud to a man whose sword arm was longer than his patience.

The seam opened into a valley just past dawn, and the valley held a village.

I stopped so fast Cassel nearly walked into my back. Smoke rose from chimneys — real smoke, woodsmoke, breakfast-smoke — and goats bleated behind a fence, and a woman was beating a rug on a line with the steady rhythm of someone who had done it every morning of her life. All perfectly ordinary. Except that I had crossed the Thornfell for six years, learned its passes the way other people learn prayers, traded with every settlement that would open its gates, and I had never seen this village. Not from above, not from below, not in the way you see things when the mountains are your only company and you memorize everything out of hunger for company at all.

"That's not possible," I said.

"What?"

"There's no village here." I pointed, absurdly, as if the village might apologize and leave. "There is no village here. The Thornfell doesn't hide valleys. It hides roads, it hides bodies, it hides me, but it does not grow villages overnight."

Cassel looked at it for a long moment. He had the soldier's habit of counting before speaking — doors, wells, sightlines. I watched him do it now, lips moving slightly. Then he said, quietly, "There are forty-one houses. A village that size pays taxes. Has a name. Appears on levy rolls."

"And?"

"And I carried dispatches through these mountains for eleven years." His voice had gone strange. Flat, the way men go flat when they're standing at the edge of something. "There was never a levy roll for this valley. There was never anything for this valley."

The woman with the rug had noticed us. She stood holding the beater, squinting, and then — this was the part that lodged under my ribs like a splinter — she smiled and waved, warm and unafraid, the way you wave at someone you've waved at a hundred times before.

"Come down!" she called. "You'll want bread! You always come down hungry!"

*You always.* I didn't move. Cassel didn't either. And the woman's smile flickered — not into suspicion, which would have made sense, but into confusion, a soft blankness, like a lamp guttering. She frowned at her own hand as if she couldn't remember why she'd raised it. Then she turned and went inside, and the door closed, and I felt something in the valley exhale.

"Well," I said. "That's either hospitality or a trap."

"In my experience," Cassel said, drawing his sword anyway, "they're frequently the same thing."

*

We went down. Of course we went down. I'd like to tell you I weighed the risks like a careful professional, but the truth is the bread smelled good and I hadn't eaten since yesterday noon and there is only so long a person can outrun hunters on an empty stomach. Survival makes cowards of principles.

They fed us in a longhouse with a turf roof and a fire pit, and they were kind, and that was the worst part.

An old man named — well, he introduced himself as Tam, and then a minute later he introduced himself as Bram, and then his granddaughter gently took his hand and said, "He's Tam," without any embarrassment at all, the way you correct a child who's mixed up his colors. The bread was dark and dense and excellent. The ale was thin. A girl about ten years old sat across from me and stared at my hair with frank curiosity.

"You're a stranger," she said.

"I am."

"You don't look like us." She considered this. "Nobody looks like us. I can't remember what we look like."

Her mother laughed from across the fire — a real laugh, easy — and said, "Ayla, don't pester the travelers," and the girl subsided, satisfied, and went back to carving a little wooden goat with a knife far too sharp for her age.

I ate my bread and felt cold all the way down, and not from the mountain air.

Because here's what I noticed, sitting in that warm room full of kind people: the house had no keepsakes. Every home I've ever been in — even smuggler dens, even exile hovels — has objects that mean things. A carved box from a dead husband. A chipped cup from a mother. Something saved, something kept. This longhouse had furniture, tools, food, clothing. Nothing else. No portraits, no heirlooms, no grave-markers visible outside the windows. The walls were bare in a way that wasn't austere. It was the bareness of a room where nothing had ever happened worth remembering.

And when I asked Tam — Tam, currently — how long his family had lived here, he smiled and said, "Oh, forever, dear," with such warmth and such total emptiness behind it that I had to look away.

Cassel caught my eye across the fire. He'd noticed too. Soldiers notice absence; it's how they stay alive. But noticing and understanding are different animals, and neither of us understood yet. We were both still telling ourselves a comfortable story: isolated valley, insular people, mountain folk who kept to themselves. People tell themselves comfortable stories the way rivers tell themselves they'll reach the sea eventually. Mostly it works until it very suddenly doesn't.

It stopped working after supper, when I asked the question I probably shouldn't have.

"The village has a name?" I said, casual, over the rim of my cup. "For the maps."

Silence. Not hostile silence — worse. Blank silence. The mother looked at Tam. Tam looked at the fire. Ayla stopped carving and looked up with the particular wide-eyed attention children give when adults break something.

"We call it home," Tam said at last, slowly, tasting each word like a man checking whether food has spoiled. "That's... we call it home."

"Yes, but—"

"It's called home." Firmer now. Agitated. His hands had begun to tremble around his cup. "Home. It's always been—" He stopped. Blinked. The agitation drained out of his face like water through sand, leaving that terrible mildness. "It's a nice evening," he said pleasantly. "Isn't it a nice evening?"

"It's a lovely evening," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved credit for.

Later, by the fire while the household slept, Cassel crouched beside me and spoke low enough that only I could hear.

"You saw it."

"I saw it."

"When he tried to reach for the name. Something pushed him away from it. You saw his face."

"I saw his face." I pulled my cloak tighter. Against my ribs, the map felt suddenly less like luggage and more like evidence. "Cassel, these people aren't hiding their history. They don't *have* it. There's a hole where it used to be and they've papered over the hole so smoothly they don't even trip on it anymore."

"Villages don't lose their names."

"No. Villages get them taken." I thought of the decree I'd heard read aloud in a border town two years ago — one of Aldric's edicts, posted by hunters in black-and-silver, striking some minor lord's title from the rolls for disloyalty. Everyone had joked about it. *Struck from the record* — as if parchment could unmake a place. We'd laughed. "What if it isn't parchment? What if it isn't a metaphor? What if somewhere in that citadel there's a crown that decided this valley owed it taxes, or loyalty, or silence, and when the valley refused, the crown reached out and took the *idea* of the valley instead?"

"That's madness."

"Probably." I met his eyes. "Say it like you believe it."

He couldn't. I watched him try, jaw working, and then give up and look at the fire. "The courier," he said finally. "Before he died. He said the king was sick in a way that wasn't sickness. He said the decrees had teeth." His hand drifted to his sword hilt, not reaching for it, just resting, the way you touch a wound to confirm it's still yours. "I thought he was delirious. Men say things when they're dying."

"He said 'thirteenth moon.'"

"He did."

"And then the hunters came for a wax-sealed scrap of leather with enough urgency to chase two strangers across half the Thornfell." I leaned back against the wall and stared up at the smoke-hole and the cold stars beyond it. "Delirious men don't get chased like that, Cassel. Truths do."

*

I slept badly, which was standard, and woke before dawn, which was also standard, but this time I woke because Ayla was standing over me.

Children move quiet; it's one of their few tactical advantages over adults. She was just there, a small shadow with the little wooden goat clutched in both hands, watching me with eyes too old for her face.

"I remembered something," she whispered.

I sat up slowly. "Did you."

"At night sometimes I remember. Then morning comes and it's gone, like water in a hand." She held out the goat. "But I made this, so I'd keep it. Grandfather says making things stays even when knowing goes."

The goat was crude and perfect, the way children carve when nobody has taught them to be ashamed. On its flank, scratched deep with that too-sharp knife, were marks. Not decoration.

Storm-script.

My breath left me in a rush. I knew those characters the way you know your own heartbeat — the old tongue of my order, the script that sang to wards and storms, the script the map wore, the script that got my people burned out of their towers and scattered across the world like seed. A child in a nameless valley had written it from a memory she couldn't hold onto, and had known — some animal part of her had known — to carve it into wood so it would last.

"Ayla." I kept my voice gentle. Careful hands, careful voice; she was skittish as a bird. "Who taught you these marks?"

"Nobody." She shrugged. "They're just in me. Like songs. Mama says I sing songs nobody taught me too, and she says stop, but I can't stop, they're *in* me." Her chin came up, defiant. "Grandfather says that's why the fog takes our remembering first from everyone else and leaves me till last. He says I'm thick-headed."

"Thick-headed," I repeated.

"Like a goat." She pressed the carving into my hands. "You take it. You're going where the fog comes from, aren't you? Everybody here knows that without knowing it. You're going to make it stop."

I looked down at the goat, at the storm-script scored into its flank, and felt the whole shape of my life tilt beneath me like ice shifting underfoot. My order had threaded wards through these mountains. My order had written the map. And if a village in a fold of the Thornfell carried storm-script in its blood — in its *children* — then my people hadn't just passed through here once upon a time. They had lived here. Hidden here. Maybe died here.

Maybe been erased here.

"Ayla," I said. "The fog. When it comes. Does anyone ever fight it? Does anyone ever keep things?"

She thought about it with the ferocious seriousness of the young. "Tam tries. He ties knots in a cord for everything he wants to keep, and every morning he looks at the knots and cries a little, and then he doesn't anymore." She said it without pity, the way children report weather. "But the smith's wife, she writes on her arm. Ink. Every night she writes what she was, and in the morning she reads it, and for a little while she's angry instead of happy, and everybody says that's worse, being angry." A pause. "I think angry is better. Angry means you know somebody took something."

I looked at this small, thick-headed, stubborn child who had decided, alone, in a valley that forgot itself nightly, to fight forgetting with a knife and a piece of wood — and something in my chest cracked along an old fault line.

"Angry is better," I agreed. "Listen to me. Keep carving. Every mark you remember, carve it somewhere permanent. Walls, beams, rocks, whatever holds still. Can you do that?"

"Grandfather will say it's strange."

"Tell him a stranger-oathbreaker-maybe-ally told you to." I glanced at Cassel, asleep against the far wall, and revised. "Tell him a friend told you to."

She beamed like I'd handed her the moon, and padded back to bed, and I sat in the dark holding a wooden goat and shaking.

Because I finally understood what the Hollow was. Not a sickness of kings. Kings were just where it nested, the way rot nests in the heartwood and shows at the branches. It ate memory. It ate *minds*, yes, it had chewed its way into Aldric's skull and worn him like a glove — but a king's mind is only one meal. A kingdom's memory is a feast. Every decree struck from the record, every village unwritten, every person dissolved into pleasant blankness — that wasn't cruelty for cruelty's sake. It was feeding. It had been feeding on my world for years, patiently, politely, and everyone had mistaken the appetite for bad governance.

And it had hunted the storm-callers for centuries, Mother Ije would later tell me, because we were the one thing that could write what it erased. Because storm-script remembers. Because we were indigestible.

But I didn't know that yet. Sitting in that bare-walled longhouse, I knew only this: the stakes had changed. Two days ago this was about survival — mine, the map's, maybe the realm's in the abstract way realms matter to people running from hunters. Now it was about a girl with a knife and a knot-cord and an old man crying over knots he couldn't read. Now it had faces.

"Cassel," I said, and he woke the way soldiers wake, instantly, hand already on steel. "Get up. We're leaving within the hour."

He blinked at me, at the goat in my hands. "Trouble?"

"Worse. Clarity."

*

We left at first light. Tam gave us bread and cheese and pressed a copper coin into Cassel's palm — payment, he insisted solemnly, for services rendered, though neither of us could guess what services. The whole village turned out to wave, warm and unafraid, and every single one of them called us by names we'd never given, names that slid off the mind like rain off slate. By the gate, Ayla hugged my waist and whispered, "Bring back the remembering," and I promised, which was stupid, because I promise nothing, promising is for people with futures they trust.

Then we climbed out of the fold, and behind us the valley exhaled again, and I felt it — actually felt it, a soft pressure sliding off the world — as forty-one houses and a hundred and some lives settled back into their beautiful, murdered peace.

I didn't cry. I want that noted for the record. I walked for three hours with my jaw set like a drawbridge before I trusted myself to speak.

"The map," Cassel said at last, watching me watch the road ahead. "It led us here, didn't it. Off the pass, through the seam. It brought us to this valley on purpose."

"It's been pulling since the courier put it in my hands." I drew it out at last, unwrapped the oilcloth, and let it fall open across my palms. The storm-script shimmered in the grey morning light, and — I swear this on every god I don't believe in — it shifted. Lines crawled like ants rearranging themselves, and new characters surfaced, fresh as wet ink, spelling out a path I hadn't seen before. Eastward. Higher. Toward a mark like a coiled serpent and a word in the old tongue that made my skin prickle:

*Ije.*

"Mother Ije," I read aloud, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, because I knew that name. Every exiled storm-caller knew that name. A legend, a ghost, a hermit who'd vanished into the high Thornfell before I was born. "The map wants us to find Mother Ije."

"Why?"

I wrapped the map up and stowed it against my ribs, next to the heartbeat it had somehow become. "Because whoever wrote it knew what I just learned the hard way. That this thing doesn't just wear crowns. It eats countries." I looked east, where the peaks went white and mean, and let the storm behind my sternum rise up and press back, eager, patient, *mine*. "And it eats memory, Cassel. Which means somewhere in that citadel is the memory of everyone it's ever swallowed. Including, I'm starting to suspect, mine."

I started walking. Behind me, a sword-belt creaked as a knight without a banner fell into step.

Above us, far off, a hawk cried once — or something shaped like a hawk — and the thirteenth moon's shadow felt, for the first time, close enough to touch.
