## 8. Storm-Script Rising

The map wanted us to go up.

That was the first sign it wasn't a map so much as a dare. Everything sane in the Thornfell pointed down — down into the valleys where there was water and firewood and a reasonable chance of not freezing to death with your boots on. But the storm-script had been crawling across its wax surface for three days now, lines rearranging themselves like ants deciding on a new route home, and every version of the route ended the same way: a jagged ink scar climbing toward the high crags above the Vale of Nine Stones.

"There's nothing up there," Cassel said for the fourth time that morning. He said it the way men say things they've already lost an argument about — thoroughly, and to no one.

"That's the idea," I said. "Hermit."

"You know that for a fact?"

"I know that when an order of women gets burned out of three kingdoms, the survivors don't move somewhere with good roads and friendly neighbors." I kicked a stone off the ledge ahead of us and listened to it fall for a suspiciously long time. "They move somewhere the king's hunters get altitude sickness just from thinking about it."

Cassel grunted. In the two weeks since the road ambush — since he'd stepped out of nowhere with a sword and a grudge and put himself between me and six arrows — he had developed an entire vocabulary of grunts. This one meant *I concede the point but reserve the right to be miserable about it.*

We climbed.

* * *

Let me tell you something about exile that nobody warns you about: you spend years learning to need nothing, and then the world sends you people who make needing things feel possible again, and it's worse than any hunger.

I'd spent five years perfecting the art of being alone in company. Leading smuggler caravans through the Thornfell is ideal training for it — everyone wants something from you, usually your survival skills and never your conversation, and you learn to give exactly what's asked. My storms stayed locked behind my teeth. My history stayed locked behind everything else. I was a guide, a toll-taker, a woman who knew where the mountain kept its secrets and sold them cheap.

Then a dying courier bled out at my fire, handed me a map written in a script I'd sworn never to read again, and whispered *thirteenth moon* like a curse with instructions attached.

Now here I was, halfway up a mountain that hated visitors, with a disgraced knight walking three paces behind me because he still didn't trust himself at my shoulder, reading my own dead order's handwriting as it moved under my fingers like something alive. The script was Oath-Hand — old Storm-Circle notation, the kind Mother Ashai used to rap my knuckles for miswriting. Every stroke of it was a small knife between my ribs. Not because it hurt. Because it was familiar. Because some traitorous part of me had missed it.

"You're doing the thing again," Cassel said.

"What thing?"

"The face. Like you bit something sour."

"It's called thinking. You should try it sometime."

"I tried it once. Didn't care for it." But his eyes stayed on me a moment too long, and I could feel him assembling questions and deciding, one by one, not to ask them yet. That was the arrangement we had: he followed, I led, and neither of us named what either of us was actually doing.

The map pulsed against my chest where I'd tucked it inside my coat. Pulsed is a strange word for paper, but there it was — a warmth, a rhythm, like a second heartbeat that didn't match mine. When I lied to myself about burning it, it went cold enough to burn. When I admitted, even silently, that I was going to climb this mountain and find whoever wrote it, it warmed like a hand on the back of my neck.

It knew me better than I liked. Maps drawn by your own people have that advantage.

* * *

We found the hermitage at dusk, if you could call it that.

It wasn't a hut. It was a wound in the mountain — a cave mouth hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and someone had woven wards into the ice itself. I felt them before I saw them: a pressure against my skin like the moment before lightning, that taste of copper and rain. Storm-work. Real storm-work, old and deep and careful, nothing like the wild sparks I rationed out of myself like a miser counting coins.

Cassel's hand went to his sword. "Trap?"

"Doorbell," I said. "For a very specific kind of guest." I raised my palm to the ice and let go of the leash I keep on myself — just a finger's width, just enough. A thread of static jumped from my skin to the falls, and the whole curtain lit blue-white from within, and then, with a groan like an old woman sitting down, the ice parted.

"Show-off," Cassel muttered.

"Says the man who announced himself at our first meeting by breaking a man's wrist."

"He deserved it."

"They always do."

Inside, the cave opened into warmth. A fire burned in a ring of black stones, and above it, on shelves cut into living rock, sat jars — dozens of them, hundreds, each one holding a little captive weather. Rain in one. Fog in another. In a jar the size of my fist, I swear on every god I stopped believing in, a tiny thunderstorm turned in circles, muttering to itself.

And in front of the fire, wrapped in a shawl the color of dried blood, sat a small brown woman with white braids and eyes like the sky before hail.

"Well," she said, in a voice like gravel settling. "Took you long enough, child. I've been keeping your seat warm for eleven years."

My whole body went cold. "You don't know me."

"I know that walk. I know that way of standing like the ground owes you money." She pointed a gnarled finger. "Okonkwo blood. Storm-Circle, southern line, the line they told us all died at Redwater. Sit down before you fall down, and introduce me to the man pretending he isn't reaching for his sword."

Cassel, to his credit, took his hand off the hilt. Mostly.

"I'm Mother Ije," she said. "Last of the Circle who still answers to the name. And you," she said to me, "are the reason I'm last. So sit, and we'll see whether that makes us family or enemies. With our lot it's often both."

I sat. It seemed safer than standing.

* * *

She fed us first. That's how I knew she was telling the truth about being one of ours — no impostor would bother feeding people before interrogating them. Barley and goat cheese and bread hard enough to qualify as a weapon, and tea brewed from pine needles that tasted like punishment but warmed like forgiveness.

She made me show her the map while we ate, and when I unrolled it across her floor, she went very still.

Not surprised. Still. There's a difference. Surprise is a door opening; stillness is a door being barred from the other side.

"Where did you get this?" she asked quietly.

"A courier. Dying. He pressed it on me and said two words — *thirteenth moon* — and then the king's hunters came, and I ran, and I've been running since."

"Hunters." Her mouth twisted. "Oathbound ones? Gray cloaks, silver oath-chains, captain with a voice like a closing ledger?"

"You know them."

"I knew their grandmothers' grandmothers." She traced one line of the storm-script with a fingertip, not touching it, hovering, the way you hover near a sleeping animal. "This is Circle work, child. Third-era notation. Whoever drew this was trained at Redwater itself — see the anchor strokes, how they curve left? That's the southern hand. Your hand." She looked up at me. "This map was drawn by someone of your line, within your lifetime. Drawn by someone who knew the Hollow would come looking, and hid a road to the citadel in a language only a storm-caller could read."

"Why hide it from everyone else?"

"Because everyone else can't open what's at the end of it." She leaned back, and the firelight made her shadow huge on the cave wall, a giantess hunched over a small stubborn flame. "Tell me what you know of the Hollow. Truly know. Not tavern talk."

"Tavern talk says the king went strange seven years ago. Decrees got crueler. Taxes, purges, villages struck from the rolls." I hesitated. "Tavern talk doesn't say much more, because tavern talk that does tends to disappear."

"And your order? What were you taught, before?"

Before. Eleven years since anyone had asked me that and meant it kindly. I looked into my cup. "That storms aren't chaos. They're pressure finding release. That a caller doesn't command weather — she negotiates with it. That our gift came from the old pact, the sky-blood, and that kings used to keep storm-callers close, as advisors. As..." I frowned. Something scratched at the back of my mind, a memory with edges worn smooth. "As wardens. I remember the word *warden*. I don't remember why."

Mother Ije nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed a diagnosis she'd dreaded.

"The Hollow is older than your kingdom," she said. "Older than the Thornfell, maybe. It is a hunger wearing a parasite's shape — it eats minds, yes, but minds are only the doorway. What it feeds on, truly, is *memory*. Names. History. The story a people tells about itself. It ate the village of Bracken Hollow until the village forgot it existed. It has been eating King Aldric for seven years, slowly, because a fast meal ends and a slow one lasts." She tapped the map. "And for four hundred years, it has eaten storm-callers first. Always first. Do you wonder why?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the jars above us, the little thunderstorm muttered in its sleep.

"Because we can see it," I said slowly. The thought arrived like weather — pressure building, directionless, then suddenly all direction. "Storm-sense. We're taught to read pressure, to feel what's coming before it breaks. A parasite in a crown wouldn't hide from us. We'd feel it the way you feel a storm front. Every caller in every generation would look at the king and say—"

"*There is something wrong with that crown,*" Mother Ije finished. "Yes. Four hundred years ago, the Circle sealed it beneath the citadel. Sealed it, bound it, set wardens over it — storm-callers in the king's court, generation after generation, watching. And the Hollow learned patience the way water learns patience. It couldn't break the seal, so it turned the world against the sealers. Whispered into royal ears. Planted fear. And eighty years ago, a king — Aldric's grandfather — declared the storm-callers heretics and traitors, and the burnings began, and the Circle scattered, and the last wardens died or fled, and the seal began to starve." Her eyes found mine, old and pitiless and grieving all at once. "Your exile, child. Redwater. The storm you couldn't control. Did you ever ask yourself why the king's soldiers were already there that day? Why they came armed, in numbers, to a village of weather-women, weeks before any decree was signed?"

The cold went through me then, the real kind, the kind that has nothing to do with altitude.

"I never—" I started.

"You never asked, because asking would mean the worst day of your life wasn't an accident. It was a harvest." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The decree that exiled your order was signed by a king whose mind was already half-eaten — Aldric's father wore the crown by then, and the Hollow had been whispering in the palace for decades. They didn't burn Redwater because you were dangerous. They burned it because you were *near the truth*, and because a scattered order can't stand ward. Every storm-caller driven into exile was one less pair of storm-senses aimed at the throne. You weren't punished for your power, girl. You were *removed* for your perception."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs just reported for duty without consulting me, and suddenly I was across the cave, hands braced on cold rock, breathing the way you breathe when the ground moves.

Eleven years. Eleven years I'd carried that storm like a stone in my chest — the lightning I couldn't hold, the fires, the screaming, the faces of the women who raised me lit orange and wrong. I'd built my whole self around that guilt. It was the load-bearing wall. Take it out and what's left standing?

Nothing. Or something new. Both options are terrifying.

Cassel shifted behind me — I heard leather creak, felt him decide not to touch my shoulder, felt him stay anyway. That's the thing about the man. He's terrible at comfort and excellent at presence.

"The courier," I managed. "He was Circle."

"One of ours, yes. Maren's boy — no, you wouldn't know her. Before your time at Redwater." Ije rose, joints crackling like green wood, and shuffled to her shelves. She came back holding a jar of clear glass filled with nothing visible at all, though the air around it bent slightly, like heat off summer stone. "He carried that map for twenty years, waiting for the right heir to the script to cross his path. Someone of the southern line. Someone strong enough to read the road and walk it." She set the jar beside the map, and the storm-script stirred toward it like grass toward sun. "He found you. Out of every exile on every road in these mountains, the map chose you. I'd call that luck, but our order stopped believing in luck around the same time we started believing in parasites."

"So the thirteenth moon." I turned back to face her. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "What happens then?"

"The moon turns thirteen times over the crown, and on the thirteenth turn, the binding completes. The Hollow stops being a tenant in Aldric's skull and becomes the landlord of the realm entire. Permanent. Irreversible. Every mind in the kingdom becomes a field it grazes, and memory goes the way of Bracken Hollow — everywhere, all at once, forever." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, the smile of someone doing arithmetic with lives. "There is one night left. One night before the turn completes. Which means, child, that the dying courier didn't hand you a map."

"No," I said. "He handed me a deadline."

"And a choice." She pressed the invisible jar into my hands. It was warm, and it hummed faintly against my palms, and I understood — with the certainty of the storm-sense I'd spent eleven years strangling — that it held weather. Bottled, held, patient weather. "I'm too old to climb to the citadel. My calling days are behind me; I keep storms now instead of making them. But I can give you this. A piece of the old pact, caught and corked. When the hour comes and you must do the thing you were exiled for—" her grip tightened on my wrist, fierce, sudden, "—you will not be alone in it. Whatever they told you at Redwater about control, forget it. Control was never our gift. *Witness* was. We see the storm coming, and we choose to stand in it."

Outside, far below in the dark valley, thunder rolled. There were no clouds. I felt it in my teeth anyway, felt it answer something in the jar, something in me, something in the map that had begun, very softly, to glow.

Cassel cleared his throat. "I hate to interrupt the prophecy," he said, "but there's smoke in the valley. Lots of it. Moving fast."

Mother Ije sighed and reached for her shawl. "Gray cloaks?"

"Worse," Cassel said, going to the cave mouth, hand on his sword at last with no pretense left. "They're carrying torches. They know she's here."

The old woman looked at me across the fire, and her hail-colored eyes were calm as deep water.

"Well then, warden," she said. "Time to find out what you're made of."
