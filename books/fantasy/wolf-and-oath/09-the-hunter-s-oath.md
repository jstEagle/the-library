## 9. The Hunter's Oath

The oath lives in my left arm.

That's not poetry. That's anatomy. When I was nineteen, kneeling on the cold marble of the Oath Hall with my sword flat across my palms, the king's own chaplain carved a sigil into my forearm with a stylus of black iron, and the spell went in like molten wire. Every hunter gets it. We call it the leash, privately, and we joke about it the way soldiers joke about anything they can't change — *mine pulls left when I'm needed in Thornfell, yours must be broken, ha ha.* But there is no breaking it. I've seen what happens to a hunter who tries. Sergeant Orlan refused an order once — one order, one — and the sigil burned through his arm like paper through flame, and he screamed for two days before his heart gave out. They gave him a hero's burial. They always do. It saves paperwork.

So understand me: I am not a woman given to doubt. Doubt is a luxury for people whose arms don't catch fire.

We'd been tracking the courier's killers — no. Wrong. Let me be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of this miserable week. We had been tracking the map's thief. The courier, Tam Weller, was found at the snowline with his throat opened and his hands empty, and Captain's orders came down from the citadel within a day: recover the map, kill whoever carries it, burn whatever sheltered them. The orders were signed *Aldric R.* in that cramped, precise hand I've known for twenty years.

I'd have followed those orders to the world's edge and off it. That was never the problem.

* * *

The problem started at the Waystone inn, three days behind the thief and losing ground.

Her name was Maren. Sixty if she was a day, ran the inn with her son, poured me ale that tasted like regret and called it their house blend. She'd seen the smugglers' caravan pass, yes. The storm-witch leading them — she'd seen her too, said the woman walked the mountain like she'd been born reading its moods. I thanked her. I asked my standard questions. And then, because I'm thorough and thoroughness is half of why I'm still captain after all these years, I asked who else had come through asking after the caravan.

"No one," she said. "No one official, you mean?"

"Maren." I put a coin on the bar, then another. "Who else."

And here is the thing that has kept me up every night since. Her face did something strange. Not fear — I know fear, I collect it professionally. It was more like... confusion. Like she was reaching for a memory and finding a hole where it should have been.

"There was a man," she said slowly. "Weeks back. He asked about the passes. I can't — I can't recall his face. Isn't that odd? I remember his boots. Good boots. And I remember he paid in crown silver, which nobody does, but I can't..." She pressed her fingers to her temples. "I can't see him."

Crown silver. The king's men pay in crown silver.

I rode out of the Waystone with that itch between my shoulder blades that I've learned not to ignore, and by the time we made camp that night I'd assembled the shape of it without wanting to: someone from the citadel had come through ahead of us, hunting the same quarry, and had done something to Maren's memory of him. Hunters don't erase memories. We don't have that craft. That's court-magic, high craft, the kind that answers to exactly one man in this kingdom.

Except the king doesn't leave the citadel anymore. Everyone knows it. Twenty years ago Aldric rode at the head of his own host; now he signs decrees behind sealed doors and hasn't been seen in open daylight since winter began.

Old kings get strange. I told myself that. I told myself it very firmly, the way you tell yourself a rope bridge will hold while you're already halfway across.

* * *

Two nights later we caught them at the mountain road, or thought we did — the witch's caravan, strung out along a switchback below us in the dark, torches doused, moving quiet as smoke. My orders were simple. Kill the carrier, take the map, no survivors among the escort.

I gave the order to loose.

And my arm — my arm *hesitated.*

Not refused. Hesitated. Half a heartbeat, no more, the way a horse balks at a shadow on the trail. The sigil flared warm against my skin, and for that half-heartbeat I felt something come down the leash that wasn't an order at all. It was more like a *taste.* Cold. Hungry. A sensation like standing in a doorway and realizing the room beyond is much, much larger than the house should allow.

Then the arrows flew, and the moment passed, and I told myself I'd imagined it, because the alternative was to start screaming and not stop.

The ambush went wrong anyway. The knight appeared — Cassel, and gods, of all the names, Cassel Vane, the oath-breaker himself, fighting at the witch's side like he had something to prove to the mountains themselves. We lost two hunters dead and the quarry besides, and I stood in the aftermath listening to my sergeant report casualties and feeling the taste still on my tongue. Cold. Hungry. Large.

"Sergeant Brann," I said, "the writ for this action. Read it to me."

He read it. Signed Aldric R., sealed in black wax, dated four days prior. Standard language. Recover, kill, burn.

"Brann. When did the king last sign a decree in front of witnesses?"

He looked at me like I'd asked him to count the stars. "Captain?"

"Indulge me."

"I — there's the harvest levy, that was signed at the autumn court, but the king didn't attend, his steward read it — and the Thornfell toll decree, that was —" He stopped. His face did the thing Maren's face had done. The reach-and-find-nothing.

"Go on," I said quietly.

"I can't actually recall," he said, "ever seeing him sign one. Which is mad, because obviously he does, they're all in his hand, I've seen the —" He flexed his fingers. "Captain, why do I feel like I'm forgetting something right now?"

"Because you are," I said. "We all are. Keep your sword close, Sergeant, and your mouth closer."

* * *

I want to say I spent that night wrestling with my conscience like an honest woman. I didn't. I spent it doing arithmetic, because conscience is just arithmetic you haven't finished yet.

Item: the king has not been seen in daylight since winter.
Item: no living witness to his signature in over a year.
Item: decrees arriving sealed in black wax, though the royal seal has been crimson for two hundred years — and when I raised this once, months ago, I found myself unable to remember raising it. I only know I raised it because I wrote it in my private log, and the log entry is in my handwriting, and my handwriting doesn't lie to me even when I lie to everything else.
Item: the taste on the leash. Cold. Hungry. Larger than the house.
Item: the purge decrees. Three villages in the eastern valleys, ordered emptied and burned for harboring "sedition," and when my riders got there, they found sheep farmers and a shrine to a river goddess and not one seditious bone among them. We burned them anyway. The leash made sure. I held the torch myself at Hollowmere, and I have washed my hands since until the skin cracked, and the skin stays dirty somehow, under it, where washing doesn't go.

Twenty years I've served that crown. I knelt in the Oath Hall at nineteen believing I was binding myself to a man, and maybe I was, then. But oaths outlive their objects. If the thing wearing the king's hand now isn't the king — if the leash runs to something else entirely, something that erases memory and tastes of hunger —

Then every order I've enforced this year has been a knife held by a stranger.

And worse, far worse, the arithmetic that wouldn't stop: *if the sigil hesitated,* then the leash knows something has changed at the other end. Spells don't hesitate. Spells execute. Whatever holds my oath now is new, or newly awake, or newly *worried* — and things that worry make mistakes, and mistakes leave traces, and traces can be followed.

I am a hunter. Tracing is what I do.

* * *

At dawn I did three things.

First, I wrote in my private log, in full, everything above, and I sealed the book with my blood, because ink can be erased and memories can be eaten, but a blood-seal on a captain's log takes a death to open and mine, I decided, would be expensive.

Second, I summoned Brann and six others — the six whose faces I trusted most, which after this week is a shorter list than it should be — and I told them nothing except that our pursuit route changed. We would no longer drive the witch toward the citadel. We would shadow her. Watch who else hunted her. Find the second set of boot-prints, the ones paid in crown silver, the ones nobody could remember.

Brann studied me a long moment. "And if the witch leads us somewhere we shouldn't go?"

"Then we'll finally learn where the leash wants us not to look," I said. "That's worth knowing, Sergeant. That might be the most valuable intelligence this kingdom has produced in a year."

Third, I took out my knife, and I turned my left arm over, and I looked at the sigil in the morning light. Black lines, old scar-tissue, the geometry of my servitude. It lay quiet. It always lies quiet when watched directly, which I had never once found suspicious until this week, and now finds suspicious as hell.

"You and me," I told it. "Let's find out how long your leash really is."

It didn't answer. Of course it didn't answer.

But for just a moment — half a heartbeat, the length of a hesitation — the lines seemed to shift, the way a sleeping animal shifts when it dreams of running.

And far to the north, past the snowline, past the burned valleys, I swear on my mother's grave I felt it pull.

Not toward the witch.

*Toward home.*
