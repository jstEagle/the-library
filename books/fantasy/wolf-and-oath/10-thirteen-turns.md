## 10. Thirteen Turns

I have lied to a lot of people in my life. Kings, captains, priests, one memorable customs officer in Vell who believed I was a cheese merchant for three days. Lying is a skill, like swordwork — you practice, you get better, and eventually you can do it in your sleep.

But I had never lied to someone the way I was lying to Yara Okonkwo, and the weight of it was starting to bend my spine.

We'd been on the road four days since the ambush on the mountain road, four days of her walking point and me falling into step like I belonged there, and every hour the confession in my chest got heavier. It sat behind my sternum like a swallowed stone. I kept telling myself I'd tell her at the next fire. Then the fire after that. Then when the weather turned. There was always a reason, and every reason was a coward wearing a sensible coat.

The truth, for the record, since I'm apparently in a confessing mood even on paper: six years ago, I stood in a courtyard in the citadel of King Aldric and watched the storm-callers of the Order of the Broken Sky be dragged out of their sanctuary in chains. I didn't swing a blade that day. I didn't have to. I was the king's man then, banner-fresh and oath-proud, and my job was to hold the gate. To make sure no one got out.

One got out. A girl, maybe fourteen, small and quick, with rain following her like a stray dog. I saw her slip through the smoke at the east wall, and I had my hand on the gate lever, and I let it fall.

I told myself for six years that was mercy. That it was the one clean thing I did that day. I have recently begun to suspect that a man who opens one door while guarding a burning house does not get to call himself a rescuer.

* * *

The fire that night was small, because small fires don't draw hunters, and we were close enough to the high passes now that hunters were less a possibility than a weather pattern. Yara sat across from me, turning the map over in her hands — though she barely needed to anymore. She'd read it so many times I suspected it was written on the inside of her eyelids.

Mother Ije sat a little apart, the way she always did, wrapped in gray wool, feeding the flames with one stick at a time like each one was a decision she was making carefully. The old storm-caller had joined us two days out from her hermitage, insisting with terrifying cheerfulness that her divination bones were "itchy," and that she'd rather itch in our company than alone.

"Ask her," Yara said, without looking up.

"Ask her what?" I asked, with the practiced innocence of a man buying time.

"Whatever you've been chewing on for four days. You make a sound when you're not saying something. Like a boot in wet mud." She looked up then, and her eyes were the gray of a sky deciding. "Out with it, Cassel."

Mother Ije, helpfully, said nothing. She just watched me over the fire with the expression of a woman who had already seen the ending of this particular story and was curious how I'd play my part.

I looked at the fire. I looked at my hands, which had held that gate lever. And I understood, finally, that if I didn't say it now, I never would, and I would walk into the citadel beside her carrying it like a knife pointed at her back whether I ever used it or not.

"Six years ago," I said, "the king's men purged your order. The sanctuary at Highmoor. You know this."

Yara went very still. Stillness, I'd learned, was what she did instead of flinching.

"I was there," I said. "I held the gate."

The fire popped. Somewhere on the mountainside, a stone came loose and clattered down, and even the mountain sounded like it was leaving the conversation.

"I didn't kill anyone," I went on, because apparently once the dam broke I was determined to drown us both. "I was ordered to hold the gate and I held the gate, and when a girl got out through the smoke — a child, Yara, she couldn't have been more than fourteen — I had the lever in my hand and I let her go. I've told myself for six years that makes it different. That makes me different." I made myself look at her. "It doesn't. I guarded the door of a burning house and I'm proud of the one person I didn't burn."

Yara set the map down. Slowly. The way you set down something when your hands want to throw it.

"The girl," she said. Her voice was flat, which was worse than if it had been shaking. "The one who got out."

"Yes."

"Small. Dark. Rain followed her."

I felt the bottom drop out of the evening. "Yes."

"That was me." She stood up. Not fast — that was the terrible part, she stood up slowly, like she was conserving herself for something. "I remember the gate. I remember thinking, whoever's on that wall, they're not looking. I remember the smoke covering me. I remember getting down the mountain and looking back and the sanctuary burning and thinking — the gods left one door open for me. One mercy." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "And it was you. It was you the whole time. The man walking beside me, sharing my fire, learning my passes — I've been trusting the lock, not the key."

"Yara—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and I swear the air around it went sharp, the way it does before weather. "Don't say you're sorry. Sorry is what men say when they want the weight moved off their chest and onto mine."

Mother Ije rose, unhurried, and put herself between us — not blocking, exactly, just present, the way a stone is present in a river. "Child," she said to Yara, "walk. Walk until the anger has somewhere to go that isn't a man's face. The bones want to speak tonight anyway, and they don't like an audience that's bleeding."

Yara looked at me one more time. And what was in her face wasn't hate. That was the worst of it. It was arithmetic. She was recalculating every kindness I'd done her, every watch I'd stood, every joke, and filing each one under a new column.

Then she walked into the dark, and I sat down by the fire like a man whose legs had been repossessed.

* * *

Mother Ije laid out her bones on a flat stone while I sat there being the most miserable man in the Thornfell, which is a competitive title.

The bones were small, yellowed, carved with characters I didn't know — though I noticed, with a fresh and unwelcome chill, that some of them matched the script on the map. Storm-script. The writing of the order I had helped bury.

"Read them, then," I said, when she'd finished arranging and rearranging. "Tell me how badly I've ruined everything."

"The bones don't do blame," she said. "Blame is cheap. The bones do timing." She tapped one bone at the center of the pattern, a long one, dark at one end. "This is the moon. Tell me what you see when you look at it at night."

"A moon."

"A moon with a flaw. A thirteenth turn." She looked up at me, and her eyes in the firelight were ancient and sorry. "Every cycle, boy, the moon turns thirteen times before it's done — the last turn is dark, a turn nobody counts. The Hollow has been waiting for the thirteenth turn of this cycle. On that night, the seal completes. Whatever the parasite is, whatever old thing wears the king like a glove — on the thirteenth turn it stops borrowing him and owns him. Permanently. The crown and the thing beneath it become one flesh, and no storm, no blade, no prayer ever pulls them apart again."

I stared at her. "How long?"

She did a small calculation with her fingers, and I watched her arrive at the answer and not enjoy it. "Two nights."

"Two nights," I repeated. "We're two days' hard ride from the citadel, we've got a map that demands payment at the gate, hunters on our trail, and the king of the realm gets eaten forever in two nights."

"Now you have the shape of it," she said. "The bones also say this: the storm that can tear it out exists. It has a name, and the name is walking around in the dark right now being furious at you."

"Then she should leave me and go save the kingdom."

"She could." Mother Ije gathered her bones back into their pouch, one by one, unhurried. "But the bones say something else, and I'll give it to you free, since the rest cost you a friendship. The bones say the gate at the end only opens for two who arrive together. Storm and oath. That's how it was written, that's how it was always written. The order knew, six years ago, that a day would come when a storm-caller and an oathbound man would have to walk into that dark side by side." She tied the pouch shut. "They didn't know which oathbound man. The bones don't do names."

"Convenient."

"The bones don't do convenient, either. They do true." She stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and looked down at me. "Go to her. Not to be forgiven — you won't be, not tonight, maybe not ever, and that's her right. Go to her so that when she stands at that gate, she knows exactly what's walking beside her. A storm doesn't fear a clean blade, boy. It's the hidden ones that cut her."

* * *

I found her at the cliff edge, a half mile up the trail, standing with her arms crossed against the wind and the whole black valley spread out below. Far to the north, past the ridgelines, a smudge of light marked the distant citadel — or I imagined it did. I imagined a lot of things those days.

"I'm not going to apologize again," I said, stopping a few paces back. "You told me not to, and you were right. Sorry moves weight. I'm not trying to move it."

"Then what are you doing?"

"Telling you the rest. The part I left out by the fire." I took a breath, and it went down like cold water. "The order sent word to the crown, years ago. Warnings about the Hollow. About what was growing under the crown. My king's council buried the warnings, and then the council decided the warners were the threat. Your people didn't fall because they were dangerous, Yara. They fell because they were right. And I helped hold the door on the wrong side of that."

The wind moved her hair. She didn't turn around.

"Mother Ije read the bones," I went on. "The thirteenth turn of the moon. Two nights from now, the Hollow finishes sealing itself into the crown. After that, nothing gets it out. And the gate — the gate only opens for a storm and an oath, together. That's what the map was written for. That's what your order died knowing."

"So you're telling me I need you."

"I'm telling you the truth, which is more than I've managed in a while. What you do with it is yours." I hesitated, and then said the thing I'd been carrying longest of all. "If you tell me to walk, I'll walk. I'll point you at the gate and I'll go, and I'll spend whatever years I have left making sure the hunters never find your trail. But if there's any chance — not forgiveness, I'm not asking for forgiveness, just — a chance that you'd rather walk into that dark with a man whose worst sin is standing in the open where you can see it, than with your back to one who's still hiding his — then I'm here. I'll be here until you tell me otherwise."

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I counted my heartbeats and got bored of it.

"You let me go," she said finally, still facing the valley. "Six years ago. You had the lever and you let me go. Why?"

"Because it was wrong," I said. "All of it. And I didn't stop it. Letting one girl through the gate was the smallest possible piece of stopping it, and I took even that piece like it made me a good man." I shook my head. "It didn't. But I'd do it again."

She turned then. Her face in the starlight was unreadable, weather without a forecast.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "I want that clear. What you did — the burning, the gate, all of it — that doesn't wash out because you're useful now. But the citadel is north, the moon turns in two nights, and I need an oathbound man who knows the inside of that place more than I need the satisfaction of watching you walk away into the dark." She picked up her pack from the rocks where she'd dropped it. "So we go. Together. And Cassel —"

"Yes?"

"When this is done, if we're both still breathing, you and I are going to finish this conversation. All of it. Every year of it."

"That's fair," I said. "I'd ask for nothing better."

"That's the problem with you," she said, and there was — almost, at the very edge of it — the ghost of the dry note I'd first heard on the mountain road. "You'd ask for nothing. That's how men like you survive. You never ask for the thing you actually want."

She shouldered her pack and started down the trail toward the fire, toward Mother Ije, toward the north. I followed, the way I had followed for four days, except that now she knew I was behind her, and had chosen — for two nights, at least, for the length of one desperate road — to let me stay there.

Behind us, low on the horizon, the moon was rising. I looked at it the way Mother Ije had taught me to look, counting its turns, and found what she'd promised I'd find: the flaw, the dark seam, the thirteenth turn waiting like a held breath.

Two nights. Somewhere north of us, in a citadel full of whispering stone, something ancient was counting too.

And I would have sworn on every broken oath I had — it was counting faster than we were.
