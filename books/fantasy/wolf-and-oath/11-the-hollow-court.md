## 11. The Hollow Court

I woke with blood on my hands.

This was, by now, a familiar way to begin a morning. Not the blood itself — though there was always blood — but the not-knowing. The gap in the ledger of me. I lay in the great bed of Aldric's Rest and studied my palms the way a man studies dice he suspects are weighted. The blood had dried in the creases, brown as old rust, and it told me nothing except that sometime between midnight and dawn, *I* — or the thing wearing my voice, my seal, my increasingly heavy crown — had done something that bled.

There was a decree on the desk by the window. I did not remember writing it.

That was the trick of it, you understand. I want to be clear about this, because history will get it wrong — history always gets kings wrong; it assumes we are either monsters or saints, when most of us are simply tired men with too many pens — I want to be very clear: I never consented. Not once. Not at the beginning, when it came to me as a whisper in the dark years after the plague took my queen, whispering things like *you are so strong* and *they will take everything from you unless* — not then, and not now. Whatever the chroniclers write, whatever the priests decide to canonize or condemn, know this: every cruel thing done under my seal these past six years was done by a prisoner forging documents with his own hand while his jailer held the pen.

The decree read:

*By royal command, the quarter known as Lowmarsh is declared seditious. Its records are stricken. Its people are remanded for questioning. Any who shelter them share their guilt.*

Lowmarsh. A fishing quarter, mostly. Salt-rough people who mended nets and sang off-key and had never in four generations produced anything more seditious than a complaint about the price of lamp oil.

I picked up the decree and I burned it in the candle flame, and I watched the wax seal — my face, my crown, pressed into red — curl and blacken like a dying insect, and I thought: *this changes nothing.* Because somewhere below, in the chancery, there were eleven copies already inked, and the criers were already being paid, and the thing in my head was already laughing.

It doesn't laugh out loud. That would almost be bearable. It laughs the way frost laughs — silently, everywhere at once, in the slow cracking of everything you thought was solid.

"You're awake early," said a voice from the doorway, warm and wrong.

Sera. My cupbearer, my reader of petitions, the only servant left in this citadel whose eyes still looked like eyes instead of windows with the shutters drawn. She carried the morning tray: bread, honey, watered wine, all of it tasteless to me now — the Hollow eats appetite before it eats memory, and memory before it eats the soul, working inward like a worm in fruit.

"Bad night," I said.

"They're all bad nights, Majesty."

"Yes," I said. "But some are worse than others, and last night someone bled."

She set down the tray. Her hands were steady. Sera's hands were always steady; it was one of the thousand small reasons I trusted her, because fear makes hands flutter and hers never did, even the day she found me standing in the throne room at 3 a.m., talking to no one, arguing with no one, losing to no one.

"Whose?" she asked quietly.

"I don't know," I said, and meant it, and hated it. "That's rather the whole of my problem, Sera. I don't know anything anymore. I wake up and the kingdom has moved in the night and everyone insists I'm the one who moved it."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something no servant should do: she crossed the room, knelt, and took my bloody hands in her clean ones.

"Majesty," she said, "the guards outside your door changed yesterday. New faces. They don't salute me anymore."

"I know."

"The captain of the watch was reassigned. No reason given."

"I know."

"And the storm-caller you've been asking about — the girl, the exile, Yara Okonkwo — the hunters found her trail two days ago and the palace hasn't slept since. Something down in the deep rooms is *excited*, Majesty. The stones hum at night. I hear them through the floor."

So did I. Gods help me, so did I. At night the citadel hummed like a struck bell, and I had learned what that sound meant: it meant the Hollow was pleased, and it was only ever pleased about one kind of thing.

"Sera," I said. "How long have you served this house?"

"Eleven years."

"Then you remember my wife."

Something flickered across her face. "Yes, Majesty."

"Queen Maren used to say that a court is just a family that learned to lie in unison." I smiled, and I felt the smile crack down the middle like river ice. "Well. Mine has become very good at lying. All of them. Every steward, every clerk, every guard who won't meet my eye — they're not corrupt, Sera, they're *frightened*, and something is teaching them to be frightened in exactly the right ways. Except you." I turned my hands over and gripped hers. "Except you. Why?"

"Because I remember what you were," she said simply. "Before. And because my grandmother came from Lowmarsh, and if the decree stands, I'll be remanded for questioning by week's end anyway. So I'd rather spend what time I have on the side of the truth."

I laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt, these days; the Hollow does not permit its host the comfort of numbness, because numbness is a kind of peace and peace is a door it cannot open. It keeps me raw. It keeps me feeling every hour of every day, so that despair stays fresh.

"Listen to me," I said, and my voice dropped low, low enough that only she could hear, low enough that I hoped — prayed — the humming stones were sleeping in. "There is a woman coming here. Storm-caller. Exiled, hunted, probably furious, certainly brave, because only the brave or the doomed walk toward this citadel. When she comes — and she will come, it wants her to come, which means I must want it too, do you see? Our wants have been braided together and I can no longer tell which threads are mine—"

"Majesty. Breathe."

"—when she comes, you will find her. You'll know how; you're cleverer than half my council ever was. And you will give her a message. My message. Word for word, because I may not have the chance to say it twice, and because parts of me forget things now, important things, and I need this written where I can't lose it."

She fetched paper from my own desk — my own paper, the king's paper, hidden in her sleeve like contraband, and there's an indictment of my reign if ever there was one: that in my own house, my own letterhead required smuggling.

I dictated:

*"To Yara Okonkwo, of the Order of the Ninefold Gale — if any of that order still breathes to receive it.*

*I am Aldric Vane, third of that name, and I am not free. There is a thing in my crown and it has been drinking me for six years. It writes my decrees. It signs my wars. Last night it spent lives I will never learn the names of, and I woke with the cost on my hands.*

*I remember your order. I remember the purge — I remember watching it happen from behind glass eyes that were mine and not mine, screaming into a throat that would not scream. I remember a child in a burned courtyard, nine years old, storm-light in her fists, and I remember the thing wanted her dead and I — whatever was left of me — I turned the soldiers' gaze aside. One moment. One small treason. It cost me a year of my own mind, and it was the best year I ever spent, because I believe you lived.*

*So hear me now. Come. Come not to kill the king but to free him. There is a seam beneath the thirteenth moon — the Hollow fears it, I feel its fear like cold iron in my teeth — and I beg you, storm-caller: tear it out. Tear it out of me though it kills me. Especially if it kills me. I have been a vessel six years. I would rather be a corpse than a crown's puppet for one day more.*

*Tell her — tell her the man remembers sparing her, and asks her pardon for every night since.*

*Aldric."*

Sera wrote it all without a tremor. When she finished, she read it back once, folded it three times, and tucked it away.

"If the hunters find that on me," she said, "they'll hang me from the gate."

"Yes."

"All right," said Sera, and stood, and straightened her apron as if she were going to fetch wine instead of carry a death warrant against the throne. "Anything else, Majesty?"

"One thing." I hesitated. Six years of learning caution the hard way — every hesitation punished, every kindness taxed — and yet. "When you find her. Tell her I'm sorry the first thing my freedom will cost her is everything else."

* * *

The Hollow let me have the morning.

Understand: it always lets me have just enough. A few hours of lucidity, a meal, a walk in the gardens where the roses bloom out of season because it likes beauty the way a miser likes coins — owned, never enjoyed. It lets me surface because my surfacing serves it. A drowning man who occasionally breaches the water looks, from the shore, like a swimmer. My lucid mornings were its alibi. On those days I sat council, I heard petitions, I smiled at ambassadors, and every lordling went home saying *the king seemed well, the king seemed himself,* and the whispers of a mad king stayed whispers.

It was during petition hour that I made my mistake.

A fishwife from — of course — Lowmarsh had walked a hundred miles to stand in my hall and ask, with the blunt courage of women who haul nets for a living, why her quarter had been declared seditious. She said her father's name. She said her grandmother's name. She said, "Your Majesty, we've fished that water since before there was a crown, and no paper can make us strangers in our own homes."

And I — lucid, foolish, briefly myself — stood up from the throne and said, loudly, in front of forty witnesses: "Madam, I rescind the decree. Go home. Tell Lowmarsh the king remembers them."

You could have heard a pin drop in a thunderstorm.

For one heartbeat, the hall was silent, and I felt something I hadn't felt in six years: *power.* Real power, mine, spent on mercy. It lasted exactly as long as the silence.

Then the cold came.

It comes up through the crown first — the crown sits heavier than gold has any right to sit — and then down the spine, vertebra by vertebra, like fingers walking down a ladder. The hall tilted. The fishwife's face went gray and distant. And inside my skull, in the voice that was becoming better acquainted with my thoughts than I was, the Hollow said:

*Interesting.*

Not angry. Anger would have been a mercy. It was interested, the way a naturalist is interested in a beetle that has done something unexpected.

*You gave back a decree. Do you know what that cost me? Three days of weaving, undone. Do you know what I do with three wasted days, little king?*

"No," I said aloud, through unmoving lips, while my body smiled graciously at the court. "*What do you do.*"

*I collect.*

That evening the bells rang wrong.

I was at my window when it started — the true dusk, the honest dark, the hour I had privately begun to think of as mine, since the Hollow slept shallowest then. Across the city, torches bloomed along the Processional Way, one after another, like a fuse burning. I counted the streets they took. I counted the direction.

They went down. Down toward the river. Toward Lowmarsh.

They called it questioning. The criers called it questioning, the clerks called it questioning, and the official record, written in my hand by a chancellor whose eyes had gone shuttered and dull two winters past, called it *a necessary culling of sedition.* What it was, was sixty households dragged into the winter street, and a granary burned because a boy threw a stone at a soldier, and a grandmother named Odessa — Sera's grandmother, though neither of us knew it yet, though the knowledge was waiting for us both like a knife in a drawer — a grandmother named Odessa put herself between a soldier and her net-mending shed and did not move again.

I stood at my window and watched the fires, and the Hollow watched with me, and it was — this is the part I will never make anyone believe — it was *polite* about it. It stood beside my grief like a courteous host at a funeral, murmuring, *You see? This is what defiance costs. Nothing personal. Merely arithmetic. You spent a decree; I collected interest.*

"I hate you," I said, to the window, to the fires, to the thing in my head.

*No, you don't,* it said, with terrible gentleness. *Hate requires knowing what you hate. You haven't met me. You've only met your reflection wearing my expression. But soon, little king — soon the moon turns her thirteenth face, and then we will finally be introduced properly. And you will stop being a tenant in your own skull and become, at last, what you were always meant to be.*

"Which is?"

*Furniture.*

* * *

Sera came at midnight, smelling of smoke, and I knew before she spoke. Her steadiness was gone; her hands shook as she poured wine neither of us drank.

"They took the whole quarter," she whispered. "My grandmother—" Her voice failed and restarted, like a lamp in wind. "They say the king ordered it. They say it in the streets, Majesty. They're saying the king has gone mad, and gods forgive me, I defended you, I *defended* you, and now I don't know what I'm defending."

"The message," I said. "Did you send it?"

"It's sent. There's a tinker leaving tomorrow for the southern passes — he owes me — it goes with him." She set down the decanter. "Majesty, if she comes... if this storm-caller actually comes... what happens to you?"

I looked at the window, at the distant orange stain on the clouds where a fishing quarter used to keep its lamps lit.

"She tears it out of me," I said. "Or she fails, and it seals itself in forever, and the man you defended stops existing entirely. Either way, Sera, the king you knew ended six years ago. Everything since has been the echo."

She knelt then, and took my hand — my clean hand, the other still bore the ghost of the morning's blood no scrubbing would lift — and she said the thing I would carry into whatever darkness waited beneath the thirteenth moon:

"Echoes travel, Majesty. That's rather what echoes do."

After she left, I sat alone with the humming stones, and far away, down in the deep rooms, the bell-note of the citadel changed pitch — rising, quickening, eager — and I understood with perfect clarity that the thing in my crown had heard the message leave.

It let it go.

It *wanted* her to come.

And somewhere south, a woman with storms in her blood was reading my plea by tinker's candlelight, walking toward a trap that had my face on it — and all I could do, all the King of Thornfell could do, locked in his own body like a beggar locked out of his own house, was press his palm to the cold glass and whisper to a stranger a hundred miles away:

*Don't trust the gates. Don't trust the servants. Don't trust the king.*

*Trust the storm.*
