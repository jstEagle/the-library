## 12. Pass of Ash

The map stopped pretending to be kind the morning we reached the edge of the burn.

I'd known it was coming — Mother Ije had told us, Cassel had told me, and even the map itself had been sulking about it for two days, its storm-script darkening like a bruise whenever I unrolled it. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it are different animals entirely. Knowing is a story you tell yourself by firelight. Standing is the thing itself, breathing ash into your lungs.

We called it the Pass of Ash because nobody had ever bothered to give it another name after what happened here. Forty years ago, before I was born, there had been a village at the mouth of this pass. A whole town, actually — millers, shepherds, a shrine to some river god whose name even Mother Ije had lost. Then the Hollow had looked at a map of the Thornfells, seen that road as a thread leading somewhere it didn't want anyone to go, and burned everything from ridgeline to riverbed. Not with soldiers. With weather. It had worn Aldric's grandfather's crown by then, or his father's — Ije wasn't sure which — and it had simply *asked* the sky for fire, and the sky, being an old servant of older things, had obeyed.

The pass still hadn't recovered. Forty years, and nothing grew taller than my knee. The trees stood where they'd died, gray and leafless, like an audience that had been waiting so long for the play to start that they'd forgotten why they came. The ground crunched underfoot — not snow, not gravel, but ash compacted into something between soil and bone. Every footstep sounded like walking on old promises.

"Well," said Bren, our smuggler chief, spitting into the gray dust. "Cheerful place."

Bren had come back to us at the foot of Mother Ije's mountain with six of his people and no apologies, which was the most apology Bren ever gave anybody. He'd left us after chapter one of this disaster — the courier, the map, the hunters — on the grounds that dead smugglers earn nothing. Then he'd heard that we were going after the king himself, and apparently the chance to rob the richest house in the realm outweighed his excellent survival instincts.

"It gets worse," I said.

"It always gets worse," said Bren. "That's how I know you're honest."

Mother Ije said nothing. She stood at the head of our little column, small and straight-backed as a fence post, her white braids tied off with copper wire, and she studied the pass the way other people study scripture. She'd walked out of her mountain hut with us without a single argument, carrying only her staff, a bag of dried fish, and a stoppered glass bottle wrapped in wool that she refused to explain. When I'd asked about it, she'd said, "It isn't yours yet." When I'd asked what that meant, she'd smiled, and I had learned over the past week that Mother Ije's smiles were things you survived rather than enjoyed.

"The wind comes through at midmorning," she said now. "From the north. It will carry sound up the pass — ours to them, theirs to us. We move quiet until noon, then we don't move at all."

"And after noon?" Cassel asked.

"After noon," said Mother Ije, "we will see."

Cassel shifted beside me. He did that when he was worried — a small settling of weight, like a man adjusting a pack he couldn't put down. He'd been doing it more since the night he'd told me about the purge. About how he'd stood in a square very much like this one, twenty years ago, with a sword in his hand and an oath in his mouth, while storm-callers burned. About how he'd let one child run — me — and called it mercy ever since, though we both knew it was cowardice wearing mercy's coat.

I hadn't forgiven him. I want to be clear about that, in case my ghost reads this someday and gets ideas. But I hadn't left him either, and I'd begun to suspect that those two facts together were what forgiveness actually looked like when it was still wet paint.

"Yara." His voice was low. "If Vess catches us in there—"

"She won't."

"You don't know that."

"I know the pass." I nodded at the dead trees, the ash, the narrow throat of stone ahead where the mountains pinched together like a fist closing. "Vess knows orders. There's a difference, and it's about forty years wide."

Cassel didn't laugh. He never laughed at my jokes, which I chose to interpret as respect.

* * *

We went in at dawn, and the pass ate us quietly.

For four hours it was almost easy, if you can call scrambling over fused rock and wading through knee-deep ash drifts easy. The map led us along the eastern wall, through a slot canyon so tight that Bren had to unload his mule and carry the cargo through on his back while swearing in three languages and inventing a fourth. The storm-script on the parchment glowed faintly whenever we faced the right direction, like a dog tugging its leash. Somewhere above us, always above, the sky stayed a flat bruised gray, neither storming nor clearing, as if it couldn't decide whom to obey.

It was during the fourth hour that I noticed Mother Ije falling behind.

Not much. A step, then two steps, then the length of a fallen tree. She moved slower than any of us and always had, but this was different. This was a woman measuring her pace against something. I dropped back to walk with her, and she didn't pretend she hadn't been doing it, because Mother Ije didn't lie, which made her the most exhausting person I have ever traveled with.

"You're tired," I said.

"I'm old," she corrected. "Tired is temporary. Old is a decision I made decades ago and stand by."

"Then ride the mule."

"The mule and I have an understanding. It carries; I don't ask questions." She glanced up at me, and her eyes were bright and black and far too amused for a woman walking into a graveyard. "You're worried about me. That's sweet. Save it."

"Save it for what?"

"For tonight." She patted my arm once, briskly, the way you'd pat a horse you liked. "Walk forward, child. Watch the sky. And when the time comes — and it will come today, I think, because the Hollow has never once in forty years failed to be exactly where it's least wanted — you do what I say. Quickly. Without arguing."

"I never argue quickly," I said. "Arguing is a slow art."

"Mm." And she smiled that smile again, and I felt cold all down my spine, and I hated her a little for it, and I loved her too, which is a thing I was still getting used to feeling about anyone.

We made the narrows at noon.

The map wanted us through a gap in the eastern wall — a chimney of stone, maybe two arm-spans wide, climbing sixty feet to a ledge that supposedly bypassed the worst of the pass and delivered us to the high trail by nightfall. It was brutal climbing, the kind of thing you do with your fingertips and your temper. Bren's people went first, hauling the packs. Then the mule, who objected loudly enough that I feared for our cover. Then Cassel. Then me.

Mother Ije went last.

She climbed well, for a liar. Hand over hand, steady, unhurried, her staff slung across her back. I watched her from the ledge, counting her progress the way you count heartbeats when you're afraid. She was ten feet below the top when the wind changed.

It came down the pass all at once — not the north wind she'd promised but a south wind, wrong by half a day, and it carried sound with it. Hooves. A lot of hooves. And beneath them, thin as a knife slipped under a door: a hunting horn.

"They're in the pass," Cassel breathed. He'd gone pale in that way he did, color draining top-down like water through sand. "Behind us. How—"

"The mule," said Bren grimly. "Or the gods hate us. Coin flip."

I unrolled the map with shaking hands. The storm-script had gone frantic, lines crawling over each other, and I read it the way you read a room full of people deciding whether to fight: fast, and mostly correctly. There was a second route off the ledge — a goat track along the western face, narrow, unmapped except by goats, dropping toward the high trail two miles on. Two miles of open cliffside. If Vess reached the base of the ledge before we cleared it—

"We go," I said. "All of us. Now."

"No," said Mother Ije.

Everyone stopped. Even the mule, who frankly should take notes.

The old woman stood on the ledge with the horn sounding behind us and the gray sky pressing down, and she looked calm the way deep water looks calm, which is to say you only understand how much is moving underneath when it takes you. She unwrapped the glass bottle from her shoulder bag and held it up to the light, and inside it something swirled — silver and blue and violet, turning slowly, like a storm caught mid-thought.

"I told you it wasn't yours yet," she said to me. "This is why."

"What is it?"

"A storm. My last one, bottled thirty years ago, back when my hands were quicker and my knees were lies I hadn't yet told myself. It's a full calling, Yara. Everything I ever was, sealed in glass." She pressed it into my hands. It was warm. Warm as a living thing. "You'll need it tomorrow night. The thirteenth turn doesn't wait for courage; it waits for weather. But today—" she looked back down the pass, toward the sound of hooves, and her voice went gentle in a way that frightened me more than the horn did, "—today I have other work."

"No," I said. It came out of me before I'd decided anything, raw and stupid and certain. "No. Ije. No."

"Child." She took my face in both her hands, and her palms smelled of copper wire and dried fish and thirty years of mountain huts, and I thought, absurdly, of my grandmother, whom I had not thought of in eleven years. "You asked me once why my order wrote the map. Why we hid a road to the citadel inside storm-script only one of us could read. Do you remember?"

"Because the Hollow hunts us. Because someone had to leave a door."

"Because someone had to leave a door," she agreed. "And doors need someone to hold them shut while others go through." She kissed my forehead, once, hard, like sealing a letter. "Hold the line for me someday. That's all I ask. Go."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do, and slow, the way you remember them.

Mother Ije stepped off the ledge.

She didn't fall — of course she didn't fall; she was a storm-caller, exiled like me, branded like me, and the air caught her the way it always catches its own, setting her down in the ash at the base of the cliff as lightly as a leaf. She turned to face down the pass, alone, a small straight-backed figure in a dead forest, and she raised her staff, and the sky — the flat, bruised, undecided sky — finally made up its mind.

It went black over the southern ridge. Thunder rolled through the pass like furniture being moved in a god's house. And Mother Ije began, in her thin clear voice, to call the storm she'd spent thirty years saving, spending it all at once, every coin in her purse, buying us hours with the only currency she had left.

"MOVE!" Bren roared, and we moved.

* * *

I won't describe the next two hours honestly, because honesty would require admitting that I climbed the western goat track with tears freezing on my face and that I heard everything.

I heard the hunters' horns falter as the storm broke over them. I heard horses screaming — Vess's riders, oathbound men and women who were following orders from a thing wearing their king's face, and I hated myself for grieving them, and I grieved them anyway. I heard thunder answer thunder, the storm-calling rising and rising, and woven through it, impossibly, I heard singing. Old words. Order-words. The lullaby dialect my grandmother used to hum, the one they beat out of us in the temples, the one I thought was extinct.

Mother Ije was singing in it. At the end. Alone in a burned pass with an army coming and the sky coming down, and she sang like a woman planting a garden she'd never sit in.

The last thing I heard, just before the wind took even that away, was a single peal of lightning so vast and white that the whole western face of the mountain lit up like dawn arriving early — and then silence, the enormous ringing silence that follows when the sky has said absolutely everything it intends to say.

We cleared the goat track at dusk. We came down onto the high trail with the citadel's towers visible at last, far across the valley, black against a red horizon, crowned in cloud. Behind us, the Pass of Ash glowed faintly, lit by fires I couldn't name and wouldn't look at.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The mule, showing more sense than the rest of us, drank from a stream and said nothing.

It was Bren, finally, who broke it, in the only language he had. "She bought us the night," he said roughly. "Maybe two. Vess's riders will be picking themselves out of ditches till midnight, and the captain herself—" he spat, "—the captain doesn't quit. So we don't either. We walk."

We walked. Cassel walked beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and he didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything, and somehow that was the kindest thing anyone had done for me all day.

But I'll tell you what I carried down off that mountain besides the bottle, warm against my chest like a second heartbeat. I carried her last instruction, the one she'd pressed into my hands along with the glass:

*When the time comes, you do what I say. Quickly. Without arguing.*

And under it, in storm-script that hadn't been there when she handed it to me — script that rose on the bottle's surface as I watched, curling silver in the dusk, written by a dead woman's last breath of power or by the storm itself or by whatever it is in this world that keeps accounts — five words:

*The thirteenth moon remembers.*

Ahead of us, the citadel's clouds parted for a moment, just long enough to show the moon rising fat and pale over King Aldric's towers.

One turn left.
