## 13. The Price of the Gate

### Yara

The gate was a lie told in stone.

That's what I thought, anyway, when Cassel and I finally stood before it on the fourth morning after Mother Ije's pyre. The pass of ash had spat us out onto a shelf of granite below the citadel's western face, and there — exactly where the map insisted — was nothing. Cliff. Lichen. A scree slope that wanted to kill us. No door, no arch, no seam.

"Your map," Cassel said, in the flat voice he used when he was being polite about disappointment, "is pointing at a rock."

"The map is never just pointing," I said. I held it up anyway, because arguing with him felt more useful than admitting my hands were shaking. Four days since Ije had turned to face Vess's hunters alone. Four days of climbing with her storm bottled in glass against my ribs like a second heart. "It points at what's true. The rock is the true part. We have to find the part that isn't."

I pressed my palm flat against the granite.

The script rose under my skin first. That's how it always worked now — the storm-script didn't live on the map anymore so much as it lived in me, waking when I came near its own kind. It crawled up my forearm like cold water running backward, and where my hand met the cliff, letters bloomed across the stone in silver-gray, as if someone had written on the mountain from inside it.

Cassel went very still beside me. He'd seen it twice before and still hadn't gotten used to it. Neither had I, if we're being honest. You don't get used to your own bones having handwriting.

*What will you pay?* said the wall.

Not out loud. Nothing so kind as sound. The words arrived the way weather does — all at once, everywhere, already inside the tent. I snatched my hand back.

"It asked something," I said.

"I heard it." Cassel had his sword half-drawn, which was the most useless thing he could have done and very much him. "Yara. What did it ask?"

"*Pay.*" I looked at the letters still glowing faintly where my hand had been. "It wants payment for opening."

"Coin?"

"Do I look like a woman carrying coin?" I'd spent the last of it in the forgotten village, buying bread from people who no longer remembered what money was for. They'd given it back to me, eventually, puzzled. "The Hollow eats minds, Cassel. Its doors don't want silver."

He understood before I finished saying it. I watched him understand — watched the color leave his face by degrees, the way dusk leaves a valley. "Memory."

"Memory."

* * *

### Cassel

There is an arithmetic that soldiers learn young: what a thing costs, weighed against what it buys. Rations against miles. Men against ground. My brother Tam taught me a cruder version of it when we were boys — *never gamble what you can't afford to lose* — and then he broke his own rule at the ford at Callowmere, gambling his life on my word that reinforcements would come.

They did not come. I have done sums ever since.

So I stood before that talking rock and I did the arithmetic honestly, which is more than I managed at Callowmere. What would the gate take? A memory. Which memory? The mountain didn't say, but mountains rarely negotiate in good faith, and neither do old magics built by dead orders. It would take what hurt most. Things always do. Pain is the only currency that can't be counterfeited.

"Then we don't open it," Yara said.

She had her arms crossed and her chin set at the angle she reserved for decisions she'd already made and dared me to contest. I was learning her angles one by one. This one meant trouble.

"We've climbed four days through a burned pass," I said. "We buried the only person who understood that map. Behind us are hunters who may or may not be our enemies now, and ahead of us is a king being eaten alive. And you want to stop here because the door has a price."

"I want to stop here because the price is *you*, you stubborn, honor-drunk —" She stopped. Recalibrated. She did that when she caught herself caring too loudly. "Whatever it takes, it'll take the worst thing you own. I know how these bargains work. My whole order worked them."

"And what did they pay with?"

Her jaw tightened. "Everything. Eventually." She turned away toward the drop, wind pulling at the gray streak in her hair — new since Ije's pyre; she hadn't noticed it yet, or pretended not to. "Find another way in. There's always another way in. Sewers, posterns, a bribed cook. There were ways into the Thornfell citadel too, once, and I knew every one of them before I was exiled for a storm I couldn't —"

She stopped again. Different reason this time. Below us, far down the scree, something glinted. Metal. A lot of it.

Vess's hunters had found the pass after all. Or what was left of them had — I counted perhaps a dozen banners' worth of movement, hours behind us but closing, and between us and any other route into the citadel.

"There is no other way in," I said quietly.

"I know," Yara said, not turning around. "I hate that you're right more than usual."

* * *

### Yara

Here's a thing nobody tells you about magic: the old kind, the real kind, is mostly manners. You don't command a storm. You ask. You don't carve a door into a mountain. You knock, and you bring a gift worthy of the visit, and you pray the thing behind the door is in a generous mood.

My order had known this. That's why their gates took memories — because a memory freely given is the only honest gift a person has. Gold can be stolen back. Blood regrows. But give away the day your daughter was born and it stays gone, and everyone knows the giver paid true.

Which is why I put myself between Cassel and the wall when he stepped forward.

"No."

"You don't even know what it would take from me."

"I know what it takes. It takes worst." I kept my palm raised toward the silvered letters, though I wasn't touching the stone anymore. "And I'm not letting you feed your brother to a door, Cassel. Not him. Not the last of him."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, startled, the look of a man caught doing something tender. "You remember what I told him about?"

"Tam. The ford." Of course I remembered. He'd confessed it on the road to Mother Ije's, voice like a man reading his own death warrant, and I'd hated him for a day and a half and then discovered, annoyingly, that I'd forgiven him somewhere in the middle of the night without filing the paperwork. "You talk about him like he died yesterday. Twenty years, Cassel. You kept him fresh that long. That's not a wound. That's a shrine."

"A shrine can burn," he said. "Shrines are for the living, Yara. He's dead either way. I'm not."

"And if the door takes him and you walk through hollow?" My voice cracked on *hollow*, which I chose to blame on the altitude. "You think I can watch that happen twice? I've spent this whole cursed journey watching the Hollow eat people. I'm not holding the plate."

Behind us, down the scree, the hunters were closer. An hour, maybe less. The arithmetic was doing itself whether we liked it or not.

Cassel looked at me for a long moment. Then he did the last thing I expected: he smiled. Small, crooked, tired — the first one I'd ever seen on him that reached his eyes.

"You'd give yours instead," he said. "Wouldn't you. Whatever it asked of you — home, your mother's face, the night of the exile — you'd hand it over without blinking."

"Don't flatter yourself. I'd do it for anyone."

"You wouldn't. You keep yourself like a miser keeps coin." He stepped past my raised arm, gently, the way you step past a guard dog you've decided to befriend. "Let me do one clean thing. One. I've broken every oath I ever swore except the ones worth keeping, and this —" he laid his bare palm against the letters — "this one I mean."

The mountain woke.

* * *

### Cassel

Pain, when it came, was not pain. That's the lie I'd tell anyone who asks. It was subtraction.

The letters drank from my hand and the world began losing things. Small first — the taste of the bread from the forgotten village, the name of the horse I rode at nineteen. Then larger, and I understood the door was sorting, weighing, deciding what hurt most, and I braced for the ford. For Tam's laugh. For the moment the water closed over his helmet and my promise drowned with him.

Take it, I thought. Take all of it. Let it buy the way.

But the mountain paused. And in the pause I heard — or felt, or remembered into existence — a voice like Yara's but older, layered, many-voiced, the sound of an entire dead order speaking through stone:

*Not yours alone. Both, or neither.*

I turned. Yara had her hand pressed to the rock beside mine. When had she — the stubborn, magnificent, impossible —

"You said you wouldn't hold the plate," she said. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I lied. Apparently I'm a liar now. Blame the company I keep."

"Yara —"

"Both," she told the mountain, and her grip tightened on mine where our hands lay together on the stone, and I did not move mine away, which tells you everything about where the last months have taken me. "Both of ours. Split the bill."

The mountain considered.

*Accepted,* it said, and the world subtracted.

I lost — I am telling this as honestly as I can, because honesty is all I have left of certain things — I lost the smell of rain on the training yard where Tam and I learned the sword. I lost my mother's lullaby, and knew I had lost it, which is its own particular grief: mourning a hole shaped like a song. And I lost the ford. All of it. The water, the shouting, the shame. Where my brother's death lived there is now a room with clean walls and nothing in it, and when I reach for his face I find instead — her. Yara. Standing on a granite shelf with wind in her hair, calling me *stubborn, honor-drunk*, her hand over mine on the stone.

The door gave me her in exchange for him.

I should grieve longer than I do. Perhaps I will, later, when there's time. But the rock split along lines of silver light, seams unfolding outward like a flower deciding to bloom after a century of patience, and beyond it lay stairs descending into the dark beneath the citadel, and Yara was swaying, pale as milk, catching herself on my arm.

"What did it take?" I asked her.

Her mouth opened. Closed. "I don't—" She frowned, searching. "Something. Something big. I can feel the shape of the hole but not the hole." She laughed, unsteady. "That's the trick of it, isn't it. You can't miss what you can't remember missing."

"Welcome to my life," I said, and steadied her, and did not let go, and she did not pull away.

Down the scree, steel rang on stone — the hunters, close enough now to matter. From the dark of the stairwell rose a draft smelling of deep earth and old bone. Somewhere far below, something that wore a king's face stirred in its sleep.

"Run first," Yara said, gripping the bottled storm against her ribs, "grieve later."

"For once," I said, drawing my sword to cover our descent, "we agree on the order of operations."
