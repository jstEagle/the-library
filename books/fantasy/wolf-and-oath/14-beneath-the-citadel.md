## 14. Beneath the Citadel

The gate took Cassel's brother's memory, swallowed it like a coin into a beggar's palm, and then—nothing. No grinding of ancient stone, no trumpet blast of destiny. Just a wall of black rock that decided, apparently, to stop being a wall.

One moment I was looking at solid stone. The next, there was a stairway spiraling down into the dark, wide enough for two, carved with grooves that might have been decorative or might have been teeth.

"Charming," Cassel said beside me. His voice had been wrong since the gate closed behind us—flat in a way that had nothing to do with grief and everything to do with a man trying very hard not to fall apart until the job was done. "I've served in this citadel eleven years. I never knew this existed."

"That's rather the point of hidden gates."

"I mean I never *felt* it." He touched the wall beside the stairway, and his hand came away dusty. "There are old ways under Aldric's seat. Every knight learns them as boys—the servant passages, the cisterns. This isn't one of them. This is older than the citadel itself."

I looked down the spiral. The dark below didn't look empty. It looked *patient*.

"Well," I said, "when has going somewhere older than we are ever gone badly?"

Cassel didn't laugh. That was how I knew the memory-loss was sitting on him harder than he'd let on. Cassel joked when he hurt; I'd learned that much about him on the road. A man who doesn't joke is a man who's run out of places to put the pain.

I lit my hand with a thread of lightning—not enough to fight with, just enough to see by—and started down.

* * *

The stairs went deeper than any cellar had a right to go. I counted four hundred steps before I stopped counting, which is the kind of thing you do when you're afraid, because counting feels like control and control is a lie you tell yourself while your feet carry you somewhere your good sense would never approve.

The walls changed as we descended. Near the top they were dressed stone, citadel work. Then rougher blocks, pre-citadel, the kind of masonry you find in ruins nobody claims. And then the walls weren't built at all—they were *carved*, cut straight out of the mountain's living bone, and covered in script.

Storm-script.

I stopped so fast Cassel nearly walked into me.

It covered every surface, spiraling downward with us, line after line after line, and it wasn't decorative. It was a ledger. I could read enough of it now—Mother Ije's teaching had burned the basics into me during those weeks in her hut—to know that much. Names. Dates by moon-turn. And beside each name, a small, precise notation I couldn't parse but recognized the shape of: the same mark that appeared on the map beside the words *thirteenth moon*.

"What does it say?" Cassel asked quietly.

"It says someone was keeping records." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Accounts."

"Of what?"

I ran my fingers along the nearest line of writing. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. "Of what got eaten."

We kept walking, because what else was there to do? The alternative was standing in a warm tunnel reading a menu written in my ancestors' handwriting, and I have some standards.

* * *

The stairs ended in a hall.

I want to describe it properly, because I think someone should, and because describing it is easier than feeling it. It was vast—a cavern hollowed from the mountain, its ceiling lost in shadow above us, its floor smooth as poured glass. Pillars stood in rows down its length, dozens of them, and each pillar was wrapped in storm-script from floor to ceiling.

They weren't pillars.

I knew it before my mind admitted it. You know things like that in your skin first. Each one was too narrow, too irregular, too *human* in its proportions. They were columns of fused bone—ribs and spines and skulls melted together into something like architecture—and the storm-script wound around them the way ivy winds around a corpse left long enough in a hedge.

Bones. Dozens of pillars' worth. Hundreds of people's worth.

Every storm-caller the Hollow had consumed. All of them, here, beneath the throne, arranged like trophies. Or like a pantry.

My light flickered. I made it steady through sheer spite.

"Yara." Cassel's hand was on my shoulder. I hadn't heard him move. "Yara. Breathe."

"I'm breathing." I was. Technically. "I'm just also furious, and those two things don't share space well."

I walked out onto the glass-smooth floor because someone had to, and because fury walks better than fear does. The hall stretched away from us, pillar after pillar, each one a person, each person a caller, each caller someone's daughter or son or mother who had once stood exactly where I was standing and known exactly what I was knowing:

*I am not the first. I am only the latest.*

And here's the thing that cracked something in me—not the bones themselves, though gods, the bones. It was the script. Because the closer I looked at the nearest pillar, the more I recognized the hand. The slant of the letters. The way the moon-notations curled at their ends.

I'd seen that handwriting before. Three weeks ago, pressed into my hands by a dying man in the snow, sealed in wax, refusing to burn.

"The courier," I said.

Cassel frowned. "What about him?"

I turned in a slow circle, reading pillar after pillar, and found what I already knew I'd find. The last column. The newest bones—the ones still pale, still sharp-edged, not yet fully fused into the stone. Wrapped in fresh script, the ink of it still somehow wet after all this time, still glowing faintly with that warm light the map had carried.

The courier's name was written there. His true name, in storm-script. And beneath it, the notation I finally understood, because Mother Ije's lessons rose up in me unbidden, patient as graves:

*Taken under the twelfth moon. Kept until the thirteenth.*

"He wasn't fleeing to us," I said. My voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "He wasn't carrying the map *out*. He was carrying it *in*. To the place where the others—" I gestured at the pillars, at all of it. "This is where they brought him. This is where they bring all of us. The map was bait, Cassel. Or a summons. Or—" I laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Or a will. Written by a dead man, addressed to whoever was stubborn enough to read it."

Cassel was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way you talk to a fuse. "You said the map shifted. When you lied about burning it."

"Yes."

"And it was marked in script only you could read."

"Yes."

"So either the Hollow made a very elaborate trap for one exiled storm-caller in the Thornfell passes—" He shook his head slowly. "—or the courier chose you. Knew what you were. Knew what you could do. And spent his last hours making sure the right person found it."

I looked at the pale bones. At the name glowing softly above them.

He'd collapsed into my fire half-dead already. Hunters on his trail. He could have given the map to anyone—any smuggler, any caravan master, anyone heading south toward safety. Instead he'd crawled toward the fire where a woman sat alone, keeping herself apart from the others, the way storm-callers sit apart. Maybe he'd smelled it on me. The old blood. The inheritance.

Maybe he'd simply known that the Hollow hunts us, and therefore the Hollow's map belonged in our hands.

"He knew my order existed," I whispered. "After all these years. After the purge. He knew there were still—" My throat closed. I finished anyway. "He died thinking we weren't finished."

Cassel said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a knife, and he knew whose hand the knives belonged to. He'd told me himself, back in chapter ten of my nightmares: he had marched against my order. He had helped make these bones possible. He stood among them now like a penitent in a cathedral built entirely of his sin.

But that conversation was had, and forgiveness deferred is not forgiveness denied, so instead I did the thing that was actually useful.

I read.

* * *

The script on the courier's pillar was different from the rest. Older pillars carried only names and dates—cold accounting, bookkeeping for monsters. His carried more. Instructions, maybe. Or a warning. The glow of it pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat slowing.

I pressed my palm flat against the bone and let the script speak.

It came into me the way storm-knowledge always comes—sideways, all at once, like weather. Not words exactly. Impressions wearing words like coats:

*If you read this, the gate opened for you, which means the price was paid, which means you are either braver or more foolish than any of us. Both, probably. We could always tell our own.*

*Listen: the Hollow cannot be killed while it sits the throne. It has grown into the crown, and the crown into the law, and the law into ten thousand minds that obey without question. Tear it out by force and the kingdom tears with it. That is why we failed. All of us. We came down here angry, and anger is just another kind of hunger, and hunger is what it eats.*

*Come differently.*

*Under the thirteenth moon it will pull the last of itself out of the king's dying mind and into the crown entire—its final molt, its moment of greatest power and greatest exposure. For one turning of the hourglass it will be nothing but itself. No king to hide inside. No law to wear.*

*A storm can take it then. But the storm must be called clean. Not in rage. In grief, if that's what you carry. In love, if you've managed to find any. It must be a storm that wants nothing for itself.*

*We wanted vengeance. Look at us. We are furniture.*

*Do not be furniture.*

The pulse of light beneath my palm stuttered, dimmed, and went out. Whatever fragment of him remained in the script—his voice, his last living thought—it had finished speaking. Spent itself delivering a letter twenty years late to the right address.

I stood there with my hand on his bones for a long time.

"Yara?" Cassel, again. Gently.

"He waited," I said. "All these years, whatever was left of him waited down here for someone to come read it." I pulled my hand back. My palm ached where the script had touched it, and when I looked down, faint silver lines traced across my skin like frost forming—the storm-script, writing itself into me the way it had begun to since Mother Ije's hut. Inheritance. Apparently it worked both directions. "And the message is: everyone who tried to kill this thing with rage became part of its house."

"Can you do it? Call it clean?"

I thought about the storm I'd been exiled for. The village of Marrow's Bend, the roofs torn open, the granary flattened, my own hands raised against my own people because I'd been afraid and afraid things break what they touch. I had spent six years building a dam against that night. Six years of flickers and trickles, of being competent and contained and lonely as a lighthouse.

And now a dead man was asking me to open the dam on purpose. Fully. Under the thirteenth moon, in the heart of the enemy's house, with everything I loved standing close enough to be swept away if I got it wrong.

"No," I said honestly. "Not yet. But I've got until the moon turns, and I'm told I'm stubborn."

That earned the ghost of a smile from him. Small mercies.

* * *

We were halfway across the hall toward the far arch—there was a far arch, because there is always a far arch, and it was glowing faintly gold, which meant it led up, which meant it led toward the throne—when the temperature dropped.

Not cold. *Absence.* The warmth drained out of the room like water through a crack, and every pillar of bones went silent in a way that bones had no business going silent, because they had been humming all along, so faintly I'd taken it for the mountain's own breathing, and now they stopped.

Both of us froze. Cassel's sword cleared its sheath with a whisper.

Above us—far above, past the lost ceiling, somewhere up in the world of throne rooms and decrees and a king eating himself alive—something noticed us.

I felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm breaks. A vast attention swinging around like a lighthouse beam, sweeping the dark, searching. Looking for intruders. Looking, I realized with a lurch, for *storm-callers*, because that was the one flavor it could never stop craving, the one taste it had hunted across centuries, and two of us had just walked into its pantry and read its grocery list aloud.

The gold-lit arch flickered.

Then, impossibly, it brightened—steady, deliberate, like a door being held open by someone on the other side. And down through the stone came a sound I had to strain to catch: footsteps. Quick ones. Light ones. Someone running the servants' ways above, moving fast, moving *toward* us, and beneath the footsteps a voice pitched low and urgent, threading down through the mountain like water through limestone:

"—if you can hear me, keep moving, the east stair, quickly—he's distracted tonight, he won't be distracted long—"

A woman's voice. Young. Terrified and doing it anyway.

Cassel looked at me. I looked at Cassel.

"Somehow," he said, "this is always the part where you say yes to something terrible."

I was already moving toward the golden light.

"Terrible's the family business," I said. "Apparently I inherited it."
