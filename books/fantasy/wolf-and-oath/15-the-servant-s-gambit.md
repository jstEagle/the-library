## 15. The Servant's Gambit

There are two kinds of courage in the citadel of King Aldric. The kind they sing about — knights charging, banners snapping, all that noise and glory — and the kind that looks like a laundress carrying a bucket of sick down a corridor while smiling at the guard who once beat her brother for stealing bread.

I have only ever had access to the second kind.

My name is Sera Vane, and for eleven years I have been invisible in this palace. Not in the poetic way. In the practical way. Servants are furniture that walks. We are eyes with no faces, hands with no names. The court looks through us the way you look through a window you've stopped noticing. This is either a curse or a superpower, depending on how much you enjoy being alive.

Lately I have decided to enjoy it very much.

* * *

The plague cart arrived at the Ash Gate an hour past midnight, which was perfect, because everything wicked happens an hour past midnight and everyone knows to stop asking questions by then.

I had bribed the gate sergeant three days earlier with a bottle of plum brandy and a lie about his wife, neither of which he deserved, and so when the cart rolled up heaving with the groans of the "sick," he waved it through with the enthusiasm of a man who wanted nothing to do with any of it. Plague is the one terror that outranks even the king's hunters. You can be tortured for treason and survive. Nobody survives being coughed on.

Inside the cart, under a layer of genuinely foul rags I had collected from the fever-ward myself — because if you're going to commit treason, do it with authentic props — lay Yara Okonkwo and Cassel of Thornmere, formerly of the Kingsguard, currently of nowhere.

I lifted the tarpaulin. Cassel emerged looking like a man who had been born under a tarpaulin and intended to die there, gray-faced and grim. Yara sat up, pushed her hair out of her face, and looked around the Ash Gate courtyard with the expression of someone appraising a horse she suspected was lame.

"This is the palace?" she said. "It smells like a latrine."

"The latrines are the clean part," I said. "Welcome to the seat of royal power. Try not to touch anything."

"You're the servant," Cassel said. "Sera." He said it like he was checking my teeth.

"I'm the servant," I agreed. "And you're two plague-bearers from the river villages, quarantined to the old infirmary wing until you die or don't. The guards have orders not to approach anyone from the quarantine. Fear does most of my work for me." I handed them each a bundle: rough-spun clothes, a clay token stamped with the fever-mark, and — this was important — a small pot of grease mixed with ash and chicken fat. "Smear that on your necks. Fever rash. Smells wrong enough that nobody will want to look closely."

Yara sniffed the pot and recoiled. "What's in it?"

"Chicken fat."

"And the rest?"

"Ash."

"Is that it?"

"Would you like there to be more?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Storm-callers, I'd been told, were dangerous, half-mythical creatures who could pull lightning out of a clear sky. This one looked like a tired woman deciding whether to trust a stranger with chicken fat. Then something in her face shifted, some weighing done, some verdict reached.

"No," she said. "That's probably exactly what it is."

They followed me through the servants' corridor — low-ceilinged, lamplit, smelling of tallow and old bread — and I narrated as we went, because knowledge keeps people alive in this place. "Guards rotate every third bell. Don't make eye contact with anyone in livery. If someone in livery speaks to you, cough. Coughing is a universal language here now."

"And the Hollow?" Yara asked quietly. "Can you feel it?"

I stopped walking.

I had expected the question eventually. I had not expected it this soon, or in a voice so gentle. Everyone else on this mad quest — the smugglers, the knight, even Captain Vess in her armored way — talked about the Hollow like weather. A force. An affliction upon the king. But Yara asked *can you feel it*, the way you'd ask someone about a wound.

"Yes," I said. "It's like... you know how a room feels different when someone has died in it? Even after they carry the body out? The whole palace is that room. Every corridor. Some nights the walls feel thick with listening. And when the king decrees something—" I swallowed. "You can hear it in the courtyards below. The way sound goes flat. Like the air itself is holding its breath before the cruelty lands."

Cassel's jaw worked. "How long?"

"Since I've served, it's gotten worse every year. But the last two moons—" I shook my head. "Two moons ago, a chambermaid named Odile was found to have kept a letter from her sister in the provinces. Just a letter. Family news. It was against a new decree — no unregistered correspondence. They didn't hang her. They made the whole east wing watch while she knelt in the snow until morning. She lost two fingers. She still works here. She still curtsies to the steward who ordered it." I started walking again, faster. "That's what I can tell you about the Hollow. It doesn't just eat the king. Whatever eats him, it eats through him. Into all of us."

Neither of them said anything. Good. There was nothing to say. That was the whole point of bringing them here.

We reached the infirmary wing — disused since the last plague scare, which the crown had declared over two years ago and then stopped funding, because the crown had stopped funding anything that helped people breathe — and I showed them the loose panel behind the linen press that connected to the undercroft stairs. From there, the tunnels beneath the throne room. Mother Ije's map had described them; my eleven years of hauling buckets had confirmed every turn of them.

"Third stair creaks," I told them. "Skip it. And whatever you find down there —" I hesitated. "There's a door with iron bands. Don't open it out of curiosity. Curiosity killed the last person who opened it, and she was a friend of mine."

"What was behind it?" Yara asked.

"Bones," I said. "A lot of bones. Now go save the kingdom so I can get back to my laundry before someone notices furniture that walks."

* * *

They say the gods reward the bold. In my experience the gods mostly ignore servants entirely, and it's the king's hunters who take an interest.

I was folding linens in the west gallery — genuinely folding linens, I want that on the record, my treason had working hours like any honest trade — when Captain Vess came around the corner with two of her hunters and the particular walk she gets when she already knows the answer to every question she's about to ask.

"Sera Vane," she said.

"Captain." I curtsied. Deep enough to be respectful, shallow enough to keep my balance. Eleven years of practice.

"The gate sergeant at Ash Gate received a bribe three nights ago. Plum brandy and, according to his statement, 'advice regarding his wife.' His wife, for the record, has been dead six years, which suggests either your bribe or your research was sloppy."

"He seemed lonely."

"He also remembers a plague cart. Two bearers. Quarantine tokens issued from a wing that hasn't housed a patient since the reign of Aldric's father." She stepped closer. Her armor made no sound, which always unnerved me; a woman that large should clank. "Where are they, Sera Vane?"

Now. Here is the moment where I should tell you I was brave. Where I planted my feet and spoke truth to power and felt the wind of history at my back.

Here is what actually happened: I vomited. A little. Into a linen basket. Fear does that to me; it always has; it comes up rather than out. Captain Vess waited with the patience of a statue while I wiped my mouth and straightened my apron, and then I looked up at her, and I saw something I had never seen in eleven years of watching hunters drag people away.

She looked tired. Not cruel-tired. *Carrying-something-heavy*-tired. Her sword arm hung oddly at her side, wrapped in a bandage that hadn't been there last week, and her eyes went, briefly, to the gallery window where the thirteenth moon was supposed to rise in four nights' time, and her expression was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"Captain," I said carefully, "may I ask you something first?"

"You may ask. I may not answer."

"Do you believe the king signed the Decree of Ashes?"

Her hand twitched toward her sword and stopped. That was answer enough. The Decree of Ashes — the purge order, the one that had burned a quarter of the capital's poor quarter three weeks ago, the one that had killed nine hundred people whose names weren't written anywhere anymore because the census bureau had been decreed redundant — that decree had a signature on it. I'd seen the copy posted in the courtyard. Elegant script. Royal seal.

But I'd also seen the king, twice, from across a great hall, and both times he'd looked like a man watching himself from very far away.

"Take her to questioning," Vess said to her hunters.

"Captain—"

"Not the cells." Something crossed her face, fast, gone again. "My quarters. Alone."

* * *

Vess's quarters were spare as a monk's cell and smelled of oilstone and, faintly, of burned cloth. She dismissed her hunters, barred the door herself, and then did the last thing in the world I expected.

She sat down across from me. Took off one gauntlet. Flexed the hand beneath — the bandaged one — and winced.

"Tell me about the cart," she said. "All of it. And understand, Sera Vane, that I am about to commit treason by listening, so if you waste my time with lies, I will run out of mercy before you run out of story."

So I told her. The courier, dead in the snowline with his bloodstained map — I knew that part secondhand, from the message Aldric's man had smuggled to me in a loaf of bread, which was its own small insanity. The storm-caller. The knight without a banner. The village that had forgotten its own name. The hermit in the high passes. The bones beneath the throne. I told her about Odile and her two fingers. About the flatness in the air when the decrees landed. About the king's eyes at the winter feast, fixed on nothing, while something behind them wore his smile like a mask worn slightly crooked.

Vess listened without moving. When I finished, she was quiet for so long I counted forty of my own heartbeats.

"My oath-spell," she said finally. "Do you know what it is?"

"Hunters swear unbreakable oaths. Everyone knows that much."

"We do. Sworn on our sword arms, bound into the bone. Break the oath, lose the arm — the spell burns it out of you, root to fingertip. Every hunter knows the price and swears anyway, because the oath isn't to a man. It's to the crown. To the law. To the idea that the person on the throne is the person the law serves." She turned her bandaged hand over, slowly. "Three weeks ago I refused a direct order. An unlawful one — a hunter's discretion, the kind we're trained to exercise. Small thing. I let a boy go instead of taking him for questioning. Nothing." She flexed the fingers. They moved, but stiffly, like a rusted hinge. "The spell punished me anyway. For disobedience to a decree that had never been spoken aloud. I never received that order. No one gave it. The oath knew it anyway."

The fire popped. Neither of us looked at it.

"So the oath isn't bound to the crown," I said slowly. "Or the crown isn't bound to the king."

"Or the king isn't the crown anymore." Vess stood. She walked to her window, where the dark mass of the citadel's heart rose against the stars, and stood there with her back to me, and for a moment she was not the most feared officer in the realm but simply a woman at a window, doing terrible sums. "Nine hundred people in the Ashes purge. I enforced the perimeter. I told myself the king must have had reasons. Kings have reasons. That's what I told recruits for twenty years — the law is the wall between us and the wild, and your job is to hold the wall." She turned. "Then a plague-bearer with chicken fat on her neck tells me the wall is standing on top of us."

"That's roughly what I said, yes."

Something almost like a smile touched her mouth and died there. "The storm-caller. Okonkwo. Is she coming for him?"

"She's already inside."

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them, the captain was gone and something rawer looked out — grief, maybe, or relief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Four nights," she said. "Until the thirteenth turn. My mother used to tell me the old stories — the thirteenth moon seals whatever the year has grown. I thought they were nursery tales." She picked up her gauntlet and put it back on, and with it, piece by piece, the captain. "Here's what happens now. Officially, you escaped during questioning. Unofficially, you're going to take me to them."

"They'll think it's a trap."

"It is a trap," Vess said. "Just not for them." She pulled a ring of keys from her belt — the hunters' master keys, the ones that opened every cell and gate in the citadel — and set them on the table between us like a gambler pushing in her whole purse. "Twenty years I held the wall, Sera Vane. Time I found out what it was holding down."

Outside, far above the gallery windows, past the frost and the smoke, something vast turned its attention through the stones of the palace — I felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm, the flatness settling over the corridors, the walls growing thick with listening —

—and somewhere beneath our feet, faint as a heartbeat through a floor, the undercroft door with the iron bands began, very softly, to open.
