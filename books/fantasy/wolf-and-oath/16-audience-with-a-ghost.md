## 16. Audience with a Ghost

The servant's corridor smelled of lye and fear.

Sera had dressed us in the gray smocks of plague-bearers, had painted the telltale blotches on our necks with crushed elderberry, and had walked us through three gates where guards shrank back like we were made of knives. Plague-bearers were untouchable. Plague-bearers were also, by the Hollow's newest decree, to be burned at week's end — but decrees moved slowly through a palace that was eating itself, and Sera had bet our lives on that slowness.

"Fourth landing," she'd whispered at the stair. "The king keeps no guards in his private rooms anymore. He dismissed them all last winter." A pause, and something old and tired in her face. "Or *it* did."

Now I stood on that fourth landing, alone, my palm sweating against the handle of a door that had no lock on the inside. Cassel was two floors below, playing lookout with Vess's stolen signal-mirror, and every part of me wanted him here instead — wanted anyone here instead — because what waited behind this door had eaten kings before it ever got around to mine.

*Not your king,* I reminded myself. *You've never sworn him anything.*

But the map against my ribs hummed like a struck bell, and storm-script crawled faintly up my forearm where the glass-shard of Mother Ije's bottled tempest had cut me, and somewhere beneath all of it, under the fear and the exhaustion and the grief for a hermit who'd died buying us hours on a mountain road, there was a child's memory: firelight, a man in a crown kneeling in the ash of my order's sanctuary, lifting a seven-year-old girl out of the smoke and saying *run, little storm. Run far, and don't look back.*

I'd spent eleven years telling myself I'd dreamed that.

I pushed the door open.

* * *

The room beyond was wrong in ways I couldn't immediately name. It took me three full breaths to understand why: there was nothing in it that could be thrown, broken, or turned into a weapon. No candelabra. No chairs. The bedframe was bolted to the floor. The window was a slit too narrow for a cat. Everything soft had been removed, everything hard had been anchored, and the whole arrangement spoke of a man who had asked — or been made to ask — for a room built like a cell.

Aldric sat on the bolted bed with his hands open on his knees, palms up, as if he'd been waiting so long for someone to arrive that his body had forgotten other shapes.

He was thinner than the coins bore him. Thinner than the wanted-sketches Vess had shown us. His crown lay on the floor beside him, and he looked at it the way you'd look at a snake you couldn't quite stop watching.

"You're not Sera," he said. His voice was rust and riverbed. "Sera comes at dusk. You came at—" he squinted toward the slit window, gauging light I couldn't see— "an hour no one comes."

"I come at dangerous hours," I said. "It's a specialty."

Something flickered across his face. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, visiting a house where smiles used to live. "You're the smuggler. The one the hunters keep losing in the Thornfell." He tilted his head. "No. That's not all you are."

My hand drifted, without permission, toward the knife at my hip.

"Don't," Aldric said quietly. "Not because I'd call for help. There's no one left to call. But because if you're going to kill me, I would like to speak first. It's been a long time since anyone spoke *to* me rather than *near* me." He lifted his eyes to mine, and they were clear. Gods, they were clear — brown and tired and human, with none of the oily gleam the court painters had started giving him. "How long do I have this clarity? Minutes, perhaps. It comes when it comes, like weather." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Like weather. Yes. Fitting, given who you are."

My spine went cold. "You know what I am."

"I knew what you were before you did." He folded his hands, and I saw the tremor in them, fine as a moth's wings. "Storm-caller. Daughter of the Thornfell sanctuaries. Do you know how many of you are left? I signed the census decrees. Or my hand did." His jaw worked. "Three. By last count, three living storm-callers in all the Nine Valleys. And two of them are in this room, if you count what shares my skull."

"The Hollow isn't a storm-caller."

"No." He said it simply, without flinching. "It's what's *left* when one is consumed. Did Mother Ije teach you nothing? Ah — no. Don't answer. Your face just told me she's dead, and I find I need a moment before I carry another death." He closed his eyes. For ten heartbeats he was perfectly still, and I understood I was looking at a man practicing stillness the way prisoners practice walking — because the walls demanded it, and because surrendering to the shaking would mean never stopping.

When he opened his eyes again, he said, "Ask me what you came to ask."

"How do you know I came to ask anything?"

"Because plague-bearers don't climb four flights to stand in a doorway judging the furniture." He gestured at the stripped room. "And because Sera described you. 'A woman with storm-scars who looks at everyone like she's deciding whether to save them or bury them.'" He shrugged. "She said it kindly."

"That *is* her kind way with me."

That almost-smile again. Then it drained away, and what remained was worse — earnestness, naked and desperate, from a man who clearly hadn't been permitted an honest feeling in years.

"The thirteenth moon," he said. "You know about it."

"Mother Ije read it. When it turns, whatever the Hollow has become becomes permanent. Sealed into the crown. Into the bloodline, maybe, forever."

"Yes. And after that, no one can pull it out. Not you. Not all the storm-callers who ever lived." He leaned forward, and the candle-gutter shadow made his face older. "I have perhaps nine days of being myself between now and then. Nine days where the thing lets me surface, because it enjoys letting me watch. But I am lucid *now*, Yara Okonkwo, and I am asking you — formally, as a man to the last free daughter of an order he failed —"

"Don't." My voice cracked on it. "Don't call me that."

"Why not?"

"Because the last time a crowned man said those words to a storm-caller, her whole world burned." I stepped into the room properly, finally, and let the door swing shut behind me. "You want to ask me something? Ask."

He didn't flinch. I gave him credit for that. Eleven years of cruelty had taught me to expect flinching from powerful men, and Aldric — hollowed, haunted, half-eaten Aldric — met my anger like it was rain he'd ordered himself to stand in.

"I'm asking you to kill me," he said. "If it comes to that. If you cannot tear it free, if the cost is too high, if I am past saving when the moment comes — I am asking you not to let it wear me past the thirteenth moon. Sworn men break their oaths. Kings break kingdoms. But you —" and here his voice did something terrible and gentle — "you I watched run through fire rather than kneel. You I trust."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us, a guard laughed at something; the sound floated up through stone like a bubble through dark water.

"I was seven," I said. My own voice surprised me — small, unarmored. "The night the sanctuary burned. Everyone says the king's hunters did it, everyone says Aldric signed the decree, and I have hated you for eleven years because hating you was easier than hating a sky full of ash." I swallowed. "But I remember hands lifting me out of the smoke. Crowned hands. And I remember thinking, even then, even at seven: *why is the king carrying me?*"

"Because I ordered the burn," Aldric said, "and I stopped it halfway through."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well — I heard them falling long before they hit bottom.

"The Hollow had been in the crown for two generations by then. My grandfather wore it smiling. My father wore it and grew cruel. I wore it and began to lose hours — whole days I could not account for. The night of your sanctuary, I surfaced inside a burning building with soot on my hands and a decree in my pocket bearing my seal, and I did not know what I had done until I found you screaming in the smoke." His voice frayed. "There were forty-one storm-callers in that sanctuary. I carried out one. One, Yara. I have done the arithmetic every night for eleven years, and it always comes out the same."

"Forty," I whispered.

"Forty." He bowed his head. "The Hollow needed your order dead because yours is the only power that can unmake it. Storm-calls unbind what binds. It hunted your teachers, burned your sanctuaries, salted your libraries — and it used my hands, my seals, my voice to do it, because a parasite that wears a king can burn the world and call it law." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he let them be. "You were not exiled for a storm you could not control. You were exiled because the storm you *could* control was the only weapon left that frightened it. Every decree that scattered your people, every bounty, every border sealed — that was not policy. That was a creature digging out your eyes."

I sat down. I didn't decide to; my legs simply ended their employment. The bolted bed frame was cold through my smock.

"All this time," I said. "All this time I thought the Thornfell cast me out. I thought my own storm was the shame."

"Your storm is the cure," Aldric said. "And it knows it. Why do you think it whispers to you at night? Why do you think the map found you, the courier found you, the script rises in your skin?" He leaned forward, urgent now. "The Hollow fears you specifically. Not storm-callers in general — *you*. The courier wasn't carrying a map to the citadel. She was carrying a map *to you*, written by your own order before the end, addressed to the one girl the fire missed."

The map hummed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. All those years of running routes for smugglers, keeping my head down, keeping the power leashed so tight it screamed — and I had never been hiding from the Thornfell at all. I'd been hiding in the exact shape of a trap, and the trap had been patient, and now it was closing, and the worst part —

The worst part was that I felt *relieved*.

Eleven years of shame, and it had never been mine.

"I can't promise you mercy," I said at last, because he deserved truth more than comfort. "I can't promise I'll be strong enough, or fast enough, or that tearing it out won't take you with it. Mother Ije thought it might. She thought freeing you might kill you both."

"Good," Aldric said.

"*Good?*"

"I have been dying for eleven years, smuggler. Slowly, by inches, in a room with no chairs. Whatever you do — tear it out, burn it out, take me with it — you will be doing me a kindness the whole court is too frightened to offer." He reached out and, hesitantly, touched the back of my hand. Crowned hands once. Just a man's hands now, trembling. "Let me give you something first. Before the clarity goes. Listen closely, because it may not come back."

I leaned in.

"It sleeps deepest in the hour before dawn. It dreams — I don't know what parasites dream, but it goes somewhere, and while it's gone, the wards on the throne room thin. Sera knows the servants' way in. Beneath the throne there's an old storm-well — your order built it, centuries ago, when the citadel was raised. The Hollow nests in it. That's where you'll find its root." His grip tightened. "And Yara — when it wakes, it will speak in my voice. It will beg. It will say things only I would know. Do not listen. Promise me you'll know the difference."

"How will I know the difference?"

"You won't," he said. "That's the horror of it. But I will hold it as long as I can, and when I can hold no longer, I'll say your mother's name."

I went cold all over. "You don't know my mother's name."

"I do," Aldric said softly. "Adaeze. She shouted it at the hunters while they dragged her. Shouted it so someone would remember. So —" his voice broke clean through — "so *someone would remember.*"

For a moment neither of us moved. Then I did the bravest thing I'd done since the snowline: I believed him.

* * *

We talked for what felt like minutes and was probably most of an hour. He told me about the storm-well, about the tunnels Sera could draw from memory, about which of his decrees had been his own small rebellions — the granary opened during the famine year, the pardon slipped to a poacher whose execution the Hollow had demanded. Little cracks in the cruelty. A man leaving marks on his own prison wall.

"You should go," he said finally, glancing at the slit window, where the light had gone the color of weak tea. "Dusk brings Sera, and dusk brings —" he flexed his fingers, watching them as if they belonged to a stranger — "the tide comes back in."

"One more question." I stood, legs stiff. "The courier. The one who brought me the map. She died on the snowline with hunters' arrows in her. Was she —"

"Maera," Aldric said, and my breath stopped. "Of the Thornfell sanctuaries. Last keeper of the storm-script." His eyes went distant. "She visited me once, years ago, disguised as a laundress. Sat where you're sitting. Told me she'd found the heir the map was written for." He smiled, and this time it reached his eyes, and it was the saddest thing I'd ever seen on a living face. "She said: *she's angry, sire. Good. Anger is just grief that refuses to lie down.*"

I laughed. It came out cracked and wet, and it might have been a sob wearing a laugh's clothing, and Aldric had the grace to pretend it was only a laugh.

"She sounded insufferable," I managed.

"She sounded right." He settled back against the wall. "Go. Finish it. And Yara — when you stand over the well, when it offers you bargains, remember: it has nothing you want. Everything it has, it stole."

I crossed to the door. My hand was on the iron ring when the temperature fell.

Not draught-fall. Not mountain-cold. This was the specific, crawling chill I'd felt once before, on the snowline, standing over a dying woman while hunters' lanterns bobbed through the trees — the chill of something *noticing*.

Behind me, Aldric's breathing changed.

"No," he whispered. "No, no — I felt it leave, I felt it *sleep* —"

"King?" I said, and hated how small my voice came out.

His head came up. And the eyes that met mine were still brown, still human, still *his* — but behind them, deep down at the bottom of them like a lamp at the end of a tunnel, something else had opened its eye.

*Found you,* said the dark under his voice, though his lips never moved. *Little storm. We wondered when you'd come home.*

"Aldric." I backed toward the door. "Fight it. Hold it. You said you'd —"

"I'm trying—" His hand shot out and caught my wrist, and for one heartbeat his grip was his own, desperate, human. "*Run,*" he gasped. "The well — it knows you're here — it will seal the —"

Then his face went smooth as poured wax, and the hand on my wrist became gentle. Tender, even. It stroked once along my pulse, and I felt my own heartbeat stutter under it like a bird in a fist.

"There now," said King Aldric, in a voice that had honey poured over iron. "No need to run. We've waited such a long time to meet properly."

The door behind me wouldn't open. The iron ring had gone cold enough to burn, and frost was feathering across it in patterns I recognized — spirals, whorls, the looping geometry of storm-script, *written backwards, written wrong.*

Outside, somewhere down four floors of stairs, a bell began to toll. Once. Twice. A third time — and then every bell in the citadel took it up, and beneath the ringing came a sound I'd hoped never to hear again: hunters' boots, dozens of them, moving fast.

"Your friends," the thing wearing my king said fondly. "The knight with the broken oath. The captain with the cracking one. The little servant with her maps and her lies." It tilted Aldric's head, studying me with his stolen eyes. "Shall I tell you what happens to them next? I so enjoy the planning. It's the only part of ruling I truly love."

Aldric — the real one, buried somewhere beneath his own face — had promised me a sign. Had promised me his last grip, his last word, his last *anything*.

His hand, still resting on my wrist, pressed once, twice, three times against my pulse. Deliberate. Rhythmic.

*Run-run-run.*

I wrenched free, spun, and drove my shoulder into the frost-locked door with every ounce of storm the leash on my soul had ever held back.

The wood didn't splinter.

The *frost* did — shrieking, in a voice like Maera's, like Mother Ije's, like forty voices that had waited eleven years in the dark for exactly this — and as the door burst open onto torchlight and ringing steel and Cassel's roar rising up the stairwell, I heard, behind me, calm as a lullaby:

"Nine days, little storm. And I already know your mother's name."
