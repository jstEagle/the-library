## 17. Chains of the Crown

There is a particular humiliation in being captured by your own former comrades. I want to say I fought well — that I went down beneath a dozen hunters, sword singing, blood on the snow like something out of a ballad. The truth is that Vess's second-in-command hit me with a weighted net from behind while I was watching Yara disappear over the wall of the aqueduct, and then a man I once shared watch-fires with kicked my legs out from under me and stepped on my hand for good measure.

His name was Dorren. He apologized while he did it. That's the worst part. The king's hunters are polite now. They were always disciplined; that was never in question. But somewhere in the last two years, the discipline had curdled into courtesy, the way meat curdles when it's turning. They bound my wrists with oath-iron — cold enough to burn, heavy enough to remind you that oaths are not metaphor — and they thanked me for my cooperation.

"You're making a mistake," I told them, mostly for form's sake. My mouth was full of blood and my pride was full of holes.

"Captain Vess says the same about you," Dorren said, not unkindly. "She says you'll have your chance to correct it."

I thought about what Yara would say if she could see me now. Probably nothing. Probably she'd just give me that look she has, the one that weighs a man and finds him slightly underweight, and then she'd go do something reckless and brilliant without me, which was, after all, how we'd gotten this far.

The aqueduct route into the citadel had been her idea. Mine had been the diversion — walk out into the open, be loud, be seen, let them take me if they had to. She'd argued against it for a full hour, which for Yara constitutes a eulogy.

"Don't die," she'd finally said. "Dying is easy and I've got enough ghosts."

So I hadn't died. I'd been netted like a trout instead, which is arguably worse, because at least ghosts get to haunt people.

* * *

They took me through the servants' postern, past the kitchens where I'd once eaten burnt bread with a young prince who laughed too easily, up through corridors I knew the way a man knows his own scars. The citadel of King Aldric had been my home for eleven years. I had sworn my oath on its threshold stone. Walking it in chains felt like reading my own obituary written by someone who never liked me much.

The throne room doors were open. Of course they were. The Hollow wanted an audience. It had always wanted audiences — that's what I understood now, what I should have understood years ago when the decrees started coming down strange and the king stopped appearing at council. Power that hides is hoarding. Power that displays is feeding.

Aldric sat on his throne, and he did not look like Aldric.

I know that sounds like madness. It looked like him — the same grey-streaked hair, the same broad shoulders that had carried a kingdom since he was nineteen. But the posture was wrong. Aldric used to sit forward, elbows on knees, like a man leaning into a conversation. This thing sat back, spread wide, draped over the throne like a garment hung on a hook. And the eyes —

The eyes were patient. Nothing human is patient like that.

"Cassel," it said, in the king's voice. It said my name the way you'd read an old inventory entry. "Knight-Captain of the Third Watch. Sworn at seventeen. Broken at thirty-four." A pause, delicate as a surgeon's probe. "You've been very busy since you broke."

"I've been honest since I broke," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is," it agreed, and the agreement frightened me more than any threat would have. "That's why you're here."

* * *

They left us alone. That was the first wrong thing. No guards, no witnesses — just me in my chains and the thing wearing my king, in a room built to hold three hundred petitioners and currently holding one dead fire in the hearth and one living lie on the throne.

It rose. It moved toward me slowly, and up close I could see what the distance had hidden: the skin around Aldric's temples, fine as cracked glaze, and beneath it — moving — something pale, something that shifted like light under ice.

"Do you know what happened to your brother?" it asked.

The question landed exactly where questions are designed to land. In the soft place. I kept my face still. Eleven years of court training does not evaporate; it just gets rusty and bitter.

"He died," I said. "In the purge of Thornhollow. Along with forty other storm-callers and their families."

"Fifty-three," it corrected gently. "You always undercount. Guilt does that. It rounds down." It circled me, slow, the hem of its robe whispering on stone. "You held the door of the eastern dormitory. You were ordered to hold it shut. You held it for four minutes before your captain relieved you. Four minutes. Do you know how many got out in four minutes?"

"Three," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "Yara Okonkwo, her mother, and a boy whose name I never learned."

"The boy's name was Tam." It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was terrible, because some part of Aldric's warmth was still in there, worn smooth like a riverstone, and the smile fit it perfectly. "You see? I remember everything, Cassel. Every name, every number, every night you woke up tasting smoke. Your king forgot none of it. Your king remembers it more clearly than you do — because remembering is what I do best."

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Honestly?" It laughed — Aldric's laugh, the one I'd heard across war-tables and feast-halls, and hearing it from this thing was like seeing a beloved house burned but standing, every room familiar and every room wrong. "What I want is so simple. What I want is what I have always wanted. I want to be finished. I have been eating this kingdom for two hundred years, knight, and I am so close, and every storm-caller I consumed brought me closer, and then your order — clever, stubborn, insufferable people — hid the last of their power where I cannot reach it without help." It leaned in. Its breath smelled of snowmelt and old copper. "And here you are. Chained to my floor. Carrying the last key in your head whether you know it or not."

"I don't know anything."

"No. But you know *her*. And she knows things she doesn't know she knows. The map chose her. The script answers her. And you — you are the only person alive she might hesitate for." It straightened. "I don't need your sword, Cassel. I need your word. Tell me where she'll surface before the thirteenth turn. One sentence. Whispered into the right ear. That's all."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" it spread its hands, and the chains' weight seemed suddenly obscene, theatrical, a prop in a play it had staged for exactly this moment, "—I restore you."

The word went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"I will lift the attaintment. Your name cleansed, your rank restored, your lands returned. Knight-Captain Cassel of the Third Watch, banner restored, honor whole. The purge will be reclassified — a tragedy of wartime, no fault assigned. Your brother's name entered in the rolls with honors." Its voice dropped, gentle as a confessor. "You would sleep, Cassel. Have you slept a whole night since Thornhollow? I can give you that. I am the only power in this kingdom that can, because I am the power that took it from you. Isn't that the shape of every debt?"

I stood there in my chains and I let myself feel it. All of it. The bed without smoke in it. The mirror without the flinch. Walking into a hall and having men rise, having men *trust* me. Saying my brother's name out loud without the number fifty-three attached to it like a millstone.

I let myself feel all of it, because I learned long ago that the only safe way through a temptation is straight through the middle of it. Around the edges is where men get lost.

Then I thought about Yara's face when Mother Ije died buying us hours on the ash pass — not grief, exactly. Arithmetic. Grief being converted, right in front of me, into resolve. And I thought about the village that had forgotten its own name, the old woman who'd pressed her hand to my arm and asked, kindly, if I was her grandson, because whoever she'd been, whatever family she'd had, had been eaten out of her like marrow from a bone.

And I thought about the courier. Bren. Dying in the snow with a map in his fists, spending his last breath on two words — *thirteenth moon* — because he believed a stranger at a campfire might finish what he couldn't.

"You killed my brother," I said.

"I gave the decree," it said. "Men swung the swords. I have never once lied to you, Cassel. It's one of my few virtues."

"Then here's my honesty in return." I raised my head and met those patient, glacial eyes. "You're offering me back the man I was before I understood what I served. I spent six years thinking my shame was mine alone — my cowardice, my failure, my broken oath. And it turns out the rot ran all the way up to the crown. Which means my shame wasn't a wound. It was a symptom. And I will not crawl back inside the disease to stop the pain."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the citadel's high galleries, a sound like a long exhalation.

"You refuse," it said at last. Not angry. Worse than angry — interested. "Even knowing what refusal costs."

"Tell me what it costs."

"It costs your brother's memory," it said simply. "Because when I am sealed beyond the thirteenth moon — if you fail — no one will remember him truly again. I am the archive of this kingdom's sins, knight. Burn me, and the record burns with me. Fifty-three becomes a rumor. Your oath-breaking becomes a footnote. Even your guilt — that faithful dog that has slept at your feet for six years — even it will starve." It tilted Aldric's head. "Who are you, Cassel, if you cannot define yourself by what you failed to save?"

And there it was. The real offer. Not the knighthood, not the clean name. The deeper hook: the promise that my guilt was my identity, and that losing it meant losing myself.

I almost laughed. Six years of carrying that weight, and the creature had correctly guessed it had grown comfortable. Familiar. Like an old chain worn smooth at the wrist.

"You're right," I said. "I don't know who I am without it."

Something flickered in the thing's expression — satisfaction beginning to assemble itself—

"But I know who I'm going to find out with," I finished. "Her. The smugglers. A hunter who broke her own chains today, if I'm not mistaken. That's the difference between us. You keep records because you can't bear to lose anything. We keep each other because we can't afford to."

The Hollow regarded me for a long moment. Then it sighed — Aldric's sigh, weary, kingly, human — and for one vertiginous instant I saw something else behind the eyes. Something small and drowning, looking out through the glass of its own face. A mouth shaping words it couldn't speak.

*Run*, the eyes said. Or maybe *hurry*. Maybe both.

Then the pale thing under the skin surged, and the king's face closed like a door.

"Take him below," the Hollow said to the guards reappearing at the doors, its voice bored now, the performance concluded. "The old cells. And send for Captain Vess. Let her look at what loyalty buys, these days."

* * *

The cells beneath the west tower were older than the kingdom. Oath-iron rings bolted into stone that predated Aldric's line, predated probably the concept of mercy. They chained me standing, arms wide, because the Hollow apparently appreciated symbolism, and they left me alone with the dark and the drip of water and my own thoughts, which were, frankly, poor company.

Here is what nobody tells you about keeping faith: it feels exactly like losing. There's no music. No warm glow of righteousness. You hang in the dark with your arms screaming and you think, *well, that's done, and now comes the part where it costs me,* and the cost arrives on schedule and pays itself out slowly, in cold and cramp and the particular loneliness of a man who has chosen a side with no banner.

My oath to Aldric had shattered six years ago in a doorway full of smoke. I'd assumed what remained of me was wreckage. But hanging there, I found something intact underneath — something that had been waiting all this time for me to aim it properly.

An oath, I realized, is just a promise with nowhere left to hide. I had made one at seventeen to a crown. I made a new one now, at forty, in the dark, to no one but myself:

Hold the door. Hold it open this time. However long it takes. Whatever it costs.

Sometime later — hours, or a night — boots on the stair. Not the measured tread of hunters. Something faster, harder, angrier. Torchlight climbed the wall before its bearer did, and then Captain Vess stood in my cell doorway, sword drawn, face grey as the stone, her left arm hanging wrong inside her vambrace as though something under the skin had been scorched.

She looked at me a long moment. Then she looked at the lock on my chains, and drew a thin pick from her bracer, and I understood that whatever had happened upstairs had broken something in her too — something she'd carried longer than I'd carried mine.

"They showed me the decrees," she said quietly, working the lock. "All of them. Signed in his hand. Written by no one." The first shackle fell. "Get up, Cassel. You're going to tell me everything, starting with how to get to her. Because in one night the thirteenth moon rises — and I have thirty hunters who still think they serve a king."

The second shackle dropped, and my arms nearly did too.

"Can you walk?" she asked.

"I can run," I said, and meant every road she pointed me down.
