## 18. Vess's Choice

I have served the crown for nineteen years, and in all that time I have learned one law above every other: the oath does not ask if you are right. It only asks if you obey.

They taught us that on the first day of training, when we were still soft enough to think it was a warning. It wasn't. It was a kindness. Captain Drenna — dead now, gods keep her — put her hand on my shoulder and said, *Vess, the day you start deciding which orders are just is the day you become something worse than an enemy. You become a judge with a sword.* I believed her. I built my whole life on believing her.

Which is why, standing in the cells beneath the hunter barracks with a plague-bearer's truth dripping off her fingers like tar, I wanted very badly to kill Sera and be done with it.

She wouldn't stop talking. That was the worst of it. Traitors beg. Traitors bargain. This woman stood in front of me with her wrists bound and her voice steady as a mason's level, telling me about the king. About what wore him.

"Say it again," I said. "Slowly."

"The Hollow." Sera lifted her chin. "It's been in the crown for longer than anyone knows. It eats his mind, Captain. It writes his decrees while he sleeps. The purge order last autumn — the one you enforced at Millbrook — he never signed it. He doesn't even remember Millbrook exists."

"Everyone remembers Millbrook," I said. "It's on the tax rolls."

"It's not anymore. Nothing is anymore. That's how it works — it doesn't just kill people, it un-writes them." Her eyes were dark and furious and terribly awake. "You've carried out eleven decrees this year that came from a thing wearing your king's face. Eleven. How many graves did you dig?"

"Enough," I said, before I could stop myself.

The word hung there between us like a blade someone had dropped.

I turned away from her because turning away from prisoners is what captains do, and I needed the ritual of it. I needed something — anything — to be simple. The cell block was quiet except for the drip of water through old stone and the breathing of the man in the third cell down. Cassel. They'd brought him in two days ago, half-dead, refusing food, refusing speech, refusing everything except the occasional look through the bars that said, with remarkable clarity for a disgraced knight, that he pitied me.

Nobody pities a captain of the king's hunters. We don't allow it.

"Captain Vess." Sera's voice again, softer now. "You already know. Don't you?"

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying around in my chest for months like a swallowed stone.

Because yes. Yes, I knew. Not the shape of it, not the name, but I knew the way a dog knows a storm before the sky turns. I knew it in the decrees that read like they'd been written by something learning humanity secondhand, from a book with pages missing. I knew it the night I delivered the writ to the village outside Corren's Ford and found the doors already painted white with ash and the people gone, and no record anywhere that the village had ever existed, and my own memory sliding off the name of it like rain off oilcloth. I knew it every time I knelt to receive new orders and felt something cold move behind the king's eyes, patient as a worm in fruit.

I had known. I had simply chosen — with tremendous discipline, with nineteen years of practice — not to.

"You're lying," I said to Sera, "or you're mad, or you're bait. Those are the only three options, and none of them change what happens next."

"What happens next?" she asked. "Tell me. Say it out loud. What does the oath require?"

*Execution at dawn,* said the oath, warm and familiar as a hearth. *The prisoner dies. The captain reports. The law continues.*

"The prisoner dies," I said.

"And then what? You go back up those stairs, and you kneel, and something behind the king's eyes looks at you and says *well done*, and you feel — what? Proud?" She shook her head slowly. "I've watched you, Captain. Through the cracks in this place. You sleep in the armory instead of your quarters. You sharpen your sword at hours when no one sharpens swords unless they're trying not to think. You know."

"I said—"

"You sharpen your sword," she repeated, relentless, "because some part of you is waiting for a fight you haven't been allowed to have yet. Nineteen years of service, and the only enemy you've ever really wanted is the one you're not permitted to see."

I hit the wall. Open-palmed, hard enough to sting, because if I'd used the fist I'd have broken something and then I'd have had to explain the broken thing. The sound went down the corridor and came back, and in the third cell I heard Cassel shift against the stones.

"He can hear all this," I said.

"Good," said Cassel's voice, low and rough from two days of silence. "About time somebody said it to her face."

***

They let me take his interrogation myself. Of course they did — I was captain; the prisoner who'd fought six hunters to a standstill in the Pass of Ash was mine by right of capture, such as it was. Such as it ever was. Half the things I owned had been handed to me by something else's decree.

I brought bread and water because that's what you do when you want to talk to a prisoner rather than break him, and I sat down on the floor across from his cell, which broke about nine regulations at once, and I ate some of the bread so he'd know it wasn't poisoned, and we sat there in the dripping quiet like two people at a funeral who weren't sure whose.

"They say you were at Thornfell," I said. "Years ago. The purge of the storm-callers."

Something moved behind his face. "I was."

"You were oathbound."

"I was oathbound," he agreed. "Do you know what I've learned since, Captain Vess? Oaths are tools. A hammer doesn't care whether you build a house or break a skull. The oath held true the whole time I was doing the worst thing I've ever done. It held perfectly. That's what nobody tells you." He leaned forward into the torchlight, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had already lost everything worth keeping and found it made him lighter, not heavier. "Your oath will hold true too. Right up until the day you understand that holding true isn't the same as being right. Then you'll have to choose which master you serve — the words, or what the words were supposed to protect."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"Then you'll be me." He almost smiled. "Free, and ruined, and still walking. There are worse fates. I've checked."

I should have left then. I had leave to leave, duty to leave, nineteen years of habit pulling me toward the stairs like a tide. Instead I heard myself ask the question I'd carried up from the cells like an ember cupped in bare hands:

"The courier. The one who died at the snowline with the map. Did you swear to him?"

"Yes."

"And you failed him."

"No." His voice didn't waver. "He gave the map to the storm-caller girl because I told him to. My failure was years older than that. But the oath — the oath I kept. I'm still keeping it. Just not the way the words were written." He studied me across the bars. "You came down here to decide something. So decide. But decide fast, Captain. The thirteenth moon turns tomorrow night, and whatever sits on that throne has spent centuries arranging itself for that night. After it, there won't be anything left to choose for."

***

I did not sleep. I sat in my quarters with my sword across my knees and did what Sera had accused me of — though not sharpening it, this time. Thinking. Which for me had always been the same motion performed more slowly.

Nineteen years. Recitations, promotions, commendations, the scar across my forearm from the border war, the men and women under my command who trusted me because I was the fixed point, the one who never bent. If I broke the oath, I didn't break it alone. Every hunter in this garrison would wake up inside my choice like a house fire starting in their own bed.

That was the argument for obedience, and it was a good one, and it was also — I finally admitted it, sitting alone at the bottom of the night — the argument cowards make. *Think of the others.* As if the others' safety required feeding them, one decree at a time, to whatever wore the crown.

There was a way to test it. Mother Ije's kind had taught the old orders their binding rites, back before the purge, and the oath-spell we hunters swore was old magic, older than the dynasty. Old magic leaves seams. And I had spent nineteen years maintaining my gear, and I knew exactly where the seam in mine ran: a single phrase, spoken against the binding, spoken with the full weight of the oath behind it, that would either prove the oath clean — or prove it hollow.

If I spoke it and nothing happened, I would be a traitor who'd confessed to empty air, and I would hang at dawn beside Sera, and that would be that.

If I spoke it and something happened—

I got up. Some part of me noted, distantly, that my hands were steadier than they'd been in months. Apparently the body decides these things before the mind consents. Apparently it always had.

***

The oath-spell lives where they put it during the rite: along the sword arm, from shoulder to wrist, a line of cold that most hunters stop noticing after the first year, the way you stop noticing a chain once you've worn it long enough. When I unstrapped my vambrace in the empty armory and rolled up my sleeve, the mark showed faintly silver in the lamplight, the way it always had. Nineteen years of silver. Nineteen years of cold.

I laid my bare hand over it and spoke the seam-phrase. Old words, in the old tongue, the ones Drenna had drilled out of us until we forgot we'd ever known them — but the body keeps its own records, and my mouth remembered.

*"By the hand that swears, I ask: whom do I truly serve?"*

For one heartbeat, nothing. And I confess that in that heartbeat I felt relief, vast and shameful, the relief of a woman about to be told the nightmare was only a nightmare—

Then the silver line woke.

It didn't glow. Glow would have been merciful. It *moved* — crawling up my arm like frost given purpose, and with it came the answer, poured directly into the meat of me, not in words but in certainty, the way you know your own name: and the answer was not *the king*. Was not *the realm*. Was not even *the law*.

The answer was a hunger. Old and patient and coiled somewhere above me in the citadel, wearing gold, and my oath — my beautiful, blameless, nineteen-year oath — was a leash running straight from my sword arm into its mouth.

I had time to think, quite calmly, *Drenna, you were wrong, there IS something worse than a judge with a sword,* and then the spell understood what I understood, and it defended itself.

Fire, from the inside. The silver line went white, then orange, searing along the channel where the oath had lived, burning out the binding the way a body burns out poison — or the way it burns out a limb it means to lose. I bit down on the strap of my dropped vambrace and did not scream, because nineteen years of command meant that if any hunter came running they'd find their captain standing upright, and gods help me, I was going to be standing upright.

When it ended I was on my knees after all, and my right arm lay in my lap like something washed ashore — alive, mine, but scorched from wrist to shoulder, the skin split along the old oath-line in a perfect seam, and beneath my fingers the nerves answered slow and wrong, a hand that would hold a sword the way a stranger holds a stranger's child. Maybe forever. Maybe not. The healers could tell me later, if there was a later.

I looked at the burn and I laughed. Actually laughed, alone on the armory floor at the black end of night, because it was so exactly, so perfectly proportionate: the price of the truth was the sword arm itself. The oath took back the only part of me it had ever truly owned.

*Well done,* whispered something far above, in the citadel, in the dark. Or perhaps I imagined it. Perhaps I hadn't. Either way, it was the last order I ever intended to obey.

***

Sera flinched when she saw the arm. "Gods, Captain—"

"It's fine." It was not fine. I flexed the fingers anyway, making them work, making them mine. "Listen carefully. Dawn is in four hours. Here's what's going to happen, and here's what I need."

I unlocked her wrists myself. She rubbed them and waited, smarter than to interrupt.

"Cassel walks free tonight. Not escapes — *walks*. Officially I'll report a cell breach, unnamed parties, under investigation." I heard myself planning treason in the flat tone I used for supply requisitions and thought, absurdly, that Drenna would have appreciated the form of it. "Then at first light I ride to the capital garrison, and I put before my hunters what I saw in that spell. All of them swore the same oath I did. All of them carry the same leash. When they learn what's on the other end—" I stopped. Flexed the burned hand again. "Some will call me traitor. Fine. Let them. I've been called worse by better kings."

"And the thirteenth moon?"

"Tomorrow night." I looked toward the narrow window slit, where the sky was going from black to bruise. "Whatever comes, it comes with me choosing it. That's all I've got left to give the crown I actually swore to — not the thing in the gold, but the idea. The realm the words were supposed to protect." I turned back to Sera. "Now. Tell me everything about the storm-caller. Everything. If she means to tear that parasite out of the king's skull tomorrow night, then she'll need a door opened from the inside."

Sera smiled for the first time since her capture, and it was not a gentle smile. "Captain," she said, "have I got a plan for you."

Down the corridor, in the third cell, keys turned in a lock that had held for two days, and Cassel stepped out into the torchlight rubbing his freed wrists, and looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors — the face of a person who has finally stopped serving the letter of an oath and started serving its soul.

"Hunters' cells open," I told him, and it cost me more than the arm ever could, and less. "Walk out, knight. And whatever you're riding toward — I'm riding with you."
