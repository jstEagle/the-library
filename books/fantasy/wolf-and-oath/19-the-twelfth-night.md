## 19. The Twelfth Night

There is a particular silence that falls on a mountain an hour before a storm. The birds stop. The wind holds its breath. Everything alive goes very still and very quiet, the way a liar does when you ask him where he was last night.

The citadel of King Aldric wore that silence at dusk on the twelfth night, and I hated it, because I knew what it meant. The Hollow knew we were coming. It had always known. And it was waiting with its hands folded like a grandmother at a funeral.

"Cheerful thought," Cassel murmured beside me. He'd learned to read my face somewhere between the Pass of Ash and here, which was either intimacy or a tactical disadvantage, and I hadn't decided which.

"I didn't say anything."

"You do that thing with your jaw. Like you're chewing on a thought and it's undercooked." He adjusted the strap of his sword — Vess's old training blade, since his had been left in a hunter's cell along with most of his dignity. "What's the plan if the gates are already open?"

I looked down from the ridge at the citadel's lower gate, standing wide as a tavern door, torches burning unattended, no guards in sight.

"That," I said, "is the problem."

* * *

We'd come down out of the high passes in the last grey hour before dark, eleven of us: me, Cassel, Vess with her burned arm strapped useless against her ribs, and eight smugglers who had followed me through Thornfell for six years without ever once asking why I flinched at thunder. Their leader, a wiry woman named Brannoc with a scar through her eyebrow and a laugh like a cart breaking an axle, had summed up our odds that morning.

"One parasite wearing a king, a hundred hunters, a fortress built by paranoid masons with too much money and too few hobbies, and us. Eleven." She'd spat over the edge of the trail. "I've had worse odds. Never by much, but I've had them."

Now we crouched behind a fallen watchtower fifty yards from the open gate, and the open gate stared back at us like a trap it was rather pleased to be.

"It's a invitation," said Fen, the youngest of Brannoc's crew, who could smell an ambush the way other people smelled rain. "You don't leave your front door open unless you want something to walk in."

"Or unless everyone inside is dead," Vess said quietly.

We all considered that. None of us liked the flavor of it.

"The throne room's beneath the central spire," Vess went on. She spoke with her left hand pressed flat against her thigh, steadying the tremor the oath-spell's burning had left in her sword arm. "There are two ways in from the ground floor — the great stair, which they'll have covered, and the servants' gallery, which Sera will have—"

"Sera," Cassel said, "is either waiting for us or in a cell, and we won't know which until we're standing in front of her."

"She got me out of a cell three days ago," Vess said. "She'll be there."

"And if she's not?"

Vess didn't answer. Her jaw did the thing mine does, and I understood suddenly why Cassel found it so irritating. It's a terrible thing, seeing your own habits on someone else's face.

"We go tonight," I said. Not because I'd found some brilliant argument, but because there wasn't one. "The moon turns tomorrow night. Whatever the Hollow means to seal into that crown, it seals it then. Tonight it still has to wear Aldric like a coat, and a man in a coat can be caught off guard. Tomorrow it might not need him anymore."

"And you?" Brannoc asked me. Not cruelly. She never asked questions cruelly; she just asked them straight, which amounts to the same wound. "You ready to call it? All of it?"

Every head turned toward me. I hate that. I've spent my whole life hating that — the moment when people look at you like you're weather about to happen instead of a woman standing in the mud.

"No," I said honestly. "But Mother Ije died buying us hours, and she didn't die so I could stand on this ridge admiring the architecture. So we go anyway."

Brannoc grinned, scar and all. "Good enough. Let's go get murdered efficiently."

* * *

The gate was exactly as much of a trap as it looked, and we walked into it anyway, because the trick with traps isn't avoiding them — anyone can avoid a trap. The trick is choosing which teeth you put your leg between.

Fen and two others went first, dressed as drunk drovers, singing a smuggler's song about a tax collector and a donkey that I will not repeat here because my exile would become permanent in several additional ways. They staggered through the gate. Nothing happened. Then nothing continued happening, all the way across the outer court, and I felt the hair rise on my arms, because ambushes that don't spring aren't absent. They're patient.

We came through in twos after that, cloaks up, weapons wrapped in sacking to look like tools. The court was empty. Not merely unguarded — *empty*. The stables stood open with the horses gone. The guardhouse door hung ajar. A pot of stew sat cold on a cookfire in the barracks kitchen, skinned over with fat, abandoned mid-evening.

"Where is everyone?" Cassel breathed.

"Gone," Vess whispered. "All of them. The whole garrison."

"Deserted?"

"No." She knelt by the cookfire, touched the cold pot, and her face went grey. "Look at this. They were eating. You don't desert mid-meal. Something *took* them, or called them, or—" She stopped. She was a hunter; she had spent fifteen years making people disappear and reading how disappearances happened, and whatever conclusion she'd reached, she swallowed it rather than say it aloud.

We moved through the citadel's throat like water finding cracks. Me in front, because the map's storm-script had started glowing faintly through my shirt the moment we crossed the threshold, warm against my chest like a second heartbeat, guiding me left when the corridors branched, tugging me away from doors that were just doors. My mother's order had written that map centuries ago, in a script only storm-callers could read, and now it hummed against my skin like it had been waiting for me specifically, which was flattering and horrifying in equal measure.

The great stair rose ahead of us, thirty feet of pale stone climbing toward the throne room doors, and it was covered — just as Vess predicted — by hunters. Two dozen of them, in black and silver, standing in perfect ranks, utterly motionless, faces blank as eggs.

They weren't guarding the stair. They were *arranged* on it. Symmetrically. Decoratively.

"They're not asleep," Vess whispered, horror creeping into her voice like damp into a wall. "They're... displayed."

"The Hollow doesn't want soldiers tonight," Cassel said. His hand found mine in the dark. His grip was steady, which I resented, because mine wasn't. "It wants witnesses."

* * *

We took the servants' gallery, because even a trap leaves you one corridor where the trap is slightly less proud of itself, and Sera was there — alive, thank every god I don't believe in — with a lamp turned low and a look on her face that made my stomach drop before she said a word.

"You came," she whispered. "Thank the saints. Come quick, come quiet."

"What is it?" Cassel asked.

"He's still in there." Sera's eyes were red-rimmed. "The king. What's left of him. But Yara—" she caught my sleeve, and her hand was shaking. "He surfaced again. This afternoon. For almost a minute. He asked for you by name. He said—" her voice cracked, steadied. "He said, *tell the storm-caller the thirteenth turn comes at moonrise tomorrow, and tell her the thing is afraid of her.*"

"Afraid of me," I repeated flatly.

"It said that?" Cassel asked sharply.

Sera nodded. "And then it stopped surfacing, and the doors sealed, and the guards—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the stair, toward the ranks of standing men. "It called every soul in the citadel to come watch. Cooks, grooms, clerks, all of them, standing in the galleries around the throne room like it's going to be a coronation." Her chin trembled. "Some of them are children. Servants' kids. It stood them in the front rows."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere far above us, through feet of stone, we heard it — faint, rhythmic, wrong. A sound like slow clapping. Or like a heartbeat that had learned to enjoy itself.

"It wants an audience," Brannoc murmured, "for whatever happens at moonrise tomorrow."

"Then tonight," I said, "we disappoint it early."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the last night before everything changes: it's mostly logistics. Rope, rations, which door sticks, who covers whom. Romance lives in songs. Reality lives in counting crossbow bolts by lamplight while a woman with a ruined arm shows you which way the throne room hinges swing.

We made our camp of sorts in the gallery's wine cellar, because if you must spend your possibly-final night in a haunted fortress, you may as well do it surrounded by decent inventory. Brannoc broke out a bottle of the good stuff — stolen, obviously, everything Brannoc owned had been stolen at least twice — and poured small cups all around.

"To Mother Ije," she said.

We drank. The wine tasted like plums and smoke and the inside of a memory. Fen cried a little and pretended he hadn't. Nobody let him pretend hard.

Then Vess laid out the throne room on the cellar floor with salt and knife-points, and we planned, and the planning was grim and thorough and took two hours, and when it was done there was nothing left to do but wait for the deep hours when the fortress above slept — or pretended to sleep — and make our move.

Cassel found me sitting on a barrel near the stairs, apart from the others, doing what I do best, which is being alone in a room full of people.

"You're chewing on that thought again," he said, settling beside me. "Undercooked."

"It's afraid of me." I turned my cup in my fingers. "The Hollow. That's what Aldric said. Why would it be afraid of *me*, Cassel? I'm one storm-caller with a stolen map and a hangover starting. It ate my entire order. It ate a *king*. What do I have that they didn't?"

Cassel was quiet for a long moment. Firelight from the single lamp moved on his face, and I saw how much older he looked than when he'd first stepped out of a snowdrift with a sword pointed at me, back when we were enemies and honesty was simpler.

"Maybe it's not afraid of what you have," he said finally. "Maybe it's afraid of what you'd give up. It feeds on minds, on memories, on kingdoms forgetting their own names. Everything it's ever taken, it took from people who wanted to keep it." He looked at me. "You've already lost your homeland. Your order. Your name in the records. You've been paying the price it charges since before it ever met you. There's nothing left it can hold over you."

"That's the saddest compliment anyone's ever given me."

"It's the only kind I know how to give. I was trained by monks and then by killers, and both curricula lacked electives."

Despite everything — despite the morning's grief, despite the standing ranks of hollow-eyed hunters upstairs, despite the heartbeat-clap sound seeping down through the stone — I laughed. Just once, short and surprised, like a sneeze. Cassel looked absurdly pleased with himself, which was also infuriating.

"If I tear the thing out of that crown tomorrow," I said, quieter now, "and the storm takes me with it — the way it took my order, the way exile was supposed to—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," he agreed. "But I'll be holding onto you while you find out, and I've got a very firm grip. Years of gripping things. It's the one skill they couldn't take away when they struck my name from the rolls."

I leaned my shoulder against his. Outside, far off, the wind had begun to move — the first restless breath of pressure rolling down off the peaks, smelling of iron and rain. Tomorrow's storm, arriving early, checking the venue.

"Twelve turns done," I said. "One to go."

* * *

We went up in the blackest hour, the hour the smugglers call the wolf's yawn, when even insomniacs lose their argument with sleep. Sera led us through passages she had walked since childhood, past doors she unlocked with keys she'd stolen one at a time over four years, saving them like coins against exactly this night. The map against my chest glowed brighter with every step, hot enough now that I could feel it through wool, and the storm-script had begun to crawl — I could see it moving under my shirt, characters shifting, rearranging, writing something new.

By the time we reached the landing above the great hall, I didn't need to unwrap it to read the message. It was pressing itself into my palm through the leather. One line, over and over:

**THE GATE IS OPEN. THE GATE IS OPEN. THE GATE IS OPEN.**

"It knows we're coming," I whispered. "It's known all along."

"Of course it has," Cassel said. "It's been reading your map since you picked it up. Probably reading *you*." He drew his sword. "Question is whether knowing helps it or hurts it."

"How could it hurt it?"

"Because," said Vess, stepping up on my other side, her dead arm strapped tight, her living hand steady on her blade, "things that think they've already won stop watching the door. I've killed forty men who made that mistake."

The throne room doors stood before us at the end of the final corridor — twin slabs of black oak banded with silver, twenty feet tall, carved with the history of a dynasty that had forgotten half its own crimes. And they were open. A hand's width each, no more. Enough darkness between them to see firelight moving within. Enough space for a voice to slip out.

And a voice did.

It came from beyond the doors, resonant and warm and terribly reasonable, the voice of a beloved grandfather or a trusted priest, and it used Aldric's throat the way a musician uses an instrument it owns outright.

"*Storm-caller.*" A pause, savoring. "*Come in. We've kept your seat.*"

Behind me, I heard the smugglers check their grips. Heard Fen exhale. Heard Brannoc mutter, "Well. It's polite. That's somehow worse."

Cassel's hand brushed mine once — brief, deliberate, a promise in the shape of a touch. Vess rolled her shoulders and set her face into the expression of a woman about to commit treason with excellent posture.

I put my hand on the door.

Inside, the great hall blazed with a thousand candles, and the galleries were packed with the taken — hundreds of empty-eyed souls standing in perfect silence, and in their center, on a throne that had grown thorns of black glass up its arms like frost, sat the thing that wore the king.

Aldric's body looked smaller than it had in any portrait, hollowed, candlelit, translucent at the edges like paper held too long against a flame. But the eyes were awake. Both of them. And for one staggering instant, looking up at me across that burning hall, the man underneath surfaced — just long enough to shape three words with cracked lips:

"*Hurry. It's feeding.*"

Then the eyes went smooth as river stones, and the thing smiled with his mouth, and raised one hand in welcome.

I stepped through the doors.

And in my palm, the bottled storm Mother Ije had died to press into my hands — the glass vial I'd carried against my heart since the Pass of Ash, the last gift of the last elder of my slaughtered order —

— shattered.

Not dropped. Not broken. *Released.* Light poured out between my fingers like dawn through a crack in a wall, and the sound that came with it was the first true thunderclap of the storm, rolling up from the floor of the world, and every candle in the hall bent sideways at once.

The Hollow's smile did not falter.

But its fingers tightened on the throne's arms, and the black glass *cracked*.

"*At last,*" it said. "*Let us begin.*"
