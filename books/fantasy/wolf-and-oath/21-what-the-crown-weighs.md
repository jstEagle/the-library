## 21. What the Crown Weighs

The storm ended the way storms do — all at once, like a door slamming on a room full of shouting people — and the silence it left behind was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

I was on my knees in the wreckage of the throne room. I don't remember getting there. One moment I was holding Yara's shoulders while lightning walked her spine, and the next the wind simply *stopped*, and we both went down like puppets with cut strings. Somewhere behind us, glass crunched. Somewhere ahead, a man who had been a king for thirty years was breathing.

I got up because getting up was the only thing I still knew how to do. My sword arm was useless — not broken, just spent, the way a rope goes limp after it has held something too heavy for too long. I crossed the hall on legs that had opinions about the matter, and I reached the dais, and I looked down at King Aldric the Unbroken, and he was weeping.

I had never seen a king weep. I had seen them angry, seen them grieve in the careful, rationed way kings grieve, seen one of them order my brother's name struck from the rolls because grief was inconvenient. But this was different. This was a man crying the way a man cries when he has come up from drowning and the first breath hurts worse than the water did.

"It's quiet," he said. His voice was a ruin. "In here. It's *quiet*."

"Your Majesty," I began, and he laughed — a cracked, terrible sound.

"Don't. Please. I heard what it called itself through my mouth for years. I heard the decrees. I signed them. My hand moved, and I thought—" He pressed the heel of his palm against his eyes. "I thought the strength was mine. All of it. The certainty. The clarity. It told me I was the strongest thing in the kingdom, and I believed it, because I wanted to."

Behind me, Yara stood in the wreckage of the shattered glass, storm-light still fading from her skin like embers going out. She looked like a woman who had been emptied and refilled with something larger than herself. She looked, if I am honest, like she was about to fall over, and I made a private note to catch her when she did.

But first — the king.

* * *

Aldric did not die that night, though I think he wanted to. The Hollow had been feeding on him for so long that its removal was less a surgery than an amputation. Mother Ije — dead these two days on the Pass of Ash, buying us hours with her life — had warned us in her dry, unhurried way: *You can pull a root from a garden, girl, but the garden remembers the shape of it.* The king's mind had grown around the parasite the way a tree grows around a nail. Tearing it out had torn other things loose.

He had maybe a season. The physicians said less. Aldric himself, when they told him, smiled for the first time in what I suspect was years and said, "Good. Then I'll know what I'm doing with it."

The first thing he did with it was abdicate.

He did it in the throne room, before the assembled remnant of a court that had spent the last week in open terror of what wore their king's face. Vess stood guard at his shoulder — her sword arm bound and blackened from the oath-spell's breaking, her face gray with pain she refused to acknowledge — and when the herald read the instrument of abdication, the hall made a sound I will remember until I die. Not cheering. Not quite. Something older than cheering. The sound of people who had been holding their breath for a decade finally letting it out.

Aldric did not hand the crown to an heir. There was no heir; the Hollow had seen to that, quietly, over the years, in ways that would take historians a generation to untangle. Instead he named a council. Seven seats. And here is the part that will be argued about in taverns for a hundred years: the first name he spoke was not a duke's, not a general's, not a bishop's.

It was the name of a village that did not exist.

"Thornmere," he said, and the name came out of him like a splinter coming out of a wound. "The village I erased. The decree I signed and did not remember signing. Its survivors will hold a seat. Not in memory of them. *In* them. Let the crown sit in the same room as what it broke."

They found the survivors — what remained of them — in the records-vaults of the citadel itself, because the Hollow had kept its trophies close. The village's ledgers, its parish rolls, the letters of its dead. And three living souls: an old woman named Bette who had spent forty years unable to remember her mother's face, and two brothers who had grown up believing they were orphans of nowhere because no document in the kingdom would admit their home had ever stood.

I watched Bette take her seat on the council dais, in her patched coat, her hands shaking, and I watched a duke's mouth go thin and a bishop's eyes go narrow, and I thought: *good*. Let them choke on it. Let every council session for the rest of the kingdom's history open with the memory of what a crown can do when it forgets it is only a circle of metal.

Yara stood beside me in the gallery. She had cleaned up, mostly. There was still a streak of soot at her jaw that no one had the courage to point out.

"They'll fight it," she said quietly. "The dukes. The guilds. A council with a forgotten woman on it — they'll call it madness."

"They'll call it a great many things," I said. "But the king is dying, and dying kings get to spend their last coin however they like. And Vess's hunters are loyal to the council now. And the smugglers know every road into this city." I glanced at her. "It will hold. Not because it is just. Because it is *inconvenient to fight*, which in my experience is the stronger foundation."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. It was the first one in three days. I counted it the way a miser counts coin.

* * *

Vess came to me that evening in the barracks infirmary, where I was having my sword arm looked at by a physician who kept clucking at it like a hen. She stood at the foot of the bed in a clean uniform, her ruined arm bound in a sling, and for a long moment neither of us said anything, because we had spent ten years on opposite sides of an oath and neither of us had a map for the country on the other side of it.

"They're calling it the Pardon of the Broken Oath," she said finally. "The council's first act. Every hunter who turned against the Hollow's orders is cleared. Every crime committed in obedience to the crown is struck from the record." Her mouth twisted. "Struck from the record. You'd think they'd have learned."

"You don't want it struck."

"I want it *read aloud*." She sat down heavily on the edge of the next bed. "I want it read aloud every year, in that hall, with the names. Mine first. I broke a spell, and it took my sword arm, and I would do it again, and I do not want the convenience of forgetting. That's how it started last time. With convenience."

The physician clucked again and gave up on my arm. Vess watched him go, then looked at me with those flat, tireless eyes that had chased us across half the Thornfell.

"Cassel. The courier. Renn." My chest went tight at the name. "I killed him. Or my hunters did, on my order, before you ever reached the snowline. I want you to know I know it. The pardon covers it. I don't want it covered."

"I know," I said. "I was there for what came after. I've been carrying what came after for a long time."

"I know that too." She stood. At the door she paused. "They're going to offer you a knighthood. The council. Bette asked for it specifically — said the man who carried the map deserved a banner. Don't let them put you in gold. You'd be terrible at it."

She left before I could decide whether to be insulted. She was right, of course. She was usually right. It was one of the many reasons I had spent a decade trying not to admire her.

* * *

They offered me the knighthood three days later, in the rebuilt council chamber, with the morning light coming through windows that still smelled of new glass. Bette did the offering herself, in a voice that had grown steadier with each day her memories came home to her. The brothers from Thornmere flanked her. The dukes watched from the sides with the expressions of men doing arithmetic.

"Cassel of Marrow's Ford," Bette read, from a scroll that had, I noted, my brother's name on it too — restored to the rolls, posthumously, in the same ink as my honor. "The council offers you a knighthood. A banner. Lands. A place at court."

I had rehearsed a refusal on the walk over. Something dignified. Something about service and swords. What came out instead was the truth, which is what happens when you spend a year around Yara Okonkwo — honesty gets into your blood like weather.

"My lady, I had a banner once. I burned it myself, the night I helped burn her order." I nodded at Yara, who stood at the chamber's edge with her arms crossed and her face carefully still. "A knighthood would be a new banner, and I would wear it the way I wore the old one — as permission to stop asking questions. I've had enough permission. What I need now is the road."

Bette looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something no one at court would have dared: she laughed, short and warm. "The road, then. But the council keeps your name in its rolls, Sir Cassel, banner or no. Some honors you don't get to refuse by walking away from them."

I bowed, because it seemed rude not to, and because my knees were grateful for the excuse.

Yara found me in the corridor afterward. She was carrying two apples she had almost certainly stolen from the council kitchens, and she tossed me one without ceremony.

"Sir Cassel," she said, savoring it.

"Not another word."

"Ser Cassel of the Road. Knight-errant of Nowhere. Defender of the Unwritten."

"I will throw this apple at you."

She grinned, bit into hers, and for a moment we just stood there in the corridor of a palace we had broken into as fugitives, eating stolen fruit in the morning sun, and I thought: *this*. Not the council, not the pardon, not the name in the rolls. This. A person who steals apples and throws your failures back at you with a grin, walking beside you down a corridor that used to be a wall between you and everything you wanted.

The king was dying. The kingdom was mending the way broken things mend — crookedly, with visible seams. And somewhere south of the city there were smugglers who owed me money, and a mountain range that knew my footsteps, and a woman beside me with storm-script rising on her palm like a road being drawn in ink that hadn't finished drying.

She looked down at that palm, suddenly, mid-bite. Frowned.

"Cassel."

"What?"

"It's writing again." She turned her hand toward the light. The script was moving — not the slow, patient crawl it had been since the storm, but quick, urgent, like a hand hurrying to finish a letter before the ink runs out. "It's never written this fast. It's never written *this much*."

I leaned in. Neither of us could read it. But I had spent a year watching her read storm-script, and I knew what her face did when it was a map and what it did when it was a warning.

This was neither.

"What does it say?" I asked.

She stared at her own palm, and I watched the color leave her face the way color leaves the sky before weather.

"It's not from the map," she said. "It's not from Mother Ije's order. It's a reply." Her fist closed slowly around the light. "Something out there just read *me*."
