## 1. The Weight of Ordinary Salt

The ninth boundary stone had gone quiet again, and Hazel Nakamura pressed her palm against it and pretended, for the hundredth time in her life, that she could hear what everyone else heard.

The stone stood at the end of the Shepherd's Trace, where the valley's western hills folded down into a shoulder of grey granite, and beyond it the world simply stopped—not with a wall or a fence or any line a cartographer might draw, but with a stillness so complete that birds turned back mid-flight and even the wind seemed to arrive at the ward-line and think better of crossing. Salt crusted the stone's base in pale rime, thick as frost in winter, thin as breath in summer. Three hundred years of tending had worn the old carvings smooth, but Hazel knew them by touch: the interlocking rings of the Guild sigil, the names of the first nine Saltwrights cut into the crown like teeth.

And beneath all of it, if you were a true Saltwright—if you carried the bloodline properly, if your ears were tuned to the right frequency of the world—you would hear the salt-song. A hum, her grandmother called it. Like standing inside a struck bell. Like the valley itself breathing through its skin.

Hazel heard nothing. She never had.

She let her hand rest on the stone anyway, letting the warmth of the afternoon sun-baked granite seep into her fingers, and performed the small theater of listening. Eyes half-closed. Head tilted. The expression she'd practiced in the polished lid of her grandmother's writing chest until it looked less like concentration and more like devotion. Around her, the high meadow grass whispered and bowed in long silver waves, and somewhere downslope a shepherd's dog barked twice at nothing, at the edge of things, at the place where the world ended.

"Nothing today," she murmured to the stone, when no one was near enough to hear. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "You and me both."

The stone said nothing back. It never did. But it was warm, and it was solid, and it had stood here longer than any living member of her family, and there was a strange comfort in that—in being silent together.

She took out her kit: the soft horsehair brush, the jar of coarse guild-salt ground fine as sugar, the little copper scoop her grandfather had hammered from a melted-down census weight. The work itself she could do. The work, in fact, she did better than anyone in the guild under sixty—better than old Maren whose hands shook too badly to pack the seams evenly, better than Tam Voss who rushed every job because his mind ran three tasks ahead of his feet. She brushed the accumulated grit from the channels cut around the stone's foot. She checked the hairline cracks for widening, tracing each one with a fingernail and marking the length in her ledger with a notation system of her own devising, precise to the finger-width. She packed fresh salt into the seam where the stone met the earth, pressing it down in layers the way Ilsa had taught her before Hazel could read—*firmly, girl, like you're setting a broken bone*—and poured a thin ring of brine around the whole, which would dry by evening into a crust bright enough to catch the last light.

It was good work. It was honest work. It was the only part of the inheritance she could carry without feeling like a fraud wearing her grandmother's coat.

The problem was tomorrow. Tomorrow was the triennial census, when the entire guild walked the ward-line together and each Saltwright, in turn, laid hands upon each stone and sang the attunement verse aloud, proving the wards held and the bloodlines held and the valley remained sealed as it had been sealed since the Founding. Tomorrow, in front of eight witnesses and whatever townsfolk drifted up to watch the procession, Hazel would stand at this stone—or one of its eighty-one siblings—and open her mouth, and either the song would come or it wouldn't.

It hadn't come in nineteen years of trying.

She sat back on her heels and looked west, past the stone, to where the land fell away beyond the ward-line. From here the world outside the valley showed itself only as a suggestion: a smudge of hills in haze, the far glint of a river she'd never seen, and above it all—the thing every child in the valley learned to stop noticing, the way you learn to stop noticing your own heartbeat—a sky with no stars. Not clouded. *Empty.* The valley's sky held sun and moon and weather and nothing else, and the oldest maps in the guild hall marked the region beyond the mountains with a single handwritten annotation in faded iron-gall ink: *No stars fall here. Do not wonder why.*

Hazel wondered why. She wondered about most things, which her grandmother said was both her finest quality and the flaw that would be the death of her.

She repacked her kit, shouldered her satchel, and began the two-hour walk back along the Trace toward Hollowmere, the market town that served as the valley's heart. The evening light went gold, then copper, then bruised violet over the eastern peaks. Lamplight bloomed in the lower windows of the town as she descended, and the smell of woodsmoke and baking bread and the mineral tang of the salt-flats mingled into something so familiar it ached—home, in every particular, home—and yet tonight the familiarity sat strangely on her, like a garment cut for someone else.

Tomorrow, the census. Tomorrow, the counting.

* * *

Guildmother Ilsa Nakamura received her in the guild hall kitchen, because the guild hall kitchen was where Ilsa received everything—petitions, complaints, visiting dignitaries, and granddaughters—with equal portions of bread, cheese, and unflinching scrutiny.

"You're late," Ilsa said, without looking up from the ledger spread across the scrubbed oak table. She was seventy-four years old and built like the mountain she lived under, a broad woman gone stooped only in the last two years, as though the weight of the guild name were finally settling onto her spine one vertebra at a time. Her hands, spotted and knuckle-swollen, moved across the ledger columns with a sureness her legs no longer possessed. "The ninth takes you four hours if you walk it proper. You've been gone six."

"The brine needed a second coat. The southern face is cracking worse than the last census."

Ilsa's pen stopped. She looked up then, and her eyes—pale grey, sharp as chipped flint—did the thing they always did, the thing Hazel both dreaded and depended on: they took inventory. Soot on the hem. Salt dust on the forearms. The particular set of Hazel's jaw that meant a question was loading behind her teeth.

"Show me your hands."

Hazel held them out. Ilsa took them in her own and turned them over, palms up, thumbs pressing into the flesh below each finger. It was an examination disguised as affection, or affection disguised as an examination; with her grandmother the two had always been indistinguishable. The pads of Hazel's fingers were raw from the brush-work, faintly whitened where the brine had dried in the creases.

"Hm," Ilsa said. "You pack a seam better than Maren did at her best."

"Maren says my lines are too fussy."

"Maren says your lines are *exact*, which frightens people who've spent forty years being merely adequate." She released Hazel's hands and returned to the ledger, but her voice softened by a degree, which for Ilsa constituted effusion. "Eat something. You look like the last week of winter."

The kitchen was warm and smelled of yesterday's stew and the bay leaves Ilsa hung from every beam whether or not she used them. Hazel cut bread and ate it standing, watching her grandmother make the small marks that would become, come morning, the official accounting of the Saltwright Guild: ninety-three stones on the ward-line, their condition, their attunement status, the members available to sing them. The guild's whole history lived in ledgers like these—two centuries of columns in a dozen different hands, recording salt and stone and song, generation after generation after generation.

Nine hands now, including Hazel's. Nine members left. The youngest, besides Hazel herself, was forty-one.

"Ilsa." She hadn't meant to say it yet. It came out anyway, the way the truest things always did. "What happens tomorrow if I can't hear the ninth?"

The pen did not stop this time, but it slowed. "Then we do what we've always done."

"We've never *done* anything. There's never been a census where a Nakamura couldn't—" She caught herself. Lowered her voice, though the hall above was empty except for mice and memory. "Every account in those ledgers has a Nakamura attuning the founding stones. Every single one since Aldric. If I stand there tomorrow and open my mouth and nothing comes out—"

"Then I will attest the stone myself, as Guildmother, and note in the record that my granddaughter assisted with maintenance and was granted deferment of her attunement due to the demands of the survey circuit." Ilsa set down the pen with deliberate calm. "As I have done at the last four censuses."

Hazel's chest tightened. "Four censuses. Twelve years."

"Thirteen. You were late to your first one. You were sick."

"I wasn't sick. You told them I was sick." She hadn't known, then—had been eleven years old, feverish with dread, hiding in the salt cellar while the procession passed the hall. She'd learned later, from a slip of Ilsa's pen, exactly what her grandmother had written in the margin beside her name. *Deferment, illness. Will attune next census.* And the next. And the next. A debt deferred so many times it had grown interest no one could calculate.

Ilsa rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and crossed to the window where the last light lay along the salt-flats like spilled milk. For a moment she only stood there, a broad dark shape against the violet sky, and Hazel thought—not for the first time—that her grandmother looked like something that had been holding a door shut for a very long time and had forgotten how to let go of the handle.

"Do you know what your great-great-grandmother Merren told the census of her day?" Ilsa asked, without turning. "When they asked her why the songs must be sung true, she said: *because the valley does not know it is loved unless someone speaks to it in its own tongue.*" She glanced back over her shoulder. "That is what the salt-songs are, Hazel. Not magic. Not a trick. A conversation, kept faithfully for three hundred years. The stones don't care who sings, so long as someone sings."

"But they *do* care. The bloodlines matter. That's the whole doctrine—"

"The doctrine is what keeps the guild honest between censuses." Ilsa came back to the table and put both hands flat upon it, and looked at her granddaughter with an intensity that made the kitchen feel suddenly smaller. "Here is what matters tomorrow: nine stones go silent in a generation if untended, and the valley goes with them. Here is what matters tomorrow: you will walk the line with the others, you will do the maintenance work that half of them cannot manage anymore, and when your turn comes to sing, I will handle the record. As I have handled it. As I will continue to handle it for as long as I have breath in me. Are we understood?"

"Yes, Guildmother."

"Don't 'Guildmother' me. I'm asking if you understand."

"Yes." Hazel looked down at her bread, at the crumbs scattered on the scarred oak. "I understand. I just—I want you to understand that I know what it costs you. Lying in the guild's own records. In your own hand. If anyone ever reads those margins closely—"

"No one reads margins closely." But Ilsa said it a beat too fast, and Hazel saw—filed away, in the part of her mind that noticed everything and understood almost none of it—that her grandmother's gaze flicked, just once, toward the stair that led up to the private archive. Toward the locked records. Toward the ledgers no one was permitted to read.

"That," Ilsa said, recovering, brisk as a slammed door, "is tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow's problem is solved. Tonight's problem is that the census company arrives at dawn and you haven't slept in two days. Go home. Sleep. Wear the grey dress; the blue one shows salt stains."

Hazel kissed her grandmother's cheek—at the corner of the eye, where the skin had gone softest—and left.

* * *

She didn't go straight home.

The town of Hollowmere at dusk on census-eve was a thing of small rituals: shutters drawn, lamps lit in sequence down the high street like a slow thought traveling, the bakery already stacking wood for the festival loaves. Children ran shrieking through the square playing some game involving chalk circles and shouting, and Hazel stepped around them and felt the familiar twist in her chest—the one that said *you will have children, and they will ask you what the salt-song sounds like, and what will you tell them?*

She found Tam Voss where she knew she would: sitting on the low wall of the well-house, feet dangling over the mouth of the town cistern, whittling a stick with the intense seriousness he brought to everything useless.

"You're doing the ninth tomorrow," he said, by way of greeting. He didn't look up from the stick. He never looked up when he was working something out; looking up interrupted him.

"Everyone's doing the ninth tomorrow."

"I'm doing the ninth tomorrow. You're doing the ninth tomorrow *alone*, because you always take the ninth alone, because it's the farthest walk and nobody else wants it." He finally glanced up. Tam had a long face and quick dark eyes and the kind of restless competence that made him excellent at everything and satisfied with nothing. "Alone, at dusk, the night before. That's not maintenance, Hazel. That's rehearsal."

The trouble with childhood friends was that they remembered who you were before you became careful.

"It's a long walk," she said. "I wanted to start early."

"Mm." He resumed whittling. Shavings curled off the stick and dropped into the cistern's darkness. "My mother asked me today if you'd ever sung at a census. I said of course, dozens of times. She said funny, she couldn't remember hearing you. I said the acoustics are bad up on the line." He blew a shaving off the blade. "I've known you since we were five, Haz. I've watched you fake-listen to that stone more times than I've watched you eat dinner. Your face does a thing. Right here." He tapped his own temple. "Like you're trying to remember where you left something."

"Tam—"

"I'm not going to say anything." He said it fast, and she believed him, and the belief hurt worse than accusation would have. "I'm not going to say anything because you're Ilsa's granddaughter and because the guild falling apart in public helps nobody. But tomorrow there are going to be eight other Saltwrights standing next to you, and Maren's getting sharp in her old age, and Old Correc's boy came up from the flats last month asking why the youngest Nakamura never leads a verse. The lie isn't getting cheaper, is what I'm saying. The lie costs more every year."

Hazel sat down on the wall beside him. Below their feet, the cistern water breathed and shifted in its stone throat, black and patient.

"What am I supposed to do?" she asked quietly. "Tell them? Stand up at the census and announce the last apprentice of the Saltwright Guild is deaf to the one thing that defines us? My grandmother has spent thirteen years covering for me. Thirteen years of falsified records, and for what—for me to burn it all down the day before her last census?" Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "She's seventy-four, Tam. There won't be another one after this. This is her accounting. Let her close her books clean."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. Down in the cistern, the water settled.

"Clean," he repeated softly. "Yeah." Then he handed her the stick he'd been whittling. It was a boundary stone, she saw—a tiny carved thing, no bigger than her thumb, complete with the ringed sigil and the nine founder-names rendered in strokes finer than thread. He'd made her one every year since they were children. She had fourteen of them lined up on her windowsill at home.

"For luck," he said. "Not that luck has anything to do with it."

* * *

She woke before dawn to a sound she had never heard before, and would spend the rest of her life trying to describe.

It came from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls of her room, from the floorboards, from the salt-crusted stones of Hollowmere itself. A hum, deep and vast and patient, like the valley drawing a breath it had been holding for three hundred years. Like a bell the size of the sky, struck once, ringing down through the bones of the earth.

Hazel lay in the dark with her heart slamming and understood, with terrible clarity, what she was hearing.

The salt-song. The real one. The one every Saltwright was born to hear, rising through the valley on the eve of the census as the wards gathered themselves for the accounting.

And it wasn't coming from the boundary stones.

It was coming from underneath. From far below, from the deep places under the mountain where no Saltwright had walked in living memory. Rising up through the roots of the valley like something turning over in its sleep.

In the guild hall across town, in the kitchen where the census ledgers lay waiting, Guildmother Ilsa Nakamura was already awake. She had been awake for hours. And when the sound came, she did not startle—she only closed her eyes, and whispered words into the dark that no one heard, in a language older than the guild, older than the valley:

"Not yet. *Not yet.*"

But the valley had begun, at last, to answer back.
