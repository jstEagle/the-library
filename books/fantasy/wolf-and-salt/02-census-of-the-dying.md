## 2. Census of the Dying

The census ledger was older than the town it recorded, and heavier than any book had a right to be.

Hazel carried it down from the guild hall's archive with both arms, her chin braced against its cracked leather spine to keep it from slipping. The stairwell smelled of tallow and cold stone, of salt worked into mortar so long ago that it had become part of the building's bones. Every surface in the Saltwright Guild hall held that faint mineral tang—the walls, the floors, the very air—and Hazel had grown up believing she could taste it the way her grandmother did, the way every true-born guildsman could: as music.

She tasted nothing. She never had.

In the counting room, the long table had already been laid out in the old way: white cloth, iron weights, a bowl of coarse gray salt from the deep cellar that no one used for anything else anymore. Nine chairs. Ilsa had counted them twice that morning, and Hazel had watched her grandmother's face each time, waiting for the number to change, knowing it wouldn't. Nine. When Hazel was a child there had been thirty. When her mother was a child, the stories said, there had been enough to fill this room shoulder to shoulder, enough voices to sing the full boundary-song at solstice until the stones themselves seemed to hum along.

Nine chairs for a guild that once kept an entire valley sealed.

"Set it on the left," Ilsa said without looking up. She stood at the window, hands folded behind her back, watching the market square fill. Census day drew everyone—farmers come down from the terraces, fisherfolk up from the tarn, children released early from their letters. They came less for the counting than for the ritual of it, the reassurance. Three times a year, spring planting, midsummer, autumn's end, the guild stood before them and read out the state of the wards, and the valley exhaled and went back to work believing itself safe.

Three hundred years of exhaling. Three hundred years of going back to work.

"The ninth stone's report is first?" Hazel asked, arranging quills, ink, the little brass counters they'd use to tally households.

Ilsa turned from the window. Her grandmother was seventy-one years old and still carried herself like the mountain she lived beneath—unhurried, permanent, impossible to argue with unless you were willing to be flattened by the argument. This morning, though, Hazel caught something in the set of her shoulders that she couldn't name. A tightness. Like a woman listening for a sound that hadn't come.

"Decay comes last," Ilsa said. "The living are counted first. That's the order."

"You've asked about the stone, then? The silence?"

"I've asked." Ilsa crossed the room and rested one hand briefly on Hazel's shoulder—a touch light as ash, gone almost before Hazel felt it. "And you'll not stand at my elbow looking like a funeral all day. We count what remains, Hazel. That's all a census has ever been."

*That's all a guild has ever been,* Hazel thought, and didn't say it.

* * *

By midmorning the counting room was full, and the business of the census unfolded with the ponderous rhythm of liturgy. Household by household the town passed before the table: names entered, births recorded, deaths struck through in the old double-line, grain stores assessed against winter, and at every hearth's entry a pinch of hearth-salt added to the great brass scale, because the wards drank from every home in the valley and the guild kept account of what each family owed the boundary.

It should have been Hazel's task to weigh the salt. In the old days, a Saltwright touched each offering and heard its note—the particular resonance of salt drawn from a household's hearth, carrying the warmth and worry of the family that kept it. Old Merrin Hale could tell a quarrel in a marriage from the sound his wife's hearth-salt made. Tam Voss's mother, before she gave up her guild seat to run the mill, claimed she could hear a pregnancy three weeks before anyone knew. The salt sang, and the Saltwrights listened, and that listening was the whole covenant between the guild and the valley: *we hear your homes, and we hold your borders.*

When Hazel took her place at the scale and pressed her fingers into the first pinch of salt, she performed the listening the way she always did. Eyes half-lidded. Head tilted. A slow breath through the nose, as if tasting something subtle. And then the small nod, and the entry in her careful hand: *Note true. Offering sufficient.*

She had been performing this since she was eleven years old. She was nineteen now, and the lie sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

No one knew. No one *could* know—not the townsfolk who brought their offerings with such solemn trust, not the eight remaining guild members who would have to explain to the valley why the Guildmother's heir was deaf to the covenant itself, and above all not Ilsa, whose whole life was the guild and whose guild, increasingly, was only Hazel. The salt-songs ran in the blood; that was the doctrine. They had thinned with every generation, but they had never simply failed to appear. Until Hazel. The wrong kind of heir in a family running out of heirs.

"Hazel." Ilsa's voice cut across the room, gentle and public. "The Aldous offering. Your reading?"

Hazel blinked down at the pinch of salt in her palm. Widow Aldous, hill farm, two goats, a son lost to the fever four winters back. She knew the shape of the woman's grief the way she knew the shape of every family in the valley—not through magic, but through nine years of paying attention, which was harder work than magic and, Hazel suspected, worth more.

"True note," Hazel said. "A little thin. She's been drawing from the same crock too long—I'll bring her fresh stock from the cellar stores tomorrow."

Ilsa's eyes rested on her a moment longer than the answer required. Then she nodded and moved on, and Hazel let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

Her grandmother covered for her. That was the truth Hazel circled on sleepless nights and refused to land on by daylight: somewhere in the last few years, Ilsa had stopped being fooled—if she ever had been fooled—and started being complicit. The readings Hazel faked, Ilsa confirmed. The songs Hazel mouthed at the boundary stones, Ilsa harmonized beneath, loud enough to drown whatever failure the stones might have reported. It was love, probably. It was certainly protection. But it felt like being wrapped in wool and buried, and some mornings Hazel woke furious at her grandmother for the kindness of it.

The morning wore on. Names were counted. Salt was weighed. The scale tipped and settled, tipped and settled, and the ledger filled with the valley's small persistent life, and outside the tall windows the autumn sun climbed toward noon over fields that had been safe for three hundred years because nine people in a stone hall pretended very hard that safety was a thing someone still maintained.

Then Tam Voss arrived, late and dusty, with news.

* * *

He came in through the side door with his riding coat still on, which was a breach of census etiquette so fundamental that three separate guild members started to scold him at once. Tam ignored all of them. He was twenty, broad-shouldered from the mill work he'd taken when his family's guild seat lapsed, with his father's sandy hair and his mother's habit of looking at doors like they owed him money. He found Hazel's eye across the crowded room and something in his face made her stomach drop before he'd said a word.

"Guildmother." He bowed, quick and graceless. "Forgive the interruption. I rode out past the tarn this morning—checking the fence lines where the high pasture meets the ward-road. I thought you'd want to know before the reading." He hesitated. "The ninth stone's not just silent. From the road you can see it's leaning. Cracked clean down the face, like a tree struck by lightning."

The room went quiet in that particular way rooms do when bad news arrives dressed as gossip. Hazel saw the townsfolk nearest the door exchange glances. The ninth stone. Everyone knew the stones the way farmers know weather—by feel, by inheritance. The youngest of the thirteen, raised only eighty years ago when the eastern marsh shifted, and the one the guild's own records called *sound beyond sound*, the strongest of the line. Its song was supposed to be audible from half a mile away on a windless night, a low silver thread that mothers pointed out to children: *hear that? That's the edge of the world. Nothing crosses it.*

"It's decay," Ilsa said, into the silence. Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice she used for verdicts. "Stone wears. The marsh ground shifts under it. We'll inspect it after the reading and re-dress the core if needed."

"Guildmother—" Tam started.

"After the *reading*, Master Voss." A flicker of steel. "The valley will not be frightened off its own census day by a cracked rock. Sit down."

Tam sat. But his eyes found Hazel's again across the room, and what was in them wasn't fear. It was the look of a man who has seen something he can't fit into the story he's been told, and wants very badly to compare notes with the only other person he knows who collects things that don't fit.

* * *

The formal reading took place at noon on the guild steps, as it had for three centuries. The eight remaining guild members stood in a crescent behind Ilsa—old Merrin, the two Ferrow sisters who finished each other's sentences and, apparently, each other's prayers, blind Brother Anselm who had memorized every verse of the boundary-song and could no longer hear if his own voice matched it, and the rest, gray heads and stooped backs, a hedge grown sparse. Hazel stood at Ilsa's right hand, the heir's position, holding the open ledger.

Ilsa spoke the census aloud to the assembled town. Four hundred and six households. Two hundred and ninety-three hearth-offerings received, thirteen remitted for hardship, one hundred new lambs, forty-one deaths, sixty-seven births—the numbers rose and fell in the old cadence, and the crowd murmured its responses like a congregation, and for a little while everything was as it had always been.

Then: "And the wards themselves." Ilsa paused. Hazel, close enough to see her grandmother's face in profile, saw her gather herself. "Twelve of the thirteen boundary stones report true. The ninth has fallen quiet. The guild will inspect it within the week and make repair as needed. There is no cause for alarm."

The crowd's murmur changed pitch. Hazel felt it move through them like wind through wheat—unease, quickly smoothed over by habit. Three hundred years of being told *there is no cause for alarm* had trained the valley well.

But Ilsa hadn't finished. Hazel knew the script; she'd copied it into her practice books at age seven. After the wards, the Guildmother named the guild's own strength, the closing assurance: *the Saltwright Guild numbers its faithful at—*

"The Saltwright Guild," Ilsa began—

—and stopped.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. To the crowd it must have looked like nothing, a breath, a pause for gravity. But Hazel stood close enough to see her grandmother's jaw tighten, to see the old woman's gaze go briefly, terribly inward, and Hazel understood that Ilsa had reached the number and found she couldn't say it. Not because she didn't know it. Because saying it aloud, here, in front of all of them, would make it real in a way ink never could.

"—numbers its faithful at nine," Ilsa said. "Nine sworn keepers, and one apprentice. Ten souls to hold what our founders built. As it has ever been: the guild is not its number. The guild is its vow."

She went on to the closing blessing, the formal words, the dismissal. The crowd dispersed slowly, in knots, talking low. Hazel stood on the steps holding the ledger against her chest and watched them go, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for years. Ten. Ten people, and of those ten, one blind man who couldn't hear the stones, two sisters past seventy, and herself, who couldn't hear anything at all. Six functioning ears in a guild that had once fielded sixty singers, guarding thirteen stones across forty miles of border, around a valley of four hundred households who believed, completely, that they were safe.

Ten souls. And one of them a fraud.

* * *

They rode out at dusk, against orders.

Not far—Tam had argued for dawn, and Hazel had argued that dawn meant witnesses, and in the end they'd compromised on the hour when the last light leaves the western ridge and the watch fires aren't yet lit, which is the hour the valley belongs to nobody. Hazel had left a note in her grandmother's study: *Gone to look at the ninth stone. Back by midnight. Don't wait up.* It was either honesty or defiance or both; she genuinely couldn't tell anymore.

The ward-road ran east from the tarn through country that grew wilder the closer it came to the boundary. Hazel had walked this road a hundred times—every apprentice did, learning the stones—but tonight it felt different, and she realized, about a mile out, why. She was listening. Actually listening, the way the others did, straining her whole self toward a sound she had no reason to expect and every reason to want. Twelve stones were supposed to be singing. If she concentrated until her head ached, she could almost convince herself she felt something—a pressure, a hum below hearing, like the memory of a bell rather than the bell. Or maybe it was just her own blood in her ears, wanting so badly to belong that it invented the belonging.

The ninth stone leaned out of the marsh grass like a rotten tooth.

Even in the dark it was wrong. The other twelve stood square and pale against the sky, each one taller than a man and carved with the founder's knotwork, but this one had broken along a diagonal seam and tilted fifteen degrees eastward, into the valley, as if it were bowing—or falling. And the crack itself, when Hazel dismounted and put her lantern close to it, was not weathering.

"Weathering crumbles," she said aloud, because saying it made it more real and less frightening, which was the trick she'd learned young. "Frost splits things outward. Look at this."

The fracture faces were smooth. Polished, almost, in a way stone never got naturally—glassy, dark, and at the center of the break, where the lantern light reached deepest into the wound, the rock was hollowed. Not eroded. *Excavated.* Someone had cored the stone out like an apple, patiently, leaving only a shell, and then the shell had finally given way under its own weight.

Tam had gone still beside her, in the way he went still when he was angriest. "How long does something like that take?"

"I don't know."

"I mean—" His jaw worked. "This isn't a season's work, Hazel. You don't hollow a boundary stone in secret overnight. You'd have to come back. Over and over. For *years*. Right here, forty feet inside the strongest ward-line in the valley, and nobody heard a thing."

"Nobody was listening," Hazel said quietly. "Six working ears, Tam. Maybe fewer. The stone went silent and the Guildmother said *decay* and we all wrote it down."

"That's not fair. There was no reason to think—"

"There was no one left to *think*." It came out sharper than she intended, and she saw it land, saw Tam's face close slightly, and hated herself for it. He wasn't the enemy. None of this was the enemy, and that was the problem—the enemy was time, and scarcity, and a story worn smooth by three hundred years of retelling until nobody could remember what it had originally been about.

She knelt at the base of the stone instead, and held the lantern low, and that was when she saw the prints.

The marsh ground near the boundary was soft black peat, the kind that held a hoofprint for a week. And there, threading through the tussock grass, crossing back and forth over the ward-line itself—the invisible line Hazel had been taught since infancy marked the absolute edge of the possible—were boot prints. Fresh. The edges still sharp, the peat not yet slumped back into them. A day old, maybe two.

They came from outside.

Hazel followed them with her eyes and then, heart hammering, with her feet, stepping carefully along the trail as it descended from the outer hills, crossed the dead line where the ninth stone had stood guard, and walked—in long even strides, unhurried, unafraid—inward. Into the valley. Past the shattered stone. Toward the tarn, toward the town, toward everything.

"Tam."

He came and stood over her, and she watched him read it the same way she had, watch his breath fog in the cold and stop moving altogether.

"They crossed," he said. "Somebody crossed the ward-line."

"The wards fell silent when the stone broke. Maybe the line's open here now. Maybe it's been open—"

"No." Tam crouched, touching the print's edge with one finger. "Look at the depth. That's a loaded rider, or a heavy man on foot. If the ward was even half-alive, crossing it would feel like walking into a river current. Nobody strolls through. These prints—" He looked up at her, and in the lantern light his face was very young and very frightened. "These prints point *in*, Hazel. Whoever made them wanted to be here."

The night wind came down off the ridge and moved the marsh grass, and somewhere behind them the broken ninth stone creaked against its own ruin, settling another degree toward the valley it had guarded for eighty years. Hazel stared at the trail of prints until her eyes watered, following them in her mind south and west, toward home.

Her grandmother had said *decay*. Had written it in the census, would enter it in the guild annals, would tell the valley again at midsummer that all was well. And Hazel understood, standing in the trampled peat at the edge of the only world she'd ever known, that her grandmother had lied—or worse, that she hadn't, that she truly believed a hollowed stone and a breached border were nothing but the honest wear of years. Either way, the story Hazel had been raised inside of—that the wards held, that the guild held them, that someone competent was minding the edge of the world—had cracked along a seam as clean and terrible as the stone's.

"Back by midnight," she said at last, mechanically. "I promised her."

"She'll ask what we found."

"Then we'll decide on the ride home whether we tell her the truth." Hazel swung up into the saddle, and her hands were steady, and her voice was steady, and underneath both, in the place where other people heard salt singing, something in her was ringing like a cracked bell. "Someone walked into our valley, Tam. Through the wall. And the first thing I thought wasn't *who*. It was *please don't let Grandmother have known*."

They rode west through the dark, two small figures on the ward-road, racing the moon home—and far behind them, at the broken stone, the peat held the prints pointing inward, patient as a signature, like the opening sentence of a letter the valley had waited three hundred years to receive.
