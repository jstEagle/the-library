## 4. The Ninth Stone

The horses did not want to go.

Hazel felt it in the way Bramble set her ears—flat back, then swiveling forward, then flat again—as if the mare were listening to something on the wind that Hazel's blood had never learned to hear. Tam's gray gelding was worse. He planted his feet at the treeline where the valley's fields gave way to the high moor, and no amount of heel or coaxing would convince him that the world beyond the last fence post was a place a sensible animal should enter.

"They know," Tam said quietly. He wasn't talking about the horses anymore.

"Stones don't rot in a night," Hazel said, though she had said the same thing to her grandmother two days ago and Ilsa Nakamura had looked at her with eyes gone old and careful, and said nothing at all. That silence had been louder than any argument. Guildmothers argued. Guildmothers dismissed. Guildmothers did not go quiet.

They led the horses past the fence line on foot, and the moment they crossed out of the ward-shadow—the faint pressure Hazel could feel against her skin like the memory of a held breath, the only gift her salt-deaf blood had ever given her—the gelding stopped fighting. Which was its own kind of answer. Whatever the animals feared lived *inside* the wards, not beyond them.

The ninth stone stood on a knoll of gorse and broken granite, three hours' ride from the guild hall, the youngest of the boundary markers and therefore the least loved. The elder stones had names and songs and generations of small devotions attached to them—wreaths at midwinter, a smear of tallow at sowing, children taught to press their palms to the warm flanks of them and feel the hum. The ninth had been raised only eighty years ago, after the eastern marshes shifted and the old line ran through open water. It was a utility stone. A workmanlike thing. Nobody sang to it.

It should still have been standing.

Hazel saw it from two hundred paces and made herself keep walking, because the alternative was to stop and let the sight finish arriving all at once. The stone had not fallen. Falling would have been weather, would have been time, would have been the ordinary decay her grandmother wanted so badly to blame. The stone had been *opened*. Its upper half lay in three pieces on the gorse like the shell of an egg cracked by something patient, and the fracture faces were not rough—they were smooth, planed, almost polished, as though the rock had parted along seams that had been cut into it long before anyone thought to look.

"It's hollow," Tam said. He had climbed the knoll ahead of her and now stood very still at the top, and Hazel heard him stop breathing for a moment.

She climbed up beside him and looked down into what remained.

The boundary stones of the Saltwright Guild were not solid. Every apprentice learned this in their first year, kneeling in the cutting yard with wet granite dust in their lungs: each stone held a core, a heart-chamber packed with layered salt and river-clay and a sliver of the founder's original blend, pressed in patterns that took a full day to set. The core was the song's body. The granite was only its throat. When a Saltwright laid hands on a living stone, they weren't touching the rock at all; they were touching the salt inside it, and the salt remembered every hand that had ever renewed it, going back three hundred years, an unbroken chain of palms and voices.

The ninth stone's core was gone.

Not crumbled. Not washed out by rain. Gone the way a tooth is gone from a socket that has healed over—cleanly, completely, with the edges grown smooth around the absence. Someone had opened the stone, taken the heart out of it, and closed it up again so carefully that no one walking past would ever have known. And then, decades later—Hazel could see it now, once she knew what she was looking at, the hairline seam where the granite had been fitted back together like a lid on a jar—someone had come back and split it open from within, bursting the shell the way a chick bursts an egg, leaving the fragments to fall where they fell.

"How long?" Tam asked. His voice had gone strange, flattened, the voice he used for numbers when the numbers were bad.

Hazel crouched at the rim of the hollowed core and touched the inner walls. They were dry. Bone-dry, in a season when everything on the moor held damp, when moss crept into every shaded crack within a week. Dry stone meant sheltered stone. Sheltered meant covered. Covered meant years.

"The core was taken first," she said. "Then the shell was mended. Then—" she gestured at the burst fragments, "—this. You don't mend a thing you mean to break. Whoever did this wanted the stone to keep *looking* alive while it was already dead."

Tam crouched beside her. He was a millwright's son with forearms like rope and a mind that wanted everything disassembled and laid out in order, and she watched him do it now, laying the pieces of the crime out in the air between them.

"So the stone dies silently. No one notices, because the outside looks whole. The census comes, the guildmother reads the line, and either she doesn't check the cores or—" He stopped.

"Or she checks and finds nothing wrong," Hazel said, "because there's nothing left to be wrong." Her own voice sounded far away to her. "A hollowed stone sings back whatever you expect to hear. There's no song in it to contradict you."

"That's not how decay works, Hazel."

"No," she agreed. "That's how *theft* works."

* * *

They worked the site the way they'd both been trained, because training is what holds when the ground opens under you. Tam paced out the scatter of fragments and found, wedged in the gorse forty feet downslope, a chunk of core-material the size of a fist: layered salt and clay, but wrong. The strata were right, the colors right, the density right—and then, at the center, a void. A bubble of emptiness where the founder's blend should have been densest. As if someone had removed a single pinch of salt from the heart of the heart and let the rest cure around the hole.

"One pinch," Tam said, holding it up to the grey light. "You take one pinch out of nine stones, over however many years, and—" He shook his head. "I can't do the arithmetic. I don't know what the arithmetic *is*."

Hazel did. Or most of it. She had spent her childhood failing to hear the salt-songs, which meant she had spent her childhood studying everything else about the craft twice as hard as the children who could simply *listen* and be done with it. She knew the theory of the wards the way a deaf musician knows harmony: on paper, in structure, in the bones. Each stone anchored a length of the ward-line. Each core fed the next. The songs didn't travel through the air between stones; they traveled through the salt itself, grain to grain, a chain of tiny resonances running the whole perimeter of the valley, three days' walk end to end. Remove a pinch from a core and you didn't break the chain—you *detuned* it. Made one link sing slightly flat. And a slightly flat link, repeated year after year, census after census, renewal after renewal, pulled every note around it slowly out of true, the way a warped frame pulls a door.

The wards hadn't failed two nights ago when the stars went out. The wards had been failing for thirty years, maybe forty, so gradually that no one had thought to listen for it, and the night the sky went black was only the night the last thread finally slipped off the last hook.

And someone had planned it that way. Someone who understood the craft better than Hazel did—better, perhaps, than anyone in the guild had in a generation. Someone patient enough to work across decades, across multiple censuses, across the tenure of at least two guildmothers, and disciplined enough never to leave a stone visibly damaged until the final night.

"This wasn't sabotage," Hazel said, standing, her knees stiff with cold. "Sabotage is quick. This is *authorship*."

"Don't say things like that," Tam said, but he said it without conviction, because he was looking at the same evidence she was.

* * *

It was Tam who found the sigil. He always found things; it was his particular grace, the way other people could sing or ride or lie beautifully. He found things because he looked at the ground the way other people looked at faces.

He was circling the knoll's base, reading the sheep-cropped turf, when he called her name—not loudly, just once, the way you call someone when you've found a body. Hazel came down the slope fast and found him standing over a patch of earth sheltered under an overhang of granite, a place where rain had never reached and the moor grass grew pale and thin.

Pressed into the bare soil, deep and crisp as a wax seal, was a mark the size of a dinner plate.

A circle. Within it, a tree rendered in negative space—a tree burned away rather than drawn, its branches reaching to the circle's edge and stopping, truncated, amputated. Around the trunk, where roots should have spread, the design dissolved instead into fine radiating lines, like ash falling upward. Like smoke remembering it used to be wood.

"I know this," Hazel said, and hated herself for knowing it. "This is a warden's sigil."

"You're sure?"

"My grandmother keeps a book of foreign marks. Court seals, trade stamps, the heraldry of the kingdoms beyond the pass. She made me memorize them when I was twelve. 'Know the shapes of the wolves,' she said, 'even if you never hear them howl.'" Hazel traced the air above the imprint without touching it. "Ashen Court. Wardens' branch. The tree is the World-Ash, and the branches cut short means authority severed from origin—it's their mark for jurisdiction, for law imposed rather than inherited. And the ash—" she swallowed, "—the ash is literal. Wardens stamp their sigils in burnt material. It's a rite. They say it means truth outlasts the thing burned to carry it."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. On the moor above them the wind moved the gorse in slow silver waves, and somewhere a curlew cried its two-note cry, indifferent, eternal.

"A warden stood here," he said finally. "Inside the wards. At the ninth stone. Long enough to press a seal into the earth."

"Not long enough. Longer." Hazel turned in a slow circle, and now that she knew to look, the moor began to confess. A flat shelf of rock near the overhang, its lichen scuffed in a pattern too regular for sheep. A ring of small stones, fire-blackened, old enough that rain had rounded their edges—but arranged, still arranged, in the deliberate tripod of a camp stove. A niche in the granite face, sheltered, sized for a bedroll, its floor worn smooth. "Someone camped here. Repeatedly. Over seasons. Over *years*, Tam. This isn't a scout's bivouac. This is a post. An observation post inside our own border, maintained across decades, and we never—"

She stopped, because the next word was *we never noticed*, and the word after that was worse, and the word after that was worse than that. We never noticed. Because we couldn't hear. Because the guild had thinned to nine souls and the youngest stone was the least loved and the east march was checked once every three years by whoever drew the short straw. Because the valley had spent three hundred years telling itself it was safe, and safety, practiced long enough, becomes a kind of blindness you hand down to your children like an heirloom.

But there was a question underneath all of that, and it would not stay underneath, and finally she said it aloud because Tam was looking at her the way people look at you when they already know the answer and are waiting to see if you'll say it yourself.

"The boot prints," she said. "From the census. Two days ago. Prints pointing inward, fresh, past the dead ward-line." She looked down at the sigil in the earth. "I told my grandmother it might be a lost shepherd. She wanted it to be a lost shepherd. But this—" her hand hovered again over the ash-tree, "—this is not a lost shepherd's mark. This is a *claim*. You seal a claim. You sign your work."

"Meaning what? That the wardens broke the wards?"

"Meaning somebody did, and wanted the Ashen Court to know it was finished. Or—" and here her voice did something she didn't authorize, dropped low, went careful, "—meaning the Court itself has been working at this since before I was born. Before my grandmother was guildmother. Patiently. From the inside. While we sang our little songs and counted our nine members and believed the worst thing in the world was a bad harvest."

Tam sat down abruptly on the flat shelf of rock, as if his legs had resigned without consulting him. He put his face in his hands for a moment, then dragged them down and looked out across the valley—the whole valley, visible from the knoll, the market town small and brown and smoking gently with morning fires, the guild hall's slate roof catching the light, the terraced fields stitched down the hillsides like seams.

"All those stories," he said. "Every festival. Every winter-tale. The guild keeps us safe. The salt holds. Nothing gets in." He laughed, one syllable, no humor in it. "My mother used to say the wards were the reason she slept at night. She'd touch the lintel-stone over our door every evening, the way you'd touch a child's head."

"I know."

"Hazel." He looked up at her. "If this was done from inside—if someone walked these moors for thirty years, camping under this rock, taking pinches of salt out of our hearts one at a time—then either the guild never knew, which means the guild is blind. Or the guild knew, which means—"

"Don't."

"—which means the guild *allowed* it."

"I said don't."

They looked at each other, and the friendship between them—which had survived a broken arm apiece, one disastrous midsummer kiss neither ever mentioned, and twenty years of shared silences—held steady under the weight, the way it always did, but Hazel could feel it flexing, could feel the load settling onto beams never built for this.

Because here was the thing she could not yet say, the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone: her grandmother had gone quiet. Not dismissive—quiet. When Hazel reported the silent ninth stone, Ilsa Nakamura had not sent riders, had not convened the guild, had not done any of the ten urgent things a guildmother does when a boundary marker dies. She had sealed the hall and told everyone to stay indoors and waited, as if she were waiting for something she had been expecting.

As if the news had arrived not as a wound but as a confirmation.

* * *

They led the horses home slower than they'd come, because there was no reason left to hurry and every reason to dread arriving. The gelding crossed back inside the ward-line without hesitation, which told Hazel the animals' fear had been of the broken stone itself, or of whatever had broken it, and not of anything still lingering on the moor. Small mercies. She catalogued them grimly, the way you catalogue supplies in a siege.

Near the last fence post, where the valley's tame world resumed, Tam stopped and looked back at the knoll, a dark thumbprint on the horizon.

"The sigil," he said. "Stamped in ash. Facing which way?"

Hazel frowned. She turned it over in her memory, the image of it, the orientation of the mark in the soil. "Facing the valley. Facing inward."

"Wardens' law," Tam said. "You told me once—jurisdiction. Authority imposed. A seal stamped facing inward is a declaration." He met her eyes. "It's not a signature, Hazel. It's a summons. It's saying: *this territory is claimed, and the claimant is coming.*"

The wind came off the moor then, cold and smelling of wet stone and distance, and Hazel stood in it and felt—for the second time in her life, and the second time in one week—that pressure at the edge of her senses, the thing beyond the wards, except the wards were dead now and the thing was not beyond anything anymore. It was in the valley. Perhaps it had been in the valley for thirty years, wearing boots, lighting small fires under a granite overhang, working with a patience that made her grandmother's longest vigil look like a child's tantrum.

"We have to tell her," Tam said. "All of it. The hollowed core, the pinch-marks, the camp, the sigil. Everything."

"Yes," Hazel said. And then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and lying to Tam Voss had never once worked: "And then we have to watch her face when we tell it. Because I think—I think she may already know. I think she may have known for a long time."

Tam didn't argue. That frightened her more than the shattered stone had.

They rode down into the valley as the light went long and copper, past fields that didn't know yet, past doors with lintel-stones that didn't know yet, toward the guild hall where a lamp was already burning in her grandmother's study window—burning, Hazel realized as they drew close, in a room where Ilsa Nakamura sat alone with the guild's oldest ledger open before her, as if she had been waiting all day for two riders to come down off the moor and give her a reason to open it.

As they clattered into the yard, the study lamp went out.
