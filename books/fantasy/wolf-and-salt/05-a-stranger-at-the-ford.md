## 5. A Stranger at the Ford

Tam Voss had grown up believing the ward-line was the edge of the world.

That was the trouble with a valley sealed for three hundred years: children raised inside it learned the shape of the sky before they learned the shape of the truth. Her father had been a boundary-walker before the guild stopped paying walkers, and she remembered riding pillion behind him along the stones as a girl, his hand pointing to the pale shimmer only Saltwrights could see and she never could. *Past that line, the world goes wrong,* he'd say. *Past it, the stars lie.* She had believed him the way you believe water is wet—without argument, without examination, because belief was cheaper than doubt and the valley had never once given her reason to spend any.

Now the ninth stone lay shattered on the moor behind her, hollowed out like a gull's egg sucked dry, and the ward-line through the ford at Marrow Water was not a wall but an open mouth.

She crouched in the alders above the crossing with Hazel beside her, both of them damp to the knees from the long creep down the streambank, and watched a rider pick her way across the dead water in the last grey light.

"One horse," Tam breathed. "One horse, one rider, sitting like a sack of turnips."

"Like what?"

"Sack of turnips. Slumped. Hurt." Tam had spent six years running her uncle's freight yard in Millbrook before he lost the lease, and she could read a rider at four hundred yards the way Ilsa Nakamura read ledgers. "Left side. Watch how she keeps her left arm clamped. And look at the horse—it keeps turning its head back at her like it expects her to fall off."

Hazel said nothing. In the three days since the stars had gone out, Tam had learned to read Hazel's silences too: this one meant *I'm afraid and I'm going anyway*, which was, Tam supposed, the whole character of her oldest friend distilled into a single held breath.

The rider reached the near bank. The horse—a rangy bay with mud to the hocks and the flat-eared misery of an animal that had been pushed too hard for too long—came up out of the shallows and stood blowing, steam rising off it into the cold. The rider swayed in the saddle. Then, deliberately, she slid down on the right side, caught the horn, and stood very still for a moment with her forehead nearly touching the saddle, breathing through something.

Tam drew her knife.

"You're sure about this," Hazel whispered. It wasn't really a question; they had argued about it all the way down from the ninth stone, or rather Hazel had argued and Tam had refused to be talked out of what she wanted to do, which was sit on the bank with a crossbow and watch whoever came through the wound in the world's skin.

"She's coming to us," Tam whispered back. "Not to the guild hall. To us. She crossed at the ford when there's a road bridge two miles south. She wanted to come in quiet, and quiet means she doesn't want the guild knowing yet, and if she doesn't want the guild knowing—" she turned the knife so the lamplight from town wouldn't catch it, "—then we want to know why first."

"And your plan is to ambush her alone in the dark with a kitchen knife."

"Fishing knife."

"Tam."

"Hazel." Tam kept her eyes on the rider. "Someone hollowed out that stone over years. Someone stamped ash into the break in a sigil neither of us knows. The stars went out like pinched candles. My mother is in Millbrook with the shutters nailed and my little brother thinks the dark is a weather. I am done waiting for grown-ups to explain things to me. If this woman is the explanation, I'm taking her."

The rider got one foot in the stirrup, then thought better of mounting, and instead took the reins and began to lead the horse up the bank toward the alder path—the path, Tam noted grimly, that ran within ten feet of their hiding place. Of course. The only path. She might have planned the ambush around a more cooperative stranger.

The woman came up through the willow-herb and bramble, leading the bay, moving carefully, and as she passed beneath the alders Tam saw her properly for the first time: tall, broad-shouldered, maybe forty, with close-cropped greying hair and skin the deep brown of old walnut. She wore no livery. She wore a long coat of good grey wool gone filthy with road-dust and worse, and beneath it, where the left arm clamped tight against her ribs, the fabric was stiff and black with dried blood. A sword hung at her hip in a plain scabbard, tied down—which meant she knew how to fight and had thought about whether she wanted to. On the breast of the coat, half-hidden by the grime, was a badge: a stylized flame rendered in pewter and enamel, grey and white and a red so dark it was nearly black.

Ashen Court. Tam had seen the mark once, years ago, on a smuggled wine bottle's seal, and her uncle had thrown the bottle in the river and made her swear she'd never speak of it.

Hazel made a small sound beside her—not fear exactly. Recognition. Tam glanced over and saw her friend staring at the badge with an expression she couldn't name, and filed it away, because there was no time to ask.

The warden stopped. Ten feet below them, head tilted, listening.

"The alders," the woman said. Her voice was low and roughened, an accent Tam didn't know—something clipped and foreign, syllables bitten short. "You've been breathing like a startled rabbit for five minutes. Come out or don't, but decide, because I haven't the strength to pretend I don't hear you."

* * *

Later, Tam would replay the next thirty seconds many times and find something different shameful in them each time. What she remembered most clearly was the sound the woman made when Tam's knife came to rest against the side of her throat—not a cry, barely a flinch, just a long exhale, like someone setting down a weight carried too far.

"Sit," Tam said, because her voice needed something to do while her hands shook. "On the ground. Slow. Left hand where I can see it."

The warden sat. It cost her visibly; her breath went ragged halfway down and she finished the motion by falling the last foot rather than lowering herself. The horse stood docile behind her, ears swiveling.

"The right hand," Tam snapped, when the woman's other hand drifted toward the sword-ties.

"It's already untied," the woman said. "If I wanted it, I'd have taken it before you found your courage." But she lifted the hand away and set both palms flat on the trampled earth, and looked up, and Tam got her first clear look at her face in the dark: tired past tiredness, cheek scored with a shallow cut crusted over, eyes that were doing arithmetic. "You're not guild. No salt-grey. You're the miller's girl. Voss. Tamara Voss."

Cold walked up Tam's spine. "How do you know my name?"

"I've been in your valley eleven days." The woman's gaze moved past Tam's shoulder, to Hazel standing in the shadow of the alders with her fists clenched. "And you must be Nakamura. The granddaughter. Hazel." Something flickered across the warden's face—not triumph, nothing so simple. More like the expression of a person who has finally located a door she has been searching a dark house for, and found it much smaller than she feared. "Hazel Nakamura. Last apprentice of the Saltwright Guild."

"Don't say my name like you know me."

"I've read your name in archives older than your valley's charter." The warden shifted, winced, and pressed her bound arm tighter. "My name is Serah Oduya. I hold rank of Warden-Second in the Ashen Court. And I am going to faint in approximately twenty seconds unless one of you lets me bind this arm, so I would suggest deciding quickly whether you want answers from a woman conscious or unconscious, because I give roughly the same quality of answer either way, but you'll have to wait longer for the second."

It was such an absurd thing to say, so bluntly practical in the middle of all that darkness, that Hazel laughed—one short, startled bark—and the sound seemed to break something open in the standoff. Tam didn't lower the knife. But she did jerk her chin toward the stream. "Water's there. Bandages are—Hazel, do you have the kit?"

Hazel did have the kit, because Hazel was the sort of person who carried a kit, and she knelt by the warden in the dark and cut the filthy sleeve back with the small scissors from her satchel, and Tam watched her oldest friend's hands go still over the wound.

"What is it?" Tam asked.

"It's burned," Hazel said slowly. "Not cut. Burned. A cauterization—but the burn is—" She looked up, and even in the dark Tam could see her face change. "The edges are silvered. Like frost-burn, but it's not cold. Serah—Warden Oduya—what did this?"

"A colleague," Oduya said, between her teeth, as Hazel cleaned it. "A former colleague now. We disagreed about whether I should continue south." She paused. "There were three of us sent into the valley ahead of the main company. I am the only one who chose to keep walking toward the guild hall instead of away from it."

"Main company," Tam repeated. The word landed in her stomach like a dropped anchor. "What main company?"

Oduya looked at her, and then at Hazel, and Tam watched her make some decision—the kind of decision, Tam thought later, that people make when they have run out of better options and are gambling on strangers.

"The Ashen Court is coming to your valley," she said. "Two hundred wardens. Fire-pikes. Breaching ordnance. They will arrive within the fortnight, and they are coming for a door."

* * *

They did not take her to the guild hall. That was Tam's insistence, and Oduya, to her surprise, agreed to it without argument—agreed almost gratefully. "Your Guildmother cannot know I'm here yet," she said. "Not until I know what she intends. Not until I've seen the ninth stone myself." Which told Tam the woman already knew about the ninth stone, which was its own small horror, but by then they were committed: they hid the bay horse in the ruined drover's barn above Millbrook, and brought Serah Oduya limping into the loft of Tam's own family's haybarn, where her mother's old apothecary supplies still gathered dust in a cedar chest, and where nobody would think to look for a foreign soldier.

Hazel boiled water. Tam sat on an overturned feed barrel with her knife across her knees and her questions loaded like bolts.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "Not the beginning you want. The beginning where the Court decides to send soldiers into a valley that hasn't let anyone in for three hundred years. Because here's what I can't get past, Warden: your badge was on a wine bottle in my uncle's cellar nine years ago. Smuggled goods. Your people have known how to get in and out. So the wards weren't perfect, and the Court wasn't ignorant, and everyone outside our walls has apparently been talking about us for generations while we sang songs about being safe forever. So talk."

Oduya, propped against the hay with her arm freshly bound and a cup of willow-bark tea untouched in her good hand, regarded Tam for a long moment.

"You're angry," she said.

"I'm asking questions."

"You're angry, and it's making you precise. That's rarer than you'd think." She drank the tea after all, grimacing at it. "Fine. The beginning. The Court's archives hold a record called the Grey Annexation—a survey conducted ninety-one years ago, after a border incident your guild probably never heard of, in which a Court patrol crossed your ward-line by accident during a fog and came back wrong. Two men. One died within the season. One lived, and gave testimony before dying, and his testimony is the reason everything that follows happened."

"Wrong how?" Hazel asked quietly.

"He said the valley had no stars." Oduya's voice was level, but she had gone careful with it, the way people go careful stepping onto ice. "He said the wards didn't keep magic out. He said they kept a *view* out—that from inside, your people see a sky, but it's a painted sky, a maintained fiction, and the real stars over this valley were hidden on purpose, because of what the founders buried here. The Court didn't believe him. Courts rarely believe madmen. But they filed the testimony, and thirty years later a Second-Rank archivist pulled it, and cross-referenced it against fragments of pre-Exodus records describing 'a door shut from the inside,' and wrote a memorandum, and the memorandum was suppressed, and resurfaced, and suppressed again, for sixty years. Until four years ago, when the current Lord Ascendant read it. And believed it."

Tam's mouth had gone dry. "Believed what? Say it plainly."

"That your valley holds a door," Oduya said, "that must never open. That your guild was founded not as refugees seeking sanctuary, but as jailers sealing themselves in with their prisoner. And that the door is failing." She looked directly at Hazel as she said it, and Tam saw her friend go white in the lamplight. "The stars going out three nights ago confirmed it. The wards aren't decaying, Nakamura. They're being *eaten*. From underneath. And the Court's doctrine says that when a containment of that magnitude fails, the Court has one duty: secure whatever is contained, and turn it to the protection of the realm."

"Turn it—" Hazel's voice cracked. "Whatever's *contained*. You mean the thing behind the door. You mean your Court wants to *use* it."

"I mean," Oduya said, "that the Lord Ascendant believes a weapon sleeps under your mountain, and that he is sending two hundred people to wake it up and put a leash on it, and that every archive I have ever read says that leash will hold for exactly as long as it takes the thing to decide otherwise." She set down the cup. Her hand, Tam noticed, was not entirely steady. "I have served the Court nineteen years. I have burned holdings, broken rings, put down hedge-magic and blood-cults and one war. I am not a gentle instrument, and I'm not asking you to believe I'm kind. I'm asking you to believe this: I have read the same archives your Guildmother has. I know what the founders buried. And I came here alone, against orders, bleeding, to tell the last Saltwrights that the fire is coming—because there may be one person alive who can stop what's under that mountain, and it isn't a warden, and it isn't a lord, and it isn't me."

Silence filled the loft. Somewhere below, a beam ticked as the night cooled. Tam became aware that she had stopped holding the knife pointed at anything.

"Why you?" Hazel asked at last. Her voice was thin. "Why does it have to be a Saltwright?"

"Because the lock is keyed to blood." Oduya said it simply, terribly, the way you'd report the weather. "The founder's covenant runs in guild lines. There are nine of you left. By my count, fewer every year. And the archives suggest—I want to be honest, they only *suggest*—that one line carries the full key, and that line is the founding line itself." She paused. "Nakamura."

Tam looked at Hazel. Hazel was looking at her own hands as though she'd never seen them before, as though they belonged to someone else, someone with a birthright she'd been told all her life she was unfit to inherit because she couldn't hear the salt-songs.

"No," Hazel said.

"Hazel—"

"No. I can't hear the songs. I've never heard them. Ask anyone, ask the Guildmother, I failed my testing twice, I'm the wrong heir, the *last* heir, the heir of a family running out of heirs—" She was standing now, backing toward the ladder, and Tam had never seen her like this, unspooling. "You've got the wrong valley. You've got the wrong girl. Whatever your archives say—"

The loft door banged below them.

All three of them froze. Hoofbeats in the yard—two horses, moving fast, and a voice Tam knew shouting her name with an urgency that stripped twenty years off it.

"That's Corren," Tam breathed. "That's—Corren Ashe. He left the guild before I was born, he—"

"Corren Ashe," Oduya said softly, and her whole body changed, pain forgotten, hand going to her sword despite the bound arm, "is listed in the Court's files as the most dangerous man in this valley. Get down."

Below, the barn door crashed open, and torchlight climbed the ladder rungs toward them.
