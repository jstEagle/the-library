## 6. Guildmother's Ledger

The guild hall had never felt like a place where secrets lived. It smelled of tallow and drying kelp, of the brine vats in the cellar where Hazel had spent her childhood hauling salt-crusted baskets, and of old paper—harmless paper, she'd always assumed, the kind that recorded grain levies and stone inspections. Now, with the stars gone and the wards fallen and the town below them howling its fear through the shutters, every shadowed corner of the building seemed to lean toward her, full of things it had been waiting three hundred years to say.

Ilsa had not slept since the sky went dark. Hazel knew because she'd watched her grandmother's lamp burn past midnight through the gap beneath the study door, a thin gold line on the floorboards that finally guttered sometime before dawn. She heard the old woman's slow tread up the back stair to her chamber, the pause at the landing—the same pause Ilsa made every night for as long as Hazel could remember, though until two nights ago she'd never wondered why—and then silence.

Hazel waited another hour anyway. She counted her own heartbeat the way she'd once been taught to count salt-grains for assay: patiently, without flinching, one by one by one. Then she rose, took the candle she'd hidden under her cot, and went down into the guildmother's study.

The lock on the study door was ceremonial. Every apprentice learned that much in their first year: that the Saltwrights kept nothing worth stealing, because everything they had belonged to the valley and the valley belonged to everyone. The iron latch lifted at a touch. What had stopped generations of curious children was not the lock but the promise—Ilsa's voice, gentle and absolute, *the records are dull, love; you'll have your whole life to be bored by them*—and the shame of doubting it.

Hazel doubted now. The boot prints pointing inward had seen to that. Serah Oduya, sitting bound and feverish in the guest room upstairs, muttering about doors, had finished the job.

Inside, the study was exactly as it had always been: the long desk scarred by knife-sharpening, the wall of pigeonholes stuffed with ledgers, the brass scale for weighing salt tribute, the faded map of the valley that covered an entire wall, stitched onto linen so old it had gone the color of weak tea. Hazel stood in the doorway a moment, feeling like a thief in a house that had raised her, and told herself—firmly, in Ilsa's own cadence—that she would only look. Only look. Reading was not betrayal. Reading was what a last apprentice owed a dying guild.

She started with the recent ledgers because starting anywhere else felt too much like deciding to find something. Census rolls. Ward-stone maintenance logs going back forty years, in her grandmother's cramped, beautiful hand. Tribute schedules. The names of the dead, recorded with dates and, in the older volumes, with small marginal notes: *loved plum wine*, *sang flat*, *afraid of thunder*. Three centuries of ordinary people keeping ordinary accounts. Her throat tightened. This was real. This was the guild she knew.

But the pigeonholes were not uniform. Behind the row of census books, set deeper than the others, there was a gap in the wood—a hole where something had been removed, or where something had been hidden behind false backs. She found the trick on her third pass: a shelf that pivoted when pressed at the lower left corner, stiff with disuse, groaning like something waking.

Behind it lay a single ledger, bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth, unmarked except for a stamp pressed into the cover in wax that had aged to the color of dried blood: nine interlocking circles around a central point. The founder's sigil. The sigil stamped in ash at the shattered ninth stone.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake, and instead they moved with the strange calm of someone walking a ward-line at night, when the only rule is to keep moving.

She opened the book.

The first pages were the charter she'd been taught—the one recited at every triennial census, the one she could recite in her sleep. *In the year of the Long Frost, we who were persecuted for our craft did withdraw into this vale, and by our art sealed it against the sorceries of the outer world, that our children might live free of terror.* She mouthed the familiar words along with the ink, comforted despite herself, and then turned the page.

And the handwriting changed.

Not the words at first—the words stayed pious, stayed the official history—but the script shifted from the careful public hand to something faster, rawer, written late at night by someone whose fingers ached. Marginalia crept in. A second voice, annotating the first. Then a third. Generations of guildmothers, Hazel realized, each one adding to the book, correcting it, confessing into it. Not a charter. A ledger in the oldest sense: a record of debts.

The first correction came on the fifth leaf, in ink so brown it had almost become part of the paper:

*We did not flee. Let no child believe we fled. We ran because we were carrying something, and the men who owned it were close enough to hear our breathing.*

Hazel sat down. She didn't remember deciding to; her legs simply folded her into Ilsa's chair, the candle wobbling, the grey ledger open across her knees like something alive.

She read.

The founders had not been peaceful salt-crafters hounded by superstitious kings. They had been wardens themselves—wardens of a different order, in service of a court far grander and crueller than any Ashen Court Serah had described. And they had been entrusted, the entry said, with the keeping of a prisoner. Or rather—with the prisoner's *door*. The text was maddeningly oblique about what lay behind it; the founders wrote around it the way one writes around a wound, in circles, never touching the center. But they wrote plainly about the theft.

*It was not ours to move,* wrote the first hand, the founding hand. *We told ourselves we took it to protect the world from those who would use it. Perhaps that is even true. But we also took it because it was power, and power is the one burden men will cross oceans to carry. We carried it here, to the deep vale between the mountains, and we built our seal not around it but around ourselves. Understand this, whoever reads after me: the wards were never meant to keep the world out. The wards were meant to keep us in. We locked the door from the inside because we could not trust ourselves with the key.*

Hazel read the passage three times. The candle burned down a thumb's width. Somewhere above her, the house ticked and settled, and the wind coming off the dead wards made the shutters whisper like a congregation murmuring before a verdict.

*Keep us in.* She thought of the boundary stones she had tended all her life, walking the circuit with her grandmother, pressing her palm to the warm granite while other children played in the market square. She had believed—had been taught to believe, had loved believing—that the stones were armor. A shield held up by nine generations of quiet, dutiful women against a hostile and magical world. That the valley's isolation was a sacrifice the guild made daily on behalf of everyone inside it.

The stones weren't armor. They were bars. And she had polished them.

She kept reading because stopping would have meant thinking, and thinking, just then, was a country she couldn't afford to enter.

The middle leaves charted the lie's growth with terrible patience. Each generation's guildmother recording, in her turn, the moment she chose to continue it. *My daughter asked why we cannot trade beyond the ford. I told her plague.* — *The boy Corren asked what is under the mountain. I told him foundations.* — *I have decided the truth serves no one living. Let it die with me.* One entry, dated a century back, in a hand that trembled: *I looked upon the seal tonight. I am old and it is time, and still I could not bring myself to open the inner archive. Forgive me, whoever comes after. I was a coward, and I gave you my cowardice for an inheritance.*

Hazel's eyes stung. She wiped them with the heel of her hand and hated herself for crying—for whom? For the liars? For herself?

Then she reached the entries in Ilsa's hand, and stopped crying altogether.

Her grandmother's script was unmistakable: the sharp ascenders, the little hooks she put on her g's. There were decades of it. Maintenance notes, yes—but interleaved with them, in the same book, in the same ink, sentences like these:

*Marta asked again today whether the songs are prayers. I said yes. God forgive me, I am getting good at this.*

*The stone-songs grow fainter. Fewer children born to hear them. I do not know if I am guarding a prison anymore or merely presiding over an empty room.*

*I went down the stair as far as the seventh landing. I have not done that since my mother died. I stood with my hand on the rock and listened. I tell myself I imagined the breathing. I will not go again.*

*I will not go again.*

*I went again.*

The last entry in Ilsa's hand was recent—the ink barely faded, the date from the spring just past:

*The ninth stone has gone silent and I have told the census it is decay. It is not decay. Decay does not leave tool-marks. Someone has been hollowing my grandmother's work for years, patient as water, and I do not know who, and I cannot ask for help without telling the guild what we are. So I will stand alone on the wall of a house I built on a lie, and I will hold it, and if it falls, let Hazel hate me. Better hatred than inheritance.*

*Let Hazel hate me.*

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Hazel sat in the dark study holding her grandmother's confessions and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water filling her lungs, that Ilsa had known—known about the hollowing, known about the danger, known the truth of everything—and had chosen to face it alone rather than share the burden. Had chosen to let Hazel go on singing her half-learned songs and tending her stones and being the wrong kind of heir, blind and beloved and useless, right up until the sky went out.

Had she protected Hazel, or had she simply never trusted her?

Both, whispered something in the back of Hazel's mind, in a voice that sounded unpleasantly like her own. Both at once. That's what love looks like when it grows inside a cage. It learns the shape of the bars and calls them ribs.

The candle guttered. She turned the final leaves. After Ilsa's entries there was a section at the back she hadn't noticed, pages sewn in later, thicker stock, and on them—in a formal, copied-out hand, as if transcribed from something older—a diagram.

It was the valley. The whole valley, drawn from above: the river, the town, the ring of boundary stones, and beneath the mountain at the valley's heart, rendered in cross-section, a descending stair. Nine landings. At the bottom, a chamber, and in the chamber a shape that the draftsman had drawn not as a door but as a seam—a jagged line through the rock itself, stitched closed with interlocking threads that the legend identified, in a dead dialect Hazel could only half-read, as *salt-light*.

Beneath the diagram, a single line, underlined twice:

*What sleeps behind the seam must never be woken, and must never be named. The songs are its muzzle. The guild is its keeper. The keeper's blood is its lock.*

Blood is its lock. Hazel stared at the words until they swam. The salt-songs—the ones she couldn't hear, the failure that had defined her entire life, the quiet grief she carried at every census when the stones sang for everyone else and went dumb for her. If the songs were keyed to bloodlines, and the bloodlines were nearly spent—

A floorboard creaked in the hall.

Hazel's head came up. For a moment she told herself it was the house settling, the wind, Tam prowling the corridors as he'd prowled them since they were children. Then the creak came again, closer, unhurried, with the particular weight of someone who had walked this hallway for sixty years and needed no light to do it.

She had perhaps ten seconds. Enough time to close the ledger and return it behind the false shelf and be standing innocently by the map when the door opened. Ten seconds, and her body refused every one of them. Her hands stayed where they were. Her thumb stayed pinned on the underlined line. Some part of her—some part that had spent twenty-two years pressing her ear to stones and hearing nothing—understood that hiding was over, that the age of pretending had ended when the stars did, and that whatever came through that door next would find her exactly as she was: seated in her grandmother's chair, holding her grandmother's sins.

The handle turned.

Ilsa filled the doorway with the candle in her hand, and for a long moment neither of them moved. Her grandmother was dressed, not undressed—she had come downstairs on purpose, or had never truly gone up—and her eyes went from Hazel's face to the grey ledger in her lap with an expression Hazel had never seen on her before. Not anger. Not even shock. Something smaller and more terrible: the look of a woman watching a dam fail, knowing she built it, knowing she built it badly, knowing all along that someday the water would win.

"You pivot shelf," Ilsa said quietly. "Lower left corner." Her voice was steady, which was somehow worse than shaking. "Your great-grandmother showed me the same way, when I was your age. I swore I'd never show anyone. I swore I'd spare you."

"Spare me," Hazel repeated. The word tasted like copper. She looked down at the ledger, at the trembling century-old confession of a coward guildmother, at Ilsa's own neat, damning lines. *I will not go again. I went again.* When she spoke again, her voice came out low and strange, almost calm, the way the valley had gone calm in the moments before the wards fell. "Three hundred years, Gran. Plague at the ford. Foundations under the mountain. Prayers instead of locks. You let me spend my whole life mourning a gift I never had, and it was never a gift at all. It was a key. And you knew."

Ilsa set her candle on the desk. The two flames stood together, hers and Hazel's, throwing their tangled shadows huge across the map of the valley—across the stitched linen mountains, the tiny inked town, the small dark shape beneath the peak that Hazel had looked at ten thousand times and never once seen.

"I knew," her grandmother said. "Sit down, Hazel. No—" she raised a hand as Hazel started to rise, the ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, "—you're sitting already. Good. Then listen, because the Court is coming, the man I loved best in this world besides you wants to blow the mountain apart, and you have just read enough to be dangerous and not enough to be safe. Ask me anything. Tonight I'll answer true."

It was everything Hazel had wanted. Everything she'd marched down here to seize. And standing there in the wreck of the family story, holding the proof of three centuries of lies, she found that the question tearing at her wasn't about doors or gods or stolen things at all.

"The ninth stone," she said. "You've known for months it wasn't decay." Her throat worked. "Who else have you told?"

"No one."

"Then I'm asking the wrong question." Hazel laid the open ledger on the desk, turned it to face her grandmother, and put her finger on the founder's underlined line—*the keeper's blood is its lock*—watching Ilsa's face as she did. "Because if blood is the lock, Gran, and the bloodlines are nearly gone, then answer me the one thing this book keeps dancing around."

Outside, far off across the dead fields, something howled—not wolf, not wind, nothing with a shape she could name—and both candles leaned as if bowing.

"What happens," Hazel said, "when the lock runs out of blood?"
