## 7. Confrontation in the Salt Cellar

The guild hall had never been quiet before. Hazel had grown up inside its sounds the way a fish grows up inside water—the creak of the drying racks in the east loft, the hiss of brine over coals in the rendering room, her grandmother's voice moving through the corridors like weather, always going somewhere, always carrying orders or comfort or both at once. Now the shutters were barred, the fires banked to embers, and every sound the building made seemed muffled, as if the whole structure were holding its breath along with the nine people left alive inside it.

Hazel couldn't hold hers.

She sat on the edge of her narrow bed in the apprentices' cell and listened to the silence press back against the walls, and beneath the silence—faint as a pulse felt through a bandage—she felt *it* again. The pressure. The thing at the edge of her senses that had arrived when the stars went out, vast and slow and patient, like an ocean deciding whether it wanted to be a wave. It didn't hurt. That was somehow worse. It *noticed* her.

Her hands wouldn't stop shaking. She looked at them in the light of the single candle she'd been allowed—one candle per person now, Ilsa had ordered, until they understood what they were rationing—and thought about boot prints pointing inward, about a hollowed stone, about Serah Oduya's face in the lantern-light at the ford, gray with blood loss and sincerity in equal measure.

*A door that must never open.*

The guild charter said the valley was a refuge. Hazel had read the charter a dozen times, copied it twice in careful apprentice script, recited its opening lines at three censuses running: *We came out of the burning lands seeking peace, and peace we made with salt and silence.* But charters could lie the way people did—smoothly, formally, with witnesses.

Ilsa's private ledger would not lie to *her*. Or if it did, it would at least be an honest kind of lying, the kind done by a woman who kept accounts of everything because she trusted nothing—not even herself—to stay true.

Hazel stood. The floorboards knew her weight and said nothing; she had learned to walk quietly here before she'd learned to read. Down the corridor, past the sealed common room where Tam was arguing with the town reeve through a barred window, past the chapel where old Master Fenwick prayed to saints who had never heard of the valley, down the stairs behind the pantry that smelled of tallow and cold iron.

The archive was not locked. It had never needed to be. For three hundred years, no one but a Guildmother had ever wanted what was inside it.

* * *

The cellar under the guild hall was older than the hall itself. Everyone knew that the way everyone knew the valley was a refuge—casually, without examining it. The walls were rough-hewn granite that predated the dressed stone above, and the shelves cut into them held ledgers going back further than any living memory: census records, ward-maintenance logs, tithe rolls, the great bound volumes of the salt-songs transcribed into notation so dense it looked like frost patterns on glass.

Hazel took down the most recent ledger first, then the one before it, then worked backward, fast, her fingers leaving damp prints on leather spines. She wasn't sure what she was looking for. A discrepancy. A gap. Something in the accounting that didn't balance.

She found it in the third volume back—a ledger from before her birth, in Ilsa's mother's hand. The ward-maintenance logs recorded, for each boundary stone, three figures: the strength of the song sung to it, the strength of the song returned, and something called only *the remainder*. The first two columns were filled with numbers in the familiar guild cipher. The third column was filled with a different cipher entirely, one Hazel had never been taught, and beside each entry, small and recurring across decades, a single word in plain script:

*Dormant.*

Dormant. As if the stones weren't just stones. As if the wards weren't just keeping something out—but keeping something *in*, and whatever it was slept, and the sleep was measured like grain, like debt, like anything else a careful woman might count.

Hazel turned pages faster. *Dormant, dormant, dormant.* Then, forty years back, in fresher ink: *Restless.* Then, in Ilsa's hand, younger and sharper than the writing Hazel knew: *Restless. Fed it the winter tithe early. Do not let the Council ask why we keep a tithe schedule at all.*

The candle guttered. Hazel realized she had stopped breathing somewhere around the word *tithe*, and started again, too loud in the stone room.

"You always did read too fast for your own good."

Ilsa stood at the foot of the stairs.

Her grandmother looked smaller than she had that morning. She had taken off the Guildmother's chain of office—the salt-white beads that had hung around Nakamura necks for six generations—and held it in one fist like a dead bird. Her hair was loose, which Hazel had seen perhaps four times in her life, all of them illnesses. But her eyes were the same eyes: level, dry, unyielding as a salt pan under sun.

"Grandmother," Hazel said. "I was just—"

"No." Ilsa came forward slowly, her boots ringing on stone worn concave by two centuries of descending feet. "Don't do that. Don't give me the apprentice apology. I've earned better than that, and so have you." She set the chain of office on the reading table with a click that sounded final as a lock turning. "You went looking. So we'll have it out. Here. Where your great-grandmother confronted me, and hers confronted her, all the way back to the first of us who decided the truth was too heavy to carry upstairs."

"The door," Hazel said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Serah Oduya says there's a door. Under the mountain."

"There is." No flinch, no deflection. Ilsa pulled out the reading chair and sat, folding her hands on the table like a woman settling in for a long night of accounts—which, Hazel thought wildly, was exactly what this was. "And you're going to sit down, and you're going to listen, because I get to tell this once and only once, and I have been rehearsing it for thirty years without ever believing I'd have the courage."

Hazel sat.

* * *

"We didn't flee magic," Ilsa said. "That's the first lie, and it's the foundation stone of everything built after. We didn't flee it. We *used* it, and what we used it on—" her jaw worked, "—what we used it on frightened the founders so badly that they chose to forget their own crime rather than live with it honestly. They built the wards inward as much as outward. They sealed themselves in with it. With *him*."

"Him?"

"They called him many things. Most of the names are in ciphers you've never been taught, because teaching you was the same as telling you, and telling you was forbidden." Ilsa's voice stayed flat, controlled, the voice she used for reading casualty lists. "But the oldest record—the founder's own hand, in the vault below this room—calls him the thing that eats the lights. The hungry dark. A god of winter that starved before your ancestors' ancestors were born, and began eating stars to fill the emptiness where its worship used to be."

The pressure at the edge of Hazel's senses shifted. Faintly. Almost like attention.

"And the wards," Hazel whispered.

"Are its muzzle. Its cage. Its—yes. Its prison. The boundary stones don't just mark the valley's edge, child. They are the bars. Every song a Saltwright has ever sung has gone into holding that door shut, and the door is not a door like yours or mine. It is a seam in the world, and the world has spent three hundred years trying to heal over it, and we have spent three hundred years not letting it, because the founders feared what healing might mean." She laughed, short and bitter. "Or perhaps they simply feared change. Fear makes excellent mortar. Ask any guild."

Hazel's mind was moving in circles, snagging on things. "The salt-songs. You told me—I failed the hearing trial, you covered for me, you said some ears come late—"

"I lied." Ilsa said it without looking away, and Hazel understood suddenly that this cost her grandmother more than the whole confession together. "I lied to the Council, and I lied to you, and I have lied at every census since you were seven years old. Not because you can't hear the songs, Hazel. Because you hear them *wrong*. Too well. Unfiltered. A trained Saltwright hears a ward-stone's song the way one hears a distant bell—shaped, softened, translated. You hear the bell *and the rope and the hand on the rope and the hunger of the thing pulling it*. Your gift isn't broken. It's unfenced. And an unfenced gift, near that door, near *him*—the founders burned children for less, in the early years. We got gentler. We didn't get wiser."

The room tilted. Hazel gripped the table's edge. All those years—the shame of the silent trial, standing among the stones while other apprentices nodded along to harmonies she couldn't find, the hot secret certainty that she was the wrong heir, the flawed one, the last of a line running out of heirs and running out of time—

"It was never a failure," she said.

"No. It was the truest thing about you. Which is why I hid it, and why hiding it may be the sin that undoes us all." Ilsa reached across the table and took Hazel's hands. Her grip was dry and strong and trembling, just slightly, and Hazel realized with a lurch that her grandmother was afraid—had maybe always been afraid, every day of her life, and had carried it alone because the ledger said *do not let the Council ask*. "Your blood is keyed to the lock. Deeper than mine. The songs run in the Nakamura line, but in you they run *raw*. If the door must be renewed—if the wards must be rebuilt—you are not the least of us, Hazel. You may be the only one left who can do it at all."

"That's why you covered for me. All these years. Not kindness." The words came out crueler than Hazel intended, and she didn't take them back. "You were preserving a tool."

"Yes." No flinching. Ilsa had never flinched in her life; it was one of the things Hazel had loved about her, and it was unbearable now. "Call it what it is. I loved you and I kept you, and I cannot tell you anymore where one ends and the other begins, and I am too old to pretend the answer doesn't matter." Her thumb moved over Hazel's knuckles, once, an old gesture from fevers and nightmares, and Hazel hated how much comfort she took from it. "But hear the rest before you hate me. The Court is coming. Oduya was honest about that much—the Ashen Vanguard rides behind her, and their captain does not want to seal that door, whatever she believes. They want to *open* it. To bind what's behind it and march it west like a siege engine. And Corren Ashe wants to collapse the mountain on top of it, which would crack the seam wide rather than close it—he doesn't know enough to understand what he'd unleash, and he hates this family too much to listen."

"Then what do you want?" Hazel heard her own voice go thin and strange. "Truly. Not the guild's want. Yours."

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. In the pause, the pressure at the edge of Hazel's senses swelled and ebbed, swelled and ebbed, patient as tide.

"I want what I have always wanted," her grandmother said finally. "For you to sing the wards whole again, and for me to die before I have to explain to your children why their mother never sleeps soundly. I want the valley safe. I want the lie to keep working, because it has worked for three hundred years, and because I am a coward in the specific shape of my love." She leaned forward, and her eyes were wet, and she did not wipe them. "Help me renew the wards. Before the Court arrives. Before Corren reaches the stair. Give me that, and I will spend my last years unpicking every lie I've told you, thread by thread, with witnesses. I swear it on the founder's grave. On your mother's name. On anything you ask."

"And if I say no? If I want the truth *first*—all of it, the founder's records, the vault, everything—before I sing one note?"

Something moved behind Ilsa's eyes. Grief, Hazel thought, and beneath the grief, a flicker of something that looked almost like pride, quickly smothered.

"Then you will be a better Guildmother than I ever was," Ilsa said softly, "and I will deserve whatever comes of it."

Hazel pulled her hands free. She stood, because sitting felt like being held, and she could not bear to be held just now. The candle flame bent toward the stairwell, toward the surface, toward the starless sky, as though even fire wanted out.

Above them, muffled through three floors of stone, a horn sounded from the direction of the market town—two long notes and a third cut short. The alarm signal. Not the Court's horns. Not yet.

Tam's whistle. Their childhood signal, the one that meant *come quick, I've found something, bring your boots.*

Hazel looked down at her grandmother—at the chain of office lying on the table between them like a border neither dared cross—and felt the truth settle over her, heavy and cold and clarifying as deep water: that love could build cages, that the finest ones were built from sacrifice and called them safety, and that she had lived her whole life inside one and called it home.

"Grandmother," she said, already moving toward the stairs, "you should know—the wards aren't just failing."

Ilsa went very still. "What do you mean?"

"When I listen now—all of it, unfiltered—the songs aren't fading." Hazel took the steps two at a time, her heart hammering with fear and something dangerously close to exhilaration. "They're being *answered*."
