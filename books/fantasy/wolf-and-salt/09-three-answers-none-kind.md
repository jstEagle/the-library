## 9. Three Answers, None Kind

The guild hall had never held this many people. Tam Voss stood at the back of the great room with his shoulder against the doorframe, the way he'd stood at a hundred town meetings before, and understood that nothing about tonight was like any meeting he'd ever sat through. The benches were full. The aisles were full. Farmers who hadn't come down from the high terraces in years stood pressed against the walls with their hats crushed in their fists, and the lamplight caught faces he'd known all his life and made them strange—drawn tight, hungry, angled upward toward the dais where Guildmother Ilsa Nakamura stood beneath the old salt-branch standard.

Above them all, where the ceiling should have shown the painted constellations his grandfather's generation had brushed there—the Shepherd, the Saltwife, the Long Wolf—there was only darkness. The stars had been gone four days now. The painters of that ceiling had worked from memory of a sky none of them could see anymore, and Tam kept finding his eyes dragged up to it, to the blankness, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

Outside, beyond the shuttered windows, something moved in the dark. He didn't look. Nobody looked anymore. Looking was how you saw it.

"Three days ago," Ilsa said, and her voice carried the way it always carried—not loud, but dense, like stone dropped in deep water, "the wards of this valley fell. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. I will not tell you the sky will mend itself by morning, because it will not."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Tam found Hazel near the front, standing slightly apart from her grandmother, arms crossed tight over her apron. She hadn't spoken since they'd come down off the mountain. She kept looking at the floor as if the answers were written there.

"I called you here because you deserve truth," Ilsa continued. "For three hundred years this valley has been sealed. Not sheltered—sealed. The wards were not protection against some vague outside danger. They were built around something specific. Something our founders buried beneath Mount Ceren, behind the deepest stair in the guild hall, behind a lock that is failing even now." She paused, and her hands, folded on the rail before her, were steady. Tam had always admired that steadiness. Tonight it looked less like courage and more like practice. "There is a door under the mountain. It is holding. But what stands behind it is waking, and we are nine guild members against whatever comes when it opens. So I am asking you: stand with us. Help us renew the wards. Help us hold."

Silence. Then a voice from the middle of the room—old Marta Kell, whose family had farmed the river bend for six generations: "Holding *what*, Guildmother? Say its name."

Ilsa's pause lasted half a heartbeat too long.

"We do not speak its name," she said. "Speaking names gives power."

"That's not an answer," Marta said. "That's another wall."

* * *

Corren Ashe came forward from the shadow by the eastern pillar, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts around a keel—uneasy, unwilling, but parting all the same. Twenty years of exile had carved him lean and gray-stubbled, and he wore no guild colors, only travel-worn leather and the ash-dusted boots of a man who had walked a long way to say something. His company of exiles hung back near the doors, watching everything, touching nothing.

"You want truth?" Corren said. "Here is truth. That woman—" he pointed at Ilsa without looking at her, and Tam saw the guildmother flinch at being made an object rather than an opponent, "—has known since before any of you were born that the wards were dying. She knew when my father served. She knew when I served. And every time the census came round, she wrote the numbers in a ledger and locked it away and told the valley all was well."

"The numbers were guild business," Ilsa said.

"The numbers were *people*," Corren snapped. "Nine left. Nine! When I took my oath there were forty. My father sang the boundary stones whole, and yours—" he swept his arm at the room, at all of them, "—yours will be the generation that learns what happens when the last song stops. Because it stops soon. Unless we end it."

He let that hang. Then, quieter, in the voice of a man reciting something he had repeated to himself ten thousand times on ten thousand roads:

"Burn it. Burn the door. Burn the stair, collapse the mountain, salt the ground so deep that nothing under it can ever climb out. Yes, the valley may die with it. Yes, some of us may die with it. But I would rather be buried under honest stone than live one more year inside a lie wearing a crown of salt." His eyes found Ilsa's and held them. "You taught me that, once. Before you chose the name over the truth."

"Corren," Ilsa said, and for just a moment the guildmother fell away and something older and wearier looked out of her face, something that might once have loved him or failed him or both, "you don't know what you're asking."

"I know exactly what I'm asking." His jaw tightened. "I've been asking it for twenty years."

From the benches, someone shouted: "If we burn it, what comes out first?" And Corren had no answer ready fast enough, and the crowd felt the gap, and the murmur turned ugly at the edges.

* * *

Serah Oduya rose last. She did not come to the dais; she stayed where she was, in the open center of the room, so that everyone could see her—the outsider, the Ashen grey of her warden's cloak still dusty from the ford where Tam himself had held a knife to her throat five nights past. She looked smaller without her horse, without her wound dressed and hidden under the cloak, but her voice was level and carried better than either of theirs.

"The Court sent me alone, unarmed, across a dead ward-line, to deliver a warning your guild ignored for two hundred years," she said. "Our archives name what lies beneath this valley. Our law forbids its release upon pain of death. I offer you the only path that does not end in fire or famine: place yourselves under Court protection. Let the Court seal the door with rites older than your guild, garrison it with wardens sworn to the same law I serve, and feed your people while your fields recover from what the dark has done to them."

"And in exchange," old Marta Kell said slowly, "we give up the valley. Give up the guild. Three hundred years of keeping, handed to strangers with fire-pikes."

"In exchange," Serah said, "your children see the stars again someday. Under Court seal, the binding can be maintained by thousands instead of nine. That is arithmetic, not conquest."

"It's surrender," Corren said.

"It's survival," Serah answered. "You propose arson. Your guildmother proposes prayer. I propose a garrison. Choose."

And the room broke.

Tam had seen arguments before—harvest disputes, boundary quarrels, the annual shouting match over the mill rights. This was not argument. This was a dam going. Farmers were on their feet. Someone threw a tankard; it struck the dais rail and burst, ale and wood splinters scattering across the guildmother's boots. Voices overlapped into a single roar with no words in it, only fury, and Tam realized with a cold drop in his stomach that the fury wasn't aimed at the door under the mountain at all. It was aimed at the silence. Three hundred years of it. Every harvest blessing and ward-song and solemn guild ceremony they had trusted since childhood, revealed overnight to be the lid of a box—and now three strangers-in-place were telling them which new master to choose, and not one of them, not one, would say what was actually in the box.

"They knew!" A young farmer—Jory Pell, Tam thought, Jory who'd been in his class at the village school—was standing on a bench, pointing at the dais. "The guild *knew*. All this time. We tithed them. We bowed to them. We named our daughters after them. And they were sitting on a grave under our feet the whole time!"

"Hold your tongue, boy," barked Dunc Voss, Tam's uncle, but Dunc's voice shook, and nobody obeyed him, least of all Jory.

"What's under the mountain?" Jory shouted, turning, demanding it of the whole room, of the world. "What's *under the mountain*?"

"Tell us!" someone else took it up. "Say its name! What did you bury? WHAT DID YOU BURY?"

Tam pushed off the doorframe and started forward without deciding to. He caught sight of Hazel moving too, pushing through the crowd toward the dais, toward her grandmother, and he saw the moment Ilsa lifted her hand for quiet—a gesture that had stilled this room for thirty years—and watched it fail. Watched it fail utterly. The guildmother's hand hung in the air above a roaring room like a branch over floodwater, and then lowered, and Tam understood that something had ended in that lowering that no song could mend.

It was Hazel who got through. She climbed onto the dais beside her grandmother and did not ask for quiet. She simply stood there, small and furious and waiting, and somehow the sight of her—one girl, sixteen years old, refusing to move—punched a hole in the noise. The room guttered, flickered, began to listen.

"You want the truth?" Hazel's voice cracked on the second word and she pushed through it anyway. "Here's mine. I'm supposed to be the last Saltwright. The heir. And until four days ago I couldn't hear the songs. Couldn't hear the stones sing. Do you know what that's like—knowing your whole family's gift passed you over? Knowing you'd inherit a lie you couldn't even keep?" Her breath shuddered. "Well, now I know why the songs stopped making sense. Because they were never songs. They were a muzzle. We weren't keeping the valley safe. We were keeping *it* asleep. And I don't know what 'it' is, because—" her voice rose, broke again, rose higher, "—because *nobody will tell us!*"

She spun to face the dais elders, to face Ilsa, to face Corren, to face Serah, and Tam watched his oldest friend in the world tear open in front of the entire valley.

"Guard it," she said. "Burn it. Seal it. Those are your answers. Guard, burn, seal. Not ONE of you will say what's behind the door. Not one of you will say its NAME." She was crying now, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like a child, hating herself for it, doing it anyway. "You'd fight each other over the lock, and you won't even tell us what the lock is FOR!"

Silence, at last—but it was worse than the roaring had been. It was the silence of a room full of people who had heard the truth in her anger: that the elders had offered them three cages and called each one a choice.

Ilsa reached for her granddaughter's shoulder. Hazel stepped away from the touch. Tam saw the guildmother's hand close on empty air and stay there, suspended, and something in her face crumpled that he had never seen crumple before—not at the fallen wards, not at the empty sky, not at the shattered ninth stone. Just at that.

Then Corren spoke from the shadows, softly, almost gently, and the gentleness was the worst thing in the room.

"She's right," he said. "None of us will say it. And do you know why, girl? Because the ones who knew its true name are all dead, and the ones who wrote it down burned the page rather than let it survive them." He looked at Hazel, and there was something almost like pity in his ash-grey eyes. "Your grandmother doesn't guard a prisoner, child. She guards a question mark. Three hundred years of songs, and the guild forgot what it was singing *about*."

"That's not true," Ilsa whispered.

"Isn't it?" Corren turned to the crowd. "Ask her what the founders bound. Ask her what the original charter says. Go on, Guildmother. Read them the charter. The real one. The one you keep locked in your ledger-room."

The room turned as one body toward Ilsa Nakamura.

And Ilsa—who had faced down the falling sky, who had lied to three generations with a straight spine and a steady voice—looked at her granddaughter, and at the crowd, and at Corren, and Tam saw her arrive at the edge of something she could not step back from.

"The charter," she said finally, very quietly, "is in my rooms. Sealed. And it will stay sealed until I decide this valley is strong enough to hear it."

"Then we're not strong enough," Hazel said. "We were never going to be strong enough. That was the point of waiting, wasn't it? Wait long enough and you never have to say it at all."

She stepped down from the dais. The crowd parted for her the way it had parted for no one else that night—all the way to the doors, all the way out into the starless dark, and Tam, after one look back at the ruined guildmother and the grim exiles and the pale warden, went after his friend.

Behind them, the argument reignited like a fire finding dry timber. Guard, burn, seal. Guard, burn, seal. The words chased them out into the square, where the darkness beyond the lamplight pressed close against the shuttered windows of every house in Hollowmere, patient as it had been for three hundred years.

Tam caught up with Hazel at the well. She was staring up at the black ceiling of the sky, tears drying on her cheeks, jaw set in a way he recognized from every dare she'd ever taken and every fence she'd ever climbed when the adults said no.

"They won't tell us," she said. "They'll die before they tell us."

"So what do we do?" Tam asked, though he already knew. He'd known since the ninth stone. Since the boot prints pointing inward. Some part of him had known since they were children sneaking up the mountain path, and Hazel had always walked ahead.

Hazel looked at him, and then past him, up the dark flank of Mount Ceren, where somewhere below the roots of the world a stair descended through three hundred years of carved and crossed-out names.

"There's a ledger in my grandmother's rooms," she said. "Sealed. Real charter, Corren says. Everything the founders wrote down." She wiped her face one last time, and when her hand came away, her expression had gone still and clear as frozen water. "Tonight. While they're all in there shouting at each other about locks."

Tam looked back at the guild hall, light spilling gold from its windows, voices boiling inside. Then up at the starless sky. Then at his best friend, who had just declared war on her own inheritance in a sentence.

"Hazel," he said. "If she catches you—"

"Then she'll finally have to answer a question," Hazel said, and started walking toward the guild hall's side door, where the key-ring hung on its old iron hook, exactly where it had hung since before either of them was born.
