## 10. Descent

The argument in the guild hall had gone on for six hours, and it had solved nothing.

Hazel stood at the edge of the great room with her back against the salt-paned window, watching the town below gutter like a candle in wind. Lamplight moved through the market square in anxious constellations—families carrying belongings to the meetinghouse, wainwrights nailing boards over their shutters as though planks could hold back a sky that had forgotten how to be a sky. Three nights since the stars went out. Three nights of the wards lying dead across the valley like cut harp strings, and the elders still circling the same arguments, wearing grooves into the floorboards with their pacing.

Ilsa stood at the head of the table, hands flat on the old charter box, and even now—even knowing what Hazel knew about what was written inside that box—her grandmother's spine did not bend. Her voice carried the way it always had in council, level and iron-gray.

"We do not act until we understand what we are acting upon."

"Then we will never act," Corren Ashe said from the far end of the table. Twenty years of exile had carved him lean and weathered, and he spoke of the guild's holiest things the way other men spoke of rot in a foundation. "You've had three hundred years to understand it, Guildmother. Your archives are full of ledgers and none of them say what's behind the door. That's not ignorance. That's design."

"Careful," Ilsa said softly, which for her was louder than shouting.

Serah Oduya sat between them like a drawn blade laid flat on the table—present, patient, dangerous. The Ashen Court's sigil was stitched into her coat, but she'd turned the collar so it faced inward, toward her own throat. She had said little in the last hour, only this, once: "My superiors will come. Not to warn. To take. When they come, every one of you will wish you had decided something before they arrived."

And Tam, sitting nearest Hazel, leaned close enough that his shoulder touched hers and murmured, "They're going to argue until the mountain comes to them."

"I know," Hazel said.

She had known for an hour.

Because while the elders argued about whether to descend, Hazel had already gone down—not all the way, not yet, but far enough. That afternoon, while the others slept or schemed, she had taken the oldest key-ring from its hook in Ilsa's study. It hung there openly, almost carelessly, among newer rings with their bright brass teeth: a ring of black iron keys, seven of them, each older than the guild hall itself, each stamped with a mark she'd never been able to read as a child and now recognized as a name worn smooth by centuries of handling. Saltwright names. Founder names. Names she would find again soon enough, carved in stone, crossed out.

She had taken one key down the stair beneath the mountain—the stair behind the cellar wall that Ilsa had told her, when she was eight, was flooded and collapsed and best left alone. It was not flooded. It was not collapsed. It went down farther than any stair she had ever climbed, and at the third landing she had found the first name.

*Mereth Saltwright,* it read, in letters cut deep enough to hold shadow. And beside it, crossing through the letters like a scar, another line: *given.*

She hadn't understood then. She understood now, standing in the guild hall listening to people who had lied to her her whole life decide how long they could afford to keep lying.

"We renew the surface wards first," Ilsa was saying. "We buy time. Then, if the Court forces the question—"

"The surface wards are dead," Serah said. "I walked the line yesterday. Whatever your ancestors built, it isn't a fence you can mend post by post. It's a single song, and someone has spent thirty years cutting verses out of it."

"And I intend to learn which verses remain," Ilsa said, "before I lead my people into a hole in the mountain after a door no living Saltwright has seen—"

"No living Saltwright has *been allowed* to see," Hazel said.

The room stopped.

It was not a loud voice. She felt it leave her like a stone dropped into deep water, small and then enormous, rippling outward through the silence until every face at the table turned toward her. Ilsa's face last, and longest.

"You've been reading my correspondence," Ilsa said quietly.

"I've been reading your charter." Hazel pushed off from the window and crossed the room, and her legs felt strange under her, too light, as though the ground were already tilting toward the stair. "Our charter. The one that says the founders didn't flee magic. They stole something. They buried it. They sealed themselves in with it and called the prison a refuge." She set both hands on the table and looked down its length—at Corren, whose expression held no triumph, only a grim confirmation; at Serah, who watched her with the focused stillness of a hawk; at the two remaining guild members whose names she barely knew, whose faces had gone gray. "Every hour we spend arguing is an hour closer to whatever the Ashen Court is marching up the pass. You want to buy time? Time is bought at the bottom of those stairs, Grandmother. Everything else is just noise."

"You don't know what's down there."

"Neither do you!" It came out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard. "That's the whole point. Three hundred years of guarding something nobody will look at. You taught me the songs. You taught me the stones. You taught me everything except the truth, and now the truth is the only thing left worth teaching." She straightened. "I'm going down. Tonight. With or without this council's blessing—and I notice the council is nine people and half of them are afraid of you, so let's not pretend it's much of either."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight her grandmother's eyes were pale as frost on a window, and for one unguarded instant Hazel saw something in them that frightened her more than anger would have: not opposition. Recognition. As if she were looking at a younger version of some woman Hazel had never met—a founder, maybe, standing in this same hall, saying the same unforgivable thing.

"The seventh key opens nothing," Ilsa said at last. "It opens the way. What happens after the way is open—" Her jaw tightened. "I buried two children and a husband, Hazel. I will not bury you blind."

"Then tell me what's down there."

Her grandmother said nothing. And that silence was answer enough, and both of them knew it.

"I'll need the key-ring back," Ilsa said finally, in a voice like a door closing on a room where something precious had been left burning. "All seven. And you'll take the east passage, not the west—the west stair lost its railing before your mother was born." She gathered the charter box against her chest like a shield. "This is not permission. This is surrender. Learn the difference; you'll need to, from now on."

* * *

They left within the hour.

Tam came because Tam always came—that was simply a law of Hazel's universe, older than the guild, older than the wards. He met her at the cellar door with a lantern, a coiled rope, his grandfather's short-handled pick slung across his back, and the particular expression of a man who has lost every argument with himself in advance.

"For the record," he said, "I think this is the stupidest thing either of us has ever done, and we once floated a hay barge over the mill weir during spring flood."

"Noted for the record."

"Good." He handed her the second lantern. "Lead on, then, before I regain my senses."

Serah was waiting in the shadows past the door, arms folded, and Hazel stopped when she saw her. "You're coming?"

"I'm coming." Serah's voice was flat as slate. "Not because I trust your judgment. Because the Court's archives mention this stair, and I would rather see it myself than describe it secondhand to people who will use it as a weapon. Also—" something flickered across her face, quickly buried, "—your grandmother fought like a woman defending a grave. I'd like to know what's in it."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said in this building all week," Tam said.

The cellar was cold and smelled of brine and old timber, of the pickling barrels and the salt-hung hams and the generations of ordinary life the guild had stacked on top of its secret like soil over a seed. Hazel knelt at the north wall, where the shelving stood, and pulled aside the rack of empty crocks exactly as she had that afternoon. Behind it: the door. Plain gray wood, iron-banded, without lock or handle—because the lock was not in the door. The lock was the ring of black keys in her fist, and the blood in her veins, and the song nobody had sung in three hundred years.

She pressed the first key against the wood and spoke the opening phrase of the founder's song—the fragment she'd found inscribed inside the charter box lid, the fragment Ilsa must have believed she'd never find.

The door did not open. It *unhappened*—the wood going soft as breath, then gone, revealing a mouth of dressed stone descending into absolute dark.

The air that rose from it smelled of deep places: cold mineral, wet rock, and underneath, faint as a memory, salt.

"Three hundred years," Tam whispered, "and it smells like the shore."

"Salt keeps," Hazel said, and stepped through.

* * *

The stair was wider than she expected—wide enough for four abreast, cut from the mountain's living granite, each step dished at its center by feet that had worn it for centuries and then, abruptly, for no centuries at all. Their lantern light slid along walls that were not blank.

The names began at the first landing.

*Mereth Saltwright. Given.*

Hazel had seen it that afternoon, but seeing it again, with Tam's lantern swinging beside hers and Serah's boots quiet on the stone, it landed differently. Deeper. She raised her light and read the lines cut beneath the first name, in the same ancient hand:

*First of the line. First to swear. First given to the keeping.*

"She was real," Tam said softly. "The founder. She's not a story in a book—she's a name on a wall."

"They're all real," Hazel said. Her voice sounded strange in the stairwell, flattened by stone, stripped of the market-square echo it was born to. "Look."

The names climbed the walls in columns, landing after landing, century after century. Some were cut deep and fresh-edged; some were worn to whispers. Every one bore the cross-stroke through it. Every one bore the word.

*Given.*

"Crossed out means dead," Tam said. He'd gone pale in the lantern light. "That's what I was told, anyway. Every guild member gets carved here when they're born, and crossed out when they die. A record of the whole line."

"Read the word under them," Serah said. She had stopped walking. Her hand rested on the wall, fingers tracing a name near eye height—*Oduya, given*—and her face had gone very still. "There are Oduyas on this wall. My family name. We have records in the Court going back nine generations, and not one of them mentions a branch that stayed behind in a hidden valley." She lifted her lantern higher, and the columns of names climbed away above them into darkness, hundreds of them, thousands. "Your guild didn't just hide itself. It collected people. Or people came here and were never recorded anywhere else again."

"Given," Hazel repeated. The word sat wrong in her mouth, heavier than it should. On the walk down from the boundary stones, Serah had told them what the Court's oldest fragments said—that the founders bound something, that the wards were not protection but containment, that the price of such binding was written in no ledger because no ledger survives its own crime. She had thought she understood. Standing here among the names, she understood differently. A binding needs an anchor. A lock keyed to bloodlines needs bloodlines. Generations of them, born into the keeping, sworn before they could speak, crossed out when the keeping was done with them.

Not a refuge. A relay of the pledged, passing the burden hand to hand down three hundred years until it reached a girl who couldn't hear the salt-songs.

"Hey." Tam's hand closed on her shoulder. "Whatever face you're making right now—stop it. We haven't seen the worst yet, and I refuse to have you fall apart on a staircase."

"That's very comforting."

"I'm a comfort professional."

She laughed despite herself—a small, ragged sound that the dark swallowed—and kept walking. But her hand trailed along the wall as she went, touching the names as she passed, the way you might touch the shoulders of sleeping people. *Aldous Saltwright, given. Maren Voss, given.* There were Vosses too. There were names she knew from the town graveyard, from the census rolls, from the market stalls—names that belonged to people whose descendants sold bread and shoes and nails in the town above, all unknowing. Everyone in the valley, probably. Everyone was on this wall somewhere, or descended from someone who was.

The whole valley was the lock. The whole valley had always been the lock.

At the fifth landing, the character of the carvings changed.

The names grew sparser. The intervals between them stretched from years to decades. And the handwriting changed—older, more hurried, the letters leaning as though cut by someone working against time. Here and there a name was begun and abandoned, the chisel-strokes stopping mid-letter, and beside several of them someone had added a second inscription in a different hand, smaller, cramped:

*Tried. Failed.*
*Tried. Refused.*
*Tried. Did not return.*

Hazel stopped before the last of these. *Corren Ashe, given.* The cross-stroke through his name was freshly cut—within the last few days, by the brightness of the groove. But beneath it, in the same new hand, someone—Ilsa, it had to be Ilsa—had carved a second line:

*Came back.*

"He knows," Tam breathed behind her. "He's seen this wall. He knew his name was here."

"Of course he knew," Serah said. "Why do you think he wants the door destroyed? He read this wall twenty years ago, and he's been running from it ever since—or toward it. With men like him it's usually both."

Hazel stood a moment longer before the name. *Came back.* Two words, and between them lived an entire unwritten history: a young man who fled the guild, the valley, the whole suffocating inheritance, and returned twenty years later angrier than he left. She thought of his face in the council hall—not triumphant, she realized now, but *grieving*. Grief weaponized into certainty, aimed at the thing that had taken everyone he'd ever—

The lantern flame bent.

Not from a draft. From below. A slow exhalation rising up the stairwell, warm where everything else was cold, carrying with it that faint salt-smell again—but stronger now, and underneath the salt, something else. Something organic and vast and slow. The smell of a cave where something sleeps. The smell of breath that has been held for a very long time.

Tam's hand found hers without either of them deciding it should.

"How much farther?" Serah asked. Her voice was steady, but she had shifted her weight onto the balls of her feet, a fighter's readiness, absurd here on a staircase in the dark.

"The charter map showed seven landings," Hazel said. "Seven keys. Seven locks." She lifted the iron ring, and the keys stirred against each other with a sound like distant bells heard through snow. "One landing left."

They went down.

The final flight was different from all the rest. The steps were uncut here—raw stone, natural, as though the stair had stopped being architecture and become descent. The walls narrowed, then opened suddenly, and the lanterns' light spilled out into a space too large for them to measure, a cavern where the beams simply died without finding far walls. The air was warm and moving, gently, rhythmically, in and out.

In and out.

"Oh," Tam whispered. "Oh, it's breathing. The mountain is breathing."

"It's not the mountain," Hazel said.

Below them, at the limit of the light, the floor fell away into a circular shaft, and rising from the shaft she could see it: not a gate, not a door, not anything her hands or her guild-training or her whole life had prepared her to name. A seam. A place where the world did not quite close, glowing with woven threads of salt-light, white and silver, dense as cloth, beautiful as frost on a winter morning—and pulsing. Slowly. Rhythmically.

In time with the breath.

And threaded through the weave, thin as hair, she saw gaps. Places where the salt-light had thinned or frayed or simply failed, decades ago, years ago, last season. The work of a patient saboteur, or the work of simple age—she couldn't tell, and suspected no one alive could.

But she could see what lay through the widest gap. Not an eye, not a shape. Just a darkness deeper than the cavern's dark, and within it, faintly, a suggestion of attention. Of something that had felt their lanterns. Felt their footsteps. Felt, perhaps, the blood-keyed lock of the stair itself being disturbed for the first time in a generation.

The seam widened. One finger's width. No more.

From somewhere beyond it—muffled, distorted, impossible—came a sound that stopped Hazel's heart and started it again in the same beat.

Humming.

A lullaby. The one Ilsa used to sing her, the one every Nakamura child learned at the knee, the one that was supposed to be theirs, private, family—

"Hazel." Serah's hand on her arm, grip like iron. "Step back. Now."

But Hazel stood at the lip of the shaft, lantern trembling, staring into the widening seam, and heard her grandmother's voice singing from behind a door that had been locked since before her grandmother was born—and understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones colder than the cavern air, that whatever waited below already knew her.

Already knew all of them.

Already knew, perhaps, every name on the wall.

The humming stopped.

And in the silence that followed, from the depths of the shaft, something said—in her grandmother's voice, gentle as bedtime, terrible as the end of the world:

"*You brought the seventh key home at last, child. Come and see what we kept from you.*"
