## 12. What Was Buried

Serah Oduya had climbed out of worse places than a stair under a mountain, but she had never climbed out of one carrying this.

The dark came up with them like water in a flooded hold. It clung to her boots, her cloak, the inside of her mouth. Behind her, the girl—Nakamura, the last Saltwright, the heir to three hundred years of silence—kept one hand flat against the carved wall as she climbed, as if the names cut into it might steady her. Perhaps they did. Serah had no names of her own to hold onto down here, only the sigil on her commission and the long habit of obedience that had carried her across half the world and into a hole in the earth where something breathed.

The breathing had stopped when they ran. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't.

* * *

They came up into gray daylight and cold wind, the mouth of the stair hidden behind a shelf of granite furred with dead moss. Tam Voss went first, sword drawn, scanning the treeline below the mountain's shoulder. Hazel came second, moving like someone walking in her sleep. Serah took the rear because rearguard was where she'd been trained to be and because she could not bear to have anything at her back just now—not even empty air.

They made camp in the lee of the rock, out of the wind, close enough to the stair-mouth that they could watch it. Nobody suggested going farther. Nobody suggested going back down, either. The stair sat between them like a wound nobody wanted to touch.

Tam built a small fire, feeding it dry pine needles first so it wouldn't smoke. He was good at small competences, this farm-boy-turned-friend; Serah had watched him all day, the way he watched Hazel, the way his fear always arrived a half-step behind his duty. She found herself cataloguing him the way she catalogued everything now: assets, liabilities, things the Court would burn without hesitation.

Hazel sat with her back against the stone and her knees drawn up and said nothing for a long time.

It was Serah who finally spoke, because someone had to, and because saying it aloud was the only way to make it stop circling inside her skull.

"The Court has a name for it," she said.

Hazel's head came up. In the firelight her face looked older than it had that morning at the ford, older than it had an hour ago at the bottom of the world. "You knew. You said the archives mentioned a door."

"I said the archives said the door must never open." Serah pulled off her gauntlets, finger by finger, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them. "I didn't lie to you. But I didn't tell you everything either, and I'm—tired of not telling things." She heard how it sounded, how it must sound to a girl who had just learned her whole life was a charter of lies, and pressed on anyway. "There's a sealed folio in the Court's deep archive in Vhal Maren. Restricted to wardens of the third rank and above. I was shown it once, when I made rank. They call it the Record of the First Binding."

"Go on," Tam said quietly.

"It's fragmentary. Older than the Court itself—the Court is old, but it isn't *that* old. Most of it is accounting. Grain tallies, tithe records, lists of names. Names crossed out." She saw Hazel flinch and understood why a heartbeat later; the stair walls were lined with exactly such lists. "But there are narrative fragments. Testimonies, maybe. Prayers, maybe. And they all describe the same event, from before your valley was sealed. A famine of light. The stars going out—not here. Everywhere. Whole kingdoms starving in the dark, crops failing, rivers freezing in high summer. And then, over the course of a single generation, the stars coming back. All except in one place."

Hazel's voice was very small. "Here."

"Here. Your valley kept its darkness. Your founders built their wards, and the stars stayed gone above you, and the rest of the world healed." Serah turned her hands palm-up toward the fire. "I used to think the guild were thieves who fled justice. That's what we're taught. A renegade order steals something precious, runs to the mountains, locks itself away with its loot. I believed it my whole career. It's a tidy story. Empires like tidy stories."

"And now?" Hazel asked.

"Now I think they stole nothing." The words came out of her slowly, each one costing more than the last. "I think they *took* something. There's a difference. A thief carries away what doesn't belong to him. What your founders did—" She stopped. Started again. "The fragments call it a king. Not a man-king. A king the way winter is a king, the way hunger is a king. They say it ate the stars. Not dimmed them. *Ate* them. Swallowed the light of the sky until the world was dying of it. And no army could kill it, because you can't put a spear into hunger. So a company of salt-mages—your founders—did the only thing left. They bound it. They wove it into a prison of salt and song, and they built the wards around it, and the wards weren't walls to keep the world out."

She met Hazel's eyes.

"They were a muzzle. To keep it from feeding again."

* * *

The fire cracked and spat. Somewhere downslope a crow called once and thought better of it.

"That's not possible," Tam said, but his voice had no conviction in it. He was staring at the stair-mouth. "Wards keep things out. That's what they do. I've lived my whole life inside them. Nothing gets in, nothing gets out. That's the whole—it's just how the valley works."

"How do you think it works?" Serah asked gently. "Three hundred years, boy. Three hundred years of perfect seals, maintained by nine people and a handful of stones. Magic doesn't run on nothing. Every working I've ever seen costs its maker something. What do you imagine your guild has been paying, all this time, and to whom?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Hazel.

Hazel had gone very still. When she spoke, it was barely above the fire's whisper. "The salt-songs. My grandmother sings them every census. I thought—I was told—they were maintenance. Tuning. Like oiling a hinge." Her hand rose unconsciously to her throat. "They're feeding it. Aren't they. The songs are how it eats without eating. Measured portions. Rationed starvation."

"That would be my reading," Serah said. "The binding fragments end with a line I've never forgotten, though I didn't understand it until today. *We give it our small darks so it will not take our great one.*"

Hazel made a sound that was almost a laugh and nothing like one. "Small darks. The silent stone. The dead years. The children born deaf to the songs—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Serah watched her decide not to say whatever came next. Watched her fold it away, the way her grandmother must have folded things away for sixty years, and understood suddenly, viscerally, what inheritance meant in this valley. It wasn't land. It wasn't name. It was the folding.

"I need to say the rest," Serah said. "While I still can."

* * *

She told them about the Hollow King.

Not the folk-tale version every warden recruit heard around training fires—the shadow-story used to frighten cadets into vigilance—but the archive version, the one written in a dead dialect on vellum so old it had gone the color of teeth. A god of the long dark, older than the gods men worshipped, that had come upon the world in the age before memory. It did not rage. That was the thing the fragments returned to again and again, the detail that had given her nightmares at nineteen. It did not rage, did not hunt, did not conquer. It simply *wanted*, the way the grave wants, patiently and without malice, and everything it wanted grew cold and went out. Stars first. Then suns, the fragments claimed, though Serah had always taken that for hyperbole. Then the warmth between people. Mothers forgetting the faces of their children, not through grief but through a kind of gentle subtraction, love eaten down to a smooth blank place.

The founders had starved it instead of killing it because killing was impossible—and because, Serah suspected now, some of them had loved it. Or pitied it. The fragments were ambiguous, grief-stricken, written by people who had done a monstrous necessary thing and knew it. *We are not heroes,* one testimony read. *We are jailers who have made ourselves cellmates. Let those who come after curse us honestly.*

"Curse us honestly," Hazel repeated. Her eyes were wet but her voice was level. "And instead we got lullabies. Refuge from magic. The brave Saltwrights keeping the valley safe." She laughed, that broken almost-laugh again. "My grandmother used to sing me to sleep with the first verse of the founder's song. I asked her once what it was about and she said, *the sea, far away.* It's about a muzzle. I've been sleeping inside a muzzle my entire life."

"The Court calls it the Hollow King," Serah said. "In the fragments it has other names. The Starving One. The Guest Below. One text just calls it *our sorrow* and leaves it at that." She fed a stick to the fire. "And here is the part I should have led with, and couldn't, because I'm a coward in my own particular way. The folio isn't sealed to protect the world from the knowledge. It's sealed to protect the Court's claim. Third rank and above, need-to-know, because the last entry—the newest one, added maybe two hundred years ago—is a resolution of the Ashen Conclave. It reads: *the Binding shall be maintained until such time as the Court finds means to turn it to account.*"

Tam's head snapped up. "Turn it to *account*?"

"They mean as a weapon." Serah's mouth tasted of ash. "A god that eats stars, leashed. Pointed at the Court's enemies. I don't know how far the plans go. I know the wardens sent to your valley aren't diplomats. I know the orders I was given were to assess the wards' integrity and report, and that when I reported they were failing, the response wasn't reinforcement. It was acceleration." She looked at the fire rather than at them. "I came to warn you because I thought you deserved to know what was coming. I stayed because I realized I'd spent my whole life serving the machinery that intends to rob your prisoner."

"You're describing my family as jailers," Hazel said, "and your empire as the next ones in line."

"Yes."

"And the door stays shut either way."

Serah didn't answer. Because the honest answer—the answer climbing in her chest since the bottom of the stair, since the breathing, since the seam widening a finger's width at the sound of a frightened girl—was *no*. Was: perhaps the shut door was itself the crime. Was: she had seen the look on Hazel Nakamura's face down there, horror and recognition tangled together, and she had felt the seam respond to the girl's presence like a lock responding to its key, and she did not know anymore which outcome she feared most.

* * *

Night came down fully. Tam took first watch at the stair-mouth, sword across his knees, and pretended not to listen. Hazel lay on her bedroll with her eyes open, and Serah sat beside her because neither of them could sleep and misery keeps better company than solitude.

"My grandmother knew," Hazel said at last, to the sky that had no stars. "All of it. She's known since before I was born. She sang the ration-songs herself. She let me grow up believing I was broken because I couldn't hear them right." A breath. "And she loves me. That's the part I can't get around. She genuinely, truly loves me, and she fed that thing for sixty years and lied to my face every day of my life, and both of those are real."

"Inherited guilt is the heaviest kind," Serah said. "Because you didn't commit it, but you're made of it. Everything you are was built on top of it. Refuse the guilt and you refuse the foundation. Accept it and it crushes you." She had learned that in the Court's own lecture halls, though the instructors had never put it so plainly. "The fragments have a word for the founders' heirs. *Debt-bearers.* Children born owing a debt contracted before their birth."

"Is that what I am?"

"I think," Serah said carefully, "that you're the first debt-bearer in three hundred years who's actually been handed the ledger. That makes you something new. Whether it makes you something better depends entirely on what you do next."

Hazel was quiet a long moment. Then: "When it spoke to me. Down there. In her voice." Her hand found Serah's sleeve and gripped it, hard, the grip of someone holding onto a ledge. "It wasn't mocking me. I've had time to think about it, and it wasn't mockery. It was—hungry. It reached for the warmest thing it could find in my head and it *held on*. Like a starving animal that's forgotten what warmth feels like." Her voice frayed. "Serah, it's been down there three hundred years, starving politely, being sung to in measured portions, and everyone who ever knew it was a person-shaped thing decided it was safer to lie about it than to look at it. What does three hundred years of that make something? What comes up out of that hole if the door ever opens?"

"Nothing good," Serah said honestly.

"And the Court wants to point it at their enemies anyway."

"The Court wants many things."

Below them, faintly, the wind moved through the valley—the valley that was a cell, the town that was a guard-post, the fields that were grown over a wound. Somewhere in that darkness, Ilsa Nakamura was gathering what remained of her guild, preparing defenses against a warden-company that intended not conquest but theft. Somewhere beyond the boundary, Corren Ashe was marching with his exiles and his certainty. And beneath all of it, under a mile of stone and a weave of salt-light, something patient turned over in its sleep and dreamed of stars.

"We should get back," Hazel said, sitting up. "Before dawn. Before anyone else reaches the door. My grandmother needs to know what the Court really wants, and Corren needs to hear it too, and—" She stopped. In the firelight her face had changed; the vertigo was still there, Serah could see it, the sick groundless feeling of a world rebuilt overnight. But underneath it something had hardened. Something with edges.

"Hazel," Serah said. "Whatever you're deciding—decide it knowing the full weight. If that door opens wrong, it won't just be the valley. It'll be every sky there is."

"Then I suppose," Hazel said, standing, pulling her coat tight against the cold, "I'd better learn the whole song."

And far below them, faint as a pulse through stone, the breathing began again.
