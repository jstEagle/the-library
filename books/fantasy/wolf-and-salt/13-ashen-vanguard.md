## 13. Ashen Vanguard

Tam Voss had learned to read the valley the way other people read letters. The way smoke leaned on a windless morning told him which hearths were burning green wood; the angle of crows over the tannery meant offal day; and when the miller's dog went silent all at once, it meant someone important was coming up the ford road.

On the third morning after Serah Oduya's warning, every dog in Hollowmere went silent at once.

Tam was on the market square steps with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands, watching Hazel argue with a cartwright about axle grease, when the sound arrived. Not hoofbeats—not yet—but something underneath them, a low tremor he felt in his back teeth, like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the light changed. It came down the eastern road as a dull red shimmer, the color of coals seen through an iron door, and the morning frost on the rooftops began to steam.

"Get inside," Tam said, and was already moving. "Hazel—get everyone inside."

She didn't need telling twice. That was one thing about Hazel Nakamura that outsiders got wrong, he thought: they saw the guild's last apprentice, half-trained, half-believed-in, and assumed hesitation. But she'd been raised by Ilsa Nakamura, and Ilsa had never hesitated in her life, only calculated. Hazel dropped her argument mid-sentence, pressed her cup into the cartwright's hands like evidence, and started shouting names in the clear carrying voice of someone who'd spent years calling sheep off high pasture.

The wardens came up the ford road at a walk. That was the worst of it, Tam thought later—the walk. No charge, no thunder. A column of perhaps two hundred, ranked four abreast, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once needed to hurry. Their armor was the gray of old ash, matte and lightless, and each of them carried a pike whose head burned without consuming—a slow orange flame wrapped around black iron, shedding heat Tam could feel from thirty yards, like standing near a bread oven left open.

At their head rode a woman on a horse too fine for the road. Her armor was the same ash-gray but cleaner, and across her back, instead of a pike, she carried a staff crowned with a lens of smoked glass. When she reached the edge of the square she reined in, looked at the town—at the shuttered windows, the empty well, the children being pulled indoors by their collars—and Tam watched her do something no warden in any story he'd ever been told had done.

She waited.

"I am Captain Vess Andrelle," she called, and her voice carried the way Serah's did, trained and thrown, but colder, like a blade laid flat against skin rather than through it. "Of the Ashen Court, Third Vigil. I come under writ of the Nine Thrones." She unrolled a scroll she did not read from, holding it up more for the shape of the gesture than its contents. "By right of discovery and peril, the Court claims the door beneath this mountain. You will yield the guild's keys, the guild's records, and any person bearing the salt-bond. You will be treated justly."

Nobody moved. Somewhere behind Tam, a shutter creaked, and he heard a child whispering questions and a mother hushing them with a hand pressed flat over small lips.

It was Corren Ashe who answered.

Tam still hadn't gotten used to him. Twenty years of stories had built Corren into a kind of weather—a storm that had passed through the valley once and left everything changed—and then he'd walked back into Hollowmere with forty exiles and a face like anyone's father grown hard. He stood now at the mouth of the guild hall lane, arms crossed, wearing a salt-stained coat that had once been guild gray and had been dyed black since, and he laughed. It was not a kind laugh.

"Justly," Corren repeated. "That's a word I've heard before. From your predecessors, Captain. They used it in the year they burned the ferry at Lowbridge because a smuggler's barge might have carried salt out. Eleven drowned. The record calls it *justly administered*." He stepped forward, out of the lane's shadow, and let the fire-light of the pikes fall across his face. "I know what your writ says. I've read the Court's own archives. Ask her." He jerked his chin toward the window of the inn where Serah Oduya sat with her wound packed and her sword in reach. "Ask your own warden what the archives say is behind that door."

Captain Andrelle's eyes flicked to the inn window and back. Something passed over her face—brief, quickly governed, but Tam caught it, because he'd spent his whole life reading faces at markets where a misread price could ruin a family. She knew. Whatever was written in those archives, she had read it, and she had come anyway.

"The Hollow King is a resource," Andrelle said. "Bound, leashed, and turned to the defense of the Nine Thrones. The Court has bound worse."

"No," Corren said quietly. "You haven't. That's rather the point."

Hazel came to stand beside him then, and Tam moved too, because there was nowhere else in the world he would rather be than at her shoulder when the sky decided to fall. Behind them the guild hall doors opened and Guildmother Ilsa Nakamura emerged, leaning on her stick, straight-backed despite the bandage Tam knew was wrapped beneath her coat from the night at the stair. The wardens' ranks rippled—some of the older ones, Tam noticed, made a small involuntary sign with their free hands, touching thumb to sternum, the way superstitious men greet a ghost.

"Guildmother," Andrelle said, and inclined her head a precise degree. "You are outnumbered twenty to one. Your wards are dead. Your stones are broken. Give me the keys and the salt-bonded, and Hollowmere keeps its roofs."

"You misunderstand what you're asking for," Ilsa said. Her voice was dry as the salt cellar, and it carried just as far. "You're asking for the leash. Fine. Take it. Walk down the stair, put your hand on the seam, and tell me what it says to you. It speaks, Captain. In voices you love. It has had three hundred years to learn how." She smiled, and it was the smile Tam remembered from his childhood, the one that preceded either candy or catastrophe. "But you didn't come to hold the leash. You came because your masters want the dog."

For a long moment nobody breathed. The fire-pikes hissed softly, a hundred small sounds like rain on hot stone.

Andrelle rolled up her scroll. "Then the Court takes by force what is refused freely. Warden law gives me latitude in contested territory." She did not say it with pleasure. That was somehow worse. "You have until the sun clears the eastern ridge."

* * *

They argued in the guild hall's great room, and it was less an argument than a demolition, because there was nothing left to argue about. Two hundred wardens with fire-pikes against nine guild members, forty exiles, and a town of farmers and smiths who had never held anything sharper than a slaughtering knife.

"We run," said Maren Ostrow, one of Corren's exiles, a broad woman with burn scars up both forearms. "Take the salt-bonded, take the keys, scatter into the high country. Let them dig for their door."

"There's nowhere to run to," Serah said. She stood by the fire with her arm bound, gray-faced but upright. "The wards are down. The moment you leave the valley you're in Court territory, and every beacon between here and the coast will be lit. And even if you reached the sea—" she shook her head. "They don't stop. I trained under Andrelle. She doesn't stop."

"Then we fight," said Joss Brandt, the smith, and half the room murmured agreement, and Tam felt his stomach drop, because he could see exactly how that fight went. Fire-pikes against hay forks. He'd seen what a single pike did to the gatepost of the inn when a nervous warden had shouldered it that morning: the oak hadn't burned so much as *unmade*, gone soft and crumbling like rotten bread.

"We fight," Corren said, and the room went quiet, because everyone had expected him to say the opposite. He looked around at them, and Tam realized he was counting heads the way a man counts before a flood. "Not to win. To buy time. There's a difference, and if you don't understand the difference you'll die stupid, and I'll be annoyed, because I promised myself I wouldn't bury anyone else." He crossed to the table where the maps lay, the ones Hazel had drawn from Ilsa's ledgers, and put his finger on the ruined ninth stone. "Here. The boundary line funnels them. Broken ground, the ford at their backs, the slope rising toward us. Two hundred men can't come abreast through the narrows—call it sixty at contact. Fire-pikes are terror weapons. They're made for crowds and sieges, not for a shield wall on bad footing in the fog off the river."

"And the fog?" Maren asked.

Corren almost smiled. "The river's been running warm since the wards fell. It'll steam till midmorning. I've fought in Ashen fog before." His voice went somewhere else when he said it, somewhere Tam wasn't invited. "It cuts both ways."

Hazel was staring at him. "Why?" she said. "Twenty years ago you wanted the door destroyed. Last week you were arguing for collapsing the mountain. Now you're drawing battle lines to defend it. Why?"

The silence stretched. Corren looked at her—looked at her the way, Tam thought, a man looks at a daughter he doesn't have—and for a moment Tam saw the age of him, all of it, the twenty lost years sitting on his shoulders like wet wool.

"Because I finally read the whole archive," he said. "Serah brought copies. I spent two nights with them." He tapped the map, on the seam beneath the mountain, the place marked with a circle and no name. "I wanted it dead because I thought it was a thing. A weapon, a god, a monster—something you could kill or bury or drown under a million tons of rock. It isn't. It's starving, girl. It ate the stars because it was hungry, and they locked it in the dark with its hunger for three hundred years, and now your Court wants to take that hunger and point it at their enemies." His hand closed into a fist on the table. "If Andrelle binds it, it won't stay bound. Things that hungry don't stay leashed. And when it breaks—and it will break—it won't distinguish between the Nine Thrones and the valley that fed it. So yes. I'll stand next to the woman who lied to me for twenty years, and next to the apprentice who can't sing, and next to every farmer in this room, because there is no version of tomorrow where fighting them is worse than not fighting them."

Nobody spoke. Out in the square, faintly, the fire-pikes hissed.

Ilsa broke it. She had been silent through all of it, seated in her high chair with her stick across her knees, and when she rose, the room rose with her out of three hundred years of habit.

"Then here is how it will be," she said. "The guild holds the narrows. Corren's company holds the flanks. The town holds the hall and the cellars, and no one—not one soul—goes near the mountain. If we fall, the stair must be sealed, and the sealing is Hazel's alone to decide." She looked at her granddaughter, and something passed between them that Tam couldn't read and didn't try to. "We were keepers," Ilsa said, to the whole room, and her voice cracked on the word like ice on a pond. "We forgot it. Today we remember."

* * *

The sun cleared the eastern ridge at mid-morning, and the fog off the river was still lying in the narrows like spilled milk, and Captain Andrelle kept her word, which Tam had known she would and hated her a little for.

They formed up on the slope above the ford among the broken boundary stones, and Tam found himself wedged between Joss Brandt and one of Corren's exiles, holding a boar spear he'd taken from above his uncle's mantel, feeling profoundly like a boy playing at war. Ahead of him the fog glowed faintly orange where the first rank of pikes entered it, a hundred little captive dawns.

Beside him, absurdly, Corren Ashe was humming. Low, tuneless, half under his breath. Tam caught enough of it to recognize the founder's song—the real one, the one Hazel had been learning from Ilsa's dying dictation—and he looked over, startled.

"Guild brat," Corren said, not looking at him. "First thing they teach you. You hum it before a fight and the fear has somewhere to go." He glanced sideways. "Your friend sings it better than I ever did. Tell her that, someday, if we're stupid enough to survive."

Then the fog lit white-orange along its whole length, and the world became noise.

Tam had imagined battles his whole life, the way farm boys do, and every imagining had been wrong. There was no clash, not at first—there was *light*, lancing through the fog in strokes, and the smell of scorched air, and screaming that might have been men or might have been the pikes themselves, because the things sang when they discharged, a high glass note that got into the teeth. The first warden line came up the slope out of the burning fog in perfect order, shields locked, and met the guild's thin wall, and the line bent—

—and held.

It held because of the stones. Tam understood it a heartbeat before it happened, because Hazel had been crouched at the base of the shattered ninth stone since dawn with her bare palm flat against the broken core, and when the warden line reached the old ward-line, the ground itself seemed to remember. Frost flashed outward from her hand in a ring, and where it crossed the warden formation the fire-pikes guttered, dimmed, starved—the old wards reaching up through her like roots through soil, drinking the magic out of the air. Not much. A breath of it. But a pike-starved warden is just a tired man in gray armor on a steep hill, and tired men on steep hills lose.

"HOLD!" Corren's voice, enormous. "Hold the line! They break at the third pass—they always break at the third pass!"

They broke at the third pass. The warden line recoiled down into the fog, re-formed, came again, and again the frost-ring flared and the pikes dimmed and the hill cost them blood they hadn't planned to spend. Tam stopped thinking entirely. There was a gap in the wall and he filled it; there was a fallen exile and he dragged him back; there was a moment—he would carry it forever—when a warden's visor lifted six inches from his face and he saw that the man behind the ash-gray steel was young, terrified, and crying, and neither of them struck, and then the press of bodies tore them apart like a river tearing apart two leaves.

Twice the line nearly folded. Both times it was Corren who stitched it shut, planting himself in the breach with a long salt-hooked billhook and a fury that looked, from the outside, less like courage than like a debt being paid in installments. Once, Tam saw him go down under two wardens and rise again because Maren Ostrow hit them both like a landslide. Once, Tam saw Ilsa Nakamura standing at the wall's center with her stick raised, singing—not the founder's song but the old census chant, the counting song every guild child learned—and the wardens faltered at the sound of it as if they'd walked into a church they'd forgotten existed.

And once—only once—Tam looked back from the wall and saw Hazel on her feet, both hands buried to the wrist in the broken stone, hair loose and streaming in the warm wind, frost blooming from her in waves, and her face was not afraid. It was the face of someone hearing music no one else could hear, and finally, *finally* understanding why.

The sun was high and white when the horns sounded recall, and the warden lines withdrew down into the fog and did not come again. Along the slope lay perhaps thirty gray shapes that did not move, and among the stones of the guild's wall, eleven who would never stand again. The exiles were digging graves before the fog had fully lifted. Nobody had to be asked.

Tam found Hazel at the ninth stone, on her knees, gray-lipped, her hands blistered where the salt-light had poured through her. He knelt and took her wrists, and her pulse was fluttering fast as a bird's.

"It's not enough," she whispered. Her eyes were fixed on the fog below, where the orange glow of the pikes still burned, patient, unhurried, regrouping. "Tam—I felt it. The whole ward-line. It's like singing through a cracked throat. I bought us hours. Maybe a day. Andrelle hasn't even committed her reserve."

"Then we take the hours," Tam said, "and we steal the day, and we figure out the rest after." He got his shoulder under her arm and hauled her upright, and together they looked down at the burning fog, and at the mountain behind them, and at the stair beneath the mountain where a seam in the world waited with its finger-width of dark.

Below them, out of the fog, a single rider came up the slope under a white banner, unarmed, walking her horse slowly.

It was Captain Andrelle, and she was coming to talk.
