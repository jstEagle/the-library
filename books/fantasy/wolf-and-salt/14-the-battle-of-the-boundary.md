## 14. The Battle of the Boundary

They came at moonrise, when the fog had burned itself down to embers and the sky was a black ceiling pressing close where the stars should have been.

Hazel heard them before she saw them—the particular crunch of many boots on frost-hard ground, the creak of leather, the wet hiss of fire-pikes being primed. The sound carried strangely in the dead air, as if the whole valley had become the inside of a bell and every noise rang off its walls.

"They're forming up on the flats," Tam said beside her. He lay flat against the ridge above the ruined ninth stone, binoculars of polished salt-glass pressed to his eyes. "Three ranks. Maybe sixty. Seventy."

"Sixty," Hazel repeated. They had nineteen. Nineteen townsfolk with hunting bows, reaping hooks, and whatever courage the last week hadn't burned out of them. Corren Ashe stood among them at the base of the ridge, exiles fanned around him, checking the edges of blades with the calm of a man who had expected to die somewhere worse.

"Sixty is fine," Tam said. "We only have to make them think we're expensive."

Hazel almost laughed. Almost. Below them, the ninth stone lay exactly as they'd left it—shattered from within, its hollow core open to the sky like a split seedpod, the warden's sigil stamped in ash still visible on the inner curve. Around it, the ward-line ran north and south like a snapped thread, and everything beyond it was darkness that moved.

The first fire-pike flared.

It was nothing like hearth-fire. It burned white and hungry, throwing shadows the wrong way, and where its light touched the frost it didn't melt so much as unmake—the grass beneath going grey and crumbling like old paper. The Ashen Court's vanguard advanced behind their own light, and the light was worse than the dark had been, because at least the dark had been honest.

"Hold until they're inside the old line," Corren called up, not loudly. His voice carried anyway. Twenty years ago this man had fled the guild; tonight he fought for it, and Hazel still hadn't decided what to feel about that. "Let them think the valley's empty. Empty things don't fight back."

*Empty things*, Hazel thought, *like a valley sealed from the world. Like a guild of nine. Like an heir who can't hear the songs.*

She put her hand flat against the earth.

She had been doing it all evening without quite admitting why. Since the wards fell, since that first terrible pressure at the edge of her senses, something had been pulling at her—not sound exactly, not the salt-songs Ilsa had sung over boundary stones her whole life, but their absence. A ringing where music should be. All day it had grown, a toothache of silence, and now, with fire-light crawling up the slope toward her home, she finally understood that the silence was not empty.

It was *full*. It was a song played so loud it had gone past hearing.

"Hazel." Tam's hand closed on her shoulder. "They're crossing the line. Whatever you're doing—"

"I'm listening," she said, which was true and useless and the most honest thing she'd said in weeks.

Her palm was pressed to a shard of the broken ninth stone that had been thrown clear in the shattering—a jagged piece of grey-white crystal, half-buried in frozen turf. And through it, through the wound in the stone, the songs came.

Not gently. Not the way Ilsa sang them, low and even, a lullaby for the land. They came the way floodwater comes through a broken door—all of them at once, every ward-song ever woven into every stone along three hundred miles of boundary, layered and tangled and screaming to be heard. She gasped. Her vision whited. For one endless moment she was every Saltwright who had ever knelt at this line, every voice that had fed the wards, grandmother after grandmother after grandmother back to the founders themselves, and under all of it, vast and slow and patient, the deep note of the mountain itself.

Then it settled—or she did—and she was lying on a cold hillside with blood in her mouth where she'd bitten her tongue, and Tam was shaking her, and the world had sound again, ordinary sound, boots and fire.

"Hazel. *Hazel.*"

"The songs," she said. Her voice came out cracked. "Tam. They were never silent for me. I couldn't hear them because I was hearing *all of them*. Every stone. Every generation. Unfiltered." She started laughing, and it came out wrong, halfway to weeping. "Grandmother spent twenty years teaching me to listen harder, and the whole time I needed to learn how to listen *less*."

Below, the vanguard reached the shattered stone. A warden officer—she could see the ash-grey braid of rank even at this distance—dismounted and laid one gauntleted hand on the ruin, and Hazel felt that touch like a finger pressed to her own breastbone. Probing. Reading the break the way you'd read a lock you intended to pick.

"He's testing the ward-line," she breathed. "He'll find the gap. When he finds the gap—"

"Then we stop letting him look." Tam rolled to his knees, bow up. "Corren! They're at the stone!"

What followed, Hazel would remember forever in fragments, because she was no longer wholly present in it. She was partly elsewhere—in the stone, in the song, in the long thread of the ward-line running away north and south like a vein of silver under the skin of the world.

Corren's ambush broke over the vanguard like weather. Arrows first, loosed from three points at once so the Court couldn't fix a single position. Then the exiles sweeping down the slopes with torches and iron, hitting the flank where the fire-pikes' light blinded their own bearers. It was brutal, fast, and desperate, and it worked for perhaps four minutes.

In those four minutes, Hazel crawled down the ridge to the broken stone.

Firelight strobed across her. Somewhere to her left a man screamed, and somewhere to her right a fire-pike discharged with a sound like the world tearing a sheet, and the grass it touched turned to grey lace. She kept low and kept moving and pressed both palms flat against the largest fragment of the ninth stone, and she *sang*.

Not the way Ilsa sang. She couldn't. The proper songs required a single clean voice, and hers would never be single again. Instead she opened herself to all of it—the flood, the tangle, the generations stacked like sediment—and let it pour through her and tried, with the desperation of a woman holding a bursting dam with her bare hands, to shape just one thread of it. Just one ward. Just this stone. Just here.

The stone drank her voice and gave nothing back.

"No," she whispered. "No, please—" She sang louder. Her throat tore. The fragment stayed dead under her hands, cold as any rock, and thirty feet away a warden turned his fire-pike toward the sound of her singing, and Tam's arrow took him in the shoulder joint, and Hazel understood with perfect clarity that she was about to die at the foot of a stone she couldn't fix.

*Ilsa,* she thought. *You covered for me my whole life, and I'm going to die proving you right.*

And then—because dying people are honest, and because she had spent her whole life trying to be less than she was—she stopped fighting the flood.

She stopped trying to pick one thread. She let all of them come. Every song at once, every generation, the full unbearable chord of three hundred years of Saltwrights, and instead of shaping it she simply *held* it, the way you hold a crying child, the way her grandmother had once held her through a fever, saying nothing, fixing nothing, just staying.

The stone woke.

Light ran through the fragment like dawn through frost—not the white hunger of the fire-pikes but a deep amber, warm, salt-colored, the color of the lamps in the guild hall on census nights. The ward-line flared along its whole broken length, a heartbeat of silver visible from ridge to ridge, and every warden on the field staggered as if struck. Hazel felt them feel it: the old seal, the old refusal, the valley saying *no* in the voices of everyone who had ever kept it.

But the stone was broken. She couldn't rebuild what wasn't there. What she could do—what her hands were already doing, guided by voices older than her family's lies—was weave the surviving fragments together, sing the shards into each other, mend the wound the way you mend a pot: not invisibly, but strongly. Gold in the cracks. Truth in the breaks.

"You're doing it," Tam said. He had come down the slope somehow and was crouched over her, an arrow nocked, turning slowly to watch her back. "Hazel. You're *doing it.*"

"I need time," she managed. The words cost her; singing and speaking pulled from the same well, and the well was draining. "However much time you can steal. I need—I need until dawn, maybe. Less if the mountain helps."

"The mountain," Tam said, "can bill us later." He stood, drew, and put an arrow through the eye-slit of a warden advancing with a sapper's charge. "CORREN!" he bellowed. "SHE NEEDS UNTIL DAWN!"

What Corren Ashe answered, Hazel didn't hear. She was too deep in the song by then—deeper than the field, deeper than the battle, down where the ward-line met the roots of the mountain and the mountain hummed its one long note. She learned things there she had no time to fear. She learned that her grandmother's voice was in the song, and her great-grandmother's, and that the crossed-out names on the stair below the mountain were not crossed out at all but *woven*, every guild member who had ever lived still holding their inch of the line. She learned that the Hollow King's breathing was faintly audible even here, even now, a slow tidal pull underneath everything, like the sea heard through a shell.

And she learned that the ward she was rebuilding was not the same ward her ancestors had built. Theirs had been a wall. Hers, mended from broken pieces by a girl who had never fit the mold, was becoming something else—something with gaps in it, seams, places where the world outside could press through and be felt. Not a prison wall.

A membrane. A skin. Something alive.

A fire-pike discharge lit the whole ridge white. She heard Tam grunt, heard him go down on one knee, heard him swear with tremendous creativity and keep shooting. Boots on frost, closer now. The warden officer's voice, clear and cold: *"Push them off the stone. Take the singer alive."*

*Alive.* The word slid into her like ice. They wanted her alive. Which meant they knew what she was doing, which meant the Court understood the ward-line better than her own guild ever had—

An arrow hissed past her ear. Hands seized her shoulders—Serah Oduya, ash-streaked, her Court uniform torn at the sleeve where something had very nearly found her, her dark face furious in the amber light. "Get *down*—"

"I can't stop." Hazel's lips were cracked, her voice a ruin. "If I stop, the seam closes and they take the gap."

"Then sing," Serah said, "and I'll hold your line," and she turned her back to Hazel and raised her blade against the advancing wardens, the traitor-warden defending the daughter of the guild that had imprisoned her masters' prisoner, and if there was a stranger image in the world that night Hazel couldn't imagine it.

Time went strange. She sang. The stone grew, shard knitting to shard, the amber light climbing. Men died near her—she heard it, smelled it, could do nothing, would not stop. Once, through the tangle of the song, she felt Ilsa's presence flare somewhere far to the south, at the guild hall, felt the old woman pouring her own failing voice into the distant stones to hold the line while her granddaughter held this one, and she sang harder, grief and gratitude indistinguishable.

Dawn was maybe an hour off when the ward sealed.

It happened all at once, the way a knot slips home. The final shard fused, the amber light steadied, and the ninth stone stood rebuilt—ugly, seamed with gold-bright cracks, half its original height, nothing like what it had been—but standing. Whole enough. The ward-line locked from ridge to ridge, and the Ashen vanguard, mid-charge, hit it like gulls hitting glass.

They didn't fall. But they stopped. And in the sudden ringing quiet, Captain Andrelle's voice carried down the flats, level and unhurried, ordering her wardens to hold.

Hazel collapsed forward against the mended stone. It was warm under her cheek, warm as a living thing, and through her ruined hearing she could feel it humming—not a wall's hum. A door's.

"We bought the hours," Tam rasped somewhere above her, laughing breathlessly, bleeding from somewhere Hazel would later dress herself. "We actually stole the—"

"Hold," Captain Andrelle said again, closer now, walking alone into the space between the armies with her gauntlets raised, empty-handed, her fire-pike grounded behind her. Her eyes found Hazel across the smoking field, across the amber-lit stone, and there was no defeat in them at all.

Only patience.

"Wardwright," the captain called, and the title landed on Hazel like a mantle she hadn't asked for. "My orders say breach your door. But orders are written by people who've never stood where you're standing." She stopped ten paces from the new-made ward, close enough that the amber light touched her ash-grey armor, and inclined her head. "I'm asking for parley. Before either of us does something the stars won't forgive."

* * *

Hazel pushed herself upright against the warm stone. Every part of her hurt; her voice was stripped down to gravel; she could still taste the song at the back of her throat like metal. Tam gripped her arm, Serah stood tense at her shoulder, and out on the flats sixty wardens waited on their captain's word.

"She's buying time too," Serah murmured. "Or feeling for weakness in what you built. Be careful."

"I know." Hazel stepped forward to the edge of the ward, into the amber light, and looked at the enemy commander across ten feet of mended air. "Captain Andrelle. You have your parley. Start talking."

The captain smiled without warmth, and behind her, far down the darkened road, Hazel saw the second column of the Ashen Court emerging from the fog—banners, siege-gear, and hundreds more.

"Andrelle was only ever the vanguard," Serah breathed.
