## 16. Ilsa's Last Song

The stair beneath the mountain had no business being warm.

Hazel felt it through the soles of her boots as she took the steps two at a time, the guild's oldest key-ring heavy against her hip, its iron teeth biting into her palm where she'd wrapped them in a strip of her own sleeve. Behind her came Tam's ragged breathing and the scrape of Serah's armor against stone older than any nation the Ashen Court claimed to remember. Above them—far above, past the sealed doors of the guild hall, past the market town with its shuttered windows—the night was burning.

She could hear it even here, muffled by a hundred yards of granite: the crackle of fire-pikes discharging in volleys, the deep organ-note of ward-stone shattering, the screams that needed no translation. The Battle of the Boundary had moved. The Court's vanguard had broken through the eastern line an hour ago, and now they were inside the valley itself, pushing toward the mountain like water finding the lowest ground.

"They'll go for the door," Hazel said, not slowing. "The captain won't waste time on the town when the prize is under our feet."

"Then we'd best not be standing in front of it admiring the view," Tam said. His voice was steady but his hands weren't; she could see them trembling on his spear-shaft, and she loved him for the honesty of it.

Serah said nothing. She had said almost nothing since the boundary, since her negotiation with the warden-captain had bought them hours instead of mercy. Her face was ash-grey in the salt-light of the stair-lamps, and every few steps she touched the hilt of her sword as if confirming it still existed. A woman who had spent her life believing in the machinery of empire, watching its gears turn over the people she'd come to warn. Hazel did not envy her. She was not sure she pitied her either. There wasn't time for anything but the next step, and the one after that.

They were three hundred stairs down when the sound above changed.

It was Ilsa who heard it first—or rather, it was Ilsa who made the sound change, because Guildmother Ilsa Nakamura had not been waiting at the top of the stair like a sensible woman. She had come down to meet them, alone, with the guild's ceremonial staff in her hands and blood already drying at her temple, and she had chosen the narrow place where the stair turned twice upon itself and widened into the little antechamber where generations of Saltwrights had paused to catch their breath before descending into the dark.

The antechamber was carved with names. Of course it was. Everything down here was carved with names.

"Grandmother—" Hazel's voice cracked on the word.

"Stop." Ilsa raised one hand, and even now, even bleeding, the gesture carried three hundred years of guild authority behind it. "Save your breath for running. They're coming."

And they were. Hazel could hear boots now—not the disciplined cadence of warden columns but the scrambling pace of sappers, of men sent ahead with drills and charges, and behind them something heavier, something that struck the stone in slow, deliberate strokes like a bell being rung from inside.

"The door," Ilsa said. "They mean to breach it tonight. Not bind it—breach it. I heard the captain give the order." Her mouth twisted. "The Court never intended to seal anything. They intend to wake it and put a leash on it, and there is no leash, child. There has never been a leash. There is only the song, and the ones willing to sing it."

"You can't fight them," Tam said. "Guildmother, you're—"

"I am the last Saltwright who knows all seven verses." Ilsa smiled, and it was the same smile she'd worn at every census, every festival, every moment when the truth would have cost more than the family could bear. "I have been the last for twenty years. Do you know what that does to a woman? It makes her very careful about which battles are worth losing."

* * *

They came up out of the dark six minutes later, and the antechamber became a battlefield the way a candle becomes a conflagration.

There were nine of them—Court sappers in grey lacquered mail, faces hidden behind ash-smeared visors, and their sergeant carried a fire-pike whose head burned the color of dying coals. They had expected an empty stair. They had not expected an old woman standing in the narrows with a staff of black salt-iron planted like a fencepost, nor the girl beside her with her hand pressed flat against the wall, nor the strange harmonic hum rising out of the stone itself as if the mountain had cleared its throat.

Hazel hadn't meant to touch the wall. It happened the way breathing happens. The stair was lined with names—every guild member ever born, carved and crossed out—and beneath her palm the stone was singing, low and endless, the song of salt and patience, and without deciding to she added her voice to it.

The first volley of fire broke against the air of the antechamber like a wave against a cliff.

Not perfectly. Nothing was perfect. The heat washed over them and the light seared their shadows onto the walls among the names, and Ilsa grunted as if struck, but the flame did not pass. It curled around the space where they stood and guttered and died, and the sergeant shouted something about the wards, about the door below, about *the Hollow King's leash is in reach*, and then he stopped shouting because Ilsa Nakamura moved faster than a woman of seventy had any right to move.

What followed was not a duel. Duels require two people who want to win gracefully. What followed was an old woman spending everything she had left in the narrowest place she could find, buying seconds with years, and Hazel understood—with a clarity that would haunt her the rest of her life—that this was exactly what her grandmother had come down the stair to do.

"Go," Ilsa snarled, staff whirling, catching a sapper's blade and turning it into the throat of his companion. "All three of you. Down. Now."

"We're not leaving you!" Tam drove his spear past her shoulder, felling a man who'd circled wide.

"You are. That's an order from your Guildmother, boy, and it's the last one I'll ever—" She staggered. A pike-butt had caught her across the ribs; Hazel heard the crack of bone from ten feet away and felt it in her own chest like a sympathetic fracture. Still the old woman stood. Still the salt-song held, fraying now at its edges, the mountain humming a note that wavered like a singer running out of breath.

Serah dragged Tam backward. Hazel resisted a heartbeat longer—Ilsa's eyes found hers across the chaos, fierce and wet and unbearably calm—and then the sergeant leveled his fire-pike point-blank at the Guildmother's chest, and Hazel's grandmother did the one thing Hazel had never seen her do.

She stepped aside. Deliberately. Opening the path downward.

*Let them through,* that look said. *But not yet. Not until you have what you need.*

Hazel ran.

* * *

The last flight of stairs ended at the threshold chamber, and the threshold chamber was nothing like Hazel had imagined through all the years of half-guessed secrets. It was round and small and terribly quiet, lit by veins of pale salt-light woven through the ceiling like frozen lightning, and at its center stood the seam.

She had no time to study it. No time to be awed or horrified or any of the things she knew she would feel later, in the long afterward, when feeling became survivable again. Behind her, up the stair, the sounds of battle had gone silent in the specific terrible way that silence follows a single discharge of fire.

*Ilsa.*

Hazel was moving back up the stairs before she'd decided to. Tam caught her arm; she shook him off; Serah didn't try at all, because Serah had heard it too, and soldiers know what that silence means.

They found her at the turn of the stair, propped against the wall of names, her staff lying broken beside her. The sergeant lay crumpled at the bottom of the flight, and two sappers with him, and the rest had evidently decided that whatever waited below was someone else's problem tonight—or perhaps Ilsa's last stand had simply taken longer than their orders allowed. Blood spread beneath her grandmother in a slow black tide, soaking into the mortar between stones carved with names going back three centuries. Nakamura. Voss. Oduya—no, not Oduya, that name was newer than the wall, but there were others, dozens of others, whole generations crossed out in careful guild script.

Ilsa's eyes opened when Hazel knelt.

"There you are," she said. Her voice was a ruin, but the dry humor in it was intact, damnable and beloved. "Always were a stubborn girl. Couldn't hear the salt-songs, couldn't follow the simplest instruction—"

"Don't." Hazel pressed her hands against the wound and felt how much blood was already outside the body, how much had already finished being her grandmother. "Don't talk. We'll get you up. We'll—"

"Hazel." Ilsa's hand rose—shaking, spotted, impossibly familiar—and closed around Hazel's wrist. Not to push her away. To hold her there. "Listen to me. For once in your life, listen without arguing, because I haven't got the time left to lose to it."

Tam crouched on Ilsa's other side. Serah stood watch on the stairs above, sword drawn, face turned away in a courtesy that was also a vigil. The mountain hummed around them. Below, faintly, through the seam at the bottom of the world, something breathed.

"The founder's song," Ilsa said. "Seven verses. You've known six since you were nine—you thought you learned them at your mother's knee, but I chose every lesson, every phrase, every silence. The sixth verse ends unresolved. Every Saltwright who ever sang it felt that ending like a missing tooth. Did you never wonder why?"

"It resolves in the seventh," Hazel whispered. "You told us the seventh was lost."

"And you believed me, because believing me was easier than doubting me, and I counted on that. God forgive me, I counted on it my whole life." Ilsa coughed, and the cough cost her something irreplaceable. "The seventh verse isn't lost. It was never lost. It's the lock, child—the true lock, the one the wards only imitate. And it cannot be sung by anyone who wants the family name more than the truth."

Hazel went very still.

"I tried for sixty years." Ilsa's laugh was a thread of sound, barely there. "Sixty years, on my knees at that seam, and it wouldn't come. Because every time I opened my mouth to sing it, I heard my mother's voice telling me what we were, what we owed, what the valley must never learn—and I wanted her right. I wanted the story to be true. Refuge. Sanctuary. Three hundred years of noble sacrifice." Her grip tightened on Hazel's wrist. "The seventh verse doesn't care what I wanted. It asks a question, and it knows a lie in the answer before the note leaves your throat. It requires wanting the truth more than the name. More than the guild. More than me."

"Grandmother—"

"I'm giving it to you." And now the fierceness cracked, and underneath it was something young and frightened and old beyond reckoning, and Hazel realized she was seeing her grandmother's face as it must have looked at nine years old, learning that her whole inheritance was a locked room. "Freely. No conditions. No *after you're gone*. That's the only way it passes—it has to be given, not kept. Do you understand? That's why it's been dying with us, generation after generation. We handed it down like property, and property can't sing."

Ilsa pulled Hazel close. Her breath was shallow and fast, each word purchased separately.

"*Salt remembers what the river forgets,*" she whispered, and the words fell into Hazel like stones into deep water. "*Wolf runs the border that the shepherd set. What we buried, we buried sleeping—*" She faltered. Her eyes searched Hazel's face with sudden desperation. "Say it back."

"*Salt remembers what the river forgets.* Wolf runs the border that the shepherd set. What we buried, we buried sleeping—"

"And the rest. The rest, girl, I can't—" Ilsa's voice failed. She swallowed, rallied, and gave Hazel four more lines in a whisper that thinned with every syllable, lines about hunger and winter and a king who ate the sky, lines about a debt unpaid and a door that opens inward, and then she stopped, mid-line, her lips still shaping a word that made no sound.

Because the last line was unfinished. Had always been unfinished. Hazel saw it land in her grandmother's expression like a key turning: the founder herself had left the final line open, waiting, three hundred years, for someone who could want the truth enough to end the song themselves.

"No," Hazel breathed. "No, you don't understand, I need the last line, I need—"

"You need nothing." Ilsa's hand slid from Hazel's wrist to her cheek, and her palm was cold. "You need nothing you don't already have. That's the whole cursed lesson, child. That's what I couldn't teach you because I never learned it myself. You've been enough since the day you were born and couldn't hear the songs—especially then. Especially then." A ghost of a smile. "You weren't deaf, my darling. You were hearing it raw. All of it. Unfiltered. The songs nearly drowned you, so you built a wall against them, and I let you believe you were broken because it was easier than believing you were more."

The mountain hummed. Below, the thing behind the seam breathed in, and breathed out, patient as winter.

"Ilsa." Serah had come down two steps, sword lowered, and her voice was stripped of all its court formality. "The sappers will return with the main column. You have minutes."

"I know what I have." Ilsa's eyes stayed on Hazel. "I've had twenty years to practice knowing." She drew a breath that rattled like wind through empty rooms. "Three things. Then you go down, and you don't look back, and you don't bury me until the stars come home. Swear it."

"I swear."

"One. Your mother's bloodline goes back to the founders—both lines, all the way down. That's why the songs hurt you. That's why you were never wrong, only too much."

Hazel's vision blurred. "Two?"

"Two. Corren Ashe is in the mountain. I smelled the smoke of his torches in the upper galleries—he came down while we fought. Whatever he means to do at that seam, he means to do it fast." Her jaw tightened around a history neither of them would name aloud, the grief of twenty years compressed into a single exhale. "He is not evil. He is worse—he is certain. Stop him without hating him if you can."

"And three?"

Ilsa Nakamura, Guildmother of the Saltwright Guild, keeper of the valley's longest lie, looked up at her granddaughter with the stars already going out behind her eyes, and spent her last breath on the simplest and hardest sentence of her long guarded life:

"Three. I was proud of you every day. I just couldn't afford to say it, because saying true things is how the song starts, and I wasn't brave enough to finish it." Her thumb traced once, feather-light, along Hazel's cheekbone. "Finish it for me."

Her hand fell.

The mountain did not stop humming. That was the unbearable part, Hazel thought distantly, kneeling in her grandmother's spreading blood while the salt-light veined overhead pulsed like a slow heartbeat. The world did not pause. The names on the walls did not weep. Somewhere below, the seam breathed in, and breathed out, and waited with a patience older than the guild, older than the lie, older than the first name cut into this stone by hands that had stolen something and buried it and called the burial sanctuary.

Tam's hand settled on her shoulder. He said nothing. There was nothing in any language worth saying.

Serah looked up the stair, then down, then at Hazel, and her voice came out quiet and level. "The sappers will be back within the hour, with the whole vanguard behind them. And Ashe may already be at the bottom." A pause. "We have to move."

Hazel closed her grandmother's eyes. Then she stood, wiped her hands on her ruined sleeves, and touched the key-ring at her hip—the oldest keys, the true keys, suddenly weightless in a way they had never been.

The seventh verse sat in her chest, unfinished, waiting for an ending only she could write.

"Down," Hazel said, and led them into the dark.
