## 17. Corren's Choice

Tam Voss had spent his whole life believing the mountain was solid.

It was a childish thing to believe, maybe, in a valley where the stars had just been handed back like coins returned by a thief with a guilty conscience, but the belief ran deep as root. The mountain was the floor beneath the world. You built on it. You buried your dead in it. You did not imagine it *ending*.

Now he stood in the dark beneath all of it, listening to Corren Ashe give orders to sappers, and the mountain felt thin as an eggshell around him.

The stair behind them was still littered with the aftermath of the battle—scorch marks where the warden-captain's fire-pikes had chewed into stone, the broken haft of Ilsa's staff lying where it had fallen, and darker stains that Tam made himself look at because Hazel had looked at them and he would not be less brave than she was. They had come down this stair two hours ago, three of them against a company, and they had won nothing except time. Time for Hazel. Time for whatever she was doing at the bottom of the world right now, past the seam, past everything.

And then Corren had taken that time and turned it into a fuse.

"Charge goes there." His voice carried down the gallery, flat and certain, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this speech for twenty years in exile. "And there. And there. Three points along the fault line, set deep. When they blow, the whole crown of the mountain comes down through the gallery, through the chamber, through whatever's left of that door. Nothing survives being buried under ten thousand tons of living rock. Not kings. Not gods."

Tam couldn't see his face. Torches guttered at intervals down the corridor, and Corren stood between them, half silhouette, half scarred old man. The Court sappers worked around him with the mechanical efficiency of people who had done terrible things before and expected to do them again. Kneeling at the walls, boring their augers, packing black clay charges into seams in the stone. One of them hummed while he worked. That was the worst part. The humming.

"You can't," Tam said, and his voice came out smaller than he wanted. He cleared his throat. "You can't do this."

Corren didn't turn around. "Go up, boy. Take the girl with you when she's finished playing prophet. In twenty minutes none of this will matter, and you'll want to be well clear of the collapse line."

"She's not *playing*—"

"I know what she's doing." Now Corren turned, and the torchlight caught the ruin of his face—the burn scars, the eye gone white as salt from some old wound, the mouth that seemed carved out of disapproval. Twenty years ago he had been the guild's brightest son, Ilsa's protégé, the man everyone said would be Guildfather someday. Then he'd walked into the night and become a story mothers used to frighten children. *Corren Ashe will get you if you wander past the stones.* Tam had grown up on those stories. He had never once imagined the monster would have hands that shook.

"Then you know she's *talking* to it," Tam pressed. "Right now. Down there. She went through the seam to—to witness it, she said. To offer it something. If you bring the mountain down while she's—"

"While she's what? Negotiating with winter?" Something moved across Corren's ruined face—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one, gone bad. "I've read the Court's archives, boy. I've read things Ilsa never let anyone in this valley see. Do you know what the Hollow King is? It's hunger with a crown. It ate the stars once already—that's why your sky was black for three hundred years. Your guild didn't seal it out of virtue. They sealed it because it was the only thing they could think to do, and then they lied about it so long they forgot they were lying." He gestured at the wall, at the charges, at the whole patient architecture of destruction. "Kill it. Kill it now, while it's still muzzled and starving and weak. That's mercy. For everyone. Including her."

*Including her.* Tam heard the crack in it, the hairline fracture running through the certainty. He thought of the ledger in Ilsa's study, the true charter, the names. He thought of what Serah had told them by firelight after the battle: *the Court knew. They always knew. They let the wards rot because desperation delivers doors.*

He thought of something else too. Something he hadn't told anyone.

"The names," Tam said.

"What?"

"On the stair. Every guild member ever born, carved into the walls. Crossed out when they died." He took a step forward, into the torchlight, making Corren look at him. "I counted them on the way down. Did you? Four thousand and some. Four thousand names, Corren. And every single one of them spent their life carrying salt up and down these stairs, singing songs they didn't understand, guarding a door they were never told the truth about. They gave everything. Their hearing, their marriages, their years—they gave it all to a lie, but they gave it, and it *held*. Three hundred years, that hunger stayed down here because of them."

"It held because they were *deceived*—"

"It held!" Tam's voice cracked up the gallery, and the humming sapper stopped. "That's what I'm saying. Whatever you feel about the lie—and believe me, I'm angrier about the lie than you are, my whole family's been salt-broke for six generations serving it—but the *holding* was real. The holding cost real people real lives, and it worked. And you want to stand on their graves and pull it all down, and you haven't even asked whether pulling it down sets the thing free instead of killing it."

Corren went very still.

Tam hadn't meant to say that last part yet. It had been sitting in him since Serah laid out the archives, since the word *Hollow King* first crossed her lips, and he had turned it over and over like a stone with a dangerous underside. But now it was said, and he watched it land, watched it go into Corren Ashe like water into a cracked foundation.

"Destruction might free it," Tam said, quieter. "That's what the Court's own records imply. A bound thing dies when its binding breaks gently, over ages. But a bound thing that gets its prison *collapsed on its head*—" He shrugged, helpless. "Nobody knows. Nobody's ever tried. You'd be running the only experiment in history, and the lab would be everyone you've ever met, and the subject would be the thing that ate the sky."

For a long moment the only sound was torchfire and the distant, rhythmic breathing of the mountain itself—or what Tam had thought was the mountain's breathing, and now understood might be something else entirely, faint and vast, three floors down.

Then Corren laughed. It was not a kind sound.

"You think I haven't considered it?" He came close, and Tam made himself hold ground, though every animal instinct in him wanted to retreat from that white eye. "I have lain awake for twenty years turning every option over like cards. Guard it, seal it, worship it, starve it. And do you know what I keep coming back to?" His voice dropped. "I keep coming back to my sister."

Tam blinked. "Your—"

"Maren Ashe. Third name on the stair, east wall, near the bottom where the oldest ones are." Corren's gaze slid past Tam's shoulder, toward the stairwell, as if he could see through stone. "She died at nineteen. Ward fever, they called it—standing watch on the boundary in winter until the cold got into her blood and her heart simply stopped wanting to beat. Nineteen years old. And do you know what her last letter home said?" His jaw worked. "*Tell Corren the songs are beautiful tonight. Tell him it's worth it.*"

The torches popped. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, the town bells were still ringing the alarm that had started days ago and never properly stopped.

"She believed in it," Corren said. "All of it. The refuge, the sacred duty, the songs. She died happy, Tam Voss. Died happy for a lie, in the service of a prison, protecting the thing that stole her sky. And when I found out—if you tell me her death meant nothing, that the holding was *real* and therefore *worth it*, then you're telling me my sister's corpse is a fair price for three hundred years of darkness. I am not prepared to pay that price anymore. I paid it once. Never again."

There it was. Tam stood in the heat of it, the grief weaponized into certainty, and understood suddenly why Ilsa had flinched at this man's name for twenty years. Not guilt. Grief. The same wound, worn on opposite bodies. Two people who had loved each other once, both trying to honor the same dead girl in ways that could not coexist.

"Hazel's down there right now," Tam said slowly, "doing exactly what you're doing."

"Don't you dare compare—"

"Exactly what you're doing." Tam stepped closer. "Looking at everything the lie cost, and refusing to pay it again. She just chose differently. She's not down there to guard it or kill it. She's down there to *tell it the truth*. All of it. Every lie, named out loud, to its face. Because she thinks—I think she's right—that three hundred years of silence is what made it a king. Things kept in the dark and never witnessed don't stay prisoners, Corren. They stay hungry. Maybe what it needs isn't a muzzle or a mountain. Maybe it needs someone to finally say: we did this to you, and it was wrong, and here is the truth you were owed."

"And if it eats her for her trouble?"

"Then I go down after her," Tam said, and was surprised to find he meant it without any performance of bravery at all. It was simply arithmetic. "And if it eats me, then Serah. And if it eats all of us, then yes—blow your mountain, and may the dark take us all kindly. But you don't get to skip to the end, Corren. You don't get to bury the experiment before the experiment has spoken. Not while she's still down there. Not while there's a chance."

Corren stared at him. Behind him, the sappers had stopped working entirely; they stood among their half-set charges like men waiting to learn whose war this actually was.

"You're nineteen?" Corren said at last. "Twenty?"

"Twenty-one."

"Gods help the valley." But something in his voice had shifted—some load-bearing certainty groaning under new weight. He turned away, paced three steps, turned back. "If I'm wrong about this—if that thing walks out of here wearing her face, or worse, wearing *no* face—then everything I've burned my life down for becomes the reason the world ends. Do you understand what you're asking me to sit with? The possibility that Maren died for a prison that should have been a confession? That I burned half of Ashfall for nothing?"

"I think," Tam said, "that's exactly what Hazel's asking it to do too. Sit with everything. Name it. Choose anyway."

Down the gallery, past the sappers, the air changed.

Tam felt it before he understood it—a pressure shift, a warmth moving through stone that had been cold since before his grandmother's grandmother. The torch flames all leaned the same direction at once, toward the depths, like iron filings finding north. And underneath everything, rising through the floor, through his boot-soles, through his teeth: a sound. Not the Hollow King's breathing. Singing. Thin and cracked and human, one voice alone in the dark below, singing a verse no living Saltwright had ever sung complete.

Hazel.

Corren's hand closed around a charge-detonator hanging at his belt—an iron plunger, primitive and absolute. His knuckles were white around it. His ruined face worked through a lifetime of calculations in the space of four seconds, and Tam watched, heart hammering, unable to do anything but bear witness to the choice taking shape in him.

Then Corren Ashe pulled the detonator free of its coupling, dropped it on the stone floor, and ground it to pieces under his heel.

"Sappers," he said hoarsely. "Rig those charges to seal the *stairwell entrance* instead. Collapse-proof it. If that thing comes up, I want the way behind that girl barricaded, not buried." He looked at Tam, and for one unguarded moment the white eye and the dark one held the same expression: terror, freely admitted. "If she fails, boy, I'll need you to remember I gave her the chance. Someone has to carve it somewhere. Even if it's just on a wall."

"Come carve it yourself," Tam said. "We're going down."

* * *

They went down together—all of them, even the sappers, who abandoned their augers with the relief of men released from a sin halfway committed. The stairwell walls rose around them, name after carved name after crossed-out name, four thousand small testaments flickering past in the torchlight, and Tam touched two of them as he passed—Maren Ashe, because Corren had earned that; and Ilsa Nakamura, freshly cut, not yet crossed out, because no one had had the heart.

The singing grew louder. The warmth grew stronger.

And at the bottom, where the seam in the world hung open wide enough to walk through, the light coming out of it was not dark at all.
