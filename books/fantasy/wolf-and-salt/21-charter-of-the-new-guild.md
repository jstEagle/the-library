## 21. Charter of the New Guild

The ink was still wet on the third line when Hazel realized she had been holding her breath.

*Let it be known,* the line read, in her own careful hand, *that the Saltwright Guild keeps no secret from its members, and no member from the world.*

She set down the pen and looked at the sentence for a long time. It was such a small thing. Eleven words and a comma. Three hundred years of guild law filled eleven shelves in the archive below, every charter amendment sealed in wax and locked behind iron, and here was the whole of the new order reduced to a sentence a child could read. Her grandmother would have called it madness. Her grandmother had died calling it that, more or less, though the last thing she had done was hand her granddaughter a song instead of a silence, and Hazel had decided to take that as a blessing.

The guild hall had changed in the weeks since the stars came back. Someone—Tam, she suspected, though he denied it with the particular vigor that meant guilt—had pried the wax seals off the archive doors and left them in a bowl on the table like hard candies. The eleven shelves stood open now. Any guild member could read any record, and any person in the valley could become a guild member, and the only requirement written anywhere was that they show up. Nine members had become forty-one in a month. Farmers mostly, and millers, a midwife, two of the ferry-keeper's sons, an old man who had spent forty years poaching in the high pines and knew the ward-line better than any living Saltwright. None of them could hear the salt-songs. Hazel had stopped pretending that mattered.

She dipped the pen again. Outside the window, the morning was doing what mornings did now: arriving honestly. The light came up gold and unfiltered over the eastern ridge, and the stars' absence from the day sky no longer felt like a wound, because she knew they were there, behind the blue, patient as seeds. For three hundred years the valley had lived under a sky like a locked room. Now the room had a door, and the door had a doorkeeper, and the doorkeeper was her.

Her left hand ached faintly, the way it always did before weather. She flexed the fingers—translucent at the tips, always, like ice held up to a window—and let the ache pass through her and out. The bond sat in her chest the way a hearthfire sits in a house: warm, contained, hers. Most days she barely felt it. Most days.

* * *

Serah Oduya arrived at midmorning, riding a gray courser that had seen better decades, with a satchel of Court correspondence that had seen worse. She dismounted at the hall's new gate—there had been no gate before; the guild had never needed one, and the building of it had been Tam's idea and Corren's hammer—and stood a moment looking up at the facade as if checking it for cracks.

"You've changed the sign," she said.

Hazel followed her gaze. The old guild sign had been a circle of nine stones, closed. The new one, carved by a carpenter's daughter who had joined the guild in its second week, showed the same nine stones with a gap in the circle, and through the gap, a road.

"It seemed honest," Hazel said.

"It seemed like an invitation." Serah swung the satchel off her shoulder and set it on the table among the draft papers without ceremony. She had lost none of her warden's economy; she still moved like someone counting exits. But the drawn-blade wariness of the ford had softened into something Hazel might, cautiously, have called collegiality. "The Court's reply. You'll want to read it before I summarize it, because my summary will contain the word *chastened* and theirs will not."

Hazel broke the seal. The Ashen Court's letterhead had changed—she noticed that first. Where the old sigil had been a tower stamped in ash, the new one was the tower with its door drawn open, light spilling out. Someone in the Court's chancery had a sense of humor, or a sense of history, or both.

The letter itself was three pages of the most exquisitely careful language Hazel had ever read. It acknowledged, in its second paragraph, that the Court's wardens had acted "beyond the spirit of their mandate" at the Battle of the Boundary. It acknowledged, in its fourth, that the Court had possessed knowledge of the Hollow King's imprisonment "for a period exceeding the current captaincy." It did not apologize. Empires, Hazel was learning, did not apologize; they *recalibrated*. But the fifth paragraph offered something the old Court never would have: formal recognition of the Saltwright Guild as a sovereign custodial order, with the Court's liaison—herself, Serah wrote in the margin, in her own sharp hand—empowered to negotiate rather than merely observe.

"They're scared," Hazel said.

"They're embarrassed," Serah corrected. "Scared comes later, when the histories are written and someone asks why the most powerful institution on the continent spent three centuries sitting on a starving god and telling no one. My superiors would very much like your charter to include a clause about the Court's ongoing role in valley affairs. A generous one."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then they will spend a decade making your life difficult in small, deniable ways, and I will spend that decade standing between you, and we will both be very tired." Serah pulled out a chair and sat, uninvited, the way she had learned to in the weeks of negotiation—like someone who had decided this table was hers too. "So let's make it generous in ways that cost them nothing and bind them anyway. That's the warden's art, Hazel. Chains made of courtesy."

Hazel sat across from her and pulled the draft between them. "Then help me write a chain."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and it was strange how good it was, the two of them bent over the same page. Serah thought in clauses and Hazel thought in songs, and where the two overlapped the charter began to take on a double strength, like rope woven of different fibers. Open membership, ratified by no bloodline. Published records, copied quarterly and sent to the Court, to the market town's reading room, and—Hazel had insisted—to the ferry-keeper, who had asked, and whose asking had to mean something now. A standing council of seven, elected, no seat heritable. And at the center of it, the sentence she had written first, the one she kept coming back to:

*No member shall be bound by a secret inherited against their will.*

Serah read it twice. "You understand this makes your own grandmother a criminal under guild law."

"I know."

"Posthumously. For three hundred years of—"

"Of exactly what I'm doing right now, except in the dark instead of the light." Hazel said it evenly, and was surprised to find it didn't hurt the way she'd expected. The grief for Ilsa had settled in her over the weeks like sediment, no longer a wound but a layer, something she built on. "The charter isn't a punishment, Serah. It's a confession. The whole guild is signing a confession. That's the only way it means anything."

Serah was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The Court could never have written that sentence."

"No," Hazel agreed. "That's rather the point of us."

* * *

Corren Ashe came at dusk, as he seemed to do everything—arriving, leaving, forgiving, being forgiven—on his own schedule, at the edge of the light. Hazel found him at the boundary stones, because he was always at the boundary stones now, and the ninth stone most of all. He had rebuilt four of the nine already, and he built them differently than the old ones: each stone bore its maker's name, the date, and a plain account of what it was for. *This stone marks the ward-line. The ward is freely given. Ask any Saltwright why.*

The ninth stone he had left for last, and Hazel understood why without asking. It was the stone that had been hollowed out, the one that had been dying for decades while the guild sang its census songs above it. Corren had set the new core in place but not sealed it, and every evening he came and sat beside the open socket like a man sitting vigil over an unfinished sentence.

"You've been at the charter all day," he said, without turning. His voice had lost some of its ash over the weeks. Not all. Some things, Hazel suspected, were permanent weather in a man.

"Serah and I finished the fourth draft."

"Four drafts." He almost smiled. "Ilsa wrote hers in one afternoon and sealed it for a century."

"Ilsa's charter sealed the valley shut. Mine has a door in it."

That got him to turn. He looked at her the way he had begun to look at her lately—searching for Ilsa in her face and, finding her instead, being surprised by it every time. "Read me the part about the stones," he said.

Hazel didn't have the draft with her, but she didn't need it. "*The boundary stones shall bear true witness,*" she recited. "*No ward shall be laid without explanation given, and no stone shall stand whose purpose is not written upon it.*"

Corren was quiet a long moment. The light was going amber behind the western ridge, and the hollow socket of the ninth stone threw a long shadow across his knees.

"Ilsa and I used to sit here," he said at last. "Twenty years ago. Before I left. She'd bring the census ledger and I'd bring wine we were both too young for, and we'd argue about whether the wards were a kindness or a cage." He put his hand flat on the broken stone, on the honest new core. "I spent twenty years certain it was a cage. She spent her whole life certain it was a kindness. And the truth was—" he exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh— "the truth was it was a muzzle, and it was mercy, and it was a lie, all three at once, and neither of us could say so because saying so was forbidden."

"Is that why you're sealing it tonight?"

He looked down at the socket. "I wanted to wait until the charter was signed. Seemed right. The first stone laid under the new law." He glanced at her sidelong. "You could do it. If you wanted. The songs come to you now, when you touch the stone."

Hazel knelt and laid her palm against the ward-stone. The ache in her hand woke at once, and beneath it, faint as a pulse under skin, the salt-song rose—not the filtered, secondhand hum the old guild had taught its apprentices, but the whole of it, raw and layered, every voice of every Saltwright who had ever sung this line, including the ones who had lied, including the one who had finally told the truth. Her hand's fingertips went glass-clear against the stone, and the song came up through the translucence like light through ice.

She sang the ward in. It took four minutes. When it was done, the ninth stone stood whole and warm under her hand, and its purpose was carved on its face for anyone to read, and the ward-line ran nine stones strong from ridge to river, every link of it freely given.

Corren pressed his palm to the stone beside hers. "There," he said quietly. "That's the first honest thing this line has ever been."

* * *

Tam found her on the walk back, materializing out of the dusk the way he had done since they were six years old, except that now he carried a guild scribe's case instead of a slingshot, and he fell into step beside her with the air of a man about to be insufferable.

"So," he said. "Guildmother."

"I'm not Guildmother."

"You're drafting the charter. You sang the ninth stone. You negotiate with empires before breakfast." He counted the offenses on his fingers. "You have an office now, Hazel. It has a desk. There is a chair behind the desk that everyone else has silently agreed not to sit in. I have watched Marta the midwife stand for an entire meeting rather than approach the chair."

"It's a perfectly ordinary chair."

"It's a throne with a cushion, and you know it." He grinned at her, that old crooked grin from the ford and the ferry and a hundred summers of the ward-line, and beneath the teasing she could hear the thing he never said outright: that he had followed her down the stair beneath the mountain expecting to die, and had come back up, and had never once stopped being amazed by it. "You spent your whole life terrified of becoming Guildmother. Wrong kind of heir, no salt-song, hiding in your grandmother's shadow. And now look. You're everything you never wanted to be."

Hazel walked a few steps in silence. The first stars were coming out—real stars, the valley's own, unhidden—and she found them without searching, the way you find the faces of people you love.

"No," she said at last. "Ilsa was everything she never wanted to be, and she made it a cage. I'm everything I never wanted to be, and I'm trying to make it a door." She glanced at him. "Besides. The charter has no Guildmother. It has a First Speaker, elected annually, and I intend to lose the first vote on purpose."

"You'll win."

"I'll lose."

"You'll win, and then you'll spend a year insisting you didn't, and Marta will stand the whole time, and the chair will get a cushion." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Admit it. Some small, honest part of you likes the work."

Hazel opened her mouth to deny it, and found, to her considerable irritation, that she couldn't. She liked the work. She liked the long table with its forty-one members, the reading room full of farmers arguing over the old ledgers, the letters from distant villages asking—hesitantly, hopefully—whether the Saltwrights would teach *them* to keep wards. She liked being asked, instead of assumed. She had spent her whole life as the wrong kind of heir to a lie. She was, it turned out, exactly the right kind of founder of the truth.

"Fine," she said. "I like it. Tell no one."

"Your secret's safe with me," Tam said solemnly, "under the new guild law, which forbids inherited secrets but says nothing about earned ones."

* * *

They signed it three days later, in the guild hall, at the long table, with the doors open to the street so that anyone could come in and watch. Forty-one names, and then a forty-second—Serah's, signed as liaison with the Court's new open-door sigil beside it—and then a forty-third, Corren's, written in a hand that shook only a little. Hazel signed last. Under her name she wrote not a title but a sentence, the first one, the one she had started with:

*The guild keeps no secret from its members, and no member from the world.*

When the last signature was dry, Marta the midwife rose, stood a moment at the far end of the table—nowhere near the chair, out of a stubbornness Hazel had come to find endearing—and said, "There's a matter for the new council. First order of business, if we're having them."

"Name it," Hazel said.

Marta's face was grave in a way that made the room go still. "A rider came through the ford this morning. Not one of ours, not one of the Court's. A woman, half-frozen, asking for the guild by its new name—the one that's only been written down since today." She set a folded paper on the table, travel-worn, sealed with wax Hazel didn't recognize: dark blue, stamped with a sigil like a star falling into open water. "She carried this. And she said to tell the First Speaker—" Marta's mouth twitched at the title— "that the stars didn't come back everywhere. And that something else is awake now, on the far side of the water, because the door was opened."

The room was very quiet. Hazel looked at the seal, and at her forty-two family members looking back at her, and at the open doors behind them, through which the honest morning light was pouring.

"Well," she said, and reached for the letter. "We did say anyone could come in."
