## 1. The Child Who Spoke First

The rain came down on Greyhollow the way it always did in autumn — sideways, mean, and smelling of river rot. Hazel Voss had learned to love it, in a way. Rain kept people moving. Moving people didn't stop to study a charm-seller's face.

He'd been Voss for ten years now. Before that he'd been other names in other towns, each one worn like a coat until it frayed, then discarded. The trick was never to get attached. A name was a handle; anyone who knew your name could pick you up and shake you. So he sold luck-knots and fever-wards under a striped awning at the edge of Charmmarket Row, charged fair prices, haggled badly on purpose, and made sure his magic stayed buried deeper than any Warden's divining rod could reach.

The charms themselves were honest work, mostly. Knots tied with intent, salt sealed with a whispered breath that wasn't quite a word. Nothing the Wardens would call sorcery. Nothing that would light up their grey lanterns or bring the black-cloaks down the Row with their iron hooks. He'd been careful for a decade. Careful the way a man is careful when he knows exactly what's waiting if he isn't.

Business was thin. Rain thinned everything in Greyhollow except the gutters. Hazel was packing his last tray of knot-charms when the boy appeared at his stall.

No footsteps. No umbrella. Bare feet on wet cobblestone, toes blue with cold, and not one drop of rain touching him. Hazel noticed that last part first, because a charm-seller notices what doesn't happen. Then he noticed the rest: a boy of seven or eight, maybe nine, in a smock too big for him, hair dark as spilled ink, standing in the downpour inside a column of dry air like a hole cut in the world.

"Buy something," Hazel said, because that's what you said to loiterers. "Or move along."

The boy looked up. His eyes were wrong — not the color, which was ordinary brown, but the depth. Like looking down a well and seeing no bottom, just your own reflection floating somewhere far above.

"You're him," the boy said. And then, in a language Hazel hadn't heard spoken aloud in fifteen years — a language of stacked syllables and meanings that fit together like lockwork — the child said a word.

Hazel's scar burned.

It sat over his heart, old and pale, a brand he told curious customers was a cooking accident. It had been quiet for ten years. Now it lit like a coal breathed upon, heat spreading through his chest, down his arm, into his fingertips where the memory of sword-hilt calluses lived even though the calluses were long gone.

He gripped the stall's edge. Kept his voice level. Ten years of practice went into keeping it level.

"Don't know what you just said, lad. Wrong stall. Try the hedge-witch on Tanner's Lane."

"That's not true." The boy said it without malice, without triumph. Simply, the way you'd report the weather. "You know every word I say. You've known them since you were six years old."

* * *

Hazel closed the stall early. He told himself it was the rain. He told himself a lot of things while he walked home through Greyhollow's crooked streets, checking reflections in dark windows out of habit, taking the long way past the Warden post out of a different kind of habit — the kind that wanted to know where the wolves slept.

The boy followed him. Barefoot. Dry. Silent as snowfall.

Hazel lived above his shop on Candlemaker's Wynd, three rooms and a leaky roof, warded with charms so mild they barely counted as magic. He climbed the stairs, shut the door, threw the bolt, and turned around.

The boy was already sitting at his table.

"How—" Hazel stopped. Breathed. Started again. "There are wards on that door."

"They're very good ones." The boy said it like a compliment. "They let me through because I'm not a threat to you. They can only tell what you believe, and some part of you believes I belong here."

"I don't believe that."

"You do." The boy folded his oversized hands on the table, patient as a stone saint. "You believed it the moment my scar spoke to yours."

"I don't have a—"

"That's not true either."

The words landed like hooks. Hazel stood with his back against the door and felt ten years of careful construction creak around him. He thought about the knife under the floorboard by the hearth. He thought about the back window, the rooftops, the routes he'd memorized. He thought about how none of it would matter, because the thing sitting at his table hadn't broken in — it had been *let* in, by whatever treacherous corner of himself still remembered who he used to be.

"What are you?" Hazel asked.

"A question," the boy said. "That's what the First Tongue calls me. A question that hasn't been answered yet." He tilted his head. "You're supposed to be better at this than anyone alive. You're the last one."

"The last what?"

"Swordspeaker." The word came out in both languages at once, doubled like a bell struck in two rooms. The scar flared again, and this time Hazel couldn't hide the flinch. "You swore the oath when you were nineteen. You broke it when you were twenty-five. Both times, it wrote itself into you. Scars remember, Hazel. Even when their owners bury them."

"Voss," Hazel said automatically. "My name is Voss."

"No," the boy said gently, terribly, "it isn't. But it's a good name. You built a whole person to wear it. I can see all the stitching." He looked around the room — at the tray of half-finished knots, the kettle, the single chair, the narrow bed — cataloguing the small honest life the way an appraiser catalogs jewels. "You did good work. It almost holds."

"*Get out.*"

The boy didn't move. Outside, the rain intensified, drumming on the roof, running loud in the streets. Inside, the silence between them got heavier.

"I can't lie," the boy said. "Did you notice? Everything I say is true. That's what I am — truth that hasn't found its shape yet. So listen carefully, because I can't soften this." He unfolded his hands. "Something has eaten the king. Something old, something hungry, something that swallows oaths whole and wears the oath-taker like a glove. It's been feeding for years, and every vow sworn in this city pours into its mouth. Every loyalty oath. Every feast-day renewal. Every wedding vow, every guild pledge, every soldier's kneeling promise. Greyhollow swears more than any city in the realm, and the king swears most of all."

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. "That's madness talk."

"It's Tuesday," the boy said, "and it's true."

"And why—" Hazel heard his own voice drop, hoarse, already knowing the answer, dreading it. "Why come to me?"

"Because the door was opened from your side." For the first time, the boy looked away, and there was something almost like sorrow in the bottomless eyes. "Ten years ago, you broke an oath sworn in the First Tongue. Do you understand what that means? Oaths are walls, Hazel. Yours cracked. Things that eat oaths follow cracks the way water follows them. You didn't just break your own word. You showed it the way in."

The scar burned steady now, a low constant ache, like a hand pressed flat against his chest. Hazel slid down until he was sitting against the door, on the floor of his own home, in front of a barefoot child who hadn't existed five hours ago.

"I buried it," he said. "I gave it up. I've been nothing but harmless for ten years."

"Yes," the boy agreed. "And it found you anyway. On the first rainy day of autumn. Because it's time."

"Time for what?"

The boy stood. In the low lamplight, for just a moment, Hazel saw something flicker across the child's face — a grown man's exhaustion, a king's burden, there and gone like a fish under ice.

"To answer the question I am," the boy said. "Come to the palace at Oathfeast. Or don't. But if you don't, the Eater finishes digesting Aldric before winter, and then it won't need a city full of mouths to feed anymore. It'll want bigger things."

He walked to the window — through the wards again, through the glass, out into the rain, which still refused to touch him — and paused, half-dissolved into the dark.

"One more true thing," the boy said. "You asked what I am. I'm the piece of the king it couldn't swallow. And when I find my shape — when the question gets answered — I'll have to choose whose I am."

Then he was gone, and Hazel sat alone on his floor with his scar burning, listening to the rain, understanding with perfect terrible clarity that the life he'd spent ten years building had just been measured for a coffin.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He took out the knife, cleaned it, put it away. He took out nothing else, because there was nothing else left of who he'd been — he'd made sure of that, burned it, drowned it, scattered it across four provinces. Only the scar remained, and scars, the boy said, remember.

Near dawn, the rain finally stopped. Hazel stood at his window watching the light come up grey over Greyhollow's thousand crooked roofs, toward the palace hill where the king's banners hung motionless in the dead air.

Oathfeast was in nine days.

He caught himself mouthing the First Tongue syllable the boy had first spoken, feeling it shape against his teeth like a key fitting a lock he'd bricked over long ago. His reflection in the dark glass watched him do it, and for half a heartbeat Hazel would have sworn the reflection wore a different face — younger, harder, a swordspeaker's face with a name he'd buried under six feet of new identities.

The past had found him. It was barefoot, and ageless, and it couldn't lie.

And some traitorous ember under his breastbone, the one ten years of ash hadn't managed to smother, was — gods forgive him — glad.
