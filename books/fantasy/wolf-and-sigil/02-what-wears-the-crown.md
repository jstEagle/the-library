## 2. What Wears the Crown

They ran through the backways of Greyhollow while the Wardens' horns still echoed off the rooftops, and the boy kept pace without breathing hard, which was the first wrong thing Hazel let himself notice.

The second wrong thing was that the rain didn't touch him. It fell around the child like water around a stone in a stream — bending, never landing. Ten years in the charm trade had taught Hazel to spot a working at forty paces. This wasn't a working. This was something older, something that hadn't been cast so much as *decided*.

"Keep moving," Hazel said, hauling him down the fishmonger's stair into the undercrofts where the city's oldest streets had been buried and forgotten. His oath-scar was still burning along his forearm, a seam of heat under the skin like a wire pulled taut. "And don't say anything else in that language."

The boy looked up at him. In the dark of the croft his eyes caught light that wasn't there.

"You are Voss," he said. Not in the First Tongue this time. In plain Greyhollow common, flat as slate. "You swore you would not be."

Hazel's stomach dropped through the floor. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"That is a lie," the boy said, without accusation. Without anything. "You cannot lie near me. I am sorry. I did not make the rule."

* * *

The interlude — what the child remembered:

*A hall of white stone. A man kneeling on marble worn smooth by centuries of knees, and the man is young, and afraid, and wearing a crown that does not fit yet.*

*"By my blood and my breath and my name," the man says, "I swear the walls of this city will not fall while I live."*

*Something vast leans close to listen. Something vast has always been listening, since the first king spoke the first vow over the first foundation stone, but it has never heard an oath like this one — not sworn for gain, not sworn in fear. Sworn the way oaths were meant to be sworn, whole.*

*It begins to eat.*

* * *

Hazel barricaded them in a smuggler's cellar off Rope Street, the kind of place he'd used twice before when the Wardens got curious about a charm-seller whose charms worked too well. He lit no lamp. The boy didn't seem to need one.

"Start from the beginning," Hazel said. "What are you?"

"I am what was left." The boy folded his hands in his lap, precise as a little clerk. "When the hunger ate the king, there was a piece it could not swallow. Too small. Too true. It put me out, the way you spit out a seed."

"The king." Hazel laughed, and it came out ugly. "King Aldric. You're telling me you're — what, his son? His bastard?"

"I am Aldric," the boy said. "The part of him that remembers being Aldric."

The silence after that lasted long enough for Hazel to hear his own heartbeat slow down deliberately, the way it did before a fight. He sat on a crate that smelled of tar and salt and made himself think.

Ten years ago, he reminded himself, he'd walked away from all of this. He'd spoken the unbinding words, broken his oath to the Order of the Sigil, scarred his own arm with the severance rite, and come to Greyhollow to sell love-charms and luck-knots to people who would never know what they almost had. That was the bargain. The order died; he lived small. Nobody followed. Nobody remembered.

Except the past had just walked barefoot through the rain to his door and called him by a name he'd burned out of every record in the kingdom.

"Why me?" he asked. "Why my door? Why my name?"

The boy tilted his head. "Because your oath broke first."

"What?"

"Oaths hold each other up." The child said it simply, the way you'd explain rain to someone who'd never felt it. "Every vow sworn in this city leans on every other. Yours leaned on the king's. When yours snapped ten years ago—" he touched his own chest, right over the heart, exactly where Hazel's scar sat under his sleeve— "it opened a crack. Small. But the hunger had been waiting at the wall for a very long time, and a crack is a crack."

The cellar got very cold, or Hazel got very cold, which amounted to the same thing.

"You're saying I did this."

"I am saying you cannot lie near me," the boy replied. "So I will not ask you if you did."

* * *

He tried to run the arithmetic anyway, alone, later, while the boy slept — or performed sleeping — in the corner. Ten years ago: the Severance, the night Hazel Voss stood in the Sigil-house and renounced his oath-name rather than carry out an order he could not stomach. The order had wanted him to bind a village of oath-breakers into servitude. He had refused. He had spoken the counter-words. And the whole lattice of sworn power that held the Marches together had shivered like a struck bell.

He'd told himself the shiver passed. He'd told himself a lot of things.

Now the boy's account assembled itself in his head with horrible neatness. King Aldric, young and earnest, swearing the great oath at his coronation — *the walls will not fall while I live* — an oath so clean and total that the Oath-Eater, the old thing under the mountain, the hunger the First Speakers had bound with the First Tongue, finally found a crack wide enough. It had crawled in through Hazel's broken vow and latched onto the king's unbreakable one, and it had been feeding ever since. Slowly. Patiently. Wearing him.

That was why the king hadn't aged in ten years. That was why the royal decrees had grown strange, why taxes became tithes, tithes became tributes, tributes became vows — why every citizen of Greyhollow now renewed their loyalty oath at the Oathfeast each season, thousands of voices reciting words they thought were patriotism. They weren't feeding the crown.

They were feeding it.

"And nobody noticed," Hazel said to the dark, "because the one people who could notice all quit or died, and I was busy selling charms."

From the corner, without opening his eyes, the boy said: "There is a feast in six days."

Hazel went still. "I know. Everyone knows. The whole city turns out."

"He will renew the great oath himself, on the high dais, before everyone. As he must. As it makes him." The boy sat up. His eyes were steady and ancient and eight years old. "Each renewal makes the hollow bigger. This one will finish it. After the feast there will be nothing left of Aldric at all — only the hunger, wearing a crown, with a hundred thousand sworn tongues to eat whenever it likes."

"So we have six days."

"You have six days," the boy said. "I do not think I have any. Look."

He pushed back the sleeve of the too-big shirt Hazel had given him. Underneath, his forearm was going translucent at the wrist — not wounded, not scarred. *Unwritten.* Like text fading off a page.

"It is putting me back," the boy said. "Bit by bit. It could not digest me, so now it is trying again, slowly, from the edges. When it finishes, I will be gone, and he will be complete, and there will be no difference between them worth naming."

Hazel stared at the fading flesh and felt the old scar ignite along his own arm, answering, resonating, two ends of the same broken circuit.

"What stops it?" he asked quietly. "What kills a thing like that?"

"An unbinding," the boy said. "A counter-oath. The same rite you used to break your own vow — but spoken *into* it, not against it. Spoken at the moment the great oath is renewed, when the Eater is widest open." He paused, and for the first time the child sounded like a child. "But the rite requires a speaker's true name. Freely given. Fully spoken. The name is the key, and the key burns in the lock, and whoever gives it—" he made a small gesture, opening fingers, letting something invisible go— "gives everything. Every oath they ever swore unwinds. Everything they were by promise becomes nothing."

Hazel looked at his scarred arm. Ten years ago he'd given up half of it already — the order, the title, the name the Sigil-house had written into his bones. He'd kept only the ordinary man, Hazel Voss, charm-monger, coward, survivor.

Six days. One feast. One chance.

"There's a gate under the palace," he said slowly, thinking aloud. "Old servants' entrance from the river cisterns. I carried messages through it once, before I was anyone worth noticing." He met the boy's eyes. "Can you get us both to the dais?"

The translucent hand closed into a fist.

"I can get us anywhere in the palace," the child said, and smiled, and it was a young king's smile, worn on a drowned boy's face. "It remembers me. Even if no one else does."
