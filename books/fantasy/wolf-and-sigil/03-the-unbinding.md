## 3. The Unbinding

The palace gates stood open on Oathfeast night, because no one had ever needed to defend them from the inside.

Hazel went through them in a Warden's cloak he'd bought off a dying man three winters ago and never worn, the hood low, his charm-satchel heavy against his hip. Around him the city poured toward the feast like water finding its level — bakers with flour still in their knuckles, dockhands, widows, children hoisted onto shoulders so they could see the king's balcony. Every one of them carrying tonight's vow in their mouth, ready to be spoken.

*Long live Aldric. Long live the crown. My word is the kingdom's word.*

Ten thousand throats. Ten thousand small surrenders.

He'd spent two days teaching himself not to flinch at it. The child walked beside him under the borrowed cloak's hem, barefoot on cold flagstones, and every few steps Hazel felt him watching the crowd with that terrible open attention, as if each face were a page he was learning by heart.

"They'll forget," the boy said quietly. Not a question. The child couldn't lie, but he could be wrong, and Hazel had learned the difference.

"After tonight," Hazel said. "When the Eater starves. What it ate comes back. Maybe not whole. But back."

The boy nodded and said nothing more, and Hazel hated how much he wanted that promise to be true, and how little proof he had.

Inside, the great hall blazed with a thousand candles. The Oath-Eater sat on the throne wearing Aldric's face, and it wore him *well* — that was the horror of it. It smiled warmly. It blessed the bread. It leaned down to touch a child's head the way kings are painted doing, and the hollow behind its eyes went all the way down.

Hazel took his place among the courtiers with his heart hammering a swordspeaker's rhythm: steady, patient, waiting for the gap between beats. The child pressed close against his leg.

The herald cried the renewal. The hall knelt. And ten thousand voices outside the windows rose together like a tide coming in —

— and the thing on the throne opened its mouth and drank.

Hazel felt it pass over him, a pressure like deep water, a hunger vast enough to have a weather. The vows poured up out of the city and into the king's body and did not come out again. Somewhere in the dark beneath the palace, something older than the crown grew fat.

*Now,* said his own scar, burning through the skin of his wrist like a second pulse. *Now or never.*

* * *

The child had never seen a room full of people kneel before.

They looked, kneeling, like a field of wheat after rain — bent all one direction, patient, grown for someone else's harvest. He understood harvests. He was one. He had been cut away from the man called Aldric and left growing in the dark of a beggar's cart, and everything since had been learning what kind of fruit he was.

The man in the grey cloak was not kneeling.

The child watched him move through the bowed heads like a heron through reeds, silent, certain, and felt the words rise in himself unbidden — the First Tongue always rose when truth was near, like water finding a crack:

*"The oath-name is the door,"* he said softly, in the old language that made candle flames stand straighter. *"A name freely given opens what nothing else can."*

The man didn't turn. "I know."

"You will refuse me again."

"I'll refuse you again."

"You gave me soup." The child said it as argument, because it was one. "You gave me your coat. You taught me which charms were real. You cannot spend me. I am the only piece of him anyone has left, and I say he is worth more than you."

"And I say," Hazel murmured, sliding the satchel from his shoulder, drawing the first chalk line where no kneeling eye would find it, "that you're not a coin to be spent. That's the whole point of you. That's why he couldn't digest you. Because somewhere in Aldric there was a part that knew a person isn't a price."

The child fell silent, because it was true, and he could not argue with true things, only with men.

Above them the Eater finished drinking, sighed through Aldric's lungs with pleasure, and raised its stolen hand for the feast to begin.

* * *

It happened during the toast.

Wine raised, eyes lifted, every gaze in the hall aimed at the throne — and Hazel stepped out of the crowd's blind edge into the open floor, chalk circle already closing around his feet, the child beside him. He pulled back his hood.

Gasps. A Warden's shout. None of it mattered. In ten years Hazel Voss had learned exactly one lesson worth the cost: there is never a good time to tell the truth, so you may as well do it fast.

"Aldric!" he called, in the First Tongue, and the word went through the hall like a blade through silk. The candles guttered. The Eater's smile froze on its borrowed face. "*I speak against the binding. I speak the counter-oath. Come down and be unmade.*"

The thing wearing the king rose from the throne, and the mask slipped just enough. What looked out through Aldric's eyes had no color in it at all, only appetite, old and patient and suddenly very interested.

"*Little swordspeaker,*" it said, and its voice came from the walls, from the floor, from Hazel's own scarred wrist. "*Your order burned. Your brothers are ash. You hid your word in trinkets and called it living. And now you come to feed yourself to me at last?*"

"I come to take back what you stole."

"Then give me a name to open the door." The hunger smiled with the king's beautiful mouth. "Give me the child. His name is half a king's; it will serve. Or give me yours, and I will let the city keep breathing while I finish my meal."

And the child stepped forward.

He stood at the circle's edge in his too-big borrowed boots and looked up at Hazel with those ancient, unlying eyes, and spoke in the First Tongue, clear as a bell over water:

*"My name is freely given. Take it. Open the door. Put me back in the man if you must — but put the man back in the world."*

"No." Hazel's voice cracked on it.

"Yes." The child's chin lifted. "You said a person is not a price. So choose, swordspeaker. Choose which person. You cannot save us both with silence."

The Wardens were closing in. The Eater waited, licking Aldric's lips. The whole blazing hall held its breath.

And Hazel understood, finally, what his scar had been trying to tell him for ten years. An oath wasn't a chain. It was a door, and doors only lock from the inside.

He dropped to one knee, took the child's small cold hands in his own, and met the hunger's eyeless gaze.

"Not his," Hazel said. "Mine. Hear me, Eater. My name is **Hazel Voss**, last of the Order of the Broken Blade, and I give it freely — not to you, but to *him*. To the man you wear. Let it be the key. Let it burn the binding out of his flesh and starve you in the doorway of your own theft."

The scar on his wrist split open like a seam of light.

"*I swear it,*" Hazel said, and the First Tongue poured out of him in a torrent he no longer controlled, words he'd buried for a decade tearing free, his oath-name igniting syllable by syllable as fuel — and the Eater screamed with the king's throat, and the chalk circle blazed white, and the child watched the man who'd given him soup begin, gently, terribly, to come apart into light.

"Hazel—" the boy cried, reaching for him.

"Don't hold on," Hazel said, smiling, already half-gone, already lighter than he'd ever been. "Whatever happens next — don't hold on to *me*."

The light swallowed the throne, the hall, the screaming hunger, the kneeling world.

And then it went out.

* * *

Silence.

The child opened his eyes in darkness that smelled of smoke and spilled wine. The hall lay wrecked and empty, candles drowned in their own wax, the Wardens fled or felled. On the dais, crumpled beneath the fallen banner of the crown, a man breathed — raggedly, humanly, awake.

Aldric opened his eyes. They were his own again, and wet.

"The child—" the king rasped.

"Here." The boy climbed the dais steps, barefoot again, boots lost somewhere in the light, and stood looking down at the man whose remnant he was. For a long moment neither spoke.

"He gave his name for us," Aldric whispered. "Where is he?"

The child turned slowly, searching the ruined hall, the shadows, the corners where a man might be standing. The First Tongue rose in him, testing the air for truth, and found only absence — an absence shaped exactly like a person.

He looked down at his own hands, then at the king, and when he spoke, it was barely a whisper, and it was true, because he could not help it:

"He isn't anywhere. There's no one here at all."

Somewhere far below the palace, in the dark where the Eater had starved, something that had once been a name drifted loose in the dark — unclaimed, unbound, and beginning, very slowly, to remember itself.
