## 4. No One, Someone

The Eater did not scream when it died. It starved.

Hazel felt it go the way you feel a fever break — a slow unwinding in his own bones, as if the thing had been living there too all these years and he'd simply never noticed. Around the throne room, the Oathfeast crowd stood frozen mid-kneel, ten thousand murmured vows hanging unfinished in the air like moths with nowhere to land.

The words he'd spoken were still cooling on his tongue. *I am Hazel Voss, oath-bound, swordspeaker, last of the Ninefold Order.* His true name, given freely into the binding — and even as he said it, he'd felt it begin to slip, letters loosening from his mouth like wet rope.

The child knelt beside the king's body, small hands pressed flat against Aldric's sunken chest.

"Is it working?" Hazel managed. His voice sounded strange. Thinner.

The boy looked up. He couldn't lie — it wasn't in him — so what he said next landed like a stone dropped down a well. "You're going to forget who you are."

"I know."

"You're smiling."

"I know that too."

* * *

It took three days for the Eater to finish unravelling, and they spent them in the palace infirmary because no one stopped them. The Wardens who found Hazel standing over the collapsed king had looked at his face, at the boy, at the black threads of something vast and starving dissolving out of Aldric's mouth like smoke — and then, one by one, they had knelt. Not to Hazel. To the king, stirring under his own weight for the first time in years.

Aldric woke on the second day. He was smaller than his statues. That was the first thing everyone noticed and no one said aloud. The man who sat up in the infirmary bed had gray in his beard and confusion in his eyes and hands that shook until he gripped the blanket and made them stop.

He remembered everything the Eater had shown him and nothing it had done. He remembered swearing his great oath — *let my body be the wall between Greyhollow and the dark* — and he remembered, distantly, the decade of hunger that followed, though it came back to him like a story told by someone else.

The third day, he asked to see the child.

They met in the infirmary garden, among winter-bare rose bushes. The boy stood with his bare feet in the frost-rimed grass — he never seemed to feel cold — and Aldric looked at him for a long time before speaking.

"I know what you are," the king said. "The part of me I couldn't be spared. My — my self. What I gave up when I swore." His voice cracked on the arithmetic of it. "Ten years old. The age I was when I first dreamed of the crown."

The child considered this with terrible honesty. "I could come back to you. It would make you whole again."

"Yes," Aldric said. "Would it?"

"It would make you what you were. Not what you are now." The boy looked toward the window, where Hazel stood pretending to inspect the glass. "He lost everything to fix you. I don't think wholeness should cost other people."

Something moved across the king's face — grief, maybe, or the beginning of wisdom. "And if I ordered you? I'm still the king."

"You can't order someone to want to come home," the child said, gently. "That's not an oath anyone can bind."

Aldric laughed then — a rusty sound, unused, but real. He knelt, which no king does, and put his hand on the boy's head the way you bless a stranger you hope will become family.

"Then stay free," he said. "But visit. I have ten years of being myself to catch up on, and I'd like to do some of it in your company."

* * *

Hazel's name finished burning away on the fourth night.

He felt it happen at midnight, lying awake in the charm shop he'd rented for a decade and would now, presumably, abandon. The oath-name went first — *Hazel Voss, swordspeaker* — unraveling out of his memory like thread pulled from a hem. Then the order's teachings, the forms, the ninefold cadences. Then, crueler, the small things: his mother's face, which had been bound into the name; the village he'd been born in; the reason he'd sworn in the first place.

By dawn he lay in an unfamiliar bed in an unfamiliar body and knew only this: yesterday he had done something brave, and there was a child downstairs making tea badly, and outside the rain was falling on Greyhollow the way it always fell, indifferent and clean.

He went downstairs.

The boy looked up from three ruined cups of tea and said, "You don't remember me."

"No." Hazel's voice was steady. He was surprised by that. "Should I?"

"I'm the one who knocked on your door in the rain." A pause. The truth coming out of him like water finding its level. "You let me in. You always let people in, even when you were pretending not to. It's the only true thing left about you. Everything else burned."

Hazel sat down across from him. Outside, Greyhollow was waking — bells ringing wrong because the Oathfeast vows hadn't been renewed, people stepping into the streets unsure whether they were still subjects of anything. The city felt, for the first time in his memory, unbound. Terrified and free in equal measure.

"What do I call you?" he asked the boy.

"What do you want to call me?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind there is, now." The boy pushed a cup of tea toward him. It was terrible. Hazel drank it anyway. "We get to choose. Both of us. That's what you bought."

Hazel turned the cup in his hands. He searched himself for grief and found, instead, a strange lightness — the specific lightness of a man putting down a load so heavy he'd forgotten it was a load. He had been someone once, and it had nearly killed him twice: once when he kept the oath, once when he broke it. Whoever he was now would be built differently. Not carved. Grown.

"Tam," he said finally. "I'll call you Tam. It was my grandfather's name, I think. Or someone's. It feels right."

The boy tried it silently, testing the shape. Then he nodded once, decisive. "Tam."

* * *

They reopened the charm shop in spring.

Not charms, exactly — Tam couldn't lie, which made the wares honest in ways the market had never seen, and Hazel discovered that a man with no past makes an excellent listener. People came to tell him things. Farmers, widows, deserters from the Wardens, a Warden-captain who arrived one morning and wept into her tea without explaining why. He sold them nothing most days. They came anyway.

King Aldric sent letters. Short ones, awkward ones, signed *Aldric* with no title, because Tam had told him once that titles were just oaths you didn't have to mean. In the latest letter, folded open on the counter between them, he wrote:

*Greyhollow is learning to live unsworn. Some days it goes badly. Most days it goes strangely well. Come to the palace when you can — both of you. There is a chair here no one sits in anymore, and a table set for three.*

Hazel read it twice and set it aside.

Tam looked up from the ledger he was teaching himself to keep. "Are we going?"

"We just got here."

"We've been here four months."

"Then we just got here four months ago." But Hazel was already reaching for paper, because the truth was he missed the old man, and the truth was he was allowed to miss things now, and that still astonished him every morning like a coin found in a coat pocket.

He wrote back three lines: *We'll come before the rains. Tell your cook. And Aldric — thank you for the chair.*

He sealed it without a sigil. He had none to seal it with. For the first time in his life — in whatever life this was — every word he spoke was freely given, bound to nothing, promised to no one.

Except, he supposed, to the boy drying cups across the counter. And the old king setting a third place. And the captain who'd be back Thursday.

That, he decided, was enough of an oath for anyone.
