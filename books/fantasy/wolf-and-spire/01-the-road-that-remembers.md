## 1. The Road That Remembers

The fever came on the third night, and by dawn the child was burning like a coal in her mother's arms.

Xanthe crouched at the mouth of the cave, watching the light climb the eastern face of Thornfell. Frost silvered the scrub pine. Her breath hung in front of her in small white flags. Behind her, in the dark of the overhang, Maren rocked little Ivo against her chest, humming a lullaby in a language that was not supposed to exist anymore.

"Stop that," Xanthe said quietly.

Maren's humming broke off. "It soothes him."

"It marks you." She turned. "Vaelish songs get people killed. You know this."

"He is *dying*, swordspeaker." Maren's voice cracked on the title — she'd picked it up from the other families on the road, the ones who still whispered it like a coin they were ashamed to spend. "If he dies, let him die hearing something true."

*Something true.* Xanthe looked away, back to the mountains. Ten years since Vael had been unwritten. Not conquered — erased. The maps redrawn overnight in every chancery from Corvane to the sea. The records burned. The names struck from parish rolls. And stranger than any of it, the forgetting: neighbors who had stood beside Vaelish families at harvest festivals blinking slowly, like men waking, unable to say why their villages felt suddenly emptier. A country did not just fall. It had been *unmade*, deliberately, thoroughly — and everyone who escaped it had been running ever since from whatever force had done the unmaking.

She'd smuggled two hundred of them through these passes. Farmers, clerks, children. People with no papers because no country existed to issue them. She knew every goat track and deadfall between the lowland shrines and the free city of Haldmere beyond the mountains. She knew which springs froze, which ledges shed stone, which way the wind turned before snow.

She did not know how to fix this.

Ivo was six years old. His skin had gone hot and dry, lips cracked, eyes rolling under lids that wouldn't stay shut. The fever had started as a cough in the lowlands and now it had teeth.

"There's a shrine," Xanthe said finally. "Half a day up the Kestrel Pass. Abandoned. There may be a spring behind it that doesn't freeze." It wasn't quite true. The spring was a maybe. But movement was better than waiting, and waiting was where fear lived.

They walked through morning cold so sharp it rang. Maren carried Ivo wrapped in both their cloaks. Two other families trailed behind — the Odels with their twin girls, old Tam with his bad hip and his endless opinions about everything. Seven souls, plus Xanthe. Eight, if you counted what she carried at her hip.

Her sword didn't look like much. Plain steel, plain scabbard, worn grip. But it was an oath-blade, one of nine forged when Vael still stood, and it was bound to her by words spoken at her knighting: *While I live and hold this steel, my word is law upon the road.* Swordspeakers could command paths to open, storms to wait, wounds to close — small magics, oath-magic, bought with promises kept. She had kept hers for ten years without fail. Every refugee delivered. Every vow honored. It was the one thing the erasure hadn't touched, and some nights it was the only proof she had that Vael had ever been real at all.

The shrine appeared around midday, tucked into a fold of the pass like a thing hiding.

Xanthe stopped so fast Tam walked into her back.

She knew this shrine. Had passed it forty times. A squat stone cell dedicated to some minor hearth-saint whose name had gone with the country, its roof fallen in, its alcove choked with moss and bird droppings. Dead stone. Dead god, probably, or never a living one. Refugees sometimes left offerings anyway — a button, a dried apple — the way drowning men throw coins into black water.

Now it was lit.

Not torchlit. Not sunlit. The broken doorway glowed from within with a soft gold warmth, steady as banked coals, and the moss on its lintel had turned green and wet as June. Snow melted in a neat ring around the walls, exposing brown grass that should have been dead until thaw.

"No," Tam said behind her, reading her stillness. "No, no. Girl, what is that?"

Xanthe didn't answer. Her sword-hand had begun, very faintly, to ache. The oath-blade hummed against her hip like a struck tuning fork, and beneath the hum she felt something worse: a *pull*, gentle and enormous, like the first tug of a current under calm water.

Ten years. Ten years of prayers going nowhere — she'd said enough of them herself, bitter ones, in the dark, addressed to gods who had either died with their country or been murdered alongside it. Nothing had ever answered.

The shrine was answering now.

"Maren." Her voice came out flat. "Wait here."

"You can't—" 

"Wait *here*."

She approached alone. Up close, the glow resolved into candlelight — actual candles, three of them, standing in a puddle of their own wax on the ruined altar, burning without flickering though the pass wind cut straight through the missing door. And on the altar, arranged with terrible neatness: bread. Fresh bread, still steaming in the cold air.

Offering accepted. Prayer answered. The mathematics of it made her stomach drop. Somewhere, something had listened to someone, and replied.

Behind her, she heard Maren gasp — and then, before Xanthe could turn, heard her footsteps crunching forward, past her, past all caution, Ivo clutched to her chest.

"*Please,*" Maren was saying, and her voice had gone strange, formal, the cadence of liturgy remembered from childhood. "*Hearth-keeper, hearth-keeper, who keeps the door and warms the hall — my son is cold. My son is burning. Let your spring run sweet—*" She was kneeling at the threshold now, rocking Ivo, tears freezing on her cheeks. "*Let your spring run sweet for him. Ashes remember. Ashes remember.*"

And then she whispered it. The last line, barely breath, in old high Vaelish — the ritual tongue, the tongue that was supposed to be dead:

"*Vel sarath oen. Vel sarath oen.*"

*I am yours to keep.*

The sword spoke.

Xanthe felt it happen before she understood it — the oath rising up through her arm like fire in a flue, seizing her jaw, her tongue. Ten years of discipline, of silence, of never speaking the old formulas aloud, and none of it mattered, because the oath was older than her caution and it recognized its duty. The blade blazed blue-white along its whole length. Her mouth opened.

"*Vel sarath oen,*" she heard herself answer. "*I am yours to keep.*"

The candles flared. The warmth rolled out of the shrine like a tide, washing over all of them — and Ivo screamed.

Not a fever scream. Xanthe spun and saw the boy arch in his mother's arms, saw the gray death-pallor flush suddenly pink, saw him draw a full, deep, easy breath, and another, his eyes clearing, focusing, fixing on the glowing doorway with an expression no six-year-old should wear: recognition. Longing.

"Hungry," Ivo said clearly. "There's bread."

The spring behind the shrine — dry for a decade, Xanthe knew it was dry — was running. She could hear it, bright and unmistakable, chuckling over stones.

Tam grabbed her arm. "What did you do? What did you *say*?"

"Nothing." Her voice shook. She sheathed the singing blade with hands that wouldn't quite obey. "I answered. Gods help me, I answered."

Maren was weeping into her son's hair, laughing, whispering thanks to a hearth-saint ten years dead. The twins were staring at the bread. Old Tam had gone the color of tallow, and Xanthe realized he alone among them understood, because he was old enough to remember what came after miracles.

"That word," she said to Maren. "Where did you learn it? Who taught you to pray like that? That rite hasn't been spoken since—"

"Since the Spire went dark." Maren looked up, and her tear-bright eyes were serene, too serene, lit from somewhere behind. "Everyone knows it, swordspeaker. Everyone from home. We've all been saying it our whole lives. We just stopped saying it out loud."

She smiled, and for half a heartbeat Xanthe saw something else move behind her face — something patient, something vast, something that had been walking beside these refugees for ten unbroken years, waiting for a single door to open.

"The ashes remember," Maren said gently. "And now, apparently, so does everything else."

Far down the pass, behind them, a bell began to ring — one clear note, then another, from a chapel Xanthe knew for certain had held nothing but bats and rubble since the night Vael fell.

It did not stop.

* * *

That night, camped hard below the tree line with Ivo sleeping warm and whole and *hungry* by the fire, Xanthe sat apart with her unsheathed sword across her knees and watched the light.

Every peak in sight wore a crown of it. Shrines, cairns, wayside niches, forgotten altars — places of worship abandoned across an entire mountain range — kindling one by one in the dark like windows lighting in a house long thought empty. Gold, all of it, the same soft hearth-gold. Something was coming home through them. Or waking. Or being *pulled* back, prayer by prayer, word by whispered word, by two hundred exiles who had carried their god out of a dead country inside themselves without ever knowing it.

Her oath-blade lay quiet now. But when she laid two fingers against the flat of it, she felt the pull again — patient, vast, glad.

Somewhere north, past every pass she knew, stood the Spire. And beneath the Spire, if the oldest stories were true, there was a door.

Xanthe had spent ten years making sure no one could ever find Vael again.

Tomorrow, she suspected, she would have to decide whether she was still willing to.
