## 2. A Priestess of Nothing

Seraphine had not prayed in six years, and the first prayer she spoke was a lie.

*I am sorry,* she said, kneeling on the frozen mud of the Thornfell pass with her palms pressed flat to the stone, the way she'd been taught in the white cloisters of Vaelor's temple. *I am coming home.*

The god answered. Of course it answered. That was the whole horror of it — that after a decade of silence, of ash and absence, Vaelor heard her as clearly as if she'd never left. Warmth spread up through her hands like sunlight through water, and Seraphine bit down on her tongue until she tasted blood, because the warmth felt good, and it was meant to. It was always meant to feel good right up until it closed around you.

*Not home,* she corrected, whispering into the dark under her hood. *Never home.*

The god said nothing else. It didn't need to. It knew where she was now.

She rose, knees cracking, and looked up at the mountain road ahead — the smuggler's path, worn smooth by ten years of desperate feet, threading up through pines black against the stars. Somewhere along this road was a woman called Eastwick. The last swordspeaker. The only person alive who could hear the old door when it sang.

Seraphine had spent four months tracking her. She had bribed, begged, and burned through every coin and favor she had. And now, standing on the road in the dark with the god's attention settling over her shoulders like a hand, she understood she had perhaps two days before its agents found her — before they found *all* of them.

She walked.

* * *

Xanthe smelled the stranger before she saw her: cold iron and incense, temple-stock, the kind of scent that didn't belong anywhere above the treeline. She shifted the child higher on her hip — feverish still, but sleeping, which was more than he'd managed two nights ago — and stepped out of the treeline onto the switchback with her hand resting easy near her sword hilt.

"You're lost," Xanthe said.

The woman pulled back her hood. Younger than expected. Cropped pale hair, a scar splitting one eyebrow, and eyes that had the particular hollowed-out look of someone who had stopped sleeping on purpose. "I'm looking for Xanthe Eastwick."

"Never heard of her."

"She's shorter than the stories," the woman said. "But the stories say she answers when someone speaks Vaelish to her. Even now. Especially now." Her voice was steady, flat, rehearsed. Then it cracked, just slightly, on the next word. "*Please.*"

That word did what the whole speech couldn't. Xanthe's hand came off her sword. "You're one of theirs."

"I *was*." The priestess — and she moved like one, Xanthe realized; there was a trained grace to her that no fugitive ever quite lost — held out her hands, palms up, the way you showed an empty blade. "I served Vaelor for nineteen years. I sang the morning office. I lit the lamps. And then the kingdom fell, and I learned what the lamps were actually for." A muscle worked in her jaw. "My name is Seraphine. And the god is awake again, Eastwick. It's answering prayers. And by spring, everyone who ever escaped it is going to wish it hadn't."

Above them, on the ridge, a nightbird cried — except it wasn't a nightbird, because nightbirds don't cry in words, even dead ones. *Come home. Come home. Come home.*

Xanthe's blood went cold. Ten years, and the language still lived in her bones, waiting.

"Inside," she said. "Now. You can explain yourself where there's walls between us and that."

* * *

The safehouse was an old shepherd's hut, three days' walk from anything, and it was full — which was the whole point of family, Wren liked to say. Full of people who owed each other nothing and chose everything anyway. There were eleven of them tonight: smugglers and strays, two brothers from the salt flats, old Marta who cooked for armies that no longer existed, and Tam, six years old, finally cool-skinned and demanding soup with the imperiousness of the recently recovered.

It was Tam who heard the first prayer.

He was sitting on the floor by the fire, drawing in the ash with a stick, when he went still. "Someone's singing," he said.

"No one's singing, love," Marta said.

"Under the floor." He frowned, tapping his stick against the stones. "They keep saying my name. But wrong. Like — like someone saying it from far away, trying to remember how it goes."

Every adult in the room had stopped moving. Xanthe caught Wren's eye across the fire. Wren, who'd been born in Vael and carried it quieter than most, whose face had just gone the color of tallow.

"That's how it starts," Wren said quietly. "When it calls, it doesn't shout. It *remembers* you."

Seraphine stood by the door, dripping snowmelt, watching them all with something terrible in her face — recognition. "It's found your people already. Faster than I thought." She turned to Xanthe. "Then I'll say what I came to say in front of everyone, since we're apparently out of time. There is a door. Under the Spire, in the heart of what used to be Vael. The old gods built it when the world was young — a way *out* of the country entirely. Not around it. Out of it. Out from under Vaelor's reach forever."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I was sent to guard it." Her mouth twisted. "Nineteen years of my life, keeping pilgrims away from a door I was told led to damnation. It leads somewhere else. I've read the sealed texts. I've seen the maps. And there is exactly one person alive who can find it, because the door only shows itself to a swordspeaker — to an oath-bound voice of the old covenant. Which makes you, Eastwick, the last key in existence."

"The oath is broken," Xanthe said. "Vael broke it when it burned."

"Vael broke the *kingdom*. The oath lives in you. You know it does. You feel it every time someone speaks that language in front of you and your body answers before your mind does." Seraphine's voice dropped. "I watched it happen on the road today. One word out of me and you nearly drew steel to defend a country that doesn't exist."

Silence, except for the fire and, faintly, horribly, Tam humming a melody under his breath that no six-year-old in Thornfell should have known.

"I can't take all of you," Xanthe said at last, to the room rather than the priestess. "The Spire sits inside the borders of a dead country that is waking up. Every mile past the pass, we're walking deeper into something that wants us back."

"Then leave us," said old Marta, stirring the soup, not looking up. "Go on. See how far any of us get, hearing our names sung under the floorboards every night. We're coming. All of us. That's what family means, girl — you don't get to save us alone."

Wren laughed, a small cracked sound. "She's got you there."

Xanthe looked around the hut — at the ash-drawing child, at the cook, at the brothers, at Wren — and felt the old dread and something warmer tangled together so tightly she couldn't tell them apart. Then she looked at Seraphine, the enemy priestess with empty hands and a liar's prayer still drying on her lips.

"One condition," Xanthe said. "If the god whispers to you — if you hear it call you home, even once — you tell me. First breath. No pride."

Something flickered across Seraphine's face. Relief, maybe. Or fear wearing relief's clothes. "First breath," she agreed.

"Then we leave tomorrow."

* * *

Seraphine kept the fourth watch, as penance, though no one had asked her to. The hut slept around her — eleven breaths, uneven and human, the sound of people who had chosen each other. She had forgotten what that sounded like. In the temple, sleep had been scheduled and shared and surveilled; here it was messy and mutual, and she sat by the banked fire listening to it like music.

Across the room, Xanthe lay on her side, not sleeping either. Their eyes met over the embers.

"Ask," Seraphine said quietly. "You've been circling it since the road."

"What did it feel like? When it answered you. On the mountain."

Seraphine considered lying. Considered how much easier everything would be if she were better at it. "Like being forgiven," she said. "For things I haven't done yet. That's how it gets you. It forgives you forward."

Xanthe was quiet a long moment. "And you walked toward us anyway."

"I walked away from it. Toward was just the direction that happened." A pause. The fire ticked. "There will be a moment, on this road, when I hear it again. When it offers me something I want. And I need—" Her voice failed and restarted. "I need one person in this world who will believe I said no."

"You have eleven," Xanthe said. "Counting the soup critic."

A laugh escaped Seraphine — short, rusty, startled out of her like a bird. Across the embers, Xanthe smiled, and it changed her whole face, and Seraphine looked away first, which was itself an answer to a question neither of them had asked aloud.

Outside, the wind moved through the pines. And beneath the wind, faint and patient and getting closer every hour, something vast began to hum a lullaby that every person in the hut had heard once, long ago, in a country that was supposed to be dead.

Tam sat up in the dark, wide awake, and finished the song.
