## 3. What the Mountain Owes

They came at moonrise, and they came smiling.

Xanthe saw the first of them on the switchback above the drowned quarry: a lantern swinging in a familiar rhythm, three short, one long — Tam's signal, Tam's walk, Tam's broad shoulders under a hood. Her heart lifted before her mind caught up and slammed it down like a gate.

Tam had been dead for six years. She'd buried him herself, in the soft ground below Ravenmere, and she had wept until there was nothing left in her to weep with.

The figure raised a hand. "Xan." The voice was right too. That was the cruelest part. "You're going the wrong way. Come home."

"Keep walking," she said over her shoulder to the others, low. Seraphine's hand found her elbow — steadying, not stopping — and the column moved on through the scree: Marta with the boy Pell asleep against her back, old Hesk limping but stubborn, young Ivo carrying the rope and the maps he'd spent three years memorizing and never once trusted. Nine of them now, counting Seraphine, who was still barely one of them at all.

"Xan." Tam again, closer now, though his boots made no sound on the stone. "The mountain misses you. The country misses you. Vaelor remembers every name."

"Then it can keep them," she said, and did not turn her head.

Behind them, more lights were kindling along the ridge. Not lanterns. Prayers. Little flames of answered prayer burning in the dark like a string of festival lamps, and standing beside each one a face she knew. The miller from Aldenford who'd crossed with her in the second year. A girl she'd carried out as an infant, grown now into a woman she'd never met, waving cheerfully, calling her name in Vaelish — *Xanthe Eastwick, sword-oath, door-warden, come home, come home* — in the accent of a country that no longer existed anywhere but in throats like these.

"They're getting braver," Hesk muttered. "Yesterday they kept to the treeline."

"Because yesterday there were fewer of them," Xanthe said. "Every prayer that gets answered feeds another one up."

* * *

They made the hollow camp an hour past midnight, in the cup of stone where she'd hidden two hundred souls over ten years, and Xanthe took first watch because she didn't trust her own sleep.

Seraphine came and sat beside her anyway, close enough that their shoulders touched. The priestess looked worse than the mountain did — gray-lipped, hands shaking around a cup of pine tea she wasn't drinking.

"The dead of Vael," Seraphine said quietly. "I should have told you sooner what they are."

"You told me. Reclaimed. Resurrected."

"I told you what I understood then." She stared into the cup. "It isn't resurrection, Xanthe. It's reclamation. The god doesn't raise its people. It *collects* them. Every soul that ever swore to Vaelor, every child born under Vaelish law, every refugee who ever whispered the old words in their sleep — they belong to the country, and the country is waking, and it wants its citizens home." She finally looked up. "Not just the dead ones. All of them. Body and soul. It doesn't distinguish. To Vaelor, everyone who escaped is not a survivor. They're a deserter."

Xanthe said nothing for a long time. Below them in the dark, the little prayer-fires had multiplied, and she could hear singing now — hundreds of voices, gentle, patient, singing the harvest rounds children learned before they could walk.

"Pell," she said finally. "Marta's boy. He was born in exile. Never set foot in Vael. He's eight years old."

"It doesn't matter. Blood counts. Memory counts. A lullaby counts." Seraphine's voice cracked. "That's how it took my order. That's how it took *me*, the first time. You don't have to want it. You only have to belong."

"And the door?"

"The door is the one seam in the country it can't mend. But it has to be found by a swordspeaker, and opened while the swordspeaker still holds her oath. After you—" She stopped.

"After I break it," Xanthe finished. "After I unbind everything, including myself."

"Yes."

"Then we walk fast tomorrow."

* * *

It was Ivo who broke.

She should have seen it coming — he'd been quiet since the quarry, quieter than his usual quiet, and when the singing started he'd pulled his hood up like a man trying to shut out weather. At dawn, when they reached the black throat of the ravine that led up to the Spire, he stopped walking.

"Ivo." Xanthe turned. "Keep moving."

He was crying without any noise at all, tears running into his beard. "I can hear my mother," he said. "In the singing. She died when I was nine, Xanthe, I never even — I don't remember her face, but I know it's her, she's singing the plum-harvest song and she's saying she forgives me for leaving—"

"Ivo—"

"She says the door is a lie." His voice went strange and calm, and that was the worst sound yet, worse than grief. "She says the god showed her. There is no door out of Vael. There never was. The priestess is leading us in a circle, and the Spire is just a tomb, and if we climb it we'll be climbing into its mouth." He looked at Seraphine with flat, terrible certainty. "She's one of *them*. She served it for thirty years. And you're all following her."

Marta had gone white, shifting Pell on her hip. Old Hesk put a hand on his knife. "Boy's been turned."

"I haven't been *turned*, I've been *told*—" Ivo's composure shattered. "Don't you understand? I heard her. My mother. What kind of monster wouldn't listen?"

"The kind that lives," Xanthe said, and hated how it sounded, and said it anyway.

Seraphine stepped forward, palms open, empty. "Ivo. Look at me. Your mother died in the fall of Corvess. I read the rolls. I burned the incense. Whatever sings to you wears her voice, but it isn't her, because your mother is *free*, and that thing in the dark is not free, it's a hook dressed in love—"

"Don't." Ivo's hand went to his belt. "Don't say that about her."

"Ivo." Xanthe kept her voice level, the way she'd talk down a spooked mule on a narrow ledge. "Ten years I've walked this road. Ten years I've heard voices too — my brother's, my captain's, half the people I failed. You think I'm deaf? I listen to every word. And then I keep walking. That's the whole job. That's the only oath that matters now."

For a moment — one breath, two — something in his face wavered toward her, reaching.

Then the singing changed key, and Ivo flinched like a struck dog, and drew his knife.

Hesk moved. Marta screamed Pell's name. It happened fast and ugly and small — three seconds of struggle on wet rock, a cry cut short, blood bright on gray stone — and when it was done Ivo lay still with Hesk's blade in him, and Hesk stood shaking, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing in any language, living or dead, that covered it.

Xanthe knelt by Ivo and closed his eyes. Under her palm his skin was already cooling far too fast, cooling like stone in winter, like something being *withdrawn* rather than lost. She understood, with a sick lurch, that the god had gotten what it wanted from him either way: a body on the mountain, a grave inside its borders, a claim.

"We bury him and go," she said. "Now. Before sundown."

"He was right about one thing," Marta whispered, clutching Pell so hard the boy woke and whimpered. "Wasn't he? About the circle. About her?"

"No," Seraphine said.

"You'd say that either way," Marta said, and turned her back on the priestess, and would not look at her again.

* * *

They climbed the last mile in silence broken only by the singing below, which never tired, never faded, patient as tide. The Spire rose out of the ravine's end like a black tooth, and at its base, half-swallowed by root and rubble, Xanthe felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the air, a draft flowing *outward*, cold and clean, smelling of nothing at all.

The door.

And standing before it, waiting, lantern held high with that same three-short-one-long rhythm, was Tam.

He looked better than he had in life. That was how she knew, finally and completely, and her oath-sense sang in her bones like a plucked wire: *not a ghost, not a man, a door held open by something vast.*

"Xan." He smiled. "One question. That's all I ask. One honest question, and then you can pass." He spread his arms, and behind him the whole hillside lit with prayer-fires, hundreds of faces she had loved or failed or both. "If the door is real — if it leads somewhere the god can't follow — why did it let you find it? Why would the country leave its door unlocked, after ten years, on the very night you come?" His smile gentled. "Ask yourself who told you where it was, Xanthe. Ask what she gets, if you all walk through."

Every head turned to Seraphine.

The priestess stood very still at the edge of the firelight, and did not deny it, and did not speak, and the door breathed its cold clean nothing into the dark between them all.
