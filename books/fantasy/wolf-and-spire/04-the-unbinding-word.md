## 4. The Unbinding Word

The Spire was not a tower anymore. It had never been a tower — that was the lie the maps told, and Xanthe had believed it for ten years like everyone else. It was a door standing upright in a field of ash, tall as grief, its frame carved from stone that remembered being a city gate.

Vael's gate. The one that had closed behind them while the country burned itself back into existence on the other side.

She stood at the threshold with her sword drawn and her oath screaming in her teeth, and behind her, holding the line, Seraphine knelt in the ash with her palms flat against the ground.

"They're coming," Seraphine said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "All of them."

"I know." Xanthe could feel them too — not footsteps but *attention*, the weight of a god turning its face toward the door it had spent a decade building. "How long can you hold it?"

"Long enough." Seraphine looked up, and there was something in her face Xanthe had only seen once before, the night she'd confessed what she'd done as Vaelor's priestess: terror worn like armor. "Say it fast, Eastwick. And don't look back at me when you do. I don't want the last thing I see to be you hesitating."

Xanthe almost smiled. Almost. "You're insufferable."

"I'm a priestess of nothing. It's the whole job description." Seraphine turned away, spread her arms, and began to speak in the old tongue — the liturgy of closing, the words that would hold the threshold against everything Vaelor sent through the dark.

Xanthe stepped through the door.

* * *

Inside was Vael.

Not the burned ruin she'd fled. Not the kingdom of memory. Vael as the god wanted it rebuilt: golden wheat under a honeyed sky, a road of white stone running toward a capital that gleamed like a promise. People stood along the road — hundreds of them, thousands — and every face was known to her. The baker from Harrowgate. The ferryman who'd carried her family across the Senn in the last summer of the old world. Her mother. Her mother, whole and warm and smiling, ten years dead and wearing it well.

"Daughter," Vaelor said, and the voice came from everywhere, from the wheat and the sky and the mouths of the beloved dead. "You brought them home. Every soul you smuggled out — you were herding them all along. You just didn't know it yet."

"I knew it the moment the shrine lit up." Xanthe walked forward, boots on white stone, sword low. "That's why I stopped praying. That's why they all did. You don't get to answer."

"The child prayed anyway." The god's amusement rolled through the fields like weather. "Children always do. They haven't learned that gods eat."

"You ate my country."

"I *am* my country. There is no difference, and there never was. That is what your oathbound kind could never forgive Me — that a border is just a wound, and I am the body trying to close it." The dead of Vael stepped closer, gentle as tide. "Come inside, swordspeaker. Bring your found family. Bring the priestess who betrayed Me. All is forgiven here. All is *kept* here. Forever is only terrible if you stand outside it."

Xanthe raised her sword.

And felt nothing.

Ten years of magic — the edge that sang, the arm that never tired, the word of power coiled in her wrist like a second pulse — and now, standing in front of her god with every ghost she loved watching, she reached for it and found it already gone. Not taken. *Spent.* She understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling: the oath had been waiting for this. Every mile of mountain, every refugee delivered, every prayer she'd refused to say — it had all been payment accumulating toward this single purchase.

The unbinding word cost everything a swordspeaker had.

It was designed to.

"Oh," Vaelor said softly, and for the first time the god sounded afraid. "Oh, you foolish, faithful little knife. You wouldn't dare."

"My name," Xanthe said, "is Xanthe Eastwick. Of Harrowgate. Of nowhere. Of wherever my people are standing." She let the sword fall from her hand. It rang on the white stone like a bell ending a service. "And I am done being yours."

She spoke the unbinding word.

There is no writing it down. There was no hearing it twice. It was not loud; it was *final*, the way a door is final, the way a grave is final, the way a mother saying *no* is final. The golden wheat went gray from the roots up. The honeyed sky cracked like old varnish. The beloved dead looked at her — her mother looked at her, last of all, longest of all — and their faces softened into something that was not anger and was not forgiveness and might have been both, and then they came apart into light, and the light came apart into nothing, and Vaelor screamed with a country's voice as the door began to close around Him.

Xanthe stood in the collapsing gold and wept without sound, because she had given the word everything, including the part of her that could have cried out loud.

The gate shut.

* * *

Seraphine was on her knees in the ash when the Spire folded inward like a held breath released. She caught Xanthe as the other woman staggered out — caught her, and went down with her, and held on.

"It's done?" she whispered.

"It's done." Xanthe's voice was a ruin. She tried to lift her right hand and couldn't; the arm lay in her lap like something borrowed. Where the oath had lived under her skin there was now only silence, vast and ordinary, the silence of a house after the fire. "I can't feel it. Seraphine. I can't feel any of it."

"I know." Seraphine pressed their foreheads together. "I heard it stop. Ten years I listened to that oath humming through you like a struck bell. Now it's quiet." Her breath hitched. "You're just a woman."

"Just a woman," Xanthe agreed, and laughed, or sobbed, or both.

Boots crunched in the ash. Tam came first, sixteen and furious-eyed, still carrying the sick child — Mira, fever-broken three days ago, now asleep and breathing easy against his shoulder, because whatever the god had begun to give back had been left behind as a gift when it died. Then Wren and Odile, the twins who'd heard prayers in their sleep for a month and now, visibly, mercifully, heard nothing. Then the rest of them, the whole ragged congregation of the road, twenty-three souls Xanthe had ferried through Thornfell and would have ferried through hell.

They gathered around the place where the Spire had been, which was now only a field, gray and ordinary, with snow starting to fall.

"Is it over?" Tam asked. "Really over? No more doors?"

"No more doors," Xanthe said. "No more country. No more god." She looked at her dead right hand, then at the living people around her, and made herself say the true thing. "And no more swordspeaker. Whatever I was — the magic, the oath, the title — I spent it. I'm sorry. I know some of you counted on—"

"Shut up, Eastwick." Wren sat down heavily in the ash beside her. "You think we followed the sword? We followed *you*. The sword was just the loudest part."

Odile snorted. "Second loudest."

They laughed — ragged, disbelieving, alive. Snow kept falling. Somewhere downslope, a real bird sang in a real tree, indifferent and perfect.

Seraphine helped Xanthe to her feet. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk. I'm just a woman. Women walk."

"Insufferable," Seraphine murmured, and took her weight anyway.

They went down the mountain together, twenty-four of them, leaving no tracks worth following, toward the valley where a stove needed lighting and a roof needed patching and an ordinary evening waited like a gift nobody had earned. At the treeline Xanthe stopped once and looked back at the empty field where a country had tried to be born.

"Goodbye," she said — not a prayer, not a word of power, just a word, hers alone, costing nothing and everything.

Then she turned and walked home under an ordinary sky, and did not look back again.
