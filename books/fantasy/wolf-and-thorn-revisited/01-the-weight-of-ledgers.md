## 1. The Weight of Ledgers

The candle above Bram Okafor's desk had burned down to a thumb's width, and the accounts still refused to balance.

He knew it wasn't his arithmetic. Three years as ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things had taught him that the numbers themselves were honest; it was the trades they described that had begun to rot. He dipped his quill, checked the entry for the fourth time, and felt the familiar cold settle behind his ribs.

*One dream of flight, sold to Maren Voss, shipwright's daughter — paid in full: six years of her father's laughter.*

The ink was dry. The trade was legal. The guildhall's charter, older than the city walls, permitted exactly this: the buying and selling of impossible things, weighed against prices no sane market would name. Bram copied each transaction into the master ledger with a copyist's precision, because that was his work, and because precision was the only prayer he knew how to offer.

Outside, rain hammered the copper roof of Ashfall's guild quarter, and somewhere below, in the Hall of Trades, the evening buyers were making their offers. Bram could hear them through the floor — the murmur of voices, the chime of the weighing scales that measured things scales were never built to measure. A woman was selling the memory of her wedding day, by the sound of the scale's long, reluctant ring. A man wanted back the courage he'd traded away during the siege, and couldn't afford.

Bram did not go down to watch. Youngest ledger-keeper in guild history, they called him, as if it were an honor. He had come to understand it was closer to being the person who wrote down what the wolves ate.

He turned the page and stopped.

The next account was one he hadn't entered, because its holder had died that morning. Elder Corvess, who had sat on the guild council for sixty-one years, who had once traded away his own reflection and ruled the Hall of Trades without ever seeing his face again — Elder Corvess had withered in his chair at the council table like a apple left too long in a cellar. Bram had watched it happen over the past year, elder by elder. First Master Ilione, gone between one autumn and the next, her hands shrinking until her rings spun loose on her fingers. Then Corvess. Then, three weeks ago, old Tammas, who had simply failed to wake, his body so light the bearers said carrying him was like carrying a bundle of sticks.

The elders were dying, and the guild's physicians could find no cause, and Bram — because he kept the ledgers, because he saw everything written down — had begun to suspect the cause was written down too, somewhere he wasn't permitted to look.

There was a vault beneath the counting floor. There was a ledger sealed behind its wall, older than any record Bram had access to, sealed by the founder herself, Wren of the First Bargain, before she vanished into legend. Every keeper learned the story as an apprentice: how Wren had descended beneath the mountain generations ago and returned with the guild's power in her hands; how she had paid a price no one would speak of; how the guild had flourished ever after, rich in miracles, poor in explanations.

*Paid a price,* Bram thought now, watching Corvess's final entry sit unbalanced on his page. *We all pay prices. That's the whole business. Why won't anyone say what hers was?*

He reached for his blotting sand and found the jar empty. Small thing. He rose, stiff from hours of sitting, and took the candle, meaning to fetch more from the supply closet at the end of the gallery.

That was when he heard the footsteps on the stair.

They were wrong for this hour. The counting gallery emptied at dusk; only Bram and the night-watch remained, and the night-watch walked with boots and boredom. These steps were uneven, dragging, wet — the sound of someone climbing through sheer refusal to stop.

Bram set his candle down and picked up the letter knife beside his inkwell, feeling foolish even as he did it. The guild had wards, oaths, protections bought at rates recorded in volumes he'd never been allowed to read. No one climbed these stairs by accident.

The stranger came through the doorway sideways, one hand braced on the frame.

He was tall, or had been; he stood stooped now, like a man carrying something enormous inside a body too small for it. Rain ran off his traveling cloak and pooled on the counting-room floor, and in the candlelight Bram saw that the cloak was dark not with water alone. There was blood on the man's side, soaked through, glistening. His face was gray as ledger-paper, and his eyes —

His eyes were the worst part. They were calm. They were the eyes of someone who had finished all his errands except one.

"You're the keeper," the man said. Not asking. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "The youngest one. Okafor."

"I'm Bram Okafor." The letter knife stayed in his hand. "The Hall of Trades is closed. If you need healing, the—"

"I don't need healing." Something like a smile crossed the ruined face. "I've already spent everything that would have healed me. Don't you know your own inventory, keeper?"

Bram went cold. Because he did know. Entry forty-one, last month: *one death, natural and imminent, purchased anonymously, paid in full.* The buyer's name had been withheld under seal. The price had been — Bram had checked twice, disbelieving — *all remaining memories of spring.*

Someone had bought this man's death, and the man had walked up four flights of stairs anyway to die here, in front of him, on purpose.

"What do you want?" Bram asked quietly.

"Want." The stranger laughed, and the laugh cost him; he swayed, caught the edge of the nearest counting desk. "Young men want. Old men owe. I'm here about a debt." He fumbled at his cloak, at the inner lining, and Bram watched him draw out a wrapped cylinder, oilcloth over something rigid, stained dark along one edge. Bloodstain or dye, Bram couldn't tell. The stranger held it out across the space between them.

"Take it."

"I can't accept unsanctioned items. There are forms. There's a receiving officer—"

"There's no *time*, boy." The word cracked like a whip, and then the stranger's voice dropped, went almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Listen to me. My name doesn't matter anymore; I spent it. What matters is what's in your vault, behind the east wall, under the counting floor. The sealed ledger. Wren's ledger."

Every hair on Bram's arms rose. "No such record exists in the guild's holdings."

"No." The stranger smiled with bloody teeth. "It exists. It just isn't *held*. There's a difference. Your masters made sure of the difference." He thrust the cylinder closer. "Take it. Read it. And then get yourself beneath the mountain before the ones hunting me find you instead, because they will, keeper, they are very good at finding, and they will burn this place to its foundations looking for what I've carried thirty years to deliver."

"The guild has protections—"

"The guild has *debts*." For the first time, the calm in the man's eyes fractured, and Bram glimpsed what lay underneath: terror, ancient and worn smooth, like a stone rolled over in a riverbed for decades. "You think your elders died of age? You think Wren's bargain runs free? Everything the guild has ever sold, every miracle in every case in the Hall of Trades — it all came from somewhere, boy. And the somewhere wants its accounting brought current. The debt is due. It's always been due. We just convinced ourselves otherwise for a hundred years."

He sagged. Bram lunged forward on instinct, catching the man's arm — it felt like gripping a bundle of kindling wrapped in skin — and eased him into the counting chair. Up close, the stranger smelled of rain and iron and something faintly sweet, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

"Who are you?" Bram asked. "What was the trade? Who *bought* your death?"

"Doesn't matter." The stranger pressed the oilcloth cylinder into Bram's hands, folding the young man's fingers closed around it with both of his own. His touch was freezing. "Matters what you do next. Promise me you'll read it. Promise me you'll count it — really count it, the way only a true keeper can. Someone has to audit us, Okafor. Someone has to finally check the sums. A hundred years, and no one's ever checked the sums."

Bram looked down at the thing in his hands. It was heavier than it should have been, the way guilt is heavy, the way inherited things are heavy. "I promise," he heard himself say.

"Good lad." The stranger slumped back, and some tension went out of him, some burden set down at last. His gaze drifted to the ceiling, to nothing. "Your mother," he murmured. "Ask about your mother. Check her entry. When you find the ledger — check the date against her—"

And he stopped. Just like that, mid-word, the way a candle stops when pinched. One breath in, none out. His hand slid from Bram's wrist and struck the floor with a sound like a dropped book.

For a long moment Bram didn't move. The rain filled the silence against the copper roof. The candle flame bent and straightened. Downstairs, faintly, he could hear the weighing scales chiming, indifferent as ever, measuring the immeasurable.

*Ask about your mother.*

His mother had died eleven years ago. He remembered the funeral, the gray sea-light, his father's hollow face. He remembered almost nothing else about her, and had always told himself that grief was simply like that — that time ate memories the way it ate everything. He had never once thought to wonder why his memories of her felt less like remembering and more like reading a description written by a stranger.

Slowly, Bram knelt beside the dead man and closed his eyes. Then he unwrapped the cylinder with hands that shook only a little.

Inside was a map. Drawn on skin — he realized with a lurch that it was skin, tanned and inked, and that the blood along its edge was fresh. It showed the mountain range north of the free cities, but not as any cartographer drew it. Beneath the mountain's roots, in lines of silver ink that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them, wound passages descending far deeper than any mine. And at the bottom, drawn in careful, terrible detail, was a door. Around the door, written in a looping antique script Bram recognized from the guild's oldest charters, ran a single line:

*Here ends the guild's fortune. Here begins its debt.*

Bram sat back on his heels among the pooling rainwater, the map open in his lap, the dead stranger cooling beside him, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever his life had been until tonight, it had just ended. Somewhere below his feet, behind a wall in a vault, a ledger waited with his mother's name in it. Somewhere beyond the walls of Ashfall, people were hunting a courier who would never be found alive again — which meant they would soon be hunting what he carried.

And upstairs, in the council chamber, the remaining elders were meeting at this very hour, deciding things in voices too low to hear.

Bram folded the map along its creases, wrapped it, and tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, where it lay cold as a second heartbeat.

Then he blew out his candle, and went to find out what the guild had bought with his mother's soul.
