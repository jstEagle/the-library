## 2. Thorn-Sworn

The summons came at the hour when even the thorn-courts pretend to sleep.

Sera Vane had been awake regardless, as she was most nights, running the whetstone along her blade in long, patient strokes. The sound filled her cell in the Thornhold like breathing — *shhh, shhh, shhh* — the only prayer she had ever trusted. When the knock came, three slow beats and one sharp, she knew before she opened the door that it would be a night with a body in it. That was how orders of the red seal always arrived: late, quiet, and smelling faintly of blood no matter how clean the parchment.

The messenger was a boy, sixteen perhaps, rain streaming off his oilskin hood. He held out a scroll wrapped in black silk and did not meet her eyes. Smart boy. Nobody liked to look too long at the knight-commander of the thorn-sworn. It was said you could see the brand through her skin if the light was wrong — the coiled briar burned into her forearm at nineteen, the year she had knelt in the mud and sworn her life to Veyran's service. Some nights she could almost feel it herself, a warmth beneath the skin like a sleeping animal.

She broke the seal. The wax crumbled like old bone.

*Recover what the courier carries. A map, drawn in blood, bound for the Guild of Impossible Things in Ashfall. The courier is to be stopped by any means. If he resists, kill him. If he surrenders, kill him anyway. Burn nothing. Leave nothing.*

There was no signature. There never was. The red seal spoke with Veyran's own voice, and Veyran's voice had no need of names.

Sera read it twice, folded it once, and held the corner to her candle until the flame took it. She watched the words curl and blacken, and felt the familiar hollowing in her chest, the one she had learned to call discipline.

*A map.* Curious. Inquisitors handled maps; inquisitors handled paper. Thorn-sworn handled people. Which meant this order wasn't really about a map at all. It was about whoever carried it, or whatever the map led to, and someone above her wanted it found by soldiers rather than scholars.

She strapped on her sword. It was called Mercy, which was a joke she had stopped finding funny around her thirtieth kill.

Outside, Ashfall's autumn rain came down in gray sheets, turning the city's cobbles into dark glass. Sera pulled up her hood and moved into the streets like water finding its level.

* * *

The courier died badly, and not by her hand.

She tracked him from the Thornhold's eastern gate by the simplest method there is: money. A man who rides hard through the night buys fresh horses, and fresh horses cost coin, and coin leaves marks on a city the way blood leaves marks on snow. Two stableboys remembered him. A ferryman remembered him better, because he had paid in guild scrip — impossible things, traded for ordinary ones.

Sera found the trail's end in a gutter behind the Lamplighter's Rest, where the rain had not yet managed to wash away everything worth knowing. The courier lay face-down in standing water, and even from ten feet away she could see the wound in his back had been made by something long and narrow and patient. Not a sword. An awl, maybe, or an inquisitor's needle-blade.

He had been dead for hours. Which meant the map —

Her stomach dropped.

The map was gone.

She knelt beside the corpse, ignoring the water soaking through her knee, and turned him over with two fingers. His eyes were open, fixed on the underside of a world he'd stopped believing in. Younger than she'd expected. A ledger-keeper's ink stains on his fingers, though he hadn't dressed like guild — plain wool, worn boots, the look of a man who traveled light because he owned light.

And in his open hand, clenched so tightly the knuckles had gone white even in death, a scrap of parchment. Sera pried it loose.

Not the map. A receipt. Guild scrip, Ashfall mint, made out in a careful clerk's hand: *one ledger-keeping apprenticeship, purchased in full, no trade required.* No trade required. In thirty years of watching the guild work its miracles across the free cities, Sera had never once seen those words. Everything had a price. That was the whole architecture of the world now — the guild bought wonders with pieces of people, and Veyran bought victories the same way, and everyone pretended the arithmetic was honest.

Someone had given this dead man something for free.

She stood, wringing rain from her sleeve, and let herself think the thought she was not permitted to think: that somewhere in Ashfall tonight there was a person holding a bloodstained map who had done nothing wrong, and that her orders were to find him and put a blade in him anyway, because a red seal did not distinguish between guilt and inconvenience.

She buried the thought where she buried all the others, in the deep cellar behind her heart where she kept the faces of everyone she had ever killed for the seal. The cellar was full. The door barely closed anymore.

Then she went hunting.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things in Ashfall stood on Coinmaker's Hill like a fat spider at the center of its web, all gilded eaves and leaded windows, its doors thrown open at every hour to anyone with something to trade. Sera watched it from a doorway across the square for most of the following day, learning its rhythms. Who went in poor and came out rich. Who went in whole and came out — less. There was a particular way people walked after selling something to the guild, she had noticed over the years. A lightness in the step, yes, but also a hollowness in the shoulders, as if they had left a bone inside those doors and their bodies knew it.

The elders were dying. Everyone in the city knew it, though no one said it plainly. Three funerals in a month, each elder laid out with more gold on the bier than a harbor-master earned in a decade. Sera had assumed assassination, plague, ambition. Standing in the rain, watching the guildhall's windows glow, she revised her theory. What if nothing was killing them? What if they were simply being *collected*, the way debts are collected, and the gold was just the guild's way of dressing up repossession?

The courier's killer would come here eventually. Whoever had taken the map wanted it delivered or destroyed, and either way the guildhall was the center of the compass. So Sera waited, and the rain waited with her, and near dusk the thing she was waiting for happened — only not the way she'd planned.

They brought the stranger out on a board.

Two guild porters carried him down the hall's broad steps while a small crowd gathered under umbrellas, and behind them walked a young man in ledger-keeper's gray, soaked through, holding the dead courier's hand. Holding it the whole way down the steps, as if letting go were a thing he couldn't figure out how to do.

Sera drifted closer, one face among forty in a rainy square, close enough to hear the young man speak.

"He didn't have to give it to me." His voice cracked in the middle. "He could have sold it. He was dying, and he — he gave it away. Do you understand? Nobody gives anything away. I balance accounts. I know what things cost. And he just *gave*—"

One of the porters murmured something gentle and useless. The young man nodded as if he'd heard it, then knelt right there in the wet cobblestones beside the covered body, heedless of the mud, and wept.

Not politely. Not the way officials weep at funerals, with the grief arranged and presentable. He wept like a man whose foundations had been pulled out, ugly and gasping, both fists pressed against his mouth to keep the sound smaller than it wanted to be. Rain ran off his chin. He shook with it.

Sera stood six feet away with her hand resting lightly on Mercy's hilt, and found that she could not draw.

It should have been simple. Find him alone. Follow him somewhere quiet. Finish it, burn the map, report the courier dead of misadventure. She had killed men harder than this — men begging, men praying, men who looked at her with their children's names in their mouths. She had done it in alleys like this one, in weather like this weather, and slept afterward without dreaming.

But he was crying over a stranger. Over a *receipt*. Over the idea that anything in the world could still be given freely, and the grief of it had broken him open in the middle of a public square where any of Veyran's spies might be watching, and Sera Vane — knight-commander, oath-sworn, thirty-one kills and a brand that itched whenever she lied — stood in the crowd and felt the cellar door behind her heart strain against its hinges.

*Duty,* she told herself. *Duty is the wall. You built it stone by stone. You don't get to feel things about the people behind it.*

The young man — Bram Okafor, she would learn; youngest ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, motherless since he was nine, precise with numbers, careless with his own safety — finally rose. He tucked something inside his coat, over his heart, and pressed his palm flat against it for a moment the way a man touches a wound. Then he walked out of the square alone, into the narrowing streets toward the river district, and Sera followed him at twenty paces with her conscience arguing in the rain.

*Kill him now,* said duty. *A knife between the shoulders in Tanner's Lane. Clean. Done. Report.*

*Look at him,* said the other voice, the buried one, the one that sounded uncomfortably like a girl she used to be, kneeling in the mud at nineteen. *He's carrying something he doesn't understand, for a man he never met, and he hasn't yet learned that kindness is the most expensive thing there is. You're going to teach him that lesson with steel?*

She followed him past the tanneries, past the shuttered chandleries, past a shrine where a beggar god's statue wore a necklace of rain. He moved without caution — a clerk's walk, a man who had never needed to check the street behind him — and twice she could have ended it and twice her hand stayed on the hilt and did nothing, and the brand on her arm grew warmer each time, as if the oath itself were taking her temperature.

At the mouth of Ropewalk, where the lane narrowed and the lamps gave out, Bram Okafor stopped walking.

He didn't turn around. He stood very still in the dark, rain dripping from his hood, and said, in a voice raw from weeping:

"You've been behind me since the guildhall. Since the square, at least." A breath. "If you're going to rob me, there's nothing worth taking. If you're going to kill me—" His hands curled at his sides. "I'd rather you did it facing me. I've had enough of people dying with their backs turned."

Sera stepped out of the shadow of the ropewalk arch, one hand raised, empty and open.

"I'm not going to kill you," she said, and heard the lie leave her mouth like a bird leaving a cage, and felt the brand on her forearm flare hot enough to sting through wool.

Bram Okafor turned. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and inside his coat, over his heart, something rustled — paper, dry in all this wet, as if it refused to be rained on.

"Then what," he said slowly, "are you going to do?"

Behind them, far up the hill, the first thin thread of smoke was already rising from the direction of the guildhall — though neither of them had looked back yet to see it, and neither of them yet knew that before morning, Ashfall's oldest ledger would be ash, and the answer to that question would decide whether either of them lived to see dawn.

Sera opened her mouth to answer, and the sky over Coinmaker's Hill began, very softly, to glow.
