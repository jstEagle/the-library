## 3. What the Guild Buys

The map would not open for fire.

Bram tried everything a ledger-keeper knows about stubborn documents. He warmed the parchment over a candle until the wax seal sweated. He flattened it beneath the guild's iron weights. He even spoke to it, once, in the small hours — the way his mother used to speak to locked doors, as if courtesy might be contagious.

It stayed shut. The courier's blood had soaked into one corner and dried to a rust-brown continent, and along that stain the ink moved when he wasn't looking: roads rearranging themselves like sleeping men turning over, coastlines breathing. A map of impossible things. Of course it kept its own accounts.

It was the third night after the stranger died at his desk, and the guildhall above him had gone quiet in the way it never went quiet. Ashfall's Guild of Impossible Things was supposed to hum at all hours — brokers whispering over trades that could not exist, clerks arguing the exchange rate on a year of borrowed luck, someone always weeping or laughing or both in the stairwell. Now the elders were dead. Four of them in nine days, each found cold in rooms still warm from their fires, each with the same faint smile, as if they'd finally finished a long calculation and liked the answer.

Bram did not believe in coincidence. Believing in coincidence was a luxury for people who didn't keep ledgers.

So he worked below, in the vault office where the guild's true books lived behind three locks and an oath, and he deciphered by candlelight what the dying man had pressed into his hands.

*The debt is due.* Those had been the stranger's last whole words before the rest dissolved into wet coughing and a grip on Bram's wrist strong enough to leave bruises shaped like fingers. *The debt is due, boy. She said you'd understand.*

She. Bram turned the phrase over the way he'd turn a suspect coin. His mother had been dead eleven years, sold out of the world by fever and poverty while the guild sent flowers and a bill for the funeral wreath. But *she* sat in the sentence like a name written in invisible ink, waiting for the right light.

On the third night, the right light came.

His candle guttered low, and in its last amber flare the map's ink shifted all at once — not drifting now but snapping into place, roads locking like tumblers in a lock. And there, where the Court Beneath the Mountain should have been marked, the parchment darkened instead into a diagram. Not geography. Architecture. A floor plan, rendered in lines fine as hair: a vault, a wall, and behind the wall a cavity drawn no larger than a thumbnail, with a symbol beside it that every guild clerk learned before they learned letters.

The founder's mark. Maren Voss, who built this place two hundred years ago out of nothing and a bargain.

The mark pointed *inward*. Behind the vault wall.

Bram sat very still for a long moment, listening to the guildhall settle overhead, timbers ticking like cooling arithmetic. Then he took the ring of vault keys from around his neck, kissed them out of habit, and let himself through the third door.

* * *

The oldest ledger was exactly where a map two centuries out of date said it would be, which frightened him more than if it hadn't been there at all.

The vault wall's eastern face looked like any other — granite block, mortar gone grey with age. But when Bram set his palm against it, following the thumbnail diagram, one block rang hollow under his knuckles. The mortar around it crumbled at a touch, as if it had been waiting to be asked. Behind it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: a book.

He carried it back to his candle and unwrapped it the way he'd unwrap anything precious and possibly cursed, slowly, with his sleeves pulled down over his hands.

The cover read, in the founder's own angular hand: *Accounts, Volume the First.*

Maren Voss's ledgers were scripture in the guild. Every clerk memorized her maxims. *A thing freely given cannot be stolen; a thing stolen cannot hold its value.* Bram had recited them at his induction with his hand on the balance-scales, feeling very serious and very small.

He opened to the first page and felt the floor tilt.

It was not a trade ledger. It was an intake record. And the entries were not goods.

*Received of Aldric Wren, cooper, one portion of the soul entire: his joy, taken in lieu of debts unpaid. Paid out: relief from creditors, twenty years' credit at the Ashfall house. Witnessed and sealed beneath the Mountain.*

Bram read it twice. Three times. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were afraid to move.

Entry after entry, page after page, in the founder's tidy hand and then in other hands, generations of them, the script evolving the way handwriting does across lifetimes but the content never changing at all. *One portion: her courage. One portion: his grief — grief sells high, note the seasonal variance.* The guild's impossible things laid bare in black ink. Every miracle the guild ever brokered, every wish granted, every luck traded, every healing worked — paid for with pieces of people, harvested here and spent there, flowing through Ashfall like water through a mill.

And the counterparty on every line, patient as death, consistent as gravity: *the Court Beneath the Mountain.*

Bram's hands were shaking. He noticed distantly, the way you notice weather. He was a ledger-keeper; he had trained his whole life to read numbers without flinching. So he made himself do the thing numbers demanded. He counted.

Two hundred years. Eleven thousand entries. Portions, fractions, slivers — the court was careful, the court took only what a seller could spare and live, mostly — but eleven thousand times a human being had walked out of this building lighter than they came in, smiling at their good fortune, not knowing what coin they'd truly paid.

He found his father somewhere near the middle. That stopped him longest. *Okafor, Deshan: one portion, his hope, against cure of the lung-rot.* His father had survived the rot. Bram had been seven and had thought it a miracle, had thanked the guild elders with a child's whole heart. They had accepted his thanks. They had recorded it, probably, in some subsidiary book.

He kept reading because stopping was unthinkable, the way looking away from a wound is unthinkable, and near the end — near the last pages, where the handwriting changed to a clerk's hand he almost recognized, rounder, hurried —

*Okafor, Liana. One portion: the whole of her love, held in trust against a debt assumed by her son, minor, per guild custom of familial surety. Term: due upon his majority or upon presentation of the map. Witnessed and sealed.*

Bram did not remember standing up. He was standing, though, and the candle had gone over, and he watched the flame drown in a spreading puddle of wax with a strange, ringing calm, and relit it, and sat back down, and read the entry again.

His mother. The last entry in the founder's ledger, entered the year she died — the year after the stranger's map began its journey, if the dating held, and dates always held, that was the one mercy of dates. *Held in trust against a debt assumed by her son.* Not sold. *Assumed.* As if he had signed something. As if being seven years old and Okafor constituted a signature.

*She said you'd understand,* the stranger had whispered.

He understood now. The map wasn't an inheritance. It was a *call note*. Somewhere beneath the mountain, his mother's love sat in a glass cage, posted as collateral against a debt the court had invented and the guild had signed. And the elders — the four elders who had died smiling in nine days, whose deaths the guild physician called natural, whose funerals Bram had helped arrange between balancing columns —

They had known. They must have known the term was running out. And whatever the court did to those who defaulted, it had begun collecting from the top.

Bram closed the ledger. His hands had stopped shaking, which surprised him. What replaced the shaking was worse and better: a cold, clean clarity, the feeling he got only when a set of books finally balanced and showed him exactly how much had been stolen, and by whom.

The guild had fed on its own members for two hundred years and called it charity. The court had eaten the difference and called it contract. And his mother's love — the warmth she'd poured into him in a drafty garret with nothing else to give — had been sitting in a vault beneath a mountain this whole time, accruing interest for monsters.

He was still sitting in that cold clarity when he smelled smoke.

* * *

It came up through the vault stairs thin at first, the way lies come — just enough to notice, easy to explain. Tallow smoke, he told himself. Someone's dropped candle. Then a second smell arrived underneath it, tar-and-sulfur sweet, the signature of pitch-fire, and with it the sound that ended all explaining: boots. Many boots, moving overhead with none of the guildhall's usual shuffle. Marching boots. Inquisitors drilled their novices in formation walking; everyone in Ashfall knew the rhythm.

Bram snuffed his candle and stood in the dark doing arithmetic faster than he'd ever done it. They had not burned the guildhall when the elders died — quiet deaths, deniable deaths. Fire meant they wanted witnesses to see ruin. Fire meant they wanted the records destroyed and the destruction *known*, terror being its own line item. Which meant whatever they feared was written down. Which meant the ledger under his arm and the map in his shirt were the only copies left in the world.

Which meant, he realized, with a sensation like stepping off a ledge, that he was the only copy left in the world.

Smoke curled down the stairwell, grey in the grey dark, tasting of the pitch. Above him the great hall was catching — he could hear it now, the dry roar of two hundred years of paper finding its purpose too late. Shelves of ledgers going up like festival lanterns. Everything his life had been organized around, becoming light and heat for men who thought burning a history was the same as ending one.

He should run. The vault had a river-grate, old and rusted, an escape route for exactly this — the founders had planned for inquisitors the way farmers plan for frost. He was already moving toward it when his feet stopped, traitor feet, and turned him back to the table where the first ledger lay.

They had come to erase the evidence. Fine. Let the evidence walk out the door instead.

Bram wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth, tied it with the cord from his ledger-roll, slung it crosswise over his chest where a courier would carry dispatches. Map inside his shirt, ledger against his heart, keys around his neck. His whole inheritance fit in an armful, and it weighed more than the vault itself.

Through the grate and down the rusted ladder, into the cold throat of Ashfall's underways, where the river ran black and the city's oldest tunnels breathed damp around him. Above, through the grate's iron teeth, the guildhall glowed like a forge, and sparks climbed the night in slow orange constellations. He watched for exactly as long as it took to memorize the shape of the flames — he was a keeper of accounts; he intended to itemize this — and then he turned his back on two hundred years of theft going up in glory.

Somewhere ahead, the tunnels bent toward the mountains, toward the passes, toward a road he had no map for except the one against his skin that insisted it knew the way.

Let them burn the books, Bram thought, walking into the dark with his mother's name riding over his heart. Books were only entries.

He was the outstanding balance. And he intended, with a calm that would have frightened anyone who loved him, to collect.
