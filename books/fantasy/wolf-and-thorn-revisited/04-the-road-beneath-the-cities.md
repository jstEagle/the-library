## 4. The Road Beneath the Cities

**Bram**

The guildhall burned like a ledger with too many debts to ever balance.

Bram ran through Ashfall's back lanes with the old ledger strapped against his chest, smoke chasing him down every street like a creditor who had finally found his address. Behind him, the tower of the Guild of Impossible Things stood silhouetted against its own destruction, windows vomiting orange light, and he could not stop thinking about what was inside it — three hundred years of accounts going up page by page, each entry a name, and each name a person who had once walked out of the counting hall lighter by one soul.

His mother's name was in that fire now. He had copied it out first thing after finding it, in his own careful hand, in a pocket ledger no inquisitor would think to take from a clerk.

*Ilse Okafor. One soul, whole. Payment rendered.*

Payment for what? That question had kept him walking when his legs wanted him to stop. The elders had never told the ledger-keepers what the guild bought *with* the souls — only that the buying power flowed down from somewhere beneath the mountain, ancient and absolute, the source of everything impossible the guild traded. Love potions that actually worked. Storms sold by the barrel. Second chances, boxed and sealed.

He turned onto Cooper's Row and stopped.

The lane dead-ended at the old cistern gate, rusted shut for thirty years, and standing between him and the wall was a woman in travel-stained grey with a sword already drawn. Rain beaded on her shoulders. Her thorn-brand showed at her collar — a black circlet of barbed ink that marked her as Thorn-Sworn, one of Veyran's hounds, though everyone knew the order had hunted maps and couriers long before Veyran had a name worth fearing.

"You're Bram Okafor," she said. Not a question. "Ledger-keeper, third chair."

He pressed his back against the wet brick. His mind did what it always did under pressure: it counted. One sword. One exit, gated. Distance, twelve feet. Time until reinforcements arrived, if she had called any — unknown. "If you're here for the map," he said, "it burned with the hall."

"Ledger-keepers lie the way other men breathe." She took a step closer, boots quiet on the slick cobbles. "I watched you carry something out of that fire. Something heavy. Paper is heavy when there's enough of it."

"I carried out the day-book. Inventory of candle stubs. Riveting stuff."

Something flickered across her face — not amusement, exactly, but the place where amusement would have gone if she'd had room for it. "My orders are simple. Recover the courier's property. Kill anyone who read it."

"Then why are we talking?"

The rain filled the silence. She looked at him for a long moment, and Bram had spent enough years reading faces across a counting desk to know what he saw there: a woman doing arithmetic of her own, and hating every number in it.

"My name is Sera," she said. "And I'm going to tell you how this ends either way, so you understand your options. If I kill you, I report the map destroyed. If I let you run, my superiors hunt you down within a week, because I'm the only thorn-knight they trust alone, which means they'll know I failed." Her grip shifted on the sword. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless you died tonight. Officially. In the fire." She lowered the blade an inch — an inch that felt like the width of the world. "I found a body in the wreckage wearing your coat. Unrecognizable. Tragic. The map lost with him. And some clerk nobody will miss walks out of Ashfall carrying nothing but grief."

Bram laughed before he could stop himself — a short, disbelieving sound. "You want me to be dead."

"I want you to be *unfindable*. There's a difference, and the difference keeps you breathing." She sheathed the sword, all at once, as if the decision cost her more drawn than sheathed. "There's a door behind that cistern gate. It opens into the underways — old smugglers' roads that run under six cities and come up near the mountain. Nobody official knows them anymore. Nobody official will look for a dead man there."

"And why does a knight of Veyran's own order betray her order for a stranger?"

She flinched. Small, but he caught it; he was trained to catch such things. "Because I've been reading those orders for eleven years," she said quietly, "and tonight I finally asked myself what they were *for*. Because I watched you weep over a dying man you'd never met, and I realized I haven't wept over anything since I took the brand." Her hand went briefly to her collar, to the thorn-circlet, and Bram saw — or thought he saw — the skin around it reddened, angry, as if the mark itself resented her hesitation. "Take the alliance or don't. But decide fast. The fire crew will find the body I left in your coat within the hour, and after that there's no walking it back. For either of us."

**Sera**

She had lied before. Every knight lied; it was half the trade, same as the killing. But she had never lied *upward* — never sat in the ash-smell of a burned guildhall dictating a report to a scribe whose quill scratched down her words into the permanent record while the man she was erasing stood forty feet away in a hooded cloak, holding a lantern, pretending to inspect the damage.

"The courier resisted," Sera said. Her voice came out level, professional, dead. "I put him down. The map was on his person and burned with him. Nothing recoverable."

The scribe wrote it. Somewhere in the war-office at Veyran's elbow, a clerk would file it beside ten thousand other entries, and Bram Okafor would cease to exist in every ledger that mattered.

*You're good at this*, she thought, disgusted with herself. *Erasing men. You'd have made a fine bookkeeper.*

They left Ashfall before dawn through the cistern gate, down stairs slick with a century of condensation, into roads cut from living rock before the cities above had names. The underways swallowed them. Bram's lantern threw their shadows huge against the walls — hers stretched tall and thin as a spear, his round-shouldered, clutching his satchel of books the way other men clutched children.

"How far to the mountain?" he asked, after the first hour of silence.

"By the surface roads, nine days. Down here—" she consulted the cracked tile-map the order kept of the old routes, one of the few things she'd allowed herself to steal, "—four days, if the galleries haven't collapsed. Fewer things trying to kill us, though. Down here the only danger is getting lost, starving, or talking to each other."

"What's wrong with talking?"

"Nothing." She walked faster. "It's just that everything I say to you is either a lie or a confession, and I haven't decided which I can afford."

He was quiet for a stretch. Then: "The body you left in my coat. Who was it?"

"A man the order killed two nights ago. A beggar off Tanner's Gate. No family to miss him." She heard how it sounded even as she said it, and hated it. "I told myself it was mercy. Using someone already dead."

"Was it?"

"I don't know yet." She adjusted the strap of her pack. "Ask me again when the brand stops burning."

He glanced at her collar. "It burns because you broke faith?"

"It burns because it *wants* to break me. That's the design. Thorn-brands don't punish disobedience outright — that would just make useful knights useless. They punish *doubt*. Slowly. Every time I hesitate, every time I choose wrong by the order's measure, it tightens a little. By degrees. Years, usually, before it reaches the heart." She smiled without any humor in it. "So you should know what you're traveling with, Okafor. You're not traveling with an ally. You're traveling with a clock."

* * *

**Bram**

On the second night they made camp in a vaulted gallery where pale roots hung through cracks in the ceiling like the strings of some buried instrument. Bram balanced the old ledger on his knees and worked by lantern light, cross-referencing names, dates, payments — because work was the only prayer he knew.

Sera watched him from across the small fire. "What are you looking for?"

"A pattern. There has to be one." He turned pages. "Three hundred years of sales, and every price recorded in the founder's hand. Look — 1141, a storm-seller sells his courage. 1187, a love-peddler sells her grief. Always whole qualities, never random pieces. And the payment the guild receives—" he flipped forward, backward, forward again, frowning, "—the payment scales. Later trades buy more. As if the court learned to charge better rates."

"Or as if the guild learned to pay worse ones."

He looked up. "That's what frightens me. That's exactly what a ledger-keeper would do. Find the spread between value and cost and live in it." He closed the book slowly. "My mother sold her soul to this court, Sera. Before I was born. And I grew up in the guildhall eating bread bought with whatever they paid her, and nobody — *nobody* — ever told me her name was in the oldest book in the building. They let me balance accounts around her grave without knowing it was a grave."

Sera fed a twig to the fire. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the knight's flatness. "My mother died owing money. Debt-gang took the house, then took me for indenture, and the order bought out the contract because I could fight. Everything I am was purchased too. So don't apologize to me about ledgers, Okafor. I've been an entry my whole life." She looked at the fire instead of at him. "That's why I didn't kill you. In case you were wondering. It wasn't conscience. Conscience is expensive and I was never paid enough."

"Liar," Bram said softly.

Her head came up sharply.

"Eleven years reading orders, you said. Tonight you asked what they were for. That's not arithmetic. That's conscience screaming loud enough to hear at last." He held her gaze across the fire. "I keep accounts for a living, Sera. I know a hidden asset when I see one."

For a moment he thought she'd draw the sword again. Instead she laughed — one short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years — and lay back against her pack with her hand resting, deliberately, on the hilt.

"Sleep, Okafor. Four days to the mountain, and I promise you the court beneath it won't be moved by bookkeeping."

"You'd be surprised," Bram said, opening the ledger again. "Bookkeeping has toppled kingdoms. It just does it very politely."

But sleep, when it finally took him, brought dreams of the mountain — vast and hollow, ringing like a struck bell — and in the dream something beneath it turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count.
