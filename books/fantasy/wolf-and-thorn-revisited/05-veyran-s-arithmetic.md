## 5. Veyran's Arithmetic

Callowmere smelled of wet stone and frying onions and, underneath it all, of fear. Bram noticed the fear the way he'd once noticed errors in a ledger — not at first, but everywhere once you knew to look. It was in the way the market vendors stacked their shutters loose, ready to bar them in seconds. In the way the city watch drilled twice a day now instead of once. In the chalked prices on the fishmongers' slates, climbing daily, because everyone had heard what was happening to the passes east of here.

"Three coppers for bread," Sera said flatly, standing beside him under an awning while rain needled the canvas overhead. "Last week it was one."

"You've been here before?"

"I've been everywhere before." She said it without pride. A woman in a gray traveling cloak with a sword wrapped in oilcloth across her back could not afford pride in a city this nervous. "Veyran's army eats as it marches. Armies that eat raise prices. Prices raise fear." She glanced sideways at him. "You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Counting. Your eyes go left to right like you're reading columns. You did it when the courier died, and you're doing it now."

Bram closed his mouth, which had indeed been silently tallying. Three coppers for bread meant grain convoys were being seized or burned. Seized or burned meant the roads east were closed or dangerous. Closed roads meant Callowmere would be hungry within a month, besieged within two.

He had been a ledger-keeper for eleven years, since he was small enough that the guildhall's counting desks reached his chin. He knew what numbers did when they moved together. They told stories whether anyone wanted them told or not.

"We need a guild contact," he said. "Someone who can read a sealed ledger without asking why."

* * *

The contact was a woman named Odile Ferrant, who ran a money-changing stall two streets off the Grain Exchange and who had once, according to the guild directory Bram had memorized as an apprentice, been third-rank in the Order of Impossible Things before some unrecorded scandal sent her west. Her stall was narrow as a coffin. Behind her, scales hung from iron hooks, weighing coins from six kingdoms, and her hands moved over them with the bored precision of someone who had weighed ten thousand identical things.

She looked at Bram's guild seal — pressed in wax on his letter of introduction, which he had carried out of the burning guildhall tucked inside his boot — for a long moment. Then she looked at Sera, standing a deliberate step behind him with her hood up.

"He's guild," she said to Sera. "What are you?"

"His protection."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what you get."

Odile's mouth twitched, almost a smile. She flipped the sign on her stall to CLOSED and lifted a hinged section of counter, waving them both through into a back room that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. There was a desk there, and on the desk, a ledger.

Not just any ledger. Bram felt his stomach drop before his mind caught up — the binding was ash-oak, the corner-guards were silver stamped with the guild's sigil, the impossible thing wrought as a knot that could only be untied by its own ends. He had balanced accounts in ledgers exactly like it every working day of his adult life.

"This is a western branch ledger," he said. "Callowmere chapter. Why do you have it?"

"Because when your guildhall burned, someone in this city got nervous, and nervous people move their records, and moving records costs money, and money comes to me." Odile sat down behind the desk, folding her hands. "I bought it from her for more than it's worth and less than she asked. Now. You're the boy the elders sent east before the fire. The one who inherited something." Her eyes flicked to his satchel, where the map lay wrapped in oilskin against his ribs. "I don't want to see it. Don't take it out. But I'll tell you what I know, because I suspect we're rowing the same boat, and I'd rather not drown alone."

"Tell us," Sera said.

"Veyran." Odile said the name the way people say the names of storms. "Everyone knows he came out of nowhere eight years ago. A border lord with no lineage worth the vellum it wasn't written on, and suddenly he's got soldiers who don't break, don't rout, don't loot — discipline like clockwork. Everyone asked where such men came from. Nobody liked any of the answers." She pulled the ledger toward her but didn't open it. "I started asking different questions three years ago, after a client paid me in coin I couldn't source. Mint-fresh, no wear, struck from dies I'd never seen. I traced the shipment. Do you know what I found?"

"No," Bram said, though his hands had gone cold.

"Purchase orders. Guild purchase orders. Our order — the Order of Impossible Things, the guild that trades in marvels, that found lost children and cured plagues and turned lead to gold for widows. Except these orders weren't for marvels." She opened the ledger at last, turning pages until she found one marked with a strip of black ribbon. "They were withdrawals. Bulk withdrawals. Look at the entry structure and tell me what you see, ledger-keeper. You'll see it faster than I did."

Bram leaned over the page. The entries were in a clerk's hand — neat, disciplined, the hand of someone trained in the Ashfall scriptorium, maybe even someone he'd shared a bench with. Each line followed the standard format: date, item, quantity, source, price paid, balance.

But the *item* column stopped him cold.

*Fragments, grade three, forty units. Source: debtor estates, northern holdings. Price: waived.*

*Fragments, grade five, twelve units. Source: voluntary conversion, Ashfall temple district. Price: annuity, lifetime.*

*Fragments, grade seven, one unit. Source: [REDACTED]. Price: see sealed annex.*

Grade seven. He knew the grading system. Every guild child learned it as arithmetic before they were old enough to question it. Grade one fragments were dross — scraps of memory, stray courage, the little bright things people sold without missing them much. Grade seven was whole. Grade seven was everything a person was, taken clean.

"These are souls," he said aloud, because saying it quietly felt like complicity. "Bulk shipments of souls. Sold to whom?"

"The buyer column is coded, but codes are just arithmetic wearing a mask." Odile tapped the cipher notation — a thorned circle enclosing a crown. "That mark appears on four hundred and eleven entries over nine years. Cross-reference the dates against military history and you get your answer. Every major consignment lands weeks before Veyran fields new regiments. Weeks, ledger-keeper. Not months. His soldiers aren't trained. They're *stocked*."

Sera had gone very still. Bram had learned to read her stillness in the days since the underways — there was the stillness of a knight assessing ground, and there was the stillness of a woman hearing something she had half-known and prayed never to confirm. This was the second kind.

"Fearless soldiers," Sera said. "Merciless. We always called it discipline. The Thorn-Sworn prided ourselves that our oaths made us harder than ordinary men." Her voice flattened. "It was never discipline. It was inventory."

"They feel nothing," Odile agreed. "Or rather — they feel exactly what they were issued and nothing else. My cousin saw them at Redford Bridge. Said they walked through arrow-fire like men walking through rain, and when they took prisoners, they didn't gloat or rage or hesitate. They processed them. Like clerks." Her eyes met Bram's. "Like *your* clerks. That's the joke of it, isn't it? He didn't invent anything. He just found the guild's machinery already running and pointed it at bigger numbers."

Bram stepped back from the desk. The room seemed to tilt slightly, the lamplight swimming. He thought about his own desk in Ashfall, the neat columns, the quiet satisfaction of a balanced account. *Impossible things,* the guild traded, and everyone in the free cities believed that meant wonders. And it did mean wonders — but every wonder was debited somewhere, against some account, and Bram had spent eleven years recording the credits without ever once following the debits to their source.

His mother's name was in the oldest ledger, sealed behind the vault wall. He had read it by candlelight while smoke still hung in the streets outside. And now he understood — not fully, not yet, but the shape of it — that her name was not an aberration. It was a line item. One entry among hundreds of thousands, feeding a machine, and the machine had grown so large that a usurper had learned to milk it.

"He's hollowing people," Bram said. "Buying them out of themselves in pieces, and the pieces go into his soldiers, and the soldiers march, and the cities fall, and then there are more debtors, more desperate families, more fragments." He heard his own voice go strange and level, the voice he used when an account refused to balance and panic would help nothing. "It's not an army. It's a circulation. Fear drives the sellers to market, and the purchases drive the fear, and around and around. Veyran isn't fighting the free cities. He's *arbitraging* them."

Odile watched him with something like respect. "And the map you inherited?"

The question landed like a dropped weight. Bram's hand went to his satchel without his permission.

"I told you I don't want to see it," Odile said. "But think, boy. Think like the clerk you are. The dying man chose *you*. Out of every guild-keeper in Ashfall, the courier put a bloodstained map in the hands of the youngest, greenest ledger-keeper in the hall, and whispered that the debt was due. Debts run to a creditor. Maps run to a place. Put the columns side by side."

Bram put them side by side. He did it the way he did all his best work: silently, eyes moving left to right.

Every soul the guild had ever sold had gone somewhere. The Court Beneath the Mountain — the court the founder had bargained with, the court the founder's own soul still hung imprisoned in, if the stranger's whisper and the map's strange cartography could be believed. The court held the master vault. The original account. And Veyran's entire war machine drew from accounts that ultimately answered to that vault.

"If the founder's contract can be broken," he said slowly, "the whole edifice comes down. Not just the guild's bargain. *Everything.* Every fragment ever withdrawn flows from the same source. Void the source—" 

"—and Veyran's invincible army wakes up mid-march," Sera finished, "as men. As frightened, grieving, furious men, standing in a battlefield they no longer have the numbness to endure." She stared at him. "Gods. You wouldn't just defeat him. You'd break his soldiers' hearts all at once."

"Then it's a good thing nobody's going to let you reach the mountain," said Odile.

She said it lightly, but her hand had drifted beneath the desk, and Sera had already turned toward the door, and Bram understood a half-second too late that the front shutter of the money-changer's stall had been closed for several minutes longer than a simple CLOSED sign required.

Boots sounded in the street outside. Many boots, moving in perfect unison, making no other sound at all.

"Not Veyran's," Sera breathed, drawing her blade from its oilcloth. "Thorn-captains. I'd know that cadence anywhere." She looked back at Bram, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before — guilt, arriving late, carrying interest. "Bram. When I tell you to run—"

The door burst inward.

* * *

The first man through was huge, gray-cloaked, his face half-hidden behind a thorned visor — and Bram knew him instantly, because Sera had described him twice on the road, always in the tone people use for old wounds: *Captain Dain Marrek. Her former mentor.*

Behind him filled the narrow doorway, blades bare.

"Serah Vane," Marrek said, and his voice was terrible in its gentleness. "The council gave you nine days. You've used six. Give me the ledger-keeper and the map, and I'll write that you died heroically buying time for civilians. The brand stays quiet another season. Everyone weeps at your statue. Everyone goes home."

Sera shifted, placing herself squarely between Bram and the doorway, and Bram saw her left hand curl open at her side — where, beneath the glove, the thorn-brand had begun, these past two days, to burn.

"Captain," she said. "You should know I'm going to make you regret the phrasing of that offer."

Outside, above the rooftops of Callowmere, the afternoon light was failing — and far to the east, though no one in the room could see it yet, the first beacon fires of Veyran's advance were being lit along the mountain road.
