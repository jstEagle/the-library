## 6. The Price of Names

They caught the scent of them at dusk on the third day out of Callowmere — woodsmoke where no camp should be, and beneath it the green-metal smell of thorn-oil, which clung to Thorn-sworn armor like a second skin.

Sera knew before she saw the firelight how many there were. Four. And one of them moved the way Master-Captain Ilse Vane moved, weight rolling heel to ball of foot, a patience in it that had once been the thing Sera admired most in the world.

She crouched beside Bram behind the fallen trunk and did not tell him any of this.

"Four," she whispered instead. "We go around through the scree."

"They're between us and the pass."

"They're between us and the pass because they know we need the pass." She watched the firelight gutter against the pines. "It's a courtesy, Bram. They're letting us see them first. That's what the burn means."

He looked at her. In the failing light his face was all ledger-lines and worry, ink-stained fingers curled around the oilskin map as if he could read his way out of this. "The burn?"

"When a Thorn-captain wants an oathbreaker found rather than killed quietly, they carry a brand-candle. Green flame. It's not for me." Her mouth was dry. "It's for whoever I've become."

*For whatever you're protecting,* Vane would have said, if Vane were here to say it. Which, by the smell of the smoke, Vane was.

Bram shifted closer, and his shoulder touched hers, and she hated herself a little for how much steadier that single point of contact made her feel. Three weeks ago she had tracked him through Ashfall's markets with a knife already loosened in its sheath. Three weeks ago she had stood over him while he wept into a dead stranger's coat, blade half-drawn, and had done nothing — and everything since had grown from that one moment of cowardice, or courage; she still hadn't decided which name to give it.

"We could split up," he said quietly. "You said yourself they want me alive. They'd follow—"

"No."

"Sera."

"*No.*" It came out harder than she meant. She softened it the only way she knew how: with truth, or part of one. "Vane doesn't chase. Vane corners. If you run, you die tired, and I die after you, and the map burns with both of us. We stay together and we choose the ground." She nodded toward the ridge above the fire, where the pines gave way to a shelf of granite split by a ravine. "There. One approach. One at a time, if they're smart. And Vane is always smart."

"And you'll fight her." He wasn't asking. He was doing arithmetic — she could see him doing it, the guild-brain turning over costs. "Your teacher."

"My teacher," Sera agreed, and stood up, and drew her sword.

* * *

The duel was called properly, because Vane was proper down to the marrow. She stepped into the moon-shadow at the edge of the granite shelf with her helm under her arm and her gray braid silvered by starlight, and she looked past Sera entirely, at Bram, the way you look at a parcel awaiting delivery.

"Bram Okafor," Vane said. Her voice carried the flat warmth of a hearthstone. "Ledger-keeper, third class. Do you know how much you cost? Two captains dead in Ashfall. A vault burned. Six weeks of inquisitor's fees. You are the most expensive item this Order has misfiled in twenty years."

"He didn't do anything," Sera said.

"He *is* something," Vane corrected mildly. "Something we were told to retrieve intact. Or was that instruction unclear?" Now she looked at Sera, and her eyes were kind, and that kindness was the cruelest weapon in the Order's armory. "Serahild. Put it down. Come home. Whatever they promised you down that mountain, it isn't real. Souls aren't currency you get to spend twice."

"You know what's in the ledgers."

"I know my orders." Vane set her helm on a stone, precisely, like a woman setting a cup on a saucer. "I taught you the forms. I never taught you to argue with me during them. Draw."

Sera drew. The sound of it rang off the granite and away down the ravine, and somewhere below, she heard Bram moving — going, obeying, running for the pass with the map pressed to his chest, and every step of his retreat bought with the distance she now had to hold alone.

Vane came in like winter coming: no hurry, no malice, total commitment.

Sera had learned swordwork from this woman's hands, and so she knew the opening exchange would be a question, not an attack — three touches offered along the low line, testing whether Sera still answered the way she'd been taught. She refused the answer. Gave back the third cut high and inside, where Vane's guard had a gap the width of a held breath, and felt the old woman's surprise as a fractional widening of her stance.

"There she is," Vane murmured, almost pleased, and then stopped being gentle.

What followed was not a fight like the ones in training yards, with their counted blows and their mercies. It was two people who knew each other completely trying to kill each other with that knowledge. Vane feinted the shoulder-cut she'd used since before Sera was born and turned it into the ankle-hook only her favored students ever survived seeing. Sera countered with the riposte she'd invented herself at fifteen, the one Vane had called insolent and privately demonstrated forty times until Sera got it right. Steel sang. Boots skated on frost-slick stone. Somewhere in it Sera took a line of fire along her forearm and gave back a gash above Vane's knee, and neither of them made a sound about either wound, because making sounds was for soldiers who hadn't been taught better.

The problem was arithmetic, and Sera was facing the wrong accountant. She was nineteen years younger and faster, but Vane had fought duels before Sera could walk, and Vane was fighting with nothing held back, and Sera — Sera was fighting with something held back, some last flinch of love, and love is a slow poison in a swordfight.

Vane's blade opened her thigh. Not deep — Vane was measuring, herding, buying minutes until the other three climbed the shelf — but enough. Sera's knee buckled, went down on stone, and she saw the end of it arriving in the old woman's eyes: the rising cut, the clean finish, the mercy of a proper death for a student who had shamed the Order.

"I'm sorry, child," Vane said, and meant it, and swung.

Sera twisted inside the arc instead of away from it. Took the flat across her collarbone hard enough to whiten the world. Got her point up from the ground like a snake striking, the ugly desperate thrust no form teaches, and felt it slide home under Vane's arm, into the gap beneath the pauldron, the same gap Vane had taught her existed.

The master-captain looked down at her own blood with something like professional approval. Then she smiled, and Sera understood, one heartbeat too late, that the duel had never been the trap. The trap was that Sera would win it.

"Oathbreaker," Vane said — formally, clearly, the word carrying down the ravine — and the green light bloomed.

The brand-candle's flame leapt from the other captain's hand, or seemed to; it crossed the distance without crossing it, the way guilt crosses a room, and struck Sera's left shoulder-blade where the thorn-oath lived in her flesh. There was no pain at first. There was cold, a terrible cold like ice drawn along the spine, and then the pain arrived all at once and forever, a burning that was somehow also a *gripping*, roots of green fire sinking through muscle and wrapping bone.

She heard herself scream. It sounded very far away.

Vane knelt beside her, breathing wetly around the wound Sera had given her, and pressed something into Sera's hand — folded, small, wax-stamped. An order. Sealed orders, carried on every hunt, to be opened when the quarry was taken or the hunter fell.

"You were never meant to read it alive," Vane said softly. "But you should have read it. All these weeks." She rose, swaying. Behind her the other captains had reached the shelf, and one of them started forward, but Vane lifted two fingers and they stopped. "Let her burn. The brand does our work slower and more honestly than we would. And the boy—" her gaze went down the dark throat of the pass, "—the boy will come back for her. They always come back. Take the others and watch the far side of the mountain. We don't chase him. We wait where he must arrive."

Then, to Sera alone, with the gentleness that had been the best and worst thing in her life: "I am sorry, child. But you knew what the thorns cost when you swore them. Everything sworn is paid."

And she limped away into the dark, leaving Sera on the stone with green fire eating her shoulder and the sealed order unopened in her fist.

* * *

Bram found her an hour later. Of course he did. She had told him to keep running and he had gone perhaps half a mile before turning back, because he was Bram, because he was constitutionally incapable of abandoning anything with a debt attached, and she was learning — God help her — that she was one.

He knelt in the frost and gathered her head into his lap and said her name over and over, uselessly, wonderfully.

"It's the oath-brand," she managed. Her voice came out scorched. "Thorn-oaths are living contracts. Break the terms and the thorns enforce. It won't kill me fast. It'll kill me *slowly*. Weeks. Maybe less, on the road." She laughed, and it hurt, everything hurt, and the laugh was worth it anyway. "Very tidy instrument. The Guild would admire it."

"Don't talk like that."

"Open your coat. There's paper — Vane gave me—" She couldn't lift her arm. He understood, dug the folded order from her stiffening fingers, cracked the wax seal with his thumbnail.

She watched him read it by the thin green glow her own body was beginning to shed, watched his face change — confusion first, then horror, then something worse than both, the look of a man finding an error in his own books.

"What is it?" she whispered. "Bram. What does it say?"

He read it aloud, slowly, because she asked him to, because some things must be spoken aloud to be believed:

*"To the knight-commander assigned: the courier Bram Okafor is to be killed upon recovery of the map, regardless of circumstance. His death is not incidental to the mission. It is the mission. The map alone suffices. Confirm receipt and proceed. — Hand of Veyran."*

Silence, except the small crackle of green fire in her flesh.

"From the start," Bram said. His voice had gone very quiet, the quiet of a man balancing a column that refuses to sum. "Before the markets. Before the stranger died. Before any of it — they sent you to kill me."

"Yes." She looked up at him, at his ink-stained hand shaking above her, and there was no longer any version of honesty worth rationing. "Yes. From the start. And I stood over you in the rain with my knife out, and I couldn't do it, and I have been lying to myself about why ever since." She pulled a breath through the burning. "I'm not going to survive this mountain, Bram. The brand sees to that. So hear me while I can still say it plainly, because I have been a coward twice over — once with the knife, and once with this."

"Don't," he said. "Save your strength."

"The hell with my strength." The green light flared with her pulse, and she let it, let it light his face, let him see all of her at last. "I love you. I have loved you since the counting desk, since you wept over a stranger and apologized to *me* for it. Too late, and badly timed, and paid for in—" she gestured, weakly, at her smoking shoulder, "—in this. But there it is. Entered in the ledger. Let the Court itself audit it."

Above them the pass stood open, black against the stars, and beyond the pass the mountain waited with its cathedral of glass and its cages full of souls — and somewhere inside it, she was certain now, certain the way she had never been certain of any order, was the thing that could pull green fire out of a person's bones.

Bram closed the order in his fist. When he spoke, the ledger-quiet had hardened into something she had not heard from him before, something with edges.

"Then we go down tonight," he said. "Because I am not letting the Court collect you, Sera. Not you. I'll put it in writing."

He lifted her, brand and all, and began to climb.
