## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not open so much as admit them.

Bram had imagined doors, hinges, some great stone jaw that would grind apart at a word from the map. Instead, on the third day beneath the earth, when Sera's brand had burned so hot he could feel it against his back as they slept curled together for warmth, the passage simply ended in smooth black rock — and then the rock was no longer there. No sound. No seam. One moment the wall; the next, air that smelled of ink and old paper and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun.

He looked down at the map in his hands. The bloodstain that had marked it since Ashfall had spread while they walked, creeping along the vellum like frost across a window, until now it covered everything except a single line of text near the bottom edge, written in the founder's own cramped hand:

*All accounts must balance.*

"Whatever happens in there," Bram said, "don't sign anything."

Sera managed something that was almost a laugh. Her face had gone grey over the weeks of their descent, and the thorn-brand at her throat had climbed to her jaw, black filaments visible beneath the skin like veins of iron ore. She'd stopped pretending the pain didn't come in waves. Now she simply rode them out, gripping his hand until her knuckles whitened, then releasing it and saying, *I'm fine*, which they both knew was a lie, and which they both let stand because the truth was too heavy to carry uphill.

"I'm a knight-commander of the Thorn," she said. "We don't sign. We swear."

"That's worse."

"It's faster." She drew her sword out of habit, looked at it, and slid it home again. "No. I go as I am. If this place wants a bargain, let it look at what's left of me and be ashamed."

They walked in.

* * *

The court was a cathedral, and the cathedral was a ledger.

That was Bram's first coherent thought, and his last for a long while, because every other thought drowned in the scale of it. The hall rose higher than any structure built by human hands — pillars of glass, each one thick as an ancient oak, climbing into darkness where they became indistinguishable from the night sky. And inside each pillar, suspended like flies in amber, hung shapes.

People.

Some were curled like sleeping children. Some were rigid, mouths open mid-scream, frozen at the moment of extraction. Some were only fragments — a pair of hands, a woman's laugh given weight and light, a soldier's courage floating alone in its column. Thousands of pillars. Tens of thousands. They receded into the dark above and away into side chapels of the vast nave, and the floor itself was polished obsidian, and on that floor were written lines of script that moved — slowly, endlessly rearranging themselves, contracts drafting and redrafting themselves in perpetuity.

"The guild ledger," Bram whispered. "It's all one ledger. Everything we ever recorded — it's just a copy."

"A copy of what?"

"Of this." His voice came out strange to him. "We never traded impossible things. We were clerks. We've been clerks for four hundred years, filing paperwork for *this*."

At the far end of the nave, where an altar would stand, hung a single pillar of clear glass, taller than the rest and finer worked, with silver fittings at its base and a chain of small bells that rang without wind. Inside it floated a woman.

She was old and young at once, the way souls are — her shape shifting as he watched, a grey-haired matriarch becoming a red-headed girl becoming something in between, all of them her, all of them true. Even caged, even after centuries, she burned. Bram knew her face. It was carved over the door of every guildhall in the free cities. It was printed on the frontispiece of every ledger.

Maren Okafor-Vey. Founder. First Ledger-Keeper. The woman who had sold herself to buy the guild its power.

And by a cruelty or a joke of history that Bram still could not fully absorb: his ancestor. His mother's mother's mother, four generations back, which meant the court had known exactly whose descendant it was tempting when the map found its way to him.

"She can see us," Sera said quietly.

She could. The shifting faces turned toward them, and though no sound crossed the glass, Bram felt attention settle on him like snowfall — patient, enormous, and unbearably sad.

"You should not have come," said a voice behind him.

* * *

The steward was tall in the way that architecture is tall, and dressed in grey robes that resolved, when Bram looked directly at them, into columns of fine print. Its face was pleasant and utterly forgettable; Bram knew he would be unable to describe it a minute after looking away. It held no weapon. It carried, instead, a writing case of black lacquer, and it opened this now with the tenderness of a parent unpacking a child's gift.

"Bram Okafor," it said. "Junior ledger-keeper, third class. Son of Ilse Okafor, deceased. Nephew of debts. Inheritor of maps." It smiled without moving its mouth. "You have your grandmother-founder's handwriting. Did you know? The slant of the numerals is identical."

"What do you want?"

"What you want. A trade." The steward began laying out instruments on a lectern of glass that had not been there a moment before: a pen, an inkwell, two sheets of vellum. "You came all this way. You watched your guildhall burn. You read the sealed entries — yes, we know about the vault wall; we keep excellent records — and you found your mother's name in our books. Ilse Okafor. Sold, not given. Purchased by intermediaries acting for a man named Veyran, who is, I note with professional interest, descended from the same line as yourself. Your family does so enjoy selling its women."

Every word landed like a blow. Bram had told himself he was prepared. He had rehearsed speeches in the dark of the underways, arguments, appeals, threats. None of them survived contact with the thing's voice.

"Ilse's fragment is here," the steward continued, gesturing, and one of the nearer pillars brightened. Inside it, a shape stirred — small, tired, unmistakably her. Bram's knees nearly went. He heard Sera catch her breath beside him. "Her laughter is here. Her stubbornness. The way she hummed when she balanced accounts. All of it, preserved perfectly. We are very careful custodians, whatever else you believe about us."

"And the price."

"For your mother?" The steward tilted its forgettable head. "For your mother, the map. For the founder—" it nodded toward the silver-fitted pillar, where Maren's shifting faces had gone very still, listening— "a trifle more. Your name."

The word dropped into the silence and lay there.

"Your name," the steward repeated gently, as if explaining interest rates to a child. "Not your life. Not your memories — those are yours to keep, such as they are. Merely the fact of you. Bram Okafor, written out of existence. Your mother's entry amended: *no issue.* Your guild records amended: *position vacant.* Every person who ever knew you will find a small hole in their memory where you stood, and around that hole they will build reasonable explanations, because people always do. You will walk out of here with your mother's soul in your arms and no one in the world — including, eventually, yourself — able to say why you came."

"And Veyran?" Sera's voice cracked like a whip. "His army of the hollow. His stolen soldiers. Does the trade cover them?"

The steward regarded her with something almost like respect. "The trade covers what was discussed. Nothing was discussed about armies."

"There's nothing to discuss," Bram said.

"Of course there isn't. There rarely is, at first." The steward dipped the pen. "But consider, before you refuse. You are a bookkeeper. Look at the arithmetic honestly, as you were trained. One map, one name, weighed against your mother entire. Against the founder's soul, freed after four hundred years of captivity — a debt of honor, if you like. Against Sera Venn, who has perhaps a season left before the brand reaches her heart, and who might, in another negotiation, be persuaded to part with—" it glanced at the brand, and Sera stepped between it and Bram, sword half-drawn despite everything— "ah. Loyalty. That's a rare vintage. But I digress. Refuse, and you leave with nothing. Accept, and you leave with everything except the ability to know what you have. Many clients have called that mercy."

Bram looked at the pillar where his mother floated.

He thought of her humming. Of teaching him to hold a pen. Of the sealed ledger and her name in faded brown ink, sold by men who balanced columns while she screamed somewhere beneath the world. He thought of Ashfall burning. He thought of Sera's hand in his through the cold miles, and the confession on the road — *they ordered me to kill you from the start* — and how she'd chosen, and what the choosing had cost her flesh.

Everything he had lost was arranged in front of him like items on a manifest. And the court was offering to sell it back, piece by piece, at prices that seemed almost reasonable, and that was the horror of it, that was the trap in the geometry: the arithmetic *worked*. He could feel it working. Give up the map, give up himself, save her. Any auditor in the free cities would call it a fair exchange.

"All accounts must balance," Bram said.

"Just so," said the steward, pleased.

"But you're quoting the wrong ledger." He reached into his coat and drew out the map — and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart these many weeks, the older thing: three pages torn from the vault wall of the Ashfall guildhall, rescued from the fire, scorched at the edges but legible. Maren Okafor-Vey's original contract. He had read it a hundred times on the road. He had read it again last night, by candle stub, while Sera slept.

The steward's pen paused above the vellum.

"In four hundred years," Bram said, and his voice did not shake, because he was, at bottom, a ledger-keeper, and numbers steadied him the way prayer steadies other men, "no one has ever audited you. Everyone comes here to negotiate. To haggle. To accept terms. But a debt honestly audited must be voided — your own law, written into the first line of your first contract, because even you wanted a witness to your fairness. So audit us." He laid the scorched pages on the glass lectern, one by one, and smoothed them flat with both hands. "Clause nine. The guild purchased power; the price was one soul, freely offered, held in trust against services rendered. Held in *trust*, steward. Not owned. And the services — read clause eleven — were rendered in full within the founder's lifetime. The debt closed four hundred years ago. Everything since has been collection without authority."

The cathedral went silent. Even the moving script on the floor stilled.

High above, in the silver-fitted pillar, the founder's shifting faces settled into one expression, and it was not sadness anymore.

It was a smile.

The steward set down its pen, slowly, and for the first time its forgettable face remembered itself enough to look afraid. "You are proposing," it said, "to file a claim against this court."

"No," Bram said, taking out his own pen — his mother's pen, the one she'd pressed into his hand at the counting desk in another life. "I'm proposing to close your books."

Behind him, far above ground, faint as memory, came the first distant thunder of Veyran's army reaching Callowmere's walls.
