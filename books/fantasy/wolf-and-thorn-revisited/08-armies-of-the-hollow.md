## 8. Armies of the Hollow

The walls of Callowmere were old when the guild was young, and they sang when the hollow soldiers touched them.

Bram heard it first as a wrongness in the stone — a thin, high keening that came up through his boots and settled in his teeth. He stood at the eastern battlements beside a militia captain who had stopped giving orders, her sword arm hanging slack, her face turned toward the plain below with the particular stillness of someone watching something they will spend the rest of their life failing to unsee.

The army did not march like an army. It flowed. Ten thousand soldiers moving in perfect silence, no drums, no shouted cadence, no ragged edge of fear or fury — just a gray tide of men and women advancing across the frost-browned fields with the smooth, terrible patience of water finding its level. Their armor was mismatched, scavenged, the plate of a dozen defeated cities worn over faces that had been scoured clean of everything but purpose.

They had bought that purpose from his guild. Fragment by fragment, trade by trade, through intermediaries Bram had signed receipts for without ever asking what the ledgers balanced against.

"They're not stopping," the captain said. Her voice sounded like someone reading aloud from a book she hated. "We've loosed everything we have. They're not even slowing."

Below the walls, arrows stood in the earth like a harvested field. The hollow soldiers walked through them. One took three shafts in the chest and did not break stride; there was nothing left inside him to flinch. Where the defenders' ballistae found gaps in the press, the fallen were simply stepped over, absorbed, their places closed by the tide behind them as though death were a clerical error to be corrected on the next page.

And where the tide met the gates, it began, quietly and without haste, to take them apart.

"Get off the wall," Sera said behind him.

She had come up the stairs leaning on the rail, and even now — even now — she moved like a knight-commander, shoulders square, chin level. But her right hand was pressed flat against her side where the thorn-brand sat beneath her shirt, and when she pulled her fingers away, Bram saw the black vines had climbed past her ribs. He could see them in the hollow of her throat now, delicate as inked lace, and where they touched skin the flesh had gone gray-white, frostbitten in high summer.

"You shouldn't be up here," he said.

"Neither should you." She looked out over the plain, and something crossed her face that was not fear. It was worse. It was recognition. "I trained some of them," she said quietly. "Before. Thorn-sworn who fell to Veyran's coin. I told myself they'd chosen it." Her jaw tightened. "Nobody chooses this. This is what happens when someone else does your choosing for you."

A section of wall two hundred paces south shuddered. Dust sifted from the mortar joints. The singing in the stone rose half a tone.

"The passes," Bram said. "How many can we get through?"

"At this rate? A third. Maybe less." Sera's eyes tracked the gray tide. "The alliance council broke apart this morning. Callowmere wants to hold the walls; Vellmark wants to save its own grain; the river lords are already gone. There is no last stand, Bram. There's just everyone dying in whatever order the arithmetic allows."

His arithmetic. His guild's arithmetic. He watched a ladder go up against the southern tower and thought: *we sold them the courage to climb it.*

* * *

Sera saw the breach coming before anyone else did, because she had made breaches herself.

It began at the postern gate — of course it did; it always began somewhere small, somewhere someone had grown tired or frightened or bought. The hollow soldiers didn't storm it. They simply opened it, the way you open a door in your own house, and the tide poured through into the streets beyond, and the screaming started, and the singing in the stones became a shriek.

She got Bram off the wall by main force, hauling him down the stairwell while the garrison above died competently and in vain. The streets of Callowmere were a lesson in everything she had spent nine years pretending not to know: the mathematics of sacrifice, civilians weighed against soldiers, soldiers weighed against time, every column of figures ending in blood. She'd been taught the math by thorn-captains who called it duty. Watching it performed here, in the smoke and the running crowds, she finally understood that duty was just the word they used so no one had to say *cost.*

Because everything cost. That was the whole of it. Every choice. She had learned that beneath the mountain, standing in that cathedral of contracts, watching the steward smile his patient, paper-smile. Everything cost, and the court kept immaculate books, and her brand was simply an invoice arriving in installments.

It burned now as they ran — a cold fire climbing her spine vertebra by vertebra, each pulse a little higher than the last. She could feel it mapping her. Learning her shape. Somewhere in the vaults of her order, a ledger entry was updating itself: *oathbreaker, status pending.*

They reached the refugee road at dusk.

It was not a road anymore. It was a river of people flowing north into the passes — carts, children, old men carrying doors because doors could become stretchers, a woman driving geese that honked indignantly at the end of the world. Behind them, Callowmere burned against a bruised sky, and the light of it turned every face on the road the color of embers.

Sera stopped walking.

Not because she chose to. Because her legs informed her, politely and without appeal, that the discussion was over. She went down onto one knee at the roadside, and Bram caught her elbow, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around stones — too busy surviving to look twice at two more casualties.

"Bram." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Listen to me."

"I'm listening."

"No, you're not. You're doing the thing you do. The adding thing." She tried to laugh and it came out as a cough. "Stop adding. Listen."

He knelt in front of her. In the firelight from the burning city his face was all planes and shadow, and his hands — those careful ledger-keeper's hands — were shaking around hers. She had kissed those hands once, in the dark under the mountain, back when she'd still believed there might be a version of this story where they both walked away.

"The steward's offer stands," she said. "You know it does. The court doesn't close. It never closes. You give them the map and your name, and they give you—" She gestured vaguely upward, at the smoke, at the fleeing country, at everything. "Whatever you ask. Enough power to stop this. Enough souls to buy an army of your own. Enough to walk into Veyran's camp tomorrow and unmake him."

"And lose my name."

"Yes."

"And forget you."

The words hung between them. Around them the river of refugees went on flowing, a child crying somewhere, cart wheels groaning, the whole world in motion except the two of them kneeling in its eddy.

"That's the price," Sera said. "That's what things cost. I told you — I've known since the duel. Since I put my blade through Aldric's guard and felt the brand light up like a struck match." She swallowed. Gray crept past her collarbone as she spoke, patient as rising water. "I begged you not to make me love you, do you understand? Back on the underways. Because I could already see the bill coming due. And now it's here, and it's me, and you're going to sit there and refuse to pay it because you're *good*, and then you're going to die being good, and none of it — none of it — will have meant anything."

"Sera—"

"Take the deal." Her hands closed hard on his wrists, and he felt how little strength was left in them, and it was like holding a bird. "Live. Be nameless. Be safe. Let the court have the ledger-keeper nobody remembers, and let me have the five minutes I get to spend knowing you before—" Her voice cracked clean through. "Before the invoice closes."

Above the passes, the sky had gone the color of a dead hearth. Somewhere behind them, the hollow soldiers were marching north along the same road, unhurried, inevitable, and the refugees ahead had begun to sense it, the way animals sense weather, the crowd's murmur tightening toward panic.

Bram looked at her for a long moment.

Then he did the thing he did. The adding thing.

"One soul honestly audited voids a debt," he said softly. "One. That's the law the steward himself confirmed. The founder's contract proves the guild overpaid — one soul, returned, unmakes the whole claim." He turned her hands over, traced the black lace creeping across her knuckles. "Veyran bought ten thousand fragments. If the court's claim falls, every purchase falls with it. Every hollow soldier wakes up inside their own body again. Mid-march. All at once."

"It won't work."

"It doesn't have to work." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she had ever seen on him. "It only has to be true. That's the entire trick of honest bookkeeping, Sera. You can't argue with it. You can only wish it weren't true."

"The steward will find a way. They always find a way—"

"Then I'll find a truer way." He stood, pulling her arm across his shoulders, lifting her back onto her feet though she swayed like a sapling. "You don't get to spend yourself buying my escape. That's not a trade. That's theft. And I'm done trading."

Ahead, the pass narrowed between walls of black rock, choked with the last of the fleeing city. Behind, Callowmere's glow was fading as the fires sank into ash. And between the two, far down the darkening road, the first rank of hollow soldiers appeared — silent, tireless, closing like a door.

Sera looked at them, and then at Bram, and felt the brand take another inch of her heart.

"There's a shrine in the pass," she said hoarsely. "Old road-god, pre-guild. If any stone in this country remembers what free giving looks like, it'll be that one." Her eyes found his. "And Bram — if the court asks for a counterweight, for collateral, for anything with your name on it—"

"I know the price of names," he said. "I read the ledger."

They turned north together, two small figures walking into the narrowing dark, and behind them the silent army came on, and ahead — faint, almost imagined, like a note held at the bottom of a well — the stones of the pass had begun, very softly, to sing.
