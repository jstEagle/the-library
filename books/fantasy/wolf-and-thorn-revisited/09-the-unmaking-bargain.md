## 9. The Unmaking Bargain

The cathedral of contracts had no ceiling that Bram could find. Its vaults climbed upward until they dissolved into a darkness shot through with veins of amber light, and along those veins hung the souls — thousands upon thousands of them, each sealed in glass like wasps in honey, each one flickering with the small stubborn light of a life that refused to be finished.

Somewhere above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, Callowmere was burning.

Bram could feel it in his teeth. He could feel everything down here in his teeth: the hum of the caged souls, the slow grinding attention of the court itself, the wet rasp of Sera's breathing where she lay propped against the base of the great ledger-desk, her thorn-brand glowing through the leather of her jerkin like a coal wrapped in cloth. Every hour it spread. Every hour another inch of her skin blackened around it, and every hour she smiled at him as if to say *don't you dare count this against yourself.*

He had spent his whole life learning not to flinch at numbers. This was the number he could not make balance.

"You are still deciding," said the steward.

It stood across the contract-floor from him, on the far side of the founder's cage — a tall figure made of folded parchment and old wax seals, its face a blank page that somehow managed to look patient. Around it, the court's lesser members had gathered: things like clerks, things like bailiffs, shapes that might once have been people who had traded too much of themselves away. Their stillness had weight. The whole cathedral leaned inward to listen.

"I'm auditing," Bram said.

His voice sounded absurdly small. So did the object in his hands — a slim book bound in cracked green leather, its pages soft as cloth from three hundred years of handling. The guild's first ledger. The founder's own hand. He had carried it through fire, through the underways, through Veyran's passes, tucked against his ribs like a second heart. It had cost him blood to keep it. It would cost more before dawn.

"An audit," the steward repeated. The word came out of its blank face slowly, the way a coin comes out of a miser's fist. "You stand in the Court Beneath the Mountain, surrounded by the sum of all bargains ever honored here, and you propose to *audit*."

"The court has one law it cannot break," Bram said. "Your own law. I've read your contracts. All of them follow the same clause — the oldest one, the binding one. A debt honestly audited must be voided. Not renegotiated. Not deferred. *Voided.* Restitution paid in full." He lifted the green ledger. "So audit me."

Silence. Even Sera stopped breathing.

The steward tilted its head, and somewhere high overhead a vein of amber light flickered. "Every soul who descends here comes to trade," it said. "They offer names. Years. Loves. Memories. You come offering arithmetic."

"Arithmetic is what your power is built on," Bram said. "You bought the guild's greatness with the founder's soul. And the guild built its ledgers on yours. But ledgers only work if they're honest. So let's be honest." His hands were shaking. He let them shake. There was no shame in trembling while you held a blade. "Open the books."

* * *

The founder's name had been Maren Vale, though the guild histories called her only the First Keeper, as if even her name had been collateral. Her handwriting filled the first pages of the green ledger in ink gone brown as dried roses: careful figures, meticulous sums, and then — on the page Bram had feared since Ashfall — the contract itself.

*To the Court Beneath the Mountain, from Maren Vale, who offers that which cannot be priced: my soul entire, in perpetual bond, in exchange for—*

And there the clause began. The exchange. The consideration. The thing the court had promised in return.

Bram read it aloud, because an audit unspoken was no audit at all, and his voice rang off the glass cages and came back strange.

"—in exchange for the guild's continuance: that the Court shall furnish to the Guild of Impossible Things such wonders as may be sold, drawn from the Court's own store, and that the Court shall claim from none other within the guild's walls so long as the debt is served."

He looked up. "Do you see it?"

"I see a contract honored for three hundred years," the steward said.

"No," Bram said. "You see a contract *misread* for three hundred years." He turned the ledger around and set it on the contract-floor between them, open, the way he had laid out a thousand disputed accounts back in the guildhall of Ashfall when two merchants stood ready to kill each other over a miscounted crate. "Read the consideration. *Such wonders as may be sold.* Wonders furnished *from the Court's own store.*"

"Yes."

"Then answer me this, steward, and answer honestly, because your law will know if you lie." He drew a breath that tasted of cold stone and burning cities. "Where does the Court's store come from?"

The blank face did not move. But the cathedral moved around it. The amber veins dimmed, brightened, dimmed again, like something vast drawing a breath.

"The store is replenished," the steward said at last, "by bonds taken. As any lender's store is."

"And who were those bonds taken *from?*"

"Debtors."

"Willing debtors?" Bram flipped forward through the green ledger, past Maren Vale's death, past generations of guild-keepers who had signed and countersigned and never once asked the question he was asking now. "Show me one contract in three hundred years where a soul came to you freely, knowing the full price, with fair notice and fair terms. Show me one. Because I've read the guild's ledgers, and the guild's ledgers say the *guild* took them — tricked them, frightened them, bought their desperation at famine prices. Your own law requires honest dealing. Your own law, steward. The one law you cannot break."

"We are not bound by the debts of mortals—"

"You're bound by your own," Bram said. "That's the whole point of a court."

* * *

Here is what Bram Okafor knew, in the marrow-deep way that only a ledger-keeper knows anything: numbers do not care who is listening. Numbers simply wait, patient as graves, for someone to read them correctly.

He had found it in the underways, three days ago, by the light of a guttering lamp while Sera slept against his shoulder with her brand burning her alive by inches. He had found it the way he'd found everything — by refusing to stop adding.

Maren Vale had offered her soul entire. That was the principal.

But the clause promised wonders *in perpetuity*, drawn from the Court's own store, and the Court's own store was replenished by the very souls the guild brought down the mountain — souls the guild harvested using powers the Court had *sold* them. Which meant the Court had been repaying its debt to the guild with currency it was simultaneously extracting from the guild's victims.

Which meant, in plain and terrible arithmetic, that the Court had been paying the guild with the guild's own money.

Three hundred years of double-counting. Three hundred years of interest charged on funds already owed, of a loan serviced by theft from both sides of the table. Any clerk in Ashfall would have been hanged for a ledger half this corrupt.

"A debt honestly audited must be voided," Bram said quietly. "Voided means voided. Not restructured. Not forgiven. Voided — as though it never existed. Restitution in full, from the party in breach." He closed the green ledger and held it against his chest. "The party in breach is the Court. The restitution is everything the Court holds that it obtained dishonestly." His voice cracked, and he let it. "Every soul. Every fragment. My mother's. The courier's. The elders who withered in their chairs. All of them. Returned."

The cathedral screamed.

There was no other word for it. The amber veins flared white, and the glass cages rang like a thousand struck bells, and the lesser creatures of the court recoiled toward the walls as if the floor had become acid. The steward stood motionless amid it all, its blank page of a face turned down toward the green ledger, reading — actually reading, Bram realized — the founder's hand. Reading the clause it had enforced faithfully for three centuries without once, apparently, doing the math.

"You understand what you demand," the steward said. Its voice had changed. Slower. Older. Almost human. "If the debt is voided, the guild loses its power. The wonders end. Everything your guild has sold, everything it is — undone."

"I know."

"If the debt is voided, the bonds dissolve. The hollow soldiers above — the usurper's army — their purchased fragments will tear free mid-march. They will fall where they stand. Some will die. Some will wake and remember what they did."

"I know."

"And there is always a price for the one who forces an audit." The steward raised its head. "The auditor stakes himself. That is the law beneath the law. You have heard it?"

Bram had heard it. In the underways, whispered by things in the dark. *The auditor stakes himself; the court takes its fee from whoever opens the books.* He had known since Callowmere. Since the map. Perhaps since the stranger died against his counting desk with a bloodstained map in his hands and the words *the debt is due* on his lips.

He looked at Sera. She was trying to rise — of course she was, thorn-brand or no, oathbreaker or no, she was trying to get to her feet to stand beside him, and her legs were failing her, and she was cursing them in the soldier's cadence he'd learned to love on the road. She caught him looking and went still.

"Don't," she said. "Whatever it's about to take — don't pay it thinking about me."

"That's the only way I know how to think about anything," Bram said.

"Bram—"

"Hey." He smiled at her, and his eyes stung, and he didn't hide it. "Keep the books with me. One last time. Ready?"

Her jaw tightened. Her hand found his, brand-blackened fingers lacing through his ink-stained ones. "Ready."

Together they opened the green ledger to the final page, and Bram wrote — in a steady hand, in the old formal script every guild child learned first — the words that ended three hundred years of theft:

*Audited in full. Debt voided. Restitution ordered: all bonds released, all souls returned, all claims unmade.*

"By the law beneath the law," Bram said aloud, "let it be so."

* * *

The fee reached for him like winter.

Not pain — nothing so simple. It was subtraction. It reached into him and began removing things, carefully, the way he himself removed entries: line by line, with a clerk's neatness. He felt it take the smell of rain on Ashfall's market roofs. He felt it take the sound of Sera laughing for the first time, deep in the underways, at some joke about thorns. He felt it take the road, and the fire, and the fear, and—

No. Not her. Please, not all of her—

He gripped the desk. He kept writing. Somewhere impossibly far away, Sera was shouting his name.

Above them — a hundred fathoms up, beyond the stone, beyond the burning gates of Callowmere — the hollow army of Veyran the Usurper marched with ten thousand faces empty of fear or mercy. And then, in the same instant, all across the frozen fields, the fragments tore free. Glass-shards of soul ripped out of ten thousand chests, streaming upward like reversed snow, and the hollow soldiers dropped their weapons and fell to their knees and *woke* — weeping, screaming, remembering — and Veyran's perfect army collapsed in the space of a single breath, mid-march, mid-siege, undone by an entry in a book.

Below them, the Court Beneath the Mountain shattered like the lie it had always been. The amber veins burst. The glass cages broke open in cascades, and the souls rose — thousands of them, silver and gold and every color grief has — pouring up through the cracking dark toward the world they'd been stolen from. Among them, last of all, a single light the color of morning left the smallest cage and paused, briefly, beside a boy bent over a ledger with tears running into the ink.

Maren Vale's soul touched the green book once, gently, like a grandmother closing her grandson's collar against the cold.

Then it, too, went home.

* * *

Bram woke on the contract-floor to silence and drifting ash and Sera's hands on his face.

The brand on her arm had cooled to a dull scar. The cathedral was a ruin of broken glass and settling dust, open now to a sky he could actually see — gray, raining, real. She was saying his name over and over, and he knew her, he knew her, he knew her face, her voice, the fact of her —

But when he tried to remember the road they had walked together, the market where she'd first watched him weep, the duel, the confession, the night she'd told him she loved him — there was nothing. Clean paper. Blank lines, awaiting entry.

"Sera?" he said. It was the right name. He was almost sure it was the right name. "Why am I crying?"

She laughed — a broken, wondering sound — and pressed her forehead to his, and held up, between them, a small ledger written in his own hand, its last entry dated yesterday:

*Paid in full. Worth it.*

"Well," she said, wiping his cheek with her thumb, "I suppose I'd better start from the beginning."
