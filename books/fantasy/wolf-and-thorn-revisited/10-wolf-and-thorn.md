## 10. Wolf and Thorn

**SERA**

The guildhall of Ashfall rose from its own ashes the way spring came to the thorn-fields: slowly, stubbornly, without asking anyone's permission.

Sera stood in the courtyard where the counting desks had once been, now open to the sky. The old vault wall had been pulled down stone by stone — Bram's order, issued in a voice that didn't sound like his, because it wasn't entirely his anymore — and the sealed ledgers were laid out on trestle tables in the sunlight for anyone to read. Anyone. That was the whole of the new law. The Guild of Impossible Things would keep no secrets behind walls again.

The clerks were rewriting everything. She watched them from beneath the broken archway, her cloak drawn close against the autumn wind, and marveled at how ordinary it looked. Ink. Paper. Young people arguing over entries. It did not look like the end of an age of stolen souls. It looked like a harvest being brought in honestly, at last.

"Knight-commander." One of the elders — Elder Vess, the only one who had survived the purges because she'd been too unimportant to burn — approached with the careful gait of someone still not certain the ground would hold. "The council has voted. The thorn-sworn are welcome here. All of them. Even you."

"I'm not knight-commander anymore," Sera said. "I broke my oath."

"You broke *their* oath," Vess said. "The one written in blood and sold by the pound. The council is drafting a new one, if you care to hear it. Shorter. Kinder. It says only this: *that which is given freely may not be taken back.*"

Sera turned the words over. They sat strangely light in her chest where the old oath had been a barbed weight for twenty years.

"And the brand?" she asked. She didn't have to explain what she meant. The scar ran from her left wrist to her elbow, silver-white against sun-browned skin, cool as riverstone since the night the court shattered. The thorn-captains' brands had been fed by the Court Beneath the Mountain; when Bram voided the debt, every brand in the world had gone quiet. Some knights had wept when theirs cooled. Some had screamed, because the pain had been the proof they were still bound to something. Sera had simply stood in the ruins of Callowmere's gate and breathed, for the first time in years, without fire under her skin.

"The brand is dead," Vess said. "But scars keep their own records. I'm told yours says you dueled Captain Maren to a standstill with your arm already burning."

"She was my mentor."

"And you loved her. And you chose him anyway." Vess smiled, small and dry. "Yes. We'd very much like you in the new guild, Knight— forgive me. *Ser* Sera. If you want a place that doesn't ask for your soul."

Sera looked past the elder, to the far corner of the courtyard, where a young man with ink-stained fingers sat at a plain wooden desk, frowning at a ledger as though it had spoken to him rudely.

Bram Okafor balanced accounts the way other men prayed. He had always done so — she knew that much from the journey, from the way he'd counted coins in a burned-out waystation, counted steps down into the dark, counted the seconds between her heartbeats when she lay fevered with the brand. He counted because counting was how he held the world still.

He did not remember her.

That was the arithmetic of the court's shattering, the part of the price that could not be refunded. Every soul torn free of the mountain had come home — except the portion of Bram himself he had staked to make his claim stand. His name had been offered to the steward, and though the court died before it could collect, the offering had cost him. Not his name. His year. The road, the rain-slick markets, the fire, the underways, Callowmere, the duel, the descent, the cathedral of contracts — all of it gone, wiped clean as a struck entry. He remembered a stranger dying at his desk. He remembered a map. Everything after was blank paper.

Everything after was her.

"He asks about you," Vess said quietly. "Every day. He writes questions in his ledger. Today's question, if you want to know, is *what color were her eyes, and why does my chest hurt when I try to remember.*"

Sera's throat tightened. "And what did you tell him?"

"Nothing. It isn't mine to tell." Vess bowed, old-fashioned and sincere. "It's yours. Whenever you're ready. Or never. Freely given, Ser Sera. That's the law now — for gifts, and for griefs, and for everything else."

She walked out across the courtyard alone.

* * *

**BRAM**

There was a woman crossing the yard toward him, and Bram Okafor could not stop watching her.

He knew her name. Everyone in the rebuilt guildhall knew her name; she was legend by now, the oathbreaker who had stood at the gates of Callowmere, the knight whose brand had burned itself out the moment the mountain fell silent. Sera. He could say it. What he couldn't do was remember saying it before, and that absence had a shape, the way a missing tooth has a shape — his tongue kept going to it.

She stopped at the edge of his desk. Up close she was taller than he expected, with a duelist's balance even standing still, and gray eyes that looked at him the way people look at a house they grew up in.

"You're staring," she said. Not unkindly.

"I apologize. You're—" He stopped. Started again. Ledger-keeper's honesty was the only kind he trusted anymore. "You're familiar to me. In a way that doesn't make sense. Like a word in a language I used to speak."

Something moved through her face — grief, quickly folded away, neat as a clerk folding a letter. "I know."

"Elder Vess says you fought for me. That you—" He glanced down at the ledger, at the entry he still couldn't explain. His own handwriting, dated the night the court fell, three words in a firm hand: *worth it.* He had no memory of writing them. He had checked the ink, the nib, the pressure of the strokes. All his. "I wrote that. I don't know what *it* was."

"No," she said softly. "You wouldn't."

"It seems important that I find out."

She was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved the loose pages on the tables behind her; somewhere a clerk laughed at something. Then she pulled up the stool across from him and sat, settling her sword — she still wore it, though the new guild needed no swords — with the ease of long habit.

"Then let's do it properly," Sera said. "You're a ledger-keeper. So we'll keep a ledger."

"A ledger of what?"

"Of you." She set a fresh page before him, turned it, and uncapped the ink bottle with a click. "One entry a day. Whatever you can bear to know. I'll answer any question you ask, and I'll ask your leave before telling you anything you haven't asked. Freely given. That's the law here now — Vess will be pleased you're learning it already."

Bram looked at the blank page. It frightened him more than the map had, more than the sealed ledgers behind the vault wall, more than the burning guildhall he'd been told about in fragments by people who spoke carefully around him. A blank page was a debt waiting to be entered, and he had learned — everyone had learned — what debts could grow into when no one audited them.

"What was I?" he asked. "Before. On the road. Was I brave?"

Her mouth curved. Not quite a smile; something older and more tired and more tender underneath it. "You were terrified almost the entire time. You went anyway. You stood in front of a god made of contracts and told it its own books were wrong — and you were right." She tapped the page. "Entry one. Your call, ledger-keeper."

His pen hovered. He found his hands weren't shaking, which surprised him.

"Start with the beginning," he said. "The markets. The rain. You found me there, didn't you? Before all this?"

For just a heartbeat her composure slipped — a hairline crack, there and gone — and Bram understood that whatever the beginning was, it had cost her something to witness.

"Yes," Sera said. "I found you in the rain. You were kneeling beside a dying man, crying over a stranger, and I'd been ordered to kill you, and I thought—" She stopped. Drew breath. "I thought, *there is no version of the world where this man deserves what they've asked me to do.* That was the first impossible thing the guild ever gave me for free."

Bram wrote it down. The date. Her name. The sentence itself, verbatim, because some things should not be paraphrased. When he finished, he looked up and found her watching his pen move with an expression he couldn't name — hope wearing patience as armor.

"Will you come back tomorrow?" he asked.

"Every day," she said. "As long as it takes. As long as you want."

"That could be a long time. Years. Maybe—" He hesitated over it, the cruelest possibility, and decided he owed her the truth as much as she owed him the story. "Maybe the memories never come back. Maybe I learn you like a stranger learns you. Maybe I'm always a little bit someone you lost."

Sera reached across the desk. Her scarred hand covered his ink-stained one, warm and steady.

"Bram," she said, and his name in her mouth sounded worn smooth, handled often, treasured. "I spent a year loving you while the world tried to take you from me. I can spend ten more teaching you back. Love that has to begin twice is still love. It might even count double."

He laughed — startled by it, the sound coming out of him unbidden — and wrote one more line beneath the first entry, small, in the margin:

*She stayed.*

* * *

**SERA**

That evening she climbed the guildhall stairs to the room they'd given her — high, west-facing, full of the last gold light — and took out the journal she'd kept on the road. Water-warped. Bloodstained at one corner. Hers.

She did not read it. Not yet. Some days she wasn't sure she ever would; some days she wanted to press it into his hands and say *here, this was us, all of it, read fast before I lose my nerve.* But Vess was right. Gifts given under pressure weren't freely given. He would get the story the way he got everything now — one honest entry at a time, at his own pace, in his own hand.

Instead she opened to the last written page and added a line of her own, beneath the record of the court's shattering:

*Day 1. He asked about the rain. He wrote down my name like it mattered. The scar aches when I'm happy — Maren never mentioned that. Let it ache.*

Below the window, the city of Ashfall glowed with lamplight, and beyond it the free cities were rebuilding, and beyond them, in the deep places under the mountain, the silence where the Court had been went on and on — not empty, exactly. Resting. Contracts, Bram had said once, in a voice she'd recorded in her journal and he would someday hear again, are only promises that forgot how to be kind. Perhaps the same was true of courts. Perhaps something slept down there that might, in another age, wake gentler.

Or perhaps not. She was a soldier still, under everything. She kept her sword by the door.

A knock. Soft. She knew the rhythm of it already — one day, and he already had a rhythm.

"Come in," Sera said, closing the journal.

Bram stood in the doorway with his ledger hugged to his chest, looking younger and older than his years at once, and behind him the evening star was rising over the mountain like the first entry in a book that had finally learned to write itself kindly.

"I finished today's page," he said. "I had a question. About the duel. About what you paid." He swallowed. "And then I had a question that isn't for the ledger at all."

Sera smiled, and let the door swing wide.
