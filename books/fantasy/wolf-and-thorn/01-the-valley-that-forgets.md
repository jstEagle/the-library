## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Thorn Valley had a way of making even the dead seem unhurried.

Emrys Villalobos noticed this every morning on the long walk up the eastern ridge, where the brambles grew thick as a fortress wall and twice as unfriendly. Down in the lowlands—so they said, so the traders said when they came through with salt and news and that particular look people got when talking about things best left alone—the dead rose restless, ghosts walked, hedge-witches muttered over boiling pots. Here, nothing stirred that wasn't supposed to. Magic couldn't follow you past the valley's edge. Neither could anything else, which was rather the point.

"Nothing," Emrys said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud was part of the ritual. "Same as yesterday."

The words fell flat in the cold air. They always did. Ten years of saying *nothing* to an empty hillside, and the hillside had yet once to disagree.

They adjusted the strap of their sword—a good blade, older than the valley's quiet, kept oiled out of habit rather than expectation—and continued the patrol. Up the ridge, along the goat track, past the shrine that wasn't a shrine anymore, down through the birch stand, across the creek, home by midday. The whole circuit took four hours if you dawdled, three if you didn't, and Emrys had gotten very good at dawdling. Dawdling was how a person filled a day that had nothing in it worth hurrying toward.

The birches were turning early this year. Gold light came down through them in coins, and Emrys walked through it with their collar up and their breath steaming, thinking—not for the first time, not for the thousandth—that this was what peace looked like, and nobody had warned them it would feel like being buried alive at a very leisurely pace.

At the crest of the ridge stood the old waystone. It had been a shrine once, before Emrys came. Pilgrims had left offerings at its base: bread, copper bits, ribbons tied to the hawthorn beside it. Now the ribbons were gray rags gone stiff with weather, and the last offering anyone had made was a chipped clay cup some traveler had abandoned eight years back, which Emrys had never moved because moving it would have required admitting someone else had been here.

Emrys stopped at the waystone, as always. Touched two fingers to its cold surface, as always. Said the words, as always—or started to.

"*Ossa, who walks beside me—*"

And stopped, as always, at the place where the prayer used to be answered.

For six years of Emrys's life, that sentence had been the beginning of a conversation. *Ossa, who walks beside me, keep my feet sure.* And something vast and warm and wordless would settle into their bones like a hand on the shoulder: *I have you, wolf-cub. Walk on.* Six years of that. Six years of never walking alone, of going into battle with a god humming under their skin like a struck bell, of feeling *seen* all the way down in a way no mortal love had ever managed.

Then the Battle of Redford Ford, and mid-scream, mid-blood, mid-charge, the bell simply... stopped.

No farewell. No explanation. Just silence where the god had been, sudden and total, like a house going dark when you're halfway down the stairs.

Emrys touched the waystone now and felt nothing, which was the expected result, and hated that they still hoped otherwise, which was the unexpected one. Ten years. You'd think a body would learn.

"You could at least send a sign," they told the stone. "A bird. A funny-shaped cloud. Anything. I'm not proud."

The stone declined to comment. It was very good at that. Ten years of practice.

* * *

Halfway down the ridge, Emrys checked the snares.

This was another part of the ritual, and the only part that produced results. Three rabbit snares along the creek line, two trout lines in the deep pool, and today—one fat brown hare, furious about the entire arrangement of its morning. Emrys thanked it quietly, in the old way, palm flat between its ears, and did the work quick and clean.

"Sorry, friend," they murmured. "You'll be stew by sundown. Better stew than forgotten."

The hare, being dead, offered no opinion. Emrys slung it over their shoulder and walked on, boots squelching through the frost-softened mud, and let themselves do the thing they only ever did alone, which was talk.

"It occurred to me," they said to the hare, conversationally, "that I've been having more conversations with dead animals than living people. Which is fine. Maren doesn't count as living, exactly, she's more of an ongoing negotiation. And the Hollis family down-valley wave at me from across the field like I'm a scarecrow that learned to walk, which is nearly companionship." A pause while they stepped over a fallen birch, silver-barked and mossy as an old dog's back. "But it occurs to me that if I died out here, it'd be three weeks before anyone noticed, and the person who found me would be Maren, and she'd take the good blanket."

The hare swung gently against their back with each step. Somewhere overhead a crow made a remark.

"I know," Emrys told it. "I know. Poor little mercenary, all alone in his nice safe valley where nobody's trying to kill him. Terrible tragedy." They snorted at themselves. "There are ten thousand soldiers in the lowlands right now who'd trade places with me in a heartbeat, and every one of them has better reasons than I do to want out."

That was true, and knowing it true didn't help even slightly, which was also true, and both truths sat in Emrys's chest like stones in a streambed, worn smooth from handling.

Because here was the shape of it, the shape Emrys tried not to look at directly, the way you don't look straight at your own face in a bad mirror:

The war was over. Had been for years. Vaelric sat the throne in the east, crowned and anointed, and the free cities paid their tributes and kept their heads down, and the great rising that everyone had whispered about—the one the faithful were supposedly waiting for, the one Emrys had crawled into this valley telling themselves they'd rest up for, heal, wait for the call—never came. The call never came. The god stayed silent. The faithful scattered or knelt or died quietly in ditches, and history rolled on like a cart with a squeaking wheel, and nobody, anywhere, was waiting for Emrys Villalobos to do a single blessed thing.

Which meant the patrol was pointless. The vigil was pointless. The oiled sword and the mended walls and the stored grain against a winter siege that would never come—all of it, pointless. A soldier's discipline aimed at nothing, firing into fog.

Emrys kept doing it anyway.

Because the alternative—the alternative was sitting down on the waystone steps and not getting up, and Emrys had looked at that alternative often enough over the years to know its face, and they weren't ready to shake hands with it. So instead there were snares to check, and a roof to patch, and a hare to skin, and a valley to walk in circles around, forever, like a dog tied to a stake.

"Wolf and thorn," they said to the crow, using the old name for themselves, the one Ossa had given them, and laughed—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. The crow, unimpressed, flew off to find better company.

* * *

Home was a cottage at the valley's heart, stone-built and stubborn, with a turf roof Emrys re-cut every autumn and a garden that produced turnips with the grim reliability of tax collectors. There was a woodpile stacked to the eaves. There was a smokehouse. There was a well. Everything a person needed to survive alone for years, laid in with methodical care, the way you'd stock a tomb.

Emrys hung the hare in the smokehouse, split kindling until their shoulders burned pleasantly, and ate lunch on the doorstep: black bread, hard cheese, a pear from the tree by the fence that fruited whether Emrys deserved it or not. The afternoon sun came down warm on the stones. Somewhere in the grass, the last crickets of the season fiddled away at their small music.

It was, objectively, a beautiful day. Emrys sat in it the way a man sits in a room where a loved one died—present, comfortable, and grieving something no one else could see.

"Maren will come by tomorrow," they said to the pear core. "She'll complain about her knees. She'll inspect my garden and tell me the turnips are a disgrace. She'll ask, again, in her roundabout witch way, why I don't come down to the Hollis farm for the harvest supper, and I'll say next year, again, and she'll look at me like—" Emrys waved the pear core vaguely. "Like that."

Like they were a wound that had scabbed over wrong. Like they were a story someone had stopped reading halfway through and never picked back up.

Maren had been exiled to the valley's border for longer than Emrys had been in it—hedge-craft, herb-lore, small magics that the lowlands' new orthodoxy frowned on—and she was, in the strict accounting of the valley, Emrys's only friend. She had never asked about the sword. She had never asked about the prayers she'd surely heard, carried on the wind, spoken to a stone that didn't answer. She had simply arrived one winter's day with a basket of onions and an opinion about the state of Emrys's chimney, and that had been that. Friendship, in the Thorn Valley, grew slow and thorny and without ceremony, like everything else.

Emrys finished the pear and went inside to mend a shirt, because a person had to do *something* with their hands, and idle hands started remembering things.

The shirt was a soldier's shirt, though it hadn't seen a war in a decade. Emrys stitched the torn cuff by firelight and let the needle click, and the clicking pulled the memories up the way it always did—not the bad ones first, but the good ones, which were worse.

A campfire in the hills above Corvane, twenty shields leaning in a ring, and Sefa laughing so hard at something that she went over backward off her log, and Ossa's warmth in Emrys's chest laughing too, the god finding mortals endlessly delightful, *look at them, wolf-cub, look how they carry each other*—

Stop.

The needle paused. Emrys breathed.

*A decade,* they reminded themselves. *Ten years since the voice went out. You survived it. You built a life around the hole where it was, the way a tree grows around a lightning scar. You are fine.*

They finished the shirt. They banked the fire. They stepped outside to bar the door, as they did every night, and paused—as they did every night—with their hand on the latch.

The sky over the valley was going bruise-colored in the west, and far off, past the ridge, past the boundary, thunder was grumbling up out of the lowlands. Storm weather coming. First real storm of autumn, by the smell of it, and it would hit the valley hard tonight, rain lashing the turf roof, wind worrying the shutters.

Emrys stood a moment longer, listening to the distant thunder, feeling the old reflex rise in them—the soldier's reflex, *bad weather means bad roads means movement means watch the ridgelines*—and then put it down again, gently, the way you set down a tool you no longer need. No armies moved in the Thorn Valley. Nothing moved in the Thorn Valley. That was the whole arrangement.

They went to close the door.

And that was when Emrys heard it—under the thunder, thin and ragged, coming up the track through the gathering dark.

Not a wolf. Not wind in the brambles.

Crying. Small, exhausted, animal crying, the kind that has cried itself hoarse and keeps going anyway, and beneath it, half-lost in the storm's first gusts, footsteps—little feet slapping mud, stumbling closer.

Emrys stood in their doorway with the latch cold under their hand, every hair on their arms standing up, ten years of silence pressing in around them like deep water—and watched a small, mud-caked shape stagger out of the brambles toward their cottage, toward the light, toward the only lit window in a valley where magic could not go.

The child looked up. Rain ran down their face. And they spoke—words Emrys had not heard spoken aloud since the world ended at Redford Ford, words that no throat in this valley should have been able to make, words in a tongue older than any kingdom, older than any road, older than the stones of the waystone itself.

Words in the First Tongue.

The latch slipped from Emrys's fingers.
