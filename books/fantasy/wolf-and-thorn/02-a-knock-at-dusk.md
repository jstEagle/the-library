## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The storm came up the valley like an old debt collector—slow, inevitable, and arriving at the worst possible hour.

Emrys had spent the afternoon doing what they did every afternoon: nothing, with great discipline. The roof needed patching where the autumn winds had peeled back shingles like a curious child peeling bark. The woodpile was a scandal. There were turnips in the garden that had achieved a state of being Emrys could only describe as *aggressively mediocre*, and they'd been meaning to do something about it for three weeks.

Instead they sat by the fire mending a fishing net they didn't need, because the river fish in the Thorn Valley were fat, stupid, and practically leapt into the basket, and a net was the sort of thing a hermit ought to have even if it never saw water.

That was the trick of exile, Emrys had learned. You performed normalcy until it stuck. Net, garden, roof. Routine as armor. If you did the same small useless things every day, eventually the days stopped screaming at you.

Mostly.

They glanced—at least once an hour, though they'd have denied it under torture—at the iron nail driven into the lintel above the hearth. Nothing hung from it. Nothing had hung there for ten years. But Emrys kept the nail polished anyway, which said something about them that they preferred not to examine.

The first thunder rolled down from the ridge around dusk, long and low, like the mountain clearing its throat before saying something rude.

"Ah," Emrys told the fire. "There it is."

They went out into the yard one last time before dark, hauling the goat—named Turnip, for reasons involving a bad winter and worse judgment—into her shed. She complained about this with the full theatrical commitment of her kind.

"Yes, yes, tragic, I know," Emrys said, latching the gate. "Storms are an outrage against goats specifically. The world is very unfair."

Turnip butted them in the hip hard enough to stagger a lesser person and settled into her straw with the smugness of the well-fed.

The rain arrived properly while Emrys was crossing back—a wall of it, sudden as an ambush, hammering the packed earth into soup. By the time they reached the door they were soaked through their oilskin, and the wind was moaning through the pines along the slope with that particular sound old soldiers knew: like distant voices calling names.

Emrys lit the lamp, put water on to heat—for tea, because even hermits deserved small luxuries—and had just gotten their boots off when someone knocked.

Three raps. Small ones. Almost apologetic.

Emrys went still.

Nobody knocked at the cottage. That was rather the point of living at the far end of a far valley behind a hill of thorns, in a place where the maps gave up and the surveyors' ink ran dry. The nearest neighbors were the Hollis family, two miles downriver, and they were good people but not *walking-in-this* people; Marta Hollis would send word ahead or wait out a storm in her own sturdy kitchen like a sensible woman.

Which meant either a lost traveler—which happened perhaps twice a year, usually fools who'd ignored the warning cairns—or trouble.

Emrys took the hatchet down from its peg out of pure habit, weighed it, and set it back. A hatchet answered questions you couldn't un-ask. They took the iron poker instead, which could be explained away, and opened the door.

A child stood on the step.

Small—seven years old, maybe eight, though it was hard to tell under the mud. And there was a remarkable quantity of mud. It was in the child's hair, which might once have been brown, in their ears, crusted thick on their bare feet and legs, smeared across what might have been a decent wool coat before some bog claimed it. The child looked like something the river had tried to eat and then reconsidered.

They stood perfectly straight in the downpour, water streaming off them, and stared up at Emrys with eyes so dark and so calm it was frankly unsettling. Most children who appeared on doorsteps during storms cried, or shivered, or at minimum had the decency to look miserable. This one simply waited, patient as a stone, as though standing in a flood at a stranger's door was a thing they'd done many times.

"Well," Emrys said. "You're a long way from anywhere."

The child said nothing.

"You lost?"

Nothing.

"Hurt? Hungry?" Emrys sighed. "Blessedly mute and covered in filth. Wonderful. Come in before the river decides it wants you after all."

They stepped aside. The child walked in—carefully wiping their feet on the threshold twice, which struck Emrys as both absurdly polite and oddly formal, a little ritual, almost reverent—and stood dripping in the middle of the room, looking around with that same unnerving stillness.

"Right." Emrys shut the door against the storm and the noise dropped away, leaving the fire's crackle and the drum of rain on the roof. "Boots—or whatever those were—off. Coat by the fire. Don't touch anything sharp."

The child obeyed each instruction with precise, unhurried compliance, and Emrys found themselves unsettled all over again. Children were loud creatures. Chaotic. This one moved through the room like a guest in a temple, hands folded, gaze traveling over everything—the net, the nail above the hearth, the poker leaning by the door—with an attention that felt less like curiosity than *inventory.*

"I'm going to find you something dry," Emrys said, digging through the cedar chest. "These belonged to—never mind. They'll be too big. Everything's too big."

The shirt went down past the child's knees. The socks stayed up only through sheer optimism. The child looked like a scarecrow that had been dressed by committee.

"Sit. Eat." Emrys pushed a bowl of last night's barley stew across the table, plus bread that had gone the far side of firm but hadn't yet achieved architecture. "Slowly. If you bolt it you'll be sick, and I'll tell you now I'm not built for sympathy."

The child ate exactly slowly. Measured spoonfuls. Chewed a fixed number of times. Emrys watched this over their own bowl and felt the hair on their neck rise, very slightly, the way it used to before ambushes.

*It's just a strange child,* they told themselves. *The lowlands are full of orphans these days. Wars make them like leaves make autumn.*

But wars also made other things, and the Thorn Valley existed precisely because things couldn't follow here. No magic. No gods. Just weather, dirt, and the honest stupidity of livestock. Whatever this child was running from, it shouldn't have been able to reach this valley at all.

"Name?" Emrys asked.

Silence.

"Where are your people? The Hollises are downriver. We can take you there tomorrow, and Marta will feed you until you're sick and fuss over your feet, and everyone will pretend this was normal."

The child looked up. And for the first time, they spoke.

"*Thessu varren oth kaelis. Oth kaelis imma.*"

Emrys's spoon stopped halfway to their mouth.

The words fell into the room and rang there like a struck bell. Ten years. Ten years since Emrys had heard the First Tongue, since the last prayer died half-finished on a blood-soaked field and the god who should have answered went quiet forever. Ten years since anyone alive had spoken it—so far as Emrys knew, the whole liturgy had gone into the ground with the faithful, and Vaelric's inquisitors had made very sure of the rest.

And here was a mud-caked child in borrowed socks, sitting at a hermit's table, saying it as easily as breathing. Not stumbling over the syllables. Not reciting something learned by rote, all wrong vowels and flattened tones, the way a scholar chokes on a dead language.

*Native,* some cold, clear part of Emrys observed, detached and horrified. *That's a native speaker.*

"*Oth kaelis imma*," the child repeated, when Emrys didn't answer. Their small brow creased—the first crack in the composure. "*Imma?*"

*I don't understand,* that would be, roughly. Or *I don't know.* The grammar of the First Tongue bent meanings the way water bent light; Emrys's grasp had always been a soldier's grasp—prayers, commands, the battle-litanies—not a scholar's.

"I understand you fine," Emrys said hoarsely, in Trade Tongue, because answering in the other felt like opening a door they'd nailed shut. "That's the problem."

The child tilted their head. Rainwater dripped from a hank of muddy hair onto the table, and they watched Emrys with those bottomless dark eyes, waiting again. Patient. Expectant. As if Emrys were the one being tested.

"Don't do that," Emrys muttered. "Don't look at me like—" Like *him*. Like the presence that used to settle behind Emrys's ribs on cold mornings, warm as a second heartbeat, attentive without ever demanding. Like something vast wearing a very small face.

Emrys stood abruptly enough to scrape the chair. They busied themselves with the kettle, pouring tea they didn't want, staring at the steam.

Think. Be practical. A child speaks a language that has been dead for a decade. Explanations: one, coincidence—no. Nobody coincidentally spoke the First Tongue; you could sooner coincidentally speak the language of birds. Two, the child was a refugee from some hidden enclave, some pocket of old believers the purges missed—possible, barely, but the tone was wrong, the *ease* was wrong, no enclave survived ten years of inquisitors and kept its children fluent. Three—

Three was the answer Emrys had been circling since the child wiped their feet twice on the threshold like a pilgrim entering a shrine.

Three was not an explanation Emrys wanted.

"What did you say?" Emrys asked finally, turning around. "What you said at the table. Say it again. Slowly."

The child considered them. Then, gravely: "*Thessu varren oth kaelis.*"

*You are far from your house,* Emrys translated silently. Old words. Roadside words—the kind travelers exchanged, the kind you offered a stranger walking toward your fire. And then: "*Oth kaelis imma.*"

*I don't know my house.*

Or—Emrys's mouth went dry as the translation surfaced from somewhere deeper than memory, somewhere the war had buried but not killed—

*I have no house.*

The kettle whistled. Neither of them moved.

Outside, the storm threw itself against the cottage, and the wind in the pines made its sound of distant voices, and for one vertiginous moment Emrys was certain—one animal, absolute certainty—that the valley's famous silence had just developed a crack in it, and something on the other side of the crack was listening.

Then the moment passed. The fire crackled. The child sat in their oversized shirt with their empty bowl and watched Emrys with the terrible patience of the very young and the very old.

"All right," Emrys said at last, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they felt, the mercenary's voice, the one that held lines. "Here's how this goes. You sleep tonight. Tomorrow we walk downriver to the Hollises, and Marta will know what to do with you, because I am the last person in this world equipped to—"

The child shook their head. Once. Firmly.

"No?"

Another shake. Then the child pointed—at Emrys, at the floor beneath their feet, at the fire—and spoke again, and this time the words were different. Older. Words Emrys hadn't heard since a hilltop at dusk ten years ago, when a dying priest had pressed a hand to Emrys's chest and gasped out the oath that had ruined and saved their life:

"*Vessa orn. Kaelis imma vessa orn.*"

*Where you stand, there is my house.*

Emrys stood very still, holding a cooling cup of tea, while the rain came down and the child watched them, and somewhere far below the fear and the disbelief, in a place Emrys had boarded over and nailed shut, something stirred and turned its face toward the door.

The child finished the tea. All of it, in one unhurried draught, like communion.

Then they folded their hands on the table, looked Emrys dead in the eye, and said—in flawless, native, impossible Trade Tongue, their first words of it all evening, pitched soft under the storm—

"The door was open. So I knocked."

* * *

Emrys did not sleep.

They told themselves it was the storm. Storms kept hermits awake; that was natural, that was weather, that was nothing. But it wasn't the thunder rattling the shutter or the rain working at the roof patch. It was the child, asleep in the cot by the fire, breathing slow and even as a bell tolling, and the way the firelight seemed to bend toward them—not flickering, not guttering, just *leaning*, subtly, the way grass leans toward a fence line.

Magic couldn't cross into the Thorn Valley. Everyone knew that. It was the one law of the place, older than any deed or borderstone: the valley was dead ground, hollow ground, the one country in the world where gods and their works simply *stopped*.

So the leaning firelight was imagination. The faint, low hum Emrys almost heard beneath the rain was the wind. And the fact that the child had slept the whole night through without once shivering, in a drafty cottage, in a wet wool coat, while the temperature outside fell hard enough to skin the puddles with ice—that was constitution. Good stock. Peasant blood.

Emrys sat in the chair with the poker across their knees and told themselves stories until dawn.

Near midnight, the child spoke in their sleep. Not crying, not thrashing—just words, murmured softly, the way other children murmured about supper or mothers. Emrys leaned forward despite every instinct screaming not to, and caught fragments of the First Tongue rolling gently through the dark: a psalm. Fragments of a psalm Emrys knew. Had sung. Had screamed, once, over the bodies of friends, waiting for an answer that never came.

*"Ossa veth kaelim..."*

*God of the quiet places...*

Emrys sat back. The firelight leaned. Outside, the storm began, at last, to spend itself against the hills.

In the grey hour before dawn, the child opened their eyes without waking-looking, the way animals do, and found Emrys across the room in the dark, and held their gaze for a long, silent moment.

Then Wren—though Emrys didn't learn the name until later, and later was a country neither of them could see from where they sat—smiled. Small. Unbearably certain.

"*Kaelis veth imma,*" the child whispered, and slept again.

*The house knows me.*

Emrys stared into the dead coals and understood, with the sick clarity of a scout reading sign, that the life they had built here—net, garden, goat, nail—had just ended. They didn't know how yet. They didn't know what was coming down the valley roads, or what the child carried, or what it would cost.

They only knew that ten years ago, a god had gone silent mid-battle, and Emrys had crawled into this magic-dead hole in the world to bury the silence.

And silence, apparently, had come knocking back.
