## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

Emrys woke before dawn out of habit rather than need, which was the way most things happened in the Thorn Valley. The fire had burned down to a red eye in the hearth. The storm had passed in the night, leaving the world rinsed and dripping, and the child was still asleep on the floor by the door—curled up like a comma, mud dried to a pale crust on their cheeks, one small hand wrapped around a crust of bread they hadn't finished.

Emrys sat with a cup of barley tea and watched them breathe and told themselves, for the hundredth time since dusk, that it meant nothing. Children turned up after storms. Children ran away from hard masters and harder harvests and wandered into other people's lives all the time. It had nothing to do with Emrys. It certainly had nothing to do with *that*.

Except the words. Those were still sitting in Emrys's chest like swallowed coals.

*Vessa-ora, vessa-kem.* Shelter me, shelter-me-not. A call-and-response so old that when it had last been spoken aloud in Emrys's presence, kingdoms had stood where there were now only fields of thorn. The child had said it at the door, mud to the eyebrows, shivering, and Emrys's body had answered before their mind caught up—the response drilled into them by ten years of silence and twenty years before that of war.

*Kem-ora,* Emrys had said back. I shelter you anyway.

The child had looked at them like they'd been handed the moon.

Emrys drank the tea, which tasted like wet hay as always, and considered the situation the way one considers a wound: carefully, from angles, without pressing too hard.

The valley was magic-dead. Everyone knew that much, even if nobody in the valley talked about it, because talking about why would require admitting there was a *why*. Spells curdled here. Charms went dull. The gods—if you believed in gods, and Emrys had stopped, officially, some years ago—could not follow you past the ridgeline. That was the entire point of the Thorn Valley. It was where people came to be forgotten by everything except the weather.

So whatever the child was carrying, it wasn't magic. Not here. Here it could only be words.

Words, though. Words were bad enough.

* * *

Wren woke mid-morning, all at once, the way soldiers wake—with eyes open and body still, taking inventory before moving. Emrys pretended not to notice, stirring a pot of porridge with more attention than porridge deserved.

"Oat," Emrys said, tapping the pot. "Porridge. You eat?"

The child's stomach answered first, a long growl that broke the tension better than any diplomacy. Wren shuffled to the table, climbed onto the bench, and ate with the focused ferocity of someone who had learned not to trust the next meal to arrive.

Emrys waited until the bowl was half gone. Then, casually—as casually as a person can do anything while their heart is trying to climb out through their ribs—they said:

*Kessa-vahn.*

Good morning. The old greeting. The one priests used, once, before the temples burned; the one soldiers traded across watch-fires; the one Emrys hadn't spoken aloud in a decade because speaking it felt like calling out into a house where everyone was dead.

Wren looked up. Porridge on their chin. And replied, easy as breathing, in a voice still rough with sleep:

*Vahn-kessa, kem-tira.* Good morning, sheltering-one.

Then, seeing the expression on Emrys's face, the child tilted their head and added something longer—a full sentence, fluid, unselfconscious—and pointed at the window, where rain was dripping off the eaves, and said what was unmistakably a complaint about the weather.

Not recitation. Not fragments. Not the broken liturgical phrases that scholars in the free cities paid gold to copy out of crumbling books. Conversation. Complaints about rain. In the First Tongue. From a child who couldn't have been older than eight.

Emrys set down the spoon very slowly, because their hands had decided to become unreliable.

"You," Emrys said, in the trade-tongue every village from here to the coast shared, "understand me?"

Wren blinked. Said nothing. Either didn't understand or was choosing not to—a distinction Emrys would spend many sleepless nights failing to resolve.

Emrys switched back. *Kessa-vahn?* Where are you from?

And Wren's face did something complicated—closed, wary, older than eight—and they answered with a shrug that was fluent in any language: nowhere. Don't ask.

* * *

The rest of the day became, without either of them agreeing to it, an examination.

Emrys told themselves it was practical. If the child was going to stay under this roof—even one night more—Emrys needed to know what they'd dragged in from the storm. And the only instrument for knowing, the only tool left in Emrys's rusted kit, was language. So: tests. Disguised as chores, mostly, because Emrys had discovered early that direct questions got shrugs, but a task got cooperation.

They fed the chickens together. Emrys counted aloud in the First Tongue—*kem, vessa, tora, hess*—and Wren corrected them on *hess*, which apparently had drifted, or which Emrys had never pronounced right to begin with. Ten years of silence will rot your accent. The correction came with a look of gentle pity that Emrys found deeply offensive from someone who still had porridge on their sleeve.

They hauled water. Emrys sang—or produced sound in the rhythm of song—one of the marching cadences, the ones that kept shields steady and feet moving toward places feet should not go. Halfway through, Wren picked it up. Not the tune; the words. And changed them. Turned a cadence about killing into something about—Emrys listened twice, disbelieving—about rain falling on a garden. About seeds deciding to be brave.

"That's not how it goes," Emrys muttered, in trade-tongue, uselessly.

Wren grinned. It was the first time Emrys had seen them grin, and it rearranged the whole child—made them suddenly, unbearably young, gap-toothed and mud-eared and delighted with themselves. They sang the garden version again, louder, drumming on the water bucket.

It should have been charming. It was charming. That was the problem. Because Emrys recognized the structure underneath the nonsense—the meter, the repetition, the way the verses folded back on each other like hands clasping. That was liturgy. That was *call-and-response*, the oldest pattern in the faith Emrys had buried along with everyone who'd taught it to them. The child was improvising hymns over a marching song the way a fish improvises swimming.

By afternoon, Emrys had run out of chores and excuses, and sat down across the table from Wren with a stick and the flat stone by the hearth, and began, deliberately, to dig.

*Tira-vessa,* they wrote, scratching the characters into the dust. The opening invocation. Shelter-the-sheltered.

Wren took the stick and completed the line without hesitation, adding three glyphs Emrys's hand remembered drawing but whose meaning lived somewhere behind a locked door. Then the child kept going. More lines. Columns of them. The script flowed off the stone and onto the table edge and Emrys sat very still and read as fast as grief allowed.

Some of it was familiar—the bones of the morning prayer, the blessing over bread, the soldier's farewell. But threaded through were passages Emrys had never seen. Longer texts. Names Emrys didn't know. A word repeated often, angular and heavy, that surfaced in Emrys's memory with a cold shock: *ossath*. The Deep One. The Below-Root. A title for Ossa that only appeared in the oldest rites—the ones the high priests whispered and never taught to common sworn swords like Emrys.

The child wrote it five times in four columns, underlined it, and looked up expectantly, as if to say: *well? Do you know this part?*

Emrys did not know that part.

Emrys knew the parts that got you killed. Knew the battle-liturgy syllable by syllable, knew the death-rite, knew the small domestic prayers for safe roads and good harvests and healthy livestock that half the army had mumbled without believing and Emrys had mumbled while believing with their entire stupid heart. Knew all of it the way you know a scar—by touch, by ache, without looking directly.

But this—this was deeper water. This was the tongue not as it was used but as it *lived*, growing new branches, telling new stories. Languages died with their gods. That was the natural order. When Ossa went silent, the First Tongue had gone silent with it, and the scholars had said so plainly: within a generation, no living speakers. Within two, no readers. A dead god's dead words.

And here was a child of perhaps eight years, complaining about rain in it. Correcting Emrys's pronunciation. Writing scripture Emrys had never been permitted to see.

There were exactly two explanations, and Emrys hated both. Either the child had been raised by someone—a hidden community, a surviving temple, something—that had kept the tongue alive in secret for a decade. Or the child hadn't learned it at all.

Either way, the child was a lantern in a world hunting light.

* * *

Evening came down gray and soft. Emrys cooked beans badly and Wren ate them enthusiastically, which was generous. Afterward the child sat by the fire turning a wooden button over and over in their fingers, and Emrys sat across from them sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, and the silence between them had changed texture. It was no longer the silence of strangers. It was the silence of two people standing at the same locked door.

Finally Emrys spoke, in the First Tongue, quietly.

*Kem-ossa.* My god. The simplest confession in the old rite. Three syllables.

Wren's head came around. Their eyes—dark, quick, far too old—fixed on Emrys with an intensity that made the fire seem dimmer.

*Kem-ossa,* the child repeated softly. Then, hesitant for the first time all day, they asked a question. A short one. Two words.

Emrys understood it immediately, the way you understand a blow. *Where is yours?*

The knife stilled against the whetstone.

"I don't know," Emrys said—in trade-tongue, because the honest answer deserved the coward's language, the tongue built for people who mean less than they say. "Ten years. Ten years, and nothing. No voice. No dream. Nothing."

Wren frowned at the trade-tongue, then at Emrys, clearly parsing tone if not words. Then they set down the button, crawled across the floor—it seemed important to the child to cross the distance bodily, not verbally—and pressed one small muddy finger against Emrys's chest, over the sternum, where the oath-scar sat beneath ten years of shirts.

And said, gently, in the First Tongue: *He is not gone. He is held.*

Held. The word landed strangely—not *dead*, not *silent*, but *held*, like something gripping, like something gripped. Emrys opened their mouth to demand what that meant, where the child had heard such a thing, whether—

A knock.

Three slow knocks at the cottage door, deliberate as a signature, and both of them went still as prey. Nobody knocked in the Thorn Valley. Neighbors shouted from the gate if they shouted at all. Knocking was a lowland habit, a courtly habit, a habit of men who traveled with papers and authority and did not expect doors to be barred against them.

Emrys rose without a sound, slid the knife from view, and put one hand lightly on Wren's head—a gesture that surprised them both, some old reflex of protection Emrys had thought amputated years ago—and moved to peer through the shutter crack.

Down the lane, through the last blue light of evening, came riders. Four of them, dark-cloaked, unhurried, their horses' breath steaming. At the shoulder of the lead rider, catching the dying light like an insect's back, glinted the badge Emrys had hoped never to see again outside of nightmares: the black sun of Vaelric's inquisition.

Behind them, Wren began, very softly, to sing.
