## 4. What the Silence Cost

The giant had a name, but Wren wasn't supposed to use it yet.

Names were like eggs. You carried them careful until they were ready. The giant was called Emrys — Wren knew because the word lived in the giant's chest the way a bird lived in a tree, and when you looked at someone properly you could hear what their name weighed. Emrys weighed heavy. Emrys weighed like a stone dropped down a well that never hit bottom.

Wren lay on the pallet by the fire and watched the giant through half-closed eyes, which was a trick. Half-closed eyes meant sleeping, and sleeping things got to keep living.

The giant moved wrong for something so big. Big things — oxen, bears, the burning men from home — they crashed and stamped. But Emrys crossed the cottage like a shadow crossing a floor, soft-footed even though one leg dragged a little at the end of every step, hitch-and-hold, hitch-and-hold, like the leg had been told once to stop working and only mostly forgot.

Wren catalogued the wounds the way they'd learned to catalogue everything: quickly, quietly, in case it mattered later.

A scar over the left eyebrow, old and white as fish-belly. A burn along the right forearm shaped like a splash of spilled milk, if milk could be made of hurt. And the hands — the hands were the worst and the best. Knuckles gone thick and knobby, a soldier's hands, killer's hands. But this morning those same hands had cracked an egg into the pan without crushing it, and had peeled the blanket up over Wren's shoulders in the night so gently that Wren had only woken a little bit.

Killer's hands doing kind things. That was the whole problem with the giant, boiled down small enough to hold.

*You're the one who ran through the fire,* Wren thought at them. *I saw.*

* * *

Here is what Wren remembered, which is to say here is what Wren survived:

The lowlands burning like a feast laid out for somebody terrible. Not house-fires — house-fires were ordinary, house-fires happened, and you put dirt on them or you cried about them. This was different. This was fire with a shape to it, fire that moved like it had orders, running along the hedgerows in straight soldier-lines while men in grey-and-gold walked behind it unhurried, poking their long iron pokers into haystacks and doorways and, once, into a dog that had barked too loud.

Wren had been carrying the egg before that night. Had been carrying it since the woman in the reeds pressed it into their hands with both of hers and said words in the Old Tongue — said *carry, carry, don't look back, don't let it crack* — and Wren hadn't understood all the words but understood *don't let it crack*, because some meanings live deeper than language, down where mothers' voices go.

So Wren ran. Through the burning lowlands, holding the egg against their sternum with both arms, and the strange thing — the thing nobody would believe — was that the fire didn't want them. It leaned away. Flames curled aside like grass under wind, and Wren ran down a road that was burning on both sides and came out the other end smelling only faintly of smoke, and behind them the world turned orange and then black and then quiet in the way that meant everyone in it was dead or gone.

They walked for days. Maybe more than days; Wren lost count somewhere around the second river. They ate green apples that fought back. They slept in ditches. Once a crow followed them for a morning, and Wren talked to it in the Old Tongue because there was no one else to talk to, and the crow looked at them sideways like even the crow thought that was strange, and flew off to tell its crow friends.

And the egg — the egg got warmer. That was the rule Wren worked out: the closer to the mountains, the warmer the egg, until by the last day it wasn't an egg anymore, it was a coal, it was a little sun tucked under their shirt, and it hummed.

It hummed the way the Old Tongue sounded. Which meant the egg was talking. Which meant—

Which meant Wren didn't finish that thought. Some thoughts were too big to pick up all at once. You circled them. You waited for them to shrink.

Then the valley. Then the place where the humming stopped so suddenly Wren staggered like they'd missed a stair. Then the cottage with smoke rising from its chimney, and hunger finally winning the argument, and Wren knocking on the door of a giant because a giant was still better than nothing.

That had been two nights ago.

* * *

Now the giant was doing something alarming again, which was: mending clothes.

Emrys sat by the window with a shirt across their knees and a needle in those terrible gentle hands, and the light came through the glass all grey and rainy, and for a moment — just a moment — the giant's face did something it hadn't done in front of Wren before. It went soft. Open. Like a door left ajar.

Then the giant noticed Wren watching, and the door slammed shut so fast it nearly caught its own coat-tails.

"You're awake," Emrys said. Their voice was gravel poured slow. "Good. Eat."

Bread appeared. Cheese, cut in a wedge so enormous it was nearly a joke. Wren took it and ate and kept the half-closed-eyes trick going, because watching-through-lashes was safer than watching-through-open, and also because the cheese was very good and it was easier to be brave with your mouth full.

"Can you understand me?" the giant asked. "Nod if you can."

Wren nodded, chewing.

"Words, too? You spoke at my door. Words I—" The giant stopped. Started again, quieter. "You speak the old language. Do you know any of mine?"

Wren considered this. Considered the risk. Considered the cheese.

"Yes," they said, in the common tongue, small and rusty. It came out crooked, like a bird that had flown too far south. "A little."

Something happened in the giant's face then that Wren couldn't name — not softness exactly, more like the ground shifting under snow before an avalanche decides. Emrys set down the needle very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands want to shake and you won't let them.

"Where did you learn it?"

Wren shrugged, which was honest, because how do you explain *the egg taught me, the egg talks, the egg has been talking longer than kingdoms have existed and I am fairly sure it is God*? You couldn't. So you shrugged, and you chewed, and you watched the giant watch you.

"I'm not going to hurt you," Emrys said.

"That's what the burning men probably said," Wren replied, and immediately regretted it, because the giant flinched — actually flinched, a whole-body thing, like the words had been thrown and hit — and then the room went very quiet except for the rain.

"I know," Emrys said finally. "That's fair." And then, after a silence long enough for Wren to eat the entire rest of the cheese: "I'll leave the door unlatched. If you want to run, you can run. I won't chase you."

Wren frowned at that. It didn't fit anything. Giants didn't say *you can run*. Burning men chased. Wolves chased. Even the crow had eventually gotten bored and left. Nothing in Wren's short hard life had ever offered to let go first.

"Why?" they asked.

The giant looked at the window, at the rain stitching the valley together, and for a second the stone-well weight of them tipped and Wren heard — really heard, down in the place where names weigh — the truest thing in the whole cottage:

*Because I know exactly what it is to run, little bird, and I would not wish my legs upon anyone.*

But what Emrys said out loud was only: "Eat your bread."

* * *

That afternoon, Wren broke the rules.

Not on purpose, exactly. Rules were slippery things and Wren was tired, and tired children drift toward warmth the way rivers drift toward the sea. The giant went out to the woodpile in the grey drizzle, and Wren was supposed to stay inside, and Wren did stay inside for almost a minute and a half.

Then curiosity did what curiosity does.

The cottage was small but the giant's life was written large across it, the way big people wrote everything. A sword above the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth, kept the way you keep a snake you've caught but haven't decided about. A shelf of wooden soldiers, carved beautifully, arranged not in battle lines but sitting down, resting, facing each other like people at a table. A cloak on a hook, army-grey, with a sigil burned off the shoulder — burned, deliberately, raggedly, by someone who had wanted it gone so badly they'd used fire instead of scissors.

And on the table, half under a whetstone: a small tin whistle, dented, worn shiny at the mouthpiece from years of breath.

Wren picked it up.

The egg — the coal, the sun, whatever-it-was under their ribs — went hot. Not painfully. More like a dog hearing its name. And before Wren could think better of it, they raised the whistle to their lips and breathed out the notes of a song they'd never learned but had always known, a slow climbing tune in the Old Tongue's own music, the song the egg hummed at night when it thought Wren was asleep.

The whistle's note came out strange. Warped. In this valley the sound bent oddly, flattened, like a bell rung underwater. But the egg sang along anyway, humming under Wren's sternum, harmonizing with a dead man's toy, and for three or four bars the cottage felt less like a hiding place and more like —

The door opened.

Emrys stood in it, dripping, an armload of wood, and looked at Wren standing there with the whistle at their lips, and the giant's face did the avalanche thing again but slower this time, and worse. Grief moved across it like weather. Wren watched ten years happen to someone in the space of a breath.

"Put it down," Emrys said. Not loud. Worse than loud. Quiet, the way ice is quiet.

Wren set it on the table. "Whose was it?"

The giant stood in the doorway dripping for a long time. Rain ran off their jaw. When they finally spoke, the gravel in their voice had washed away and left something raw underneath.

"It belonged to a man named Corvin. He played it badly. He died playing it." Emrys came in, stacked the wood with great precision, and did not look at Wren at all. "You shouldn't have touched it."

"You left it out."

"I left it out because it doesn't matter anymore." And that was a lie — Wren could taste lies now, they came off people sour, like milk turning — but it was a lie the giant needed, the way a wound needs a bandage even a dirty one, so Wren let it stand.

But something had shifted between them, and Wren felt it settle. The giant had seen the egg move. Not known, maybe — but suspected, the way you suspect weather coming in your bad knee. All afternoon Emrys kept glancing at Wren's chest, quick furtive looks, and once reached out as if to touch and pulled the hand back like the stove was hot.

At dusk, the giant cooked stew and said, abruptly: "When they come looking — and they will come looking — you hide in the root cellar and you don't come out, no matter what you hear. No matter what you *hear me do*. Promise me."

"They?" said Wren.

"The men with the pokers." Emrys's eyes were on the ridgeline through the window, where the rain had thinned and the last light lay red as the lowlands had lain. "They're already asking at the valley's edge about something stolen. Something small." A pause. "Something that sings."

Wren's hand drifted to their sternum. The egg pulsed once against their palm, warm and stubborn, like a heart refusing to learn fear.

"How do you know they're coming?"

Emrys turned from the window, and in the fading light the scars stood out white and the killer's hands hung quiet at their sides, and the giant said the thing that made Wren's stomach drop through the floorboards into the cold dark earth below:

"Because ten years ago, I was one of them."
