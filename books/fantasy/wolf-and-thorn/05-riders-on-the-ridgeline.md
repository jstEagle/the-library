## 5. Riders on the Ridgeline

Emrys had a system for bad days.

It went like this: wake before dawn, check the door latch (unlatched, because Wren still couldn't work latches and Emrys had stopped pretending that wasn't deliberate), eat whatever porridge hadn't gone wrong overnight, and then invent something urgent to do with their hands until the day gave up and became night again.

It was not a sophisticated system. But it had kept them breathing for ten years, and breathing was the whole of the contract as far as Emrys was concerned.

The morning the riders came over the ridgeline, Emrys was elbow-deep in the rain barrel trying to fish out Wren's boots, which the child had thrown in for reasons known only to Wren, when the dog started up.

They didn't have a dog.

Emrys's head came up so fast they cracked it on the barrel's rim, which was fitting, because the barking was coming from Halvard's place down the slope — Halvard who *did* have a dog, an enormous shaggy idiot named Turnip who barked at everything except things worth barking at. Turnip barking like this meant strangers. Strangers on the ridge road, where nobody came, because the ridge road led nowhere except out of the valley, and nobody in the Thorn Valley wanted out.

Emrys stood very still, dripping to the elbows, and listened.

Four horses, maybe five. The particular cadence of hooves on wet stone. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable if you'd spent a decade listening for it: the thin metallic song of chainmail that someone had tried to muffle with cloth.

Soldiers who didn't want to be heard were worse than soldiers who did.

"Boots," Emrys said to the house in general. "Now."

Wren appeared in the doorway looking like a small mud-colored spirit, hair full of straw, one boot already on the wrong foot. They said nothing — Wren rarely said anything in the common tongue, though the words that lived behind their teeth were old and heavy and made Emrys's chest ache every time they slipped out — but their dark eyes went from Emrys's face to the ridge road and back, and something in them sharpened. Children who had run through burning country learned fast what hoofbeats meant.

"Inside," Emrys said. "Under the floor. You remember the floor?"

Wren remembered the floor. The loose board by the hearth, the crawl-space beneath barely deep enough for a child and certainly not for a grown mercenary, packed with dried herbs that smelled like a witch's apology. Emrys had dug it out in the first week, back when the child was a stranger instead of a — well. Whatever Wren was now. A responsibility. A catastrophe wearing a too-small coat. A thing that spoke the First Tongue in its sleep.

A stolen relic, Emrys thought, and didn't know yet where the words had come from.

The board slid home. Emrys scattered turnips over it — actual turnips, the valley's one reliable crop, which Halvard swore by and everyone else endured — and then stood in the middle of their own kitchen doing the last thing any sensible person would do.

They put water on to boil.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, the grim little sums Emrys ran without meaning to: men riding quiet on the ridge road would come to the cottage whether or not there was smoke from the chimney. Smoke said someone was home and unafraid. An empty cold house said someone had run, and running men got chased. So: water, fire, a face like a bored hermit's. Ten years of practice at being no one.

The knock, when it came, was polite. That was somehow the worst part.

* * *

There were four of them. Emrys counted before opening the door, through the gap in the shutter, the way you count anything that might kill you. Four riders, three waiting at the gate with the easy slouch of men who'd been in saddles too long, and one already dismounted, standing in the yard with his hands folded like a priest at a funeral he was enjoying.

He was younger than Emrys expected. Inquisitors in the stories were old, gray, patient as rot. This one was maybe thirty, clean-shaven, with the kind of handsome face that had never once been hit. His cloak was good wool dyed the deep blue of Vaelric's house, and under it, when the wind moved the fabric just so, the gleam of a breastplate worked with the sun-and-spear sigil of the usurper's court.

"Good morning," the inquisitor said. His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant, the way a well-made trap is pleasant right up until the spring. "I wonder if I might trouble you for a cup of that tea I can smell."

"You can trouble me," Emrys said. "The tea's weak. Water's bad. Welcome to the valley."

His smile widened a fraction. "Honest hospitality. How refreshing." He stepped past Emrys into the cottage without asking, which told Emrys what kind of morning this was going to be, and looked around with the mild curiosity of a man browsing a market stall. The herbs. The turnip basket. The single chair, the narrow bed, the hearth. "You live alone?"

"I live alone."

"No family?" He picked up a wooden spoon, examined it, set it down exactly where it had been — a courtesy, or a demonstration that he noticed where everything was. "No children passing through? It's remarkable country, but hardly a place anyone comes to on purpose."

"People wash up here," Emrys said. "Valley takes all kinds."

"It does." He turned, and for the first time his eyes settled on Emrys properly — cataloguing, weighing. The scars on the forearms. The way Emrys stood with weight even on both feet, hands loose, the stance of someone whose body had been taught things their hermit costume disagreed with. "May I ask your name?"

"Brannoc." The lie came out smooth, and Emrys felt it leave like something cut loose — a rope slipping off a dock. Ten years in the valley and they had never once lied to a soul. There had been no need. Lying was a war skill, and Emrys had retired from wars. Except apparently not, because the name sat in their mouth like it had always lived there. "Hermit. Turnips. Mostly."

"And before the turnips, Brannoc?"

"Before the turnips," Emrys said, "there were other turnips."

Something flickered at the corner of the inquisitor's mouth. Almost respect. "My name is Serel," he said. "I serve the Lord Protector's Commission of Inquiry. We're seeking a stolen relic — an object of some religious significance, taken from a temple in the lowlands during the recent troubles. It may have passed through this valley." He produced a folded paper, opened it with two fingers, and held it out. "We have a description."

Emrys took the paper. Read it. Kept their face the way the valley had taught them to keep it: like weathered wood, like nothing.

The description was not of an object.

*A child,* it said, in clerk's ink, *of indeterminate age, six to ten years. Dark-haired. May bear marks upon the skin. Speaks an unidentified language. Considered dangerous. Reward offered for recovery intact; substantial additional reward for information leading to recovery.*

Intact. Someone wanted the child whole. Emrys filed that away in the place where old mercenary habits still kept their shelves in order — intact meant valuable, valuable meant leverage, leverage meant the men holding the leash were afraid of what the dog might do if it got loose.

"Haven't seen any children," Emrys said, handing the paper back. "Valley's not kind to them. Too far from anywhere, winters are mean, and the soil grows turnips and grudges."

"Mm." Serel folded the paper away with care. He drifted toward the hearth, and Emrys's heartbeat went slow and enormous, each beat landing like a hammer in a deep well, because he was drifting toward the loose board, toward the turnip basket, and Wren — clever, silent Wren — was underneath it, breathing the way frightened children breathe, which is to say not at all. "Strange thing about this valley," Serel said conversationally. "Our maps show it, but our instruments don't. No resonance. No echo. Like walking into a room where the sound has been swept out. Do you notice that? Living here?"

"Notice what?"

"Exactly." He smiled at Emrys as though Emrys had answered a question correctly. "Magic doesn't hold here. Which makes it, wouldn't you say, an excellent place to hide something magical? Or someone." He crouched by the hearth, and Emrys watched his hand hover over the floorboards, one finger extending, tracing along the seams of the wood like a man reading a letter written in grain—

—and the kettle screamed.

Not boiled-over screaming. Kicked-off-the-hook, hurled-across-the-room screaming, and Emrys moved without deciding to move, caught the kettle barehanded out of the air with a hiss of scalded skin, and set it down hard on the table, and the whole maneuver took perhaps two seconds and was the least hermit-like thing they had done in a decade.

Silence.

Serel straightened slowly and looked at Emrys with new eyes. Pleasant eyes. Interested eyes.

"Reflexes," Emrys said. "Kettles are dangerous."

"So they are." He studied them a moment longer, and then, blessedly, unbearably, he laughed — a light, charming laugh — and shook his head as if at his own suspicions. "Well, Brannoc of the turnips. I'll take that tea after all. Weak, you said?"

"Weak," Emrys agreed, and poured it with steady hands, and hated themselves with a thoroughness that surprised even them.

* * *

Serel drank his tea standing, asked six more questions that were shaped like small talk and loaded like crossbows — how long had Brannoc lived here, had anyone passed through lately, did Brannoc know the widow Maren at the border (careful, careful, *she keeps to herself*, true and useless), had Brannoc ever served in the wars (no, ma'am, only turnips) — and then, at last, he set down his cup, thanked Emrys warmly for the hospitality, and said the sentence Emrys had been waiting for since the moment the dog began to bark.

"We're riding the valley rim for three more days. If you recall anything — a child's voice in the night, footprints too small for an adult, anything at all — you'll send word to the camp at Gray Ford. There's coin in it." He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and looked back with that pleasant, terrible smile. "And there's worse than no coin in it, for those who knew and said nothing. You understand. Everyone understands. It's the one law that needs no herald."

"I understand," Emrys said.

"Good man." He mounted, gathered his reins, and paused once more, head tilted, like a bird considering a worm. "Odd thing. Your dog next door has been barking since we arrived and hasn't stopped. Dogs usually settle. As if he smells something he doesn't like." A beat. "Or something he's protecting."

"Turnip hates everyone," Emrys said. "It's mutual."

Serel held the look a moment longer — long enough for Emrys to feel the exact shape of the blade not yet drawn — and then laughed again, touched his brow in mock salute, and rode off down the slope to rejoin his men, and the four of them went up the ridge road together, small and orderly and patient as rot, and disappeared into the gray morning.

Emrys stood in the yard until the hoofbeats faded entirely, and then a while longer, counting their own heartbeats, which had not slowed.

Then they went inside, knelt by the hearth, and lifted the board.

Wren looked up at them from the dark, dusted with herb-flecks, eyes huge and dry — the child had learned somewhere, badly, young, that crying made noise and noise made death. Their small hand crept out and gripped Emrys's sleeve. Not pulling. Just holding. Checking that the giant was still there, still solid, still theirs.

"They'll be back," Emrys said quietly, uselessly, in the common tongue. Then, because the child deserved better than useless: "*Seth-vel. Ossa-kiri.*" Safe now. Under the god's eye. Words Emrys hadn't spoken aloud in ten years, and they came out rusted and half-broken and true.

Wren's grip tightened. And the child answered — soft, grave, in the language older than kingdoms — with a word Emrys did remember, one of the few liturgical fragments that had survived the decade of silence:

*Vessel.*

Which was a strange thing to call yourself, Emrys thought. Stranger still, the way the child said it. Not like a name. Like a duty.

* * *

That evening Emrys packed.

Not frantically — frantic packing was for people who still believed they might stay ahead of things. Methodically. Dried meat, the good knife and the other good knife, flint, the coil of oiled rope they'd sworn ten years ago they'd never need again. Maren's herbs, because Maren would be insufferable about it otherwise. Every coin under the floorboard, which was not many coins, because hermits earned poorly.

Wren sat on the bed and watched, hugging their knees, understanding perfectly what packing meant. Children always understood. It was adults who needed lies.

At some point the child said, in that old, old tongue, a question. Emrys knew enough of the grammar to catch its shape even now: *Where are we going?*

And Emrys stood in the middle of the cottage that had been their tomb and their sanctuary for ten years, holding a bag that contained everything they owned, and found that they didn't have a lie ready. They'd used up their first one on Serel. Apparently lying was like bleeding; there was only so much in a person.

"I don't know," Emrys said honestly, in the common tongue, and Wren frowned, and Emrys translated anyway, clumsily, into the First Tongue, word by word like carrying water in cupped hands: *I don't know where. But not alone.*

The frown eased. The child nodded once, solemn as a small magistrate, as if this were an acceptable answer to the great questions of life.

Outside, the wind came down off the ridgeline, and Emrys could have sworn — though they'd swear nothing, ever again, not after today — that beneath it, very far off toward the lowlands, there was something almost like a voice. Not words. Not yet. Just a pressure against the world's skin, the way a sleeper presses a hand against a wall.

Ten years of silence, and tonight the silence had a texture. Tonight it sounded less like absence and more like waiting.

Emrys banked the fire, unlatched the door out of habit, and understood — with a sick, settling clarity, calm as deep water, terrible as a vow — that the choice had been made days ago. Maybe weeks. Maybe the first stormy dusk, at a knock on the door. Maybe ten years ago, mid-battle, when a god's voice died in their skull and they had crawled into a hole in the world rather than live with it.

The inquisitors were on the ridgeline. The child was in the bed. And tomorrow, or the day after, Emrys Villalobos — hermit, turnip farmer, liar for exactly one day — was going to break the only promise that had kept them alive.

They slept badly, which was to say normally, and dreamed of nothing at all.
