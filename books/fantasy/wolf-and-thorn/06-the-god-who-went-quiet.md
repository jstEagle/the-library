## 6. The God Who Went Quiet

Sleep had never been kind to Emrys Villalobos, but in the Thorn Valley it was at least honest about it. No dreams. Just the dark, and the small sounds of the cottage settling, and the child breathing in the corner by the hearth like a bellows with a leak in it.

Emrys lay awake and listened to that ragged little breath and thought, as they did most nights: *I could have stayed a soldier.*

The thought arrived the way rain arrived — inevitable, unwelcome, soaking through everything.

*You should have stayed a soldier.*

That voice was not theirs. It hadn't been anyone's for ten years. But old habits of listening died hard, and so Emrys lay there in the dark, listening to a god who no longer spoke, and remembered how it had all begun.

* * *

The swearing had happened on a Tuesday, which was somehow the funniest part.

Emrys had been twenty-two and already tired of being alive in the specific way that mercenaries got tired — not the dramatic weariness of poets but the flat arithmetic of it. Six years of contracts. Four scars worth counting. One friend worth keeping, and Sefa was currently three provinces away getting drunk on someone else's coin.

The war against the Ashen Compact had gone badly enough that the free cities were scraping the barrel, and the barrel contained people like Emrys, who had once thrown a sergeant off a bridge for kicking a dog and were therefore considered "difficult."

They'd been marched to a hilltop shrine outside Vellis Mark to be blessed before battle. Most of the company knelt through the liturgy the way men kneel through anything — impatiently, thinking about supper.

Emrys had not knelt. Not out of defiance. They simply couldn't make their knees agree to it, standing in front of the shrine of Ossa, the Quiet God, the Wolf That Waits, whose statues always showed him kneeling himself — head bowed, one hand pressed to the earth as if listening for something buried.

And the god had said, into the marrow of Emrys's bones, in a voice like a door opening in an empty house:

*"You're the only one who isn't pretending."*

Emrys had said, out loud, in front of two hundred soldiers and a very startled priest: "Pretending what?"

Nobody heard the answer but them. The god's reply came warm and dry as sun-baked stone:

*"Praying. I don't need prayers, little wolf. I need someone willing to stand where I can't, and do what I won't, and come home after. That's all a god ever needs. Someone to carry the part of the work that has hands."*

"You could have led with that," Emrys muttered.

The whole hilltop felt, briefly, like it smiled. Somewhere a crow laughed — actual laughter, which crows should not be capable of — and the priest swore for the rest of his life that Ossa himself had marked Emrys Villalobos that day.

What he'd actually done was simpler and worse. He'd asked. And Emrys, knees finally cooperating, had knelt in the dust and answered: *"All right. But I keep my name. And you talk to me. None of this silent-treatment business. If we're doing this, we do it like partners."*

*"Agreed,"* said the Wolf That Waits, and the agreement had held for nine years.

Nine years of a voice in Emrys's head commenting on their cooking (*"More salt. Mortals fear flavor."*), their tactics (*"Feint left. Also, your shield-sister is about to be flanked; tell her, tell her NOW—"*) and their love life (*"—"*). On that last subject the god maintained a respectful and deeply smug silence, which was its own commentary.

Nine years. And then the field at Harrow's Ford, and the middle of the battle, and the silence.

* * *

Harrow's Ford was the kind of battle historians would later describe with words like *pivotal*, which is what historians call a slaughter they weren't standing in.

Emrys remembered it in pieces, the way you remember any bad day: mud to the knee, the Compact's drummers beating their skull-rhythm, Sefa somewhere on the left wing shouting her shield-line into shape, the god's voice steady under it all like a riverbed —

*"Hold the ford until dusk. Dusk belongs to us. Trust the thirteenth hour, little wolf—"*

— and then, between one heartbeat and the next, nothing.

Not quiet. Quiet implies room around a sound. This was absence, sudden and total, like a lamp going out in a cellar. Emrys stood in the churned red water of the ford with a sword in each hand and called the god's name inside their own skull and heard only themselves, echoing.

They killed four men in the next minute without noticing any of them. That was the horror of it, later — the body kept working, kept fighting beautifully, because nine years of partnership lived in the muscles now. But Emrys was alone behind their own eyes for the first time since they were thirteen years old, and the loneliness was so vast it had weather. It had seasons.

"Dusk," Emrys whispered, spitting blood. "You said trust the thirteenth hour. You absolute *bastard*, where are you?"

Dusk came anyway. The Compact broke at dusk, exactly as promised, though whether by the god's design or coincidence or sheer exhaustion, nobody could say. The free cities called it a miracle. Emrys stood in the wreckage calling a name that didn't answer, and understood that miracles, like everything else, arrive unaccompanied.

They waited three days at the ford. Then three weeks at the ruined shrine outside Vellis Mark, sitting where the kneeling statue knelt, talking aloud to it like a madwoman because if the god had gone deaf rather than dead, then maybe loud would reach where soft had failed.

Nothing. Ten thousand soldiers across the old faith reported the same: the Voice was gone. Some said slain. Some said sleeping. Some said he'd finished whatever he'd been listening for, down there beneath the world, and gone home.

Emrys said nothing. Emrys packed a bag, walked east until the maps went vague, and found the place every exile eventually finds — a valley so steeped in iron and cold stone that magic curdled and died crossing its borders. A place with no gods in it at all. A hole in the world's hearing.

If the god wouldn't speak again, Emrys decided, then Emrys would go where the silence couldn't find them either. Fair was fair.

It took eleven days to walk into the Thorn Valley, and on the last day, at the border ridge, Emrys stopped and turned around and shouted at the empty sky, hoarse and furious and fourteen kinds of grieving:

"If you're dead, fine! If you're asleep, FINE! But if you just *left* — if you walked out on me mid-battle without a word — then I hope wherever you are, it's boring, and the food's terrible, and nobody appreciates you!"

A bird started up somewhere. Just a bird.

Emrys cried the whole way down into the valley, and built the cottage that winter, and told themselves the tears were from the wind.

* * *

Guilt is a patient tenant. It doesn't bang on the walls. It just redecorates, slowly, until one day you look around your life and realize every piece of furniture was chosen by someone else.

In the valley's tenth year, Emrys had gotten good at not looking. Routine helped. Patrol the eastern fence line. Mend the roof before the rains. Trade honey and smoked fish at the crossroads market, where old Tomas would complain about the price of everything and Emrys would overcharge him gently and slip the difference back into his coat pocket, and both of them would pretend this was normal commerce.

"You're a strange one," Tomas liked to say, sucking his teeth. "Soldier's shoulders on a farmer. What'd you run from, eh? A wife? Taxes?"

"A god," Emrys said, once, drunk on Tomas's plum brandy, and Tomas had laughed so hard he fell off his stool, and Emrys had laughed too, and it was almost true enough to be funny.

Because here was the thing Emrys did not say, did not think in daylight, kept folded up in the locked room at the back of the skull where the worst things live:

The god hadn't abandoned them. Emrys had failed *first*.

It was a small failure, as failures went. A village called Lowmark, in the eighth year, on the edge of the Compact's territory. Ossa had sent word — a feeling more than words, the way he sometimes spoke when the matter mattered — *"There is something wrong at Lowmark. Go and see. Take no soldiers; soldiers frighten what sleeps there. Go quietly, little wolf."*

And Emrys had been tired. Contract-worn, knee-aching, three days out from a fever. And there was a wedding in the company, Sefa dancing with a quartermaster's daughter, cheap wine going around, and Emrys had thought: *one night. The wrongness will keep one night. Gods are patient; it's their whole trade.*

One night became two. On the third morning, riders brought news: Lowmark, burned. Not raided — burned from the inside, all two hundred souls, every building, every well poisoned after. The Compact's work, everyone said. Inquisitors of the new order, sweeping the borderlands for "god-touched contamination."

Emrys rode to the ashes and stood in them for a day and a night, and the god said nothing, and Emrys understood — with the awful clarity that only arrives too late — that the silence had started *there*. Not at Harrow's Ford. At Lowmark. Three days of silence while two hundred people burned, because the god's one pair of hands in the world had wanted wine and a dance.

He had needed Emrys, and Emrys had chosen the wedding. And whatever came to Lowmark on that third night — whatever the god had sent one exhausted mercenary to prevent — it had taken something from him. Broken something. The Voice at Harrow's Ford had been a dying fire's last light, and Emrys had spent ten years telling themselves a kinder story.

*He left me.* Safer, that story. *He left me,* instead of *I left him, when it counted, for a cup of wine.*

Sefa had seen it differently, back when Sefa still talked to them. She'd found Emrys at the ruined shrine in week two of the vigil, sitting in the rain, and crouched down and said, in her blunt way:

"He's a god, Emrys. Whatever ate Lowmark, do you truly believe you'd have made a dent in it? He didn't send you to save them. He sent you to *witness*. And you witnessed plenty, after. Don't build a coffin out of one missed night."

But Sefa had never carried a god. She didn't know that the guilt wasn't rational. Guilt never is. It just sits in the chest like a swallowed stone, and every year the stone gets a little heavier, and eventually you arrange your whole life around its weight and call the arrangement peace.

So: the valley. The cottage. The bees, because even exiles need something that hums. Ten years of a life shaped precisely around the space where a voice used to be.

Until a storm knocked a mud-caked child against Emrys's door, and the child opened their mouth, and the First Tongue came out — fluent, native, *impossible* — and every locked room in Emrys's skull blew open at once.

* * *

Now Emrys lay awake past midnight, listening to Wren breathe, and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding for six days.

A child speaking the god's own language. Out of nowhere. Out of the lowlands, where — if the rumors drifting up the trade road meant anything — the usurper Vaelric's inquisitors were burning villages and asking after relics.

*God-touched contamination,* they'd called it in Emrys's day, too. Some phrases outlive the regimes that coin them.

And here was the thought that finally dragged Emrys upright in the dark, heart slamming, hands cold:

*Lowmark wasn't random.*

Two hundred souls, burned in one night, in a village the god specifically flagged as *wrong* — three years before his Voice went silent forever. Something had happened at Lowmark. Something had been *started* there, or *stopped* there, or *hidden* there. And now a child comes walking out of those same lowlands wearing the god's tongue like a second skin, and the first thing Vaelric's hounds ask after, at every crossroads, is a stolen relic.

Emrys sat on the edge of the bed in the black room and felt the old stone in their chest shift for the first time in ten years. Not lighter. Just... awake. Paying attention.

Because there was a second arithmetic, underneath the first, and it went like this: if the god had gone silent because something broke at Lowmark — something Emrys failed to stop — then the child in the hearth-corner might be the other half of that sentence. The continuation. The thing the god had hidden away with his last strength, the way a dying man hides a letter in a floorboard.

Which meant the silence had never been abandonment at all.

Which meant Emrys had spent a decade grieving a betrayal that had actually been a sacrifice — and the person who'd paid for it was asleep by Emrys's fire, small and mud-streaked and hunted.

"Oh," Emrys said aloud, to no one, to the dark, to the ghost of a voice. "Oh, you *idiot*. Both of us. Two idiots."

Wren stirred. In sleep, the child murmured something in the First Tongue — a fragment of liturgy, Emrys realized with a lurch, half a psalm older than kingdoms — and rolled over, and went on breathing.

Outside, far off toward the western ridge, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then a long, carrying silence, the kind a valley makes when it's holding its breath.

Emrys pulled on boots, took down the sword they hadn't cleaned in a year, and stood a moment in the doorway with the child's warmth at their back and the cold coming in under the door.

Tomorrow, they decided. Tomorrow they'd walk to the valley's edge and put questions to Maren the hedge-witch, who knew more than a hedge-witch should and charged less than she ought. Because if Vaelric's riders were already sniffing along the ridgeline —

Well. Emrys had failed one god at Lowmark by staying comfortable.

They were not going to make the same mistake twice.
