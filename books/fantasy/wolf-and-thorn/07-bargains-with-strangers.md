## 7. Bargains with Strangers

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn, and the valley wore it the way an old soldier wears a borrowed coat—wrong in every seam, but grateful all the same.

Emrys stood at their doorway with a cup of barley tea going cold in one hand and watched Wren investigate the woodpile.

The child had been with them four days now. Four days of mud tracked across clean floors, of questions asked in two languages and answered in neither, of small catastrophes—the spilled flour, the terrified chicken, the afternoon Wren had decided the hearth was for climbing—and Emrys had discovered something alarming about themselves: they did not hate it.

That was the alarming part. They had spent ten years building a life with no soft edges, a life that could be abandoned in ten minutes flat if the wind turned wrong, and this child had been in the valley less than a week and already the door latch had developed a habit of staying where Wren could reach it. Already there were two bowls drying by the basin instead of one.

*Foolish*, Emrys told their own reflection in the water barrel. The reflection, as usual, declined to argue.

Wren had found a beetle under the third log and was holding it up to the grey morning light, saying something long and liquid and utterly serious. The First Tongue. Even now, four days in, hearing it made the back of Emrys's neck prickle like a hand resting there. It was the language Ossa had used at the binding-oath, at the war-councils, at the last terrible moment on the field at Carrowmere when the voice had simply—stopped. Mid-sentence. Mid-word, almost. Like a man struck down between heartbeats.

And here was a feral child chattering away in it over a beetle.

"You'll want to put that down before it puts itself somewhere worse," Emrys said, in Common, because speaking the god's tongue aloud still felt like pressing on a bruise.

Wren looked up. The beetle was relocated with great ceremony into the moss beside the woodpile, and then the child said something else—a question, by the tilt of it—and pointed east, toward the valley's border, toward the thin grey line where the thorns thickened and the world went strange.

Emrys followed the pointing finger and felt their stomach drop half an inch.

"No," they said.

Wren said it again. Pointed again. Insistent.

"I said no."

The child folded their arms in a way that was so precisely Emrys's own gesture from yesterday's argument about washing behind the ears that it took real effort not to laugh. That would only encourage them.

But Wren was right, damn it all. Emrys had been circling the thought since the second night, when the child had slept by the fire glowing—not brightly, nothing a stranger would notice, just a faint gold shimmer under the skin, like lamplight through a closed shutter—and Emrys had lain awake doing arithmetic they didn't like. A child who spoke the dead language. A child who glowed. A valley that swallowed magic whole, which meant the glow only showed here, in the one place in the world it couldn't be seen from outside—but children grew. Children wandered. And Vaelric's inquisitors were already sniffing along the ridgelines asking after a stolen relic, and relics, Emrys knew, were rarely described in detail until after you'd found them.

There was exactly one person in the Thorn Valley who might know what lived in a child's blood. One person who'd been exiled from three different provinces for knowing too much about exactly such things.

Maren.

Who lived a hard day's walk east. Who hadn't spoken more than forty words to Emrys in six years. Who charged for her knowledge in coin, favors, or secrets, whichever wounded most.

"Fine," Emrys told Wren, draining the cold tea. "But you're carrying your own bread."

* * *

They left at first light with the valley steaming around them, the rain-water rising off the bracken in slow silver ribbons. Wren walked like the valley belonged to them—hopping the drainage ruts, greeting crows with great formality, stopping twice to taste things Emrys had to confiscate. The bread lasted until midmorning. The dried apples lasted considerably less.

It should have been miserable. Ten years ago it would have been: ten years ago Emrys had marched with three hundred sworn swords and a god's voice in their ear, and a day's walk with a mud-child would have been beneath mention. But the valley changed the scale of things. Without magic, without war, a day was just a day, and the road was just a road, and somewhere around noon Emrys caught themselves teaching Wren which mushrooms would kill you—which ones, specifically, and in what order the dying happened, because the child had asked *why*, in Common, in a cracked little voice, and deserved better than *because*.

"Your grandmother teach you plants?" Wren asked, later. One of perhaps thirty Common words, deployed like a soldier who'd learned to make each one count.

"No one taught me. I learned by eating the wrong ones."

A solemn nod. *Wisdom*, the nod said. *Earned the hard way.*

By late afternoon the trees began to change—or rather, to stop pretending. The Thorn Valley earned its name honestly enough in its middle reaches, but at the eastern border the thorn-brakes rose into walls twenty feet high, black-barked, interwoven, older than any road. Magic couldn't live in the valley; everyone knew that. What fewer people asked was what the valley *did* with everything it refused. Maren claimed the brakes drank it. Maren claimed a lot of things.

Her cottage sat in a clearing where the brake-wall curved aside like a jaw deciding not to bite. It was built of stone and stubbornness, chimney leaning, garden riotous, and a fence of woven hazel that served no purpose whatsoever except to declare that the woman inside believed in boundaries.

Emrys stopped at the gate. "Stay close," they murmured, "and let me do the talking."

Wren, naturally, walked straight past them and knocked on the door.

There was a pause, a shuffling, and then the door opened on Maren: small, brown as a nut, wrapped in seven shawls against a chill that wasn't present, with eyes the color of river ice. She looked at Wren. She looked at Emrys. Her expression suggested she had been expecting both for years and found them disappointing.

"Well," she said. "The hermit's grown a child. Come in before my onions see you and gossip."

* * *

Inside smelled of smoke, tansy, and something coppery underneath that made Emrys's hackles lift. Maren's cottage always smelled slightly of blood, and she had never once explained why, and Emrys had long ago decided some mysteries were cheaper unopened.

She set out three cups anyway, which meant she'd been watching the road. Which meant she'd known they were coming since at least dawn.

"Hedge-witches," Emrys said, sitting. "Can't even ambush one properly."

"Ambush implies I care." Maren lowered herself into her chair with the groan of a much larger woman. Her gaze slid to Wren, who had stationed themself by the fire with the wary precision of a creature that had learned rooms could turn dangerous. Something flickered across the old woman's face—not surprise. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite. Emrys didn't love either option.

"Eat?" she offered Wren, and got a shake of the head.

"They don't eat much in front of strangers," Emrys said.

"And am I a stranger, mercenary? After six years of you buying feverfew off me and pretending we're not neighbors?"

"You're a hedge-witch who reads people's blood for money. Everyone's a stranger until the transaction closes."

Maren smiled, showing teeth gone yellow at the edges. "Still the charmer. Go on, then. You've dragged something across my threshold and you're pretending it's about feverfew. Ask."

Emrys had rehearsed this on the road, a careful oblique approach, questions dressed as complaints. Sitting in the copper-smelling warmth of the cottage with the child's eyes on their back, they abandoned all of it.

"There's something in their blood," they said. "I need to know what."

Silence. The fire popped. Outside, a crow said something rude.

"Do you know," Maren said slowly, "in thirty years of this trade, how many people have walked through that door and asked me that question plainly?"

"How many?"

"One other. And he paid very well indeed, and what I told him is currently being used to hunt children through the lowlands." She turned her cup on its saucer, once, twice. "So you understand why plainness makes me nervous."

"Vaelric's people came to you?"

"Rode to the brake-line three weeks back. Polite as priests. Asked whether anything *unusual* had crossed into the valley lately. Offered me a purse that would've bought this cottage, the garden, and my funeral besides." She snorted. "I told them the valley eats magic and spits out fools, and they wrote it down like scripture. Inquisitors. Give a man a title and he stops listening."

"What did you actually tell them?"

"Nothing. Which is different from lying, whatever your god used to say." Her eyes came up sharp. "Yes, I know whose tongue the child chirps in. You think I've survived three exiles being unobservant?"

Emrys kept their face still, mostly. "Then you know why I'm asking."

"I know why you're *not* asking, which is more interesting." Maren set the cup down and leaned forward, and for a moment the shawls and the stoop fell away and Emrys saw the woman who had once, allegedly, talked a plague out of a walled city. "Show me their hands."

"Wren," Emrys said softly, in the child's own tongue—the first time they'd spoken it aloud in ten years, and the word came out rough as a stone turned in a dry socket. *Come here.* Wren crossed the room and stood before the witch, chin up, hands out, trusting absolutely, and Emrys's chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

Maren turned the small palms over. Ran one cracked thumb across the lifeline, then pressed hard, nail whitening—and Wren flinched, and where the thumb had been, light bloomed. Gold, faint, rising through the skin like sunken treasure disturbed from silt. For one heartbeat the whole palm glowed, and in the glow Emrys saw—not patterns, exactly. Movement. Something turning over in its sleep beneath the surface of the child, vast and slow.

Maren snatched her hand back and sat very still.

"Well?" Emrys said.

"Well," the witch repeated. She wiped her thumb on her shawl with unusual care, as though the light might stain. "I have read soldiers' blood and kings' blood. I read a drowned girl once whose veins ran with river-light till the week she died. This—" she nodded at Wren, who was examining their own palm with scientific disappointment that it had stopped glowing— "this isn't power in the blood. This is blood *belonging* to something else. There's a tenant in that child, mercenary. An old one. Old enough that I don't have a word for it, and I collect words the way you collect scars."

"The old religion says the gods walked in flesh once," Emrys said carefully. "Before the Sundering. Before they went quiet."

"The old religion also says you can cure baldness with bat fat, so let's not build a cathedral on it yet." But Maren's voice lacked its usual scorn, and her eyes stayed on Wren a beat too long. "What I can tell you is this: it's alive, it's aware, and it's getting stronger. Whatever sleeps in this child is waking up. Slowly. The way a fire wakes in wet wood—first the steam, then the hiss, then the flame. And when it flames—" she spread her hands— "everyone for a hundred miles will feel the heat. Including, I promise you, the men on the ridgeline."

Emrys sat back. The chair creaked. In the hearth, the fire had settled to embers, and Wren's face above them was lit orange and calm, watching the two adults with the patient expression of someone who understood that important things were being decided over their head and had decided to allow it.

"The purse," Emrys said. "The one the inquisitors offered. How large, exactly?"

Maren's eyebrows climbed. "Ah."

"I'm asking as a matter of tactics."

"You're asking because part of you wants to know what your soul goes for these days, and you're hoping it's insulting." The witch poured herself more tea from a pot that had clearly been waiting. "Four hundred crowns. Plus a pardon. Full amnesty, lands restored, the right to bear arms again. Everything they took from you at Carrowmere, handed back with ribbons on it."

The words landed in Emrys's stomach like stones dropped down a well. Four hundred crowns was a fortune. The pardon was—something Emrys had trained themself never to name even in the privacy of their own skull, and here it was, priced and itemized, available for the price of one small sleeping passenger.

"That's a lot of feverfew," they said.

"It is." Maren studied them over the rim of her cup. "For what it's worth—and it's worth nothing, sentiment never is—I don't think you came here to ask my price. I think you came here to find out if I'd already sold you. Because if I had, you'd want to know how much time you'd have to run."

"And did you?"

"Mercenary. I've been exiled from three provinces for knowing things. Do you imagine I'd spend my fourth exile informing on the only interesting thing to cross this valley in a decade?" She waved a hand at Wren. "Besides. Look at it. When's the last time the world gave anyone a miracle, and us the privilege of tripping over it?"

"It's not a miracle. It's a target."

"It's both. Most miracles are." Maren set her cup down and fixed Emrys with the look of a physician delivering the part of the diagnosis she'd been saving. "Now listen, because I'll say this once and charge you for it anyway. That thing in the child—it knows you. Not *of* you. *You*. When I touched it, it turned toward you like a plant turns to a window. Whatever oath you swore and whatever broke it, the bond didn't break. Bonds like that don't. They just go quiet and wait." She stood, joints crackling like green kindling. "You can't outrun it and you can't sell it without selling yourself, and I suspect you figured both of those out on the walk here and wanted an old woman to confirm it. Consider it confirmed. That'll be two bits of silver for the reading and one for the advice, and the advice was free, so three bits total, and mind the onions on your way out."

Emrys paid. Of course they paid. Some rituals outlasted gods.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the brake-wall, halfway home, because Wren's legs were short and dusk had come down like a lid. Emrys built a small fire, fed the child dried apples and half their own ration without noticing they'd done it, and sat watching the sparks climb toward stars that the valley showed in unusual abundance—as if, having no magic to swallow, it hoarded light instead.

Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, the way children do, some observation about crows abandoned at the frontier of dreams. Within minutes the faint gold shimmer woke beneath their skin, pulsing gently with breath. Lamplight through a shutter.

Emrys sat a long while doing arithmetic.

Four hundred crowns. A pardon. The right to stand in a town square without checking rooftops. Ten years of exile traded for one night's knife-work, and no one in the free cities would ever know, and the child—well. The child would sleep. That was what the inquisitors promised, wasn't it? A quiet end. Cleaner than whatever was waking.

And against that: a beetle carried in cupped hands with enormous ceremony. A question about mushrooms asked in a cracked voice, wanting the *why* of things. A door latch learned by heart. A palm that glowed like sunken treasure.

*It knows you,* Maren had said. *Not of you. You.*

Emrys looked at the small sleeper, and at the gold light rising and falling with each breath, and felt the truth of it settle into their bones with the weight of a blade accepted: the choice had been made already. Somewhere back at the cottage door, or on the road, or the first storm-tossed night—somewhere the knife had come down on the side of keeping, and all that remained was to learn what keeping cost.

They banked the fire, pulled their cloak around the child's shoulders, and lay back to watch the stars.

Somewhere east, beyond the brakes, beyond the ridgeline, a horse screamed—a short, sharp sound, human-made, cut off fast. Then another. Then silence, the particular silence of men working hard to be quiet.

Emrys was on their feet with a knife drawn before their eyes had fully opened, heart hammering the old war-rhythm, and beside them Wren slept on, glowing softly, a beacon in the dark—and out along the brake-wall, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of many boots moving together through wet bracken.

Coming this way. Coming, Emrys realized with sick clarity, straight for the only lit thing in the valley.
