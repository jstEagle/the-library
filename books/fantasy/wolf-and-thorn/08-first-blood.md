## 8. First Blood

The morning the inquisitors came, Emrys was teaching Wren how to gut a fish.

It was not going well.

"No," Emrys said, for the fourth time, "you hold the knife like you're shaking someone's hand, not like you're stabbing them."

Wren adjusted their grip with great solemnity and then immediately stabbed the fish again, which was already dead, which had been dead for an hour, and looked up with an expression of pure defiant pride.

"That's — all right," Emrys sighed. "That's enthusiasm. We'll work with enthusiasm." They took the knife back before the trout could be reduced to paste. "In my defense of your honor, I should say that when I was your age, I once tried to scale a fish with a war axe."

Wren's eyes went wide. Then narrow. Then wide again, in the particular rhythm that meant *you are lying to me, giant, and I am deciding whether to forgive it.*

"True story. My shield-sister Sefa laughed so hard she fell in the river. The fish was fine. Better than fine. It achieved a kind of local fame."

The child made the small clicking sound at the back of their throat that served them for laughter, and Emrys felt the familiar ache underneath it — the one shaped like a voice that used to say things worth laughing about. Ten years since Ossa had spoken. Ten years of doing the talking for both of them, badly.

They were working on the third fish when Wren went still.

Emrys knew every version of the child's stillness by now: the sleep-stillness, the sulk-stillness, the *I have eaten something I found in the woods and do not wish to discuss it* stillness. This was none of those. This was the one from the first night, mud-caked and burning-eyed on the doorstep. The listening one.

"What is it?" Emrys asked quietly.

Wren pointed — not at the road, not at the tree line, but at the ground. At the vibrations coming up through it, faint as a heartbeat. Hooves. Many. Moving slow.

Emrys had spent eleven years as a soldier and nine as a hermit, and the second job had not rusted the first. They set down the fish, wiped their hands on their apron out of pure old habit, and crossed to the window in three silent strides.

Four riders on the western ridge. Not valley folk — valley folk rode ponies and slouched like the ride was a personal insult. These sat tall, disciplined, in grey cloaks that drank the morning light. And on the shoulder of the nearest rider's cloak, when the sun caught it just wrong: a silver sigil. A tower over a broken crown.

Vaelric's mark.

"They've come for you, little bird," Emrys said, without turning around. "Or for whatever they think you are."

* * *

There had been a plan, technically. Emrys had made plans the way other people made soup — often, in large quantities, mostly for comfort. If riders came: take Wren through the root cellar, out the sinkhole, along the dry streambed to Maren's, and let the witch's wards argue on their behalf. It was a good plan. It had contingencies. It had even been rehearsed twice, which for Emrys counted as obsessive.

What actually happened was that the riders did not come to the door.

They split up.

Two circled toward the back of the cottage where the cellar hatch sat under a washline. One dismounted at the gate and began walking up the path with the unhurried gait of a man who has never once been told no. The fourth stayed mounted on the ridge, watching, a spyglass glinting.

"Change of plans," Emrys murmured. "Cellar's out. Under the bed, Wren. Don't come out no matter what you hear. Especially if what you hear is quiet."

Wren grabbed Emrys's sleeve. Their eyes were furious and frightened in equal measure, and they said something in the First Tongue — rapid, insistent — that Emrys only half understood after ten years of silence. Something like *don't hide me* and something like *they burn us.*

"I know," Emrys said. "Under the bed. Please."

And the miracle of it — the thing Emrys would turn over and over in the years after, or would have, if the years after had gone differently — was that Wren went.

The man at the gate knocked. Politely. That was somehow the worst part.

Emrys opened the door holding a fish knife, because there had been no time to find anything better, and immediately wished they'd chosen anything else. A fish knife says *hermit*. It does not say *please leave*.

"Inquisitor Halven," the man said. He was perhaps fifty, weathered, with the kind of face that had been kind once and had gotten tired of it. His eyes went past Emrys into the cottage, cataloguing: the two bowls, the small stool, the child-sized mending pile. "Forgive the intrusion, friend. We're searching for a stolen relic. Small. Might present as a child."

"Valley doesn't get much thieving," Emrys said. "Nothing here worth stealing except my onions, and I'd fight God himself over those."

Halven smiled thinly. "May we look?"

"The valley charter says no armed men past the boundary stones without the headman's word."

"The valley charter," Halven said gently, "is a piece of paper signed by a king who no longer rules. May we *look*, friend? Or shall I remember your face?"

And there it was. Not a question at all.

Emrys had lied exactly once in ten years, and it had been four days ago, to these same grey cloaks on the road. It had felt like swallowing a stone. Now the second lie came easier, which was its own kind of horror.

"Ain't seen any children but mine own nephew," Emrys said, jerking a thumb eastward. "Visits sometimes. You're welcome to walk to his village and count every brat from here to the mill, takes an hour, I'll lend you boots."

Something flickered across Halven's face — not belief. Recognition. He was studying Emrys the way Emrys studied terrain, and Emrys realized, with a cold drop through the stomach, that the man wasn't looking at a hermit at all.

"Hold," Halven called softly, to the men at the back of the cottage. "Hold and come round front."

That was when the man by the washline stepped into view, and Emrys stopped breathing.

He was younger than Emrys remembered. Everyone was. But there was the gap in his teeth from a spear butt at Redford Bridge, and the way he carried his left shoulder slightly high from the scar he'd earned the same day, and — Emrys's hands remembered before their mind did — the way he held a blade, thumb along the flat, a farm boy's grip trained into a killer's.

Doss. Private Doss, Fourth Company, who had shared a cookfire with Emrys for two years of the war. Who had sung badly and laughed easily and once given Emrys his last apple during the starvation march and refused to speak of it after.

Who now wore Vaelric's tower-and-crown, and whose eyes passed over Emrys's face with nothing in them at all.

"You," Emrys said. The word came out cracked.

Doss tilted his head. "Do I know you, friend?"

And the terrible answer, the one Emrys would carry like a stone in the chest for the rest of however long they lasted, was: *no.* No, he didn't. Because Doss hadn't survived Redford Bridge — Emrys had *seen* him fall, had knelt beside him in the churned mud while the god's voice screamed through their skull and the world came apart, had closed Doss's eyes with two fingers and promised him, in the First Tongue, in the language of oaths, *it ends soon, brother, hold on, it ends soon.*

Except it hadn't ended. Doss was standing in the morning light wearing another king's colors, alive, and either Emrys's memory had failed them at the worst possible moment or —

or the god's last gift, in the moment before the silence, had been mercy. Had been a lie told gently to a dying soldier's friend. *He's gone. Let him go.*

Which meant the war had never ended for anyone else. Which meant Emrys had crawled into this valley to escape a grief that had, in some ways, been a gift-wrapped illusion. Which meant ten years of mourning might have been — 

The thought didn't finish. There wasn't time.

Because Halven's gaze slid past Emrys's shoulder, into the dim interior of the cottage, and found the small pair of eyes peering out from under the bed frame, because Wren was eight years old and brave and had never once in their short ferocious life done what they were told.

"There," Halven said quietly. "You see? No charter needed after all."

* * *

Later — in the years after, in the hollow years, in the years when Emrys would wake at night reaching for a name they could no longer find — Emrys would remember almost nothing of the next minute intact. Only pieces, the way a storm leaves debris.

The piece of Halven drawing steel and saying, almost regretfully, "Relics are property of the Crown. So are relics that walk."

The piece of Doss lunging through the doorway, quick and professional, and Emrys moving to meet him — not thinking, *thinking was the enemy* — body remembering everything the mind had buried. The parry. The step inside his reach. The fish knife going up under the jaw into the brain, the way they taught you in the Third Company when you had a short blade and no time.

The piece of the second man from the washline coming around the corner and Emrys taking his wrist, breaking it, taking his own knife and giving it back to him under the ribs. Old drill. Older than the war. Older than grief.

The piece of Halven running — actually running, the inquisitor's discipline cracking into plain animal terror — and getting six strides down the path before Emrys's throwing arm, which had not been consulted, put the gutting knife between his shoulder blades.

And the piece that stayed longest, the piece that would outlive all the others:

Wren, standing in the doorway, watching. Not screaming. Not crying. Watching with the ancient, unblinking attention of something far older than eight years old, and saying, very softly, in the First Tongue, words Emrys understood perfectly and would give anything never to understand again:

*"So that is what you were, before the valley."*

* * *

Afterward, there was blood to wash off, and bodies to deal with, and a horse on the ridge to consider — the fourth rider had wheeled away at the first sound of violence, galloping west with the spyglass still clutched in one fist, which meant that within a day, within two at most, the grey cloaks would come back with more than four swords.

Emrys did all of it with the strange mechanical calm that combat always left behind, the calm they had once believed was gone forever. They dragged what needed dragging. They burned what needed burning, though the smoke would be visible for miles and hiding it was a fool's errand anyway; the rider had seen enough. They cleaned the fish knife, because some part of their mind insisted on the ritual of it, insisted that the tool that had killed two men this morning should go back in the drawer clean, next to the spoons.

Then they sat down on the doorstep, and shook.

Not from fear. From the other thing. The discovery. The killer had not died at Redford Bridge. The killer had not drowned in the Thorn Valley's magic-dead soil. The killer had been sitting inside them for nine years, patient as a seed, wearing a hermit's apron and telling jokes about fish, waiting for one bad morning to bloom.

"You're crying," Wren observed, from the doorway. Not unkindly. Curiously, the way children observe weather.

"I'm — " Emrys laughed, wet and awful. "Yeah. Yeah, little bird. Seems I am."

Wren considered this. Then they came and sat down on the step beside Emrys, close enough that their small shoulder pressed against Emrys's arm, and said nothing for a long time, which was the most generous thing anyone had ever done for them.

Finally the child spoke, in the common tongue, carefully, the way they spoke it when the words mattered most:

"He knew you. The one with the broken teeth. Before."

"Yes."

"And you thought he was dead."

"Yes."

Wren nodded slowly, absorbing this, filing it somewhere behind those too-old eyes. "The others will come," they said. It wasn't a question. Children of wars know how sieges begin.

"Yes. More than before. Angrier."

"So we run."

Emrys looked at the child. At the garden they'd planted in spring, the beans just coming up. At the drawer with the clean spoons. Nine years of building a life out of silence and routine, and it had taken one morning to show them what it had been built on top of.

"We run," Emrys agreed, and stood, and went inside to pack.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, Wren paused at the threshold and glanced back once at the scorch-mark where the bodies had burned — and murmured something low in the First Tongue, something that made the ashes stir, briefly, like breath.

Something was waking up. And it wanted to go home.
