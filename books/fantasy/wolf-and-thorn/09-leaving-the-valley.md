## 9. Leaving the Valley

Wren had counted the stones on the cottage wall four hundred and eleven times before the giant finally said they were leaving.

Not said, exactly. The giant did not say much of anything. But that morning Emrys had come out with two packs instead of one — a small pack, sized for someone with small shoulders — and had set it down by the door without ceremony, the way you'd leave out a bowl for a cat you had not yet decided to keep.

"That's yours," Emrys said. "If you want it."

Wren touched it. It was stiff leather, new-stitched, and inside there was bread wrapped in cloth, a flint, a knife with a wooden handle worn smooth by somebody else's hand. Somebody else's hand. That was the thing about everything in this valley: it all came from before. From when people still had people.

"Where are we going?" Wren asked, in the words-with-many-edges, the tongue the giant sometimes answered in but never started.

"Away," said Emrys, in the soft-tongue, and began saddling the mule as though *away* were a place on a map, just past the river bend, next to the village where nobody smiled.

* * *

They walked for most of the day, and Wren learned three things about traveling with a mercenary.

One: mercenaries do not walk like people. They walk like weather coming. Emrys moved through the trees in a way that made no sound at all, which seemed unfair given how much noise the giant's boots ought to have made, and every so often would stop without stopping — just flow into stillness, head tilted, listening to some argument between birds that Wren couldn't hear.

Two: mercenaries feed their mules first. Always. Wren had watched this happen now twice and found it both ridiculous and secretly wonderful. The mule's name was Barley, and he had long eyelashes and an opinion about everything, and when Wren scratched his nose he made a face like an old man being told good news.

Three: mercenaries count things. Steps. Streams. The number of times Wren looked over their shoulder. Wren knew they were being counted because once, when Wren turned around quickly, Emrys was already looking away, very interested in a shrub.

"Why are we going?" Wren asked, around midday, mouth full of bread.

Emrys was quiet for so long that Wren thought the question had been swallowed by the forest. Then: "Because men came to my door with questions. Because I killed some of them. Because more will come." A pause. "Because you shouldn't be here."

"You could have sold me," Wren said. "The witch-lady said they pay for things like me."

Emrys's jaw did the thing it did — tightened, went white at the corner, like someone biting down on a word. "Yes," the giant said. "I could have."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

And here Wren got something rare, which was a whole sentence from Emrys Villalobos delivered while actually looking at them: "Because I've been paid before. For other things. It doesn't buy what they tell you it buys."

Wren didn't understand this. But they filed it away with the other giant-things, in the place where they kept the memory of Emrys standing in the doorway during the storm, holding a lantern up against the rain like a man defending a hill with nothing but light.

* * *

The boundary came at dusk, and it came without warning.

That was the strange part. Wren had expected a wall, or at least a line — a scar in the ground, a row of dead trees, something. But the Thorn Valley simply went on being itself, grey-green and quiet, and then it stopped. There was a stream, no wider than Barley was long, chuckling over stones like any stream anywhere.

Emrys stopped at its edge and stood there a long time. Long enough that Barley drank twice.

"It won't hurt you," Emrys said at last, and Wren understood the giant was talking to themselves as much as to him.

"What happens?" Wren asked.

"I don't know," Emrys said. "I've never crossed back."

That stopped Wren cold. Ten years. The witch Maren had said it, hadn't she — ten years in the magic-dead land, hiding from a god who wouldn't speak. Wren tried to imagine staying in one place for ten years, even a good place, even a safe one, and their skin prickled all over. The valley wasn't bad. But it was a room. And rooms, Wren knew now, in the deep bone-way children know things, were only ever temporary. Sooner or later the door opened or the walls came down.

"Come on, then," Wren said, and took the giant's hand, and stepped across the stream.

* * *

Later, Wren would try to describe it, and fail.

It was like the moment when you've been cold for so long you've forgotten you're cold, and then someone puts a blanket over your shoulders. Except the blanket was everywhere. It was in the ground and the air and the water and it rushed into Wren through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands and the crown of their head, all at once, like the world exhaling after holding its breath.

It was also — this was the important part, the part Wren would remember — it was *glad*.

The magic knew them. That was the horrible, wonderful truth of it. Whatever lived coiled in Wren's blood, whatever made the witch-lady cross herself and make the giant lie to soldiers — it belonged to this. To the wide green singing world outside the silent valley. And the world recognized it, the way a dog recognizes its own name.

"Oh," Wren said. Just that. Just *oh*, and then they laughed, because it tickled, actually tickled, like drinking something fizzy, like running downhill too fast, like —

Like home.

They heard Emrys make a sound behind them. Not a word. More like the noise a door makes when it hasn't been opened in years and the hinges are arguing about it.

Wren turned around and saw why.

The giant was on their knees in the shallows of the stream, and the look on Emrys's face was the look of someone who has taken off a heavy coat after forgetting they were wearing it — except the coat was grief, and it had been on so long the giant had grown around it, and now it was gone and there was nothing underneath but a person, blinking, wet-eyed, astonished.

"Don't look at me," Emrys said hoarsely.

So Wren didn't. Wren looked at the sky instead, which had gone the color of plum skins, and at the fireflies beginning to stitch themselves into the dark, and at their own hands, which were —

Which were glowing.

Just faintly. Just along the old scars and the lines of the palms, a soft gold light, like holding a candle behind your fingers. Wren watched it move when they breathed. Watched it brighten when they laughed, dim when they thought hard about the burning lowlands, brighten again when Barley shoved his nose into their hair.

"Is this normal?" Wren asked.

"No," said Emrys, who had gotten up and was now looking at Wren's hands with an expression Wren was starting to learn meant *trouble*. The giant dug into their own pack and came out with a cloak, grey-brown, the color of nothing much, and wrapped it around Wren's shoulders until only their face showed. "Keep it under the wool. If anyone sees—"

"Sees what? This?" Wren held up a glowing finger, delighted despite everything. It really was rather pretty.

"*Yes.*"

But that was the trouble with being eight, or nine, or however old Wren was (they'd stopped counting; counting made the burning lowlands come back). The trouble with being small was that wonder always arrived ahead of fear. The light felt like being told a secret by the whole world. How could a secret that lovely be dangerous?

They camped that night in a stand of birches, and Wren fell asleep listening to the magic hum in the grass, and dreamed of a tower made of singing, and woke at moonset because Barley was screaming.

* * *

Well. Not screaming. But making the loud worried bray that meant *something is wrong and I am a mule and therefore it is your problem.*

Wren sat up. The fire had burned to orange eyes. Emrys was already awake — of course Emrys was already awake, Emrys slept the way cats sleep, technically asleep, actually listening — and the giant's hand was pressed flat against Wren's sternum, holding them down gently, and the giant's face was turned toward the south.

"Listen," Emrys whispered.

Wren listened. Crickets. Wind. Barley's offended breathing. And underneath it, far off but getting closer, a sound Wren had heard before, in the worst dream they had, the one about the burning lowlands:

Horses. Many horses. And men's voices carrying on the night air in that clipped, confident way men's voices carry when they believe the night belongs to them.

Then Wren noticed the second thing, the worse thing.

Their own hands, resting in their lap, were shining. Not faintly anymore. Bright enough to read by. Bright enough that the moths had abandoned Emrys's dying fire entirely and were spiraling around Wren's fingers like the light was a flower and they were drunk on it.

And Wren was humming. They realized it with a lurch of horror — they were humming in their sleep, some song they'd never learned, and the song was in the many-edged tongue, and every note made their glow pulse brighter, and every pulse made the crickets go quiet for a mile around, like the whole countryside was turning its ears toward them.

*Stop,* Wren told themselves. *Stop it, stop, be quiet, be dark.*

The light flickered — and surged. Because here was the terrible joke of it, the thing nobody had told them: it fed on wanting. The harder Wren wished to hide, the brighter the magic rose to comfort them, wrapping them in gold like a mother wrapping a child in blankets, and the blankets were on fire, and the fire was visible from the ridgeline, probably, possibly, certainly—

"Wren." Emrys's voice, low and steady, right beside their ear. "Look at me. Not the sound. Me."

Wren looked. The giant's eyes were very dark and very calm, the way deep water is calm.

"Breathe slow. Think of something small. Something that doesn't matter at all."

"Nothing about me matters less than anything," Wren whispered, and was startled by their own bitterness, and the light guttered — just slightly — at the edge of it.

"There," Emrys said. "That. Keep that."

So Wren kept it. They kept the small bitter coal of *nobody ever tells me anything* and thought about Barley's opinion of rivers and the itch of the wool cloak and the way the giant counted their glances over the shoulder, and slowly, slowly, the glow sank beneath their skin like a coin dropped in deep water, until only the faintest shimmer remained along their knuckles, and then not even that.

The voices on the wind passed to the east. Faded. Went.

For a long time nobody moved. Then Emrys let out a breath that sounded like it had been held since the stream.

"They're hunting something," the giant murmured, mostly to themselves. "Riding the boundary at night, spread wide. That's not a patrol. That's a net." A pause. Wren watched the giant look down at their own hands — empty hands, hands that had done killing three days ago and were clean anyway, which was somehow the worst part — and then look at Wren, curled small under the grey cloak, one last ember of light fading from the tip of one finger like a firefly deciding against it.

"The witch said you carried something alive," Emrys said quietly. "She didn't say it sang."

"I didn't know," Wren said. Their voice came out very small. "I didn't know it did that. I'm sorry. I'll be dark, I promise, I'll—"

"Stop." The giant's hand hovered near Wren's shoulder, not quite touching, the way it always did, as if contact had to be earned in installments. "You don't apologize for it. You hear me? Whatever you are, you don't apologize for it. We just—" Emrys stopped. Started again. "We get you somewhere they can't reach. That's all. That's the whole plan."

"That's not a plan," Wren said. "That's a wish."

Something happened to the giant's mouth then that Wren had never seen before and would spend a long time trying to see again: it bent upward, at one corner, briefly, like a crack of dawn.

"Welcome to my entire life," Emrys said, and began kicking dirt over the coals.

But when Wren rolled back into their blanket, they kept their hands tucked underneath them, and they did not sing, and they listened — because now they knew how — to the giant sitting up through the rest of the dark hours, watching the southern ridgeline, where far away and getting closer, torches moved like a slow constellation falling toward the earth.
