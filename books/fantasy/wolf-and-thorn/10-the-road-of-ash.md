## 10. The Road of Ash

The Thorn Valley ended the way most mercies end: without ceremony.

One step, and the world went quiet in a new way — not the deep, magic-dead hush Emrys had lived inside for ten years, but something thinner, watchful. The air tasted of woodsmoke and wet iron. Wren's hand tightened in theirs, and when Emrys glanced down, the child was glowing.

Not much. A faint gold shimmer under the skin, like sunlight caught behind frosted glass, pooling at the wrists and throat. Enough to be seen from a hundred paces on an overcast day. Enough to be seen by anyone Vaelric paid to see it, which was everyone.

"Wren." Emrys crouched, keeping their voice low and even, the voice they'd used on spooked horses and greener soldiers than they liked to remember. "Remember what I said about lamps?"

Wren nodded solemnly. The child took everything Emrys said with the gravity of scripture, which would have been flattering if Emrys hadn't been making half of it up as they went.

"You're a lamp," Emrys said. "Right now you're a lamp with the shutter open."

"Shutter," Wren repeated, in accented Common, then again in the First Tongue, where it came out as something like *veil-of-the-eye*, which Emrys privately thought was better.

"Close the shutter. Tuck the light down small. Like hiding a coal under ash so it stays lit but no one sees it glow."

That was the trick of it — the thing Maren had told them at her crooked house on the border, pressing a bundle of dried herbs into Wren's hands and saying, *It's alive in there, Emrys. Alive things want to shine.* You couldn't smother it. You could only teach it manners.

Wren squeezed their eyes shut. Their little brow furrowed. The glow flickered, guttered, flared bright enough that Emrys's shadow jumped on the road — then, slowly, like a fist unclenching in reverse, it drew inward and dimmed to almost nothing. Almost.

"There," Emrys said. "Good. Nearly invisible."

"I am not nearly invisible," Wren said with enormous dignity. "I am *completely* invisible."

"You're completely visible to me."

"That is because you are looking for me."

"It's because," Emrys said, standing and taking the small hand again, "you're still leaking light out your ears like a kettle. But we'll work on it."

* * *

They walked south through country Emrys did not recognize, though they had marched through it once, ten years ago, when it had been fields.

Now it was ash. Not burned — conscripted. That was the difference Vaelric had taught the world: fire leaves ruins, but requisition leaves *absence*. Whole villages stood intact, doors swinging, hearths cold, every soul between fifteen and fifty gone into the usurper's levies. The old ones remained where they were too stubborn or too slow to leave, sitting in doorways like furniture nobody wanted, watching the road with eyes that had stopped hoping for anything to come up it except worse news.

The first village they passed through was called Hallowmere, according to a signpost someone had used for knife practice. A woman sat outside the last occupied house, shelling beans into a bowl that never seemed to fill. She watched them come and did not run, which told Emrys she'd already decided there was nothing left worth taking.

"You'll be wanting water," she said. It wasn't a question. It wasn't charity either. It was arithmetic: two travelers, one of them small, walking south. Water was cheaper than watching them die on her road.

"Please," Emrys said.

She fetched a jug and looked at Wren a long moment. "Yours?"

"For now."

"Hm." She set the jug down. "Mine went north in the spring levy. Both boys. The magistrate said it was an honor." Her voice was perfectly flat, the way ground goes flat after something heavy rolls over it. "He said it while his men ate our pig."

Emrys had heard this speech before, in a dozen villages, in three wars, wearing different colors. They knew all the correct responses. They gave none of them. Some griefs don't want company; they just want a witness who doesn't look away.

"The war hasn't started yet," the woman said, apropos of nothing. "Everyone says it will. But he's already taken everything a war takes. So either it started years ago and nobody told us, or—" she shrugged, and a bean escaped the bowl, and she didn't pick it up, "—or there's no such thing as before a war. Only during."

Emrys drank the water. It tasted of the well and of nothing else, and it was the best water they'd had in ten years.

At the edge of the village, Wren tugged their sleeve. "Why did the old mother give us water if her face is sad?"

"Because sad faces can still have hands."

Wren considered this. "In the First Tongue there are nine words for giving. One of them means *giving that costs the giver*. That is what she did."

"Yes," Emrys said quietly. "It is."

They walked on. Behind them the woman went back to her beans, and the bowl never filled, and somewhere north her sons were learning to carry spears for a man who called their taking an honor.

* * *

The second village had been emptied more recently, and less politely.

Ashburn, the charred sign said — or had said; the last word was only a suggestion now. Here the conscription had been resisted. Here the king's men — Vaelric called himself king now, though half the world hadn't agreed to it yet — had made an example. The granary was a black shell. Three gallows stood at the crossroads with the ropes cut short and empty, because the dead had been left hanging long enough and then dumped in a trench outside town, which the crows showed you the way to whether you asked or not.

An old man was nailing planks over a broken window. He worked slowly, and every nail got hit four times, and Emrys understood that the hammering was the point, not the window.

"They came at night," the man said, without being asked, hammering. "Forty of them. Took the grain, took the boys, hanged the miller because he locked his own gate against his own king." Hammer. Hammer. Hammer. "His own *king*." He spat with tremendous precision into the dust. "You going south?"

"We are."

"Corvane way?"

"Perhaps."

"Then tell them." He turned, and his face was ordinary and wrecked, a face from a thousand villages, and Emrys's chest did something complicated and unwelcome. "Tell them it's coming. Tell them he counts his army in our children. Tell them—" the hammer hand trembled, and he mastered it, "—tell them we held our gate for one hour. One hour. Write that down somewhere."

"I'll write it down," Emrys said, and meant it, and hated how little it weighed.

They camped that night in the shell of the granary, out of the wind. Emrys built a small fire, shielded by stones, and shared dried fish and hard bread with Wren, who ate with the focused ferocity of a child who remembered hunger in a way that suggested the burning lowlands had not been kind.

"Emrys." Wren's mouth was full. "What is Corvane?"

"A city. Big. Loud. Full of people selling things, including each other."

"Is it free? The man said free cities."

"It's free the way a cat is free. Nobody owns it, and it does exactly as it likes, and it will take your hand off if you're careless." Emrys fed a stick to the fire. "There are people there who might help us. And people who might sell us. Sometimes the same person."

Wren chewed thoughtfully. "Which kind are you?"

Emrys laughed — a short, startled sound, surprised out of them. "Ask me again tomorrow."

* * *

The lesson came on the third morning, in a birch wood that still believed in itself.

Emrys had been thinking about it since Hallowmere: the woman's sharp eyes, the way travelers read each other on roads these days. Wren's light was manageable in shadow, but shadows end, and markets happen at noon, and a child who glowed like banked coals was a child who got reported. So: school.

"Sit," Emrys said, and Wren sat cross-legged in the moss with the immediate obedience of someone who had apparently decided Emrys was some combination of parent, general, and weather. "Today we learn the second rule of hiding."

"What was the first rule?"

"Don't be seen."

"And the second?"

"Be boring." Emrys held up a hand before the indignation arrived. "Listen. Anyone hunting a relic expects a relic. They expect glow, song, wonder, a child who walks like a candle in a procession. So we don't give them that. We give them a tired traveler and a grubby kid who complains about their feet. Boring is armor. Boring is the best spell I know."

"But the light wants out."

"The light wants out," Emrys agreed. "So give it somewhere small to go. Don't push it down — that's like holding a door shut against a river. Instead..." They searched for the image. Ten years of silence had rusted the language of teaching. "You know how a bird folds its wings? Not fighting the wing. Folding it. Fold the light into your bones. Let it sleep there."

Wren closed their eyes. The glow stirred, flared—

—and then folded. Truly folded, this time, drawn down through skin and flesh into something deep and quiet, and for a moment the child sitting in the moss was just a child, muddy and thin and ordinary, and something in Emrys's chest ached at both the success and the necessity of it.

"I did it," Wren whispered. Their eyes flew open, shining with ordinary triumph. "I'm boring!"

"The boringest," Emrys confirmed gravely. "No one will ever look at you twice."

But Wren's face did something complicated then, the way children's faces do — joy sliding sideways into something older. "When the god wakes," Wren said carefully, "will I still need to fold?"

And there it was, the question underneath the lesson, the one Emrys had been stepping around for days. Because Wren talked about waking the god the way other children talked about spring — certain, simple, *when*, not *if* — and every time, Emrys felt the words land like stones in still water.

"We'll see," Emrys said, which was a coward's answer, delivered in a soldier's voice.

Wren accepted it the way children accept adult evasions: completely, and with the clear understanding that the real answer would be collected later, with interest.

* * *

They struck the main road on the fourth day, and the main road was where the war showed its teeth.

Convoys, first. Wagons groaning north under grain, iron, timber — the whole bleeding wealth of the countryside flowing toward Vaelric's citadel like blood toward a wound. Then the columns. Soldiers, mostly young, marching in the mud with the particular blankness of boys trying not to think. And then the ones Emrys couldn't look away from: the stragglers pressed into service without uniforms, roped in file, guarded by men with whips. Farmers. Smiths. A boy who couldn't have been fourteen, carrying a pike taller than himself, stumbling.

Wren watched them go past with wide, unblinking eyes. "Are those the bad men?"

"No," Emrys said. "Those are the stolen men. The bad men are the ones on horses at the front."

A rider did pass at the column's head — an officer in Vaelric's black-and-gold, and behind him two figures in gray that made Emrys's spine go cold and their hand drift, without any decision being made, toward the knife under their cloak. Inquisitors. Even at a distance, even hooded, you knew them: they rode the way owls fly, silent and wrong, and the peasants along the verge bowed their heads as they passed, quickly, like people ducking weather.

The officer's gaze swept the roadside — swept *them*, a beat, a bored flicker across a tired traveler and a grubby child — and moved on.

Boring, Emrys thought. Bless you, boring.

Only when the column had crawled over the hill did Emrys let their breath out. Wren was staring up at them.

"Your heart is loud," the child said. "I can hear it when I hold your hand. It was shouting."

"Was it."

"It was saying *run*."

"Your heart," Emrys said, "should mind its own business." But they shifted Wren to their hip and walked faster anyway, and neither of them mentioned it again.

That evening, in the lee of a fallen wall, Emrys scratched letters in the dirt with a stick while Wren watched. Names, mostly. Roads. Distances guessed at. And at the top, because a dying man at Ashburn had asked and Emrys kept their promises the way they kept knives — close, and always:

*Held the gate one hour.*

"What is that?" Wren asked.

"A message."

"For whom?"

"For whoever's still listening." Emrys wiped it away before the words could finish drying, because that was the whole art of survival now: hold the truth, carry the truth, and never let the truth touch the ground where it could be read. "Come on. Corvane by tomorrow night, if the road behaves."

"Will the road behave?"

"The road," Emrys said, shouldering the pack, "is the only thing between here and there that might."

* * *

They saw Corvane at dusk on the fifth day, and it was beautiful the way a wolf is beautiful: from a distance, in bad light, before it notices you.

The city sat astride its river with a thousand lamps kindling along walls and bridges, and smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, and bells ringing the evening hours in three competing tones because even the temples of a free city could not agree on anything. Beyond the walls, refugee camps clung to the ditches like barnacles — the price of freedom, charged in advance, collected from the desperate.

Wren glowed.

Just barely — the folding held, mostly — but the city ahead was full of lamps and lanterns and things that noticed light, and as the dark deepened around them, the child's skin answered it, a faint gold answering a thousand fires, like recognizing like.

"Fold deeper," Emrys murmured.

"I'm trying. There is a lot of light here. It keeps... talking to mine."

"Tell yours we're not receiving."

Wren giggled — actually giggled, there on the ash road with refugee fires smoking in the ditches — and Emrys found, to their considerable irritation, that they were smiling too. Ten years of not smiling, and this child had cracked it in five days. There was probably a lesson in that. Emrys declined to learn it.

Ahead, the South Gate stood open in the wall, torchlit and crowded, guarded by city militia in mismatched armor — free men, paid badly, watching everyone. And leaning against the gatehouse pillar, arms crossed, watching the road with professional patience, stood a figure Emrys's body recognized a full three seconds before their mind consent to.

Broad shoulders. Cropped silver hair. A scar along the jaw that Emrys had watched get earned, eleven years ago, on a field that no longer existed.

Sefa.

Here. Of all the cities, all the gates, all the nights — here, watching this road, tonight.

Every sensible instinct Emrys owned said *turn around, find another gate, walk another week*. But the camps were behind them, the road was dark, Wren's light was talking to the lamplight, and there was no other way in before full dark.

"Emrys?" Wren whispered. "Who is that?"

"Someone I used to know," Emrys said, and put their hand on the child's shoulder, and walked toward the gate, and toward the woman who knew their face, their name, their history — and who sold things for a living.

"*Someone I used to know,*" Wren repeated softly, unconvinced. "Your heart is shouting again."

"Quiet," Emrys muttered. "So is mine."
