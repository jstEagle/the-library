## 11. An Old Comrade's Knife

Corvane smelled like fish and money in equal measure, and Emrys had never decided which was worse.

They came down off the pilgrims' bridge at midmorning, when the fog still hung thick enough to hide a face, with Wren's hood pulled low and one of Emrys's hands resting on the child's shoulder like an anchor. The city rose around them in tiers—tile roofs the color of rusted copper, canals cutting silver scars between the streets, laundry strung from window to window like bunting for a festival no one had announced. Somewhere below, a bell was ringing the hour. Somewhere else, someone was cheating someone at dice. Corvane did both at once, always.

"Big," Wren said. It was one of the few words they'd surrendered to the common tongue without a fight. They used it for everything: the sea, the harbor cranes, a particularly ambitious loaf of bread. Emrys had begun to suspect it was less a word than a philosophy.

"It's loud," Emrys said. "Stay close. Don't sing."

"I don't sing."

"You hummed yesterday. You hummed the litany of the third watch." Emrys kept their voice light, but their eyes were working the crowd—the dockhands, the fishwives, the two men leaning against a spice stall who were standing like soldiers pretending not to be soldiers. "You know what happens if you hum the litany of the third watch in a city where half the people still remember it."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a small magistrate. "People cry?"

"People *look*," Emrys said. "And looking is how it starts. Someone looks, then someone asks, then someone sells you to someone who pays in gold instead of questions." They steered the child left, away from the soldiers-not-soldiers, down a stair cut into the canal bank. "So. No humming."

Wren hummed something else instead—three notes, defiant, tuneless. A compromise. Emrys let it go. There was a version of themself, ten years younger, who would have laughed at that. They missed that person the way you miss a tooth: mostly at mealtimes, mostly in silence.

* * *

Sefa Ironmark kept her shop above a dyer's yard on Saltwife Row, which meant the whole building smelled permanently of mordant and old wool, which meant, Sefa had once explained over far too much wine, that nobody ever wanted to linger long enough to ask inconvenient questions. It had been a joke then. Emrys suspected it was policy now.

The door at the top of the stairs had no sign, only three iron nails driven into the wood in a triangle. Emrys stood before it for a long moment, longer than any lockpick had ever made them stand, and felt Wren's small hand find theirs.

"You're scared," Wren observed.

"I'm deciding."

"That's scared with extra steps."

Emrys looked down. The child gazed back up with those unsettling, unchildlike gray eyes, utterly serene, entirely correct.

"Ten years ago I would have kicked this door in," Emrys said.

"And now?"

"Now I knock." They knocked.

It took a while. Long enough that Emrys counted the boats passing on the canal below—seven—and was starting to compose a retreat when the door opened on a chain and one eye appeared in the gap. Brown, sharp, ringed with kohl that had been applied by someone in a hurry or someone drunk. Probably both.

"We're closed," the eye said.

"Sefa."

The eye did not blink. "I know that voice," it said slowly, "and I have spent eleven years hoping it was dead, because if it wasn't dead, it would eventually show up on my step, and look at that. Look at *that*." The chain rattled off. The door swung wide. "Get in before the whole row sees you. And who in all the hells is this?"

"This is Wren."

Wren looked up at Sefa Ironmark—all five foot ten of her, scarred knuckles, cropped iron-gray hair, a knife riding each hip out of pure habit—and delivered their verdict with the flat certainty of a child reading weather. "She's sad."

"*Charming,*" Sefa said. "Does it do anything besides insult me?"

"They speak the First Tongue," Emrys said quietly, and watched eleven years fall off Sefa's face and then pile right back on, doubled.

The shop beyond the door was exactly as remembered: shelves of paper, jars of ink, ledgers stacked like fortifications, and in the corner a cot where Sefa clearly slept because comfort was for people who hadn't burned a bridge behind them. She poured three cups of tea without asking, which was its own kind of welcome, and sat across from Emrys with her cup untouched and her eyes moving from Emrys to Wren and back like a woman counting coins she didn't trust.

"Eleven years," she said. "Not ten. Eleven, because you didn't write after the first year either, so really we should count from the battle, which makes it twelve, and now I'm angry again. Sit down properly, Emrys, you're looming."

"I'm sitting."

"You sit like you're about to leave. You always did." She finally drank her tea. "Word was you crawled into Thorn Valley and died there. Word was very specific about it. I may have spread some of that word myself, come to think of it. Helped you along."

"I know. Thank you."

"Don't thank me, it ruins it." She set the cup down hard enough to rattle. "Say the thing you came to say. Or better, say nothing, drink your tea, and take the child and go somewhere I'm not, because I can see it in your face, Emrys. I've seen that face before. It's the face you wore the day before the Godswar went bad."

Emrys wrapped both hands around the cup. Warmth, at least. Small mercies. "Vaelric's inquisitors are in the valley. They found us. We killed three of them."

"Killed," Sefa repeated. "Past tense. So they'll be missed."

"They'll be reported."

"So it's a matter of weeks, then. Days if Vaelric's feeling thorough." She rubbed her jaw. "What are they calling the relic?"

"A stolen relic. Their words."

"Mm." Sefa looked at Wren again, and this time she looked properly, the way she'd once looked at battlefield maps—with a professional's hunger and a soldier's dread fighting openly behind her eyes. "And what does the child actually carry? Because I've heard things, Emrys. Bad things, whispered things, the kind of things I get paid to listen to and pretend not to hear. Things about a god under a mountain. Things about bloodlines and seals. And every one of those whispers has a price tag hanging off it that would buy this whole street."

"Sefa."

"I'm just describing the market," she said. "I haven't done anything yet."

That was the worst part. She hadn't. Not yet.

* * *

They talked until the light through the dyer's windows turned amber, and it was almost good, that was the trap of it. Sefa told the story of how she'd taken a spear through the thigh at Redford Ford and been carried out of the war on a hay cart, cursing, and woken up in Corvane with a limp and a talent for knowing things. Emrys told her about Maren's goats. Wren fell asleep against Emrys's side halfway through the goat story, which everyone pretended not to notice and everyone noticed.

For an hour it was the old company again. For an hour Emrys could smell woodsmoke and rain on canvas and hear Halric laughing too loud at his own jokes, and it was so easy, so *warm*, that Emrys nearly forgot what Sefa did for a living now. Nearly forgot that information was her trade the way killing had been theirs, and that a person doesn't stop being what fed them.

It was Wren's sleeping weight against their ribs that brought it all back.

"Sefa," Emrys said. "I need a way south. Out of Corvane before the week turns. Quiet roads, quiet boats, someone who doesn't ask."

"I know someone."

"I need them cheap."

"You need them *free*," Sefa translated, "because you're broke, because you're Emrys." She sighed, and something in the sigh had a hinge in it, a door swinging. "I can do it. I'll even do it tonight. But—" and there it was, the other shoe, dropping in slow motion, Emrys watching it fall the whole way down— "you have to tell me what the child is. All of it. Not for the coin. For me. Because I've spent twelve years telling myself we lost that war because our god abandoned us, and if that's wrong, Emrys, if it's something worse, I need to know it before I put my neck on your road."

Emrys looked at her for a long time.

Every instinct honed across two wars said: lie. Lie the way you lied to the inquisitors, smooth and quick and clean. But Sefa had held the shield-wall beside them for six years. Sefa had dragged Halric's body two miles through mud because Emrys couldn't carry him alone. Some lies cost more than truth.

"The god didn't abandon us," Emrys said. "The god was taken. Sealed. Imprisoned by Vaelric's line, and everything since—the silence, the losing, all of it—was built on that theft." They nodded down at Wren. "And this child is the last living piece of Ossa. That's the relic. That's what the inquisitors want. That's why we're running."

Sefa was silent for a long moment. Outside, a boat horn sounded; somewhere a woman shouted cheerfully at a man about eels.

"Well," Sefa said at last, hoarse. "That's worse."

"Yes."

"That's so much worse, Emrys. Do you understand what that means? If it's true—if people believe it's true—there's no more peace to keep anywhere. There's faithful in every city waiting for exactly that sentence. There's Vaelric with four legions and a claim that dies the moment that child opens their mouth in front of the right crowd." She stood up abruptly and walked to the window, arms crossed, back rigid. "You're not carrying a relic. You're carrying a war in a hood."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Because here's my problem, old friend. Here's my honest problem. I have a price list. Everyone in my business has a price list, it's how we stay alive. And there is a name on my list—a very patient, very well-funded name—who pays fifty times what your road costs for exactly the thing sleeping against your ribs right now. And I am looking at that child, and I am thinking: fifty times. And I hate myself for thinking it, but thinking isn't doing, Emrys, that's the whole point of thinking, you do it so you don't have to be surprised by yourself later—"

"Sefa."

"—and I need you to give me a reason," she finished, voice cracking on it, "a reason that weighs more than fifty times, because right now I've got nothing but twelve years of missing you and that's worth maybe ten."

The room was very quiet. Wren slept on, breathing soft and even, glowing—not visibly, not yet, but Emrys could feel it now, a warmth like a coal wrapped in cloth, the god's last ember keeping itself warm against a mortal chest.

Emrys drew their knife.

Sefa didn't flinch—that was the old training in both of them—but her hand drifted to her own hip and stopped, deliberately short of the grip, a choice made visible.

Emrys laid the knife on the table between them. Flat. Handle toward Sefa.

"There's my reason," they said. "If you sell them, I won't hunt you. I want you to understand that. I'll be dead inside a month anyway—inquisitors, legions, whatever comes—and hunting you would waste the time I have left. So here's the deal, Sefa. If you're going to betray me, do it now, while I'm sitting here, so I can watch you decide. Take the knife if you need it. I won't stop you. I'd deserve it—I taught you everything you know about picking your moment."

"That's a horrible bargain."

"It's the only honest one I have left."

They stared at each other across the table, across twelve years, across the knife lying between them like a border drawn in steel. Sefa's jaw worked. Her hand hovered near her hip, near the table, near nothing. Outside the window the light was failing, and somewhere down on Saltwife Row a lamplighter was making his rounds, and the whole world seemed to hold its breath around the two of them and the sleeping fragment of a god.

"You always did fight dirty," Sefa said finally, and her voice was wrecked. "Putting it *there*. Where I have to *look* at it."

"You asked for a reason that weighed."

"I asked for a *good* reason."

"Same thing, most days."

Sefa laughed—one broken note, nothing like the old laugh—and pressed both palms flat against the table as if steadying herself against a swell. She looked at the knife. She looked at Wren. She looked at Emrys, and something moved behind her eyes like weather changing, though which way it would break, Emrys genuinely could not read.

"Get your things," Sefa said. "Both of you. Now. Tonight, before I change my mind or someone changes it for me."

Emrys reached for the knife—

"Leave it," Sefa said sharply. "Leave it right there. I'm not done deciding."

And Emrys, who had faced down cavalry charges and burning temples and ten years of silence, picked up the sleeping child very carefully and stepped back from the table, and understood that the most dangerous ground they had crossed in twelve years lay between that knife and that door—and that they had not yet, by a long measure, reached the far side of it.
