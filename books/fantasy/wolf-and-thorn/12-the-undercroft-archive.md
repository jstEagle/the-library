## 12. The Undercroft Archive

Maren's clue had come wrapped in a loaf of black bread, which was very like her. She never said anything plainly when she could bake it into something instead.

*The undercroft of the Chantry of Saint Vessin, Corvane,* the note read, inked in her cramped, spider-legged hand. *Third stair down, count the steps wrong on purpose. Ask the door what it is afraid of. Bring bread for the rats; they remember kindness better than archivists do.*

And then, beneath that, smaller, as though she'd hesitated before writing it: *You were lied to, Emrys. We all were.*

Emrys had sat with that note for two days while Wren demolished the loaf around it, crumbs flying like confetti at a funeral.

"Rats," Wren had said, in trade-tongue now—improving by the week, though their accent turned every word into something slightly musical, slightly wrong. "She wants you to feed rats."

"She wants me to bribe them."

"That's worse."

"It's Corvane," Emrys had said. "Everything's a bribe here. At least the rats are honest about it."

Now, three nights later, standing in the shadowed mouth of an alley across from the Chantry of Saint Vessin, Emrys missed the valley so fiercely it felt like a physical ache. In the Thorn Valley, nothing worked—not magic, not gods, not the small creeping horrors that lived in the corners of the world elsewhere. Here, everything worked. Which meant everything could be aimed at you.

Wren crouched beside them in the muck, hood up, gloved to the wrists. The gloves had been Maren's idea, and Maren's making: woven through with valley-thorn fiber, dull and gray as old rope. They didn't stop the glow entirely. But they slowed it, smothered it, turned a beacon into a candle cupped behind shut fingers.

"You don't have to come," Emrys said, for the fourth time.

"I know." Wren picked at a loose thread on their cuff. "You keep saying it like I'll change my mind if you say it enough. Sefa said that too. About things she wanted to do alone." A pause. "She didn't do them alone, did she?"

Emrys's chest did something complicated. "No. And it got her killed."

"So maybe doing things alone gets people killed slower." Wren shrugged, a child's shrug, boneless and absolute. "I'm coming."

Emrys opened their mouth to argue—and closed it again, because there was no argument left. There hadn't been since the ridgeline, since the lie told to the inquisitor with the kind eyes, since the first blood in ten years spilled red on the cottage floor. The choice had been made a long time ago. Everything since was just walking.

"All right," they said. "But you stay behind me, and if I say *run*, you run, and if I say *hide*, you hide, and if I say—"

"*Jump off a bridge,* you jump off a bridge. You've said." Wren grinned up at them, gap-toothed, utterly unrepentant. "I'm not going to jump off a bridge."

"One day you will, and you'll feel very foolish about all this."

"I already feel foolish. It doesn't help."

Emrys huffed something that was almost a laugh, checked the street one last time—the lamplighters had passed an hour ago; the watch wouldn't circle again until the bell of terce—and moved.

* * *

Getting into the chantry was insultingly easy. That should have been the first warning.

The Chantry of Saint Vessin was one of those buildings Corvane produced the way other cities produced beggars: abundantly, and without anyone quite intending to. It had been a granary once, then a shrine, then a chantry, then half-abandoned when the diocese ran out of money or interest or both. Its doors stood open to vagrants and drunks and the occasional pious widow, and its undercroft—

Its undercroft was another matter entirely.

Emrys found the third stair down behind the vestry, exactly where Maren's note promised, and counted the steps wrong on purpose, which meant counting them right: fourteen down instead of the thirteen the staircase actually held. On the fourteenth step—a step that wasn't there, a step made only of intention—the air went thick as tallow.

There was a door where no door should be. Old iron, older wood, unmarked by hinge or handle.

*Ask the door what it is afraid of,* Maren had written.

Emrys stared at it. "This is absurd," they muttered.

Behind them, Wren whispered, "What's absurd?"

"The door. Maren says to ask it what it's afraid of."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a scholar. Then they stepped forward, past Emrys's restraining arm, and pressed one gloved palm flat against the iron.

In the First Tongue—soft, wondering—they asked it.

The door shuddered.

Not opened. *Shuddered*, like a horse hearing a wolf, and the thick air curdled, and somewhere far above them a candle guttered though no wind reached the stairs. And the lock, which had waited perhaps three hundred years for someone who spoke the language it had been warded in, clicked open with a sound like a sigh.

Emrys looked down at the child. The child looked up at Emrys.

"I didn't do anything," Wren said.

"No. Of course not. Doors just fall in love with you everywhere we go."

"Is that strange?"

"Wren, I have watched you befriend a guard dog, a toll collector, and a horse that bit everyone else it met. Yes. It's strange. Come on."

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and vellum and cold stone, and underneath it all—faint, so faint Emrys might have imagined it—a scent they knew from a lifetime ago. Incense. Myrrh and ash. The smell of Ossa's chapels.

Their heart stumbled. They kept walking.

* * *

The undercroft archive was a vault of shelves carved directly into bedrock, and it was organized according to some system comprehensible only to a madman or a genius, possibly both. Scrolls lay in niches labeled in dead scripts. Codices rested spine-out in racks of blackened oak. And along the far wall, behind a grate of iron bars, behind a second locked gate that did not shudder for Wren because apparently even miracles had limits, sat the restricted collection.

Emrys spent two hours among the open shelves first, and learned three things.

First: that the Chantry of Saint Vessin had been, in its forgotten middle life, a repository for records the Church of the Nine wanted lost but couldn't quite bring themselves to burn. Archivists were like that. Hoarders of embarrassment.

Second: that Vaelric's name appeared in documents older than Vaelric. *Vaelric* as a family name. *House Vaelric*, stretching back generations, always near the centers of power, always just offstage of the great events. Emrys had known the usurper claimed an old bloodline. They hadn't known how old, or how consistently the bloodline surfaced wherever the records grew thin—as though someone had gone through history with a knife, scraping.

Third: that they were not the first person recently to go looking. Someone had left a lamp stub, fresh tallow, less than a season old, tucked into a niche near the restricted grate. Someone else wanted these answers too.

That thought kept company with them while they worked the barred gate—slowly, with picks, because Wren's door-charming had limits and Emrys refused to find out what happened if a warded archive screamed for help. The picks were old friends. Ten years of quiet hadn't rusted that particular skill; a mercenary's hands remembered the way a scarred dog remembers cruelty.

"Tell me again why we're stealing books," Wren whispered. They'd made a nest of fallen scrolls in the corner and were watching Emrys work with their chin on their knees.

"Not stealing. Borrowing without permission."

"What's the difference?"

"Stealing means we'd sell them."

"We're not going to sell them?"

"I'm going to *read* them. There's a difference."

"There isn't."

"There is," Emrys said, and felt the pick catch, turn, and the lock give, "and it's the difference between me being a thief and me being a desperate fool, and I'd rather be a desperate fool. Hold the lamp."

The restricted collection was thinner than the open shelves. Thinner, older, and stranger. Emrys pulled volumes by feel and lamplight: a genealogy of houses long extinct; a martyrology with pages razored out; and then, wedged flat at the bottom of a rack as though someone had hoped the shelf itself would forget it, a codex bound in green leather gone stiff as bark, its title stamped in letters worn nearly smooth:

*ON THE SILENCING OF VOICES: AN ACCOUNT COMPILED FOR THE COUNCIL OF THE NINE, IN THE YEAR OF THE FIRST USURPATION.*

Emrys's hands had gone still on the cover. Beside them, Wren had gone still too—still in that particular way, the way they went still whenever something of the god came close. Like a deer hearing a sound too low for human ears.

"Read it," Wren whispered. Not *read it aloud*. Just: *read it.* As if they already knew.

Emrys opened the codex, and the decade cracked down its length.

* * *

The account was written by a chantry scribe whose name history had not bothered to keep, in the careful, terrified hand of a man documenting a crime he could not prevent and dared not name aloud. He wrote around it. He wrote in circles. But circles, Emrys discovered, can enclose a truth as neatly as any statement, and the truth inside this one was simple, and enormous, and had been waiting eleven years to gut them.

Ossa had not gone silent.

Ossa had been silenced.

The year after the war began—the war Emrys had bled in, the war that broke the old kingdoms and crowned the first usurper—a delegation had gone from the new court to the high places where Ossa's voice was strongest. The scribe recorded their departure with courtly blandness: *the Lord Protector's brother, certain clerics of the reform party, and men-at-arms sufficient to the journey.* He recorded their return with less blandness and more terror, in passages the censor had missed because they were written as scripture commentary, interleaved with verses, hidden in exegesis the way Maren hid warnings in bread.

They had gone down, the commentary said, into the god's own ground. Into the deep places where the Voice rose like water from a wellspring. And there they had done *a work of binding*, using rites the scribe described only in fragments—chains of consecrated iron, a seal cut in living stone, a circle of names renounced. *For a voice may be stopped,* he wrote, *not only by killing the speaker, but by building a wall around the speaking place. And a god's voice has a place, as a river has a spring, though fools believe otherwise.*

He named the rite's architects obliquely, in the fashion of a frightened man: *the brothers of the Protector's house, who took upon themselves the burden of the silence.* The House that would become House Vaelric. The bloodline that had held the seal ever since—generation after generation, keepers of the prison, feeding on the legitimacy of a throne built over a chained god.

*And the faithful scattered,* the scribe wrote, in the last passage the censor's knife had spared, *hearing only absence where their god had been, believing themselves abandoned. This, I think, was the cruelest part of the work. Not the chains. The letting them believe.*

Emrys set the codex down very carefully on the rack, the way one sets down something that might explode, and found that their hands were shaking anyway.

Eleven years.

Eleven years in the Thorn Valley, in the magic-dead quiet, telling themselves the story night after night until it wore grooves in them like water wears stone: *Ossa left. Ossa fell silent mid-battle, mid-prayer, mid-scream, and never spoke again, and whatever I did—or failed to do—it was enough that the god gave up on me.* Eleven years of guilt dressed up as grief, grief dressed up as exile, exile dressed up as peace. They had built their whole hollow life on top of an abandonment that had never happened.

The god hadn't abandoned them.

The god had been screaming into a sealed room the whole time.

"Oh," Emrys said aloud. It came out very small.

Beside them, Wren touched their sleeve. When Emrys looked down, the child's eyes were wet, and wide, and terribly old.

"Now you know," Wren said. "Why I ran. Why they burned—" They stopped. Started again, in the First Tongue, words falling out soft and broken, and Emrys understood maybe one in three, but the shape of it needed no translation. *They put Him away and they are looking for the pieces and I am a piece and I ran and ran and ran.*

"How much do you remember?" Emrys asked quietly. "Of before. Of the sealing."

Wren's face did something complicated—closed like a fist, opened like a wound. "I remember—" they began.

And stopped.

Because every lamp in the undercroft guttered at once, and above them, faint through three floors of stone, a bell began to ring—not the hour-bell, not the watch-bell, but the chantry's alarm, cracked and urgent, and beneath it came the sound Emrys had hoped never to hear again so soon: boots. Many boots. On the stairs that led down to a door that should have stayed shut.

Wren grabbed Emrys's hand. Their grip was burning hot through the thorn-fiber glove.

"They followed us," the child breathed. "Or they were already here. The ones who left the lamp—"

Emrys snuffed the light with a bare palm, hissed against the sting, and pulled Wren back into the deep shadow between the shelves, heart slamming, mind racing through exits that weren't there. One way in. One way out. Fourteen steps and a door that loved a child, and between them and that door, descending now through the dark, voices—one of them lifted in a cadence Emrys knew from the worst years of their life.

An inquisitor's cadence. Reading a rite.

And the rite, as far as Emrys could tell, was one for opening things that were meant to stay closed.
