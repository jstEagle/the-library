## 13. What Wren Remembers

Wren had learned that the giant's face did many things.

There was the face for when Wren tracked mud across the floor (thunder-brows, but soft eyes). There was the face for when Wren ate the last of the dried plums without asking (a long sigh, like wind through a hollow reed, and then a second helping appeared anyway). There was the face for when Wren sang in their sleep and the candle flames leaned toward the bed like flowers turning to sun — that face was the hardest one. It went very still, the way a rabbit goes still when it hears something in the grass.

Tonight, sitting on the floor of the cottage with a blanket around their shoulders because Maren said cold made the light worse, Wren decided it was time to use words properly. Big words. True ones.

The giant — Emrys, Emrys, the name fit in the mouth like a smooth stone — was sharpening a knife by the fire. They were always sharpening a knife. Wren suspected some of the sharpening was just an excuse not to talk.

"I am going to tell you a thing," Wren announced, in the First Tongue, because the true things only fit in that language. The other one — the trade-tongue, all its corners and clatter — felt like trying to carry water in a net.

Emrys's hands stopped moving.

"The whole thing?" they asked carefully. "Or a piece of it?"

"The whole thing." Wren pulled the blanket tighter. "You will want to sit down. Maren says when people hear big things, their legs forget what they are for."

A sound came out of Emrys that might have been a laugh if it had been left in the oven longer. They set down the knife and sat.

* * *

Wren began at the beginning, which was also, as far as Wren could tell, the end of everything else.

"Before I was me, I was part of Him."

The fire popped. Emrys did not move.

"You know Him," Wren said. "I have seen you know Him. When I speak His tongue, your face does the rabbit-thing." They frowned, searching for better words. This was the trouble with being small and new: all the biggest ideas had to squeeze through a very little door. "He is old. Older than the kingdoms you have names for. He used to be everywhere — in the roots, in the rain, in the space between one breath and the next. People sang to Him and He sang back. That was how it worked. Song out, song home."

"And then?"

"And then the man with the iron crown." Wren's hands curled in the blanket. The memory didn't come as pictures, exactly. It came as weather. Pressure. A great weight pressing down and down. "His bloodline made a prison. Not a cage with bars — a cage made of *silence*. They took Him and they folded Him small and they buried Him under their citadel, and they built their whole kingdom on top like a cork in a bottle. And every king after kept the cork pressed down. Because a god under your floor makes a fine foundation, if you don't mind the screaming."

Emrys made a small sound, low in the chest.

"When they sealed Him," Wren went on, "the ones who loved Him knew what would happen. A god cannot stay whole in a hole. He would go... crumbly. Like bread left out. So the faithful did a desperate thing." Wren touched their own chest, right over the place where the light lived. "They took the last unbroken piece of Him and they hid it. In flesh. In a baby. In *me*."

The silence stretched so long that Wren began counting the knots in the blanket to keep from fidgeting. Eleven. Twelve.

"Say it again," Emrys said at last, very quietly.

"I am not a child *of* Ossa." Wren pronounced each word slowly, the way you set down eggs. "I am a piece *of* Ossa. The last one. The rest of Him is under Vaelric's citadel, asleep and folded up small, and I am the bit that got away."

* * *

Emrys stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there looking at nothing, shoulders rising and falling in a way that Wren had learned meant *big feelings being carried somewhere private.*

Wren waited. Waiting was a skill. Wren had gotten very good at waiting, hiding in ditches, holding breath, being small and quiet while soldiers passed. But this kind of waiting was different. This kind had hope in it, and hope was heavier than fear. Fear just wanted you to run. Hope wanted you to *stay*.

"You were at the battle," Wren said softly. It wasn't a question. "The day He went quiet. You heard the silence start."

"Yes." Emrys still faced the window. "Mid-swing. One moment He was there, in my bones, telling me where the next blow would fall. And then—" Their hand opened and closed at their side. "Nothing. Ten years of nothing. I thought He'd abandoned us. I thought we'd failed some test, or He'd simply grown bored of his little soldiers." A rough exhale. "And the whole time—"

"He was calling," Wren said. "All this time. You couldn't hear Him because He was buried. But He never stopped." Wren hugged their knees. "That's why I found you, I think. Or why you found me. A piece knows its own. When I crossed the valley line and the magic came back into me, I dreamed of a wolf standing over a thornbush, guarding it. I think the wolf was you."

"Thorns don't need guarding," Emrys muttered. "Thorns do fine on their own."

"This one did," Wren said, "because it was growing around something that hurt."

Emrys turned then, and their face did a new thing — something cracked-open and raw, the thunder-brows gone entirely. They crossed the room and crouched down to Wren's level, which they almost never did, and looked at them the way people look at fires they're afraid to touch.

"How much of you is Him?" they asked. "Do you know? Are you — a child who carries a piece? Or are you the piece, wearing a child?"

Wren considered this honestly, tilting their head.

"Yes," they said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." Wren spread their hands. "When I dream, sometimes I am small and running through burning fields, and sometimes I am vast and folded in the dark under stones, and both dreams are mine. I remember being a baby in a basket on a riverbank. I also remember the world before rivers had names. Both are true. I don't know which one is *me*." They shrugged, small under the blanket. "Maybe that's what happens when gods get sewn into people. The seams show on the inside."

Something wet caught the firelight on Emrys's cheek. They wiped it away fast, like it was evidence.

"They gave you to a stranger's arms," Emrys said hoarsely. "A baby. They put a god in a *baby* and sent it running through a war."

"There wasn't anyone else," Wren said simply. "Everyone who could hold a piece of Him was dead or hunted. The priestess who carried me — she was old, and her heart was bad, and she made it as far as the lowlands before it stopped. She told me — she told the *baby*, I suppose, but I heard — 'Run to the valley that forgets. Find the one the god still holds. Even silent, He holds them.'" Wren smiled a little. "She said it in the First Tongue. That's why I speak it. It was the last thing anyone sang over me."

Emrys sat down fully on the floor then, cross-legged, like their legs had finally taken Maren's advice. For a long time they just breathed.

"I swore to Him," they said finally. "Ten years ago. Blood-oath, full liturgy, the whole foolish ceremony. And when He went quiet, I decided the oath died with His voice. I came here to bury it." They looked at Wren, and their eyes were terrible and bright. "But oaths to gods don't die, do they. They just wait."

"I don't know," Wren admitted. "I'm new. At being a person, I mean. I've been alive longer than that, but only *awake* since the riverbank." They scooted closer, blanket and all, until they were nearly touching Emrys's knee. "Can I ask you a thing? A real thing?"

"Anything."

"When you find out what I am — what I'm *for* — will you look at me different? Will I stop being Wren and start being a relic?" The question had been living in Wren's chest for days, sharp-cornered, and it hurt coming out. "Because the inquisitors already look at me like a thing. A stolen relic, they said. Things can be picked up. Things can be put down. Things can be *broken on purpose so no one else gets them.*" Wren's voice wobbled and they hated it and pushed on anyway. "I'd rather be a person who frightens you than a thing that comforts you. Do you understand?"

Emrys reached out — slow, the way you move near a wild bird — and rested one scarred hand on top of Wren's head.

"Wren," they said. "You ate my plums, tracked mud on my floor, and called my knife-sharpening 'angry knitting.' No power in this world or under it could make me mistake you for a relic."

"Angry knitting is accurate," Wren said, sniffing.

"It is extremely accurate." But the hand stayed where it was, warm and heavy, and Wren let their eyes close under it, just for a moment, just to feel what it was like to be guarded instead of hidden.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to orange bones and Emrys lay breathing slow on their cot, Wren sat awake and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding.

If Wren was a piece of Ossa, then pieces could be returned. That was surely the point of a piece — that was what pieces were *for*. Somewhere south, under a usurper's citadel, the rest of Him lay folded in the dark, and every year He grew more crumbly, more scattered, more forgotten. And here was the missing bit, wrapped in a blanket, eating plums, learning to whistle.

It fit together so neatly it was almost funny. Almost.

Because Wren had also figured out — small person, big ears, lots of listening at doors — what returning a piece might cost. Maren's voice, low and urgent through the shutter: *a fragment that size, given back whole, the vessel doesn't survive it, child. Whatever's left afterward isn't the child anymore.*

Wren looked at their own hands in the dark. Small hands. Chapped from the road. Good at whistling, bad at braiding, recently taught to skip stones by a giant with thunder-brows who pretended not to smile.

Was being remembered worth dying for?

They didn't know yet. But they were starting to suspect they'd have to decide soon — because far off past the ridge, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Wren could feel the other pieces of Him pulling. And pulling. And pulling.

Outside, the wind changed direction, carrying something on it that made the light under Wren's skin flicker awake: hoofbeats. Many. Coming down the valley road.

Wren slid off the bed and padded to Emrys's cot and shook the giant's shoulder, whispering in the oldest language in the world:

"Wake up. Someone's coming, and I don't think they brought plums."
