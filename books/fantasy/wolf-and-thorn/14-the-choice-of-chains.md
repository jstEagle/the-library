## 14. The Choice of Chains

The hoofbeats turned out to be a wool merchant's caravan, six carts and a nervous boy driving an ox named Duchess, and by the time they'd passed, Wren had extracted a plum from the boy through sheer force of staring. Emrys watched from behind a hedgerow with a hand on their knife until the last cart creaked out of sight, then sat down heavily in the wet grass and did not get up again for a long while.

"You can come out," they said finally.

Wren emerged from the hedge with twigs in their hair and the plum already half-eaten. "I knew it was wool."

"You hid anyway."

"Hiding's free." Wren shrugged, which was the kind of wisdom that would have sounded less devastating from anyone taller than a fencepost. They plopped down next to Emrys and offered the plum.

Emrys took it. It was overripe and bruised on one side, and it was the best thing they'd eaten in a week, and they hated how much that was true.

They were three days from Corvane now, camped in the green shoulder of country between the ash road and the river, close enough to see its watchfires smudge the evening sky like a bruise healing wrong. Maren's clues were folded inside Emrys's coat, copied out in her cramped, furious handwriting: the undercroft beneath the Archive of Saint Verane, the seal that opened for no key, the door that remembered names. And beyond that — if the archive said what Maren thought it said — the faithful. The scattered remnant of Ossa's people, hidden in the free cities like embers carried in cupped hands.

Emrys had spent ten years believing those hands were empty. That everyone who had ever knelt to the silent god had either died or quietly gotten up, dusted off their knees, and gone back to farming. The idea that some of them were still kneeling — still waiting — was a thing Emrys didn't know how to hold yet. It kept slipping. Like trying to carry water in a fist.

Beside them, Wren hummed. Not a tune. A thread of the First Tongue, low and content, the way other children hummed while they ate. The light under their skin pulsed gently with each note, gold-green, firefly-soft.

"Stop that," Emrys said, without heat.

"Why?"

"Because the last time you sang in your sleep, a shepherd two fields over crossed himself and told his wife the dead were walking." This was true. It had also been funny, in the way that most things involving Wren were funny right up until they weren't.

Wren stopped humming. Then started again, quieter, out of what Emrys suspected was spite.

Emrys let them. There were worse sounds in the world than a god humming with its mouth full of plum.

* * *

That night Emrys could not sleep, and so did what soldiers had done since the first soldier: cleaned weapons that were already clean, counted supplies that hadn't changed since noon, and thought about murder.

Not in the abstract. In the specific. In the very specific.

There was a way this ended well for almost everyone, and Emrys had been circling it for days the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. Wren was the last fragment of a god. Vaelric wanted that fragment because whoever held it could claim — or break — the divine right his whole usurped throne stood on. If there was no fragment, there was nothing to claim. No relic for the inquisitors to chase. No beacon glowing through bedrolls and rain-slick streets. Just a mute child and a mercenary nobody remembered, walking out of history the way they'd walked into it.

One knife. One moment. Wren wouldn't even understand — couldn't understand, maybe, not fully, though Emrys suspected with a sick rolling feeling that the child understood far more than they ever said. Wren had looked at Emrys across the fire two nights ago and asked, in that flat, terrible, childlike way, *"If I stop, does He die all the way?"* And Emrys had lied and said they didn't know.

Emrys knew exactly how it felt when a god died. They'd felt it happen. Mid-battle, mid-prayer, mid-breath — the voice going out like a snuffed lamp, leaving ten thousand sworn swords standing in the smoke holding prayers that landed nowhere. Emrys had crawled into the Thorn Valley afterward because magic couldn't follow them there, and neither, it turned out, could grief. You could bury anything in ground that forgot.

So yes. Emrys knew the shape of the mercy. It was quick. It was certain. It was the only version of this story where no armies marched and no cities burned and no ancient war woke up hungry after a hundred years of sleep.

And it required killing the only living thing in the world that still spoke the language Emrys dreamed in.

Emrys lay on their back under the leaning alder and turned the problem over, and over, and over, like a stone worn smooth by handling.

* * *

Sometime past midnight, Wren appeared at the edge of the firelight carrying the knife.

Not their own — they didn't have one. Emrys's second blade, the short one kept in the boot, which the child must have lifted while Emrys slept badly, which meant the child had gotten better at lifting things, which was a development Emrys filed away under *later problems.*

They held it wrong. Both hands, like a bread knife, tip wobbling. Their face was solemn in the fire-glow, and the light under their skin had gone still, which somehow was worse than the singing.

"You're thinking about it," Wren said.

Emrys sat up slowly. "Thinking about what."

"The choosing." Wren came closer and stood over Emrys, small and straight-backed, the knife held out in front of them like an offering or a dare. "You've got your quiet face on. Your quiet face is loud."

"That doesn't make sense."

"It does in the old tongue." Wren sat down cross-legged, set the knife carefully on the ground between them, and looked at the fire instead of at Emrys, which was a kindness Emrys hadn't earned. "In the old tongue there's a word for when someone carries a heavy thing alone even though there are two people. *Sessah.* It means 'the weight refuses company.' Maren used to do it. She'd go behind her hut and be loud-loud-quiet and come back and say nothing was wrong."

"She talked to you about me?"

"She talked about everything. She talked more than anyone I ever met." Wren's mouth twitched. "She said you were the kind of person who'd apologize to furniture before moving it."

"Maren," Emrys said, "talks too much."

"*Sessah,*" Wren agreed, and pointed at Emrys, matter-of-fact as naming a color.

The fire popped. Somewhere downriver a night bird called the three-note call that Emrys had learned, in another life, meant *watch posted, all clear.* Old habits. Old war. Everything in them was old war.

"Wren." Emrys made their voice steady. It cost something. "Why did you bring me my knife?"

Wren considered the question with the total seriousness they brought to plums, knots, and theology. "Because you keep looking at yours. And looking at mine. And if you're going to decide, you should decide with it out where I can see it. Not hiding it in *sessah.*"

"And then what? What do you think happens if I use it?"

"I think it hurts," Wren said simply. "For both of us. I think He goes dark all the way and the pulling stops, and I stop hearing Him under everything, and it's quiet like the valley was quiet." They picked up a twig and snapped it, once. "And I think you'd have to live after. And I don't think you'd be any good at it."

Emrys laughed. It came out cracked down the middle. "You don't know that."

"I know you gave me the bigger share of the eggs even when you were hungriest. I know you checked the road twice for hoofprints before letting me wash my feet in the stream. I know you sleep facing the door and put yourself between me and every stranger we pass, including the wool boy, who was eleven." Wren ticked these off on small fingers, unhurried. "People who are good at surviving alone don't do that. So if you kill me, you'll have killed the last person you were being good for. And then you'll go back in the valley, or somewhere like it, and be quiet forever, and that's not living after. That's just dying slow with extra steps."

The fire burned. Emrys found they could not look away from the child's face — round-cheeked, mud-scraped, absurdly young, and carrying a verdict older than kingdoms.

"When did you learn to talk like that?" Emrys asked hoarsely.

"I listen. It's mostly what I do." Wren shrugged. "Also Maren said you were predictable. She said — she said, *that one will chew a decision for a week rather than swallow it, and meanwhile everyone else has eaten.*"

"She said that, did she."

"Word for word. Well. Word for word-ish. Her accent was strange."

Emrys dragged a hand down their face. Ten years of silence, one season of this child, and somehow Wren had already mapped every fault line in them. There was no privacy left. There was only the choice, laid out in the open where they could see it, which was — Emrys realized with a lurch — exactly why the knife was sitting there in the dirt between them.

* * *

Near dawn, Emrys walked away from the cold fire and stood on the rise above the river road, alone, and finally let themselves look at the other side of the scale.

Corvane smoked softly against the brightening sky. Beyond it, past the free cities, past the pilgrimage roads grown over with weeds, the faithful — if they existed — waited without knowing they waited. And beyond *them*, eastward, the citadel of the usurper, squatting on its black rock like a toad on a grave. Underneath which — if the archive confirmed it, if Maren's wild threads led anywhere true — Ossa had not died at all. Had not abandoned them. Had been *taken.* Sealed. Chained by the bloodline that now wore a crown and called itself rightful.

Ten years Emrys had grieved a god who never left them. Ten years of feeling abandoned, betrayed, unchosen — and all of it might have been a lie told by chains. Every bitter prayer in the valley, every night spent hating the silence, might have been grief misaddressed, like letters sent to a house where the person had been kidnapped years ago.

If that was true, then Wren wasn't a relic. Wasn't a weapon, even. Wren was a *key* — no. Worse. Wren was a *hand reaching out from under a locked door,* and the question was whether anyone on this side was still willing to open it.

Opening it meant war. Not maybe-war. Not possibly-war. The old faiths had torn the world apart once already; Emrys had personally buried enough of that war to fill a valley. Vaelric's inquisitors would burn every city that harbored the faithful. The faithful would rise, because people who had knelt in secret for a century did not rise gently. And somewhere in the middle of it, a child who liked plums and spoke in the oldest language alive would either become a banner or a casualty, and banners got shot at.

Keeping it shut meant peace. The gray, grinding, kneel-to-your-betters peace of Vaelric's new order, built on a stolen god and a sealed door, lasting exactly as long as no one found out. Which was another way of saying: built on a lie, and lies under thrones had a habit of coming up through the floorboards eventually, usually during somebody else's reign, at ten times the price.

Or there was the third option. The one sitting in the dirt by a cold fire, held in small competent hands.

*End it. Keep the peace. Keep surviving.*

*Survive what?* asked a voice in Emrys that sounded uncomfortably like Maren. *Survive toward what? You've done ten years of surviving. Show me the harvest.*

Emrys stood on the hill until the sun cleared the ridgeline and lit the road gold, and understood, with the same sick clarity as the day they'd lied to the inquisitor at the valley's edge, that the choice had already been made. Probably it had been made the night a mud-caked child knocked on a hermit's door speaking the First Tongue. Probably it had been made the moment Emrys opened the door at all — because Emrys, who had spent a decade making sure no door of theirs ever opened onto the world, had heard that small knocking in the storm and gotten up.

Everything since had just been chewing.

They went back down the slope. Wren was awake, sitting by the rekindled fire, and when Emrys came into the light the child read their face in one glance — the loud quiet face — and something in those too-old eyes eased.

"Sessah's over?" Wren asked.

"Sessah's over." Emrys crouched, took the short knife from where it still lay beside the fire ring, and — deliberately, so Wren could see every motion — slid it back into their boot. Then held out a hand. "We're going to Corvane. There are people there who need to see you, and a library I need to burgle. After that—" Emrys exhaled. "After that we go east. To the citadel. To Him."

"To the pulling."

"To the pulling."

Wren nodded once, gravely, and put their small hand in Emrys's scarred one, and the light under their skin flared briefly gold — hope-shaped, Emrys thought, or as close as light ever got to it.

They struck camp. They took the river road north. And they had covered perhaps half a mile, the city walls rising ahead through morning haze, when Wren stopped walking so abruptly that Emrys nearly pulled them off their feet.

"What?"

"There's someone behind us." Wren's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice they used for real danger. "Been behind us since the ridge. Not inquisitors. Wrong boots." A pause, and then, with dawning bewilderment: "I know the way they walk. I've heard that walk before. Under the burning."

Emrys turned, hand drifting to the hilt — and saw, perhaps forty paces back on the empty road, a figure in a travel-stained soldier's cloak push back her hood, revealing a face Emrys had last seen across a battlefield, a decade ago, on the worst day of their life.

A face Emrys had buried with their whole past.

*Sefa.*

"Well," Emrys breathed. "*Hell.*"
