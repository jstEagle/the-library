## 15. Sefa's Price

The rain had followed them into Corvane, thinning to a gray drizzle that turned the city's cobbles the color of old pewter. Emrys stood at the window of a safehouse above a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, watching water bead and run down glass gone cloudy with age, and tried very hard not to think about how much the whole arrangement smelled of a trap.

It didn't help that Wren kept humming.

The child sat cross-legged on the floor, wrapped in one of Emrys's spare shirts like a small ghost in gray wool, sorting candle stubs by color with the fierce concentration of a general deploying troops. Every so often—unbidden, unconscious—a thread of melody slipped out of them, soft and strange, in that language older than kingdoms. Each time it happened, the nearest candle flame would lean toward them like a flower toward sun.

"Wren." Emrys kept their voice low. "We talked about the singing."

Wren looked up, unrepentant. "Wasn't singing. Was counting."

"In the First Tongue."

"Counting *is* different in First Tongue. It sounds prettier."

Emrys pinched the bridge of their nose. Ten years ago they had knelt in blood and ash and sworn themselves body and bone to a god whose voice could shake mountains. Now they spent their mornings negotiating with that god's last fragment over candle-stub etiquette. There was probably a lesson in there. They were in no mood to learn it.

"Pretty gets us killed," they said. "Prettier than anything else in this city, maybe. So we count in Common. Quietly. Like normal children who have never glowed in their lives."

"I only glowed *once*."

"You glowed once on a public road."

"And then you taught me the breathing thing, and I haven't done it since." Wren held up the candle stubs as evidence, one in each fist, like scales balancing. "See? Dark as anyone."

It was true, more or less. The breathing technique Maren had taught them—the trick of tucking the light down inside, banking it like a fire for the night—had held through three days in Corvane's crooked streets. Three days of waiting for Sefa's message, which was the other reason Emrys's stomach had been tied in knots since dawn.

Because here was the arithmetic Emrys couldn't stop doing: Sefa knew where they were. Sefa knew *what* Wren was, or close enough to sell the shape of it. And Sefa had debts, and Vaelric's inquisitors paid well, and ten years of shared bread and spilled blood apparently weighed less these days than a purse of usurper's coin.

Or none of that was true, and Sefa was still Sefa, and the knot in Emrys's stomach was just an old soldier's habit of expecting the worst from everyone they'd ever loved.

Both possibilities hurt. That was the trouble.

A knock came at the street door. Three raps, pause, two more—Sefa's old signal, unchanged since the war, which meant either she was genuinely here or someone had tortured her signal out of her, and Emrys found they couldn't decide which prospect was worse.

"Stay behind the counter," Emrys murmured. "If I say 'the wax is poor this season,' you go out the back and find Maren's cousin at the fish market. You remember?"

"The one who smells like herring and sadness."

"That's the one."

Wren nodded solemnly and folded themselves behind the counter with the candle stubs, vanishing almost entirely except for one sock-clad foot. Emrys took a breath, unbarred the door, and opened it onto the drizzle.

Sefa stood on the step with her hood back and her hands visible, the way you approached a wary animal. Gray had come into her cropped hair. A scar Emrys didn't remember giving her time to earn ran from her ear to her jaw. Otherwise she was exactly the same: broad-shouldered, sharp-eyed, with the particular stillness of a woman who had made a living for a decade noticing things before they noticed her.

"Emrys Villalobos," she said. "You look terrible."

"You look prosperous."

"Information is a growth industry." Her eyes went past Emrys's shoulder, quick and professional, cataloguing the room. Emrys watched her do it and hated how familiar it felt—two soldiers reading the same battlefield. "You going to invite me in, or are we doing this on the step like strangers?"

"We're doing it on the step."

"Fair." She rocked back on her heels. Rain dripped off the eaves between them. "I owe you an apology."

"That's new."

"Shut up and let me spend it." Her jaw worked. "I sold word of you. Not everything—I gave them the city, not the street. Bought myself three days to think about what I'd done, which was generous of me, because I needed about four."

Emrys said nothing. The knot in their stomach pulled tight.

"Here's what you need to know, and then I'll let you decide whether to spit on me or thank me," Sefa said. "Four inquisitors crossed the south gate this morning. Not the usual sort—the inner circle, the ones who wear the white cords. They're asking after a relic, same as at the valley. And Emrys—" she hesitated, and Emrys saw, for the first time, actual fear move behind her eyes, "—they already know the child sings. Someone talked. Not me. But someone did, and now they're sweeping Tallow Row, chandler's shops first, because the man who runs the chandlers' guild owes my broker a favor and my broker sold him the favor cheap."

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less." She reached into her coat, slow and deliberate, and Emrys's hand drifted toward the knife at their belt before she produced, of all things, a paper packet. "Dried plums. For the little one. Don't give me that look, I remembered they liked them, don't ask me how I remember things like that after ten years, I don't know either."

* * *

They ended up inside after all, because the rain thickened and because Emrys, against every instinct honed across twenty years of violence, decided to believe her exactly as far as the plan required and no further. Sefa spread a hand-drawn map of the district on the counter—she'd brought a map to an apology, which told Emrys everything about how far past apology she already was—and traced the sweep pattern with a blunt finger.

"They'll come down Tallow Row from the north, pair by pair, door to door. The chandler's cellar connects to the dye-house next door, the dye-house has a loading yard, and the yard opens onto Cooper's Lane. From there, the canal steps. There's a barge—"

"What barge?"

"The kind of barge that exists when I pay for it." She glanced toward the counter, where Wren had re-emerged and was regarding her with the frank, unnerving stare of a child deciding something important. "Hello, Wren."

"You knew my favorite fruit," Wren said.

"Plums. Yes."

"Why?"

Sefa opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Emrys with something close to panic, as if the child had asked her to defuse a siege engine. "I just did," she said finally.

Wren considered this with terrible gravity, then took the packet of dried plums and retreated under the counter again, judgment suspended but not dismissed.

Emrys studied the map, memorizing it, and let themselves feel—for one treacherous moment—what it might be like if this were real. If Sefa had actually crawled back across whatever line she'd crossed. Ten years ago she'd been the one who dragged Emrys out of the mud at Cael Ford, laughing, swearing, bleeding from a scalp wound she'd insist for years had been a graze. The idea that the woman who'd done that had also sold their location to Vaelric's white-cords sat in Emrys's chest like a swallowed stone.

"Why?" they asked quietly. "The first time. Why sell me at all?"

Sefa didn't flinch, which Emrys respected even as it cut. "Because I'm tired, Emrys. Because the cities are falling piece by piece and everyone pretends otherwise, and a woman can only watch the tide come in for so long before she starts thinking about learning to swim with the flood. Because they offered me a place in the new order instead of a shallow grave in it, and I took it, and I've been sick about it for three days straight." She met their eyes. "That's the truth. All of it. I know it doesn't weigh enough to balance the scale. It's what I have."

"It doesn't," Emrys said.

"No," Sefa agreed. "But here's something extra." She tapped the map, at the canal steps. "The barge leaves at moonrise whether you're on it or not. The pilot's name is Doss. Tell him the eel got fat. He'll know." She rolled the map and pushed it across the counter. "And Emrys—when they come, and they will come early, because white-cords never do anything at the advertised hour—you keep walking. Whatever happens behind you, you keep walking. Promise me."

"Don't make promises you need from me, Sefa."

"Just promise."

Emrys looked at her—at the scar, the gray hair, the hands that had once hauled them out of the mud—and said, "I promise," knowing even then that promises between soldiers were the first casualties of any fight.

* * *

The white-cords came forty minutes early, as Sefa had predicted, and they came loudly, because subtlety had apparently gone out of fashion along with honor.

Emrys heard them from the stairwell: boots in the street, the chandler downstairs stammering, and then—cutting through it all—a voice like oiled iron saying, "Search the upper floor. The relic will be disguised. They always are."

Emrys pressed Wren flat against the wall beside the cellar hatch. "Change of plan," they breathed. "No fish market. Cellar, dye-house, Cooper's Lane, canal. You remember the order?"

"Cellar, dye-house, lane, canal." Wren's face had gone pale but set, and there was something in their expression that made Emrys's heart lurch—an echo, absurdly, of soldiers before a breach. "What happens to you?"

"I'm right behind you."

"You're lying," Wren said, without heat, the way children report weather. "Your lying face does a thing."

"*Go.*"

Wren went. The hatch dropped shut with barely a whisper, and Emrys turned to face the stairs as boots thundered up them, and understood with perfect clarity that they had never intended to leave by that cellar at all. Somebody had to hold the door. Somebody always had to hold the door. It was the oldest job in the world, and they had volunteered for it in every life they could remember.

Three white-cords came through the doorway in good order, blades drawn, and behind them a fourth figure—tall, gray-robed, a face like a folded letter. The tall one looked at Emrys with the mild interest of a clerk finding a misfiled document.

"Emrys Villalobos," he said. "Sworn of Ossa. We wondered where the dog had hidden itself."

"Careful," Emrys said, drawing steel. "I bite."

What followed was short and ugly and took the length of the stairwell landing. Two of the white-cords were good—trained, coordinated, used to working together. The third was young and eager and died fast. Emrys fought the way they always had, economically, without flourish, giving ground only to buy angles, and they might even have managed all four if the tall one hadn't simply stepped back and spoken a word that made the air go stiff as frozen leather. Magic. Even here, in the city's stone heart, the usurper's people carried their leash-gifts like jewelry.

Emrys's sword arm slowed. The two remaining white-cords pressed in, smiling now, and the tall one said, "Hold the left one alive," and Emrys thought—with a strange, floating calm—that Wren was below and moving and that was the whole of the victory available today—

And then the window exploded inward.

Not the window. Sefa came through it, or rather through the space where the shutter had been, having climbed the dye-house roof in the rain, and she hit the nearer white-cord like a landslide, and after that it was noise and blood and Emrys fighting free of the stiffness in their limbs while the tall gray-robed figure recoiled from a blade he had not budgeted for.

"Sefa—"

"GO!" She had her back to his now, hers and Emrys's, the old formation, the oldest one, shield-sisters holding a door together. Blood ran from her hairline into her eyes and she laughed the way she'd laughed at Cael Ford. "You promised you'd keep walking! Keep your promise, Villalobos, it's the only one you've ever kept clean!"

The tall one recovered. Emrys saw it happen—saw the word gathering behind his teeth again, the leash-gift coiling—and lunged, but too slowly, hampered by the second white-cord, and it was Sefa who broke away, Sefa who threw herself into the gap between the gray robe and Emrys, Sefa whose blade found him first and whose side took his dying counterstroke in trade.

She went down laughing. Of course she did.

Emrys killed the last white-cord in three strokes they would never afterward remember making, and then there was no time at all, because shouting had started in the street and torches were being lit in windows, and the dead do not wait and neither do the living.

Kneeling, Emrys gathered her up. "Don't," they said, uselessly. "Don't you dare."

"Is the little one clear?" Her voice had gone strangely light, the way voices do.

"Clear. Moving. Safe."

"Then it balances." She caught their wrist with a grip still strong enough to surprise. Her eyes found theirs, sharp to the last, professional to the end. "The scale. I told you it wouldn't balance. It balances now. Tell me it balances."

Emrys—who had buried friends before, in numbers, in mud, in wars won and lost and forgotten by everyone but the survivors—found they could not speak around the thing in their throat. So they lied, gently, the way you lie to the dying: "It balances, Sefa. It balances."

"Good," she said. "Buy the eel dinner. Doss. Tell him—" and the sentence never finished, and her grip eased, and the rain came through the broken window and fell on both of them alike.

* * *

The barge slid out of Corvane under a rising moon, its hold stacked with honest barrels over less honest passengers. Emrys sat in the dark between them with Wren curled against their side, small and warm and mercifully asleep, one hand still clutching a dried plum.

They had washed the blood off in the canal steps before boarding. Their hands looked like their own again. They did not feel like it.

"She saved us," Wren said softly, without opening their eyes. Children always knew when you thought they were sleeping. "The fruit lady."

"Yes."

"Even though she sold us first."

"Yes."

Wren was quiet a while. The water chuckled against the hull. Somewhere behind them, Corvane's bells had begun to ring—not alarm, not yet, just the hour—but Emrys knew the alarms would come, and the riders, and the long gray road east toward the citadel and the prison beneath it. Toward the god who had gone quiet. Toward the end of this, whatever the end was.

"Why do people do that?" Wren asked. "Be bad and then be good? Does the bad wash out?"

Emrys thought about Sefa's face in the rain, laughing, spending her last breath buying back a debt. Thought about the answer they owed a child versus the answer they owed themselves.

"I don't know," they said honestly. "Maybe nothing washes out. Maybe you just add to it. Maybe that's all any of us get to do—keep adding until the adding means something."

Wren considered this, thumbing the plum. "She added enough," the child decided, with the terrible finality of the very young. "I counted."

And Emrys, staring east where the road waited, felt the grief arrive properly at last—too late to be useful, too late to share, arriving exactly as redemption always does: after the price is paid, when all that remains is to carry what it bought.

"Hold on to that," they said quietly. "Counting. Remember she balanced."

Ahead, the river bent toward open country, and somewhere past the dark hills the pilgrimage road began, and every mile of it led toward a prison, a god, and a choice Emrys could already feel tightening around their throat like a chain.

The barge moved on into the night, carrying them toward all of it.
