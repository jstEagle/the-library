## 16. Flight Through the Free Cities

**Emrys**

The bell above Corvane's fish market began to toll out of rhythm—three quick strikes, a pause, then two—and Emrys's body understood before their mind did. *Alarm. The quay. Run.*

"Off the street," they said, and hauled Wren into the shadow of a drying shed as the first torchlight bloomed at the harbor mouth.

The faithful had gotten them off the *Grey Gull* and through the customs gate with a forged manifest and a customs officer who owed someone a very old debt. That was the last thing that had gone quietly. Now there were six of them threading the dawn market: two dockhands who smelled honestly of brine, a baker's wife named Odile with flour still on her forearms, a lean young man everyone called Sparrow because he refused to give another name, and an old woman, Yeva, who moved like she'd been climbing Corvane since before it had stairs.

And Sefa's knife wound, freshly bound under Emrys's coat, pulled with every step. They didn't think about that. They couldn't afford to think about that.

"Inquisitors?" Wren whispered.

"Worse. Paid ones." Emrys peered between the drying racks. "Inquisitors believe things. Hired blades just want to finish."

Below, men in grey-and-gold livery were fanning through the market stalls, flipping fish onto the cobbles, shoving a fruit-seller against his own cart. One held up something small—a scrap of cloth, maybe, or a drawing. The description had reached them fast. Too fast. Someone in the harbor chain had sold them inside an hour.

*Vaelric's coin buys quicker than his faith,* Emrys thought, and felt the old cold settle behind their ribs.

Odile appeared beside them, breathless, cheerful in the way of people for whom catastrophe was a familiar houseguest. "Roofs," she said. "The canal quarter's a maze if you know it from above. Below, you're dead."

"I don't climb roofs," Emrys said.

"You do today." Odile pressed a heel of bread into Wren's hands and grinned. "Eat while you run. It's the only way to eat in this city anymore."

Then she was shouting at the greys—something about her stall, her rights, her late husband's service to the city watch—and every head turned, and Emrys took Wren by the collar and ran for the drainpipe at the tanner's corner.

The roof of Corvane was another country. Emrys had forgotten that. Ten years in a valley where nothing changed, and here whole lives went on three stories up: laundry lines strung between chimneys like bunting, pigeons kept in wicker cages, a man asleep on a warm slate roof with his hat over his face as though armies could pass beneath him and be damned. Emrys vaulted a gutter, landed hard enough to make the wound shout, and kept moving.

Wren kept pace. That was the thing that kept startling them—the child ran like water finding its level, sure-footed on slick tile, ducking lines without looking. Something in Wren had been made for flight. Emrys tried not to wonder what.

Behind them, boots hit the drainpipe. Two greys on the roofs already, and more below, cutting angles.

"Sparrow!" Emrys called.

"Seen 'em!" Sparrow was already moving along the ridgeline, light as his name, gesturing left. "Canal! There's a boat—Yeva's cousin—go, go!"

They went.

* * *

**Wren**

The giant was hurt. Wren had known since the boat, from the way the giant's breath went thin and careful, like someone carrying a full cup across a slippery floor. Wren didn't say anything. Saying things made the giant make a face—not an angry face, exactly. A face like a door closing partway.

But the running was good. The running, Wren could do.

Corvane from above was a sea that had decided to be a city instead. Roofs rolled in waves, brown and red and rust, and the canals cut through them like bright scars. Wren had never seen so many people making so much noise about so little—a woman screaming cheerfully at a cat, boys throwing walnuts at each other, someone playing a fiddle badly two streets away and nobody minding. In the burning lowlands there had only ever been one sound worth listening for, and after it came, there was no other sound at all.

Wren missed the burning places and hated missing them. That was the worst trick of surviving: your heart kept living in the place that killed everything else.

"Hop!" said Sparrow, and Wren hopped—a gap between rooftops, four feet of morning air—and landed rolling the way the giant had shown them on the second day, shoulder-first, up and moving. Somewhere below, a grey soldier swore and hit a wall.

The glow was starting again. Wren felt it first in the fingertips, a warmth like holding your hands near a fire you weren't supposed to touch. Under the skin, faint gold, the way pond water looks when the sun gets under it. *Hide it*, the giant had said. *Think of cold things.* So Wren thought of snow, of the valley's grey river, of the giant's face when they talked about the god they wouldn't name. The glow dimmed to almost nothing. Almost.

Sparrow glanced back, saw the shimmer anyway, and didn't flinch. He just smiled—quick, crooked—and threw Wren an apple from some stranger's windowsill.

"Sorry, neighbor!" he called down through the window, not sorry at all, and ran on.

That was the strange thing about these people, Wren thought, catching the apple mid-stride. They were all going to die or worse if the greys caught them, and they kept handing out apples. Odile had given bread. The old woman, Yeva, had spent half the night braiding Wren's hair tight to the skull—"so no one grabs it, child"—her fingers gentle as falling ash. None of them had asked what Wren was. Not once. They looked at Wren and saw a child to be carried, not a thing to be traded, and Wren did not have words for what that did to the inside of the chest. It was too big. It hurt like growing.

Ahead, the roofs ended at a canal, wide and green-brown, barges nosing along it slow as sleeping cattle. A flat-bottomed skiff waited under a bridge arch, poled by a boy of perhaps fifteen who saluted Sparrow with two fingers.

"Yeva's cousin's son," Sparrow announced, as though this explained everything. To him, apparently, it did.

* * *

**Emrys**

The skiff was a coffin with a sail, and Emrys loved it immediately, because coffins don't attract attention.

They slid under the bridge arch and into the canal traffic, and for eleven glorious minutes Corvane simply swallowed them—another boat among hundreds, another tired face, Wren tucked under an oilcloth among the fishing nets. Emrys let their breathing slow. The wound throbbed in time with the pole-strokes. Somewhere overhead the greys would be casting along the rooftops like gulls after offal, and finding nothing but angry chimney-owners.

"They're good," Emrys said quietly. "Your people."

"They're ours," Sparrow corrected, without heat. He sat coiling rope, watching the banks. "You've been gone ten years. Somebody had to keep the lamps lit." He nodded at Wren's covered shape. "Word came down the pilgrim line when Maren sent her birds. Old codes. Half the hedge-folk in three provinces are watching the roads now, whether they pray or not. Vaelric burned a lot of grandmothers, you understand. People remember their grandmothers."

Emrys looked at him. Young, lean, unafraid, mending netting on a getaway boat as if it were any Tuesday. "You know what the child is?"

"No." Sparrow's eyes came up, steady. "I know what the child is *to us*. That's the whole of my theology." Then, softer, with the ghost of a grin: "Also I know the child stole an apple off the Widow Casque's sill and I'll hear about it for a year, so there's that to answer for."

Despite everything—despite Sefa's blood drying stiff in the lining of their coat—Emrys laughed. It came out cracked and rusty, like a gate opening after a decade, but it came. Wren peeked out from under the oilcloth, saw them laughing, and beamed like sunrise, and Emrys had to look away at the water because some feelings are too large to survive being witnessed.

The feeling lasted until the second bridge.

It started as a wrongness in the sound of the city—a hole in the noise ahead, the particular silence of a crowd pressing back from something. Then gold sashes under the third bridge's arch, and the long barrels of snare-pikes angled down at the water, and a voice amplified by a speaking-trumpet, bored and terrible:

"*The boat under the arch. Come alongside. All passengers will be counted.*"

They'd closed the canal. Of course they'd closed the canal. Emrys's hand found the knife at their hip; the wound screamed; the arithmetic was short and ugly. Six of them, two blades, a skiff, thirty yards of open water to the next cover.

Then Yeva's cousin's son—the fifteen-year-old with the salute—did the most Corvanian thing Emrys had ever seen. He stood up in the skiff, cupped his hands, and bellowed:

"*HEY! You lot! Is this about the tax? Because I paid the tax! My da paid the tax!*"

And the crowd on the embankment—who had been pressing back, who had been silent—turned like one animal. Because everybody in Corvane hated the tax. Everybody always hated the tax. It took the greys precious seconds to realize they weren't facing one smuggler's skiff but the accumulated grievance of an entire canal district, and in those seconds Yeva's cousin's son swung the pole, the skiff shot sideways into a reedy inlet no map had ever bothered with, and the shouting behind them rose into something joyful and furious and general.

"Go," Sparrow breathed, grinning like a wolf. "*Go*, old man, before they remember boats exist."

They abandoned the skiff in the reeds and went, wet to the waist, into the labyrinth behind the dyke-walls.

* * *

**Wren**

After that there was a lot of running again, but different running—through gaps in walls and along the tops of dykes and once straight through somebody's kitchen, where a grandmother looked up from her porridge, took in the armed giant, the glowing child, and the winded young man, and pointed silently at the back door while shielding her pot with her body, which seemed to Wren the correct set of priorities entirely.

They came out at last onto an old road.

Wren knew it was old the way you know a song is old—not by how it looked but by how it felt under the feet. Cobbles worn into waves. Waystones with carvings weathered to suggestions. And along both sides, at intervals, little iron lamps, unlit, rusted, patient. Hundreds of them, marching toward the hills.

"The pilgrimage road," the giant said. Their voice did something strange on the word—went hollow, like a shell held to the ear.

"People stopped walking it when the god stopped answering," Sparrow said. He wasn't grinning now. He stood on the road's edge with his bare feet on the stones, and he looked, suddenly, much older than his rope-coiled hands. "But we kept the lamps. Every family on the line keeps one lamp. My gran's gran kept hers through the purges. Never lit. Just... kept. In case." He shrugged, embarrassed, and became young again. "In case somebody came needing the road."

The faithful gathered around them in the last of the morning: Odile with flour on her arms and a bruise blooming on her cheekbone from a grey's baton; Yeva; the boy from the skiff, soaked and triumphant; a dozen others melting out of hedgerows and culverts, strangers who had never met each other and moved together like a single flock. They brought food. They brought dry cloaks. One old man knelt and re-tied Wren's bootlaces properly, muttering, as if armies were no excuse for poor knots.

Nobody asked for anything. That was the miracle Wren kept turning over like a stone. Nobody said *what will you give us*. Nobody said *prove what you are first*. They had lit nothing and believed everything, for ten years, in the dark, alone, and here at the end of it all they asked only: *which way do you need to go?*

Wren felt the glow rise and this time did not fight it. Gold light spilled through their sleeves, pooled on the old stones, touched the rusted lamps—and along the road, one by one, far as the eye could follow, the lamps caught. Not fire. A soft white bloom, like dawn remembering itself, running away toward the hills in a long bright ribbon.

The faithful made a sound Wren had never heard a crowd make. Not cheering. Lower than that, and older. The sound of a held breath finally let go.

"Oh," said Odile, wiping her eyes with a floured hand, leaving white streaks. "Oh, look at that. Look at *that*."

The giant was staring at the road, and their face was doing the thing Wren had learned to read: the door-face. But this time the door wasn't closing. This time it was standing open, and whatever was behind it was breaking the giant's heart in some quiet, necessary way.

"We should move," the giant said at last, roughly. "Light like that is visible from the citadel. From the *moon*."

"Let it be visible," Yeva said placidly, packing bread into a satchel. "Let him look at it. Let him lie awake wondering what's coming up his grandfather's road." She handed the satchel to Emrys and patted their arm twice, brisk as a drumbeat. "First shrine is two days' walk. Ask for Brother Aldous. Tell him the lamps are lit—he'll either weep or feed you, possibly both."

Sparrow walked with them as far as the first bend, then stopped, and stuck out his hand to Wren with tremendous formality.

"Safe roads, apple-thief."

Wren shook it solemnly. "Safe roads, Sparrow."

"And you." He clasped the giant's forearm, warrior-fashion, and held it a moment longer than needed. "Ten years I heard about the Wolf of the Vale. The hermit who buried the war." His grin flickered, went serious underneath. "Turns out you were just waiting for something worth digging up."

Then he was gone, back down the road into the crowd of ordinary brave people, and Emrys and Wren walked on alone—but not the same kind of alone as before. The lamps burned beside them all the way to the horizon, and behind them, faint but stubborn, Corvane rang with the sound of a city fighting its tax collectors and winning.

Wren walked close to the giant's side and matched their long strides, three to every two, and thought about apples, and grandmothers, and doors left open.

Two days to the first shrine. Beyond it, the hills. Beyond the hills, the citadel, and the thing beneath it that sang to Wren in their sleep, low and patient, like a voice calling up through deep water: *soon. soon. soon.*

That night, camped in a shrine-cave with the lamps glowing outside like fallen stars, Wren woke the giant with a hand on their arm.

"It's talking louder," Wren whispered. "The voice under the mountain. It knows we're coming."

Emrys was silent a long moment. Outside, a lamp flared brighter, as if leaning in to listen.

"Then we'd better walk faster," Emrys said.
