## 17. The Pilgrimage Road

The road had a name older than any kingdom, and the stones remembered it even when people didn't.

Wren felt it under their bare feet — or their one bare foot, anyway. Emrys had wrapped the other in strips of blanket after the second day of walking, muttering something about "children with no sense and less boots," and now Wren hopped along on a foot that smelled like sheep wool and felt like being loved by accident.

That was how Wren thought of Emrys sometimes. Being loved by accident. Like the mercenary hadn't meant for it to happen, and was still arguing about it internally, but it had happened all the same.

"Stop smiling," Emrys said without looking back. They always knew. It was unsettling, being known like that.

"I wasn't."

"You were humming and smiling. That's your whole face doing treason."

Wren grinned wider, which made Emrys sigh the big sigh they saved for moments of profound disappointment, mostly involving Wren's eating habits, sleeping habits, or general habit of existing joyfully. Then the mercenary kept climbing, shoulders squared against the wind, and Wren followed, and the road went on remembering its old name under them both.

They could feel it if they tried. *Ossarion's Way*, the stones said, in the deep slow voice that stone used. *The way the faithful walked. The way the god went down.*

Going down. That was the direction of everything now.

* * *

Wren had stopped pretending not to know what was happening inside them.

It wasn't frightening, exactly. It was more like — like standing at the edge of sleep and hearing someone call your name from very far away, except the someone was enormous, and patient, and sad, and the name being called wasn't exactly Wren's. Or it was. Both names were theirs now. The small one and the vast one. Some mornings Wren woke up and couldn't remember which one had been dreaming.

Their light came easier these days. In Corvane, Emrys had taught them to hide it — breathe it down, fold it small, tuck it behind the ribs where no inquisitor's glass could catch it. Wren had gotten good at folding. But folding took strength, and strength was a well with a bottom you could suddenly see, and lately Wren saw the bottom every time they reached down.

Three days out from Corvane, they'd started needing to sit down more often. Five days out, Emrys had started carrying them piggyback up the steep stretches while pretending this was a tactical decision. ("Faster," Emrys said. "You walk like a drunk duck." But their hands held on carefully, the way you hold something you've decided matters.)

Now, nine days out, the pilgrimage road climbed through hills gone gold and brown with late autumn, and Wren rode on Emrys's back through the morning because their own legs had opinions they didn't like anymore.

"You're lighter," Emrys said. Just once, quietly, like they hadn't meant to say it aloud. Then: "Eat more."

"There's only hardtack left."

"Eat more hardtack."

"It tastes like a roof shingle."

"A nutritious roof shingle. Eat."

Wren ate, chewing against Emrys's shoulder blade, watching the road unspool ahead between the hills. Far off — a day, maybe two — a dark shape sat on the horizon like a thumb pressed down on the world. The prison-citadel. Vaelric's seat. Even from here, Wren could feel what slept beneath it: a vastness turning over in its chains, slow as seasons, dreaming in a language Wren understood perfectly.

*Soon,* the vastness said. Not to Wren. To itself. The way a prisoner counts days.

Wren swallowed the last bite of roof-shingle bread and rested their cheek against Emrys's neck. "Tell me a story," they said. "A dumb one. From before."

Emrys went stiff for a moment — they always did at *before* — and then, as they had begun doing lately, in these gold hills where no one could see, they let themselves be soft.

"All right. There was a cook in our company named Bram. Built like a beer barrel. Sweetest man alive unless you touched his knives. One night Sefa — she was my shield-sister — Sefa decides she'll help him make stew, because she's Sefa, and helping is what she does whether you want it or not."

"What happened?"

"She salted it. She salted it the way you'd salt a siege ramp. Forty men ate that stew because we were soldiers and soldiers eat anything, and then forty men spent the entire next battle losing ground every time they had to stop and think about water."

Wren laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough halfway up, and Emrys's hand came back to steady them, and neither of them mentioned either thing.

* * *

They made camp in the shell of an old pilgrim shrine — just walls now, and a stone basin where offerings had once been left. Moss grew in the shape of hands on the altar. Someone had carved words above it so long ago the letters had gone soft:

**HE HEARS EVEN HERE.**

"He doesn't hear here," Emrys said, setting down their pack. Their voice had that flat carefulness they got around holy things. "This whole stretch of the old road is thin. Magic pools in it like rainwater. That's why the faithful built the shrines along it. But he still can't—" A pause. The jaw worked. "He still can't answer."

"He can," Wren said. "Through me. Sometimes. When I'm asleep."

Emrys went very still, the way they did before violence or truth. "What does he say?"

"That he's sorry." Wren traced the moss-hands on the altar. "That he never wanted anyone to carry his weight. That's most of what gods say, I think. Sorry and soon and *don't be afraid*. They're not very original."

Something moved through Emrys's face — too fast to name, too old to be new. They crouched by the fire pit and began arranging sticks with great concentration. "Did he say anything else? Last night?"

Wren hesitated.

Because yes. Yes, he had. Last night, in the space between one dream and the next, the vastness had turned toward them fully for the first time — not sideways, not murmuring, but *looking*, the way a mountain would look if mountains could grieve — and it had said: *When I rise, the small one must pour out entirely. There is no other door.*

And Wren had said, in the dream-language that wasn't really words: *I know. I've always known.*

And the mountain-grief had shaken itself and said the third thing gods say: *Don't be afraid.*

"Wren."

"He says the road gets steep past the shrine," Wren lied, lightly, easily, the way Emrys had taught them to lie to inquisitors. "He complains like you do."

Emrys looked at them for a long moment across the little fire they were building. Firelight caught the scar at their temple, the grey coming in early at their temples, the eyes that had seen too much and hidden from it for ten years and come back out of hiding for a child they'd found in the mud.

"You're getting better at lying," Emrys said. "I hate it."

"You taught me."

"I taught you to lie to bad people. Not to me." They struck flint, and sparks bloomed, and they didn't look up. "Whatever it is. Whenever you're ready. I'm not going anywhere."

Wren hugged their knees and watched the fire catch and said nothing, because the truth was a door they weren't strong enough to open yet, and also because — and this was the strange part, the part that ached — some selfish small piece of them wanted a few more days of hardtack jokes and piggyback rides and being told to eat more. A few more days of being just Wren, the mud-child, the drunk-duck walker, instead of the door.

* * *

The next evening, the weakness got honest.

They'd crossed into the steepest country, where the road clung to hillsides and the citadel filled half the sky whenever the trees broke, black and certain as a closed fist. And Wren folded their light small for the hundredth time and felt the well-bottom clearly, unmistakably, like stepping on a stair that isn't there.

They woke on their back beside the trail with Emrys's face above them, terrified and furious in equal measure, saying their name in the flat tone that meant the flat tone had cracked.

"I'm fine," Wren said.

"You fell over."

"I tripped."

"On flat ground. While standing still." Emrys's hands were shaking. Wren had never seen that. Ten years of buried war, three inquisitors killed in a doorway, a city burned to rooftops and canals behind them — steady hands through all of it. Shaking now, hovering over a child who'd fainted on a road. "How long have you been hiding this?"

Wren considered several answers. Chose honesty, finally, because Emrys had asked them to and because they were tired of doors.

"The light takes more out of me every time I fold it. Because there's more of him in me waking up, and less of me holding the rest down. It's like..." They searched for the shape of it. "You know when you carry water in your cupped hands, and you have to walk slower and slower or it spills? I'm walking slower and slower. And eventually—" They shrugged. "Eventually I'm just the river."

The silence that followed had weight. Wind moved the gold grass. Somewhere below, a crow complained about existence, which Wren found deeply relatable.

"So don't fold it," Emrys said roughly. "Let it show. We're two days out. If inquisitors see us, they see us, we'll fight—"

"And every mile I glow brighter, and every soldier between here and the gates knows exactly what's coming, and Vaelric has two days to move him deeper or build a wall of bodies around the vault. You said yourself — surprise is the only knife we have left." Wren sat up slowly. Their body felt borrowed, secondhand, worn at the elbows. "Besides. Folding isn't what's spending me. He's waking up either way. The folding just slows it. A little. For a while."

"How long is 'a while'?"

Wren smiled at them. Small and real. "You already know."

Emrys stood up abruptly and walked six paces away and stood with their back turned, and Wren let them have it, because Emrys carried things alone as a matter of religious principle and needed time to admit other people into the church.

When they came back, they sat down close — closer than usual, knee touching knee, which for Emrys was practically an embrace — and stared at the citadel on the horizon.

"When this is done," they said, "if we live. I'm taking you somewhere green. Somewhere with no roads at all. You can hum and smile and commit facial treason all day long, and nobody will tell you to eat more hardtack, because there will be actual food, because I'll steal it from rich people, which is basically farming."

"That's not farming."

"It's agriculture adjacent." But their voice caught on the joke, and Wren leaned their head against the mercenary's arm, and for a while they just watched the dark come down over the hills together.

Then Wren asked it. The question they'd been carrying since the shrine, since the dream, maybe since the burning lowlands — the question that had waited until now because it could only be asked in the dark, side by side, where faces didn't count.

"Emrys. If you could be forgotten — truly, completely, everyone who ever loved you forgetting you were ever there — but it would save everyone. Would you do it?"

The wind. The grass. The crow, still complaining.

"Yes," Emrys said at last. No hesitation. Which was its own kind of terrible.

"But it wouldn't hurt? Being forgotten?"

"It would hurt." Quietly. Honestly. "But hurting is cheap. I've paid it my whole life. Other things cost more."

Wren turned that over. Held it up to the stars.

"I don't think so," they said. "I think being remembered is the whole point. I think — look." They pointed at the ruined shrine back down the slope, barely visible, the moss-hands, the soft old letters. "Those people are all dead. Nobody knows their names. But somebody remembers that they *loved him*, and carved it in stone, and here we are, a thousand years later, still walking the road because of what they left." Their voice wobbled and they pushed on. "If I pour out and wake him and nobody ever knew there was a Wren — then the god wakes up owing his life to nobody. And that's wrong. That's a lie built into the foundation of everything. I want to be *in* the story, Emrys. Even just one line. 'And a child opened the door.' That's all. Is that worth dying for?"

Emrys was silent so long that Wren thought they wouldn't answer.

Then: "Yes," Emrys said, and their voice was wrecked and certain all at once. "Yes, little bird. It is. And I swear to you — by whatever's left of the god, and whatever's left of me — *you will be in the story.* If I have to carve it myself. If I have to tell it to every drunk in every free city until they're sick of it. You will not be a secret. I promise."

Wren hadn't realized they were crying until Emrys wiped their face with a rough, careful thumb, grumbling about "leaking children" in a voice that was leaking too.

They slept pressed back to back, the way travelers do for warmth and the way friends do for everything else.

* * *

Wren woke before dawn to the taste of iron and the feeling of the world tilting.

Not fainting. Something else. The vastness beneath the horizon was *moving* — thrashing, straining, chains groaning in frequencies only bone could hear — and Wren sat bolt upright in the grey light with their own light pouring off them unfurled and blazing, silver-gold, a beacon on the hillside, and they couldn't fold it, couldn't find the edges of themselves to fold it with—

Down the valley, in the dark of the pre-dawn road, torches answered. Many torches. Moving fast, in formation, toward the flare of light that was a child sitting up on the old pilgrimage road wearing the dawn before the dawn.

Emrys was already awake, already armed, already hauling Wren up by the wrist.

"Run," they said. "*Now.*"

Behind them, far beneath the black citadel, something ancient turned toward its fragment and roared — soundless, vast, and full of hope.

Ahead, the torches spread out across the road like fingers closing.

Wren ran.
