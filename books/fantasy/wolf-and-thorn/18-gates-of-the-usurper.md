## 18. Gates of the Usurper

The citadel of Vaelric had been built by men who wanted the world to know they were afraid of nothing, which meant, as Emrys had learned long ago, that they were afraid of everything.

It rose out of the city of Karveth like a fist through the palm of a hand—black stone, iron-crowned, its towers bristling with spearpoints and banners the color of dried blood. Below it, the city sprawled in the amber light of a festival evening, all lanterns and roasting meat and music spilling from every doorway. The eve-of-war feast, they were calling it. Tomorrow the armies marched. Tonight, everyone drank as if the drinking could be forgiven in advance.

Emrys walked through the crowd in a servant's grey, carrying a tray of wine cups they had no intention of delivering, and felt the old familiar shape of the soldier settle back over their shoulders like a coat that had never quite been put away.

*You look ridiculous,* they told themselves. *You are a hermit. You have moss opinions. You have not worn a uniform in ten years.*

But the body remembered. That was the trouble with bodies. Ten years of silence in the Thorn Valley, and Emrys's feet still found the rhythm of a patrol, their eyes still swept rooftops without being asked, their hands still knew exactly where a knife sat against a forearm. The valley had buried the war. It had never buried the soldier.

Wren was a mile away by now, tucked into the cellar of a dyer's shop with two of the faithful—a round-faced woman named Ilsa who smelled of indigo, and a boy of perhaps sixteen called Tam, who had taken one look at Wren's faint glow and sworn on every god he didn't believe in that he'd die before letting anyone touch them. Emrys had left them with instructions, a knife, and a promise.

*One night,* Emrys had told Wren, crouching to the child's level in the dyer's dimness. *I go in, I find out what Vaelric knows, I come back. You stay. You sing nothing. You glow nothing. You are a perfectly ordinary child made of mud and bad manners.*

Wren had considered this gravely, the way they considered everything, and then said in the First Tongue, *You will come back?*

*Yes.*

*Swear it on the god.*

Emrys had hesitated a half-second too long, and Wren's eyes—those too-old eyes in a too-small face—had seen the hesitation and filed it away somewhere behind the innocence. Emrys had sworn anyway. On the god. On the valley. On the memory of a hearth they could no longer quite picture.

Now the citadel gates loomed ahead, and the line of servants and carters waiting to pass through them smelled of sweat, onions, and the particular terror of people about to serve dinner to a tyrant.

* * *

Getting in was almost insultingly easy. That was the first thing that frightened Emrys.

The gate guards were checking for weapons, not for faces. A sergeant with a waxed mustache waved the tray through with a bored flick of two fingers, and Emrys was inside the walls of the most fortified place on the continent, carrying nothing but a tray, three knives, and a decade of grief.

The kitchens were a cathedral of noise. Forty cooks, a hundred scullions, geese hanging in rows like executed nobles, and a head chef with forearms like hams who directed the chaos by shouting in three languages, two of which appeared to be invented. Emrys slid into the current of it the way water slides into a crack. Carried the tray where it was pointed. Set it down. Picked up another. Nodded. Kept moving. Within a quarter hour, nobody could have said whether the grey-clad servant with the unremarkable face had always been there, and that was exactly the point.

The feast itself was to be held in the Long Hall, a vaulted cavern of a room that could seat four hundred and had, if the archives were right, been built on the foundations of something much older. Emrys carried wine through its service doors and let their eyes do what their hands were busy with.

Four hundred guests. Half of them officers in Vaelric's black and red, the other half merchants and minor lords who had decided early which way the wind blew and had dressed accordingly. At the far end, on a dais beneath a banner stitched with the usurper's sigil—a thorned crown over a broken circle—sat the man himself.

Emrys had seen Vaelric twice before, ten years ago, from across battlefields. He had been a younger man then, hungry-looking, all sharp angles and sharper ambition. The man on the dais was still lean, still sharp, but the hunger had been fed for a decade and it had changed him the way feeding changes any animal. He was broader through the shoulders. His beard was oiled and threaded with gold. He wore black silk and a circlet of iron, and he laughed easily at the jokes of the men flanking him, and his laugh had the warmth of a well-run abattoir.

And beside him, on a cushion, like a lapdog—

Emrys's blood went cold and then went cold again, colder.

It was a cage. Small, gilded, no bigger than a loaf of bread. And inside it, coiled and faintly luminous, was a fragment of light. Not Wren's light—smaller, dimmer, sadder—but unmistakably the same *kind* of light, the same pale gold that had leaked from Wren's sleeping skin on the road, that had lit the undercroft archive like a held breath.

A fragment of Ossa. On a cushion. At a feast. Like a trophy.

The tray in Emrys's hands trembled, and they set it down with exaggerated care among a hundred identical trays, and stood very still, and breathed.

*He has a piece of him already.*

* * *

The problem with infiltrations, Emrys reflected, hauling a cask of wine down a service stair because a steward had barked at them and drawing attention to oneself by refusing was worse than a sore back, was that the waiting was always worse than the fighting. Fighting was simple. Waiting meant thinking, and thinking meant remembering, and remembering meant the undercroft archive, the wall of sealed names, the moment when the truth had come apart in their hands like wet bread.

*Ossa was not silent. Ossa was taken.*

And now here was proof, glowing in a gilded cage on a dais, and Emrys had to stand in a corner holding a wine jug while lords toasted the campaign that would grind the free cities into paste, and smile the blank smile of a servant, and die a little more with every cup poured.

It was near midnight when the opportunity came. The feast had gone loose and loud; the head chef had begun drinking his own cooking sherry; and a harried chamberlain was pulling servants aside and sending them down into the underlevels with trays for the *other* guests—the ones not invited to the hall. Emrys volunteered before anyone else could. A servant who volunteers is a servant no one questions. That was in no manual anywhere, but it should have been.

The stairs went down farther than they should have. Emrys counted the turns the way they'd once counted paces on night marches—four flights, a landing, a corridor lit by lamps of green glass, and the air changing. That was the thing no one warned you about prisons: you felt them before you saw them. The air got thick. The stone got old. And beneath everything, so faint it might have been imagination, there was a pressure, like the silence in the Thorn Valley, but *wrong*—the valley's silence had been an absence, a hole where magic should be. This was a silence with weight. A silence being *held down*.

Emrys knew that silence. They had spent ten years inside a smaller version of it, wearing it like a hair shirt.

Two guards at the end of the corridor. Emrys walked toward them with the tray and the blank face, and the guards—fed, bored, half-drunk on the feast leavings—waved them past a door of black iron banded in bronze, and Emrys went through it, and stopped.

The vault beyond was not large. It was a circle of stone, older than the citadel, older than the city, its walls carved with the same broken-circle sigil that hung above Vaelric's feast—except here, Emrys understood at last, the sigil was not decoration. It was the *original*. Vaelric's whole heraldry, his whole claim, his whole dynasty, was built on the shape of a prison.

And the prison was not empty.

The light in the gilded cage upstairs had been a candle. This was the fire it had been struck from. In the center of the vault, sunk into the floor in a basin of black stone, something vast and dim turned over in its sleep—a slow churn of gold and grey, like a storm seen through deep water. Chains of a metal Emrys had no name for ran from the basin to the walls, and along every chain, at every link, the broken circle was carved, and every carving was stained dark with old blood.

Emrys stood with the wine tray in their hands and their heart slamming, and the thing in the basin turned again, and for one vertiginous moment Emrys felt—*felt*, the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck—a attention. Vast. Slow. Sad beyond describing.

*Little wolf,* the silence said, without words, in a voice like a memory of a voice. *You brought me nothing. You brought me only yourself, and you are not enough.*

Emrys's knees nearly went. They set the tray down because their hands had stopped being reliable, and they stood in the oldest prison in the world, and they thought: *I know that voice. I swore on that voice. I buried that voice in the valley and told myself it had abandoned me, and it has been down here in the dark for ten years listening to me hate it.*

The grief that went through them then was not like the other griefs. The other griefs had been for themselves—for the war, the valley, the wasted decade. This one was for the god. For ten years of a friend's silence mistaken for betrayal, while the friend screamed where no one could hear.

*I'm sorry,* Emrys thought, uselessly, the way you say it to graves. *I'm sorry. I didn't know. I didn't—*

Footsteps. Above, in the corridor. Voices—two sets of guards snapping to attention, and a third set of footsteps, unhurried, soft-soled, descending.

Emrys did the only thing there was to do. They picked up the tray, turned to the wine, and began, with the practiced tedium of ten thousand servants before them, to dust the bottles.

The door opened, and Vaelric came down the stairs.

* * *

He was not what Emrys expected, which was somehow worse. Emrys had braced for the tyrant from the stories—armored, thunderous, a man who filled rooms by taking all the air out of them. The man who entered the vault was quiet. He dismissed his guards with a flick, and when the door shut behind him he stood for a moment in the green lamplight with his hands clasped behind his back, and he looked, for all the world, like a scholar visiting a favorite library.

Then he saw the servant, and his head tilted, and Emrys watched the calculation move behind his eyes like a fish under ice.

"You're not the usual boy," Vaelric said.

"No, my lord. Marta's boy took ill. I was sent down with the wine."

"Wine." Vaelric's gaze went to the tray, and lingered there a moment too long, and then moved to Emrys's face with the unhurried thoroughness of a man appraising a horse. "I don't drink, down here. It dulls the ear. You can hear him better sober." He smiled. It was a genuine smile, which was the most frightening thing Emrys had seen all night. "Do you hear him, servant? The whisper in the stones? Most people can't. It takes a certain... attunement."

Emrys kept the face blank and the shoulders stupid and said, in the flattest voice they owned, "I hear the chains, my lord. That's all."

"Mm." Vaelric walked past them—close, deliberately close, the way a man walks past a dog he hasn't decided whether to kick—and stood at the basin's edge, and the light in the deep turned toward him the way a beaten animal turns toward the sound of its keeper's boots. "Ten years I've stood here," he said conversationally. "Do you know what I've learned? That a god is only a lock with the door painted on. All that power, and it cannot do one thing unless someone *opens* it. From the inside or the out." He turned. "It needs a key, servant. Do you know what the key is?"

Emrys poured wine into a cup they knew would never be drunk and said nothing.

"A fragment," Vaelric said, softly, delightedly, a man sharing a secret with a child. "A piece of the divine, hidden in mortal flesh, walking around in the world wearing a face. My grandfather caught one. You saw it upstairs—my little nightlight. Enough to study. Enough to prove the theory." His voice dropped, and the delight in it curdled into something older and hungrier. "But not enough to *open*. And now my inquisitors tell me a second fragment surfaced in the west. A child. Feral. Speaking the First Tongue. And the trail goes cold in a valley that eats magic, and the only soul who's lived in that valley for ten years is a mercenary who vanished off the face of the earth after the battle at Red Ford."

The vault was very quiet. The chains were very still. Somewhere in the deep water of the basin, the god was listening, and Emrys could feel it listening, and it took every scrap of ten years' discipline to keep pouring wine.

"I don't know anything about that, my lord."

"No," Vaelric agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. A hermit wouldn't. A servant wouldn't." He began to walk a slow circle, and Emrys tracked him in the corner of their eye the way you track a blade. "But here's what interests me. The inquisitors found a cottage, burned, three of their men dead in the snow. And the men who did it were good—*very* good—old-style good, the kind of killing they stopped teaching after the war. And the hermit who lived there was gone, along with the child, and the hermit's axe was missing from the woodpile." He stopped. He smiled again. "You know, I had a captain at Red Ford who fought like that. Emrys Villalobos. Ossa's own wolf, the men called them. Sworn shield of a silent god." He tilted his head. "Dead, we assumed. Everyone from that war assumed everyone was dead. It was the fashion."

Emrys set down the wine cup. Their hands were perfectly steady, which surprised them, and would have surprised everyone who had ever known them, and they heard themselves say, in the flat servant's voice, "Sounds like a dangerous person, my lord. I hope they catch them."

"Oh, I'll do better than catch them." Vaelric looked back at the basin, and his voice went soft with something like love, which was the worst thing yet. "I'm going to *give* them to him. The child walks into my citadel—my soldiers have seen the glow on the roads, it's only a matter of days—and when they do, I'll open this door with a key made of a child, and I will be the man who owns a god. And every army, every city, every prayer on this continent will answer to the House of Vaelric." He turned, and for the first time he looked at Emrys fully, and his eyes were patient and cold and absolutely certain. "Unless someone gets to the child first. And the only person in the world who might is a dead mercenary with a missing axe."

The silence stretched. In the basin, the dim gold turned, and turned.

*Little wolf,* the silence said, without words. *He is wrong about one thing. It is not a door. It is a mouth. And he has never once wondered what it eats.*

Emrys lifted the tray.

"More wine, my lord?" they said.

And Vaelric laughed—warm, genuine, terrible—and shook his head. "No. But you may leave the tray, and you may leave, and you may thank whatever god you still keep that I have a feast to return to and four hundred witnesses who expect me at it." His eyes glittered. "Run along, servant. And do give my regards to the child."

Emrys walked to the door with their heart a war drum and their face a wall, and their hand was on the iron when Vaelric spoke once more, lightly, almost idly, to the basin rather than to them:

"Oh—and wolf? The gates seal at dawn. The city with them. Do try to be interesting."

Emrys climbed the stairs into the noise and the lantern-light, past the guards, past the feast, out through the servant's gate into the cold, and did not run until the citadel was a black shape behind them and the festival streets swallowed them whole. Then they ran—through the dyer's quarter, down the reeking alley, hammering on the dyer's cellar door with the flat of their hand.

Ilsa opened it, knife already drawn, and behind her Wren looked up from a blanket in the indigo-smelling dark, eyes huge and luminous in the gloom, and Emrys stood in the doorway gasping like a landed fish and said the words they'd carried up four flights of stairs and half a city:

"He knows. He's known all along. Wren—the gates seal at dawn. We're already inside the trap."

And Wren, terrible and small and calm, said in the First Tongue: *I know. I felt him touch the god. That is why we came here, isn't it? To the mouth.*

The cellar was silent. Emrys looked at the child, and the child looked back, patient as the deep water of a prison, and dawn was four hours away.

"We go down," Emrys said, and heard their own voice like a stranger's. "Tonight. Before he seals us in. We go down to the god."
