## 19. The Prison Beneath

### Emrys

The way down was older than Vaelric's citadel. That much Emrys understood within the first hundred steps, when the torchlight caught walls that had not been cut by any mason's hand but grown—grown like bone, like the inside of a tooth, pale and faintly warm and curved in ways that suggested they were passing down through something that might, at some point in its long life, have been alive.

"This is wrong," Emrys said, for the third time since the cellar.

"You said," Wren replied.

"The citadel is two hundred years old. This—" Emrys pressed a palm against the wall and snatched it back. Warm. Warm as skin. "This is older."

"Everything under him is older." Wren walked ahead without hesitation, small feet sure on stone worn smooth by no one knew how many centuries of no one walking it. "That's his whole trick, isn't it? He built a house on top of something old and told everyone the house was the world."

Emrys had stopped being surprised by the things that came out of the child's mouth. Mostly. There was still a part of them—a part that had spent ten years learning to be a hermit, a dull person, a person who fixed roofs and counted firewood—that wanted to sit down on these impossible warm stairs and have a very long cry about how a nine-year-old could talk like a funeral oracle.

The rest of them kept walking.

The descent went on far longer than the citadel had depth. Emrys counted steps out of habit, lost count around four hundred when the air changed—thicker, sweeter, tasting of rain on hot stone—and gave up counting entirely. The torches they'd brought burned low and slow, as if the flame itself were growing drowsy.

"Wren." Emrys kept their voice level. Practice. Ten years of practice at sounding calm while everything underneath screamed. "When we find what we find. When we're standing in front of it. I need you to tell me what you're going to do before you do it. Can you do that?"

Wren stopped. Turned. In the guttering light their eyes did the thing they sometimes did—not a child's eyes at all but deep water, patient water, water that had been waiting somewhere dark for a very long time.

"You want me to promise not to disappear."

"I want you to promise not to *decide* anything alone."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "You decide things alone all the time. You decided to come down here alone. You decided in Maren's hut. You decided on the road of ash, when you buried those soldiers even though we didn't have a shovel and you used a pot lid."

"That was a *tactical* decision—"

"You cried after."

"I did not cry after. I had ash in my eye."

"Both eyes?"

"*Wren.*"

But the child smiled then—the real smile, the crooked gap-toothed one that belonged entirely to the mortal boy and not to whatever ancient fragment wore him like a coat—and Emrys felt the smile land somewhere under their ribs where nothing had landed in ten years.

"All right," Wren said. "I'll tell you first. Even if you won't like it."

"I never like it."

"No," Wren agreed cheerfully, and kept walking down.

* * *

The prison, when they reached it, was beautiful.

Emrys had imagined chains. Iron and stone and screaming, the whole apparatus of cruelty that Vaelric's bloodline had perfected above ground. What they found instead was a vault like the inside of a seed—a round chamber, high-domed, its walls grown over with veins of pale gold that pulsed, slowly, in a rhythm Emrys recognized before their mind allowed them to name it.

A heartbeat. The chamber breathed at the pace of a sleeping heart.

And at its center, suspended a hand's breadth above the floor, hung a knot of darkness no larger than a curled-up child. Not shadow exactly. Shadow would have been kinder. It was more like a hole shaped like a god—a place where the world had been folded over and sewn shut, gold threads crossing it in layer after layer after layer, seals upon seals, each one stitched by different hands across two centuries of careful, patient, loving cruelty.

The First Tongue rose in Emrys's throat unbidden, half-remembered liturgy from another lifetime:

*Sleep, shepherd. Sleep, storm-that-sings. The fold is closed. The wolf is bound.*

They hadn't spoken aloud. They were almost sure they hadn't spoken aloud. But the gold threads shivered, and the folded dark turned toward them—not moving, nothing so crude as movement—and Emrys felt attention settle over them like snowfall. Old attention. Enormous attention. Attention that had been waiting so long for anything at all to notice it that the noticing itself was nearly unbearable, a hunger given shape.

*Oh,* Emrys thought, with the last coherent corner of their mind. *Oh, you're awake in there. You've been awake the whole time.*

Ten years of silence. Ten years of believing themselves abandoned by a god who had gone quiet mid-battle and left them kneeling in the mud of a burning field. And the god had not been silent. The god had been *sealed*—conscious, aware, screaming perhaps, or worse than screaming, listening—for ten years while Emrys crawled into a magic-dead valley and licked their wounds and called it survival and called it wisdom and called it anything but what it was.

"Emrys." Wren's voice came from very far away, then very close, as the child took their hand. Small fingers. Steady. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like the goat did. Before it died."

"That goat was *old*, Wren—"

The god's attention pressed closer, and words arrived—not sound, not language, just meaning blooming directly behind Emrys's eyes like frost forming on glass:

*Little wolf. You came back.*

Emrys had prepared for this moment. On the road of ash, in Sefa's safehouse, in the long sleepless hours before infiltrating the citadel, they had rehearsed what they would say to Ossa. There had been drafts. There had been, at one low point, an actual list. *Where were you. Why did you leave us. Do you know what it cost.* A decade of grief organized into questions sharp enough to cut.

What came out instead, wet and cracked and entirely unbefitting a hardened mercenary, was:

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry I stopped looking. I thought—I thought you'd left *us*—"

*You could not have looked. You could not have known. The seal was made to be unfoundable.* A pause, and then something in the vast attention gentled, the way a large hand gentles. *You carried me anyway. Ten years in dead country, carrying my name like a coal under your tongue. Little wolf. You did not fail the fold.*

Emrys stood in the breathing gold-lit dark and wept the way they hadn't wept since the battle, since before the valley, since they were someone who had a god and a company and a reason. Wren held their hand the entire time and pretended, with great dignity, not to notice.

* * *

### Wren

The god was glad. Wren felt it the way they felt weather coming—pressure behind the eyes, a taste like lightning in the back of the throat. Gladness so large it frightened the small body it moved through. The god was glad to see the wolf, and the wolf was crying, and neither of those facts could be helped, so Wren simply held on and waited for the feelings to get smaller, the way you wait out a storm under an eave.

While he waited he looked at the seal.

He had seen it before, in fragments—in the memory-bits that weren't quite his, that lived in him like seeds in soil. He had seen hands stitching gold into the dark, generation after generation. He had felt the first stitch go in, long ago, from the inside: the shock of it, the cold. He had been hidden away that same night, folded into a dying woman's hope, sent out into the world as a baby who would grow up not knowing what he carried until the carrying started to show.

The stitches were failing. Wren could see that now, close up. Gold thread frayed at a dozen points, and through the gaps leaked warmth—the god's warmth, leaking out into the world for two hundred years in quantities too small for anyone to notice but large enough, maybe, to make a valley's crops grow strange and a hedge-witch's herbs turn sweet and a feral child glow in his sleep.

"He knows the seal is breaking," Wren said aloud, because some thoughts needed air. "That's why the feast. That's why he let us in. He doesn't need to catch us anymore. He needs us to open it *for* him."

The god's attention shifted to Wren, and what it carried was not gladness anymore. It was sorrow, huge and slow and terribly gentle, the sorrow of something that had watched this exact moment approach for two centuries.

*Little fragment. You know what opening requires.*

"Yes," Wren said.

"Don't," Emrys said.

It came out fast, ugly, reflexive—the voice of the soldier, not the hermit. Wren turned and found the wolf looking at him with an expression he had learned to read on the road of ash. It was the expression from the buried soldiers. From Sefa's knife. From every place where Emrys Villalobos saw a cost approaching and understood, with sick clarity, that they were going to be the one to pay it.

"There has to be another way," Emrys said.

"There is not," Wren said, gently, because it was true, and because lying to the wolf about this one thing would be the cruelest thing he could ever do. "The seal isn't a lock, it's a *knot*. It was tied with blood and belief and two hundred years of your family being afraid—" he caught himself; not *your family*, not anymore, "—of *Vaelric's* family being afraid. Fear holds it shut. You can't unlock fear. You can only give it something better to hold onto."

"And that's you."

"That's me." Wren smiled, gap-toothed, to take the edge off. It didn't work. It never worked on Emrys. "I'm the fragment. The piece of him that got sent outside when the door closed. If I go back—if I choose to go back, willingly, freely, *singing*—then the knot has to untie. Because the knot's whole job was keeping him in, and I'm the proof that he was never only in. Do you see? I'm not the key. I'm the crack in the door."

Emrys sat down. Actually sat down, right there on the warm gold-veined floor of a god's prison, knees folding like a puppet with cut strings, and put their head in their hands, and for a long moment said nothing at all.

Wren sat down next to them. It seemed polite.

"You promised to tell me first," Emrys said finally, muffled, into their palms.

"I am telling you first."

"You're telling me first and then doing it anyway!"

"Well." Wren tipped his head, considering. "Yes. But the telling-first was the promise. You never said anything about the doing."

There was a silence, and then—from behind the hands, cracked and horrified and helplessly fond—a laugh. One short, broken laugh, the kind that lives next door to sobbing. Wren counted it a victory. He had been collecting Emrys's laughs all the way from the Thorn Valley, one at a time, like a magpie collecting buttons. He had four now. He intended to remember all of them.

* * *

### Emrys

The footsteps came before the doors opened. Of course they did. Emrys had known, since the gates failed to seal at dawn, since the too-easy passage down through the citadel's bones, that this vault was a stage and they had walked onto it willingly. But knowing a trap and hearing its owner arrive are different animals.

The far wall opened without a door—stone parting along seams Emrys hadn't seen—and Vaelric stepped through with six of his gray-cloaked guard, and he was smiling.

That was the worst part. Emrys had expected triumph, or menace, or the cold theatricality of a man who had rehearsed this moment. Instead Usurper Vaelric looked *relieved*. He looked like a man setting down a weight carried so long his spine had grown around it.

"So it's true," Vaelric said softly. His eyes went past Emrys entirely—to the knot of folded dark, to the fraying gold, and something naked crossed his face. Something that looked, horribly, like longing. "My grandfather wrote of it. And his mother before him. Every heir learns the truth on the night of their crowning, and every heir spends the rest of their life pretending the throne is why they can't sleep." He laughed, short and unsteady. "Do you know what it is, to inherit a prison? To be handed the keys at seven years old and told: keep it fed, keep it feared, never let it wake, and never, *ever* ask what it wants?"

"I know what it is to carry something that heavy," Emrys said.

"You know nothing." Vaelric's voice cracked and he didn't seem to care. "I have fed it fear for thirty years. Whole villages. Rebellions. My own brother, when he asked too many questions about the vaults." His gaze finally landed on Wren, and softened into something Emrys recognized from mirrors—the look of a drowning man sighting a rope. "And now the seal is failing, and my grandfather's solution was always the same: more fear, tighter knots, until the day comes when fear runs out. But you—" he stepped forward, hands open, and his guards moved with him, "—you are not fear. You are the missing piece. Give yourself to me, child, and I will bind you into the seal properly. No more leaks. No more failing. Another two hundred years of quiet, and no one else ever has to know what's under their feet."

"You'd feed it me?" Wren asked. Curious. Almost kind.

"I'd *use* you. As the seal was meant to be used—as something living, freely woven in." Vaelric's jaw tightened. "It is not cruelty. It is maintenance."

"It's chaining a piece of a god to a door," Wren said, "and calling it carpentry."

The guards' hands drifted to their weapons. Emrys rose slowly, placing themselves between the child and the throne, an old motion, worn smooth by ten years of disuse and somehow still perfect.

"You could walk away," Emrys said to Vaelric. Quietly. Honestly. Because some part of them—the part that had once sworn oaths beside men whose faces they'd almost remembered—wanted, absurdly, to give this ruined inheritor of a terrible house one clean exit. "Seal the vault. Tell your heirs nothing new. Let the knot fray on its own time."

"And wake to a god with two centuries of grievance and no anchor? No." Vaelric shook his head, and the relief drained out of his face, leaving the usurper underneath. "It wakes on my terms or not at all. Take the child. Kill the mercenary if they resist. *Gently*, if you can manage it—we may yet need the wolf's cooperation."

Six blades cleared six scabbards.

And in the center of the chamber, the folded dark unfolded—one seam, then another, gold threads snapping like harp strings—and a voice that was not sound filled the vault from wall to wall to the marrow of everyone standing in it, and it spoke in the First Tongue, and it spoke to the smallest person in the room:

*Little fragment. Last ember of my fire. Are you certain?*

Wren took Emrys's hand one more time—quick, firm, a handshake rather than a clinging, the grip of someone concluding business they had already concluded—and grinned up at the wolf with all the gap-toothed terror and joy of a child about to jump off the high rock into deep water.

"Told you I'd tell you first," he said. "Watch."

And he stepped into the dark, singing.
