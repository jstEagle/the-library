## 20. Wolf and Thorn

The vault beneath Vaelric's citadel smelled of old stone and older lies.

Emrys had fought in a hundred places—the salt marshes at Corvane's edge, the burning terraces of the lowlands, a bridge in the rain that they could no longer name without flinching—but never anywhere like this. The prison-chamber was vast and round, carved from rock that predated the kingdom above it, and every surface was covered in chains. Not iron. Not bronze. Chains of script, lines of the First Tongue wound into spirals so dense they blurred together, binding something at the center of the room the way roots bind a stone.

At the center knelt Wren.

The child had stopped looking like a child an hour ago. Their skin held light now, faint and pulsing, like a lamp behind paper. When they breathed, the chains of script dimmed; when they exhaled, they brightened. They were singing—had been singing since they knelt down—a song in the First Tongue that Emrys knew from another life, another war, another self. It was a lullaby. Ossa's faithful had sung it over their dead.

*You don't have to do this,* Emrys had told them, and Wren had smiled with that terrible, gentle innocence and said, *Neither do you. But we're both going to.*

Now the doors at the top of the stair boomed, once, twice, and began to come apart.

Emrys turned from the god-in-the-child and faced the stair, sword drawn, back to the singing, and felt something in their chest settle into place like a shield-boss seating into a grip. Ten years of hiding. Ten years of mud and quiet patrols and pretending the silence in their skull was peace. All of it had been leading here, down these stairs, into the dark under the usurper's house.

The doors burst.

Vaelric's guard came through in column, six abreast, in black-lacquered plate with the stag-skull sigil of the new dynasty on their breasts. Behind them, unhurried, walked Vaelric himself in white and gold, and behind *him*, the inquisitor Sable-eyed men and women with censer-chains swinging censers full of ash that made Emrys's teeth ache.

"Emrys Villalobos." Vaelric's voice carried beautifully. He'd practiced it, Emrys thought. Practiced saying the name of a mercenary nobody had thought about in a decade. "Step away from the vessel."

"The vessel has a name," Emrys said. "It's Wren. They like the way rain sounds on a roof. They steal honey when they think I'm not looking." They rolled their shoulders. "I'm not stepping anywhere."

Vaelric sighed, the sigh of a man disappointed by the weather. "Kill them."

* * *

Emrys had spent ten years telling themselves the killer was dead.

They'd buried the wolf somewhere on the road into Thorn Valley, they'd believed—left it in a ditch with the rest of the war, the banners and the screams and the liturgy. The hermit who lived in the cottage kept bees and mended fences and flinched at loud noises. That was all.

The wolf stood up now, shook itself off, and went to work.

The first guard died on the stairs because he expected a hermit and got ten years of muscle memory instead: a parry that broke his wrist, a pommel-strike that folded him over his own shield, a thrust that found the gap under his helm before he finished falling. The second and third came together, shields high, disciplined—and discipline meant nothing against someone who had drilled against Ossa's own temple-guard, who had learned footwork from soldiers who'd been killing since before Vaelric learned to walk.

*Left,* said something deep and wordless in Emrys's body, and they went left, and the spear meant for their ribs scraped past, and they took the spearman's legs out and ended him quickly, because some habits of mercy survived even this.

Three more came up the stair in a wedge. Emrys gave ground, giving the stair to them, letting the narrowness work—two could fight where four couldn't stand. A blade kissed their shoulder and opened it. They barely felt it. Somewhere behind them, Wren's song climbed a note, and the chains of script flared so bright the shadows jumped.

"You're wasting your life for a *god*," one of the guards snarled through his visor, pressing forward.

"No," Emrys said, and killed him. "For a child."

That left the inquisitors, and the inquisitors did not charge. They swung their censers, and the ash inside them woke, and the air filled with a whispering that Emrys recognized with a sick lurch—it was counter-song, anti-liturgy, words designed to smother the First Tongue the way sand smothers a flame. Wren's voice wavered. The light guttered.

Emrys came off the stairs like something falling out of a storm.

Later—if there was a later—they would not remember most of it. They remembered the weight of a censer-chain wrapping their forearm and jerking; remembered using the chain as a leash to haul its owner off balance; remembered the flat, ugly economy of close work, elbow and edge and boot. One inquisitor fled up the stairs screaming a word that wasn't a prayer. The other two did not flee anywhere ever again.

When it was done, Emrys stood in the center of the vault, bleeding from three places, breathing like a bellows, and looked up at the balcony where Vaelric stood alone.

The usurper had not run. That was the worst part. He watched Emrys with the expression of a man watching a flood take his fields—not fear, exactly. Recalculation.

"You should have taken my offer," Vaelric said. "In Corvane. You never even heard it, did you? My people tried."

"I don't read letters from kings who burn villages."

"I burn *nothing*," Vaelric said, and for one moment his composure cracked and something younger and hungrier looked out. "I am trying to hold a kingdom together while a god rots under my floor and its faithful multiply in the free cities like rats! Do you know what happens if it wakes? Do you know what happened *last* time?"

"Yes," Emrys said quietly. "I was there. I swore to it. And then it went silent, and I thought—" Their throat closed. "I thought it abandoned us. I crawled into a hole in the world to get away from that. Ten years, Vaelric. You know what I've learned? Silence isn't abandonment. Sometimes it's a scream with no mouth."

Below them, Wren's song changed key.

* * *

The chains of script began to move.

Not break—*move*, flowing like water finding a new channel, spiraling inward toward the kneeling child, and the light in Wren's skin rose until Emrys had to shield their eyes. The vault filled with a pressure that was not sound and not wind but *attention*, the whole chamber turning its face toward what was happening at its heart.

And then, for the first time in ten years, Emrys heard the First Tongue spoken by something that wasn't a child or a memory.

It did not come as sound. It arrived the way weather arrives—in the bones first.

***Little wolf.***

Emrys dropped to one knee. Not by choice. The name hit them like a hand laid on the crown of the head, gentle and enormous, and grief came up out of the decade-old grave inside them and broke open all at once. *You're alive. You were alive the whole time.*

***I was taken. Sealed. The first usurper feared me, and his blood has fed the seal ever since. This child carries what remains of me outside the walls. But a god cannot wake into nothing, little wolf. A god needs an anchor. A memory of belonging—one true and whole—to hold onto, or I will rise formless and mad, and everything your child-fragment dies for will be wasted.***

"What memory?" Emrys's voice came out wrecked. "Whose?"

***One freely given. One that belongs.*** The attention turned on Emrys fully then, and they understood, and understanding was like the spear-thrust they'd dodged on the stairs—except this one they didn't dodge.

"No," they said. "No, take mine, take anything else—take the war, take the killing, take—"

***Those are not memories of belonging. Those are memories of surviving. I cannot anchor myself to survival.*** And there was something in the god's voice that might have been apology. ***It must be home, little wolf. It must be the valley. The door. The small years. Everything you built after you crawled out of the war. That is the belonging you made with your own hands, and it is the only thing in this vault freely given.***

Emrys looked at Wren.

The child's eyes were open now, and they were not a child's eyes—they were dawn-colored, horizon-wide, ancient and brand-new at once. But behind the vastness, Wren was still in there, and Wren was crying, and Wren shook their head.

"Don't," Wren whispered, in the common tongue, in their own small voice, the last time they would ever use it. "Don't do it. I'll find another way. I'll stay asleep. I don't mind sleeping, Emrys, I don't mind—"

"You minded," Emrys said. "You told me on the road. You said being forgotten was the loneliest thing there is." They laughed, wet and cracked. "You little thief. You stole honey. You hummed off-key. You asked me why the moon followed us and I told you it didn't and you said *then why does it feel like it does.*"

Wren sobbed once, a small human sound in the middle of all that light.

"That's mine," Emrys said. "That's the one. Give me a minute with it first."

And the god—merciful, even now, even chained—went quiet, and let them have it.

Emrys sat down on the cold stone, sword across their knees, and held the memory up to the light one last time. The cottage with the crooked door they'd never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting they were staying. The bees. Maren scolding them for tracking mud. The first morning Wren slept by the hearth and Emrys had sat awake all night just listening to them breathe, terrified and certain, certain and terrified, thinking: *oh. Oh, this is what I almost let the war take from me without my ever knowing it existed.*

Ten years of hollow routine, and underneath it, the whole time, this. Home. Not a place. A door left unlatched.

"Take care of them," Emrys said to the god. "Whoever they come back as. Take care of them, or I'll haunt you, and I'm very good at haunting."

***Little wolf,*** said Ossa, and the tenderness in it nearly undid them, ***when I am risen, I will remember for both of us.***

"Yeah, well." Emrys wiped their face with a bloody sleeve. "You were always a bad liar."

They closed their eyes.

They gave it up the way you give up a rope over a long drop—hand over hand, unable to stop, feeling each finger loosen. The valley went first, the green walls of it, the magic-dead hush. Then the patrol paths, the fence-mending, the taste of well water. Then the cottage—the crooked door, the hearthstone, the smell of bread. Then Wren's arrival, the knock at dusk, the storm, the mud-caked child speaking words out of a dead language—

—and then the middle years simply weren't there anymore, and Emrys reached for them and found smooth blank stone where a life had been, and the last thing to go was not a scene but a feeling, warm and wordless, the feeling of *why*, the reason under everything, and they held onto it with both hands right up until they couldn't tell anyone, least of all themselves, what it had been.

The light went out like a candle pinched between fingers.

* * *

Silence.

Then stone grinding against stone, deep below, deep everywhere—the chains of script unwinding at last, ten centuries of seal dissolving into ordinary darkness. The vault's air changed, the way air changes before rain.

Emrys knelt on the floor of a place they did not recognize, holding a sword they understood but could not say why, bleeding from wounds they could not account for, beside a scorch-mark shaped like a kneeling child.

There was a man on a balcony above them, white and gold, backing away, shouting orders to guards who were already running.

There was a presence filling the vault like water fills a cup—vast, waking, *glad*—and it looked at Emrys with something that felt unbearably like love, and Emrys felt nothing at all.

Something precious had been paid. They knew that the way you know a missing tooth—with the tongue, again and again, finding only absence. They searched themselves for grief and found a shape where grief should be, an outline, a door-shaped hole in the middle of their chest with nothing behind it.

"Why," Emrys said aloud, to the god, to the dark, to no one. "Why does it feel like I lost something?"

The presence gathered itself, rising through the citadel's bones toward the war waiting overhead, and paused—one moment, one impossible moment of attention, tender as a hand on the crown of the head.

***Because you did, little wolf. Come and see what it bought.***

Above them, muffled through a thousand tons of stone, the first horn of Vaelric's army sounded—and faltered, mid-note, as the god it had been built to bury opened its eyes.
