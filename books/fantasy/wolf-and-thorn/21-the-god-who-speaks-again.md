## 21. The God Who Speaks Again

The stairs out of the vault were longer going up than they had been coming down.

Emrys knew this was impossible. They had counted the steps on the way in—one hundred and forty, each one slick with the dark of ten sealed years—and now there were one hundred and forty again, no more, and still their legs insisted otherwise. Legs were like that after you held a doorway against thirty armed men with nothing but a borrowed spear and a god's worth of stubbornness. Legs kept their own ledgers.

They climbed anyway. One step. Another. The stone beneath their boots had begun to hum.

Not a sound, exactly. More a *rightness*, as if the citadel of Vaelric the Usurper had spent its whole life holding its breath and had finally—*finally*—been given permission to exhale. The hum climbed through Emrys's bones and settled somewhere behind the ribs, warm as a hearth they couldn't remember lighting.

*Come and see what it bought,* Ossa had said. And so Emrys climbed, to see.

At the top of the stair, a door stood open onto night sky and firelight, and Emrys stepped through into a world that had stopped making sense in all the best ways.

---

The feast-hall of the citadel was a shambles, but not from battle. It was a shambles the way a church is a shambles when everyone in it has fallen to their knees at once. Tables overturned. Wine spreading across flagstones like something spilled from a broken vessel. Hundreds of Vaelric's officers and lords and hangers-on down on the stones with their hands over their ears—or over their hearts, which amounted to the same thing—because the voice that had just spoken through every wall of the fortress was a voice their grandfathers had been taught did not exist.

Ossa was speaking.

Emrys could not hear it the way the others heard it. That was the first wrongness, though it took them a long moment to name it. They stood in the doorway watching a hall full of people weep and clutch and murmur *the god, the god, the god walks again*, and inside their own chest there was only the hum, wordless, like a song heard through three walls and a lifetime.

They should have felt it. They had been bound to that voice since they were nineteen years old and stupid enough to kneel in a shrine and mean it. Every mercenary in the old companies used to joke about what it was like—the god's word arriving in your skull like a hand on your shoulder, familiar as your own name. Emrys had carried that voice through eleven battles and out the other side of the worst day of the war, and then carried its silence for ten years after.

And now the silence was broken, the voice was back, filling the world—and Emrys stood in the middle of it hearing nothing but an echo of warmth.

*Later,* they told themselves, because there was a war happening above them and grief is a luxury that keeps until morning. There would be time, later, to stand very still and work out exactly what was missing. Later.

They picked up the spear they'd dropped by the door and went looking for the fighting.

---

There wasn't any.

That was the second wrongness, and honestly the more disorienting of the two. Emrys came up the last spiral stair expecting carnage—the eve-of-war feast had held four thousand soldiers, and four thousand soldiers do not simply stop—but the ramparts were full of men standing very still, staring east, and the plain beyond the walls was full of more of them doing the same.

Vaelric's great host had come to besiege the free cities. Emrys had seen the musters on the road: the ash-villages stripped of sons, the supply trains miles long, the banners sewn with the usurper's thorn-and-crown. They had marched out of the citadel's shadow that evening in perfect order, forty thousand strong, torches like a river of embers flowing toward the lowlands.

Now the river had stopped.

Torches stood motionless across the whole dark plain, as far as sight reached. Men had set down their arms—not thrown them, *set* them, carefully, the way you put down something you're not sure belongs to you anymore. Here and there small knots of soldiers knelt in the trampled grass. Elsewhere others simply wandered, dazed, between the tents, asking questions of anyone who'd answer: *did you hear it? Did you hear her? Is it true what she's saying?*

Because Ossa wasn't just awake. Ossa was *talking*. To all of them. At once. And whatever the god was saying, it was not a battle cry.

Emrys leaned against the parapet and listened to forty thousand people fail to have a war.

A young officer near the stairwell—a boy, really, sixteen if that, with a captain's sash too big for him—turned and saw Emrys standing there bloody and gray-faced with a spear, and his hand went to his sword, and then didn't finish the journey. He stared. Something moved behind his eyes, some inherited thing waking up.

"You," he said slowly. "You were below."

"I was below."

"The god came up through here." His voice had gone strange, reverent, half-frightened. "Through the stone. Through *us*. I felt—" He pressed a fist to his sternum, hunting for words. "I felt like I'd been wearing someone else's coat my whole life and she just told me whose it was." He looked out at the motionless plain. "The Lord Protector said the old faith was a lie. Said the gods died before our grandsires were born, that's why the throne had to take their place. But she's—" His throat worked. "She says the throne buried her. She says it *by name*. She remembers every seal. She remembers who laid them."

"Dozens of generations of legitimacy," Emrys said quietly, "unraveling in an evening."

The boy looked at them—at the blood, the scars, the thousand-yard stillness of a soldier between fights—and something in his face decided something. He unslung the too-big sash and held it out.

"I don't know who you are," he said, "but you smell like the bottom of the world, and the bottom of the world is where she was. So. If the cities send anyone to accept surrender, tell them we'll be here." A wet, astonished almost-laugh. "Where else would we go?"

Emrys took the sash because refusing seemed crueler than taking it. The boy saluted—clumsily, wrongly, some half-remembered form from a drill manual—and turned back to watch the plain, where here and there the torches were beginning, one by one, to gutter out as their bearers forgot why they'd ever been lit.

---

Dawn found Emrys in the courtyard, because sleep was a country they no longer had papers for.

The free cities' forces arrived at midmorning, or rather, they *arrived at a fortress that was no longer defended* and spent several confused hours trying to work out how to assault it politely. Emrys watched from the wall as the Corvane banners came up the road—the same banners they'd run under three nights ago, over rooftops and canals, with inquisitors' crossbow bolts singing past their ears—and felt precisely nothing about any of it. Not pride, not relief, not even the ordinary animal satisfaction of having survived. Just observation. Just the hum behind the ribs, and the ledger in their chest with a line item they couldn't read.

Maren found them there around noon. The hedge-witch had crossed half the valley and most of two provinces to get here, and she looked it: road-dust to the knees, gray hair escaping its braid, walking stick worn shiny at the grip. She planted herself beside Emrys on the wall and studied the plain for a while, where the surrender was organizing itself into something almost festive. Someone down there had started a cookfire. Two armies sharing soup. The world gone soft in the head overnight.

"You look terrible," Maren said.

"Thank you. I practiced."

"No, I mean—" She turned, and her eyes did the thing they always did, the seeing-through-things thing that had made Emrys avoid her for six years and trust her absolutely for the last month. "You look *absent*. Like the house is standing but nobody's home." Her gaze sharpened. "What did you give it?"

"Give what."

"Don't." Gently. "Don't do that. You held that door with your whole self, I saw the aftermath, and gods don't wake for free. Especially not *this* god, and especially not from *you*." She paused. "What's the name of the valley?"

"The—" Emrys opened their mouth. Closed it. Reached for the answer the way you reach for a stair in the dark and find nothing there. "It has a name. Thorn Valley. Everyone calls it—" But even as they said it they could feel the shape of the knowledge: correct, complete, and utterly empty. They knew the valley had a name the way you know a stranger's hometown. "Thorn Valley," they repeated, and heard how flat it landed.

"And the cottage. Describe the cottage."

"Stone. Low roof. A—" Nothing. A blank where a decade of mornings should have been. The kettle they'd drunk from every day for ten years. The chair by the window. The notch in the doorframe. All of it gone, and worse than gone: *unmourned*, because you cannot mourn a thing you cannot remember losing. Emrys stood very still on the wall and let that fact arrive, all of it, like cold water finding every seam.

"Oh," they said.

Maren took their arm. For once she didn't say anything wise at all, which was the wisest thing available.

"There was a child," Emrys said, after a while. Their own voice sounded foreign. "I remember there was a child. I remember that remembering matters, somehow. I wrote it on my own arm so I wouldn't forget that I've forgotten." They pushed back the sleeve. The ink had smeared with blood and sweat, but it was legible: **WREN. YOU FOUGHT FOR WREN. DO NOT LET ANYONE TELL YOU OTHERWISE.**

"Well," said Maren, thickly, "then you fought for Wren."

"That's all I know about it." Emrys laughed, or made the noise that stands next to laughing when laughing isn't home. "Ten years of my life, Maren. Gone. And I'm standing in the middle of the victory it bought, and I feel like a man reading someone else's letter. Everyone down there is weeping with joy and I keep checking myself for grief like a man patting his pockets for keys. I know something precious was paid. I can feel the *shape* of the hole. I just—" Their jaw tightened. "I can't miss what I can't remember."

Maren squeezed their arm once, hard, and let go.

"You will," she said. "Not the memories. Those are spent; even gods can't counterfeit those. But belonging isn't a memory, mercenary. It's a habit. And habits survive a lot." She nodded down at the courtyard, where the boy with the too-big sash was directing the distribution of soup with the desperate competence of someone who'd never fed anyone in his life. "Besides. You left a door open somewhere. People like us always do."

"What people?"

"People who leave doors open without knowing why." She started down the stair, leaning hard on her stick. "Come on. The cities will want statements, and you're the only person alive who was under the world when she woke. Try to look heroic. Or at least vertical."

---

Evening again. The citadel's great bells rang for the first time in living memory—not alarm, just *ringing*, because apparently when a god wakes the first thing mortals want is noise that means something good.

Emrys stood alone on the eastern wall, above the plain where forty thousand former invaders were becoming, tent by tent, merely people. Somewhere below, Ossa's presence moved through the celebrating city like weather, and Emrys felt it pass through them too—that wordless warmth, the hum, the echo of a voice they'd once known better than their own heartbeat.

*Little wolf,* the warmth seemed to say, though it said nothing at all. *Come and see.*

*I see it,* Emrys thought back, and got no answer, and understood finally that they never would again—not as they once had. The bond was spent along with everything else. What remained was what remained for everyone: a god in the world, and a mortal standing under the sky.

It should have hurt more, they thought distantly. Instead it just felt like the space where hurting used to live.

They turned to go down—to statements, to soup, to the long dull holy work of building peace—and stopped.

On the road below, at the edge of the torchlight, someone small was standing very still, looking up at the wall. Looking, Emrys realized with a jolt that had no memory attached to it and hit like a hammer anyway, directly at *them*.

A slight figure. Mud-colored travel clothes. Hair the color of river-stone catching the light. And faintly, unmistakably, around the edges of the small shape, a glow—fading now, dimming hour by hour as whatever had lived inside it went home—but still there, still burning, like a coal refusing the dark.

Emrys's hand rose to their arm. To the smudged ink. *WREN. YOU FOUGHT FOR WREN.*

The figure on the road tilted its head. Even at this distance, even across the whole gulf of everything forgotten, Emrys could feel the question in it—the way you can feel someone deciding whether to knock.

They didn't remember this person. They didn't remember anything.

But their feet were already moving toward the stairs, and their hand was already reaching back, without asking permission, to unlatch a door that was, apparently, always unlatched.
