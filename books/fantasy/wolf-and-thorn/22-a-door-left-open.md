## 22. A Door Left Open

Wren had been a god for exactly eleven days, and it was the worst job they had ever had.

Not that they'd held many jobs. There had been the being-a-fragment job, which mostly involved hiding inside a child's body and trying not to glow during thunderstorms. And there had been the dying job, which was brief and bright and mostly felt like being poured out of oneself like water from a cup. But this — being reborn in mortal flesh beside an awakened god who was now very large and very loud and terribly, *terribly* behind on everything —

"You're sulking," said Ossa.

"I'm not sulking. Gods don't sulk."

"You were a fragment of me for a thousand years. You are technically sulking at yourself."

Wren pulled their knees up tighter on the hillside. The grass here was the deep green of the lowlands, wet with morning, and below them Ossa's new temple — still more scaffolding than temple, honestly — buzzed with pilgrims and stonemasons and one extremely enthusiastic choir that had already been asked twice to stop practicing before dawn. Somewhere under all that noise, the god's prison vault sat empty, its seals broken, its long dark sentence finally over.

It should have felt like triumph. Everyone kept telling Wren it did.

"Where is Emrys?" they asked.

Ossa was quiet for a moment. When gods go quiet, ordinary people find it awe-inspiring. Wren found it annoying, because they knew exactly what the silence meant.

"You know where Emrys is," Wren said.

"The valley," Ossa admitted.

"They didn't come to the waking."

"No."

"They gave—" Wren's throat tightened around the words. "They gave everything. Every memory. The valley, the cottage, the war, the *reason*. Me." They had watched it happen from inside the dissolution, felt Emrys's hand steady on their shoulder while everything Emrys was got poured into the seal like lamp oil into a flame. "And now they're sitting alone in a hut at the edge of Thorn Valley, and they don't remember why any of it mattered."

"They remember how to survive," Ossa said gently. "That's what I could give back. Instincts live deeper than memory. But the rest—" The god sighed, and the wind moved through the valley below as if sighing with him. "A memory of belonging was the anchor. That was always the price. They paid it without hesitation. I have spent eleven days trying to decide whether that makes me grateful or ashamed, and I have concluded it makes me both."

"Then we go get them back."

"Wren—"

"Not the memories. I know we can't do the memories. Maren explained it, and Sefa died for it, and you've explained it, and I've thrown things about it." Wren stood up, brushing grass off their knees — their knees, their own small mortal knees, which ached when they crouched too long and which they loved fiercely for exactly that reason. "But nobody ever said we couldn't make *new* ones."

* * *

The journey took nine days, because Wren's new body was eight years old and had legs to match, and because Ossa insisted on walking with them as far as the pilgrimage road before turning back toward the cities, where a god newly awake apparently had appointments. "Legitimacy," he'd said, "does not maintain itself," and then he'd looked so tired that Wren hugged his enormous shadowy knee until he laughed.

The last stretch Wren walked alone.

Well. Alone except for the sparrow.

The sparrow had joined them outside Corvane, a small brown thing with one crooked wing feather, and it had refused every attempt at being shooed away. Wren suspected — strongly — that it was one of Ossa's eyes, because the god had never learned to let go of anything either, and because when Wren talked to it, it tilted its head in a way that was frankly judgmental.

"Don't look at me like that," Wren told it, cresting the last ridge as the sun went down. "You gave up a whole divinity once too. Probably. I don't actually know your theology."

Below them lay Thorn Valley, gray-green and magic-dead and exactly as silent as Wren remembered. Except — no. Not exactly. The silence felt different now. Before, it had been a lid. Now it was just a valley that happened to be quiet, the way some people are quiet, the way some mornings are quiet. Not a cage. A resting place.

The cottage sat where it had always sat, small and smoke-stained, its garden half-gone to weeds.

And the door was open.

Not broken open, not sagging off hinges. Just — unlatched. Swung a hand's width inward, letting out a thread of firelight, the way a door stands when someone inside expects company and isn't going to admit it.

Wren's heart did something complicated.

They went down through the bracken, quiet as they'd learned to be in another life, and stopped at the threshold, and looked in.

Emrys sat at the table with a whetstone and a knife, working the blade in slow, even strokes. Ten years of exile hadn't softened them; if anything, they looked carved rather than lived-in now, all edges and stillness, their hair gone silver at the temples though Wren would have sworn only weeks had passed since the vault. A bowl of stew cooled untouched beside them. Two bowls' worth of steam rose from the pot on the fire.

Two bowls.

Wren stared at the second bowl until their eyes burned.

Emrys hadn't looked up. "Whatever you're selling," they said, voice flat as valley stone, "I don't want it. Whatever you're fleeing, keep moving. And if you're one of Vaelric's—"

"It's me."

The whetstone stopped.

For a long moment there was no sound at all, not even the fire, and Wren understood suddenly what it must be like to be Emrys: to have a stranger say two words and feel your whole chest try to answer a question your mind has lost the shape of.

Then Emrys turned, and their eyes — gray, wary, a soldier's eyes — found Wren standing in the doorway, small and mud-splattered and crying without meaning to.

"I don't know you," Emrys said slowly.

"I know," Wren said.

"But my hands want to put down the knife." Emrys looked at their own hands as if betrayed by them. "And there are two bowls. I made two bowls. I don't make two bowls. I have made one bowl every night for months and tonight I made two and I don't know *why*."

"Because some things outlast memory," Wren said. "Maren says so. She also says you owe her forty coppers and a jar of honey, by the way. She was very specific about the honey."

Something flickered across Emrys's face — not recognition, nothing so kind, but the ghost of a reflex, like a scar itching in old weather. "Hedge-witch at the border. Talks too much. Charges double." A pause. "She's alive?"

"She's alive. She's furious. She's been feeding rumors about you to anyone who'll listen — mostly flattering ones, which worried everyone considerably."

To Wren's astonishment, the corner of Emrys's mouth twitched. It wasn't quite a smile. It was the foundation a smile might someday be built on, and after everything, Wren decided it was the most beautiful thing they had ever seen.

"You talk like someone who knows me," Emrys said.

"You talk like someone I know," Wren corrected. "That's worse for you, probably."

* * *

Emrys fed them stew, because whatever else had been burned away, hospitality had sunk too deep into the bones to burn. They ate in a silence that was awkward but not hostile, the fire popping, rain starting against the roof — the first rain, Wren noted, since they'd arrived, and somehow the valley seemed to approve of the visit.

"You came out of the storm once before," Emrys said abruptly. "Didn't you. I don't remember it. But I remember *not* remembering, if that makes sense. There's a hole in me shaped like a knock at a door."

Wren set down their spoon. "Do you want me to tell you?"

Emrys was quiet a long time. Outside, the rain thickened.

"Maren comes by sometimes," they said at last. "She won't tell me anything. Says it wouldn't hold, says grief needs somewhere to sit and mine's just a bare floor." Their jaw tightened. "I hate it. I hate having a hole and not even knowing what fell out of it. Some nights I wake up reaching for someone who isn't there and I don't know who. Some nights I dream about a battle and a voice going silent mid-word, and I wake up with tears on my face for a god whose name I can't recall." They looked up. "So yes. Tell me. Even if it won't hold. I'd rather have it slip through my fingers than never touch it at all."

So Wren told them.

All of it. The storm and the mud and the First Tongue spilling out of a starving child. The liturgies tested by firelight. The riders on the ridgeline, and the lie Emrys told — the first lie in ten years — and the way their face had looked afterward, sick and clear and certain. Maren's warning. The three men at the door, and the blood, and the way Wren had watched from the woodpile and understood that monsters and guardians wear the same face depending on where you stand. The boundary crossing, and the light pouring out of Wren's skin like dawn through shutters. The ash-road villages. Sefa, and her knife, and her terrible beautiful choice. The archive under Corvane. The truth: *imprisoned, not silent.* The road. The feast. The vault.

And the end. Emrys's hand on Wren's shoulder. The memories rising out of them like heat-shimmer. The words Emrys spoke into the breaking seal — *take it, take all of it, take the valley and the home and the reason, take why I fought, take why I stayed, take everything, just let them wake* —

By the end, Wren was sobbing again, and Emrys sat very still across the table, and the fire had burned low, and neither of them noticed.

"That person," Emrys said quietly, when Wren had run out of words. "The one who did all that. I don't remember being them. But I think—" They flexed their fingers, the way Wren had seen old soldiers do when an old wound spoke up. "I think I'd have liked them."

"You'd have loved them," Wren said. "Everyone did. Eventually. After the scowling."

"There was scowling?"

"So much scowling."

"Hm." Emrys almost smiled again. Progress. Then their face sobered. "The god. Ossa. Is he—"

"Awake. Loud. Terrible at delegating. He cried at the temple dedication and pretended it was dust." Wren hesitated. "He wants to see you. He says — he says a debt like yours doesn't get repaid, only honored. He says you're welcome at his hearth for as long as you live, and after, if you're stubborn enough to keep going."

Emrys looked into the fire for a long time.

"And the child," they said finally. "The one from the storm. Did they—"

Wren slid off the bench, crossed the small room, and climbed onto the bench beside Emrys, the way they had on a hundred nights that Emrys no longer owned. Up close, the soldier smelled of woodsmoke and whetstone and the same soap they'd used forever, because bodies keep habits even when hearts lose histories.

"They made it," Wren said. "They're right here."

Emrys went very still. Their eyes searched Wren's face — the mud, the tears, the small stubborn chin — hunting for something they couldn't name and finding it anyway, the way a compass finds north without understanding magnetism.

"I don't remember you," Emrys whispered.

"You will," Wren said. "Or you won't, and I'll remember for both of us. That's the deal. That's always been the deal." They leaned their head against Emrys's arm, deliberately, boldly, the way you lean on something you've decided belongs to you. "You left the door unlatched."

"I told you. I don't know why."

"You do, though. Down deep, where the bowls live. Where the instincts live. You knew someone was coming home." Wren closed their eyes, listening to Emrys's heartbeat, steady as a drumbeat marching out of a war. "Some things outlast memory, Emrys. Love is one of them. It goes all the way down, past the forgetting, all the way to the bone. You can't dig it out. Believe me. People tried."

* * *

They stayed the night, both of them, in the little cottage with its two bowls and its unlatched door, and in the morning Wren woke to find Emrys already up, standing in the garden pulling weeds with the grim efficiency of someone laying siege to a fortress.

"I'm coming with you," Emrys announced, without turning around.

Wren blinked sleep out of their eyes. "You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." Emrys yanked a weed free and glared at it. "I'm choosing to. I woke up at dawn with the feeling that if I stayed in this valley one more day I'd rot, and I have learned over these past months to trust the things I feel without reasons for. So." They stood, brushing dirt off their hands, and fixed Wren with a look that had clearly ended careers and possibly small wars. "There's a god who owes me a conversation, a hedge-witch who owes me forty coppers — no wait, I owe her, which is somehow worse — and a city full of people building something. I don't remember any of it. I'm going anyway."

Wren grinned so hard their face hurt.

They were halfway down the valley path, the sparrow wheeling overhead and the cottage door left — of course — unlatched behind them, when Emrys spoke again, quieter.

"The child. The one in your story. In the vault, at the end. What happened to them? Your telling skipped it."

Wren looked up at the gray morning sky, at the light just beginning to spill over the ridgeline, gold and ordinary and free.

"They grew up," Wren said. "Ask them yourself sometime. They're very fond of scowling soldiers."

And far ahead, past the valley's edge where the world turned green and singing, the road waited — long, unwritten, and wide enough for two.

* * *

*The end.*
