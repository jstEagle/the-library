## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink was still wet on the last border when Bram Hartwell realized she had drawn the valley wrong.

Not wrong in any way a surveyor would fault her for. The rivers ran where rivers ran. The mountains stood where mountains stood, their western faces hatched with careful shadow, their eastern slopes left pale because she'd never seen them from above and refused to pretend otherwise. The village of Hollowmere sat precisely where it had always sat, its crooked lanes rendered in the fine, patient line-work that had made her name — such as it was — among the handful of people who ever needed maps at all.

No, the error was smaller than that, and larger. She had drawn the valley as if someone might use the map to leave it.

Bram sat back in her chair and looked at what she'd made: three weeks of work, a commission from the Council of Elders, intended for the archive beneath the meeting hall where documents went when no one needed them anymore. *A Complete and True Survey of the Vale of Hollowmere and Its Surrounding Heights,* the title cartouche read, in letters she had gilded herself. And there, along the southern edge where the river Thistle narrowed into the gorge called the Needle's Eye, she had drawn the road continuing — a confident double line, mile-marked, running out of the valley toward whatever lay beyond the mountains that ringed them like a closed hand.

There was no road there. There hadn't been one in living memory. The gorge was passable only on foot, and no one passed it, because —

Because of the rule. Because of the thing everyone knew and no one said aloud, the way you don't say aloud that the sky is blue or that bread needs yeast. *Magic cannot cross our borders.* The words weren't written anywhere. They didn't need to be. A child in Hollowmere learned them the way they learned to tie their shoes, absorbed without instruction, and by adulthood the knowledge had sunk so deep it no longer felt like knowledge at all. It felt like gravity.

What it meant, in practice, was this: nothing strange came in, and no one strange went out. Crops grew honest and slow. Weather behaved within reason. The sick recovered or died according to nature's old arithmetic, and no midwife in the vale had ever delivered a child with too many fingers or eyes the color of storm-light. Once, generations ago — so the oldest stories went — things had been otherwise. Wonders had walked the high passes. But the founders had chosen this valley precisely because it was forgettable, and the valley had honored their choice by forgetting everything else.

Including, Bram sometimes thought, the way out.

She dipped her pen, hovered it over the false road, and did not correct it.

It was a small rebellion. No one would ever see the map; it would go into the dark below the meeting hall and sleep there until paper-eating beetles made a meal of her careful coastlines. But she left the road anyway, all thirty miles of invented highway, winding up out of the gorge and over the shoulder of the world. On the original — she always kept an original — she would draw it true. But somewhere, on this one copy meant for no one's eyes, the valley would have a door.

"You're muttering again," came a voice through the open shutter of her workroom window, followed by a face: Mrs. Tamlin from the bakery two doors down, sixty years old and built like a flour sack, peering in with the frank entitlement of long acquaintance. "And you've got ink on your chin."

"I'm working."

"Working is just muttering with tools." Her eyes found the map and softened, the way everyone's did. "Beautiful. Your mother would've been proud. She drew lovely too, before — well." Before she died. Before your father followed her inside the year. Before you were alone at twenty-six in a house full of paper and silence. Mrs. Tamlin didn't say any of it; she didn't have to. In Hollowmere, grief was a communal garment and everyone insisted on helping you wear it. "Supper's on me tonight. Mutton pie. Don't argue."

"Thank you," Bram said, because arguing took longer.

The face withdrew. The street sounds resumed: cart wheels, a dog, children shrieking at some game whose rules changed hourly. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Sounds that had once been enough and now, lately, were not.

Bram touched her chin, found the ink, and wiped it absently against her sleeve, adding one more stain to a garment already constellated with them. Then she looked at the map again, at her secret road escaping south, and let herself feel the thing she tried to feel only in small doses, rationed like medicine:

*Want.*

She wanted — and the wanting rose in her like water finding a crack — she wanted to walk that road. Not because Hollowmere was cruel. It wasn't. It was kind in the suffocating way of places that love you by knowing everything about you. She wanted to leave because she had finished this valley. She had mapped every stream, counted every ridge, interviewed every shepherd and charcoal-burner about the high country she couldn't climb. There was nothing left in it that her eye could discover. The world beyond the mountains was, for her, a blank page — and Bram Hartwell had built her whole life around the conviction that a blank page was not empty but *waiting.*

Except the page could never be turned. Magic could not cross the borders, which meant wonder could not come in — and apparently neither could curiosity go out, because in four hundred years, no one who climbed toward the passes had ever come back with a story worth the walking. Either they turned back, or the mountains simply declined to be crossed, folding the trails behind them like a hand closing.

She had tested it once, at seventeen. Three days up the western scree, alone, telling her aunt — no. Not her aunt. Telling *no one*, because her parents were dead by then and her guardian, her mother's sister Vessa, had gone off years before that on one of her own restless pilgrimages and never returned, sending word twice a decade at most. Bram had gotten as far as a saddle between two peaks where the air went thin and strange, and had stood looking north at range after range after range, blue fading into blue, a world stacked to the horizon — and then a fog had come up out of nowhere, soft and total, and turned her gently around like a shepherd turning a strayed lamb. She'd walked down into the valley without deciding to. By the time she reached Hollowmere she could no longer remember why she'd gone up.

That was the valley's real magic, she thought now, cleaning her pens one by one in the failing light. Not wards or walls. *Forgetting.* It forgot you, and taught you to forget it right back.

She blew out the lamp. Outside, the evening had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had picked up — properly picked up, the kind of wind that came maybe twice a year, moaning down the chimneys and setting the shutters talking. Rain arrived in fat, deliberate drops. Somewhere a shutter banged its applause.

* * *

The storm spent itself against the house all night like something demanding entry.

Bram worked late anyway — lamplight and rain-sound were her favorite company, the only combination under which difficult coastlines surrendered willingly — and she was still awake past midnight, copying the true version of the valley onto fresh stock, when the knock came.

Three knocks. Weak ones. The third barely landed at all.

She froze, pen lifted. No one knocked at midnight in Hollowmere. No one *arrived* at midnight in Hollowmere; arrivals happened by daylight, on market days, announced by a week's gossip beforehand. Visitors were practically a civic event.

The knock came again, and this time she heard what lay underneath it: not insistence but *collapse*. Someone leaning on the door. Someone holding themselves upright by will and doorframe.

Bram set down the pen. Every sensible voice she owned told her to wait for morning, to listen through the wood, to fetch Mr. Aldous the reeve. But another voice — the one that kept drawing roads off the edge of maps — said: *a stranger. At night. In a storm. In a valley where strangers do not happen.*

She opened the door.

The woman fell forward into the doorway and Bram caught her — caught an armful of soaked wool and cold skin and blood, blood slicking her palms before she understood where it was coming from. The stranger was perhaps fifty, tall, gray-haired, with a face weathered by distances Bram had only imagined and eyes that even now, even half-shuttered with pain, were the sharpest things in the room. Her left side was soaked dark from ribs to hip. A wound — a bad one, a *wrong* one, though Bram couldn't have said how she knew. Something about the edges of it.

"Water," Bram managed. "I'll — sit down, sit—"

"No time." The grip on Bram's wrist was astonishingly strong. Those sharp eyes fixed on her face and widened slightly, as if confirming something long suspected, and the woman almost smiled. "You look like her. Good. Good — that saves time I don't have."

"Like who? Who are you?"

"My name doesn't matter anymore." She swayed; Bram braced her toward the chair by the fire, but the woman shook her head, refusing it, staying upright by fury alone. "Listen. The valley — you live in the valley. You don't know what it's for. None of them do. It isn't safety, girl. It's a *hiding place.*"

The fire popped. Rain hammered the roof. Bram heard her own heartbeat.

"There's a war," the woman said. "Older than your valley, older than its forgetting. Two thrones, both burned to ash, and the peace between them held together by one thread — the last fire. And that fire is dying." Her breath hitched; her hand pressed harder against her ruined side. "They'll come looking. Both sides. Looking for anyone who can feed it — or anyone they can use to snuff it out. And there is exactly one person alive who can do either."

The room tilted. Bram heard herself say, stupidly: "You need a surgeon. There's a man in Marrow Lane—"

"He can't help what kills me." The woman fumbled at her cloak, at a inner pocket, and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with handling. Even before it touched Bram's hands, she felt it — a faint warmth through the wet wool, like a bird's heartbeat. "Then take this. Take it and run. Out of the valley, north, follow it — it knows the way better than you ever will—"

"What is it?"

The woman pressed the bundle into her hands and folded Bram's fingers over it, closing them like a coffin lid. Up close, her eyes were extraordinary — amber shot through with gold, the pupils narrowing briefly in the firelight into something vertical, something *not human* — and then widening again, and Bram would spend many nights afterward convincing herself she'd imagined it.

"It's a map," the woman whispered. "Of course it's a map. You're a mapmaker. Fate has a sense of humor." Blood flecked her lips now. She was sliding down despite everything, and Bram caught her, eased her to the floorboards, knelt in the spreading dark. "Don't let them have it. Don't let either of them have *you.* Find the last fire. Find—"

Her gaze drifted past Bram's shoulder, toward the window, the storm, the north.

"—find my niece," she finished, very softly. "Tell her I'm sorry I missed her growing up. Tell her the valley was the wrong cage. Tell her—"

But the sentence never found its ending. The sharp eyes lost their focus, and the strong hand went slack in Bram's, and the storm filled the silence with rain.

Outside, thunder rolled down off the mountains — from the north, though the storm had come from the south. And in Bram Hartwell's closed fists, warm as a living thing, the wrapped bundle pulsed once, twice, like a second heart learning hers.
