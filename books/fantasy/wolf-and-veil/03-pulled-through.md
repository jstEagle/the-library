## 3. Pulled Through

The pen did not touch the paper.

Bram had meant it to. She had sat down at her worktable with the lamp trimmed low and the stranger's map spread flat beneath her palm, and she had meant to do the simplest, sanest thing a cartographer could do to a document that moved: she had meant to draw on it. One line, her own hand, her own ink — a border, perhaps, or an X in the corner of that pulsing eastern route, something small and stubborn and *hers* to say *no* to the thing that wanted to lead her somewhere.

But the nib hovered a hair's breadth above the vellum, and the vellum breathed.

She felt it against her skin — not wind, not warmth exactly, but a pressure, like standing at the mouth of a cave where something vast exhales. The route eastward darkened as she watched, ink welling up from within the fibers as though the map were bleeding from underneath, and Bram Hartwell, who had spent twenty-six years drawing lines that stayed where she put them, felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"No," she said aloud, to the map, to the storm rattling her shutters, to herself. "You don't get to tell me where to go."

The lamp guttered.

That was the first sign, though she did not know it yet — the flame bending sideways, toward the map, like iron filings toward a lodestone. The second sign was the smell: hot stone and rain-on-ash, the same scent that had clung to the dying stranger's coat when Bram had dragged her in from the flood two nights ago. The third sign was that Bram could no longer lift the pen away.

Her hand simply would not come up.

The fingers holding the nib pressed down instead, trembling, dragged by nothing visible across the vellum until the point touched the beginning of the eastern route — and the ink took her.

Not her ink. Not her line. The map drank the touch of metal and woke all along its length; rivers she had not drawn began to run, actual water glinting in the lamplight, actual current pulling at the nib as if it were a boat's keel. The mountains rose by increments she could measure against her thumbnail. And the route east — the route drawn *for her* — flared white-hot beneath her fingertip, and the heat ran up her arm and into her chest and closed around her heart like a fist made of compass-points.

Bram screamed. She was distantly aware of that — aware of her own voice cracking against the rafters, aware of the chair going over backward, aware that she was being pulled forward across the table with a strength no living thing should have possessed, her knees slamming the wood, her free hand scrabbling for purchase among her own instruments, her dividers scattering like startled birds.

The last thing she saw of the valley — her valley, the valley that forgot, the valley no one left — was her own front door bursting inward under the storm's weight, and beyond it the border-hills dissolving into grey rain, and she thought, absurdly, with the clarity of the drowning: *I never even finished the commission.*

Then the table was gone. The lamp was gone. The floor was gone.

The map was not gone. The map was everywhere — vast beneath her, around her, unfolding upward past the sky itself, its rivers suddenly deep enough to drown in, its mountains tall enough to break against. Bram fell into Veyra the way a dropped stitch falls into cloth: swallowed whole, snagged, held.

* * *

She landed in moss.

It saved her life, probably — a drift of it knee-deep and springy at the base of a tree whose trunk would have taken six men linking hands to circle. She lay there for a long moment doing the arithmetic of survival: breathing, yes; bones, apparently intact; the map, still clenched in her right fist with such force that her nails had cut crescents into her own palm. Her pen was gone. Her boots were soaked. The air tasted green and old, like the inside of a church built before churches had names.

Above her, branches interlaced so thickly that the sky showed only in coins of gold light, drifting slowly, as if the sun were moving at the pace of someone reading.

"Right," Bram said, to the moss, because saying things aloud kept the screaming on the far side of her teeth. "Right."

She got up. Her legs held, mostly. She looked around.

The forest stretched in every direction, but it was not silent, and it was not empty, and it was — this was the part that made her breath go thin and high — it was *labeled*. Not with signs. With names. As she turned, slow and careful, words surfaced in the bark around her the way the map's routes had surfaced in ink: pale letters rising out of the grain like scars remembering how they'd been made. The great tree beside her bore, at the height of her shoulder, a single word worn smooth by centuries:

*HARTWELL.*

Bram stumbled back from it. "No." Her voice came out cracked. "No, that's — you don't know that name. No one outside the valley knows that name. I've never told anyone that name—"

Another tree answered her, forty feet off, its letters fresher, sharper: *THE STRANGER.* And beneath it, smaller, almost tender: *SHE CAME HOME TO DIE.*

"You're wrong," Bram whispered. "You're all wrong."

But the trees of Veyra did not lie, and some animal place deep in her — the same place that had known, watching the stranger bleed out on her hearthstones, that the woman's face was shaped like her mother's — understood perfectly well what they were telling her. They remembered names. They remembered everyone who passed. And they had been waiting for hers.

She ran.

It was not a decision; it was the body's ancient veto over the mind's objections. She crashed through ferns taller than herself, slipped on root-stairs, caught herself on trunks that whispered their labels under her scraping fingers — *KEALEN'S PATH, WORN BY TWENTY YEARS OF BOOTS* — wait, that one stopped her cold for half a heartbeat before terror shoved her onward again. Light came and went in shifting columns. Somewhere behind her, water laughed in a way water shouldn't. Somewhere ahead, everything was unknown, which after a lifetime of mapping every inch of a single valley was both the most frightening and — she hated herself for noticing — the most exhilarating thing she had ever seen.

Then the ground ended.

She skidded to the lip of a ravine, arms pinwheeling, pebbles ticking down into shadow. Across the gap the forest continued, indifferent. There was no bridge. There was no path down that she could see. And behind her, for the first time since she'd landed, she heard footsteps.

Not many. One set. Unhurried. The tread of someone who had never once needed to run from anything in these woods, because the woods were on his side.

Bram turned.

He stood between two labeled trees as if he'd grown there: a man in grey-green leathers gone soft and colorless with weather, a long knife already bare in his right hand — when had he drawn it? Had she even seen him move? His hair was dark, tied back carelessly, and his eyes were the exact amber of the coins of light overhead, and they were looking at her with an expression that made her blood try to leave her body through the soles of her feet.

It was not hatred. Hatred she might have survived. It was *procedure*. He looked at her the way she looked at a blank sheet of vellum: assessing what would need to be done, and finding it simple.

Something low and huge shifted in the shadows behind him, and resolved — slowly, terribly — into a wolf the size of a pony, grey as ash, gold-eyed, silent.

"Stop," Bram said.

The man tilted his head slightly, as though she had produced an interesting sound, the way a bird makes.

"I said stop." Terror had her voice now, and she let it, because fighting it took strength she needed elsewhere. "Whatever you think I am — whoever sent me — I didn't choose any of this. I was in my house. There was a storm. A dying woman gave me a map and I tried to burn it and it—" her fist tightened around the folded thing, and the man's eyes snapped to it, and everything about him changed.

It was a small change. That was the worst part. His weight settled back onto his heel; the knife came up from relaxed-low into a line aimed at her heart; and the wolf behind him flowed forward without a sound, flanking, patient, certain.

"The map," he said. His voice was quiet, roughened by disuse, like a door opened rarely. "Show me your hands."

"If I show you my hands you'll see the map, and then you'll kill me."

"Yes," he agreed, without cruelty, without apology, and the honesty of it was somehow worse than a lie. "So I'll ask only once more."

Bram Hartwell stood at the edge of a ravine in a forest that knew her name, with a knife pointed at her heart and a wolf crouched to leap, and every instinct she owned — the valley's instincts, the instincts of a woman raised where magic could not cross the borders and therefore could be pretended not to exist — every one of those instincts told her to beg. To cry. To give him whatever story would keep her breathing another minute.

Instead, absurdly, she thought of her father's oldest lesson, given over a drafting table while his big hands guided hers along a ruler: *A line is a promise, Bram. Draw it true, or don't draw it at all.*

She lifted her chin.

"No," she said.

The sentinel's eyes narrowed. "No?"

"No. You want to know what I carry? Fine. It's a map. It's a living, breathing, murderous map that dragged me through it against my will, and I have been in your forest for less time than it takes to boil water, and I am done — *done* — being pushed around by things that are bigger than me. Not the storm. Not the map." Her finger came up, shaking, and leveled at him, and she found to her astonishment that the shaking was anger now, clean and burning, the best fuel she'd ever handled. "*And not you.* So here is my counteroffer, knife-man: you can kill me, and learn nothing, and spend the rest of your life wondering whether the woman at the ravine's edge had answers you needed. Or you can lower that blade, and I will show you the map, and we will both find out together why your trees wrote my family name on their bark before I ever arrived."

The word hung between them: *before.*

She watched it land. Watched his gaze flick — just once, involuntary — to the tree at his shoulder, where letters had risen fresh and pale in the grain: *BRAM HARTWELL, WHO REFUSED.*

His knife did not lower. But it stopped advancing, and the wolf's ears swiveled forward, and the sentinel studied her for a long moment with the expression of a man re-reading a page he had been sure he'd memorized.

"You refused," he said at last. Slowly, as if tasting the shape of it. "The trees don't usually write that."

"Well," Bram said, and her voice only shook a little, and she counted that as a victory, "they clearly haven't met anyone from my valley."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of a door deciding whether to open. He whistled — two notes, low — and the wolf sat, though its golden eyes never left her, and its nose lifted once, twice, testing the air near her in a way that struck Bram as oddly deliberate. Almost confused.

"Kaelen," he said finally. Not a handshake. An offering, grudging, like a coin flipped across a table.

"Bram."

"I know." He nodded at the trees, at her name written on a hundred trunks down every direction she could see. "They told me before you did."

And then his eyes went, again, unwillingly, to the folded map crushed in her fist — and this time Bram saw what crossed his face before he buried it, and it was not procedure, and it was not hatred.

It was grief.

"Walk in front of me," Kaelen said quietly, "and pray your map doesn't open. Because if what you're carrying is what I think it is, girl, then the kindest thing these woods will ever do for you is the thing I nearly did just now."

The wolf rose, silent as falling snow, and fell in at Bram's flank — close enough that she could feel its breath, warm and strange, against her hand.

She walked.
