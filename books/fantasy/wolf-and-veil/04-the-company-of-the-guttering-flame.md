## 4. The Company of the Guttering Flame

The wolf smelled the stranger before Kaelen saw her again.

Ash had gone still at the tree line, ears forward, nose working the air with the particular intensity that meant *something is wrong and I am deciding how wrong*. Kaelen crouched beside him in the bracken, one hand resting on the ruff of his neck, and watched the woman he'd spared an hour ago pick her way down the deer trail toward the hollow where their fire burned.

She walked like someone who had never been hunted. That was the first thing he noticed, and it unsettled him more than the map-shaped bundle clutched to her chest. She stepped on dry branches without flinching. She looked up at the canopy instead of scanning the shadows for eyes. Whatever world she'd come from — and Kaelen had seen the way she'd stared at the trees that remembered names, at the moss that glowed faintly where the old roads ran — it was not a world that taught a person to be afraid of forests.

*You should have killed her,* said the voice in his head that sounded like his dead master, Garrick. *You should have put your knife in her throat the moment you saw what she carried.*

*I don't know what she carries,* he answered it, as he always did. *I know what she holds. Those aren't the same thing.*

Ash huffed, low, and Kaelen couldn't tell if the wolf agreed or thought he was being a fool. Lately Ash's opinions on that question had grown harder to read.

He rose and moved ahead of her, silent through the undergrowth out of habit rather than stealth, and stepped onto the trail ten paces in front of her just to watch her reaction. She startled badly — stumbled back, nearly went down, caught herself against a birch whose bark shivered and briefly showed, in its grain, the shape of a face. She didn't notice the face. She noticed him.

"You," she said. "You held a knife on me this morning."

"I did."

"Are you going to again?"

"No." He turned and walked. After a moment, footsteps followed. He heard her mutter something under her breath — something about *wolves*, and *manners*, and *at least the wolf introduced himself* — and something in his chest that had been clenched since dawn loosened by a single degree. He ignored it.

The hollow opened around them: a ring of standing stones softened by centuries of moss, a fire pit banked to embers, three bedrolls, a cook pot, a cart with one wheel mended in four places. Home, such as home was. Sable looked up from stirring something that steamed green. Dorn sat sharpening a sword that had not needed sharpening in twenty years, because sharpening gave his hands something honest to do. Pip was upside down in the branches of the memory-oak at the hollow's edge, hanging by her knees, and dropped the eight feet to the ground the moment she saw the stranger.

"A person," Pip announced, as though reporting weather. "A whole entire person. Not a scout, not a corpse. Where've you been keeping her, Kael?"

"In the forest," Kaelen said. "Where I found her."

Sable set down her spoon. Her eyes — pale, quick, the eyes of a woman who had buried two husbands and outlived every prediction made about her — went from the stranger to the bundle in her arms, and stayed there.

"Well," said the hedge-witch softly. "That's a map."

* * *

They sat her by the fire because there was nowhere else to sit her, and fed her because Dorn had cooked enough for six and sulked when food went uneaten, and questioned her because they were who they were.

Her name was Bram Hartwell. She was a cartographer. She came from a valley whose name none of them recognized, though Sable's mouth had gone strange and careful when Bram described it — a valley no one left, a valley magic could not touch, a valley that forgot. She had inherited the map from a dying stranger two nights ago. She had tried to burn it. It had pulled her through instead.

"She tried to burn it," Dorn repeated. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray-bearded, with a knight's bearing ruined by a knight's exile and a cook's apron tied over both. "On purpose."

"It wouldn't catch," Bram said. She held her hands out to the fire, and Kaelen noted that she held them like a person who had never learned to be warm outdoors. "The flame just — bent around it. And then it wasn't paper anymore, it was — I don't have the word. It was pulling. Like a river under my feet."

"The word," Sable said, "is *wanting*." She had not taken her eyes off the bundle since Bram set it in her lap. "Maps are made of wanting, girl. Every line on one is somebody's hunger — for land, for gold, for a road home. Most maps are dead wants, dried into ink. A living map is a want that never got satisfied." She leaned forward. "May I?"

Bram hesitated. Kaelen watched her hesitate — watched her look at each of them in turn, cataloguing, deciding, the way a person decides whether strangers are shelter or threat. He knew which answer most people would give. Four ragged souls around a dying fire in the middle of Veyra's deepwood were not, by any reasonable measure, shelter.

But she unfolded the map.

It lay across her knees like a sleeping animal, and even from where Kaelen sat — deliberately at the edge of the firelight, deliberately not looking at it, deliberately failing at both — he could feel it. The way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. The parchment breathed. Its lines crawled, slow as roots growing, redrawing themselves in the margins while everyone watched.

Pip crept closer on her knees, the way children creep toward anything dangerous, which is to say quickly and with total commitment. "It's got our forest on it," she whispered. "Look — there's the hollow. There's the stones. There's *us*." She pointed, delighted, at four small marks near a ring of drawn stones that had not existed on the parchment an hour ago. "It knows where we are. Kael, it knows where we are."

"I see it," Kaelen said quietly.

And he did see it, and what he saw made the skin crawl along his arms beneath his sleeves. Because the marks were right. All four of them, placed precisely, rendered in a hand that was not Bram Hartwell's — a firmer hand, an older hand, a hand that had drawn these lines long before tonight. The map had known where they were before she arrived. Which meant whoever had made it had known. Which meant—

Which meant he needed to stop thinking about it in front of witnesses.

"Who drew it?" Dorn asked. His sword lay across his knees now instead of his whetstone, and Kaelen doubted the man had noticed moving it. Old instincts. "Your dying stranger. Did she say her name?"

"Veyra," Bram said. "She said her name was Veyra Hartwell. She said—" Bram stopped. Her jaw worked. "She said *find the last fire*. And then she died."

The hollow went very quiet. The fire cracked. Somewhere above, the memory-oak rustled though there was no wind, murmuring names to itself — it did that on evenings when something important passed beneath it, and Kaelen made a private note that the oak had been whispering since sundown.

Sable sat back slowly. "Hartwell," she said. "That name hasn't crossed the Veil in thirty years. Not since—"

"Don't," Kaelen said.

It came out sharper than he intended. Three heads turned to him. Bram's included. He kept his face the flat, closed thing he'd spent fifteen years making it, and reached for a reason that wasn't the true one. "Names are bait," he said. "Half the realm would kill to hear a name like that spoken over a fire. We're seven miles from the Duskmere road. Sound carries."

It was a good reason. It was even true. It simply was not the reason, and Sable's eyes told him she knew it, and he looked at the fire until she let it go.

* * *

Later, when Bram had fallen asleep sitting up with the map still in her lap — she'd refused to surrender it, and none of them had pushed, though Kaelen saw Dorn's hand twitch toward it once and then deliberately flatten against his thigh — the company held its council the way it always did: in low voices, within earshot of the fire, trusting the crackle to cover them.

"She stays the night," Dorn said. It wasn't a suggestion. Dorn's rulings were few and absolute, the prerogative of the oldest and the largest. "She's half-frozen and she doesn't know which berries kill. She leaves tomorrow without us, she's dead inside two days."

"She shouldn't have come at all," Kaelen said.

"She didn't *come*, Kael. She fell." Sable was grinding something in her mortar — valerian, by the smell, though Kaelen suspected the valerian was a cover for whatever she actually ground. "Through a map. Do you understand what he's telling us, Dorn? A map pulled her across the Veil. The Veil that nothing crosses. The Veil that has held since the Burning Peace." She tapped the mortar against the stone. "Either the Veil is failing, or that map is older than the Veil, or — and I'll thank the stars not to say the third thing aloud with her sleeping ten feet away."

"Say it anyway," Dorn said. "Quietly."

Sable glanced at the sleeping woman. Lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Or the map has finally found what it was drawn to find."

Kaelen's hands were fists in his lap. He made them open. One finger at a time.

"And the oath?" he asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later, though pride seemed a thin and bitter meal. "We swore it on the stones, all of us. Even Pip, small as she was. No carrier of a living map dies by our hand. No matter what they carry. No matter *who*."

"That's the oath," Sable agreed. "So she lives. That's settled, and I'm glad it's settled, and I notice you're the one who needed it settled aloud." Her pale eyes rested on him with terrible gentleness. "What I want to know, wolf-bonded, is why you rode hard enough to lather your horse bringing her here instead of turning her loose in the deepwood. You've turned people loose before. You've done worse than turn them loose. So tell me why this one came home with you."

Because she walked like no one had ever hunted her. Because she cursed the trees for having faces and then apologized to one. Because when his knife was at her throat she had been terrified, yes, but underneath the terror had been something worse — recognition, as if she'd expected the blade, as if she'd been waiting her whole quiet valley life for someone to finally hold one.

"Because the scouts will find her trail," he said. "Better she's behind our blades than ahead of them."

It was true. It was also not the reason. Sable held his gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, the nod of a woman banking a fire she intended to return to.

"Pip," Dorn called softly. "You awake?"

"Obviously." Pip materialized out of the dark between the stones, upside-down again somehow, hanging off the low branch of the memory-oak with her hair trailing in the dirt. She'd been listening the whole time. They all pretended otherwise; it was the family ritual.

"You searched her pack while we talked," Dorn said.

"I inspected it."

"What did you find?"

Pip flipped down and landed lightly. For a moment the performance drained out of her face, and what remained was the sharp, unsentimental mind that had kept her alive in the Duskmere gutters before any of them had found her. "Rice. A spare shirt. A compass that points wrong — it keeps trying to point at the map. Charcoal stubs, three pens, a knife for sharpening them. And this." She held up a scrap of parchment, torn, singed at one edge. "It was sewn into her coat lining. Sloppy stitching. Not hers — she'd have done it neater, she does everything neat."

Sable took it. Held it near the embers. Kaelen saw her go still.

"What is it?" Dorn asked.

"A fragment," Sable said. "Of another map. Older. And in the corner, where it isn't burned—" She turned it so the firelight caught the ink. Two lines of script, in a hand Kaelen did not know and yet felt he should: *the heir sleeps in the valley that forgets.*

The words hung in the smoke.

Above them, the memory-oak shivered and began, very softly, to whisper a name. Not *Kaelen*. Not *Sable*, or *Dorn*, or *Pip*.

It whispered *Hartwell*, over and over, in a voice like wind through winter branches, and in her sleep by the fire, the stranger stirred and murmured as though answering.

Kaelen looked at Ash. The wolf was staring at Bram with his head cocked, ears pricked, and his nostrils flared — and then Ash looked back at Kaelen, and there was something in the amber eyes that Kaelen had never seen there before, something that stopped his breath.

His wolf, who did not lie, who could not lie, who had never once in nine years shown him anything but the plain truth of his senses — his wolf looked afraid.

*Tomorrow,* Kaelen promised him silently. *Tomorrow I'll know what you smell.*

But some part of him, the part that had been clenched since dawn, already knew.
