## 7. The Toll Bridge at Duskmere

They came down out of the high pines on the third morning with the river already audible below them — a low, ceaseless muttering, like a crowd that never quite disperses. Duskmere. Kaelen had named it the night before without looking up from his whetstone, and Sable had made a small sound in her throat, half blessing and half curse, and Dorn had said nothing at all, which from Dorn was its own kind of warning.

Bram heard the water before she saw it, and when she did see it she stopped walking.

The river was not blue or brown or any color a cartographer would trust herself to mix. It ran the color of dusk itself — a deep violet-grey shot through with threads of gold where the early sun caught it — and it moved too slowly for so wide a body of water, as though it were reluctant to go anywhere at all. Mist hung over it in long horizontal banners. And across it, where any sensible world would have built a bridge of timber or stone, there stood instead an arch of living wood: two trees bent toward each other over the water until their crowns met and fused, roots gripping opposite banks, their interlaced branches forming a span perhaps forty paces long. Lanterns hung from those branches. They burned without flame — small globes of pale light, dozens of them, swaying gently though there was no wind.

"That's not a bridge," Bram said. "That's a *tree*."

"It's both," Pip said cheerfully, trotting past her with his hands jammed in pockets far too big for him. "Best not to think about it too hard. Thinking makes it worse."

"Everything here makes it worse," Bram muttered, but her hand had already gone to the satchel where the map slept, and she felt it stir against her hip — a slow ripple, like a cat stretching — and she wondered, not for the first time, whether the map had known about this bridge before any of them had.

The company came to a halt at the near bank. Kaelen stood a moment with Ash pressed against his leg, the wolf's ears flat, a growl building low in his chest like a stone rolling in a barrel.

"It knows we're here," Kaelen said quietly.

"They always know." Sable pushed back her hood. Her grey hair, escaping its braid, seemed to catch what little light the lanterns gave. "Duskmere is old. Older than the war, older than the thrones, older than anything either army would care to admit. The toll-keepers don't fight for either side. They take payment from everyone, which is more than can be said for anyone else in Veyra." She turned to Bram, and there was something in her face Bram hadn't seen before — something that looked almost like apology. "You'll want to hear how the toll works before you're standing on the planks."

"I assumed coin."

Sable's mouth twisted. "Coin. Yes." She glanced at Dorn, who shook his head once, slowly, and then at Pip, who had gone uncharacteristically still. "No one carries enough coin. That's rather the point. The toll-keepers of Duskmere take memories. One crossing, one memory, freely given. They hold them in the lanterns—" she nodded at the pale globes swaying overhead "—and I'm told they keep them warm. Whether that's comfort or cruelty depends on who you ask."

Bram felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs.

"*Memories.*"

"A memory you choose," Sable said carefully. "They'll show you a few, if you dither. Best not to dither. Choose before you step onto the wood, pay at the center, and walk on. Don't look back at your lantern. It's rude, and it doesn't help."

"And if someone refuses to pay?"

Dorn spoke for the first time, his voice like gravel settling. "Then the tree keeps them. There are lanterns under the eaves nobody's claimed in two hundred years." He said it the way he said everything — flatly, as a man reciting inventory of disasters — and then he shouldered his pack and started toward the bridge, because apparently that was all the discussion the matter required.

* * *

Bram lagged at the foot of the span while the others crossed ahead of her.

She told herself it was caution. A cartographer's habit: measure the ground before trusting it, test the footing, note the load-bearing limbs. But the truth sat in her chest, plain and heavy as a millstone, and by now she was tired of lying to herself about it. She had left everything behind in the valley — everything except the memories of it, and those she carried folded inside her like the map was folded inside its satchel, creased along lines she'd worn smooth with handling. Her mother's kitchen. The smell of ink and beeswax. The way the valley fog came off the fields at dawn in slow white rivers. Her father's hands guiding hers over vellum, saying *always draw the border twice, Bram, once for the land and once for the truth.*

That was all she had left of home. And the bridge wanted to eat some piece of it.

"You're doing the face," Pip said.

She startled. The boy had doubled back without a sound — of course he had; he moved like a rumor — and now he stood beside her at the rail-thin root, looking up at her with those quick dark eyes that missed absolutely nothing.

"What face?"

"The one where you're being brave about something you haven't agreed to yet." He rocked on his heels. "I've got a whole catalogue. That's the 'I'll cross when I'm ready' face. Under it is usually the 'this place is horrible' face, which is fair, because it is."

Bram huffed out something that was almost a laugh. "You didn't have to come back for me."

"Nope." Pip shrugged. "But Dorn crosses slow and Sable talks the whole time and Kaelen walks like the bridge owes him money. You're the only interesting one to wait for." He hesitated, and then, quieter, in a voice stripped of its usual performance: "It took my sister's memory. When we crossed. Before I found the company. She paid for both of us because I was little and they'd have taken mine, and mine weren't worth much anyway." His jaw tightened. "She chose wrong. Or maybe there wasn't a right choice. But she came off that bridge crying and wouldn't say why, and three days later she asked me our mother's name like she was asking the weather."

Bram went cold. "Pip—"

"So choose something you can stand to lose," he said. "That's all. Not the best thing. Not the biggest thing. Something true, but survivable." He stuck out his hand with sudden brightness, the mask snapping back into place. "Come on. Bridge won't bite. Probably."

She took his hand. His fingers were small and grimy and steady, and she found, absurdly, that they steadied her too.

* * *

The planks of the bridge were warm underfoot.

That was the first wrongness — warmth, in the mist-chill morning, rising through her boot soles like the memory of a hearth. The second wrongness was the sound. Each plank, as she stepped on it, hummed faintly, and the hums braided together into something almost like speech, almost like a name being read aloud very slowly from a very long list. The lanterns brightened as she passed beneath them, each one leaning — she could swear they leaned — toward her like flowers toward sun.

Halfway across, the tree spoke.

It did not open a mouth, because it had none. The voice simply arrived, everywhere at once, patient and vast and neither male nor female nor old nor young: **a toll is owed**.

"I know," Bram said. Her voice sounded very small over the slow violet water.

**choose**.

And the choosing was not like choosing at all. It was like opening a drawer she hadn't known was in her own chest. Memories rose into the lantern-light and hung there, translucent, offered: her mother singing off-key while kneading bread. The first map she ever finished alone, aged nine, of the garden, with north pointing confidently the wrong direction. The winter her father coughed blood into a cloth and hid it, and she pretended not to see, and they spent one last golden autumn drawing the valley together, border drawn twice, once for the land and once for the truth.

All of them precious. All of them her. And the tree waited, courteous and bottomless, willing to take whichever she offered, and she understood suddenly why Dorn crossed in silence and why Sable blessed-and-cursed the place and why no one carries enough coin: because every memory feels irreplaceable until you survive losing it, and the tree does not tell you which losses you will survive.

Her hand drifted to the satchel. The map stirred, urgent, and she pressed it down.

*Not that,* she thought. *Never that. Whatever else I lose, not the thing that brought me here, not the thing that might be the only reason any of us—*

Then she caught herself. Caught the thought mid-shape. Because that was the trap, wasn't it — the slow arithmetic of the road, begun three days after she'd been dragged through ink and storm into a forest that remembered names. Every mile, she had traded something. The valley's safety, when she'd crossed its dead borders. Her certainty that maps obeyed their makers. Sleep, mostly. And now the tree wanted more, and she could feel herself beginning to weigh her own childhood like a merchant weighing flour, and something in her rebelled at the cheapness of it.

She chose the smallest memory she owned.

It was nothing. It was a summer afternoon when she was six, before her father taught her letters, before anything. She had fallen asleep in the grass behind the house and woken at dusk with dew in her hair, and for one disoriented minute she had not known where she was, and then she had seen the smoke rising from the chimney and heard her mother calling her name, and the fear had melted into a warmth so complete that even now, decades later, the mere thought of it loosened her shoulders. *Lost, and then found. That was all it was. Lost, and then found.*

She held it up to the lanterns like a coin between two fingers.

"This one."

**given**, said the tree, and it was done.

She felt it go. That was the horror of it — there was no pain, no tearing, just a soft subtraction, a page lifted cleanly from a book. For a heartbeat she groped for it the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. *There was a day, there was grass, there was—* and then the shape of the absence closed over and smoothed itself, and she knew only that she had paid, and that something was gone, and that she could not miss what she could not remember missing.

A lantern above her flared briefly, warmly gold, and dimmed to a contented glow. Somewhere inside it, a six-year-old girl woke in dewy grass forever, and never heard her mother call.

Bram walked the rest of the span with her eyes fixed on the far bank and did not look back, because it was rude, and it wouldn't help.

* * *

On the far side, the company waited among the ferns, and none of them asked what she'd paid, because that was evidently the etiquette of grief in Veyra: you didn't inquire after another person's amputations.

But Pip fell into step beside her again, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and after a quarter mile he said, conversationally, "I paid a memory of eating a stolen peach. First thing I ever stole. Whole orchard, moonlight, juice running down my wrist. Figured if I had to lose something, better something sticky."

"Why that one?"

"Because I remember stealing it." He grinned sideways at her. "I just don't remember liking it anymore. Peaches taste like nothing to me now. Saddest thing in the world, a thief who can't enjoy fruit." Then, softer: "What'd you give them?"

"Something small," Bram said. "I don't remember."

Pip was quiet for a moment. Then he reached up and took her hand again, the way a child does without embarrassment, and laced his dirty fingers through hers, and kept walking. "Yeah," he said. "Me neither."

They walked like that for a long time, the child and the map-carrier, down out of the mists of Duskmere into country neither of them had ever seen — Bram because she had never seen it, and Pip because he had given away whatever he'd once known of it. Behind them, the lanterns of the bridge glowed on in the fog, dozens of small warm lights, each one holding someone's afternoon, someone's wedding, someone's last sight of a door closing. Ahead, the map shifted in its satchel with a patience that was starting to feel less like guidance and more like appetite.

North, it whispered. Toward the fire.

And Bram, who had lost a memory she couldn't name and gained a child's hand in hers, walked on into the deepening green — and did not notice, because no one had told her to watch for it, that the map had begun, in its margins, in a hand that was not the stranger's and not hers, to write names.
