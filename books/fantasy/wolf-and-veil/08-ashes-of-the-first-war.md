## 8. Ashes of the First War

Dorn had walked through the Emberwood for three days without speaking, and it was not the silence of a man who had nothing to say. It was the silence of a man listening.

The others thought he was dour by nature — Sable joked about it over her cookfire, calling him *the knight who mourns his own shadow*, and Pip had taken to counting his grunts on a string of knots she kept in her pocket, one knot per word, so that a day with more than twenty became an occasion. He let them laugh. There were worse things to be than the quiet one. The quiet one heard things.

He heard the forest changing.

For three days the trees had been growing older around them. Not taller — older. The birches near the valley's edge gave way to oaks, the oaks to ironwoods whose bark was ridged like scar tissue, and now, as they climbed the last ridge before the high moor, the trees themselves began to thin as though afraid to go further. Their leaves came in gray at the edges. Moss hung from the branches in long pale curtains that did not stir when the wind moved, because there was no wind here. There hadn't been wind here for sixty years.

Dorn stopped at the crest of the ridge and put out one arm, and behind him the little column halted: Kaelen first, hand already on his axe out of habit, then Sable with her satchel of bottles clinking softly, then Pip riding on the mule between Bram and the packs. The girl had gone quiet an hour ago without knowing why. Children always went quiet here. Animals too. The mule had refused the ridge twice before Dorn coaxed it up with a dried apple and lies about what waited on the other side.

What waited was the Ashen Field.

It spread below them like a second sky fallen wrong-side-up. A plain of gray grass, gray earth, gray stone, stretching to a horizon where the mountains stood in ranks like mourners. And across all of it — the army.

Or what the dragon-fire had left of one.

They stood where they had stood, the men of the Veyric host, ten thousand strong on the morning of the last day, frozen mid-stride into shapes of cinder and glass. Some knelt. Some ran. One officer near the front still held his sword raised, his mouth open around a command that had died in his throat, his body become a pillar of black glass that caught the light and threw it back in colors no living thing should wear. Here and there among them, patches of color survived: a banner fused to its pole, its heraldry burned to a smear of crimson; a shield boss of polished bronze; the gilt hilt of a sword whose owner had been reaching for it when the sky came down.

The whole field smelled faintly of rain that never fell.

"Sixty years," Sable said quietly beside him. She always said it, every time, as if the number needed confirming against some private ledger. "And it still looks like yesterday."

"It is yesterday," Dorn said. "Here, it is always yesterday."

Behind them, Bram made a small sound. Dorn turned enough to see her standing at the edge of the descent, the folded map clutched against her chest with both hands, her face gone the color of the ash below. She had asked, two nights ago, what lay between them and the Emberhold. He had told her: bad roads, worse weather, a toll bridge. He had not told her this. None of them had. It was the sort of thing you could not tell a person; you could only bring them to the ridge and let the field do its own telling.

Kaelen's wolf pressed close against the cartographer's leg — Ash, who feared nothing on this earth except, perhaps, this place, and showed that fear only by refusing to leave Bram's side. Dorn marked that too. He marked everything. It was the habit of a man who had once worn a knight's oath like armor and found, too late, how much of it had rusted through.

"We go around," he said. "There's a track along the eastern rise. We don't cross the field."

"Why?" Pip asked, in the way children ask — not because she didn't understand, but because she wanted the words said aloud so they'd be real. "What happened?"

Dorn looked out over the glass soldiers for a long moment. Then he sat down on the ridge-stone, slowly, the way old men sit, though he was not yet old — the war had merely borrowed sixty years from him in advance.

"Sit," he said. "All of you. This will take the length of a fire, and we'll make no miles today besides."

* * *

They built the cookfire small, down in the lee of the ridge where the smoke would not rise above the tree line. Superstition, maybe. Or courtesy. In a place where ten thousand men had been turned to candle-wax by a single breath, you did not light large fires and wave them about.

Pip sat closest, cross-legged, chin on her fists. Bram sat a little apart, but not far; Kaelen had settled himself between her and the field without appearing to have done so deliberately, and Dorn noted that too, and filed it away with the other things he had been filing away since the night the stranger's map dragged her into their world. The sentinel watched her like a hawk watches a lamb it has sworn to protect from other hawks. Dorn had seen that look before, on younger men, in another life, and he knew what usually came of it, and he kept his counsel.

"The war," Dorn began, "was not fought over gold. Or borders. Or any of the things wars are supposed to be fought over, which are lies anyway." He fed a twig to the flames. "It was fought over a question. Who speaks for Veyra."

Sable snorted softly. "Who *owned* Veyra, he means."

"No," Dorn said. "That's the thing people get wrong, and it matters that you get it right, girl, if you're going to carry whatever it is you're carrying." He nodded at the map against Bram's ribs. "Veyra isn't land. Land can be owned. Veyra is the *agreement* — the old compact between the rooted things and the winged things and the walking things. Before either throne existed, the dragon kept the compact. Dragon-fire was the seal on it. As long as the fire burned true, the forests remembered their names, the rivers kept their courses, magic flowed where it should and stopped where it shouldn't. Your valley—" he tipped his head toward Bram, "—is what happens when the seal weakens. Dead ground. Magic can't cross it. A hole in the world's memory."

Bram's hands tightened on the map. "I grew up in a hole," she said. Not a question. Her voice was flat in the way of someone setting a stone down carefully because they're not sure yet whether it's heavy.

"You grew up in a wound," Dorn corrected gently. "Wounds are where the fighting got in."

He let the fire crackle a moment. Out on the plain, the light was beginning to fail, and the glass figures caught the last of it and glowed — amber, rose, a bruised violet — as though the dead were being lit from within by something patient.

"Two houses rose to speak for the walking things. House Valcrest of the eastern marches, and House Merrow of the western fens. Both claimed the right to tend the compact alongside the dragon. Both had blood old enough to argue it. For four hundred years they balanced each other — married into each other, poisoned each other, made peace and broke it, and through all of it the dragon kept the flame and neither house dared push past the point of breaking."

"What changed?" Kaelen asked. He rarely spoke during Dorn's histories; when he did, it was because the answer mattered to him personally. Dorn wondered which part of this one would.

"Greed wore patience out," Dorn said. "The last generation of both houses decided the balance was a cage. Valcrest wanted the fenlands' river-roads. Merrow wanted the marches' iron. Each convinced itself the other would strike first. And both of them made the same fatal arithmetic: the dragon is old, the dragon is tired, the dragon will not spend its fire on us. Fire is claim, you see. Dragon-breath doesn't burn the innocent. It burns *illegitimacy*. It burns the lie in a thing. So each house told itself: our cause is just, our claim is true, the fire cannot touch us. Only them."

"And they were both wrong," Sable said.

"They were both *half* right," Dorn said, "which is far worse. That was the horror of it. When the armies met here, on this moor, both claims were rotten — not false outright, but braided through with theft and oath-breaking and kin-slavery, every sin of four hundred years soaked into the roots. And Vess—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the name, and he smoothed it over, "—Vess came down out of the Emberhold to stand between them, as the compact demanded. And Vess breathed."

He gestured, one open hand taking in the entire gray horizon.

"Not at one army. At *both*. At the claim itself. The fire sought the illegitimacy in the bloodlines and followed it home. Every soldier carrying either house's warrant — every man sworn under those banners, every officer bearing their seals, every courier, every tax-collector, everyone whose service was stitched to either claim — the fire read them like a ledger and closed the account. Ten thousand people, and not one of them a king or queen. Farmers' sons. Cooks. Boys who'd signed on for boots and bread." His jaw worked. "The two royal lines themselves weren't even here. They were safe in their halls, hundreds of miles apart. But the fire followed the thread of their claim down through everyone who served it, and when it reached the end of the thread—" he snapped his fingers, and Pip flinched, "—it found nothing left to burn. Both houses ended that day without a single royal death. Annihilated by inheritance. You can kill a dynasty without touching a single drop of royal blood, if the claim itself is what burns."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere out on the field, a sheet of loose glass ticked against another in a wind that did not exist, and Dorn chose not to wonder about it.

"So there's been no ruler since," Bram said at last. Her voice was careful, feeling its way. "No claim at all."

"There's been the truce," Sable said. "Which isn't peace. It's a held breath. The remnant factions — the old banners' survivors and their descendants, the Oathbreakers of the east and the Fenborn of the west — they've spent sixty years circling each other, waiting for legitimacy to fall out of the sky and land on one of them. Because that's the other half of what Dorn isn't saying." She glanced at the old knight. "Go on. Tell her the rest. She'll hear it eventually anyway, and better from us than from them."

Dorn looked at Bram for a while before answering. Behind her, Kaelen had gone very still — the particular stillness of a man listening to his own doom being described in the third person — but Bram's eyes stayed on Dorn, and Dorn owed her truth more than he owed Kaelen's comfort.

"The fire gutters," he said. "You've seen it — you'll see it worse at the Emberhold. Sixty years of tending by no one, because tending requires a legitimate claimant, and we burned the last of those along with their armies. The flame dims a little more each year. And when it fails entirely—" he spread his hand toward the plain again, "—the compact dissolves. The truce dies with it. Every faction that has spent six decades sharpening knives in the dark comes out swinging, and there will be no dragon-fire left to stop them, because a guttering flame can barely boil a kettle. The forests will forget their names. The borders between what magic may touch and what it may not will fray like old rope. Everything your valley is, everything the whole realm fears becoming — that happens everywhere, all at once, forever."

"But the fire responds to claim," Bram said slowly. Dorn watched her assemble the thought piece by piece, like a mason fitting stones. "If tending requires a legitimate claimant... then a legitimate claimant could feed it. Restore it. Keep the truce alive."

"Yes."

"And if such a claimant existed," she went on, quieter now, each word laid down like something breakable, "every faction on both sides would want them. Not dead. *Owned.* A living heir to either house — or to both — would be a weapon no forge could match. Legitimacy falling out of the sky." She almost smiled, and it was the worst smile Dorn had ever seen. "Landing on somebody."

Nobody answered. The fire popped. On the moor below, dusk pooled in the hollows between the glass soldiers until the whole field seemed to be filling slowly with dark water, and the dead stood hip-deep in evening, patient as they had been for sixty years, patient as they would be for sixty more.

* * *

Dorn took the first watch, as he always did when they camped within sight of the field. He told himself it was habit. It was penance, really, though he no longer troubled himself to distinguish between the two; they wore the same coat.

He had not been in either army. That was the bitter joke of his life. He had been a knight of no house at all in those days — a hedge-sworn sword doing escort work on the river-roads — and when the summons came for all sworn men to march, his lord had looked at Dorn's common-born face and his unlanded name and decided he was worth more guarding granaries. Everyone he had trained beside marched east to this moor. Everyone he had trained beside was somewhere down in that gray glass, mid-stride, forever.

He had come here once, twenty years ago, alone, and walked the rows until he found a shield-boss he recognized — a boar's head, dented, that had belonged to a boy named Tam who used to cheat at dice and sing badly and dream of a farm in the hill country. Dorn had stood in front of the glass shape that held Tam's last stride for the length of a day, and then he had walked out of Veyra altogether and not come back for five years. Exile was easier to carry at a distance. Most griefs were.

Now he sat with his back against the ridge-stone, sword across his knees, watching the field drink the moonlight, and he thought about the map.

He had seen what the others had seen — a strange document that moved like a sleeping animal, that redrew itself nightly, that had pulled a valley-born woman bodily across the boundary of the impossible. But Dorn had also seen the marginalia. Old eyes read old scripts. Two nights ago, while the others slept, he had looked over Bram's shoulder at a page of annotations in a faded brown ink, and he had seen the sigils sketched there — a tower and a crescent, side by side, the arms of Valcrest and Merrow entwined — and beneath them, in a careful antique hand, a family name written three times in three different generations.

A name that matched the one Bram had given them, the night Kaelen nearly killed her and the company instead gave her soup.

He had said nothing. He had told himself he was waiting for certainty, and perhaps that was even partly true. But Dorn knew himself well enough, these days, to know the other truth underneath: he was an old man who had buried one company of young people already, and the thought of what would have to be done — what Kaelen was oath-bound to do, what the factions would do, what the whole starving realm would do — if the girl's blood was what he believed it was, made him want to sit on this ridge until he turned to glass himself.

Out on the plain, the light shifted. Just slightly. Just at the field's far edge, where a lone figure of glass stood apart from the ranks — a woman, by its outline, arms flung wide, facing not the enemy but the sky.

For sixty years the Ashen Field had not moved. Dorn knew this the way he knew his own scars.

But tonight, as the moon cleared the shoulder of the mountain, the glass woman's shadow fell *toward* the ridge — toward the campfire, toward the sleepers, toward the girl with the map — and shadows, Dorn knew, do not fall against the direction of the light.

He was on his feet with his sword drawn before he understood why, heart hammering like a siege drum, as somewhere down among the ten thousand dead, something that had been still for sixty years began, very softly, to glow.
