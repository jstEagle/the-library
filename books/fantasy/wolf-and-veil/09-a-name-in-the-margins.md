## 9. A Name in the Margins

The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals by the time the camp finally went quiet.

Bram sat alone at the edge of its light with the map spread across her knees, weighted at the corners by four smooth stones she'd gathered from the streambed that afternoon. It was a ritual now, this nightly examination — the map redrew itself while they slept, ink crawling like something alive beneath the vellum's skin, and she had learned to study it before it changed, to memorize what it wanted to show her and wonder, always, *why*.

Tonight the path to the Emberhold ran bold as a vein of iron through the western ranges. She could nearly recite it: down from the high pass, along the shore of the grey lake, through the ash-forest where even the wolves walked softly. Three days' travel if the weather held. Four if it didn't.

But it was not the path that kept her awake tonight.

It was the edges.

She had noticed them days ago — small marks in the margins, faint as breath on glass, clustered along the borders of the vellum like frost along a windowpane. At first she'd taken them for ornament, the idle flourishes of whoever had drawn the map originally. But the map was never idle. Every line on it meant something. Every mark had a purpose, even if she couldn't read it yet.

And so, night after night, she had copied them.

Her satchel held a second book now, a cheap thing of rough paper she'd bought from a peddler in Duskmere before the toll bridge took her childhood memory of learning to swim. On its pages, in her careful cartographer's hand, she transcribed each marginal glyph she could coax into visibility — holding the map close to the fire, angling it against the moonlight, sometimes breathing on the vellum the way one breathes on a mirror. The marks came and went. They were shy. Or perhaps they were waiting for something.

Tonight, holding the parchment above the coals until the heat stung her knuckles, she watched the last glyph she hadn't yet copied rise slowly to the surface of the margin like a body rising in deep water.

It was not a glyph at all.

It was writing.

* * *

The letters were old — an angular script she recognized only because her mother had once owned a prayer book written in it, a slim volume with a cracked leather cover that Bram had been forbidden to touch and had touched anyway, tracing the unfamiliar characters by candlelight and inventing meanings for them, stories about queens and drowned cities and doors that opened onto the sky. Her mother had caught her once. Had gone white as bone and taken the book away, and burned it in the kitchen stove the following morning while Bram watched through a crack in the door, bewildered by the grief on her mother's face. Grief, though she hadn't known the word for it then. Grief over *ink*.

Now she understood.

The words in the margin were few. Seven lines, maybe eight, written small and cramped, the hand growing shakier toward the end — as though the writer had been hurrying, or dying, or both. Bram read them three times before their meaning finished sinking through her skin and into the marrow beneath.

*The blood of Aldemar runs in the child of Hartwell.*
*I carried the proof as far as I could. The valley keeps her safe; the valley forgets.*
*If you read this, heir of two houses, know what you are:*
*Last of Vane. Last of Corvane.*
*Daughter of the line that broke the world.*

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, Ash lifted his head and loosed a single low sound, half growl, half question, and then settled again when Kaelen murmured something in his sleep.

Bram did not move for a long time.

The names went through her like cold water through cloth. *Vane.* *Corvane.* She knew those names — not from her mother's forbidden prayer book, but from Dorn's voice, low and heavy, as they'd ridden through the frozen battlefield two weeks past. He had named the armies that died there. House Vane of the eastern throne, dragon-bonded, proud, their banners a white tower on black. House Corvane of the west, sea-kings and storm-callers, their sigil a veiled woman holding a sword. Two royal lines, he'd said. Two thrones. One war that ate everything, ended only when the last dragon breathed her fire across the field and sealed the peace in ash.

Both lines annihilated, Dorn had said. To the last child. That was the whole of it — that was why the peace held, why the dragon's guttering flame still bound the truce. There was no one left to claim either crown. No one left worth killing.

Except.

Bram looked down at her own hands, still steady around the stones that pinned the map, and felt something in her chest come loose and begin to drift, untethered, like a boat cut from its mooring.

Hartwell. Her father's name. A plain name, a valley name, a name that meant nothing anywhere outside the forty miles of hills and orchards where she'd been born. But her mother — her mother had never spoken of where she came from. Her mother who flinched at open flame. Who burned a prayer book in an alphabet no valley scribe could read. Who taught Bram to draw maps and made her swear, laughing, swearing too hard and too often, that she would never, ever leave the valley. *Nothing crosses the borders*, everyone said. Magic couldn't get in. And nothing — nothing — got out.

The valley doesn't forget, the margin said. The valley *forgets*. It had been a cage built of forgetting, and her mother had built it around her daughter brick by brick, and Bram had spent twenty-six years pressing her face against walls she'd thought were the shape of the world.

"Ilse," she whispered. Her mother's name. Ilse Hartwell, dead six years now, buried under the rowan tree behind the house, who had kissed Bram's forehead every night and never once told her what she was kissing.

Unless Ilse wasn't her mother's name either.

The stranger — the woman who had bled out on Bram's floor during the storm, whose last word had been *fire*, whose folded map had dragged Bram bodily into another world — the stranger had known her. Not known *of* her. Known *her*. Bram remembered now, remembered with a sick lurch, the way the woman's cracked lips had shaped themselves around something just before the word *fire* — a sound, half-formed, that Bram had taken for a gasp. It hadn't been a gasp.

It had been *Bramble*. A name no one had called her since she was small enough to answer to it. Her mother's private name for her, used exactly twice in public in her whole life and never explained.

The stranger had known the name a dying mother uses for her child.

"Aunt," Bram said aloud, testing the word, and the word broke apart in her mouth like wet paper.

* * *

She didn't remember standing. She was walking before she knew it, out past the ring of stones, into the dark beyond the firelight, the map crushed against her chest with both arms as though it might fly off into the night without her. The forest of Veyra closed around her, trees tall and patient, and she had learned enough by now to know they were not empty — the trees here remembered things, Sable said, remembered names, and sometimes if you pressed your palm to the bark you could feel the ghost of some old joy or old sorrow humming up through the wood.

"Talk to me," she said to the nearest trunk, and hated how young her voice sounded. "You remember names. Someone tell me who I am."

The tree said nothing, of course. But beneath her palm, faint as a pulse, she felt something stir — recognition, or pity, she couldn't tell which, and it was unbearable either way. She snatched her hand back.

Behind her, a branch snapped. Deliberately.

She turned, heart slamming, and found Kaelen standing ten feet away with Ash at his knee, the wolf's eyes catching the distant firelight like two coins at the bottom of a well. Kaelen had his cloak on. He'd been on watch, or coming off it, and he stood the way he always stood — weight balanced, hands loose, a man who could be moving in any direction within half a heartbeat.

"You shouldn't wander," he said.

"Shouldn't I." Her voice came out strange. Flat. "You'd know all about what I should do, wouldn't you."

His head tilted slightly. In the dark, she couldn't read his expression, but she felt him go still in a new way — the stillness of a man listening for something under the words.

"The map showed me something tonight," she said. She hadn't decided to say it. The words simply came, pushed out of her by the pressure of everything she was holding. "Writing in the margins. Old writing. Do you want to know what it says? It says the blood of Aldemar runs in the child of Hartwell. It says I'm the last of Vane and the last of Corvane. It calls me—" her breath hitched, and she hated that, hated the crack in it— "it calls me *heir of two houses*. Both of them, Kaelen. Both thrones. The ones that don't exist anymore. The ones that killed each other down to the last child."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, an owl called, and the forest swallowed the sound.

"Except there is one child," Bram said. "One last child. And she's been sleeping twelve feet from you for a month, drawing maps in the dirt with sticks, asking you to teach her the roads."

"Bram—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to surprise them both. She wrapped her arms tighter around the map. "Don't tell me it isn't true. Don't tell me it's a mistake, or a trick, or the fever of a tired mind. I've been reading maps my whole life. I know handwriting when I see it. I know *intent* when I see it. That map has been trying to tell me something since the night it pulled me through, and tonight it finally managed it." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "My aunt. The stranger in the storm. She was my aunt. She crossed whatever wall my mother built to keep me blind, she carried that map across half your world looking for — what? For help? For someone to take it? And she died on my floor calling me by the name my mother only ever used when she thought no one was listening."

Ash whined, low. Kaelen's hand came to rest on the wolf's neck, and Bram realized distantly that the wolf was staring at her with an intensity that had nothing to do with threat — that had seemed, lately, more and more like *recognition*, and oh, oh, he had known, hadn't he. The wolf had known. Wolves do not lie, Sable said. Wolves do not lie, and Ash had never once scented Bram as human.

"How long," Bram asked quietly, "has Ash known?"

Kaelen didn't answer. His silence was an answer entire, and it landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well — she heard it fall and fall and never heard it stop.

"That long," she said.

"Bram." His voice was rough. "What you're saying — what the map says — if it's true—"

"*If.*" She stepped closer, close enough to see his eyes now, and saw what lived in them: not disbelief. Not confusion. Something worse. Something that looked terribly like grief arriving early. "Say the rest of it, sentinel. If it's true, then what? Say the sentence you've been carrying around behind your teeth since the day you found me in the forest with your blade at my throat. Because I've seen how you look at me when you think I won't notice, and I always notice — I notice everything, it's what I *do*, I notice roads and borders and the way a man's weight shifts before he decides to kill — so say it."

He said nothing. The fire crackled behind him. Ash pressed against his leg, trembling very slightly, like a creature caught between two storms.

And Bram understood, suddenly and completely, with the terrible clarity that comes only after everything else has shattered, that he could not say it. That whatever sentence he carried, it was not for her ears — or not yet — or not ever, unless the world grew crueler than it already was. She understood that the man sworn to guard her had been guarding her from himself.

"You should sleep," he said at last, hoarse. "We ride at dawn. Emberhold's three days out."

"Yes," Bram said. "We ride at dawn."

She turned and walked back toward the fire, and behind her she heard neither footstep nor voice — only, when she glanced back despite herself, the sight of Kaelen standing alone among the remembering trees with his head bowed, and Ash circling him once, twice, like a thought that would not settle.

At the edge of the firelight she stopped. Sable sat propped against her pack, ostensibly asleep, her sharp dark eyes open and fixed on Bram with an expression that had been waiting for this moment for a long time.

"Well," the hedge-witch said softly. "Now you know."

"Now I know." Bram looked down at the map against her chest, at the seven lines of shaky script already fading back into the vellum, retreating to wherever such things waited. "Sable. When we reach the Emberhold — when Vess sees me — what happens to a person who is heir to the thing the dragon's fire is holding shut?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice was gentle in the way that only truly frightened people manage.

"Everything," she said. "Everything happens. And most of it will have claws."

Above the sleeping camp, far to the east, the sky had begun to bruise toward morning, and somewhere out in that greying darkness — Bram could feel it now, could feel it the way one feels weather changing — something had caught the scent of her blood and turned, patiently, in her direction.
