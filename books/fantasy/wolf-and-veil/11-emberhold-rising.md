## 11. Emberhold Rising

The mountain announced itself long before they saw it.

Bram felt it first in the map — she always did now, that faint tug at her fingertips like a fish testing a line — and then in her teeth, a low hum of pressure that made her jaw ache. By midday of the third day out of Duskmere, the air itself had changed: warmer, tasting faintly of struck flint and old iron, as though the whole sky had been held too close to a forge.

"There," Dorn said, and stopped walking.

The road had climbed through black pine for hours, the trees growing sparser and stranger as they rose, their needles silvered with ash. Now the forest simply gave up, surrendering to a slope of grey scree littered with bones — not animal bones, Bram realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach, but armor. Pauldrons fused to ribcages. A helmet grown into the rock like a barnacle. A sword so large it could only have been wielded by two hands and a giant's heart, its blade run through with veins of glittering glass where dragon-fire had melted it and time had let it set.

Beyond the bonefield, the mountain rose.

It did not look like a mountain anymore. It looked like a fortress that had swallowed a volcano, or a volcano that had decided to become one — towers of black basalt spiraling up from the caldera's rim, walls of poured stone veined with channels that glowed a dull, sullen orange, like embers banked beneath grey ash. The whole structure was built in rings, descending inward, tier upon tier, and at the summit, where smoke curled lazily into the wine-dark sky, something vast shifted against the clouds.

Bram's breath left her in a thin white ribbon.

"Emberhold," Sable said quietly. The hedge-witch had gone still in a way Bram had learned to read as reverence rather than fear — though there was fear in her too, threaded through it like wire through wax. "I'd begun to think I'd never see it."

"You've *seen* it?" Pip scrambled up onto a boulder, squinting. "Before? When?"

"Once. From much further away than this." Sable's eyes didn't leave the smoking peak. "When I was younger than you, and the fire was brighter."

"The fire," Kaelen said. His voice was flat, careful, the voice he used when he was holding something down. "Look at it."

Bram looked. She had been looking — she couldn't stop — but now she understood what he meant. The glowing channels in the walls pulsed unevenly, guttering like candle flames in a draft. Whole sections of the great spiral had gone dark entirely, cold stone among the burning, a pattern of dead veins spreading upward like frost across a window. And the smoke at the summit came in fits and starts, thick and then thin, thick and then thin, the breathing of something very old and very tired.

"It's dying," Bram said.

No one contradicted her. That was answer enough.

* * *

They climbed the last approach as the light failed, following a road of fitted obsidian that wound up the caldera's outer wall. The heat grew steadily, pleasantly at first and then less so, until Bram had stripped off her cloak and rolled her sleeves and still sweat ran down her spine. The map rode against her chest inside her coat, and it had grown warm too — warmer than her own skin, she was almost certain, though she hadn't dared take it out and look.

Kaelen walked beside her. He had been walking beside her more often since Duskmere, since the toll-bridge and the memory she'd paid and the night after, when she'd cried herself quiet against his shoulder by a fire he'd built without being asked. Neither of them had named what was growing between them. Naming things, Bram had learned on this journey, was dangerous; names were how the world got hold of you.

But his presence steadied her now, and she suspected he knew it, and she suspected that was why he kept doing it.

"How does a dragon build all this?" she asked, mostly to keep her mind off the climb.

"She didn't." Dorn answered from ahead, not turning. His armored tread rang on the glassy road. "The First Flame did. Or so the stories say. Vess inherited it the way everything here gets inherited — badly, and too late."

"The stories also say she ate a hundred knights at the Battle of Cindermarch," Pip offered cheerfully, dropping back to walk between them. "And that she sleeps on a pile of crowns. And that if you say her true name backwards three times she has to give you a wish."

"Do any of those have truth in them?" Bram asked.

Pip considered. "She definitely ate some knights."

The gate — if it could be called a gate — was an archway of black stone forty feet high, carved with figures that Bram's cartographer's eye itched to study properly: dragons and men intertwined, crowned figures kneeling, rivers of fire flowing between cities whose names had been worn smooth by centuries of hot wind. She memorized what she could. She always memorized. It was how she prayed, if she prayed at all.

Beyond the arch, the caldera opened around them like a cupped hand, and Bram forgot how to walk for a moment.

The inner fortress descended in those great concentric tiers she'd seen from below, and at the bottom of everything lay a lake of fire — not raging, not roaring, but *glowing*, a slow amber sea perhaps half a mile across, its surface shifting like the skin of cooling wax. Light from it painted the underside of every tier in gold. Bridges of dark stone arched over it, radiating outward from a central island where a single colossal shape lay coiled.

Bram had seen dragons in engravings. She had drawn them herself, once or twice, for children's books commissioned by merchants who wanted their sons to dream of slaying them. Nothing had prepared her for scale. The creature on the island was larger than the chapel in her valley town. Larger than the town square. Her mind kept trying to fold the sight into something manageable and failing, again and again, like a map too big for its page.

"That's—" Pip began.

"*Vess*," said a voice like a landslide learning to speak, "is trying to sleep."

The sound came from everywhere and nowhere, resonating up through the stone under their feet, through the soles of Bram's boots, through the bones of her legs. On the island, the coiled shape stirred. A head the size of a haywain lifted on a neck like a felled tree, and eyes opened — molten gold, slit-pupiled, ancient beyond anything Bram had words for. They swept across the five of them standing tiny on the bridge, and Bram felt those eyes pass over her like a hand passing over a flame, feeling for heat.

"So," the dragon said. Her voice was dry as old paper, sardonic as a jilted bride. "Sable Wren, who owes me a debt she's forgotten. Dorn of the Broken Banner, who still wears his shame like it's armor. A wolf and his wolf. And—" the golden eyes narrowed fractionally, "—a child thief, and a little gray bird of a woman with ink on her fingers."

"I'm a cartographer," Bram said, before she could stop herself.

A silence. Somewhere far above, stone ticked and settled in the heat.

"A cartographer," Vess repeated, tasting the word. Smoke curled from her nostrils in twin lazy ribbons. "How useful. Do come down, little cartographer. All of you. My stairs are long and my patience is shorter, and I have not had visitors worth the name in eleven years."

Her head lowered back toward the fire-lake, but her eyes stayed open, fixed on Bram, unblinking, until the company had crossed half the first bridge.

* * *

The descent took an hour. The tiers were hollowed with galleries and chambers, most of them dark, some of them clearly inhabited once — Bram passed a hall of shattered statues, a library whose shelves held nothing but ash, a courtyard where a fountain had run dry so long ago that mineral deposits had built a frozen white waterfall of their own. This had been a city once, she realized. Not a lair. A *city*, built around the fire, alive because of it.

Now it echoed.

"Eleven years," Bram murmured to Sable as they walked. "Since the last visitors?"

"Since the last *worth the name*," Sable corrected softly. Her face was unreadable in the amber light. "There are pilgrims, sometimes. Factions sending spies. Vess eats the ones who annoy her and ignores the rest. But people who come to *speak* with her—" the witch shook her head. "The world has forgotten how to listen to dragons. Or perhaps it never knew."

At the bottommost tier, a final stair led down to the island itself, across a span of stone so hot that Dorn wrapped his gauntlets in wet cloth before crossing. The fire-lake lapped at the island's edges with a sound like slow breathing. And there, close enough now that Bram could see the individual scales — each one the size of a dinner plate, gunmetal-grey edged in dull copper, thousands upon thousands of them, many cracked, some missing altogether, the flesh beneath scarred pale — the dragon watched them come.

Up close, the dying was unmistakable. Vess's hide hung loose in places, as if the body beneath had shrunk away from its own armor. Her breath came in long, labored draws that stirred the smoke. One of her forelegs lay at rest near the edge of the island, and Bram saw that the claws had gone brittle, chipped, translucent at the tips like old horn. The great golden eyes were clouded at the rims, cataract-pale, though the pupils within still burned sharp as knives.

"Well," Vess said. "The famous company of the Guttering Flame. Four humans, a wolf, and a debt. Sit. Stand. Fall down if you must; the stone is warm and no one has dusted in a century."

They settled along the island's edge, keeping a respectful distance that Vess visibly found amusing. Ash pressed close against Kaelen's leg, ears flat, a low continuous growl rumbling in his chest that he silenced with a hand on the wolf's ruff. Bram noticed the wolf would not look at the dragon directly. Wolves, she thought, probably understood better than people did what they were looking at.

"Now." Vess's head swung around with terrible grace, settling low so that her gaze could travel across each of them in turn. "You have walked a long way through a world that is quietly coming apart at its seams, and you have come to me, which means either desperation or destiny, and in my experience they smell identical. So. Speak your business."

Dorn started to speak — Bram saw him draw the breath — and Vess's tail moved.

It was a small movement, a flick, barely a gesture, and yet the displaced air alone staggered the knight sideways and shut his mouth with a click of teeth.

"Not you," the dragon said. "You I know. Your business I can recite: the truce frays, the factions stir, the fire gutters, and you want to know how long. I will tell you later, and you will not enjoy it." Her gaze slid on. "Witch. Sentinel. Child. Later, all of you. There is one among you I have not yet finished smelling."

The words dropped into Bram's stomach like stones into a well.

The golden eyes came to rest on her. This close, she could see her own reflection in them, tiny and wavering, a gray smudge of a woman framed in fire.

"You," Vess said.

Bram's mouth was dry. "Me."

"You carry paper that smells of blood and old kings. You carry it against your heart like a second heartbeat — do you think I cannot hear it? Do you think I cannot *smell* it?" The dragon's nostrils flared, drawing a long, deliberate breath, and the wind of it pushed Bram's hair back from her face. "Hartwell blood. Veil-marked. Twice-royal. Sitting on my island in a coat that doesn't fit her, telling me she's a *cartographer*."

The world went very quiet. Behind her, Bram heard Kaelen shift — heard the particular quality of his stillness, the kind that preceded violence — and heard Sable's soft intake of breath.

"I don't know what those words mean," Bram said. It wasn't entirely a lie. She knew the words now; she'd found her family name in the map's margins nine nights ago and had told no one, had carried the knowledge like a coal cupped in bare hands. But she didn't know what they *meant*, not truly, not yet. "I'm a mapmaker from a valley where nothing magical has ever happened. I didn't ask for any of this. I didn't know any of this existed a season ago."

"No," Vess agreed. "You didn't." Something moved behind the molten eyes — not menace, exactly. Something older and wearier. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. "That is precisely how it always begins. No one ever asks for the blood. They're just born with it, and then the world arrives to collect."

"And what does the world collect?" Bram asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "From me?"

For a long moment the dragon said nothing. The fire-lake breathed. High above, the caldera rim glowed against the darkening sky, and Bram thought about the darkened channels in the walls, the dead veins, the guttering.

"What the world collects," Vess said finally, "depends on what you choose to give it. Which is a crueler arrangement than most people expect." She drew her head back, up, away, the moment breaking like a thread under tension. "You will stay. All of you. The Emberhold has room, and I have time, and you have questions you don't yet know how to ask. We will get to them."

She turned her gaze toward the fire-lake, and Bram thought the audience was over — but as the company rose stiffly to withdraw, the dragon spoke once more, so quietly that only Bram caught it, pitched beneath the crackle of the flames like a secret folded into a letter:

"Little cartographer. When you understand what you carry — and you will, soon, sooner than you'd like — remember that I smelled it the moment you arrived, and told no one." The golden eye slid toward her, one slit pupil fixed and bright. "Ask yourself why a dying dragon keeps a dying secret. Then ask yourself what she might want from the one person in the world who could give it."

Bram stood rooted, her heart hammering against the folded map, against the hidden name written in its margins, against the second heartbeat the dragon claimed to hear.

"Come along, Bram," Sable called gently from the stair. "Let her rest."

Bram went. But at the top of the steps she looked back once, and found the dragon still watching her across the amber water — and above them both, high on the caldera wall, another channel of ancient fire flickered, stuttered, and went dark.

Vess's eyes closed slowly, as if in answer.

As if counting.
