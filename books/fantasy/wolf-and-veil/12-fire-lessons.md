## 12. Fire Lessons

Bram did not sleep.

She lay in the guest hollow the company had been given — a chamber carved into the caldera's inner wall, its floor worn smooth by centuries of feet, its single window a slit of glass that looked out over the amber lake and the great shape coiled upon it — and she listened to the fire die by inches.

She had learned, in her first night at Emberhold, to read the channels. Ancient veins of dragon-fire ran through the fortress like blood through a body: rivers of ember-light set into the stone, flowing up from the lake where Vess slept, branching through corridors and stairwells, feeding braziers that never needed tending, warming stone that had not been cold in a thousand years. The people of the Emberhold — a few dozen keepers, quiet and grey-robed, who spoke of the dragon the way farmers speak of weather — moved through this light as fish move through water.

But the light was failing. Bram had counted four darkened channels since her arrival. They went out the way stars go out — not with drama, but with a kind of embarrassed flicker, a stutter, a dimming that made you doubt you'd ever seen them lit at all. And each time one died, Vess's eyes closed a little more.

Now, in the small hours, she rose.

The map was in her pack, where it always was, folded against the hidden name. *Hartwell.* Written twice, once for each throne, in two different hands, centuries apart. She had not told the company what she'd found. She had barely admitted it to herself. But the name sat against her spine all night like a coal wrapped in cloth, and sleep was impossible while it burned.

She dressed, took no lamp — the dying channels gave enough light to walk by, if you didn't mind walking through a place that seemed to be holding its breath — and descended toward the caldera floor.

* * *

The heat met her at the last stair, a wall of it, gentle but absolute, like stepping into a mouth that meant no harm. The amber lake spread before her, luminous, thick as honey and moving with the same slow patience. And on it, half-submerged, Vess.

Up close, the last dragon was less majestic than terrible and more terrible than either. Her scales were the color of cooled lava, black shot through with fading orange seams, and many of those seams had gone dark — whole fields of scale gone matte and dead, like ash after the fire has passed through. She was vast beyond sense; Bram's eye kept trying to measure her and sliding off, the way an eye slides off the sun. One foreleg alone, folded beneath her breast, was the size of the cottage in the valley where Bram had grown up drawing maps of places she would never see.

"You come quietly," Vess said, without opening her eyes. "Most heirs come loudly."

"I'm not—" Bram stopped. The denial died somewhere in her throat, because they both knew what it was worth. She stood at the lake's edge, barefoot without having planned it, the heat soaking up through her soles. "You knew. That first day. You smelled it."

"I smell bloodlines the way you smell rain coming." The dragon's eye opened at last — a slit of molten gold, ancient and amused and unbearably tired. "Yours is a loud one, little cartographer. Two thrones' worth of shouting in one small body. I am surprised the whole valley didn't hear it."

"The valley can't hear anything." Bram heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't try to soften it. "Magic doesn't cross our borders. Nothing crosses our borders."

"Mm." Something moved in the golden eye that might have been pity. "And yet here you are."

Here she was. Bram looked down at her own hands — ink-stained, steady, hands that had drawn a thousand coastlines and crossed none of them — and felt the truth of it settle over her like the heat itself. Here she was. Heir to houses she'd never heard of a season ago. Carrier of a dead woman's secret. Standing at the bedside of the last fire in the world.

"Tell me what's happening to it," she said. "The flame. Sable won't say. Dorn won't say. They just look at the walls when the channels go out, like men watching a wound they can't close."

Vess was silent a long moment. Steam curled off the lake between them.

"Come closer, then," the dragon said. "And do not be afraid. Fear smells worse than bloodline, and I have had enough of both."

* * *

Bram waded in to her knees. The amber liquid was warm as bathwater and held her the way a hand holds a bird — firmly, carefully, with the constant suggestion that it could do otherwise. When she reached the great head, Vess turned one eye to her, and that eye was a window; Bram had the sudden vertiginous sense of looking down into a depth of years that made her lifetime seem a candle-flicker.

"Do you know what dragon-fire is?" Vess asked.

"Fire," Bram said. "Older fire."

"Everyone says older fire." A sound rolled through the dragon's chest that might have been laughter in some earlier age, before it had worn down to this dry rumble. "It is not fire. Fire burns wood, oil, fat — things. Dragon-fire burns *right*. It burns legitimacy. Do you understand me? Every flame I have ever breathed was fed not by fuel but by claim. By rightness. When the old kings rode to war behind my ancestors, the fire roared because their cause was true — true to *them*, at least, and true enough. And when a king lost his right — through treachery, through broken oath, through the slow rot of a crown worn too long — his dragons guttered under him, mid-flight, mid-battle, and he fell, and everyone knew why."

Bram thought of the battlefield Dorn had shown them. The field frozen mid-slaughter, soldiers locked in glass and ash, preserved by a breath of fire so total it had ended a war in a single exhalation. "The First War," she said slowly. "The peace. Your fire sealed it."

"My fire *is* the peace." For the first time, weariness cracked the sardonic voice wide open, and Bram saw the thing beneath: not a beast, not a monument, but a creature carrying something enormous alone for a very long time. "When both royal lines burned and neither heir remained, there was no one left with the right to unmake the truce. My breath froze the armies because there was nothing in any soldier's heart that could stand against a claim that no longer existed. Dead lines make perfect peace, little mapmaker. The dead want nothing."

"And now?"

"Now there is a living hand again, isn't there." The golden eye fixed on her, and Bram felt it the way she'd feel a finger pressed to her sternum. "Two lines, one body. The war's whole argument, alive and walking around and denying it beautifully."

"I don't want a throne."

"No one wants a throne. That is nearly the definition of a good ruler, and why there are so few." Vess shifted, and the lake shivered around Bram's legs. "But wanting is not the coin, child. *Legitimacy* is the coin. Right, not desire. My fire gutters because for three hundred years no legitimate claimant has tended it. The keepers feed me their devotion — it keeps my heart beating, barely. But devotion is not claim. I have been burning down my own body to hold the truce. Scale by scale. Channel by channel. You have watched me count."

The dark scales. The dead seams. Bram's throat tightened. "How long?"

"How long until I am ash and the truce dies with me?" The dragon considered. "A year, if I am careful. Less, now that I know what stands at the edge of my lake. Hope is expensive. I had stopped spending on it."

*What do I do,* Bram almost asked, and caught herself, because asking was the same as admitting, and admitting would change everything — Kaelen, the oath, the company, the fragile warm thing she had built among these people out of trust and shared bread and Pip's laughter. If she knelt at this lake and tended this fire, she became the heir in more than ink. She became the thing Kaelen was sworn to kill.

Vess watched her wrestle with it, and the ancient eye softened in a way Bram would remember all her life.

"I will teach you nothing tonight," the dragon said. "I will only show you. What you do after is your own weight, and no one else's — not even mine. Hold out your hand."

* * *

Bram held out her hand.

Vess brought her muzzle close — close enough that Bram could see the individual scales of her face, could feel breath like a forge door opening, could see deep in the golden eye a reflection of her own pale, frightened face — and exhaled. Not flame. Not heat, exactly. Something thinner and stranger, like light made breathable, like the moment just before dawn poured directly into her palm.

And the map — folded in her pack on the shore, ten paces away — flared.

Bram felt it through her whole body. Not pain; resonance. As if every line she'd ever drawn, every border she'd ever inked, every coastline she'd traced from secondhand accounts suddenly remembered what maps were for: not paper, but territory. Not record, but claim. The cartographer's oldest secret, the one no master in the valley had ever spoken aloud — that to draw a land truly is to possess it, and to be possessed by it.

"That," Vess said, withdrawing, "is what feeds me. That is what the fire eats. Not your blood. Your *right* — the right your blood carries. The right to draw borders and have them be real."

Bram stared at her open palm. In its center, faint as breath on cold glass, a small light lingered and faded.

"It hurt," she said, and found it wasn't quite true. "No. It didn't. It felt like—"

"Like coming home to a house you didn't know you owned," Vess said. "Yes. That feeling is the trap and the truth both. Be careful which one you feed."

* * *

She should have gone back to bed. She knew that even as she climbed from the lake, wrung out her hem, and did not go back to bed.

Instead she sat on the caldera shore with the map unfolded across her knees, and she looked at it — really looked — for the first time since finding the name. The realm of Veyra spread across it in her aunt's sure strokes: the forests that remembered names, the roads Kaelen had taught her, Duskmere's toll bridge, the ash-fields of the First War, and at the center the volcano-fortress, drawn with a precision that bordered on love. And along the margins, the marginalia she had once taken for decoration — little crowned serpents, twin sigils intertwined — now legible as what they were. Heraldry. Her heraldry. Both houses.

The map stirred under her fingers. It had done this since the beginning — the slow crawl of ink, the paths that redrew themselves nightly — but now, having touched the dragon's breath, Bram felt the movement differently. It wasn't restless. It was *hungry*. It wanted to be finished. It wanted a hand that had the right to finish it.

She looked at the darkened channel above, high on the caldera wall, where the fire had died the night she arrived.

And before she could talk herself out of it — before Kaelen or Sable or duty or fear could arrive to argue — Bram pressed her palm flat against the map, against the drawn image of Emberhold, and gave it what she had.

Not much. Not the whole claim; she hadn't the faintest idea how, and suspected she didn't want to know. Just a thread of it. Just the feeling the dragon's breath had left in her hand — that strange warmth, that rightness, that homecoming — pushed down through her fingers into the ink.

The map drank it. She felt it drink, the way soil drinks rain, and for one vertiginous heartbeat every line on the page glowed gold.

Above her, high on the caldera wall, the dead channel flickered.

Stuttered.

And relit.

The light ran along the ancient vein like blood returning to a numb limb, spilling down through the stone, and somewhere in the fortress a keeper cried out — joy, Bram realized later, or alarm, or both. On the lake, Vess's eyes opened fully for the first time since Bram had arrived, and the molten gaze found her across the amber water, and the dragon said nothing at all.

Which was, Bram understood, its own kind of promise. *Your weight. No one else's.*

She refolded the map. She walked back up through the waking corridors with wet boots and burning hands and a secret seated in her chest where the hidden name used to be — no, beside it; the name and the fire were learning to share the space. And she told herself, step by step, that tomorrow she would think about what she had done. Tomorrow she would weigh it properly, consider Kaelen, consider the oath, consider the armies she now knew were gathering at the edges of the map's attention like storm fronts.

Tomorrow.

Tonight she had fed a fire that had been starving for three hundred years, and the peace of the world had steadied under her palm like a pulse, and for the first time since a dying stranger had collapsed in her doorway, Bram Hartwell knew exactly what her hands were for.

She was halfway to her hollow, warm and terrified and quietly alight, when she rounded a corner and stopped dead.

Kaelen stood in the corridor ahead, dressed for the night watch, Ash a grey shadow at his knee. Between them and her, one of the newly relit channels flowed bright in the stone wall, gilding his face, and he was staring at it — staring at the impossible, resurrected fire — with an expression Bram had never seen him wear.

Not wonder.

Recognition. The look of a man doing arithmetic he had dreaded for weeks, and finally getting an answer he couldn't pretend away.

His eyes came up. Found hers. Found her wet boots, her flushed face, the hour.

"Bram," he said quietly. "Where have you been?"
