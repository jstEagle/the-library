## 15. Pip's Bargain

Pip had stolen things worth more than her own weight in silver. She had lifted a bishop's ring off his finger while he slept, a falconer's prize gyrfalcon from under its hooded perch, a jeweled knife from a merchant who kept it strapped to his chest like a second heart. None of it had ever made her hands shake.

The map did.

She lay awake in the cold camp below the ridge where the torches had finally stopped coming — the scouts had turned back at Ash's warning call, or pretended to — and she listened to the company breathe. Dorn's snores came slow and even, a man who could wake at a dropped coin but chose not to. Sable murmured in her sleep, half-words of hedge-cant that smelled faintly of rosemary when the wind carried them. Kaelen didn't snore at all; he lay on his side with his back to the fire and his hand resting on Ash's flank, and even asleep he looked like a drawn bow.

And Bram slept with the map beneath her head.

That was the whole trouble of it, really. Bram slept with it under her head the way a child sleeps on a loaf of bread in a famine winter, one arm curled over it, and every night the thing glowed faintly through the canvas of her pack, through her cloak, through her dreams — Pip was sure of it — a soft gold pulse like a heartbeat seen through skin.

*You're not stealing from her,* Pip told herself, for the hundredth time since the thought had first slunk into her mind three days ago, somewhere past the ash-fields, when Dorn had split the last heel of bread four ways and given Pip the largest quarter without comment, claiming he wasn't hungry. *You're stealing for her. For all of them.*

It didn't help. Lying about it to yourself is just lying twice.

Because here was the truth Pip had learned before she'd learned to read, learned in the gutter markets of Corvane before Sable had caught her lifting the old woman's herb-pouch and instead of turning her in had fed her stew and asked her name: hunger makes thieves of everyone eventually. The company was starving. Not starving-starving, not yet — Dorn still gave away his share and Sable still brewed nettle tea as if it were a kindness rather than a filler — but the passes were closed behind them, the scouts on the ridges were multiplying, and the Emberhold approaches were barren rock where nothing grew and nothing grazed. They had maybe four days of food if they ate small. Two if anyone got hurt.

And there was a buyer.

Pip had seen him at the toll bridge at Duskmere, standing apart from the memory-toll-keepers in a grey traveling cloak, watching their company cross with the particular patience of a man doing arithmetic. He'd spoken to her while the others paid their memories — while Bram had stood pale and hollow-eyed afterward, having given up something that made her cry without tears. The grey man had crouched to Pip's level, which grown men sometimes did because they thought it disarmed children, and said, *If you ever come across certain documents, girl, certain papers that move — there are folk in the low country who pay houses for such papers.* He'd pressed a token into her palm: a sliver of obsidian shaped like a fang. *Show this. Name your price.*

She'd thrown it in the river.

Then she'd gone back after dark and fished it out again, because Pip threw nothing away that might someday be food.

Now she turned the token over and over between her fingers in the dark, feeling its edges bite her thumb, and watched Bram sleep, and hated herself in the particular thorough way she hated everyone, including especially herself.

The company had chosen her. That was the poison in it. Nobody had ever chosen Pip before; people used her, fed her, pointed her at windows, but choosing — sitting her down at a fire and saying *you're with us now, little shadow, eat first* — that was new, and new things were fragile, and fragile things needed feeding, and feeding cost money, and money lived in other people's pockets.

Or rolled up in other people's maps.

* * *

She moved an hour before dawn, in the dead-grey time when even wolves doze deepest.

She'd planned it the way she planned everything, obsessively, in the theater of her skull where she rehearsed a theft forty times until her body knew it better than her mind. The trick with stealing from someone sleeping beside a wolf was not stealth — Ash would hear a moth land — it was misdirection. So she'd spent the evening being annoying. Asking Kaelen questions about wolf-bonding until he snapped at her, picking at Dorn's armor straps, begging Sable for another cup of tea she didn't want. A child who pesters all evening is a child whose absence registers as mercy. They'd be relieved she was quiet. Relief was the best blindfold ever invented.

She crawled the last two lengths on her belly, close enough to smell woodsmoke in Bram's hair, and eased the pack strap free of the sleeping woman's arm with the delicacy of a surgeon. Bram sighed and turned. Pip froze, one finger still hooked under leather, counting Bram's breaths the way other people counted sheep.

Four breaths. Six. Ten.

She drew the pack away, inch by inch, and the moment it cleared Bram's arm the map woke.

She felt it before she saw it — a warmth against her hip like a coal wrapped in wool — and when she glanced down, faint light seeped through the seams of the pack, gold as yolk, pulsing gently. Pip had spent weeks around the thing and never once been alone with it. Alone with it, it seemed almost eager. Like a dog hearing its leash.

She slipped out of the camp into the rocks above the stream, put a hundred paces between herself and the sleeping company, and only then let herself look.

The map unrolled across her knees and moved.

She'd seen it shift before, out of the corner of her eye, the way you see things in mirrors — a line redrawn here, a name faded there. But held openly, in the dark, away from its keeper, it was shameless. Rivers slid like slow worms. The mountains along the northern edge breathed, rising and falling in a rhythm Pip realized with a lurch matched her own breathing. And there, glowing fresh and wet as spilled ink, the road ahead: the switchbacks up the scree slopes, the black notch in the caldera wall, the word *EMBERHOLD* burning softly at the center of it all.

Around the edges, in margins crowded with a fine, cramped hand, were notes. Pip could read — Sable had beaten letters into her over two winters, sitting by fires exactly like this one — but these were hard, dense, the handwriting of someone writing fast and afraid.

*The fire fails by the season's turn,* one line said. *Without a claim to feed it, the truce dies with it.*

Another: *Both lines ended. Both lines ended. Both lines—* and then scratched out so violently the nib had torn the vellum.

And a third, smaller, written in different ink, older, brownish, and Pip went very still, because she knew what dried blood looked like on paper:

*The heir lives in the valley that forgets. She has my brother's stubborn chin and my mother's hands. If you find this before I reach her, forgive me. I meant to give her a choice. Everyone else will only give her a crown, or a grave.*

Pip sat in the freezing dark with the map breathing under her hands and understood, all at once, the way you understand a trap you're already standing in.

Bram wasn't carrying a treasure. Bram was carrying a target. The grey man at Duskmere hadn't wanted to buy a document — he'd wanted to buy a person, or the location of one, and Pip was holding the only address in the world.

She thought about the obsidian fang in her pocket. Houses, he'd said. Whole houses. Enough food to stuff Dorn stupid and buy Sable a proper cottage and keep Kaelen in arrowheads for life. Enough to never be hungry again, never watch the bread get split smaller and smaller, never lie awake doing arithmetic with her ribs.

All she had to do was sell her family.

The word arrived without asking permission and sat down in her chest and refused to leave. *Family.* When had that happened? When had the ragged lot of them — the knight with the broken oath, the witch with no coven, the sentinel who barely spoke, the valley-woman who cried silently after paying memory-tolls, the wolf who let her sleep curled against his flank in the coldest nights — when had they become the thing she'd spent nine years on the streets pretending she didn't want?

She thought of Dorn giving her the biggest quarter of bread. Of Sable teaching her letters, patient as stone, saying *again, little shadow, you'll thank me when you can read contracts before you sign them.* Of Kaelen, who never smiled at her, quietly repositioning himself on the march so he always walked on her blind side, between her and the drop.

Of Bram. Who had crossed a world she'd never known existed, who had paid a childhood at a toll bridge, who slept on a death warrant every night and didn't know it, and who had still, every single evening, checked that Pip's blanket was tucked against the wind.

Pip was a thief. Thieves survive by knowing exactly what things are worth.

She looked down at the map, glowing and helpless in her lap, and finally understood what it was worth, and it wasn't houses.

* * *

The sun was barely up when she came back into camp, and nobody was fooled for a moment. She'd known they wouldn't be. That was the other lesson of a lifetime thieving: you can steal anything from anyone except forgiveness.

Kaelen stood with his arms folded, Ash at his knee, and his face was carved out of the same granite as the slopes around them. Sable had her hands on her hips. Dorn sat by the dead fire sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, which was how Dorn prayed.

"I took it," Pip said, before any of them could build a speech. Her voice came out steadier than she'd budgeted for. "I was going to sell it. There was a man at Duskmere — grey cloak, obsidian fang token — offering houses for documents that move. I have his token. I was going to find him."

Silence. Somewhere upslope a rock cracked in the morning frost like a distant door.

"You'd have gotten us all killed," Kaelen said. Flat. Not angry, which was worse. "Not robbed. Killed. You understand that."

"Yes." She swallowed. "I know what it costs now. That's why I brought it back." Her hands shook suddenly, now, of all times, now that it was over, and she hated them for it. "I read it. Some of it. There's writing in the margins, in the corners where nobody looks, because nobody ever looks at the corners, that's the whole point of corners—" She was babbling and she made herself stop, made herself look at Bram, who had taken the map and was holding it against her chest without seeming to know she was doing it. "There's a note. About the heir in the valley that forgets. About someone's brother's stubborn chin."

Bram went white. Not startled-white. Understanding-white, the color of a person catching up to a truth that had been walking beside her for miles.

"You knew," Pip said. "Part of you knew. That's why you sleep on it like a shield."

"Pip," Sable began gently.

"Don't *Pip* me." The shake got into her voice after all. "I stole it because we're starving and I couldn't stand watching Dorn pretend he wasn't hungry. That's the truth, ugly as it comes. And I'm bringing it back because—" She stopped. Looked around the circle of them, at the faces she'd memorized without meaning to, the way she memorized exits. "Because I've sold things my whole life. Everything I ever stole, I'd have sold, if the price was right. I never had anything I wouldn't. And then I read some scratchy old dead woman's note about choice, and I looked at you lot, and I finally owned something worth more than houses, and I am *not selling it.*"

Her voice cracked on the last word and she stood there, twelve years old and furious about it, fists balled, waiting to be thrown out.

Dorn laughed. It was a short, rough sound, more cough than mirth, but it broke the tension like ice cracking off a roof. "First honest thief I've ever met," he said. "They usually lie about returning things too."

"Starving," Kaelen repeated, and his gaze went around the camp — the empty provisions rack, the thin faces — and something in it shifted, guilt moving behind granite. "We should have spoken of it. Instead we all pretended."

"We're none of us good at needing things," Sable said. She crossed to Pip and knelt, and took the girl's face in both her weathered hands, thumbs brushing the grime off her cheeks the way she did every few days whether it needed doing or not. "You came back. Do you know how rare that is? In all my years, everyone who ever took from me — the ones who mattered were the ones who came back."

"I want it noted," Pip said thickly, "that I also came back with the token. Evidence. I'm basically a soldier now."

"Basically," Dorn agreed gravely.

But it was Bram who settled it. She crossed the camp, map still clutched to her sternum, and crouched down to Pip's level — the same level the grey man had chosen at Duskmere, and the difference between those two gestures was a whole moral education — and looked at her with eyes that had been crying without tears at a toll bridge and were dry now.

"You read the margins," Bram said quietly. "Then you know what I am. What they'll pay for."

Pip nodded.

"And you brought it back anyway."

"You're not a price," Pip said. "I did the sums. You don't come out higher than the rest of them."

Bram laughed — a surprised, broken-edged sound — and pulled her into a hug that smelled of woodsmoke and cold mornings, and Pip let herself be held for exactly four seconds, which was three seconds longer than she'd allowed anyone in nine years.

"Right," said Kaelen, and his voice had changed, the granite ground down to something urgent. He was looking not at them but at the treeline beyond the stream, where the frost still smoked in the early light. "We move within the hour. Because if our little shadow could find that buyer in one day of looking—"

"—then his masters already know roughly where we are," Sable finished grimly.

Ash raised his head from his paws. His ears swiveled north, toward the scree slopes, toward the black notch in the caldera wall where the Emberhold waited. Then the big wolf stood, hackles lifting along his spine, and loosed a sound none of them had heard from him before — low, continuous, a growl with no end in it, aimed at the empty hills.

"He smells them," Kaelen said quietly. "More than scouts, this time."

Pip dug the obsidian fang from her pocket, weighed it once in her palm — houses, whole houses — and hurled it as far as her arm could throw, out over the stream, into the rocks where no one would ever fish it out again.

"Emberhold?" she asked, falling into place at Bram's side as the company shouldered their packs.

"Emberhold," Bram said, and unrolled the living map against the morning wind to show them the way.
