## 16. The Siege of Emberhold

Sable felt the armies coming the way a tooth feels frost — not as sound, not as sight, but as a slow ache spreading through everything she touched. She had been reading the land's hum since before dawn, palms flat against the black glass of the caldera's rim, and what she read made her old bones want to lie down and refuse the day.

Two hosts. Two hearts beating out of step with each other, both marching on the same mountain.

She climbed down from the rim on legs that complained at every rung of the switchback stair, past the terraces where Dorn was drilling Pip in spear-work — badly, on both their parts, but with the fierce good humor that had kept this ragged company alive through years that would have broken better soldiers — and found Kaelen at the gatehouse with Ash pressed against his side like a shadow given weight.

"You feel it too," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Feel it? I've been tasting it." Sable lowered herself onto the bench beside the brazier, which burned low and orange and wrong. Dragon-fire never flickered. That had always been its horror and its comfort — a flame that did not dance, that stood like a pillar of judgment, indifferent to wind. This morning it guttered. It leaned. It threw shadows that moved when they shouldn't.

"How long?" Kaelen asked.

"Since we brought her up the mountain." She said it plainly because there was no kindness left in dressing it. "The fire's been starving for decades, boy. But it's been *changing* since Bram started feeding it. And things change fastest just before they break."

Kaelen's jaw worked. Since the cold camp — since his confession and Bram's fury and the terrible, fragile honesty that had followed — he had been walking around like a man who had set down a stone so heavy he'd forgotten he'd been carrying it, only to find his hands still shaped to hold it. He looked at the guttering flame the way other men looked at open graves.

"The scouts," he said. "Vess says they've stopped circling. They've committed."

"Both of them?"

"Both." He named them without naming them, the way everyone in Veyra had learned to. The Ashen Host out of the west, wearing the colors of the fallen House Vane — the line that had ruled the northern throne before dragon-fire ended the war by ending everyone who had a right to either crown. And from the south and east, the Concord of the Veiled Sun, carrying the banners of House Merrow, or what claimed to be House Merrow, which after ninety years of annihilation amounted to anyone ambitious enough to sew a merrow-lily onto a standard.

"And each of them thinks," Sable said slowly, "that if they can put their hands on the last heir, they own the fire."

"They don't know she's here. Not certainly."

"No. But they know something woke the flame." She nodded toward the caldera's heart, where the light had steadied, faintly, over the past week — a pulse where there had been flatline. "Fire that's dying doesn't get better on its own. Every hedge-caster and war-priest in either army can smell that. So they come. Not for her, perhaps. At first. For whatever's feeding it. And then—" she spread her knotted hands, "—then someone finds out what she is, and it stops being a march and becomes a hunt."

Below them, in the courtyard, Bram was helping Dorn stack the last of the fire-barrels along the gate wall. Her sleeves were rolled up, her dark hair tied back with a strip of red cloth Pip had stolen for her from gods-knew-where, and she was laughing at something the girl had said — a short, surprised laugh, the laugh of a woman still learning that laughter could be hers.

Sable watched Kaelen watch her, and felt the dread in her chest tighten another notch. Because she had known Kaelen since he was a half-starved boy the wolves wouldn't leave alone, and she had never once seen him look at anything the way he looked at that woman. And she had also read the omens three nights running, and every one of them said the same thing: *the mountain will be ringed by morning.*

*Every choice is narrowing,* she thought. *And narrowed choices are how good people end up doing monstrous things with clear consciences.*

* *

By midday the first dust-plumes were visible from the walls, two smudges on opposite horizons like bruises forming on the sky. By late afternoon you could see the glint of them — spear-points and banner-hardware catching the low sun, an ocean of small bright cruelties flowing toward the mountain's roots.

Sable spent those hours doing the unglamorous arithmetic of siege. Counting arrows: too few. Counting barrels of oil and kindling: fewer than that. Counting defenders: seven, if you counted the wolf, and the dragon, and a cartographer whose only weapon was a map that occasionally tried to kill her.

"We should go," Dorn said quietly, beside her on the wall. The old knight had cleaned and oiled his armor until it shone like a promise, though they both knew shine meant nothing to an arrow. "Slip out the postern tonight, before the rings close. Take the high passes. I know routes the Ashen scouts have never mapped."

"And leave the fire untended? Leave *her*?"

"She's the reason the armies are coming. Every league we put between Bram Hartwell and this mountain is a league closer to peace."

"That was true a month ago." Sable didn't look away from the approaching dust. "It isn't true now. Ask your armor why."

Dorn frowned at her. She tapped her own sternum. "Because the fire's stronger than it was. Feel the air up here — the old wards in the rock are waking. Whatever Bram's been feeding Vess, it's holding more than the truce together. There are things sleeping under this caldera that were bound by the First Burning, and if the flame goes out while the armies stand inside the gates—" She let the sentence die, because the rest of it was the kind of thing you didn't say aloud within earshot of frightened people. "We run, and maybe she lives. We stay, and maybe everything lives. Those are our choices now, Dorn. Narrow ones. I hate narrow ones."

"I've noticed," he said dryly, and put his hand briefly on her shoulder, the way he'd done for thirty years across a hundred lost causes, and went back down to make the walls ready anyway.

That was the thing about Dorn, Sable thought. He'd argue with her until the stars burned out, and then do exactly what needed doing without one word of grievance. Exile had taught him that much: causes came and went, but the work in front of your hands stayed honest.

Pip appeared at her elbow, silent as the child was never silent when there wasn't trouble brewing. "Sable. The dragon wants you. Says the map-girl's asking questions again, and Vess says—" the child dropped into a fair imitation of the dragon's gravel-and-ember voice— "*if the witch won't come tell the heir true things, I'll tell her untrue ones, and see which she likes better.*"

Sable sighed. "That creature has gotten *worse* since the fire picked up."

* *

Down in the deep gallery beneath the Emberhold — the corridor of fused glass where Vess coiled through the dark like a river of cooling lava — the air was warm and tasted of iron and old smoke. Bram sat on a shelf of black stone with the living map spread across her knees, and even from ten paces Sable could see it moving: roads crawling like veins, the margins filling and emptying with script no living hand had written.

"Show me," Bram said, without looking up. Her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "Don't soften it. Don't do the thing where you all decide among yourselves what I can bear. Show me what happens tomorrow."

So Sable sat down on the warm stone beside her and told her. Both armies. The numbers. The arrows counted and uncounted. The truth about what slept beneath the caldera, bound by the first great burning, and what might wake if the fire failed while armed men stood on the bindings.

Bram listened the way she drew maps, Sable thought — completely, hungrily, committing every line to memory as though a thing understood precisely could be survived precisely.

"My aunt crossed a dead valley," Bram said at last, "to bring me a folded piece of skin, and died on my floor before she could say why. And I used to think the cruelest thing fate ever did to me was drag me here." She folded the map closed, gently, the way you'd close a dying man's eyes. "Now I think the cruelest thing is that it waited. All these years, the fire was failing, the heirs were ash, and nobody came for me until there was barely time to matter."

"There's time," Sable said.

"There's tomorrow," Bram corrected, and smiled, and it was the smile of someone standing at the edge of a very tall cliff deciding to enjoy the view. "Go on, Sable. Go count your barrels. I'm going to sit with Vess a while. Someone should keep the old lizard company while she pretends she doesn't need it."

Behind them in the dark, vast eyes opened like twin furnaces banked low, and a voice like grinding continents said, "I heard that, inkling."

Sable left them to it — the dying god and the doomed girl, keeping each other company in the warm dark — and climbed back toward the light, and hated every stair.

* *

The armies arrived in the last hour of daylight, which was tactically stupid and psychologically brilliant, and told Sable that whoever commanded the Ashen Host understood that sieges were fought mostly in the mind.

They came out of the western passes like a spill of ink, rank on rank, and halted just beyond bowshot in a silence so complete it rang. No drums. No shouts. Nine thousand men and women standing in the dusk, watching the fortress, waiting. And from the south, an hour behind, the Veiled Sun's columns flowed into position with equal discipline, torches lit in deliberate answer — a second ring of firelight closing around the mountain's knees.

From the gatehouse wall, the view was almost beautiful. Sable had seen enough beauty used as a weapon in her long life to distrust it completely.

"They're not attacking tonight," Dorn murmured. "No ladders, no rams. They're showing us the shape of tomorrow."

"They're negotiating," Sable said. "With us and with each other. Look at the center — the Ashen command tent and the Sun's pavilion are pitched facing each other. There'll be envoys crossing that ground before midnight. Truce parley. They hate each other nearly as much as they want what's inside these walls, and each one fears the other will take it first."

"What do they want?"

"An audience." She watched the distant torches. "Each commander will claim they only wish to speak with the keeper of the flame. Neither will name her. Neither will admit they know. But somewhere in one of those camps is a man or woman who has already guessed, because the fire answered her, and answers like that get reported." She turned. "Kaelen. When they send the envoy up — and they will send one envoy, alone, disarmed, because even vultures observe the forms — I want to be the one who meets them at the gate."

"Why you?"

"Because I'm old, I'm harmless-looking, and I lie better than any of you." She smiled thinly. "Also because I want to hear which lies they choose. A lie tells you more than a confession, if you listen right."

The envoy came two hours past full dark, as predicted: a single rider in grey and gold, bearing a white branch of rowan — the old sign of parley — and reining up fifty paces from the gate while horns from both camps sounded the same three notes, a harmony of mutual suspicion.

Sable met him on the causeway with Dorn at her shoulder and Kaelen visible on the wall above, bow strung but lowered. The envoy was younger than she'd expected, with the careful blankness of a man chosen precisely because nothing showed on his face.

"Keeper of the mountain," he said, inclining his head. "My lords of the Ashen Host and the Concord of the Veiled Sun send joint greeting."

"Joint," Sable said pleasantly. "How cozy. Do they share a blanket at night, your lords?"

Something flickered behind the young man's blankness — the ghost of a swallowed smile. "They send a question. The flame above you has strengthened. The old binding-songs stir in the earth; every listening order in Veyra has felt it. The lords wish to know: who tends the fire?"

"A dragon tends the fire," Sable said. "As she has for ninety years. Your lords' grandfathers burned the world rather than share a throne; forgive me if their grandchildren's curiosity seems a little rich."

"The dragon fades. Everyone knows the dragon fades." His eyes lifted, just slightly, past her shoulder — to the gate, to the walls, measuring, counting, searching. "But fading fires do not revive. Something changed. My lords ask only to see the change. Offer hospitality to whichever hand restored the flame. No harm is intended to the mountain's household. You have my word."

"Your word," Sable repeated softly. "Given for two armies. How many men does each army hold, that your single word must cover them all?"

The blankness held, but barely. "Nine thousand. Eleven. The counts vary; desertion is common in holy causes."

"And if the mountain's household declines this generous offer of hospitality?"

The envoy let the silence stretch, and in that stretch Sable heard everything: the creak of eleven thousand throats swallowing, the arithmetic of ladders not yet built, the patience of hunger.

"Then the lords will grieve," he said, "that the flame's restoration could not be protected. Fires are fragile things, keeper. Especially new ones."

He bowed, turned his horse, and rode back down the causeway into the sea of torchlight, and Sable stood watching until the ranks closed behind him like water.

"Dorn," she said, without turning. "Wake everyone. Wake *her*. Wake the dragon. And start moving the wounded-corner supplies down to the inner gallery, because as of tonight, I no longer believe we hold the outer wall."

* *

They gathered in the gatehouse long after midnight — all of them, even Vess, who filled the lower yard like a hillside that had learned to breathe, her scales dull as cooled slag in the torchlight, her ancient head resting on her forefolds so her furnace-eyes could regard them all at once.

Sable laid it out plainly, because plainness was the last mercy left. "Two armies. Twenty thousand between them. We have one wall, one dragon, one wolf, one knight, one thief, one witch, and one mapmaker. Tomorrow they parley with each other, and by tomorrow night one of three things happens: they agree to split whatever's in here, they fight each other on our doorstep, or they join — and gods help us all if they join, because a shared enemy is the only sacrament both those churches recognize."

"Then we give them nothing to split," Kaelen said. "We take Bram out. Tonight, through the postern, ahead of the full closure—"

"No."

Everyone turned. Bram stood in the gatehouse doorway, and Sable saw at once that something had settled in her face — some final grain of decision, sifted and sorted, come to rest. She'd seen that look before, on faces much older, in moments much worse.

"The fire holds the binding under this mountain," Bram said. "If I run and the flame gutters while twenty thousand soldiers stand on what's buried here, everything dies — them, us, the valley, probably the world's whole eastern half. And if I stay and they take me—" she glanced at Vess, "—they make me a weapon, or worse, a *banner*, and the war starts over with my face on it. So." She took a breath. "There's a third option. Isn't there, Vess? You've known it since the day you smelled my blood. You've all known it. You've all been so careful not to say it."

The silence in the yard went absolute. Even the guttering flame above seemed to lean in.

"Child," Vess said at last, and her voice was gentler than Sable had ever heard it, gentle the way mountains are gentle to the rain, "there are doors in that direction I will not open for you. Not tonight. Tonight we hold the wall. Tonight we are seven, and a wolf, and one impossible old lizard, and that has been enough against worse odds than these."

"It hasn't," Dorn said quietly. "But we've never once been sorry."

Above them, far off on the western ridge, a horn sounded — one note, then two, then the long rising call that no army used except for one purpose. Sable knew it from the First War's histories, from songs she'd learned before she learned her letters.

The assault signal.

"Not tomorrow, then," Sable breathed. "Tonight. Gods' teeth — they're not even going to pretend to parley first."

Torches began moving on the slopes below, dozens of them, then hundreds, flowing downhill toward the gates like embers rolling off a burning field — and from the southern lines, answering horns, and the deep groan of something enormous being wheeled forward on wooden wheels.

Kaelen drew his sword, and the sound of it was very small and very loud at once. Ash rose beside him with her hackles up and her lips peeled back from her teeth, and her growl rolled across the yard like the first word of a storm.

"Bram," Sable said, gripping the woman's arm, turning her away from the walls, away from the torches, toward the dark mouth of the deep gallery where the last fire stood guttering. "Whatever you were about to decide just now — whatever door you saw —"

"I know," Bram said. And she was already moving toward the flame.
