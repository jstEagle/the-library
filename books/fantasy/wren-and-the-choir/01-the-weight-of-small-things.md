## 1. The Weight of Small Things

The rain in Vell never fell straight down. It came slantwise off the harbor, tasting of brine and chimney smoke, and it fell hardest on the nights when Coral had the most work to do. Tonight was one of those nights.

They stood beneath the eave of the Lanternmongers' Row, watching the widow's window three streets over. The light behind the glass was the color of weak tea — a single oil lamp turned low, the kind of light a person kept for company they didn't want to admit they needed. Coral had been watching it for an hour. Watching was most of the work, if you did it properly. The taking itself took almost no time at all.

*Rule one,* their old instructor's voice said in memory: *Never take what you cannot carry.* Coral had seven years of other people's carried things stacked inside them now, and the weight of them had settled into their bones like river silt. They had learned to walk with it. They had learned to sleep with it, curled around the quiet pressure like a body learning to share its bed with stones.

They crossed the street when the lamplighter passed and left his shadow behind him. The widow's door was latched with a simple iron bolt — Vell's wards ate magic alive, but they did nothing against a bent knife and patience — and the stairwell smelled of boiled cabbage and old lavender. On the third landing, Coral paused beneath the window they'd watched from below, and let themselves become small. This was the part that couldn't be taught, the part that had made the Conservatory masters weep with envy before they wept with fear: the folding-down of a self until it fit through the cracks of a room unnoticed.

The widow sat by her lamp with her hands folded on a table, as though waiting for someone to arrive who had died long ago. Her name was Odile Brask, and everyone in the tenement knew she had buried her husband nine winters past and her son two winters after that, and that she spoke to neither of them anymore because grief, unlike love, learns manners.

Coral crouched in the corner of her room, in the shadow behind the loom, and breathed.

Taking a memory was not like theft in any way the wardens' manuals described. There was no cutting, no tearing. It was more like lifting a ring of keys from a hook while the owner slept: done gently enough, done *lovingly*, the absence went unfelt for hours, sometimes days. The trick was to take only one thing, and to leave everything else hanging undisturbed around the space where it had been.

Coral reached out along the thin silver thread of the woman's attention and found what they were looking for, because they always found it — people kept their best days close to the surface, worn smooth from handling. A wedding day. Nineteen years old and raining, of course, raining the way Vell always rained, but she hadn't minded because Tomas held an oilcloth over both their heads and complained about his soaked boots the entire walk to the chapel, and she remembered thinking, with a clarity that still ached across nineteen years, *I will keep this. I will keep this exact moment forever.*

Coral kept it for her.

It came away soft and warm and heavy as bread fresh from an oven, and Coral caught it in the cupped hollow of their chest where stolen things went, and felt it settle among the others with a small sighing sound only they could hear. In the chair, Odile Brask blinked slowly, frowned faintly at her own hands, and decided she was tired. She banked her lamp and went to bed without knowing she was poorer than she'd been at supper.

Coral let themselves out the window and down the drainpipe into the rain.

* * *

The night market of Vell ran in the drowned quarter, in the streets the sea had claimed a generation ago and the city had simply agreed to abandon. At high tide the water came to your knees; at low tide it left the cobblestones gleaming black and slick as beetle shells, strung with lanterns on lines between the drowned doorframes. Everything sold there was technically contraband, which in Vell meant it was merely taxed less honestly.

Coral arrived as the stalls were lighting and took up their usual position near the old customs house, whose marble steps rose out of the shallows like the jaw of something patient. They set out their tray: small glass charms on knotted cords, each one holding a curl of colored smoke that moved when you looked at it directly.

The memories were not visible, exactly. That was the artistry. Coral wrapped each stolen hour inside a shell of harmless suggestion — *good fortune, safe roads, a lucky day* — so that what the buyer actually received was the feeling of the memory without its content. The widow's wedding day would go home tonight around some fishwife's neck as a charm against storms, and all night the fishwife would dream of warm bread and wet boots and being nineteen and certain, and she would wake believing herself blessed, and she would be right, in a way no warden could prove.

"Charms," Coral called, soft-voiced. "Luck-charms. Fresh luck." The words tasted like ash every time, and they had stopped noticing years ago, and they noticed again now, tonight, for no reason they cared to examine.

The buyers came in ones and twos, as they always did. A dockhand wanting safety for a crossing. A young man with a bruised face buying a charm for courage he didn't know was really somebody else's wedding morning. An old man who bought nothing but stood looking at the tray a long time and finally said, "My wife used to buy these. Said they were warmer than church." He shuffled off into the dark before Coral could decide whether that was a compliment or an accusation.

Business was good. Business was always good. Grief and hope were the two great markets of Vell, and Coral dealt in both, and the coins piled up in their pocket with the same weightless heaviness as everything else they owned.

Then the woman came.

She was perhaps fifty, gray-streaked, dressed in mourning that had been good once and mended often, and she picked up the newest charm — the widow's wedding, barely two hours cold — and held it up to the lantern light. The little ghost of smoke inside stirred and turned gold.

"This one's different," she said.

"They're all different."

"No." She turned it in her fingers. Her eyes, when they lifted, were sharp and red-rimmed and terribly awake. "This one's *full*. Like a jar versus an empty jar. You can tell." She smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile. "You're new at hiding it, whoever you are. Or you're tired. Which is worse, for your kind."

Coral's hand was already closing around the tray's edge, calculating the distance to the water, the crowd, the dark mouth of the flooded arcade. Seven years in Vell and no one had ever looked at them and seen anything but a charm-seller with a talent for suggestion — because Vell's wards smothered the Choir-magic down to a whisper, because they had spent seven years learning to sing under the floorboards of their own voice.

"How much?" the woman asked.

"For the charm?"

"For whatever's really in it." She set three silver marks on the tray, too many, deliberately. "I buried my daughter this spring. I don't want luck. I want one hour of not being in this city with this hole in me. Sell me the warm one."

And there it was. The oldest temptation, the one Coral had built every rule to protect themselves from: someone standing in front of them, asking openly for exactly the thing they had just stolen from someone else.

*Rule two: Never sell the truth of a thing. Rule three: Never look too long at what you've taken. Rule four: Never, ever give it back.*

Because giving it back meant admitting it was theirs to hold. And once you admitted that, the whole edifice came down — every charm, every coin, seven years of quiet survival — and underneath it was just a conduit of the outlawed Choir, standing in a forbidden market, holding a widow's joy in their chest like a coal they had no right to.

"It's a luck-charm," Coral said quietly. "Safe roads, gentle weather. That's what it does."

The woman looked at them for a long moment. Whatever she saw in their face made her nod slowly, as if confirming something sad she'd suspected. She took the charm anyway, and paid the three marks, and tied it around her neck with hands that shook slightly, and walked off into the drowned streets murmuring to herself — and even from behind, Coral could see the change in her shoulders, the borrowed certainty settling over her like a coat.

That was the worst part. Not the taking. The taking was clean. The worst part was how well it worked — how a stolen hour, warmed over and resold, could genuinely mend a stranger's night. The whole city ran on secondhand grace, and nobody knew, and everybody was fed.

Coral packed the tray as the tide began to come in, black water sliding up the customs-house steps, licking at the lantern-lines. Their pocket held eleven marks and change. Their chest held a wedding day that belonged to Odile Brask, and beside it a fisherman's last morning with his father, and a child's first snow, and a soldier's letter read by firelight, and hundreds more, thousands, a library of other people's brightest hours pressed flat inside them like flowers in a book no one would ever open.

Some nights the weight was nothing. Some nights it was everything. Tonight it was everything, and Coral walked home through the rising water with the rain coming down sideways, and thought, as they thought every night, *one more year. One more year of quiet, and then—*

Then what? They had never finished the sentence. Finishing it required a destination, and destinations were dangerous, and so the sentence trailed off every time like a road dissolving into fog.

Their rooms above the chandler's shop on Saltpetre Lane were cold and neat and held nothing of themselves. No portraits, no letters, no keepsakes. A person who might be searched at any hour could afford no evidence of a life. Coral banked the stove, counted the coins twice, and lay down on the narrow bed with their boots off and their coat on, which was how you slept in Vell if you were smart.

Sleep came slowly. And when it came, it brought the dreams it always brought — not their own dreams, they had none of those left worth dreaming, but fragments rising from the hoard. A kitchen smelling of cardamom. Hands braiding a child's hair. Someone singing low, wordless, a melody Coral recognized with a jolt of homesickness for a place they could not name —

They woke before dawn with wet eyes and no memory of why, which was its own familiar mercy.

Down in the street, the first lamplighter was making his rounds, and the fog off the harbor was thick enough to drink. Coral stood at the window watching the city assemble itself out of the gray, and told themselves, in the flat competent voice they used for everything: *Today you'll restock. Today you'll find another widow, another bridegroom, another soldier. Today you'll be careful, and quiet, and invisible, one more day.*

It was a good plan. It had worked for seven years.

Which is why it unsettled them, more than the dream, more than the sharp-eyed mourner, when they opened their door at first light and found, pinned to the frame with a rusted nail, a printed notice — one of dozens papering the lane, the walls, the chapel doors:

*BY ORDER OF THE WARDENS OF VELL: In light of recent irregularities, the penalty for crimes of sorcery is hereby raised to the GLEANING. Report resonance. Report song. Report your neighbors.*

Below it, freshly inked, someone had added a single line in a hurried hand:

*The stars are wrong. God help us all.*

Coral read it twice. The stars had been perfectly ordinary last night — they had checked, the way they checked every night, an old habit from the Conservatory they'd never managed to break.

But the notice was dated yesterday.
