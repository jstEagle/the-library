## 2. Seven Years of Quiet

The wards woke with the dawn.

Coral had learned to feel them the way sailors feel weather in their teeth — a low hum beneath the cobblestones, a pressure behind the eyes, a faint taste of copper at the back of the throat that meant Vell was awake and watching and remembering what it was for. The city's anti-magic lattice ran under every street like a second set of veins, silver-bright and merciless, and every morning it pressed against Coral's gift the way a hand presses down on a lid.

They lay still in their rented room above the chandler's shop and let the pressure settle. It never quite settled. It pooled instead, gathering in the hollows of them — behind the sternum, in the wrists, along the jaw where a song wanted to live. Seven years of this. Seven years of waking to a city that hummed *no*.

They got up anyway. They dressed in gray. They ate bread that tasted of nothing much, which was a mercy, because Coral had learned that everything with a strong flavor carried a strong memory, and strong memories were dangerous things to swallow before you'd decided what to do with them.

The widow's wedding day was still folded in their chest from last night, tucked behind the ribs like a letter they hadn't opened. They could feel it there, warm as a coal. Somewhere across the city, an old woman was going about her morning without the memory of the best afternoon of her life, and she would not miss it — that was the trick of it, the terrible elegant trick — because Coral always took the memories people were already losing. The ones gone soft at the edges. The ones the mind had begun to shed like winter coat. A wedding fifty years past, worn smooth by repetition, kept alive now only in the telling: those could be lifted whole and the thief would leave no wound.

That was Coral's rule, the first of many rules, the rule above all rules: *never take what someone is still holding.*

The second rule was quieter, and Coral broke it a little every day just by existing: *never let them see what you are.*

* * *

Seven years ago there had been a conservatory in the hills, white-walled, singing.

Coral remembered it in pieces now, deliberately broken pieces, because whole memories of the Choir were contraband even inside their own skull. If a warden ever pulled them apart — and wardens could pull, they had ways, needles and hymns and long patient questions — Coral did not want to be caught carrying anything that sang. So they had shattered their childhood into shards and buried the shards in different corners of themselves, and some mornings, reaching for one, they found only the hole where it had been, and had to decide whether the forgetting was theirs or something they'd done on purpose.

But some things stayed sharp despite everything.

The practice hall at dawn, with its windows like tall pale graves. Forty children standing in rows, breathing together until forty breaths became one breath, and then the Master — Master Ellery Vane, thin as a censer-chain, with hands that moved like herons wading — would lift his arms, and the first note would rise out of the floor itself, out of the stone, out of the ground the conservatory stood on, and the children would catch it and carry it.

That was how the Choir worked. That was what nobody outside ever understood. The singers didn't make the music; they *caught* it. There was a sound underneath the world — Coral had heard it once, truly heard it, the year they turned twelve — a vast slow chord that the land itself was humming, and the Choir's gift was the ability to reach down into that chord and pluck it, bend it, turn it toward a purpose. Healing. Growing. Mending walls. Carrying messages through solid rock. And other purposes, taught last, taught only to the chosen: the taking of memory, the folding of a person's years into a charm small enough to wear at the throat.

Coral had been chosen. Of course Coral had been chosen. They had been the best of their year, the one whose voice sat closest to the ground-chord, the one the Masters watched with bright hungry eyes.

And then there had been the Rite.

They did not let themselves remember the Rite in order. In order it went: a girl named Wren — no. No, that wasn't right either; that name belonged to something else, something later, something Coral kept in a locked room at the very bottom of themselves and visited only in dreams. In order it went: a cold room below the conservatory, and a boy strapped gently, kindly, into a chair of white wood, and Master Vane saying *this is a mercy, Coral, he is dying anyway, and his last years will live forever in the reliquary where the whole Choir can visit them,* and Coral standing at the edge of the circle with their hands shaking, understanding — twelve years old and understanding completely, in the way children understand things, all at once and forever — that the mercy was real and the theft was also real and both things were true at the same time, and that if they sang the taking-note their voice would do it beautifully, because their voice did everything beautifully, and that afterward they would be the kind of person who did this, permanently, the way a bell is permanently the shape of its casting.

They had refused.

They remembered the refusal better than anything. Not the words — there hadn't been words, or if there had been, the wards of Vell have since eaten them — but the feeling of it, the whole-body *no*, the sensation of reaching down for the ground-chord and finding, for the first time in their life, that their own heart was louder.

What came after the refusal lived in fragments. Running. Rain. A road. Someone shouting their true name across a courtyard — their *true* name, the one given at their first singing, the one that resonated, the one that could call them home across any distance — and Coral running faster, running until their lungs tore, running out of the hills and down into the flat gray country and across the border into Vell on a night when the wards were being renewed and the gates stood open and drunk carters waved them through without looking at their face.

They had been fourteen. They had arrived in Vell with rain in their shoes and a voice full of broken music and not one coin, and they had stood in the night market among the fish-stalls and the fortune-tellers and understood, with a clarity that has never left them, that they had traded everything for nothing except the right to keep their hands empty.

* * *

Now they walked to work.

Work, for Coral Underhill, meant the Shambles — the crooked lane behind the tanneries where the charm-sellers clustered, licensed and unlicensed, honest and otherwise. Coral was unlicensed but discreet, which in Vell amounted to nearly the same thing. They sold luck. Everyone in the Shambles sold luck; it was the one commodity the wardens didn't question, because luck couldn't be proven and therefore couldn't be prosecuted. Dried clover. Knucklebones. Rabbit feet. And, three stalls down from the clover-seller, at a table so cluttered with junk it looked like an accident, a quiet young person with gray eyes who sold small glass beads on leather cords and swore, when asked, that they were only beads.

They were not only beads.

Each bead held a memory — a good one, a lucky one, a moment when the world had said *yes* to somebody. A sailor's first sight of open water. A baker pulling the perfect loaf. A child's first snow. Coral whispered each one into its bead with a technique that was half Choir-craft and half their own invention, refined over seven years of necessity: a hum pitched below hearing, a breath shaped like a word, and the memory would come up out of their chest and pour itself into the glass like honey into a jar. The buyer wore the bead and felt, without knowing why, that the world was on their side. Luck, they called it. Coral called it what it was: other people's joy, rented out by the hour.

It was a good living, if you didn't think about it. Coral had gotten very good at not thinking about it, in the way that a person gets good at walking on a rope over a long drop — total concentration, total discipline, and never, ever looking down.

The wards made it possible. That was the joke of it, the bitter private joke Coral told themselves on bad nights: the thing that suffocated them also saved them. Inside Vell's lattice, their gift was muffled to a whisper. They couldn't take a memory by force here, couldn't sing, couldn't do any of the things that would make a warden come running — the wards would feel it first and strangle it before it finished. All they could do was the slow gentle work, the coaxing, the voluntary trade: a memory freely given, or near enough to freely given that the difference wouldn't show. The wards held them down the way a heavy coat holds down a person in wind. Suffocating. Also, possibly, the only reason they were still free.

Some days Coral loved the wards. Most days they hated them. Every day they needed them, and needing the thing that presses you under is its own particular loneliness, one Coral had stopped trying to explain to anyone years ago, mostly because there was no anyone.

* * *

The trouble came on a Thursday, as trouble often did, in the form of money — specifically, the lack of it.

Rent was due to the chandler, a broad red-faced woman named Mrs. Hobb who accepted late payment exactly once and never twice. The market had been thin all week. And sitting in Coral's inventory, wrapped in a scrap of wool at the bottom of their lockbox, was a memory they had no business owning.

Their mother's.

They had taken it the night they fled. That was the part they never said aloud, the part that lived in the deepest locked room, deeper than Wren, deeper than the Rite. On the last night, before the running, before the rain, Coral had gone to the cottage where their mother lived alone below the conservatory hill — a weaver, a quiet woman, a woman who had given a child to the Choir because the Choir took the gifted children whether you gave them or not, and it was easier to call it giving — and Coral had knelt by her chair while she slept, and they had done the thing they had sworn never to do.

They had taken her memory of them.

Not all of it. Just the brightest strand: the evening Coral was born, the first time she held them, the smell of rain on the roof and the lamp turned low and the enormous terrified tenderness of a new mother counting ten fingers, ten toes. Coral had taken it because leaving it behind felt worse than stealing it. Because if they left it, their mother would wake tomorrow with a son-shaped hole in her heart and no idea what had been cut out, and she would spend years brushing against the absence like a tongue against a missing tooth. But if Coral took it — took it cleanly, took the whole bright strand — then the absence would close over smooth, the way water closes over a stone, and she would grieve a child lost to the Choir the way parents were supposed to grieve, cleanly, with a grave to put it in.

That was what Coral had told themselves, fourteen years old and shaking, kneeling in the dark with their hands cupped around their mother's sleeping joy. Whether it was true, they had spent seven years not deciding.

The memory sat in the wool. It was the most beautiful thing they owned. It was worth more than six months' rent — a birth-memory, a first-love-grade memory, the kind Mother Quill's people paid in gold for, the kind that could buy Coral a winter of safety, a new lockbox, a room with a door that actually locked.

Mrs. Hobb's knock came at dusk, polite and final. *"Friday,"* she said through the door, and did not say what would happen Saturday.

Coral sat on the floor with the lockbox open and the wool-wrapped bundle in their hands, and the room was very quiet, and the wards hummed their low copper *no* beneath the floorboards, and Coral unwrapped the bundle and looked at what they had become.

The memory rose out of the cloth like heat off a summer road. They didn't need to open it fully; they knew it by its weight, the way you know a beloved book by the thickness of it in your hand. Rain on a roof. Lamp-oil and clean linen. A heartbeat not their own, quick and new and astonishing. Their mother's voice, young, saying a name — saying *their* name, the small everyday name, not the true resonant name the Choir gave them but the one she'd chosen, the one nobody in Vell had ever called them because nobody in Vell knew it.

Coral had sold hundreds of memories. They knew exactly what the transaction looked like from the seller's side: the client leaned in, Coral breathed, the bead warmed, done. They knew what it looked like from the buyer's side: warmth, luck, a borrowed yes. What they had never once done, in seven years, was stand on the third side — the side of the person the memory was *about*. The widow in chapter one of their own private ledger had at least known what she was selling. Their mother had never been asked. Their mother did not know the memory existed. Their mother, for all Coral knew, was dead — the Choir did not let its people write letters, and Coral had stopped checking, years ago, because checking meant hoping and hoping was a debt that collected interest.

If they sold it, the memory would go to some merchant's wife who wanted luck for a childbirth of her own, and it would serve her well, it would serve her *beautifully*, and somewhere in the world the only proof that Coral Underhill had ever been born to anybody, had ever been held and counted and loved at first sight — would sit in a stranger's jewelry box, humming quietly, doing its work, belonging to no one.

Coral held it for a long time. The candle burned down a thumb's width. Below the floor, the wards hummed. Somewhere in the walls, Mrs. Hobb's cat scratched at something only it could hear.

In the end Coral rewrapped the bundle, tied the string the way their mother had taught them to tie string — a weaver's knot, left over right, snug — and put it back in the box, and paid Mrs. Hobb with the coins they'd been saving against a rainy day that had apparently arrived early, and went to bed hungry, and did not sleep.

This was the cost of staying hidden. Not the hunger. Hunger was simple. The cost was this: that every escape is also a wall, and every wall you build to keep the world out also keeps you in, and after seven years Coral could no longer tell which parts of themselves were the person who had run and which parts were the shape the running had carved. They had wanted to be free of the Choir. Instead they had built a smaller conservatory inside their own chest, with stricter masters, and the master was need, and the lessons never ended, and the tuition was everything.

* * *

On Friday they went back to the market with an empty inventory and a plan to buy stock — which meant, in Coral's trade, an evening of listening.

Listening was the true work. Anyone could lift a memory; the Choir had proved that with a hundred years of reliquaries. What Coral had learned in Vell was the harder craft: finding the memories that wanted to go. You sat in a tavern or a bathhouse or the long queue at the charity bakery, and you listened — not with your ears, though your ears helped, but with the muffled deep-down instrument the wards couldn't entirely silence. Grief had a pitch. Nostalgia had a pitch. And underneath both, rarest of all, there was the particular frequency of a memory that had grown too heavy for its keeper — the wedding told so many times it had become a performance, the dead husband invoked so often the widow was more married to the story than to the man. Those memories practically climbed into your hands. Taking them was less theft than rescue, or so Coral told the small tribunal that convened inside them on every such occasion, and most nights the tribunal adjourned without a verdict, and Coral slept anyway, because the body is merciful that way.

Friday's queue at the bakery yielded nothing. Friday's tavern yielded two modest trades — a cooper's memory of his daughter's first steps, a ferryman's memory of a summer so fine he'd begun to wear it out — and Coral was turning toward home through the fish-market square, counting coins that would cover rent and not much else, when they saw the boy.

He was maybe nine, maybe ten, small for either, with a face like a ferret and hair that looked cut with a knife, and he was standing on an overturned crate in the corner of the square where the lamplight gave out, and he was selling weather.

*"Bottled rain!"* the boy shouted, holding up little stoppered vials that sloshed with something gray. *"Genuine storm-rain, caught fresh off the rooftops! Luck for travelers! Cures warts! Makes your sweetheart write back!"* He shook a vial at a passing fishwife, who laughed and kept walking. He shook another at a pair of dockhands, who did not laugh. His voice was going hoarse at the edges, and his feet were bare, and it was the last week of autumn and frost was coming.

Coral should have walked past. Coral had a rule about strays, and the rule was *don't*, and the rule existed because Coral knew with terrible precision what happened to people who got close to them — the widow whose wedding they carried, the cooper, the ferryman, the whole long ledger of the used-up — and because a child was not a memory, could not be whispered into a bead and handed off, and would therefore simply stay, accumulating, becoming a weight that the wards and the hunger and the wardens would eventually crush between them.

But the boy had stopped shouting. He was looking directly at Coral, across the whole dark square, with the flat unblinking stare of a creature deciding whether you were predator or meal — and as their eyes met, the vial in the boy's raised hand caught the distant lamplight, and the gray stuff inside it swirled, all on its own, against the stillness of his grip.

Coral stood very still. The wards hummed their copper note. Nothing in Vell moved against its own stillness. Nothing in Vell *could*.

The boy grinned — quick, feral, gap-toothed — and pocketed the vial, and shouted again into the empty square: *"Bottled rain! Last chance tonight!"*

And Coral, who had spent seven years learning exactly how much silence cost, and who had just paid a week's savings to avoid selling the last piece of their mother, heard themselves think, with a sensation like a floorboard giving way: *oh no.*
