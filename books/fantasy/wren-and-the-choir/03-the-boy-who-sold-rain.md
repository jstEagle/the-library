## 3. The Boy Who Sold Rain

Coral had a rule about strays: they were someone else's problem.

Seven years of rules, and most of them existed for good reason. Never work the same street twice in a month. Never sell a memory to anyone who might remember you selling it. Never let the wards hear you hum. And never — never — look too long at anything that made your chest do the thing it was doing right now, standing in the mouth of Tanner's Square at half past midnight, watching a boy no older than nine or ten hawk bottled weather to an empty square like the world owed him an audience.

The boy had set up shop on an overturned fish crate. His wares gleamed in the lamplight: a dozen stoppered vials arranged on a scrap of oilcloth with the care of a jeweler laying out rubies. Inside them moved things that should not have fit. One held a curl of fog, slow-turning, patient as a cat. Another rattled with what looked and sounded like sleet, though the night was dry as old paper. A third — the one he'd been shouting about — churned with gray rain, fine as sifted flour, striking the glass from within.

Coral knew fraud when they saw it. Coral *was* fraud, professionally speaking; they'd built a life on making the impossible look like sleight-of-hand so that nobody ever asked the dangerous question, which was: what if some of it isn't sleight?

But this wasn't sleight. This wasn't even magic, not properly. The vials didn't hum. They didn't pull at the copper taste behind Coral's teeth the way true working always did, the way the widow's wedding-day memory still faintly did, folded into its charm-casing in their coat pocket, sold but not yet delivered. Whatever the boy had done, he'd done it the way a magpie does things — by accident, by appetite, by being exactly the kind of creature the world couldn't be bothered to explain.

"Bottled rain!" the boy called again, to the square, to the shuttered windows, to the one rat bold enough to cross his pitch. "Storm-grade! Honest fog! Sleet at half price, I'm dying here!"

"Nobody's buying," Coral said, before they could stop themselves.

The boy spun. He was small and wiry, all elbows and grin, with hair the color of wet sand cut raggedly short — self-inflicted, probably, with a knife, in front of a mirror that lied. His eyes were quick brown things that went straight to Coral's hands first, then pockets, then face, in that order. A professional's inventory.

"They're not buying *yet*," he corrected. "That's why I'm standing here shouting. That's the whole trade, missus. Standing where the money can see you."

"I'm not a missus."

"Right." He squinted. "Mister, then."

"Not that either."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Boss," he decided. "Everyone's got to be something." Then his gaze dropped to Coral's coat, to the shape of the charm-case pressing against the wool, and the grin sharpened into something older than his face. "What've you got in there? Feels heavy. Heavy means valuable. Valuable means—"

"Mine," Coral said.

"Everything's mine until somebody bigger says otherwise," the boy said cheerfully, "and you haven't said otherwise loud enough yet." But he stepped back off the crate, hands raised, palms out — the universal grammar of the street, *no harm meant, none taken, we're only talking* — and Coral understood with a sinking feeling that this child knew more languages than most scholars and every one of them was survival.

"What are you?" Coral asked. It came out wrong, too blunt, the question of someone who had spent seven years asking nothing of anyone.

The boy shrugged. "Pip."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I've got that doesn't run." He hopped down off the crate, and up close Coral could see the details the lamplight had been kind about: the hollows under his cheekbones, the chapped split in his lower lip, the way his coat hung on him like a borrowed argument. Winter was three weeks out and this child had been wintering badly for years. "Bottled rain," Pip said again, softer now, holding up the storm-vial between two fingers. "Found it honest, boss. There was rain coming off the roof gutters on Coppersmith Lane, three nights back, and I caught it. You have to catch it *going somewhere*, see? Catch falling water, it's just water. Catch moving water, it remembers moving."

He said it the way children say true things: plainly, without understanding that they should be afraid.

Coral felt the floorboard creak again. Somewhere across the city, the anti-magic wards hummed their copper note through every stone of Vell, and here was a boy describing, in a fish-market voice, the exact principle on which Coral's entire outlawed existence depended. Things remembered. Things carried. Things, if you were careful and damned and gentle enough, could be persuaded to give what they remembered to someone else.

"You should put those away," Coral said quietly. "And never say that sentence again. Not to me, not to anyone."

"Why?"

"Because the wardens post men on Coppersmith Lane. Because 'catching' is a word they know. Because—" Coral stopped. Because seven years ago, in a conservatory full of children who could all do worse than bottle rain, Coral had learned what happened to the ones who were caught saying sentences like that. The Gleaning, the wardens called it now, in their polite new notices going up on the market boards. Before, they'd had other words. Older ones.

Pip was watching Coral's face with that unnerving, unchildlike attention. "You've seen someone get took," he said. Not a question.

"I've seen a lot of things."

"Yeah." He crouched, gathering his vials into the oilcloth with practiced speed, and for a moment his back was to Coral and his shoulders were very small and very straight, the way shoulders get when they've been holding up something alone for too long. When he turned around, the grin was reinstalled, seamless as a stage flat. "So. Boss. You buyin', or am I movin' along?"

"I'm not buying weather."

"Everybody buys weather eventually. Weather's the one thing everybody's got opinions about and no power over." He held up the fog-vial, turning it so the lamplight swam inside. "Fog's for hiding. Burglars like fog. Wives like fog, when the husband comes home mean. Storm's for anger — you uncork it over somebody's shop who did you wrong, and there's not a trace of you anywhere, just weather being weather. Rain's for—" He paused, and something flickered behind the salesman's patter, something younger. "Rain's for missing things. People sleep better with rain going. Even fake rain. *Especially* fake rain, maybe. Sounds like somewhere else is getting washed."

Coral stood in the empty square and heard, underneath the patter, the whole shape of the child's life laid out like goods on oilcloth: orphan-shaped, winter-coming, clever past the point of safety. The chest did the thing again. Coral hated it. The chest had been doing fewer and fewer things over seven years, and Coral had come to regard that as a professional accomplishment, like a swordsman's calluses.

"I'm leaving," Coral announced, to the square, to the boy, to their own traitorous ribs.

"Course you are," Pip agreed pleasantly. "Everybody leaves. That's the other thing everybody does."

* * *

Coral made it four streets.

They counted them later, angrily, the way you count the steps of a fall. Four streets of Vell at midnight, past the shuttered chandleries and the tannery reek and the shrine-niche where somebody had left a stub of candle burning for a saint whose name had worn off the stone, and at every step the boy's voice followed them, not because he was following — Coral checked twice, and lost him once in the crooked lanes around Bellwether Court, which itself was impressive — but because the words had stuck.

*Sounds like somewhere else is getting washed.*

Coral stopped under a gutter-lamp that buzzed and spat, and took stock of themselves with the cold bookkeeping they used instead of prayer.

Assets: one charm-case containing the widow Undermill's wedding day, to be delivered tomorrow for six silver and a promise of more work. One room above a dye-house, rented monthly, containing a bed, a lock, and nothing else worth naming. One body that ached at twenty-six the way bodies should ache at sixty, because carrying other people's best moments was heavier work than carrying coal. One name — not their real one; their real one had stayed behind at the conservatory, folded up small and hidden in the last place the Choir would ever think to look, which was nowhere, because Coral had spent seven years becoming nobody with tremendous discipline.

Liabilities: everything else.

Including, apparently, a sudden and catastrophic inability to walk away from a feral child selling weather in a condemned square.

"It's not tenderness," Coral told the gutter-lamp. The lamp, a municipal object with no imagination, buzzed. "It's risk management. If the wardens take him, he'll talk. Children always talk. They'll ask him who he's shown the trick to, and he'll say *a person in Tanner's Square*, and they'll start pulling threads, and my thread is attached to everything." They nodded, satisfied. This was airtight. This was the kind of reasoning that had kept them alive while brighter, braver conduits died singing.

The lamp buzzed again, skeptically.

"Oh, shut up."

Finding him again took less than an hour, which told Coral something about how well the boy actually knew Vell — or how little Vell had left to offer him. Coral tracked him the way they tracked everything now, by feel rather than sight: not magic, never magic, just seven years of reading a city's grain the way a carpenter reads wood. Pip ran a circuit. Tanner's Square to Bellwether Court to the night market's edge, where the licensed stalls closed early and the unlicensed ones opened late, and finally to the colonnade behind the Salt Exchange, where the wind off the harbor pooled and the watchmen didn't bother walking after the second bell.

He was asleep when Coral found him. Curled on a bench in the colonnade with the oilcloth bundle hugged to his chest like a stuffed animal, boots on, coat buttoned wrong, one thumb hooked through the loop of the storm-vial. In sleep the salesman fell off his face entirely, and what was left was just a kid with chapped lips and sand-colored hair, breathing in the small fast way of people who expect to be woken badly.

Coral stood over him for a long time.

There was a version of this where Coral walked away and it was the smart thing and everyone lived longer. Coral rehearsed that version thoroughly. Then they crouched, and eased the storm-vial's loop gently free of the boy's thumb so it wouldn't crack if he rolled, and the touch — barely a touch, knuckle against knuckle — sent a whisper of resonance up Coral's arm like a struck string.

The boy's sleep was full of weather. That was all. Nothing trained, nothing taught, no Choir in him, no Rite waiting — just a natural, artless, accidental talent, the kind the old texts had called *leakage*, the kind the conservatory had harvested like maple sap. Coral had been leakage once. Everyone at the conservatory had started as leakage. That was how they found you: not by hunting magic, but by listening for children who talked to rain.

"No," Coral said aloud, very quietly. "Absolutely not."

Pip woke the way street kids wake — all at once, silently, hand already closing on the nearest hard object. His eyes found Coral, catalogued, and relaxed by perhaps five percent.

"Boss," he said. "You came back."

"I came back to tell you to leave Vell."

"Where would I go?"

"Anywhere. South. The river towns."

"The river towns drowned two years ago." He sat up, working a crick out of his neck. "You know that, right? Everybody knows that. Where do you think all these extra people came from?" He gestured at the dark city around them with its crowded tenements and its doubled-up beds and its soup lines that started earlier every year. "Vell's where the map stops having edges. Everything runs downhill to Vell."

Downhill. Coral thought of the wards humming over the city, the great copper-net of quiet that had been raised generations ago to keep the Choir's magic out — and which kept everything else out too, in its way. Kept the country out. Kept the truth out. Coral had thought of the wards as a wall protecting them. It occurred to them now, with a slow unpleasant shift, that walls also trap.

"Here's what happens," Coral said, and heard their own voice go crisp and cold, the voice they used for negotiations, the voice that cost them something every time they used it. "You don't sell those vials in the open again. Not in squares, not at markets. If a warden sees glass weather, they won't arrest you — they'll send for a Gleaner, and a Gleaner doesn't arrest anybody. Do you understand what the Gleaning is?"

Pip's grin flickered. For a heartbeat the salesman was gone and something scared and young looked out. "They posted notices," he said. "On the market board. Big red wax seals. My friend Jemmy read it to me." He swallowed. "Says magic crimes mean the Gleaning now. Doesn't say what the Gleaning *is*."

"It's when they take everything you are," Coral said, "and leave the rest."

Silence, except the harbor wind worrying the colonnade ropes.

Then Pip, because he was who he was, rallied visibly and held up the fog-vial like a shield. "So you're saying I need a partner. Somebody respectable. Somebody who sells *charms*, legal-adjacent, established—"

"I'm saying you need to throw those in the harbor."

"I'm saying you already looked at them like you wanted one."

"I did not."

"You looked at the fog one for eleven seconds, boss. I count."

Coral stared at him. Eleven seconds. They had, in fact, looked at the fog vial for roughly eleven seconds, thinking about how useful fog was, thinking about the delivery tomorrow, thinking about the route past the warden-post on Cordwainer Street where a pocketful of concealment would be worth real money—

And the boy read that whole ledger off their face the way Coral read cities. Off the grain. Naturally. Accidentally. Like leakage.

The floorboard didn't creak this time. It gave way entirely.

* * *

The notices were everywhere by morning.

Coral saw the first one on the board outside the chandlery, and then another at the Cordwainer gate, and then simply everywhere, as if the wardens had worked through the night nailing red-sealed paper to every surface in Vell that would hold a nail. The script was the Wardenate's official hand, round and courteous as a dinner invitation:

*By order of the Provost-Warden of Vell: in light of recent irregularities beyond the borders, the penalties for thaumaturgical offense are hereby revised. All acts of unlicensed working, however minor, shall henceforth be subject to GLEANING. Citizens are reminded that vigilance is a civic virtue. Report resonance. Report humming. Report dreams that arrive from elsewhere.*

*Report dreams that arrive from elsewhere.* Coral read that line three times. Someone in the Wardenate knew something. Someone had felt the same tremor under the city that Coral had been pretending not to feel for a week — the wards humming a half-tone sharper, the copper note gone slightly sour, like a bell with a crack in it dreaming of falling.

Around the board, morning crowds gathered and muttered. A fishwife crossed herself with a fish still in her hand. Two clerks from the Salt Exchange read with the particular blankness of men deciding not to have read anything. An old woman near Coral whispered to her neighbor: "Gleaning. My grandmother's grandmother had a word for that. From before the Quiet. They'd stand in the doorways and listen for children singing, and the ones they heard..." She didn't finish. Her neighbor shushed her with real fear, glancing at the board as though the notice might report her for reporting.

Coral pulled their collar up and moved on, and became aware — the way you become aware of a splinter — of a small presence matching their stride.

"I read mine myself," Pip said, falling in beside them, uninvited, bundled now in a coat that Coral recognized with horror as approximately their own size and style, acquired sometime overnight by methods Coral declined to investigate. "I can read. Mostly. Jemmy taught me letters off gravestones, so I'm strongest on names and dates." He peered up. "You went white, boss. Whiter. Is it the Gleaning bit or the dreams bit?"

"Both. Neither. Walk slower and look stupider."

"I can't look stupider. This is my floor."

They walked. The city went about its morning around them, buying bread, hauling barrels, nailing up fear. At the corner of Cordwainer Street, Coral stopped at a shuttered stall, counted out coins, and bought two roasted chestnuts and a heel of yesterday's bread, and handed half to Pip before the higher functions could veto the transaction.

Pip looked at the food. Looked at Coral. Looked at the food again with the expression of a boy recalculating everything he knew about the universe.

"This is a hiring," he said.

"It is not a hiring."

"You bought me breakfast. Breakfast is wages. Wages mean employment." He was already eating, talking around the chestnut with total contentment. "What are the terms?"

"There are no terms. Eat your bread."

"Room and board?"

"*No terms.*"

"Because I sleep in the colonnade anyway, so room's negotiable, and honestly the board's the main attraction—"

"Pip."

"—and I'm cheap, boss, I'm famously cheap, Jemmy says you couldn't pay me to have pride—"

"*Pip.*"

He stopped. Chestnut halfway to his mouth, grin at full sail, waiting.

Coral stood in the morning crowd with a heel of bread going cold in their hand and seven years of rules stacked up behind them like a card house, and heard themselves say, in the tone of someone signing something in blood: "Rule one. You never tell anyone what you can do. Rule two, you don't sell vials in the open, ever. Rule three—" they hesitated, because rule three arrived in their mouth fully formed, unbidden, warm and terrifying — "you eat breakfast at my table. Every morning. That's not negotiable either."

Pip blinked. For the first time since Tanner's Square, he had absolutely nothing to sell.

Then the grin came back — different, somehow, less lacquered, with something actual underneath it, something that made Coral's chest do the thing so hard it was nearly audible — and he stuck out a grubby, chapped, entirely sincere hand.

"Deal, boss," he said. "Best contract I ever signed. What's the business called?"

"It's not a business."

"Everything's a business. What do I call you?"

"Coral."

"Coral." He tried it out, rolling it around like a stolen coin. "Weird name. Good weird. Like weather." He shook the proffered hand with tremendous formality, sealing terms no court would recognize, and then, because he was who he was, immediately ruined the moment: "Eleven seconds, by the way. On the fog vial. I never forget a sale."

Across the square, past the crowd, past the muttering fishwife and the frightened old woman, a warden in gray-and-copper was nailing one last notice to the board outside the chandlery. He smoothed the paper flat with a gloved hand, stepped back, and — idly, habitually, the way a man checks his boot for mud — turned his head and swept the square with a listener's eyes.

His gaze passed over the crowd. Passed over Coral.

Stopped. Came back.

Coral kept walking, unhurried, a citizen buying bread, a nobody with tremendous discipline, while between their shoulder blades the copper note of the wards seemed, for one half-second, to sharpen — almost as if something far away had heard the sound of a contract being signed, and had begun, patiently, to follow it home.
