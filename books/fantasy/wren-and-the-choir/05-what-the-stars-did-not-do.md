## 5. What the Stars Did Not Do

Coral had always counted the stars the way other people counted money.

It was a habit from the conservatory, from the years before the running — the Choir taught its children the sky as a ledger, every star a note in the great unfinished hymn, and even now, seven years and a stolen life later, Coral could not walk home from the night market without tallying them. Four hundred and eleven visible from the roof of the tannery on a clear autumn night. Four hundred and twelve if the air was cold enough to burn off the river haze, and you stood on the chimney where the tiles were loose and the view opened east past the ward-towers to the hills.

They were not counting for beauty. They were counting because a sky that stayed the same was a sky you could hide under.

That night the market had been good to them. A fishmonger's grief for a drowned brother, lifted so cleanly the man would only feel it as a lightness he couldn't name — sold within the hour to a moneylender who paid in silver for the comfort of sorrow that wasn't his. A baker's memory of her first loaf, warm and imperfect and triumphant. A soldier's last sight of the border hills, green and starred, which Coral had bought rather than sold, because it had made their chest ache in a way they didn't want examined.

Coral walked the long way home, as always, past the ward-towers where the anti-magic wards hummed their flat gray note. The hum sat in their teeth like a stone. It always had. Seven years in Vell and Coral had never once stopped hearing it — the sound of a city holding its breath against them.

They climbed the tannery roof. They sat on the loose tiles. They began the count.

One. Two. Three. The Warden's Lamp, red and patient in the south. The Sisters, the two white stars that the ferry-children said were drowned girls holding hands. The pale scatter above the eastern hills where the Choir's old maps had marked the Door, though no one in Vell remembered what the maps had meant—

Coral stopped.

Between one breath and the next, between *four hundred and nine* and *four hundred and ten*, the sky went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — all of it, everywhere, at once, the way a hundred candles die when a door slams in a sealed room. The Sisters vanished mid-glint. The Warden's Lamp, that red constancy sailors had steered by for longer than Vell had walls, simply was not there, and the dark that replaced it was not the dark of night but the dark of *absence*, a black with texture to it, a black that pressed.

Coral's hand found the chimney. The tiles were cold. Their own breathing was the loudest thing in the world.

And then — beneath the ward-hum, beneath the fear, beneath seven years of careful silence — the stolen memories woke.

They had never done this. That was the first thing Coral understood, crouched on the roof with the sky gone wrong: in seven years of carrying other people's best moments, the cargo had never once stirred. A stolen memory was a dead thing, a pressed flower, a coin. It sat where you put it. It had no voice.

This one did.

*North,* whispered the fishmonger's grief, in the fishmonger's voice, though the fishmonger was a mile away and asleep. *North and east, where the river bends twice.*

*Below,* whispered the baker's first loaf, warm as an oven mouth. *Below the below. Beneath the singing.*

*The Door,* whispered the soldier's green hills, and the soldier's voice was young and terrified and certain, *the Door, the Door, the Door—*

Coral pressed both hands over their mouth. The words kept coming, dozens of voices, hundreds, every memory they had ever taken rising in the dark like fish coming up to feed, and all of them saying the same thing in a hundred borrowed tongues. Coordinates. Bearings. A map made of other people's joys, and every point on the map was the same point.

The stars had gone out, and the memories knew why.

"No," Coral said into their palms. It came out very small. "No, no, you're dead things, you're *coins*, be quiet—"

The sky did not answer. The memories did not stop.

Far to the south, across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then all of them, every bell in Vell at once, and under the bells came the sound Coral had dreaded every day for seven years: the ward-towers changing their note. The flat gray hum shivered, wavered, and climbed into something higher, thinner, frightened — the sound of a great machine straining at a weight it had never been asked to hold.

Coral stayed on the roof until their legs would obey them. Then they went inside, and did not sleep, and listened to the voices in their head sing a country home.

* * *

Sable Fen was mid-hymn when the dark came down.

The flooded crypt under the old chantry was his church, his theater, and his confessional, all three, though the six people standing waist-deep in cold water among the drowned pews would have called it none of those things. They had paid their coppers to a boy at the stairhead. They came for the singing, which was illegal, and for the feeling it gave them, which was worse than illegal, though none of them could have named it. Sable could. He had been trained to name it. It was the Choir, and it lived in him like a splinter in the heart, and every night he sang it small enough to pass for beauty.

He was in the third verse of the Lament for the Founding — a hymn about exile, about a people carried away from their true country, which he had always assumed was allegory, the way priests assume everything is allegory when the truth would cost them — when the light through the crypt's high grates went wrong.

He stopped singing. The water lapped. Someone in the third pew said, "Why'd you stop?" and someone else said, "Look up," and then everyone looked up, and no one said anything at all.

The grates showed a slice of sky, a finger's width of it, and the stars in that slice were gone. Not covered. *Gone*, the way a word is gone from the tip of the tongue — the shape of the sky still there, the places where the stars had been still there, and nothing in them.

Sable's voice, when he found it, did not sound like his own.

"Everyone out," he said. "Now. Don't run, don't scream, walk. Go home. Lock your doors and don't look up again tonight."

They went. He stood in the flooding dark among the drowned pews and felt the hymn he had half-sung still hanging in the water, unfinished, resonant, and — for the first time in his life — *answered*.

Because the Choir had a doctrine for this. Every priest learned it in their first year, and every priest learned to smile at it, the way you smile at a child's fear of the dark. *The stars are the god's eyes,* the doctrine said. *While they shine, the god sleeps. If they ever go out —*

If they ever went out, the doctrine said, it would mean the god had opened its eyes.

Sable had spent eleven years telling himself the god was a story the founders told to keep young priests obedient. He had testified, once, years ago, against a child who could actually hear the thing he'd only read about — he had stood in a cold marble hall and said the words that got them cast out, and he had told himself every night since that he'd done his duty, that the Choir was dangerous, that the child was dangerous, that duty was a kind of love.

He stood in the black water and the unfinished hymn rang in his bones, and every note of it was pointing somewhere, the way a compass points, the way a wound points at the knife.

*North and east,* said the Lament for the Founding, in words he had sung a thousand times and never once heard. *Where the river of the sky bends twice. Where the Door stands open in the side of the world.*

"Oh," Sable said, to no one, in the dark. "Oh, it was never allegory."

He climbed the stairs, wrung out his robes, and went up into a starless city already catching fire with panic — bonfires in the squares, wardens' horns, a mob around the notice-boards where fresh bills were being pasted up by torchlight. He read the nearest one by a stranger's flame.

SORCERY IS SUSPECTED. THE GLEANING IS RESUMED. REPORT ALL WHO HEAR WHAT OTHERS CANNOT.

The ink was still wet. The bill had been printed before the stars went out.

Sable stood very still in the firelight, a disgraced priest with a splinter in his heart, and understood that someone in Vell had known. Someone had printed the bills in advance, the way you print bills for an execution.

And he thought — with guilt so old it had worn smooth as a river stone — of a voice he had heard once in a night market crowd, a voice that resonated wrong, that rang like a struck bell among clods of clay. He had told himself he'd imagined it. He had spent a season telling himself that.

He turned north, toward the night market, and began to walk.

* * *

Pip was on the warehouse roof with his best bottle — thick glass, wax-stoppered, stolen from an apothecary who owed him — trying to catch the wind off the river, when the stars went out.

He did not scream. Screaming was for people who had someone to scream to. What Pip did was go still, the way he went still when a watchman's lantern swung toward him, and take inventory: two bottles empty, one bottle with fog in it, half a meat pie, a knife, a roof, a city, and a sky with nothing in it.

The sky with nothing in it was the problem.

He had heard old dockhands talk about the night the stars would go out. It was a story for scaring children off the piers: *the night the stars go out, the sea comes up to collect.* Pip had always assumed it was like the rest of the dockhands' stories — mostly lies with a little weather in them. But the stars had gone out, and the sea, as far as he could tell from the roof, was staying exactly where it was supposed to be, and somehow that was worse. It meant the story was wrong. It meant nobody knew what was coming, not even the liars.

Down in the street, Vell was coming apart the way Pip knew cities came apart — not all at once but in pieces, in the order of fear. First the dogs. Then the bells. Then the fires, little ones, careless ones, a lamp dropped in a doorway, a bonfire in the fish square with people dancing around it because dancing was better than thinking. Then the wardens, in their gray-and-iron, moving in files with their lanterns hooded, and the wardens were the worst, because wardens meant the Gleaning, and the Gleaning meant that tomorrow morning there would be fewer people in Vell and the bills would explain why.

Pip crouched on the roof edge with his fog-bottle and watched, and did what he always did with things too big to hold: he put them in the bottle. Not really. But he looked at the starless sky through the thick curved glass, and it shrank, and a shrunken thing you could hold was a thing you could survive.

That was when he saw her — the charm-seller, the quiet one, the one who'd fed him stew in the winter and pretended not to. Coral. Crossing the square below with their collar up, walking fast, walking wrong — not thief-fast but *frightened*-fast, head down, hands pressed flat against their own temples like someone trying to keep their skull on.

Pip had followed a lot of people in his life. It was the closest thing he had to a trade. He had never followed anyone out of worry before, and the newness of it sat in his chest like a swallowed coal as he dropped from the warehouse roof to the awning to the stack of crates to the cobbles, and ran, low and quick, in Coral's shadow.

* * *

By midnight, Vell had stopped being a city and become a rumor of one.

Coral moved through it in a haze of voices. The memories would not quiet. Every stolen joy in their head was awake and singing directions, and the horror of it was not the noise — the horror was that the directions *agreed*. A thousand memories, taken from a thousand strangers who had never met, all pointing the same way, all singing the same song, and Coral knew that song. They had been trained to know it, before the running, before the wards, before the small gray life. It was the Choir. The whole Choir, the complete music, the thing the conservatory had only ever taught in fragments because the whole of it was forbidden even to the Choir —

— because the whole of it, whispered the soldier's green hills, *is the god's name.*

Coral stopped in the mouth of an alley off the fish square, where a bonfire roared and a crowd had gathered around a warden on a crate. The warden's voice was the voice of every warden, brass and boredom and fear underneath.

"—the stars are an enchantment, cast by traitors within the walls. The Warden-Commander has invoked the old statutes. The Gleaning is resumed as of this hour. Any person found practicing, harboring, or *hearing*—"

The crowd made a sound Coral had never heard a crowd make. Not a gasp, not a roar. A *flinch* — a thousand bodies moving at once away from something invisible, and every one of them, in that flinch, glancing up at the empty sky and then quickly down, as if the dark were a debt collector who might notice them looking.

Coral looked up too. They couldn't help it. And in the textureless black, in the places where the Sisters had been, the memories sang so loudly now that their teeth ached with it, and beneath the singing — very far away, very deep, the way you hear the sea inside a shell — something else.

Something breathing.

Slow. Enormous. The breath of a sleeper turning toward the surface after a long, long time, and even asleep, even at that unimaginable depth, its breath moved the whole sky the way a whale's passage moves the water above it.

Coral had spent seven years telling themselves they had escaped. Standing in the firelit alley with a god's breathing in their skull and a map of other people's happiness pointing north and east, Coral understood, with the calm that arrives at the center of catastrophes, that they had never escaped anything. They had only been standing very still, in a country-sized hiding place, waiting for the sky to notice them.

The sky had noticed.

"Coral."

They spun. Sable Fen stood at the alley mouth, firelight on his face, water still darkening the hem of his robes, and his eyes were not afraid in the way the crowd was afraid. His eyes were afraid in the way Coral's were — the specific fear of someone who knows the name of the thing.

"You heard it," he said. Not a question. "The singing. Under the dark."

"I don't know what you're talking about." The lie came out automatic, worn smooth by use, and Sable flinched as if the lie itself were familiar — as if he'd heard it before, or said it.

"I testified once," Sable said, very quietly, under the bonfire's roar. "Against someone who could hear it. I've regretted it every night since, and I never knew why until tonight." He took one step closer. The fire threw his shadow long across the cobbles, and the shadow reached Coral before he did. "The stars are the god's eyes, Coral. The Choir taught us that and made us laugh at it. And they're out. And you're the only person in this city whose face I've ever seen do what your face is doing now."

Somewhere behind Coral, light as a cat on the crates, someone small was listening — though neither of them knew it yet.

Coral's hands were shaking. The memories sang. The god breathed. North and east, where the river bends twice.

"I don't know what you want from me," Coral said.

"Neither do I," said Sable Fen, honestly, terribly. "Yet. But the wardens are going door to door by morning, and whatever you are, you can't be it *here*."

Above them, over the burning square, over the flinching city, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath — and in the deepest dark, patient as a child counting to some enormous number, the god went on breathing.

Coral looked north, where the memories pointed, and for the first time in seven years, they were afraid to stay more than they were afraid to run.
