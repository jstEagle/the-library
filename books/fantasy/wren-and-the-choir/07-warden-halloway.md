## 7. Warden Halloway

The ferry into Vell ran at night, as all ferries did now, and Warden Halloway stood at the rail and watched the city come up out of the dark like a jaw opening.

Vell had no stars above it. That was the first thing she always noticed, the thing that never stopped feeling like a small violence: the city's wardens had bought the sky the way they'd bought everything else, paying in wards and silver to keep the heavens out. What hung over Vell instead was the glow of its own lamps, a brown-gold ceiling of smoke and light, close enough to touch if you were a fool, and beneath it the city itself — the terraces, the spires, the night market already burning in the low quarter like a bed of coals someone had raked into the shape of streets.

She had been coming to this city for nineteen years. She had never once gotten used to the sky.

"You'll want your papers ready," said the ferryman, not looking at her. He was a broad man with river-mud dried in the creases of his knuckles, and he had said nothing the whole crossing until now, which meant he had been deciding whether to say it. "There's a warden-check at the water stairs tonight. Unusual."

"I'm aware," Halloway said.

He glanced at her then, at the gray traveling cloak, at the warden's iron at her collar that she had not bothered to hide. His face did the arithmetic people's faces always did — *trouble, but not my trouble* — and he turned back to his ropes.

The check at the water stairs was three junior wardens and a clerk with a lantern, and they waved her through before she'd fully produced her seal, because the seal did the work of her face. One of the juniors, a girl with a healing sunburn across her nose, looked at Halloway's cloak a moment too long. Halloway knew what the girl was seeing: the rank-braids, the border-service marks, the particular worn quality of a cloak that had been to the edge of the country and come back. Nobody came back from the border anymore. Nobody had, in eleven days.

That was why she was here.

She climbed the water stairs into the city proper and felt the wards close over her like the surface of a lake. Everyone felt it, though most Vellish had never known anything else and so had no name for the sensation: a faint pressure behind the eyes, a flattening, a silence with texture to it. In Vell, magic did not so much fail as become embarrassed — a spell cast here would work the way a whispered word works in a library, technically audible, socially impossible. The city's founders had built the wards into the very stones, into the mortar and the bells and the bones under the streets, and they had built them for one purpose above all others.

To keep out the Choir.

Halloway stood a moment in the customs yard and let the silence settle into her the way it always did, cold and clean, and told herself — as she had told herself on the road, on the ferry, on the border road before that, walking past villages that had forgotten their own names — *you are glad of it. You are glad of anything that makes it harder.*

She did not entirely believe herself. She had stopped entirely believing herself some years ago, and had learned to work anyway, the way a man with a bad knee learns to walk.

* * *

The Wardenhouse in Vell occupied a former conservatory.

Halloway had pointed that out to the local commander, once, years ago — *do you know what this building was?* — and he had looked at her with the blank courtesy of a man being told the history of his own house. They all knew. It was in the founding charters. The Choir had not been driven out of Vell; it had been *digested*. Its halls became courts, its practice-rooms became interrogation chambers, its resonant chapel — built with acoustic stones that could carry a whisper from wall to wall — became the records vault, because the same property that let a hymn travel let a clerk's voice carry to every corner, and there was nowhere in the vault a thief could hide.

Halloway had spent the first night of her assignment in that vault, reading.

The file on the border collapse was thin, which frightened her more than thickness would have. Eleven days ago, at the turn of the tide, every warden-post along the Ashenmoor frontier had reported the same thing in the same hour: the wards had not broken. They had *ended*. Not shattered, not breached, not burned out — simply stopped, all along a thousand miles of border, the way a song stops. And when the wardens on the wall had looked outward, past the wards, past the frontier, into the country that had always been there —

There wasn't one.

The reports used different words. *Static. Fog. Nothing. A gray like wet paper.* One warden, a veteran named Oss with thirty years' service and a reputation for never embellishing, had written a single sentence that Halloway had read eleven times: *The hills beyond the wall did not fall down. They were never put up.*

And the stars. She had stood on the border wall herself, three days ago, and looked up into a sky with no stars in it — not clouded, not hidden, *absent*, a black so complete it made her eyes water, as if she were looking not up at the heavens but down into a well. The astronomers in the capital were calling it an atmospheric phenomenon. The priests of the small surviving orders were calling it a mourning. The wardens' own analysts, in the annex she was not cleared to see, had begun using a word from the old charters, a word from the time of the founding, and the word was *unraveling*.

And one more thing. One thing that had put her on a ferry with a gray cloak and an old ache in her chest.

In the last hour before the wards ended, every listening-post on the border had recorded a sound. Not through the air — through the *wards themselves*, the way a tuning fork picks up a note from across a room. A sound the instruments could not transcribe and the analysts could not name, except that one of them, a young man with ink on his fingers and a founding-era primer open on his desk, had looked up and said, very quietly:

"That's a voice. Someone's *singing*."

The Choir had been dead for two hundred years. Its last singers had been burned or bound or scattered so thoroughly that the word for what they did survived only as a curse and a children's rhyme. But someone had sung at the border the night the country's edge came undone, and the wards — built to strangle exactly that voice — had ended like candles in a draft.

Which meant someone was still alive who could sing.

Which meant, Halloway thought, climbing the Wardenhouse stairs with the vault's acoustic stones humming faintly under her boots, that she had been sent to find the one person in the world she had spent seven years hoping never to see again.

* * *

Her name had been different then.

Not her family name — that was hers, worn like a scar, useful. Her *name*, the one the Conservatory had given her, the one that was less a name than a note: a low, steady tone meant to anchor the voices around it. She had been an anchor. That was the joke of her life, if it was a joke: the Choir had trained her to be the fixed point that other voices turned around, and she had spent the rest of her life being exactly that, in exactly the wrong institution.

She had been twenty-three, a junior instructor in the Conservatory at Harrow's Bend, and Coral Underhill had been sixteen, and had sung like nothing Halloway had ever heard before or since.

That was the truth of it, and she made herself hold the truth of it now, in the dark of her borrowed cell in the Vell Wardenhouse, because duty that will not look at what it betrayed is only cruelty wearing a uniform. Coral had sung like *weather*. The other students produced notes; Coral produced *conditions* — you listened and felt a change in pressure, a warmth, a coming rain. The old masters had wept, actually wept, standing in the back of the practice hall with their fists pressed to their mouths. The last true conduit in two hundred years, they said. The voice the founders had written about. The voice that could, if the old books were to be believed, do what the founders themselves had only claimed to do.

Halloway had been assigned as Coral's tutor in theory, which was the Conservatory's way of saying *handler-in-training*. She had walked the girl — no, not a girl, she corrected herself now, as she had corrected herself then; Coral had never let anyone settle them comfortably into a word, and even at sixteen had looked at Halloway with a level gray gaze that declined to be *girl* or *boy* or anything but *Coral* — she had walked Coral through the scale-structures and the memory-grammars, and Coral had learned everything in half the expected time and spent the surplus hours asking questions.

*Why do we only sing to the land? Why never from it?*

*What's behind the border, really? Have you ever been?*

*If the Choir stole Ashenmoor — and the charters say "preserved," but the charters also say the founders were humble, and no humble person writes their own humility into a charter — if they stole it, then who did they steal it from, and where did those people go?*

Halloway had reported every one of those questions. That was the procedure. Questions of that class went into the record, and the record went to the Rite-masters, and the Rite-masters adjusted the student's training accordingly, and everyone understood this the way everyone understands weather. Coral had known, too. Coral had watched Halloway write the questions down and had never once flinched, and that — more than the voice, more than the talent — had been the thing Halloway had loved, though she had not used the word then and had only begun allowing herself the word recently, in the privacy of border nights, as one allows oneself a grief long after the funeral.

She had loved them the way you love a fire in a wet winter. From a necessary distance. With both fear and gratitude.

And then, seven years ago, the Rite had come.

She remembered the chamber. She remembered the cold. She remembered the way the Rite-masters had explained it to Coral in their gentle, procedural voices — *a small taking, only a beginning, a few memories to open the conduit fully; you will not miss what you give; no one misses what they give* — and she remembered Coral looking at her across that cold room, not pleading, never pleading, just *looking*, as if to say: *you taught me what this costs. You made me memorize the accounts. You know better than anyone in this building what it is to carry other people's best moments, and you know what it does to a person to have their own taken.*

And Halloway had looked away.

That was the whole of her sin, and it was so small, and it was everything. She looked away, and in looking away she gave her answer, and Coral read the answer, and that night Coral was gone — out through the wards that should have caught them, out through country that should have known their footsteps, gone so completely that seven years of searching found nothing but a rumor in a river town and a luck-charm of impossible quality in a market stall.

Halloway had written the report of the defection herself. She had written it in the flat, procedural hand the service demanded, and she had written it at a desk with a candle, and she had written the sentence — *the subject was warned of the Rite's necessity and fled regardless* — and she had known as the ink dried that she had chosen, and that the choice was made, and that she would live inside it the way you live inside a coat that no longer fits: every day, in every weather, aware of the seams.

The Rite-masters had asked her, afterward, whether she had any attachment to the subject that might compromise her future service. It was a standard question. She had said no. It was the second lie of her life, and the first had been the looking away.

* * *

Morning came gray through the cell's high window, and with it the briefing.

Commander Vess was a soft-bodied man with a hard voice, the kind of officer the service grew in cities, and he received Halloway in a room that had once, by the moldings on the ceiling, been a practice hall. The acoustic stones were still in the walls. She could feel them the way you feel a missing tooth — a wrongness, a readiness.

"The border situation is contained," Vess began, and Halloway let him have the word, because arguing with *contained* was like arguing with a tide. "What is not contained is the cause. The analysts believe the wards did not fail. They believe they were *sung down*. Which means there is a singer, and the singer is in this country, and the First Circle wants the singer found before the panic finds a shape worse than panic." He slid a folder across the table. "You trained at Harrow's Bend. You knew the last cohort. You are, as of yesterday, the senior living authority on Choir voice-craft in the service. Congratulations. Find them."

Halloway opened the folder. Inside was a map of Vell's low quarter, marked with the locations of the night market, the memory-stalls, the charm-sellers — because of course the analysts had gone there first, the way water goes downhill. Someone who could sing the Choir would need coin, and coin in Vell's shadow economy came from memories, and memories were sold in the low quarter by the pound.

And beneath the map, a single page in a clerk's careful hand: a list of charm-sellers of unusual quality, compiled over years by informants, and at the top of the list, an entry Halloway had read before, seven years ago, in a river town, and had filed under *coincidence* because the alternative had been unbearable.

*Vendor unknown. Sells only "bright" memories — weddings, first snows, homecomings. Quality exceptional. Refuses to sell grief. Some customers report the charms feel "warmer than they should." No name. Description: young, slight, gray-eyed, keeps their hood up. Calls themself only "Wren."*

Wren. A small brown singing bird. A child's rhyme from the old Choir primers: *the wren sings what the wall remembers.*

Seven years, and they had been here, in this city, under this suffocating sky, selling other people's joy in a night market, and Halloway had known, had *known* the first time she'd read the report, and had closed the file and said nothing, because saying something would have made the choice again, and she had not yet had the courage to make it twice.

She had the courage now. Or she had something now, that could pass for courage at a distance.

"Whoever it is," Vess was saying, "they brought down a thousand miles of wards with a song. The First Circle's instruction is capture, not kill — a voice like that is a strategic asset, especially with the border in its current... condition." His mouth tightened around the word. "But if capture proves impossible, they are not to be permitted to sing again. Am I understood?"

"Understood," Halloway said.

She took the folder. She felt the acoustic stones in the walls listening, the way they had always listened, and she thought of a sixteen-year-old asking *where did those people go?* in a voice like weather, and she thought of a border wall with no country behind it, and she thought of the sentence she had written in candlelight seven years ago, and she understood — with the terrible clarity that arrives only after the choice is long past undoing — that she had not been sent to Vell to find Coral Underhill.

She had been sent to finish the report.

* * *

She worked the low quarter that night, and the night after, and she was good at it, because she had been trained in the same conservatory as the person she was hunting and knew the shape of their habits the way you know the shape of your own hand.

She walked the night market in a trader's cloak and bought luck-charms from a dozen stalls and held each one to her ear, because a Choir-trained ear could hear what a charm carried: the residue of the voice that had lifted the memory out, the way a wine carries the taste of the barrel. Most charms were dead things, scooped by crude mnemonic tongs, all the warmth boiled out. Two or three were better. And one — bought from a stall at the market's edge, from a vendor who kept their hood up and their answers short — was *alive*. She stood in the coal-smelling dark of the market with the charm in her palm and heard, underneath the widow's wedding-day it carried, a voice. Young. Level. Careful. A voice that had learned to sing *quietly*, the way a fugitive learns to walk — and underneath the quiet, a depth like a well, like the border sky, like something that could bring down a thousand miles of wards without raising its voice.

She did not let her hands shake until she was back in her cell.

Then she sat on the narrow bed with the acoustic stones humming in the walls around her like a held breath, and she took out her report-book, and she wrote the location of the stall, and the time, and the description, and the word *Wren*, in her flat procedural hand, and she stopped with her pen above the final line — *recommend immediate apprehension* — and she sat there for a long time while the wardens' bells rang the hour and the city's brown-gold ceiling of light pressed down and down and down.

She thought: *they brought down the wards. They are the reason there is no country behind the border. Whatever is happening out there in the gray, whatever is eating the villages and the stars, it began with their voice, and every day I hesitate is a day the unraveling walks closer to Vell, to the low quarter, to the ferry stairs, to everything.*

She thought: *they were sixteen, and they looked at me across a cold room, and I looked away.*

She thought: *both of these things are true, and duty is what remains when you refuse to let either one eat the other, and if that is impossible then duty is impossible, and I have been a warden for nineteen years, so it cannot be impossible.*

She finished the sentence.

She sealed it. She carried it down the corridor to the dispatch room, where a night clerk took it without looking up, and she stood a moment watching the report go into the satchel that would carry it to the apprehension teams at dawn — teams with mnemonic tongs and silence-irons and orders she herself had helped draft — and she felt the old seam of the coat that no longer fits press against her throat like a hand.

Then she went back up the stairs, and took her gray cloak off its hook, and checked the iron at her collar, and began — quietly, alone, ahead of her own orders — to plan how she would be the one to make the arrest. Because if it must be done, then it must be done by her, and no one else, and no one else's hands.

In the low quarter below her window, the night market was lighting its coals, and somewhere in it a hooded vendor was laying out other people's brightest hours on a cloth, and did not yet know that the report had gone in, and that the net was closing, and that by week's end the price of a memory in Vell would be measured in blood.

Halloway watched the market glow, and whispered — to no one, to the acoustic stones, to the sixteen-year-old in the cold room seven years gone — "I'm sorry. I'm coming. One of those is an apology and one of them is a threat, and I no longer know which is which."

And far across the city, in a flooded crypt where a disgraced priest was singing to a handful of coin-payers, a voice that Halloway could not yet hear was about to recognize, in its audience, the one resonance in all of Vell it had been hiding from for seven years.
