## 8. A Debt Paid in Memories

The invitation came on a Thursday, which Coral would later think was fitting, because Thursdays in Vell were the days the fishmongers threw out their unsold stock and the gulls went mad over the harbor, and everything about Mother Quill's summons had that same flavor — abundance rotting into opportunity.

It arrived as a paper boat.

Coral found it on the sill of their garret window when they climbed home through the gray end of morning, salt-stiff and hollow with hunger, three coins poorer than they'd hoped to be and one bruise richer where a porter had grabbed their wrist too hard. The paper boat sat on the stone as if it had sailed there through the air itself, folded from a page torn out of a ledger — Coral could see the ghost of columns, numbers half-erased, debts crossed out in red ink. Inside its hull, written in a hand like a spider walking through honey:

*You have something I want. The Drowned Bell, midnight. Come alone. Come paid.*

No signature. None needed. There were perhaps four people in Vell who could afford to fold messages out of ledger pages, and only one of them signed her threats with perfume instead of ink. When Coral lifted the little boat to their nose, they caught it: crushed violet and something older beneath, like the smell of a locked room.

Mother Quill.

Coral set the boat on their table and looked at it for a long time while the light came up mean and thin through the window. They thought about not going. They made tea out of pine needles and the last heel of yesterday's bread, and they thought about it very seriously, the way you think seriously about a door in a floor you know opens onto nothing.

But passage out of Vell cost money. Real money. Not charm-money, not the trickle of coppers they earned selling widows' wedding days to superstitious merchants. A berth on a smuggling run — and after last night, after the stars had gone out like snuffed candles and the whole city had stood in the streets looking up at that terrible clean black, whispering *sorcery* in voices that shook — passage was going to cost more than ever. Captains who'd once taken coin now took blood-oaths. Everyone wanted out, and no one wanted company.

Except, apparently, Mother Quill wanted *them*.

"You have something I want."

Coral turned the phrase over while the pine-needle tea went cold. What did a crime-lord want from a memory-thief? Not charms; Quill didn't deal in trinkets. Information, then. Or leverage. Or —

Or something Coral had, without knowing it, been carrying for seven years like a stone sewn into the lining of a coat.

They drank the tea. It tasted like the forest outside Vell, which was to say like a story everyone told but no one had seen. Then they put on their second-best coat — the one without blood on the cuff — and went back down into the city to wait for midnight.

* * *

The Drowned Bell wasn't a bell anymore and hadn't been for a hundred years. It was a chapel that the sea had claimed during some forgotten flood, then abandoned halfway, so that its lower half lay beneath black water and its upper half rose above the tide-line like a drowned man's shoulder breaking the surface. Vell had built around it, then over it, then forgotten it entirely, which was how Mother Quill had come to own it. She owned most of what Vell forgot.

Coral went at midnight, alone as instructed, down a stair that smelled of kelp and old wax, past a doorway where two of Quill's people stood with the stillness of furniture. One of them patted them down for weapons and found none, because Coral's weapon was the kind you couldn't confiscate.

The flooded nave opened up beyond, vast and dripping, lit by lanterns hung on wires above the waterline. Candles floated everywhere on little wooden rafts, hundreds of them, their flames doubled in the black water below so that it looked as though the chapel burned twice — once above, once beneath, in the inverted world where the reflection-people lived. In the center of the flooded floor, on a rafted platform of dark timber, sat a table. And behind the table sat Mother Quill.

She was smaller than her reputation. That was always the trick of her, people said — she looked like somebody's grandmother, a soft round woman in gray wool with silver hair pinned in a bun and reading spectacles pushed up on her forehead, hands folded on the table as though she'd just finished darning socks. Only her eyes contradicted the picture. They were pale, almost colorless, and they did not blink as often as eyes should. Coral had heard a drunk in the night market once describe them as "the eyes of a woman counting," and he'd been right, and he'd also vanished the next week, and those two facts were probably related.

"Coral Underhill." Her voice was warm as fresh bread. Everything about her was warm except the eyes. "Sit. You're punctual. I do hate it when they're late, and I hate it worse when they're early — eagerness is just fear wearing its good coat."

There was nowhere to sit except across from her. Coral sat. The platform swayed faintly beneath them, breathing with the water.

"You know why I sent for you," Quill said.

"I can guess," Coral said. "Everyone wants luck-charms since the stars went out. You want supply."

Quill smiled, and it was a genuinely lovely smile, and it reached all the way to her mouth and stopped dead at the border of her eyes like a traveler refused entry at a gate. "Supply," she repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. Well. Partly. The city has gone mad for comfort, hasn't it? Every merchant wife wants a piece of someone else's happiness to hold in the dark. And you" — she unfolded her hands, laid them flat — "you are the best supplier in Vell. Don't look surprised. Did you think I didn't know? Seven years, child, selling stolen memories as charms in my market — yes, mine, I own the night market, I own the stalls, I own the lamplighters — and never once paying me tithe. Do you know why I let you?"

"I assumed you hadn't noticed."

"I noticed the first week." She said it gently, the way you tell a child the truth about a pet. "I let you because your product is honest. Do you understand how rare that is? Everything else in this city is a lie wearing paint — false relics, false passports, false wine, false loyalty. But your charms are real memories, really taken. People pay me for lies and feel cheated, and pay you for truth and feel saved. I've kept you like a gardener keeps a strange flower. But the stars went out, Coral Underhill, and strange flowers become valuable in ways that get gardens burned down. So tonight we stop pretending we don't know each other. Tonight we do business properly."

"And the business is?"

"A memory," Quill said. "One specific memory. Yours."

Coral felt the cold move through them, slow as the tide under the platform. "My memories aren't for sale."

"Everything is for sale. It's merely a question of price." She produced, from somewhere within the gray wool, a small velvet bag and poured its contents onto the table with a sound like rain on a roof. Coins — but not copper, not silver. Old gold, pre-Choir gold, stamped with stars that had gone out a week ago. Enough to buy a berth on any ship in Vell. Enough to buy three berths, and provisions, and silence from every harbormaster on the coast.

"Passage," Quill said, watching Coral's face with those unblinking pale eyes. "Out of the city. Out ahead of whatever's coming — because something is coming, isn't it? You feel it more than most. I'm told you flinched when the sky went dark, and I'm told you've been asking after ships. Smugglers' ships. Which means you know things, or you are things, and either way I find myself curious." She nudged the gold an inch toward Coral. "The price is one memory. Your choice which. Something small. Something you won't miss. Sell it to me willingly, cleanly, and walk out rich enough to vanish forever. Refuse—" the smile again, lovely and doorless, "—and I'll simply take a different sort of interest in you. I have people who are very good at making thieves remember things they'd rather forget. Interrogation is just theft with worse manners."

Coral sat very still. Under the table, their hands pressed flat against their thighs, feeling for the hum there, the low constant chord they carried always — the Choir-magic coiled in them like a second pulse, outlawed, hidden, the thing that would see them Gleaned if any warden ever learned of it. Selling a memory meant opening that magic. Meant singing, even wordlessly, even inwardly, in front of a woman whose eyes missed nothing.

But passage. Passage was Pip fed for a season. Passage was Sable Fen's crew and open water and the whispers in their skull finally answered — because since the night the stars died, Coral's stolen memories had begun to stir, murmuring coordinates, tugging them toward the broken border like fish on lines. They needed out. Out was the only direction left.

"One memory," Coral said. "Small. My choice."

"My choice," Quill agreed pleasantly. "Within reason."

"How do I know you'll honor it?"

"You don't." She folded her hands again, grandmotherly, patient as sediment. "That's what makes it business instead of charity."

* * *

Taking a memory from yourself was a thing Coral had done exactly once before, seven years ago, and had sworn never to do again. Taking from another person was easy — you found the bright thread of their remembering, sang it loose the way you'd ease a splinter from skin, caught it in glass. But your own memories knew you. They held on.

They chose carefully, there on the swaying platform with Quill watching and the candlelight doubling itself in the black water. Something true but shallow. Something with weight enough to satisfy her and no map in it, no name that mattered, no fragment of the Choir.

They chose a morning. An ordinary morning, five years back: rain on the garret window, bread frying in drippings bought with the first honest week of charm-sales, the small fierce pride of having survived a year in Vell alone. A good memory. A safe memory. Nothing in it but weather and bread and being alive.

They began the song inside themselves, silent, the way they'd practiced a thousand times in a thousand dark rooms — the note that loosened, the note that lifted, the thread rising out of the chest like a filament of dawn. They felt the morning come free, golden and dripping, and reached to catch it in the glass vial they'd brought—

—and the god moved.

Coral didn't have a name for it yet; wouldn't for a long time yet. All they knew, in that moment, was that the stirring things inside them — the thousands of stolen memories that had woken with the dying of the stars — surged toward the exit all at once, like birds through an opened window, and the morning-memory tangled in the flock, and the song slipped.

For one half of one heartbeat, Coral showed Mother Quill everything.

Not everything — a sliver, a shard, a single frame snatched from a filmstrip — but it was enough. It was the Door.

They saw it reflected in Quill's face even as they saw it themselves, dragged up from wherever it lived inside them, in some memory they'd never knowingly stolen: a door standing alone in a field of gray static, impossibly tall, its wood carved all over with sleeping faces, and behind its seams a light like the memory of sunrise, and around its base the dimmed stars hanging low like lanterns in a child's bedroom, and a sound — Coral felt rather than heard it, felt Quill feel it — a vast slow breathing, as though something enormous slept just on the other side, dreaming them all.

Then Coral slammed the song shut, caught the golden morning-thread out of the air, and sealed it into the vial with hands that shook only slightly, which they counted as a triumph.

Silence. Candleflames leaned. Somewhere out in the flooded nave, water lapped against stone, patient as arithmetic.

Mother Quill had gone very still. She had removed her spectacles from her forehead without appearing to move at all, and she was holding them in one hand, and she was looking at Coral with her head tilted slightly, the way a woman looks at a lock she has suddenly realized opens onto a palace.

"Well," she said softly.

"That's not — " Coral began, and stopped, because there was no sentence in any language that could follow *that's not what it looks like*. It was exactly what it looked like. It was a door out of the world, glimpsed by the most dangerous woman in Vell.

"What," said Mother Quill, in the voice of a grandmother asking a grandchild to pass the jam, "*was that?*"

"The memory got away from me." Coral set the vial on the table between them, next to the little heap of star-stamped gold. "Memories are slippery. Sometimes they show neighboring houses. You saw a door. Doors are common."

"Doors are common," Quill agreed. She picked up the vial, uncorked it, and held it to the candlelight, and Coral watched her drink the morning — watch it go into her the way color goes into cloth, watch her expression soften into something briefly, genuinely young. Rain on a window. Bread in a pan. Being glad, for no reason, to be alive. She closed her eyes for the length of a breath.

When she opened them, the counting had resumed behind the paleness, faster now.

"It's a lovely morning," she said. "Shallow, though. You gave me a puddle when you could have given me an ocean, and we both know it, because I just watched you almost give me one by accident." She slid the gold across the table. It made its rain-sound again. "Payment, as agreed. We're square. Take it, buy your ship, go be someone else's problem."

Coral reached for the coins.

"But." Quill's hand came down flat over the pile, not grabbing — resting, the way a cat rests a paw on a mouse it has no intention of eating yet. "Before you go. Indulge an old woman's curiosity. That door. Where does it stand?"

"I don't know."

"You do. Not in your head — I saw that, it's not a place you've visited, it's a place you *contain*, which is far more interesting. But you'll find it. Whatever woke in you when the stars went out is pulling you toward it like a tide pulls driftwood. I've watched you this week, Coral Underhill. You sleep four hours and spend the rest walking the walls. You've asked Brannoc the ferryman about his boat twice. You sold a luck-charm to a harbormaster's clerk for less than it was worth just to hear him complain about which captains are bribable." She smiled the doorless smile. "You're leaving. Fine. Everyone's leaving, or wants to. But here is what I want you to carry out of my chapel along with your gold: when you find that door — and you will — you will send word to me first. Before wardens. Before priests. Before anyone. You'll send word to Mother Quill, and Mother Quill will make you rich beyond the dreams of the people whose mornings you sell. Because that door is worth more than this entire drowning city, and I intend to be standing in front of it when it opens, and I would vastly prefer to arrive as a friend."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," Quill said mildly, lifting her hand off the gold, "and you leave with your payment, and I respect it, because I keep my bargains — it's the foundation of everything I've built. But I keep my *interests* too, and those I keep longer. Go home, child. Sleep. Sail, if you can. And remember that in Vell, nothing is ever truly sold." Her colorless eyes glinted, candle-doubled in their depths. "Only lent."

* * *

Coral walked out through the drowned chapel with the gold heavy in their coat and the taste of the Door still at the back of their throat, like iron, like a struck bell. Above the waterline, the candles burned on their rafts. Below it, in the black mirror-world, their reflections burned too, and Coral avoided looking at them, because lately the reflections had started looking back a half-second late, and they couldn't bear to wonder what that meant.

The stair up to the street was narrow and wet. Halfway up, a shape detached itself from the shadow of the doorway where Quill's guards had stood — a small shape, quick, familiar, smelling of chimney soot and river mud.

"Pip." Coral grabbed him by the collar before he could bolt. "What in the name of every drowned saint are you doing here?"

"Following you," Pip said, with the injured dignity of a cat that has been caught somewhere obviously fascinating. He was eleven, maybe twelve, all elbows and sharp chin, hair cut badly by his own knife, eyes that catalogued exits the way other children catalogued constellations. "You went out at midnight dressed like a funeral. Obviously I followed. That's what family does."

"We are not — " Coral began, and gave up, because arguing the point with Pip was like arguing with weather. "Did you hear anything? In there?"

Pip's face did something complicated. Coral watched it happen: the flicker, the calculation, the decision. Eleven years old and already fluent in the grammar of secrets.

"Heard the water," Pip said. "And an old lady talking. And you singing without singing — I felt it in my teeth." He touched his own molars, demonstrating. "What'd you sell?"

"A morning."

"Was it worth it?"

Coral thought of the gold against their ribs. Thought of the Door hanging in the gray static, carved with sleeping faces. Thought of Mother Quill saying *only lent* in her warm bread voice, and of the tide already pulling them toward a broken border and whatever waited on the other side of it.

"No," Coral said honestly. "Come on. It's freezing, and if we hurry we can catch Sable before his crypt crowd thins out. There are people I need you to meet."

Pip fell into step beside them, and neither of them spoke again until they were up in the street, under the starless sky, walking fast through the dark toward the sound of a forbidden hymn — and neither of them noticed, because neither of them ever looked back at the Drowned Bell, the paper boat that had brought the summons slipping out of Coral's coat pocket, unfolding itself as it fell, and drifting back down the stair on a breath of candle-warm air, home to the woman who counted.

Nor did they see her, in the flooded nave, turning a blank sheet of ledger paper over in her fingers, beginning — slowly, pleasantly, the way one begins all the worst things — to fold.
