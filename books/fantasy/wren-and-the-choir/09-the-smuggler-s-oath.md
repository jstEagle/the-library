## 9. The Smuggler's Oath

The *Drowned Lark* did not look like a ship that could carry anyone anywhere. She looked like a ship that had already drowned once and been talked out of it — hull patched with mismatched planking, sails the color of old teeth, a name painted on her stern so many times the letters had become a smudge of grey beneath newer greys. She lay low in the water at Vell's least reputable quay, wedged between a fish-oil barge and a customs house whose windows had been bricked up to avoid the temptation of looking at anything.

Sable had told Coral to come at dusk, wearing nothing that gleamed.

Coral came at dusk wearing a coat with one silver button, because of course they did. They stood on the rain-slick boards of the quay with their hood down and their chin up, and Sable thought, not for the first time since the flooded crypt, that conduits were apparently born without the instinct for self-preservation that kept lesser criminals alive.

"You could have worn the button," he said. "Truly. Why not hang a bell around your neck as well."

"It's a good button." Coral touched it once, briefly, the way other people touched charms. "I've had worse weeks than this one."

"You've had worse weeks this week."

That earned him something almost like a smile — quick, crooked, gone before it could be taxed. Sable turned and led them up the gangplank, and felt Coral's hesitation in the way their footsteps changed behind him: lighter, readier, the walk of someone cataloguing exits. He knew that walk. He'd worn it himself for eleven years, back when he was still Father Sable Fen of the Choir of Ashenmoor, before the trials, before the defrocking, before he learned that hymns sounded just as holy sung in a flooded crypt for coppers dropped into a hat.

"Rules," he said quietly as they crossed the deck. "No one aboard knows what you are except me. Not yet. If it comes out later, it comes out on my head, which is a head nobody wants. You don't sing. You don't hum. If you feel the Choir moving in you — and I know you feel it, I heard it in your voice across forty feet of standing water — you bite it down until we're clear of the walls."

"And if biting it down isn't enough?"

Sable looked at the city behind them. The wardens' new notices were pasted on every corner now, black ink on yellow paper, the word GLEANING printed in letters tall enough to read from a moving cart. Three arrests this week. One of the arrested had been a woman who sold love-potions made of nothing but river water and confidence. The wards were fraying, everyone could feel it, and frightened institutions always reached for the oldest tools first.

"Then we're both going to learn how fast I can lie," he said.

* * *

Brannoc was waiting for them in the stern, and Brannoc was impossible to wait for anything else beside. He filled half the aftdeck the way weather fills a valley — a broad, salt-cured man with a beard braided into three ropes and hands like mooring posts. He was mending a net that Sable was fairly sure had not been whole in years and never needed to be.

"This is them?" Brannoc said, not looking up. "The passenger?"

"The passenger."

"The passenger who pays in memories." Brannoc tied off a knot with terrible gentleness, the way big men often handled small things. "I want it understood that I voted against this voyage."

"You voted against it and then volunteered your boat."

"I voted against it *loudly*, and then volunteered my boat, and those are different things." Now he looked up, and his eyes went over Coral with the unhurried thoroughness of a man assessing cargo that might bite. "You'll pardon an old ferryman's caution. I've carried pilgrims, plague-runners, one tax collector I still regret not drowning, and twice — *twice* — actual ghosts. In my experience the trouble always starts with the quiet ones sitting near the rail, looking like they're listening to something under the water."

Coral didn't flinch. "Is there something under the water?"

"There's always something under the water," Brannoc said comfortably. "Question is whether it's got opinions." He jerked his beard toward the companionway. "Go on down. Twins are arguing about supper, Tansy's winning. Mind the third stair. It minds nobody."

The hold of the *Drowned Lark* smelled of tar, dried kelp, lamp oil, and underneath all of it something older and stranger — a smell Sable had no name for, though Tansy claimed it was simply the smell of a ship that had been loved badly by many people. Lamplight swung with the river's breathing. And around the central mast, at a table scarred by decades of knives and cards and one memorable axe, sat the rest of the crew.

The twins he saw first, because the twins were unavoidable. Isolde and Maren sat shoulder to shoulder on the same bench, identical down to the freckles scattered across their noses like a careless constellation, dressed in matching grey wool, and they were conducting an argument in the particular silence of people who had done it so long they no longer needed words. Between them on the table lay a chart of the northern sky, and both of them had one finger pressed flat upon it, on different dead stars.

"Tansy," Sable said, "this is Coral. Coral — Tansy Merrivale, Isolde, Maren. Crew of the *Lark*. Family, such as it is."

Old Tansy sat with her back to the mast, a small woman folded into herself like a letter, knitting something that might have been a scarf and might have been a shroud. Her eyes were the pale milky blue of very old ice, and when she looked up at Coral, she looked for a long time.

"Well," she said at last. "There you are."

"Mistress—" Coral began.

"No, no." She waved the knitting at them. "Don't do the polite thing. I know what I said, and I meant it. Sit down, child, you're making the floor nervous."

Coral sat. The twins withdrew their fingers from the chart in unison, which somehow made the room quieter.

"You said—" Coral glanced at Sable. "What did she say?"

"She said she dreamed about you last week," Maren said. Or Isolde. With the twins it took Sable a full minute of listening to sort them, even after four years. "Before Sable ever brought your name home."

"Said I dreamed about a thief with a choir in their chest," Isolde said. "Standing in a doorway that wasn't a doorway."

"You said a singer with stolen light in their pockets," Maren said.

"I said *thief*."

"You said *singer*, Isolde, I was *there*—"

"Ladies," Sable said, and the argument subsided into the tolerant truce of long practice. He pulled a crate over and sat, elbows on knees, and looked around the lamplit circle of them — the ferryman overhead pretending not to listen through the deckboards, the twins with their dead stars, the old woman with her endless knitting — and felt the familiar ache in his chest that he had stopped calling guilt years ago and had never successfully renamed.

He had promised these people honesty when he asked them for this. So he gave it.

"Coral can hear the Choir," he said. "Not the way I do — I hear it the way a deaf man reads lips, shapes without sound. Coral hears it living. They're a conduit. Maybe one of the last ones the wards haven't found."

The silence had texture. Sable watched each of them take it differently: the twins going still as held breath, Brannoc's boots ceasing their pacing overhead, and Tansy — Tansy setting down her knitting with the deliberation of a judge laying aside a gavel.

"And the Door," Tansy said. "Tell them about the Door, Sable. Since we're being honest tonight."

Coral looked between them. "What Door?"

"The one you've been dreaming about," Tansy said mildly. "Or will be. Go on, Sable. Tell them why we agreed to sail a dying ship out of a dying city for a stranger."

So Sable told it. How Tansy had come to him three nights after the stars went out, waking him with a lantern held too close to his face, saying *the land is coming loose, priest, I can feel it in my bones the way sailors feel weather, and my bones are not from here.* How Tansy remembered a country outside this one — real grass, real tides, a sky where the stars were only stars and not, as they had learned this week, something more like lamps. How Tansy had been born beyond whatever boundary enclosed Ashenmoor and had crossed into it young, before the crossing closed, and had spent sixty years aging wrong in a country that was not quite a country. How the unraveling had started slow and was now accelerating nightly, gray static chewing inward from the borders, villages forgetting themselves, roads ending mid-stride.

And how Tansy believed — with the flat certainty of a woman who had outlived every person who might have contradicted her — that there was a Door at the heart of the land, the seam where the theft had been stitched shut, and that it would open for a conduit and no one else.

"That's why you're here," Tansy said to Coral, not unkindly. "You didn't know it yet. But that's why. Things come home when a house is falling down."

"I'm not—" Coral's hands had curled closed on the table, knuckles whitening, and Sable saw the effort it cost them to keep the voice level. "I'm a memory-thief from Vell. I steal wedding days and first kisses and sell them to people who want to feel lucky. I don't open doors. I don't save countries. I'm not—"

"A hero," Tansy finished. "Good. Heroes are useless. What I need is a thief who can hear the lock." She picked her knitting back up. "The question is whether you'll sail with us. So ask us what you actually want to ask, child, instead of staring at your fists."

Coral stared at their fists a moment longer anyway. Then: "Why? Why does any of you care? A ferryman, two star-navigators, a priest who sings in ditches, and a grandmother. None of you owe this country anything. When it goes — if it goes — you could be anywhere else. Why sail toward the wound instead of away from it?"

It was Brannoc who answered, coming down the companionway stairs with the third step's usual treacherous groan, folding himself onto the bench so that the whole table rocked.

"Because my daughter lives in Harrowmere," he said simply. "Ferry-town, north coast. My granddaughter has my nose and my temper. If the land unravels to the water, they unravel with it, and I will not be the man who sailed away and let it happen while he had a boat and a reason."

"My sister," Maren said.

"Our sister," Isolde corrected. "She married a warden in Vell and thinks we're dead, but she's ours. And besides—" she laid her finger on the dead star again, "we navigate by stars that went out. Every chart we own is a map of a dead sky now. We'd like to know why before we die of it."

"We're not going to die of it," Maren said.

"You don't know that."

"Neither do you."

Tansy let them finish, then looked at Coral over her needles, and her milky eyes were suddenly very clear and very old.

"I remember a country that doesn't exist anymore," she said. "Real grass under my feet. An ocean with a horizon on both ends. Rain that fell because of weather and not because the land couldn't decide. I have carried that country alone inside my skull for sixty years, and it is heavy, child, heavier than you can imagine, and I am tired." The needles paused. "If this land of ours is a stolen memory — and everything since the stars went out says it is — then somewhere there is a world it was stolen *from*. And I would like, before I go, to stand on grass that is grass."

The lamp swung. Somewhere above them the river slapped the hull, patient as a debt collector.

"And you," Coral said finally, turning to Sable. Last, deliberately last. "What's your reason?"

And there it was — the question he had been avoiding since the crypt, since he'd watched Coral Underhill's face change in the lamplight and known that voice, that resonance, the harmonic signature of a conservatory training he had helped destroy. He could give them the easy answer. The crew needs you answer. The Door answer.

He gave the true one instead, because Tansy had demanded honesty and because lying to a conduit seemed, lately, like lying in front of a mirror.

"Because seven years ago I stood in a marble hall and testified against a student rather than lose my own place in the Choir," he said. "I told the Rectors what I'd heard in your voice. I watched them mark your name for the Rite. And then I sang vespers that night like a man with clean hands." His throat was dry. "I've been singing in ditches ever since because marble halls make liars comfortable. This is the first thing I've done since that I'm not ashamed of. That's my reason. It isn't noble. It's a debt."

The silence stretched. Coral looked at him for a long moment, and Sable braced for the anger — deserved anger, righteous anger, the kind that would end this before it began.

Instead Coral said, quietly: "The Rite. Did they—"

"They performed it on others. On you they never got the chance. You ran the same night, and the wards ate your trail, and for seven years the Choir believed you dead or gone beyond finding." He hesitated. "Until the stars went out. Until things started waking up. Now they believe otherwise. Which means wardens are coming, and not gentle ones. That's part of why it has to be soon."

Coral absorbed that the way they seemed to absorb everything — silently, entirely, some inner ledger taking the entry. Then they looked around the table: at the twins with their dead stars, at Brannoc filling the bench, at Tansy with her knitting and her impossible memory, at Sable with his clean-hands confession hanging in the lamplight.

"All right," Coral said. "I'll sail with you. Toward the Door. But I set terms."

"Terms," Brannoc rumbled, delighted. "The passenger sets terms."

"First — I'm not cargo. I steer my own passage. Second — nobody touches my satchel. Third—" Coral's voice caught, just slightly, on something, and smoothed over. "Third. If we find my mother's village on the way, we stop. Whatever else is happening. We stop."

Tansy's needles went still.

"Where is her village?" Isolde asked.

"Emberlow. South and east, past the Ford of Nails." Coral hesitated. "It may not be there anymore. I know that. But if it is—"

"Then we stop," Tansy said, in the tone of a woman closing a bargain, and held out one small hand across the scarred table.

It was Brannoc who supplied the form of the oath, hauling a bottle up from below — not wine, but dark river-brandy, the kind smugglers drank and citizens survived — and pouring five cups, then a sixth for Coral, then, after a glance at the companionway, a seventh that he carried up the stairs to the wheel where the night watchman stood. The crew of the *Lark* did not swear oaths lightly; Sable knew the words were older than any of them, ferry-words, river-words, words traded hand to hand since before Vell had walls.

They spoke them together, palms stacked over the brandy: *By water that remembers, by stars that keep faith, by the door that opens only to the honest lost* — Tansy's addition, sixty years in the making — *we carry each other or we drown together.*

When their hands broke apart, Coral drank the brandy without flinching, which told Sable yet another unwelcome thing about the childhood they'd fled.

"So," Maren said, producing the chart of the dead sky and smoothing it flat between the cups. "South and east. Past Emberlow, if Emberlow exists. To the heart of the unraveling." Her finger traced the route, past the Ford, past the gray static creeping inward on the margins of every map, inward, inward, to a blank space at the center where no cartographer had ever bothered to put anything.

"To the Conservatory lands," Isolde murmured, and Sable saw Coral go very still.

"You know that region?" Coral asked carefully.

"Everyone knows it," Maren said. "Nobody goes there. The old Choir grounds. Even before the stars went out, the maps showed nothing — forests that don't grow, roads that curve back on themselves. Fishermen won't fish those waters." She tapped the blank space twice. "Whatever's at the center of this, the land's been hiding it for centuries."

Above them, faint through the deckboards, the watchman's voice drifted down, casual, calling the hour and the weather — and then stopping. Starting again. Stopping.

Brannoc frowned and hauled himself up the stairs to see. The lamplight swung. Tansy resumed her knitting, but slowly, watching the companionway, and Sable noticed her fingers had gone white around the needles.

From the wheel, Brannoc's voice, stripped of its comfort: "Twins. Up here. Now."

On the far bank of the river, where the gray static had been chewing at the farmlands for days, the darkness had begun — very faintly, very terribly — to glow. And out of that glow, drifting across the water toward the city, came a sound none of them could name and all of them, in their bones, recognized.

A sound like a country remembering it was screaming.
