## 10. Leaving Vell

The sewers of Vell had three names, depending on who you asked. The city fathers called them the Underways and pretended they were an engineering triumph. The smugglers called them the Drown and spoke of them the way sailors speak of a jealous sea. And Pip called them *the wet dark*, with the reverence of a boy who had lived in them for two years and considered them home.

Coral called them a mercy, because for seven years the anti-magic wards woven through Vell's stones had pressed against their gift like a hand over a mouth, and down here the wards were oldest, thinnest, worn to lace by a century of floodwater. Down here Coral could almost breathe.

"Stay behind me," Brannoc said, though he was the one carrying the lantern, and though he stood a head and a half taller than anyone in the line. He said it anyway. It seemed to be how he prayed.

The water came to mid-calf, black and warm as tea left too long, moving with a slow muscular current that tugged at boot-soles. The tunnel above them was ribbed with brick gone soft as old bread; roots had broken through in pale ropes that dripped when touched. Somewhere ahead, something splashed with a rhythm too regular to be an animal — Isolde said it was the city's heart, still beating in the masonry, and she said it in a voice that made no one laugh.

They moved in single file: Brannoc first with the lantern hooded to a coin of amber light, then Isolde, then Maren with her arm bound from the ford — no, not yet, the ford hadn't happened; Maren walked whole and watchful, her hand never far from the knife at her hip — then Tansy, small and dry as a seed pod, refusing all offers of help with the particular ferocity of the very old, then Sable, and Coral last, because someone had to watch the way they'd come.

The way they'd come was closing. Above them, muffled by thirty feet of brick and earth, Vell was screaming.

It had been screaming for two days. Since the stars went out, since the border wards shattered like a dropped plate, since the first refugees staggered through the gates babbling about villages that had forgotten themselves — gray fields, gray roads, gray people standing in doorways smiling at nothing. The wardens had responded the way institutions always respond: with lists. Notices pasted on every wall, ink still sharp:

*BY ORDER OF THE WARDENS OF VELL: ANY PERSON FOUND PRACTICING OR HARBOURING CHOIR-MAGIC SHALL BE SUBJECT TO THE GLEANING. REPORT ALL RESONANTS TO YOUR DISTRICT HOUSE.*

And beneath that, smaller, as if the clerk had hesitated: *The Gleaning is final.*

"They're sweeping the Lamplight Quarter tonight," Sable had told them, an hour ago, arriving breathless at Brannoc's ferry-house with his collar torn and his knuckles split. "House to house. They have a resonator with them — some poor creature on a leash, singing names, listening for echoes." His eyes found Coral's and held them. "They'll find you by morning."

"I could hide," Coral had said, because hiding was the only skill they'd practiced longer than theft.

"You hid for seven years," Sable said. "Look what it bought you." He hadn't meant it unkindly. That was the worst of it. He'd said it the way you say *the tide is coming in*.

So now there were six of them wading through the Drown toward the river gate, and the lantern swung its little coin of light across the water, and overhead Vell screamed and screamed.

* * *

The gate was called the Weir Gate, and Brannoc had bribed its keeper for eleven years without once using it.

"It's for emergencies," the keeper had always said. "Real ones. You open that gate, half the river comes in with you, and the Underways flood to the second landing, and everyone downstream drowns, and I hang."

"And if we pay you enough—"

"No amount buys the river, ferryman."

But the keeper wasn't at his post tonight. His post was a stone alcove beside the great iron portcullis, grooved with wheel-tracks where winches had run for generations, and he was there in the sense that his keys were there, hanging from a nail, and his supper was there, going cold on a ledge, and his hat was there, floating gently in six inches of standing water.

He himself was nowhere at all.

"He forgot himself," Tansy said quietly. She picked up the hat, wrung it out with two efficient twists, and set it on the ledge beside the supper, arranging it neatly, as if order mattered, as if neatness were a kind of prayer. She looked at the empty alcove for a long moment. "That's the third one this week. They just — go. Mid-thing. One minute ladling soup, next minute staring, and then there's no one home to stare."

"The unraveling's inside the walls now," Maren said. Her voice was flat, which was how Maren said frightened. "I told you it would come inside."

"You told us," Isolde agreed. The twins exchanged a glance over their sister's head, the quick wordless conference they conducted constantly, a language of eyebrow and shoulder. Whatever it concluded, neither said aloud.

Sable lifted the lantern toward the portcullis. Beyond it, visible through the bars, the river ran — but wrong. Where the water should have caught light it caught nothing, and where it should have reflected the drowned stars it showed instead a faint gray shimmering, like the surface of a pond full of static. The river of Vell was forgetting how to be water at its edges.

"That's the Ash," Sable said softly. He crossed himself with the old Choir-gesture, thumb to sternum, lips to fingers, a motion his body remembered even though his faith had supposedly died. "It's eating up the riverbed."

"Then we go now," Brannoc said, "or we don't go," and put his hands on the winch.

* * *

The winch fought him. Of course it did. Eleven years of disuse, salt in the gears, and above everything the deep structural objection of any machine asked to do the one thing it was built never to do. Brannoc threw his whole barrel chest into it, boots braced, veins standing in his neck, and the drum gave a shriek of rust and turned one grudging quarter-turn.

Something above them shifted. Dust sifted down through the root-holes, fine and gray.

"Faster," Maren said.

"I'm aware," Brannoc gritted.

The second turn was easier. By the fourth the portcullis was lifting, water sheeting off the bars in silver threads, and Coral felt it before they heard it — a change in the pressure of the air, a taste like struck flint at the back of the throat.

Magic. Thin, ragged, leaking through the broken wards like smoke through a torn curtain, but unmistakably present, and Coral's gift woke toward it like a plant toward a window. Seven years of pressing that instinct flat, of keeping the lid screwed down so tight they sometimes forgot there was anything underneath, and here in the Drown the lid had always been loosest. Now it was coming off on its own.

"Coral." Sable's voice, low. "Your hands."

Coral looked down. Their fingertips were glowing. Not bright — a pearl-gray luminescence, memory-light, the color of the charms they sold in the night market, and along their forearms beneath the rolled sleeves the faint tracery of the resonance-lines was surfacing, pale script rising through skin like words through developing paper.

They clenched their fists. The light didn't go out. It soaked into the skin and stayed, banked, a fire under ash.

"Not here," Coral said. "Not now. Move."

They went through the gate in a crouch, under the rising bars, into the space between the portcullis and the river, and that was when the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section of vaulting near the alcove, twenty feet of brick softened by flood and age and perhaps by something else, something that had simply forgotten how to hold together — the mortar between the bricks had gone gray and grainy, static-stuff, and when it let go it didn't crash so much as *dissolve*, collapsing into itself with a sound like sand pouring through an hourglass the size of a house.

Brannoc dove clear. Tansy did not. She was closest, and she was old, and the falling mass came down between her and the rest of them with a whump of displaced air, and the dust when it cleared was gray, gray as the fields beyond the walls, gray as the starless sky.

"Tansy!" Isolde's voice cracked on it.

A cough from within the rubble-pile. Then, muffled, furious, entirely herself: "I'm fine. My hat, however, is ruined."

She emerged through a gap like a badger from a collapsed sett, gray to the elbows, her braid dusted white, her eyes fierce. But behind her, where the vaulting had fallen, the passage was sealed. And in front of them, beyond the half-lifted portcullis, the river waited — the gray-shimmering river, the Ash creeping up its bed, and the winch-drum sat silent with its rope paid out, and the mechanism that would hold the gate open had sheared somewhere inside the wall with a sound none of them had heard over the collapse.

The portcullis began to sink.

Slowly. Rust grinding against rust. An inch, and a shudder, and another inch.

"Hold it," Maren said. "Someone get under the bar and hold it."

Brannoc tried. He got his shoulders under the lowest crossbar and pushed until his face went purple, and the portcullis slowed, trembled, continued down. Two hundred years of iron doesn't care about a strong back. Another inch. Brannoc's knees buckled; Isolde threw herself under beside him; even together they were losing, the bar descending through their grip in slow jerks, and beyond it the Ash-water crept closer, and behind them the rubble sealed them in, and above them — Coral heard it now, distantly, threading down through the broken vaulting — boots. Many boots. Wardens in the Underways, drawn by the collapse, sweeping downward with their leashed resonator, and that poor creature would be singing names right now, listening for echoes, getting closer.

Everyone was shouting. Brannoc red-faced, Isolde cursing in ferry-cant, Maren saying *the river, the river,* and Tansy, remarkably calm, saying, "Coral."

Just that. Just the name, in the voice of a woman who had watched countries be born and did not startle easily.

Coral stepped forward.

Every rule they had, every law they'd written for themselves across seven years of survival — *never sing, never glow, never let them see* — every one of those rules stood up inside them and said *no*. And Coral looked at each rule in turn the way you look at old friends across a table, and thanked them, and broke them all.

"Sable," they said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Cover your ears or don't. I won't ask twice."

"I'm not covering my ears," Sable said. "I've spent my whole life waiting to hear this."

Coral opened their hands.

The song started in the sternum. That was how it always started, though they'd never told anyone, though they'd barely admitted it to themselves — not in the throat, not in the head, but lower, in the place where the ribs hinge, the body's own keystone. It rose through them like water finding its level, and the resonance-lines on their arms blazed from tracery to script to full illumination, and the note that left them was not sound exactly, or not only sound. It was memory given voice. It was the chord of every held thing.

They sang the gate.

Not open — open was done, open was gravity's problem now. They sang it *held*. They poured the note into the iron the way they'd poured memories into charms for seven years, except this time the memory they gave it was weight itself: the memory of a mountain's patience, stolen long ago from a stonemason who'd spent forty years carving a single doorway and had loved every hour of it. The portcullis shuddered. Stopped. Hung suspended on a thread of pearl light, iron remembering what it meant to stand.

The cost arrived immediately, the way costs do.

It wasn't pain, precisely. It was subtraction. Coral felt the stonemason's forty years thinning in them, going translucent, the way a charm thins as its buyer wears it down. They had carried that memory for five years, had taken it out on bad nights like a comfort-object, the patient hands, the love of slow work. Now it drained through their palms into the gate, feeding the iron, and they let it go, and it hurt in the specific way of giving away something you'd promised yourself you'd keep forever.

"How long?" Maren demanded. She had stopped fighting the gate and was looking at Coral instead, at the lit lines on their arms, and her expression was doing something complicated.

"As long as I last," Coral said. The note cost breath to sustain; they shaped the words around it. "Go. All of you. Through. Now."

"We're not leaving you—" Isolde started.

"*Now.*"

They went. Tansy first, ducking under the suspended bar with surprising grace, then Maren, hauling Isolde, then Brannoc, who paused under the glowing gate and looked up at Coral with an expression Coral couldn't read — awe, maybe, or grief, or the particular sorrow of a man watching someone burn a bridge he'd hoped might still be crossed someday.

Then Sable. Sable stopped directly in front of Coral, close enough that the pearl light of the song lit both their faces, and for one suspended moment — one note of it — neither of them moved.

"Seven years I've wondered what you'd look like," Sable said, "singing."

"Don't waste it," Coral managed. "Go."

He went. And Coral held the gate alone in the dark with the wardens' boots coming closer and their stolen mountain draining through their hands, and thought, with the strange clarity that arrives at the exact center of catastrophe: *so this is what I am for.*

* * *

The other side of the gate was a landing of wet stone, and beyond it the gray river, and beyond that —

Beyond that, Isolde was already shouting about the boat, which was moored fifty yards downstream in a slip cut into the embankment, and the plan had been to reach it quietly, cast off quietly, and be a rumor by dawn. Plans. Coral released the note at last, letting the portcullis drop behind them with a boom that echoed down every tunnel in the Drown like a slammed door on seven years of hiding, and ran, stumbling, drained to a husk, the world graying at the edges.

Boots behind them. Lanternlight. A voice amplified by a speaking-trumpet, distorted, official:

*"RESONANT ACTIVITY, GATE NINE. SEAL THE UPPER LANDINGS. NOTHING COMES OUT OF THE DROWN TONIGHT."*

They reached the boat. Brannoc shoved off. The current took them — the real current, the living water, though even here gray filaments drifted in the eddies like hairs in old milk — and Vell fell away behind them, its walls rising black against a sky with no stars, its windows lit with panic, its bells beginning to ring alarm after alarm after alarm.

Coral sat down hard in the bilge, shaking. Sable crouched beside them immediately, one hand hovering near their shoulder, not quite touching, asking permission even now.

"You gave it a memory," he said. "To hold the gate. I felt it — the chord changed when you fed it. What did it cost?"

"A stonemason," Coral said. Their voice was a rasp. "Forty years of loving slow work. Worth it." They looked up at him, and their mouth did something that wanted to be a smile. "You know the worst part? I've been saving myself for nothing. Hoarding. Seven years of *don't use it, don't show them, stay safe* — and the second it mattered, I didn't even hesitate."

"No," Sable said quietly. "That's the best part."

Maren was at the tiller. Isolde was bailing. Tansy sat wrapped in Brannoc's spare coat, gray dust still in her braid, looking back at Vell with an expression Coral couldn't name — not nostalgia, not fear. Recognition, maybe. Like a woman watching a country she'd already seen die once.

And then Pip stood up from under the tarpaulin amidships.

He was soaked, filthy, clutching his satchel of bottled weather to his chest, and he had the specific rigid stillness of a child who has been holding very still for a very long time and knows the stillness is about to stop working.

Nobody moved. Even the river seemed to wait.

"You," Brannoc said finally, with the weariness of a man whose prayers have just been answered sarcastically, "were supposed to be at the ferry-house."

"There are wardens at the ferry-house," Pip said.

"There are wardens *everywhere*," Maren snapped. "That's rather the point of tonight!"

"They'd have Gleaned him," Pip said, chin up, defiant, terrified, pointing a small grimy finger at Coral. "He's got the same — he glows the same — I heard the resonator lady say it, before I ran. She said *there's a strong one in the Lamplight Quarter, a conduit, unregistered.*" His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it and kept going anyway. "You'd have gone back for me. Everyone says don't take strays and then everyone goes back for strays. So I made it easy."

Silence, except the water, and the bells, and far upstream the gray shimmer of the Ash, patiently, patiently advancing.

Tansy laughed. It was a dry, rustling sound, like wind through winter reeds, and everyone turned to look at her, because no one had heard her laugh before.

"Oh, keep him," she said. "Any child who can out-stubborn a warden cordon deserves drowning somewhere more interesting than Vell." She settled deeper into the coat. "Besides. The land's coming apart, the stars are dead, the river's turning to static, and we're sailing toward a Door at the end of the world with a thief, a priest, and a pair of twins who navigate by nothing." She looked around at all of them, one by one, and her old eyes were suddenly very bright. "We were never going to be sensible again. It's past time someone told the boy."

Pip's face did something complicated and young. He climbed the rest of the way out from under the tarpaulin, dripping, and wedged himself into the bilge beside Coral, close, the way small animals press close to warmth, and after a moment his cold bony shoulder came to rest against Coral's arm.

Coral didn't move away. Outside the walls of the only home they'd known for seven years, under a starless sky, on a river that was forgetting how to be a river, they sat with a stowaway pressed against their side and let themselves be leaned on.

Ahead, the gray was widening. Where the river met the country beyond Vell, the water simply stopped being water — a line of faint shimmer stretching bank to bank, and past it, where fields and forests and the road south should have been, nothing. Static. The unfinished sketch of a world.

Isolde saw it first. Her hand found her sister's arm.

"Maren," she said. "The map's wrong."

Maren followed her twin's gaze to the gray line, and then further, to the place where their heading and the world disagreed, and Coral watched her understand — really understand, for the first time — that the charts her family had trusted for three generations ended here, at the edge of a country that had never existed at all.

"Then we need a new map," Maren said at last, and turned to Coral, and her eyes were hard and asking.

And Coral, hollowed out, glowing faintly still at the fingertips, with a child asleep against their shoulder before they'd even noticed the exhaustion taking him — Coral looked at the gray line where the world stopped, and felt, stirring in the vaults of their stolen memories, the first whisper of coordinates.

They knew the way.

That was the terrible thing. Some part of them had known the way all along.
