## 11. The Gray Country

Isolde had navigated by dead stars for eleven years, and she had thought herself past the point where the sky could frighten her.

She and her sister had learned their trade in the drowned quarter of Vell, reading tide-charts by candlelight, memorizing the drift of currents no living pilot had charted before them. When they took to smuggling, they'd learned the older art: the stars that had gone out still pulled. A dead star's gravity did not vanish with its light. You could not see it, but you could feel it, the way you could feel a hand hovering an inch above your shoulder without touching. Maren said it was nonsense, that Isolde simply had a good memory for angles, and Isolde let her believe it, because a navigator who seemed touched by something uncanny was either revered or thrown overboard, depending on the crew.

So when the stars went out — all of them, all at once, like a chandelier snuffed between one heartbeat and the next — Isolde had felt it as a physical loss. Eleven years of listening to the pull of dead lights, and now even the dead had stopped speaking.

But that was Vell. That was behind walls, amid panic, wardens ringing bells, priests burning incense against sorcery in every open square.

This was different.

This was the countryside beyond the border, on the morning after the wards fell, and Isolde stood at the edge of the smugglers' road with her boots planted on packed dirt that simply *stopped*, three paces ahead of her, in a line so clean it might have been ruled with a carpenter's edge. Beyond the line there was no cliff, no river, no forest floor. There was gray. Not fog-gray, not stone-gray, not the gray of overcast water. Gray the way an unwritten page is gray. It went on forever, flat and depthless, and when she tilted her head she could almost see shapes in it — the suggestion of hills, the ghost of a hedgerow — the way you see figures in smoke.

"Road ends," Brannoc said behind her. He said it the way he said most things, slow and heavy, like setting down cargo. "Road just ends."

"It doesn't end," Maren said. She was crouched at the line, one hand hovering over the gray without touching. "It stops being finished. Look at the edge of it. The stones get smaller, then they're pebbles, then they're suggestions of pebbles." She glanced up at Isolde, and Isolde saw the thing in her sister's face that she had seen only twice before: the night their father's boat went down, and the night the twins had decided to leave Vell for good. Fear, worn openly, because there was nowhere to put it. "Someone was making this road and never got around to finishing it."

"Roads aren't made," Isolde said. "Roads happen. Feet make roads."

Maren looked at her. "Then whose feet were these?"

Nobody answered. Behind them, Sable Fen stood with his hands folded and his eyes half-closed, murmuring something under his breath that might have been prayer or might have been counting. Pip sat on Brannoc's shoulders, uncharacteristically silent, gripping the big man's hair with both fists. And Coral Underhill stood apart from all of them, facing the gray with their head tipped slightly, as though listening to a voice too quiet for anyone else to catch.

"Coral," Isolde said. "You're our map now. Which way?"

Coral didn't answer immediately. Their lips moved. Then they raised one arm, slowly, and pointed not along the road but out into the gray itself, off at an angle where nothing was — where nothing could be, because nothing was what the gray was made of.

"There," Coral said. "The Waystation. It remembers being a shrine once. It wants us to come."

*Wants*, Isolde noted. Not *waits*. She filed that away with everything else she was filing away this morning, in the drawer she kept for things too large to think about until evening.

They stepped off the road together, all six of them, and the gray took their feet without complaint. It felt like walking on cold sand, or on the memory of sand — firm enough to bear weight, yielding enough to leave prints, except that their prints filled in behind them almost immediately, smoothing themselves flat, as though the land were embarrassed to be walked on and hurried to erase the evidence. Isolde looked back twice. By the third look, there was no trail at all.

"We'll lose the road," Brannoc rumbled.

"We've lost it already," Isolde said. "Look."

Ahead of them, maybe half a mile out, the gray thickened into something. It resolved slowly, the way a print develops in a darkroom — first the idea of trees, then the shape of trees, then trees themselves, birches with white bark and small trembling leaves, growing denser as they approached until there was a wood around them with birdsong in it. Except the birdsong came in phrases. Three notes, pause, silence where the fourth should be. Three notes, pause, nothing. As if the bird had been invented by someone who understood music but not birds.

Pip slid down from Brannoc's shoulders and caught one of the falling leaves. He held it up to the light. It was green on one side and blank white on the other, like a coin worn smooth on one face. He turned it over twice, said, "Huh," and pocketed it. Isolde found herself absurdly comforted by that — a child collecting the world's mistakes as curiosities, filing them away in pockets instead of dread.

They walked through the unfinished forest for two hours. Isolde kept track by habit, marking time the old way, by the angle of light through branches that sometimes had leaves and sometimes had only the pale sketches of leaves, drawn in silver on the air. Twice the forest thinned ahead of them into gray again, and twice Coral turned them gently aside, following some thread only they could hear, and each time the gray gave way rather than be crossed. Once they passed a stream that ran uphill, quietly, without any sense of effort, and Maren knelt beside it and laughed — a short, sharp laugh with no humor in it at all.

"I charted waters my whole life," she said. "And this place can't even be bothered to obey itself."

"Maybe it hasn't been told yet," Sable said softly. He had not spoken much since the border fell. He walked with his priest's collar hidden under a scarf, but he walked like a priest anyway, hands loose, head inclined, listening the way he must have listened in his crypt below Vell, singing hymns to a god everyone said was dead. "Maybe someone is supposed to tell it, and nobody has."

"Who tells a country what it is?" Maren demanded.

Sable looked at Coral. Only for a moment. Then he looked away, and Isolde saw it, and said nothing, because she was good at seeing things and better at keeping them.

Near midday they came upon a house.

It stood alone in a clearing, timber-framed, thatched, with a garden plot and a well and a goat tethered by the fence. Smoke rose from its chimney. Everything about it was perfect, complete, lovingly detailed down to the iron hinge on the gate — and that was how Isolde knew, before anyone said anything, that something was wrong. The forest had been vague at its edges, sketchy, apologetic. This house was more real than anything else they had seen all day. Too real. Real the way a portrait is more real than a face.

A woman was hanging washing on a line.

"Hoy!" Brannoc called, because Brannoc was Brannoc. "Travelers! Any chance of water?"

The woman turned. She smiled — a wide, warm, unhurried smile — and waved, and went back to her washing. Brannoc tried the gate. It opened. He crossed the yard, asked about water again, louder. The woman smiled and waved again, the same smile, the same wave, exactly the same, the arc of her arm identical, and went back to her washing.

"The same," Maren whispered. "That was the same. Exactly the same."

Pip picked up a stone and threw it, gently, toward her. It passed through her shoulder without disturbing the linen, without disturbing anything, and landed in the garden among beans that were painted onto rows of dark soil.

"She's not here," Coral said quietly. They had come up to the fence, and their face had gone very still, in the particular way Isolde had begun to recognize as the look of someone hearing something unbearable. "Or rather — she is. She's a memory. Someone remembered this house, this morning, this exact hour, and the land is playing it back. But whoever remembered it—" Coral's voice caught, very slightly, and resumed. "Whoever remembered it isn't remembering anymore. The recording plays, but the player is gone."

The washing flapped in a wind that touched nothing else in the clearing. The woman hummed four bars of a lullaby, started over, hummed the same four bars. Isolde stood at the gate and listened until she couldn't anymore.

They walked on. Nobody suggested resting near the house. Nobody suggested anything for a long while.

* * *

In the afternoon the gray changed tactics.

Isolde noticed it first as a wrongness in her sense of direction — a faint nausea, like a compass needle trembling between poles. She stopped, closed her eyes, and listened inward for the dead stars, out of pure habit, and found nothing, which she expected, but beneath the nothing she found something else: a faint dragging sensation, southeast, like a current pulling at her ankles.

"Something's drawing us off-course," she said. "Southeast. Feel it?"

Maren felt it. So did Brannoc, though he described it as "a tug, like the tide going out." Even Pip admitted to a pull, "like when you remember you left the stove lit." Only Coral shook their head — and then paused, and frowned, and said, "Not pulling me. Pulling at me. Trying to."

"The land?" Sable asked.

"The holes in the land," Coral said. "Places that were never finished. They want... material. I don't know how else to say it. We walk near a gap in the world and the gap notices we're full, and it wants what we have." They looked down at their own hands. "I can hear it clearer than any of you. Which means I should lead. And which means if I start walking somewhere none of you chose to go, someone should stop me."

"I'll stop you," Brannoc said simply.

"You'd try."

"I'd manage."

It was Pip, of course, who found the first of the gaps. He ran ahead the way he always did, an irrepressible scrap of motion in the gray afternoon, and suddenly he wasn't there. One moment he was ten yards out, the next the space where he had been was empty — not hiding-himset-empty but *unoccupied*-empty, a patch of gray with no boy in it at all.

The scream Pip made was very small, and it came from everywhere and nowhere, thin as a whistle across water.

Brannoc moved faster than Isolde had ever seen him move. Coral moved faster still — and Isolde watched, from five paces away, as Coral knelt at the edge of the gap and did something with their voice. Not words. A sound, low and round and resonant, a single sustained note that Isolde felt in her sternum and her teeth, and the note *pushed*. The gray recoiled from it like a hand from a hot stove. And out of the recoiling gray came Pip, tumbling, gasping, clutching his own arms as though confirming he still had them, and Coral caught him and held him and sang the low note again, softer, until the boy stopped shaking.

"What did it feel like?" Isolde heard Maren ask him later, while they made camp in the lee of a half-finished hillside that at least cast a shadow, and therefore existed properly.

"Like being forgotten," Pip said. His voice was steady, but his hands weren't, and he kept them busy feeding twigs to the fire. "Not scary-forgotten. Like — you know when you walk into a room and forget why? All over. Everywhere. Like I was a why, and somebody forgot me." He fed the fire another twig. "Coral's voice fixed it. Coral's voice is like — like somebody saying your name in the dark."

Isolde lay back and looked up. The sky overhead was the worst thing yet, worse than the ground, because the ground at least pretended. The sky was gray from horizon to horizon, cloudless, sourceless, lit evenly by nothing, and there were no stars at all — not dimmed, not veiled, *absent*, as if the night had been sketched in and never inked. In Vell, when the stars went out, there had been screaming and bells and priests in the streets. Out here, under this blank ceiling, Isolde understood for the first time that Vell's terror had been a luxury. Vell had lost its stars. This country had apparently never finished having them.

Beside her, her sister spoke without preamble, the way Maren always did when she'd been thinking too long.

"If Coral can push the gray away with a song," Maren said, "then Coral knows what this place is. Or suspects. Or is hearing it told to them right now." She turned her head. "Do you trust them?"

Isolde considered the question honestly, because her sister deserved honesty. Across the small fire, Coral sat awake, wrapped in their cloak, watching the darkness where the darkness should have been. Sable sat close — closer than necessary — and neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had the quality of a conversation held in a language Isolde didn't know.

"They stole memories for seven years and lied to everyone including themselves," Isolde said at last. "Yes. I trust them."

"How do you figure that?"

"Because they haven't run." Isolde nodded toward the gray country rolling away on every side. "Anyone who wanted to use whatever they are would use it and go. They're walking toward the middle of it. Toward the Door. People only walk toward the wound if they mean to heal it."

Maren was quiet a moment. Then: "Father used to say the sea doesn't forgive, it just forgets. Maybe that's what this place needs. To be forgiven by someone who remembers it." She pulled her blanket up. "Go to sleep, Isolde. One of us has to keep watch, and you listen better in the dark."

Isolde kept the middle watch. Nothing came out of the gray. But sometime deep in the night she heard Coral singing — barely a breath of it, a cradle-song, tuneless and low — and heard the land around their campfire grow quieter, as though even the unfinished world leaned in to listen.

* * *

They reached the Waystation at dawn, or at what the gray country offered up as dawn — a slow brightening of the sourceless light, like an eye opening behind cloth.

It stood on a low rise where no rise had been visible the evening before: a shrine-station of the old road-network, one of the rest-shrines that had dotted Ashenmoor in the days when people still traveled it, before the borders closed. Isolde knew the type from charts and hearsay. Stone walls, a slate roof, a bell niche, a threshold inscribed with the old blessing — *Rest, traveler; the road remembers you* — and, inside, a lamp that was supposed to burn perpetually.

The lamp was burning.

That was the first impossible thing. The second was that the shrine was whole. Not maintained-whole, not repaired-whole, but untouched-whole, its stone crisp-edged, its roof un-mossed, in a country where forests were sketches and houses were echoes. It stood out of the gray the way the woman at the wash-line had stood out of it — more real than its surroundings — but differently. The woman had been a recording. This was something else. This felt *attended to*.

Coral stopped at the bottom of the rise and would not come further.

Isolde watched them struggle with it. Watched their jaw set, watched them take one step and stop, take another. Whatever whisper-thread they had followed for a day and a half across the unfinished land, it ended here, at the base of this hill, and something at the top of it frightened them more than the gray did.

"What is it?" Isolde asked, coming to stand beside them.

"It knows what I am," Coral said. Their voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut from inside. "Shrines like this were Choir property. Waystations. Rest-points on the singing-roads. If any part of this country still works the way it was designed to work, it will know a conduit when one walks in." They laughed, shortly, without humor. "Seven years I hid from the wards of Vell. Now I'm walking up to a building built specifically to detect me."

"You could wait here," Sable said. "We could look inside first."

"No." Coral straightened. "No. It called me. I heard it calling for two days. You don't get to call someone across a dying country and then hide when they arrive." They looked at Isolde. "Watch the gray while I'm inside. If it starts pulling, sing low and loud, any note, it doesn't matter — it hates being pushed at. Buy me minutes."

Isolde nodded. She watched Coral climb the rise, each step deliberate, watched Sable fall into step beside them uninvited, watched the two of them cross the threshold of the Waystation together.

Inside, the lamp flame stood perfectly still. On the wall behind the altar hung a map of the old singing-roads, inked on vellum, complete and legible, and Isolde heard Sable's breath catch audibly at the sight of it — a map of the country as it had been meant to be, every road finished, every station marked. And beneath the altar, set into the stone, a basin of clear water that had not been there a moment ago, and in the water—

Coral reached out and touched the surface, and the shrine *sang*.

It was not loud. It was a chord, felt more than heard, rising up through the floor and the walls and the bones of everyone standing inside it, and Isolde, waiting outside on the slope with her hand on her knife, felt it pass through her like a hand passing through wheat. For one instant the gray country around the shrine became something else. Grass. Green, wet, starred — no, not starred, but *star-ready*, a night sky full of room where stars belonged — warm wind moving through real grass, the smell of rain on soil, the sound of a bell ringing in a village she had never visited and suddenly grieved for. For one instant the land remembered itself, whole and alive and loved.

Then it was gone. The gray closed back over the hillside like water over a dropped stone. But the crew stood frozen around the campfire remains, Brannoc with his mouth open, Maren with tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face, Pip clutching his bottled storms to his chest, and Isolde stood with her heart hammering and her skin prickling and one thought repeating in her mind like a bell:

*It can come back. Whatever this was, it can come back.*

Coral came out of the shrine slowly, looking older and younger at once. In their hands they carried the map, rolled tight, and something else — a small clay charm, plain and unglazed, that they closed their fingers around without showing anyone.

"Well?" Maren managed.

"The Waystation's a waypoint," Coral said. "There are others. Each one remembers a piece of the country, and each one we wake makes the next stretch of road solid enough to walk." They unrolled the map a few inches and weighted it with a stone. Inked lines ran across it like veins. "The first route runs west-southwest, toward the ford at Marrow. After that—"

"After that, the Conservatory," Sable said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't touched the map. He was staring at the middle distance with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"You know that already," Coral said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I suspected. The hymns I sang in the crypt — the old ones, the forbidden ones — they're all road-songs, Coral. Every hymn in the Choir's book is a map. I've been singing coordinates for years without knowing it." He looked up, and Isolde saw guilt move across his face like weather. "The routes all converge on one place. The place where the Choir trained its conduits. Where you were raised."

Silence, except for the wind in the half-real birches, blowing three notes at a time.

"So we go to the ford," Isolde said into the quiet, because someone had to say the practical thing, because someone always had to, because that was what she was for. "West-southwest. We wake what we can on the way. And we stay ahead of whatever's chasing us, because something is — I've felt it since last night. Southeast. Like a tide going out." She met Coral's eyes. "How long before the wardens find us?"

Coral's hand closed around the little clay charm, and for just a moment their expression flickered — grief, or guilt, or recognition, Isolde couldn't tell which.

"Sooner than we'd like," Coral said.

Far to the southeast, where the gray met the horizon, the light bent strangely — as if the unfinished land had begun, very quietly, to lean.
