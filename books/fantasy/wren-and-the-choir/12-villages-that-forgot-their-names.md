## 12. Villages That Forgot Their Names

The town announced itself by its silence.

Coral felt it before they saw it — a hush in the memory-whispers that had been their only compass since the border fell. The fragments in their head had grown louder each day since Vell, murmuring directions in voices Coral almost recognized: *left at the river that remembers being called Silverwane; follow the road until the road stops believing in itself.* But as the crew crested the last hill above the valley, the whispers went quiet all at once, like birds stopping mid-song.

Below them lay a town of perhaps two hundred souls, tucked into a fold of hills that were — Coral noticed with a lurch — slightly wrong. Not gray-static wrong, like the countryside beyond the walls. Worse. These hills were *overwritten*, too green, too smooth, the way a child colors over an old drawing without bothering to match the lines beneath. Somewhere under that grass there had been other hills, and the land hadn't bothered to erase them so much as paint past them.

"Smoke from the chimneys," Brannoc said, shielding his eyes. "That's something."

"People," Isolde said, "or the shape of people."

Sable stood beside Coral at the crest, his priest's coat long since traded for a smuggler's oilskin, his hands folded in the old devotional posture he probably didn't know he still used when he was afraid. "You feel it too," he said. It wasn't a question.

"The quiet," Coral said. "Yes."

They descended into the town together, because that was what they did now — six of them moving through an unraveling country like a needle through failing cloth. Pip rode on Brannoc's shoulders, pretending to steer him by the ears. Maren walked with her arm bound against her ribs, the ford-wound still angry red beneath its dressing, refusing with her whole silent self to be carried. Tansy brought up the rear, and Coral tried not to look at how translucent her shadow had become in the flat starless light.

*Try not to notice,* Coral told themselves. *Noticing is a kind of naming, and this land punishes names.*

The first person they met was a man sweeping his doorstep. He looked up as they passed, smiled — a wide, warm, empty smile — and said, "Good morning."

It was not morning. There was no morning anymore, no evening either, just the endless pewter light of a sky with nothing in it. But Coral found themselves answering, "Good morning," because the alternative was to scream.

The streets were clean. The houses were whole. Laundry hung between windows, drying in a wind that smelled of nothing at all. And every single person they passed looked up, smiled that same warm hollow smile, and greeted them as though the world were fine, as though the stars had never gone out, as though nothing older than last week had ever happened.

"They're not unhappy," Maren said quietly. She had stopped walking. Her hand was pressed flat against a well's stone rim, reading it like a page. "I've seen grief. I've seen fear. This isn't either." She lifted her hand and looked at it as if it had betrayed her. "There's nothing underneath. The well doesn't remember water being drawn. The stones don't remember hands."

"Places don't remember things," Brannoc said, but his voice wavered on it, because after the Gray Country none of them believed that anymore.

Tansy touched a fencepost as they went, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal you're afraid might be dead. "This wood was cut somewhere else," she murmured. "Or it was never cut anywhere. It's just... asserted." She shook her head. "In my country, we'd have called this a haunting. Here I think it's the opposite. An un-haunting."

A woman came out of the bakery with a tray of rolls and offered them around, smiling. They smelled of bread, technically, the way a drawing of fire smells of warmth. Pip took one anyway and chewed thoughtfully. "Tastes like the idea of bread," he reported. "If you didn't think about it too hard, it was good."

"Don't eat anything here if you can help it," Coral said, and heard their own voice come out sharper than intended. Pip flinched, and Coral softened it: "Food that doesn't come from anywhere makes you into someone who's from nowhere. Trust me. I've spent seven years learning what borrowed things do to a person."

The town square held a fountain, dry, and a notice board with announcements pinned to it — a harvest festival dated for a season that would never come, a lost dog, a wedding announcement. All of them dated within the last nine days. Nothing older. Coral searched the board twice, looking for yellowed paper, curled corners, any scrap of a time before the night the stars went out. The oldest document in the town was nine days old, and it was a recipe for plum preserves contributed by someone named Marta Ossel, who was standing right across the square selling apples and smiling at everyone.

"Marta Ossel," Coral said, crossing to her. "You wrote the plum preserves recipe?"

"Oh, yes!" The woman beamed. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with flour still dusting her forearms and a smear of it on one cheekbone. Her smile was genuine. That was the worst part. Every smile in this town was genuine, the way a mirror's reflection is genuine. "Everyone says I should enter it in the festival."

"Which festival?"

Her brow creased — a tiny flicker, a candle guttering — and then smoothed. "The harvest festival! In autumn."

"It's not autumn," Coral said gently. "What season is it?"

Marta looked up at the blank pewter sky, and for one terrible half-second her face went loose and drifting, a boat untied from its mooring. Then she smiled again. "Nice weather for it, whatever it is!"

Behind Coral, Sable made a small sound. Coral turned. The priest had gone the color of tallow.

"I know her," he whispered. "Coral. I *know* her. Not — not from here. From seminary texts. Marta Ossel of Lowmarsh wrote hymns. Simple ones, harvest hymns, the kind we weren't allowed to sing after the Reforms. There's a woodcut of her in the *Antiphonal of the Provinces*. She died eighty years ago."

The square seemed to tilt. The apples in Marta's cart gleamed, wax-perfect, painted by a god who remembered fruit but not orchards.

"That's not possible," Isolde said, but she said it the way sailors say it about storms they can see coming.

"She wouldn't know," Coral said. Their own voice sounded far away. "Ask her. Ask her anything older than nine days."

Isolde stepped forward, and the twins were frightening when they worked together even now — Maren taking Marta's hands, Isolde leaning close, both wearing their gentlest expressions. "Marta," Maren said. "Tell us about your mother."

"My mother?" Marta smiled warmly. "She's lovely. She makes the best stew."

"What's her name?"

Another flicker. Another smoothing-over, like ice reforming over dark water. "Mother! We call her Mum."

"Where do you live?"

"Here!"

"What town is this?"

Marta's smile widened, and beneath it, for the first time, Coral saw something move — not expression, exactly. Pressure. The way a wall moves just before it falls. "Home," Marta said. "This is home." And then, with the serene confidence of a sleepwalker: "Isn't it?"

* * *

They gathered at the edge of the square, in the shadow of a shrine whose niche was empty — no statue, no saint, just a smooth oval absence where devotion should have been fixed to something.

"We could keep going," Brannoc said. He said it heavily, already knowing the answer. "Around the town. Toward the Waystation your head keeps muttering about. People aren't our errand, Coral. The Door is."

"The Door is for these people," Coral said. "Every hour we walk past towns like this, more of them forget which direction they were facing. You heard Tansy. The unraveling is speeding up. Regions go dark nightly now." They looked back at the square, at the warm, vacant faces moving through their warm, vacant morning. "And besides. I need to understand what's happening to them before I can trust what my own head is telling me. If these people are just... set dressing, if they're puppets the land animates to look alive, then maybe the Door only matters for us. For real people. But if they're real—"

"If they're real," Sable finished quietly, "then everything changes."

"Everything has already changed," Maren said. "The question is whether it changed into something worth saving."

Pip tugged at Coral's sleeve. He had been uncharacteristically quiet since they entered the town, and now Coral saw why: he was clutching one of his little glass bottles, the ones he swore could catch weather, and staring at it with an expression much too old for his face.

"It's like my bottles," he said. "When I catch rain, sometimes the rain forgets it was a storm. It just sits there being wet." He looked up. "Somebody caught this whole place, Coral. And shook it."

Coral crouched to his level. "Then somebody needs to shake it back."

"You know how?"

Coral did not answer, because the answer was *yes*, and the answer was *it will cost me*, and the answer was *they will see me do it*, and all three of those answers frightened them in different proportions.

But Tansy was watching them with her faded, patient eyes, and she said, "You're a Conduit, child. Whatever else you've been running from — that's what you are. Conduits don't hold memories. They pass them through. So pass something through." She nodded toward the square, toward Marta Ossel selling her impossible apples. "Give the woman back one true thing and see what happens. If the land objects—" her mouth quirked, "—well. Then we'll finally learn who's listening."

* * *

Marta was happy to have visitors. She ushered them into her kitchen — clean, warm, furnished with objects that had the unsettling glossiness of things recently imagined — and poured tea that steamed convincingly and tasted of steam.

Coral sat across from her and let the others drift to the edges of the room. Brannoc filled the doorway like a door himself. The twins examined the shelves. Sable sat very still, watching Coral with an intensity that Coral refused to meet, because meeting it would mean admitting what they were about to do.

"So," Coral said. "Tell me about plums."

"Oh, they're wonderful this year!" Marta clasped her hands. "Sweetest crop in—"

"In how long? Sweetest crop in how long?"

The pause stretched. Marta's smile held, held, held — and Coral watched the exact moment the performance of happiness ran out of script. Her hands stilled in her lap. Her eyes drifted to the window, to the featureless sky, and something behind them reached for a memory of blue and found the shelf empty.

"I don't..." she began. "I was going to say something. About last year. Or the year before." Her fingers pressed against her temples. "There was a — there was a bad summer once, wasn't there? The plums were small and sour and Father said—" She stopped. Her breath caught audibly. "Who is Father? I had a — I had someone who called me—"

"Marta." Coral leaned forward. Their heart was slamming. Seven years of hiding magic, of burying the Choir's training so deep they'd nearly buried the girl-and-boy-and-neither-they'd-been beneath it, and now they sat in a stranger's kitchen preparing to do the one thing the wards of Vell had been built to prevent. "I'm going to give you something back. It might hurt. It might feel like drowning for a moment. Can you be brave with me?"

Marta looked at them — really looked, for the first time, her empty smile falling away like a mask slipping its string. "You have a singer's face," she said vaguely. "My grandmother used to say some people carry songs behind their teeth. Are you one of those?"

"Yes," Coral said. "Hold out your hands."

What Coral chose mattered, and they knew it. Not their mother — never their mother, that memory was load-bearing. Not Vell, not the conservatory, not Sable singing in the flooded crypt with candlelight swimming on black water. They reached instead for something small and borrowed and safe: the widow's memory, the wedding day. The one they'd stolen in chapter one of their life of theft, lifted from a laughing bride in Vell's night market, sold and re-stolen and kept because it was the brightest thing they owned. A memory of bells and rain-sweet air and a young man lifting a young woman off her feet while the whole street cheered.

*I'll give it back someday,* Coral had promised themselves, the way thieves promise everything. *To her, or to someone.*

They cupped Marta's hands and sang.

Not aloud — or barely aloud, a thread of sound, three notes of the old scale that the Choir had built the world's remembering on. The notes left Coral's chest and became something else in the passage: light, warmth, the smell of rain on hot cobblestones. They felt the memory leave them the way blood leaves a opened vein, and they guided it, the way they'd been trained at eight years old to guide it, down through their palms into the hollow woman in front of them.

Marta gasped.

For a moment she simply stared, pupils blown wide, tears starting without any expression attached to them — pure physiological overflow, the body's protest at being refilled. Then her face began to move through weather. Confusion. Wonder. Grief — a sharp, specific grief, some wedding-guest long dead, some argument about table settings, the texture of an actual life. Joy so sudden it looked like pain. Her grip crushed Coral's fingers white.

"Bells," she whispered. "There were bells, and the street was — no. No, that's not mine, those are someone's bells—" Her eyes flew up and found Coral's, and they were fierce and wet and *present*, more present than anyone in this town had been, more present than most people anywhere. "Those are yours. Someone else's. Why are you giving me someone else's—"

"Because yours were taken," Coral said. "And I needed to open the door."

Marta shuddered, and then — Coral felt it happen, felt it through their joined hands like a current finding ground — the borrowed memory passed *through* her, out into the town, into whatever web of forgetting held it, and snagged. Caught. Pulled other threads with it.

Marta screamed.

It was not a scream of pain. It was the scream of a woman surfacing from deep water all at once, sixty years of submerged life detonating in her chest. She tore her hands free and staggered up, knocking the tea things flying, and began to weep and laugh and speak all at once:

"The blight summer — Father said the trees would recover, he was right, they always recovered — my name is Marta Ossel, I wrote hymns, I wrote *hymns*, they sang them at Lowmarsh before the Reforms — I had daughters, I had *daughters*, where are my daughters, they'd be — oh, oh no, oh no, how long, how long has it been, the stars, the stars went out and everyone just — we just *smiled*—"

She rounded on Coral, and her face was terrible and magnificent, eighty years of living crammed behind eyes that had been vacant an hour ago.

"You gave me back myself," she said. "Why does no one else have themselves? What did you *do*?"

"I don't fully know yet," Coral said honestly. "But I think you might."

Marta stood trembling among her broken teacups, breathing like a bellows. And slowly, horribly, understanding arrived in her face — not confusion this time but recognition, the recognition of someone seeing the shape of a prison they'd been inside too long to see.

"This isn't our country," she whispered. "Coral. Whoever you are. Listen to me. I remembered — when you opened me, everything poured through, and I caught the edge of it — Ashenmoor isn't a country. It was never a country. It's a *room*. A room somebody built inside the world, a stolen room, walled off with everything in it — the fields, the borders, the histories, us — stolen from outside and brought in here, like — like things put in a jar." Her voice cracked. "I remembered grass that grew under a sky with a sun in it. I have never seen a sun, Coral. I am eighty years old and I have never once seen the sun, and until this moment I didn't know there was anything to miss."

The kitchen was silent except for her ragged breathing. Behind Coral, he heard Tansy sit down slowly, as if her legs had given out.

"A stolen room," Sable repeated hoarsely. "Stolen by whom?"

"I don't know. I couldn't hold onto it — it slipped, it's still slipping—" Marta pressed her fists against her eyes. "But I remembered the edge of it. The seam. Where they sewed us in." She lowered her hands, and her gaze fixed on Coral with terrible clarity. "And I remembered something else. Something that was following the seam, looking for it, the way a moth looks at a lamp. You. Your voice. You've been hearing it too, haven't you? The whispers. The map in your head."

Coral's blood went cold. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because the seam sings," Marta said simply. "Everything in this place was stitched from memory — the founders' memory, whoever they were — and every stitch hums. Most people can't hear it. But conduits hear it. And the thing at the center of the seam, the thing all these walls were built to hide—"

She stopped. Outside, in the square, the flat light dimmed — not like cloud, but like a page turning, like the land briefly consulting its next thought.

"—it knows you're here," Marta finished. "It's been calling you since the stars went out. Haven't you wondered why the whispers started *then*?"

Coral had. Every night since the border fell, they had wondered, lying awake in the wagon while the crew slept, why the map-fragments had chosen that moment to wake. They had told themselves it was the wards breaking, the land destabilizing, chance.

But chance didn't whisper coordinates in your dead mother's voice.

"The Waystation," Coral said. "East of here. My head says there's a Waystation in the hills east of here, and that we have to reach it before—"

"Before it finishes forgetting itself," Marta agreed. "Go. Go quickly." She gripped Coral's hands again, hard, her restored life blazing in her face. "And Coral — take my hymns. The harvest ones. Sing them on the road. If the land is made of memory, then memory is the only weapon that works here, and I'd rather mine cut for something than fade holding nothing." A broken laugh escaped her. "Eighty years I lived in a lie and smiled. Don't let them make you smile."

They left her standing in her impossible kitchen, straightening her teacups, humming a harvest hymn under her breath in a voice that shook but did not stop.

Outside, the crew waited in the flat light, and every face wore the same expression Coral felt carved into their own bones.

"A room," Brannoc said at last. "The whole country's a room. Then what's it a room *for*?"

No one answered. But Coral felt the whispers stir in the vaults of their skull, and for the first time they did not sound like a map.

They sounded like something on the other side of a wall, pressing its ear against it, waiting — patiently, desperately, the way only a prisoner can wait — to find out whether the footsteps overhead were coming closer, or passing by.

Tansy looked east, toward the unseen Waystation, and said quietly: "We should hurry. I don't think we're the only ones who've heard it calling."
