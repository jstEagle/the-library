## 13. Tansy's Long Memory

The fire had burned down to that particular orange that only exists in the last hour before dawn, and Tansy sat apart from the others the way she always did — close enough to feel their warmth, far enough that no one would see her hands.

Her hands were wrong. She had known it for sixty years, and she had never once said it aloud.

They were an old woman's hands in every way but one: they were still changing. The knuckles swelled, the skin thinned, the veins rose like roots under paper — and yet they went on doing it, year after year, decade after decade, as if her body had missed some instruction, some final page of the letter that said *stop here*. Everyone else she had ever known arrived at a certain age and simply held there, weathered but finished, like houses whose builders had declared them complete. Tansy kept aging. She was ninety-one years old by her own careful count, and she looked it, and then she looked more than it, and no one in Vell's night markets or the river camps or the gray country beyond ever remarked on it, because no one else could see what was missing from the arithmetic.

Only the land itself, perhaps. And lately, the land was beginning to count.

She turned her palms toward the embers. In the firelight the skin glowed thin as onion skin, and beneath it — she leaned closer, though she didn't need to; she had checked a hundred times — the blue lines of her veins were growing fainter. Not faded. *Fainter*. As if something were deciding, slowly, whether she was worth drawing at all.

"Tansy." Coral's voice, low, from across the dead fire. They hadn't slept either. None of them really slept anymore, not since the ford, not since Maren's arm and the sound Coral's magic had made when it tore the wardens' line open like wet cloth. "You should rest."

"I'll rest when the ground stops forgetting how to hold things," Tansy said, and heard Isolde snort softly in her sleep-turn, and almost smiled. The girl pretended to sleep through half of everything. It was how she loved people, Tansy suspected — by listening to them without being seen to.

Coral came around the fire anyway and settled beside her, folding their long frame down with the care of someone who had learned to make themselves small. A thief's habit, even now. Especially now.

"You've been watching me," Coral said. Not accusatory. Just true.

"I've been watching everyone. It's all I do these days. Watch." Tansy poked the embers with a stick and watched the sparks climb and die. "But yes, child. I've been watching you most of all. Because you're going to ask me soon what I know about the Door, and I'd rather tell you sitting up than have you dig it out of me sideways."

Coral went very still. In the seven days since the ford, Tansy had learned that stillness was the thief's second language. When Coral stopped moving entirely, it meant something inside them was moving too fast.

"You know about the Door?"

"I know about doors." She laughed, a dry rustle. "I'm older than I look, which is saying something, because I look like a dried apple left on a windowsill. But listen. Before I tell you anything, I need you to understand what I am, because it changes what everything means. And I need Brannoc awake for it, and the twins, and your priest. This is a story that shouldn't be told twice."

* * *

They woke the camp gently, the way sailors wake each other in bad weather — no shouting, just names spoken soft and close. Sable Fen came out of his bedroll already dressed, as priests do, or as disgraced priests do, who have spent years sleeping in crypts where waking slowly could cost you your boots and sometimes your fingers. He built the fire up without being asked. Pip materialized out of the dark with an armful of dry brush, having clearly been awake the whole time and eavesdropping on principle. Maren sat propped against a stone with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest, gray-faced but present, and Isolde sat pressed against her sister's good side so closely the two of them might have been one person wearing two coats.

Tansy waited until the fire was strong and every face was lit, because this part mattered. Some truths you can tell in the dark. Others need witnesses.

"I wasn't born here," she said.

Pip blinked. "Course you weren't, nobody's born in ditches—"

"Here," Tansy said, and gestured upward, past the starless sky, past the smoke, at the whole vast unbeing of Ashenmoor. "In this country. In this *memory*."

The word landed the way she had known it would — like a stone dropped into still water, rings spreading through every face. Sable's lips parted. Coral's eyes went sharp and hungry, the way they went when a lock presented itself.

"Sixty-one years ago," Tansy said, "I was a girl of thirty living in a port city called Merrowgate, on a coast you've never heard of, because it isn't here. It's out there. Beyond whatever edge this place has. My father fished herring. My mother dyed wool, and our whole street smelled of mordant and salt, and I had a sweetheart named Aldous who played the fiddle badly and kissed worse." Her voice caught on his name the way it always did, a small hitch in an old wheel. "I remember the sea. Do any of you? Truly? Not rivers, not the drowned canals under Vell. The *sea*. The smell of it at low tide, green and rotten and alive. The way the horizon doesn't end, it just gives up pretending."

No one spoke. Brannoc, the big ferryman, had gone pale in the firelight.

"Merrowgate is real," Tansy said. "It exists right now, tonight, under stars that are burning. And I stood on its docks one morning sixty-one years ago, waiting for a ship, and the world *folded*. That's the only way I can say it. No light, no thunder. One moment the harbor gulls, the next—" she snapped her fingers, and the crack of it made Pip flinch, "—gray. Static. Like the fields outside Vell's walls, like everything your Coral has seen beyond the border. And when the world unfolded again, I was standing in a wheat field I didn't know, under a sky I didn't know, and the sun was in the wrong quarter, and a farmer was staring at me and asking, kindly enough, if I'd lost my way. And I said, where am I? And he said, *Ashenmoor*, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world. As if it had always been the world."

Sable made a small sound. Tansy looked at him — the runaway priest, the keeper of forbidden hymns — and saw that he had understood before anyone else. Of course he had. Faith teaches you to recognize revelation, even when it arrives as an old woman by a fire.

"The founders," he said quietly. "They stole people too. Not just the land."

"They stole whoever was standing where the scissors fell," Tansy said. "I don't know how many. Hundreds, thousands, over however long it took them to cut this shape out of the world. There will be others like me scattered through Ashenmoor like burrs in a fleece. Most of them are probably dead now. Or worse — most of them probably *forgot*, eventually, because memory needs neighbors. You remember a thing by remembering it beside other things. Take the sea away from a woman and give her sixty years of wheat fields, and the sea goes thin. Goes quiet." She spread her hands. "Mine hasn't gone quiet. And that, children, brings us to the part you're not going to like."

Maren shifted against her sister. "Your hands," she said suddenly. "I've seen them. At the ford, when you bound my arm — your veins. They barely showed."

Tansy inclined her head, pleased despite herself. The twins missed nothing; it was why she liked them best after the priest. "Sharp girl. Yes. Here is what I am, and I want you to hear it plainly: I am a stranger inside a story. Every person born in Ashenmoor was born *of* it — woven from the god's dreaming, sustained by it, held in its mind like a tune held in a singer's throat. Their bodies obey the rules of the memory. They age the way the memory says people age, they sicken the way the memory allows, they die on schedule. But I was cut in from outside. I am foreign matter. A splinter in the god's dream." She lifted her hand toward the firelight and let them all see the faintness of her, the transparency gathering at her fingertips like frost forming in reverse. "And a dream, given time, works splinters out."

"That's why you age wrong," Coral breathed. "That's why nobody notices. You're not being kept alive. You're being—"

"*Overlooked*," Tansy agreed. "For sixty-one years, the land has been too busy dreaming itself to notice one extra old woman. I aged past the memory's pattern, and the pattern had no answer for me, so it shrugged and moved on. But the dream is failing now. The stars went out. The borders came down. The god is waking, or drowning, whichever image frightens you less — and a drowning man notices the water in his lungs." She closed her hand. "I'm fading. I've felt it for months, but since the night the stars went out, I've felt it daily. Little erasures. Yesterday I couldn't recall the name of my mother's third loom-cat until nearly noon. Today I had to fight for Aldous's face. It comes back when I fight. But the fights are getting harder, and I can feel the country leaning on me now, gentle as snowfall, saying *lie down, lie down, be part of the field.*"

Isolde had taken Maren's hand. Pip had crawled, without seeming to notice he'd done it, close enough to touch Tany's knee, and sat gripping her skirt with both fists like a small anchor.

"So we get you out," Brannoc rumbled. "Through the Door, same as us. You come with us to Merrowgate, old mother, and you drink something with rum in it and see the sea again—"

"And that," Tansy said, "is the second thing I have to tell you, and it's worse." She looked at Coral, because this part belonged to them. "You've been hearing the whispers. The map-fragments. Coordinates. Don't deny it — you talk in your sleep, child, and what you say isn't words, it's *places*. You're a Conduit. One of the last. Perhaps the last. And the Door at the heart of this country — the door back into the real — it isn't a door like a door in a wall. It's a wound in the memory, and wounds don't open for keys. They open for pressure. For a voice singing at exactly the frequency the founders used to cut the country out in the first place. The Choir-magic. The full song, sung by someone who can carry it." She let that sit. "There may be three people alive who could attempt it, and two of them are wardens who'd rather burn the door shut. So it's you, Coral Underhill. It was always going to be you. The whispers aren't leading you to the Door by accident. The Door has been calling its locksmith home."

Coral's face in the firelight was a study in controlled catastrophe. Tansy watched them take it in — the weight settling onto shoulders already bent from carrying a thousand stolen lives — and felt the old ache, the exile's ache, the ache of watching young people be handed terrible things by a world that never asked permission first.

"But you know more," Sable said softly. He had gone very still, the way he did in his crypts, listening for something beneath. "You didn't bring us all out here to tell us you're dying and Coral's chosen. There's a price. There's always a price. What does opening it cost?"

Tansy smiled, and hated herself for smiling, because she had practiced that smile for weeks and it still worked, damn it, it still softened them.

"It costs what all great songs cost," she said. "A voice. The Conduit must sing themselves into the wound — pour their own memory through it to hold it open while the people pass. Not all at once. Enough. Enough that what walks out the far side won't be quite what walked in." She reached out and took Coral's hand, those clever cold thieving fingers, and squeezed. "I don't know the exact arithmetic. But I know the founders paid it once themselves, to cut the country free. And I know that whatever you've hoarded in that head of yours, child, you'll be spending it. All of it. Down to the last bright coin."

The silence afterward was enormous. Pip broke it, because Pip always broke silences, it was his nature, like ice breaking on a spring river.

"Then we find another way," he said fiercely. "We find another Conduit. We find—"

"There is no another way," Tansy said gently. "But there is a *this* way done well, and done late, and done with everyone you love standing behind you. Which is more than most of us get." She looked around the circle of faces — the ferryman, the twins, the thief-priest, the wild child, the conduit — and something in her old foreign heart turned over. Sixty-one years a stranger, and here, in a dying memory, she had finally assembled a family. The land had a cruel sense of humor.

"Now sleep," she said, rising stiffly, her joints announcing themselves like a ship in rough water. "All of you. We walk north tomorrow, toward the Waystations, and I'll teach you what I remember of the real world as we go — the stars' true names, the taste of bread that wasn't dreamed. Someone in this company ought to carry proof of what's out there waiting." She paused at the edge of the firelight and added, quieter, almost to herself: "While I still remember it."

She had turned away when Sable's voice stopped her.

"Tansy. Your sweetheart. Aldous." His eyes were kind and terrible. "If the Door opens — he'd be sixty years older. Would you go to him?"

Tansy stood a long moment with her back to the fire, feeling the country lean on her like a patient creditor.

"He'd be ninety-one," she said at last. "Same as me, near enough. Maybe we'd both be too old to kiss well, and that would be a mercy." She smiled, and this time it wasn't practiced. "But I'd like to stand on a dock again and smell the herring. Even once. Even for an hour."

She walked into the dark to check the picket line, because checking things was what she did instead of weeping, and it was there — crouched by the gray mare, running her translucent fingers through its mane — that she saw it.

Far off to the northeast, beyond the ruined skyline, beyond the static fields: a light. Not firelight. Not lightning. A column of white radiance rising off the land like steam off a kettle, tall and straight and utterly silent, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable on the wind, the sound of many voices singing in unison.

Wardens' hymns. Halloway's wardens. Marching.

Tansy closed her fist around the fading pulse in her wrist and went to wake the camp.
