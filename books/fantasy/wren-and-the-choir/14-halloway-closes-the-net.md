## 14. Halloway Closes the Net

The rain in Vell had a taste to it now, and Warden Halloway had learned to name it. Copper and ash and something older underneath — the smell of a wound that had stopped bleeding because the body around it was forgetting how blood worked. She stood at the window of the warden-house on Coriander Street with her third cup of chicory going cold in her hand, watching the gray static crawl along the rooftops of the lower city like fog that had opinions. It moved wrong. Fog obeyed wind. This obeyed nothing she could see, and it ate the edges of things — a chimney pot here, a weathervane there, a whole stretch of gutter dissolving into soft white noise as though some enormous hand were rubbing the city out of existence one detail at a time.

Seven days since the stars went out. Six since the border wards shattered like sugar glass. Five since the first refugees came through the Salt Gate with their eyes full of holes where their childhoods used to be.

She had read the reports until her vision swam. Village after village, each one intact — houses standing, wells drawing, bread rising in ovens — and not one soul in any of them able to say their own name. A woman in Millbrook had been found milking a cow that no longer existed, her hands passing through its flank, smiling. A man in Harrow's End had set his table for six every night for a family he could not describe, could not count, could not remember whether they had ever lived there at all. The static took the outlying districts first, then the roads between them, and now it stood at the gates of Vell itself like a patient creditor.

And somewhere in this drowning city was the person who could hear the Choir.

Halloway knew it the way you know weather coming in your knees. The Conservatory had trained eleven conduits in the last generation before the Rites were sealed away. Ten were accounted for — dead, scattered, or so thoroughly ward-marked they couldn't hum a lullaby without tripping every alarm from here to the coast. The eleventh had fled seven years ago, on the night of the Refusal, and had vanished into Vell's ten thousand alleys like a drop of water returning to the sea.

Coral Underhill.

She said the name aloud sometimes when she was alone, just to feel the shape of it in her mouth. She had taught that child to breathe in four-count time. She had held their hands over the resonance bowls and felt the first tremor of what lived inside them — something vast, something old, something that made the hair on her arms stand up even through the gloves. She had loved them. Not the way the ballads meant it, or maybe exactly the way the ballads meant it; she had never decided, and the not-deciding had become its own kind of wound, scabbed over with duty, itching in damp weather.

And when the order came down — *the fugitive is to be found, questioned, and Gleaned* — she had signed it. Her own signature, in her own ink, on the warrant with Coral's description in her own handwriting: *height slight, voice low for their years, left-handed, scar on the right palm from a resonance bowl cracked in training.* She remembered writing it. She remembered the pen scratching. She remembered thinking, with a clarity that still shamed her: *if I do not write this neatly, someone less careful will write it crooked.*

That was the arithmetic she had lived by ever since. Love, entered into evidence. Duty, executed precisely. And the small cold room in her chest where the two things touched, kept locked, kept dark, inspected once a year like a boiler license.

* * *

Mother Quill's establishment sat above a chandlery on Rope Walk, behind a door painted the green of deep water. Halloway had known about it for years, the way a fisherman knows about a sandbar — useful information, never acted upon, because Quill paid her tithe to the wardens' benevolent fund and kept worse predators off her streets than herself. There had been an understanding. Cities ran on understandings the way clocks ran on springs, and Halloway had spent twenty years keeping Vell's clock wound.

But the stars were out now, and understandings were among the first casualties.

Her informants had brought her the thread three nights ago: Quill had bought something impossible. A memory, sold loose in the night market, containing a glimpse of a Door — a Door beneath the old Conservatory, a Door out of the world. Quill had seen it. Quill had begun asking questions in the wrong quarters, hiring the wrong people, and most damning of all, Quill had stopped paying her tithe, which in Quill's private language meant she believed she would soon have no further need of wardens.

Halloway took her with eight officers, before dawn, through the chandlery's cellar, up through a trapdoor that smelled of tallow and river rot. No battering ram. No shouting. She had learned long ago that the loudest arrests were the ones where someone died, and dead merchants told no stories.

They found Mother Quill at her breakfast table, a small silver fork suspended halfway to her mouth, a dish of preserved plums gleaming like garnets before her. She did not startle. That was the thing about Quill — she had the composure of a woman who had already survived her own death several times and found it negotiable.

"Warden." She set the fork down with a small precise click. "You'll take tea."

"I'll take the memory," Halloway said. "The one with the Door in it."

Quill smiled. It was a good smile, warm as a banked fire, and entirely false, and both of them knew it, and neither of them minded. "Ah. You've been listening at keyholes again. Sit down, Warden. You look like a woman who hasn't slept in a week, and I don't sell to the sleepless — bad trade. They see what they want to see."

"Mother Quill, you are under arrest for trafficking in stolen memory, sedition, and—" Halloway paused, because the next charge on her list was *conspiracy against the continued existence of Ashenmoor*, and saying it aloud made her feel like a madwoman. "—and obstruction. Your establishment is seized. Your ledgers are forfeit. Your people will be questioned gently if you cooperate and thoroughly if you don't."

Quill poured the tea anyway. Steam rose between them, smelling of cardamom and smoke. "Obstruction," she repeated, tasting the word. "Of what, precisely? Of the country ending? Warden, look out your own window. The world outside your walls has gone the color of unwritten paper. Half my suppliers have forgotten which side of a bargain they stood on. And you come to me talking about *obstruction*." She lifted her cup. "Sit. Drink. Then arrest me. I'm told the last cups of anything taste sweeter."

Halloway should have cuffed her then. Twenty years of procedure said cuff her, search her, march her out. Instead she sat, because her officers were searching the premises anyway, and because there was a question burning a hole through her discipline, and because — she admitted it only to herself, in the locked dark room — she wanted to know if the Door was real, and if it was real, whether it was a way to save this country instead of end it.

"Tell me about the Door," she said.

Quill drank her tea like a woman being paid by the hour. "A seller came to me. Small, quick, nervous — a conduit, or near enough. Sold me a charm. In it, I saw a chamber under the Conservatory. Stars hanging in it like lanterns in a chapel. And a Door." Her eyes, for the first time, lost their merchant's polish and showed the raw thing underneath: hunger, and fear, and hunger wearing fear's coat. "Not a door in a wall, Warden. A door in the *world*. Standing open a crack, with light coming through that wasn't starlight, wasn't lamplight — light like morning. Real morning. The kind we haven't seen since the sky went out."

"You believe that's salvation."

"I believe it's *leverage*," Quill said, "which in my experience is the same thing wearing better clothes. Everyone in this city will want through that Door. The rich will pay. The desperate will fight. Whoever holds the key holds Ashenmoor's last real asset." She set down her cup and folded her hands, plump and ringed and steady. "But you didn't come for leverage. You came because you're afraid. So let me ask you, Warden — what does the Choir say happens if that Door opens all the way?"

And there it was. The question Halloway had carried for five days like a stone in her boot.

Because she had gone into the Conservatory archives herself, alone, at night, with a lamp and her old credentials that still opened doors doors shouldn't open anymore. She had read the founders' sealed correspondence. She had read the Rite protocols, the containment doctrine, the pages written in a hand that shook. *The vessel sleeps. The vessel must continue to sleep. If the vessel wakes fully, if the boundary is breached from within, the release of its nature will unmake everything contained with it.* Everything contained. Every field, every village, every forgetful cow-milking woman in Millbrook. Every soul born inside the stolen country, which was to say — everyone Halloway had sworn to protect, everyone she had ever loved, everyone including—

Including a thief with a scar on the right palm who was, right now, somewhere in this unraveling city, almost certainly walking toward that Door with the terrible innocence of someone who thought they could fix things.

"The Choir says the Door kills us all," Halloway said quietly. "Every soul in Ashenmoor. That's what opening it means. That's what your morning light is, Mother Quill. The last thing a dying god sees on its way out — and we go with it."

Quill's smile flickered — genuinely flickered, for half a heartbeat — before the merchant rebuilt it brick by brick. "Old stories," she said. "Written by the people who built the cage. Cagemen always say the bird's wings are dangerous."

"The cage is the only reason the bird hasn't burned down the house."

"And if the house is already burning?" Quill spread her hands toward the window, toward the gray. "Warden. Be honest with yourself, just once, in this nice kitchen. The country is *unraveling*. Whatever your founders stole, they stole badly, and the theft is failing. The Door isn't the disease. The Door might be the only exit sign in a building that's coming down. And you want to nail it shut because a book told you to."

Halloway stood. Her chair scraped, loud in the quiet. "Here is what happens now," she said. "You will tell my scribes everything you know about the seller. Description, price, meeting place, every word exchanged. You will give me your ledgers, your contacts, your little empire entire. And in exchange, when this is over — whatever 'over' means — I will remember that you cooperated."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you go to the Gleaning blocks with the other traffickers, and in a week you won't remember your own name, and neither will I, and none of it will have mattered." Halloway leaned down, close enough to smell the cardamom on the woman's breath, and heard her own voice come out flat and cold and very tired. "I don't have time to be your friend, Quill. The clock is running. Can't you feel it? The country is forgetting itself faster every night. Whatever's happening under that Conservatory, it's happening *now*."

For a long moment Mother Quill looked up at her, and Halloway saw her doing the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Halloway did, the same everyone did now, weighing survival against comfort against the odds of anyone enforcing anything ever again. Then Quill sighed, a great theatrical sigh, and reached beneath the table.

Halloway's hand went to her baton.

Quill drew out not a weapon but a ledger — small, black, bound in eel-skin — and laid it on the table between them like a queen surrendering a city. "The seller," she said. "Small. Young. Voice like a boy's but not a boy. Left-handed." She watched Halloway's face as she said it, and whatever she saw there made her own expression change — soften, almost, into something like pity. "You know them."

"I know them."

"Then you know they weren't selling me the Door, Warden. They were selling me a *mistake*. They showed me that glimpse by accident — I saw it in the edges of something else they were peddling. They've been trying to stay hidden for seven years, same as anyone with sense." Quill tilted her head. "Which means they're not marching an army to the Door. Which means whoever opens it will be one frightened person, alone, trying to do something kind. And you're going to stop them." She picked up her cold tea and saluted Halloway with it, mockingly, mournfully. "Duty's a hard mistress. I prefer coin. Coin doesn't ask you to break things you love."

"They aren't mine to love," Halloway said. "They broke the law."

"Mm," said Mother Quill. "So did the founders, from what I hear. Funny how the law only breaks in one direction."

* * *

They marched her out through the chandlery at first light, wrists bound in warden-cord, flanked by four officers, past the early fish-carts and the shuttered stalls. Quill went gracefully, chin up, nodding to acquaintances in the crowd like a magistrate on progress. Only at the corner of Rope Walk did she pause, look back at Halloway, and call out — loudly enough for the whole street, loudly enough for the report that would inevitably reach every ear in Vell:

"They went east, Warden! Toward the old shrine road! Ask your ghosts why!"

The crowd murmured. The officers stiffened. Halloway kept her face still as pond ice, but inside, the locked dark room blew its door wide open.

East. The shrine road. The first Waystation lay along the shrine road — she knew it, she had picnicked there as a cadet, before the wards, before everything, when the land beyond the walls had still been green and starred and *real*, or as real as anything was here. If Coral was moving east, they weren't hiding anymore. They were traveling. Gathering people, perhaps. Heading for the Conservatory, and the Door beneath it, with the certainty of someone following a map only they could read.

She went back to the warden-house and gave the orders that needed giving. Roadblocks on the shrine road. Riders to every garrison east of the walls. The Gleaning blocks on standby — and she hated herself for that order, hated the sound of it, *standby*, as though souls were garrison stores. She wrote dispatches until her hand cramped, and every dispatch was precise, and every precision was a small betrayal of the girl she'd once taught to breathe in fours.

Toward evening, her lieutenant brought her the day's refugee interviews, and one line in the transcript stopped her breath.

*A ferryman named Brannoc, a priest answering to Sable Fen, twin sisters, an old woman, and a child. Seen crossing the mill ford at dusk, heading east. Traveling with a guide of slight build, low voice, left-handed.*

Left-handed.

Halloway sat for a long time in the lamplight with the transcript in her lap. Outside, the static had reached the far shore of the river; she could see it from her window, a pale wall where the farmlands used to be, and it was closer tonight than it had been last night. Closer every night. Eating the world in patient mouthfuls while two armies crawled toward the same door — hers to nail it shut, theirs to open it, and behind them all, the vast sleeping thing whose nightmare was coming true.

She thought about Coral at eleven years old, standing in the resonance hall, singing a note that made the bowls weep. She thought about the order she would give when she caught up with them. *Questioned and Gleaned.* She thought about the alternative — letting them reach the Door, letting them open it, betting every life in the country on the mercy of a frightened child-god and a thief's good intentions.

She could not. However much she loved them — however much the locked room ached — she could not bet ten million souls on love. Love was not a doctrine. Love had no chain of command. When love was wrong, it was wrong catastrophically, and no one ever wrote the ballad about the warden who trusted too much.

When duty was wrong, at least it was wrong in formation.

She rose, buckled on her sword, and called for her horse.

"Ride through the night," she told her lieutenant. "We make the shrine road by dawn. And Lieutenant — no one takes the conduit alive but me. That's not sentiment. That's jurisdiction."

It was not jurisdiction. She knew it was not jurisdiction even as she said it, and the knowing changed nothing, and she rode east into the starless dark with her conviction buttoned to her throat like armor, plate over plate over plate, and beneath all of it, pressed against her heart where no one would ever find it, the small worn shape of a resonance bowl shard she had kept for seven years — the one that had cracked in a child's palm, the one she had never turned in as evidence, the one that sang, very faintly, whenever Coral Underhill was near.

Tonight, riding east, she could swear it was humming.
