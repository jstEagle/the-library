## 15. The First Waystation

The whisper in Coral's skull changed key on the third morning out of the ford.

They had learned to read it the way sailors read weather — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper rain at the back of the throat. Most days it was static, the sound of a country forgetting itself in real time: *north by northwest, past the orchards that are no longer orchards, past the river with no name.* But this morning it resolved into something almost melodic, three notes rising, and Coral sat up in the bedroll before dawn with their heart going hard against their ribs.

"What is it?" Sable was already awake. He was always already awake; Coral suspected he slept the way cats did, one ear open for the world's next cruelty.

"It wants me to go somewhere."

"The whispers?"

"Not whispers anymore." Coral pressed two fingers to their temple, as if they could hold the melody still long enough to look at it. "It's — singing. Or trying to. Like someone humming through a wall."

Maren, propped against the wagon wheel with her bandaged arm cradled like something breakable, watched them with the flat gray patience of a woman who had navigated by dead stars and trusted nothing else since. "Waystations," she said.

Isolde looked up from the fire she was coaxing out of damp peat. "What about them?"

"Before the stars went out. Before all this." Maren's good hand sketched a shape in the air, a tower, a spire. "There were shrines along every pilgrim road in Ashenmoor. The Choir built them. Places where the land was — I don't know the word. *Anchored.* Travelers said you could feel it. That the grass was greener for a mile around them." Her eyes came to Coral, and there was no accusation in them, only arithmetic. "You're hearing one."

Coral realized they were standing. They didn't remember getting up.

*Three notes rising,* the land sang inside them, patient as water wearing stone. *Come and see what you were.*

* * *

They found it just past noon, where the road gave out mid-stride into gray sketch-lines and then, absurdly, picked itself up again on the far side as if embarrassed to have been caught unfinished.

The waystation stood on a low hill that should not have existed — everything else on the horizon had gone soft and provisional, hills becoming suggestions of hills, but this one held its shape the way a held breath holds a body. It was a small thing, smaller than Coral had imagined: a round shrine of pale stone no taller than Brannoc, its roof fallen in, its doorway a mouth full of dark. Lichen grew on it in whorls that might have been carved or might have grown. Around its base, in a circle perhaps thirty feet wide, the grass was green.

Not gray. Not sketch-grass, the half-drawn stuff that bent without wind and had no smell. Green. Wet-looking, deep, the green of a memory of summer so vivid it hurt.

And above it — Coral's breath left them in a single thin sound — above the ruined roof, where the sky had been starless and wrong for weeks, there were stars.

Only a handful. A scatter, faint as salt grains spilled on black cloth, burning in a circle directly over the shrine like candles lit for someone not yet home.

"Saints," Brannoc breathed. The big ferryman had taken off his hat without seeming to notice he'd done it. "It's — the sky's *broken* everywhere else, how is it—"

"Because somebody's still keeping this bit," Tansy said quietly.

The old woman had not spoken much since the ford, since Maren's arm, since the night the wardens' horns had chased them across a river of static. She stood at the edge of the green circle now, and she would not step inside it. Coral watched her stand there, hands folded, face turned up to those few impossible stars, and saw something in her expression they had no name for. It looked like a woman looking through a window at a country she'd been told no longer existed.

"You know what this is," Coral said.

"I know what it's *like*." Tansy's voice was dry as seed-husks. "Places like this were common, once. Where I come from." She didn't say where that was. She never said. "We called them god-stones. Places where something vast had leaned against the world, and the world remembered the weight." She looked at Coral then, and her old eyes were terribly kind. "Go on, then. You've been walking toward this since Vell. We've all been pretending we didn't notice."

* * *

The inside of the shrine was cold and smelled of dust and, beneath the dust, faintly of incense — myrrh and something resinous Coral couldn't name, a smell that reached into them and found a door they'd thought sealed seven years ago.

The Conservatory had smelled like this.

Their hands shook. They made themselves look at the walls instead, at the faded frescoes: robed figures in procession, their faces worn to smooth blanks, all of them facing inward toward a central figure that had been painted larger than the others and gilded, though only flakes of the gold remained. Below it, running in a band around the whole circumference of the wall, was notation.

Coral knew it the way you know your own handwriting. Knew it in their spine, in the scar-tissue of their throat. The old script of the Choir — not words, never words, the founders had believed words were too small — but pitch and duration and dynamic rendered in a grammar of curves and dots and lines that broke like birdtracks across the stone.

*Three notes rising.* There. Just above the blank-faced golden figure. The same three notes that had been humming behind Coral's eyes since dawn, except here they were written down, fixed, *composed*, part of something longer that wound on around the wall and out of reach.

"They used to sing these," Sable said from the doorway.

Coral hadn't heard him come in. He stood with the last of the daylight behind him, and his face was unreadable, which meant it was very readable indeed.

"At the seminary," he went on, when Coral said nothing. "First-year acolytes. Before they taught us anything dangerous, they brought us to a waystation — mine was outside Callowmere, a ruin even then — and they made us sing the anchor-hymn. All of us together, badly, cracking on the high parts." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "They told us it was just tradition. A nice outing. Fresh air for boys who'd been breathing incense all winter." He stepped fully inside, and his boots scuffed on fallen roof-slate. "They never told us the hymn was load-bearing."

"Would it have mattered?"

Sable considered this with the terrible honesty that was, Coral was learning, both the best and worst thing about him. "No," he said. "I'd have sung anyway. I liked the way it felt." He glanced sideways at Coral. "You know the feeling."

It wasn't a question. Coral looked at the notation winding around the walls and felt the feeling now, rising in them like water in a well: the sense of being *played*, of being an instrument that some vast patient thing had set down and not yet picked back up.

"If I sing it," Coral said, "the land remembers itself. Here. Maybe only here, maybe only for a while. And if Halloway's wardens are within fifty miles, they'll feel it like a bonfire in a dark field."

"Yes," Sable said.

"And if I'm wrong about what happens—"

"Then we'll be wrong together, which has been our general policy so far." He moved closer. Outside, Isolde's voice rose — she and Brannoc were arguing about whether to bring the wagon within the circle, whether the green would hold a horse, whether anything anywhere could be trusted to hold anything ever again. Ordinary voices. The sound of people who had decided, without discussing it, to give Coral room.

"Sable," Coral said. "Why aren't you telling me not to? Everyone else would. Pip would hide behind the wagon and give me that look. Maren would list the risks in order of likelihood. Even Tansy—" 

"Tansy wants you to do it more than anyone. Haven't you seen her face?" He was close enough now that Coral could see the gray in his stubble, the chapped windburn on his cheeks, the exhaustion he wore like everyone in this unraveling country wore exhaustion — as a garment that had stopped being removable. "Coral. We have been walking through a country that is being *erased*. I have watched a forest turn into a rumor. I held Maren down while Brannoc tied off her arm because the static got into the wound and wouldn't stop *unmaking* it." His voice stayed level. His hands didn't. "Every day I ask myself what any of it is for — the walking, the rationing, the songs I still catch myself humming when I think no one can hear. And today there is grass that smells like grass and stars that burn like stars, and you're asking me why I won't tell you not to open the window?" He exhaled. "Sing the hymn, Coral. Let us see, once, what we're trying to save."

* * *

Later, Coral would try to describe it to themselves and fail, the way you fail to describe a color to someone born blind, but they tried anyway, alone, in the dark, because it seemed important not to let it blur.

They stood in the center of the shrine, under the hole in the roof, under those handful of borrowed stars. The crew gathered at the doorway — all of them, even Pip, who had been sullen and strange since the ford and who looked now, for the first time in days, simply young. Maren leaned against the doorframe with her bandaged arm held close. Tansy stayed outside the circle. She had taken off her hat too.

The first note was easy. It lived in Coral's chest already, had lived there for seven years, coiled and starving. The second note cost more — it wanted the voice they'd had at fifteen, before the Rite, before the running, before they had taught themselves to sing small — and Coral paid it. The third note rose, and rose, and somewhere in the rising Coral stopped choosing the sound and the sound began choosing them, and the hymn came up out of the stone and out of the dead composers and out of the god-shaped silence underneath everything, and Coral opened their mouth and let it through.

The grass heard first.

It happened outward from the shrine in a ring, like a stone dropped in a pond made of green: the grass deepened, thickened, lay down and rose again in an actual wind that had actual smell in it — loam, clover, cut hay, rain that hadn't fallen yet. The hill remembered its height. Beyond it, the sketch-forest at the road's edge filled in — Coral watched it happen, watched gray suggestion become trunk, become bark, become canopy going gold and rust at the edges because it was autumn, it was autumn and the trees knew it. A bird started up somewhere, some small brown thing with an ordinary song, and Pip made a sound like he'd been struck.

The sky went last, and best. The handful of stars multiplied — ten, fifty, hundreds, thousands, wheeling slowly into place overhead in constellations Coral knew by other names, the names the Choir had given them, the Wain and the Weaver and the Drowned Crown — and between them spread the deep blue-black of a true night sky, unbroken, whole, a ceiling instead of a wound.

And warmth. Warmth poured down out of that remembered sky like something forgiven.

Coral sang until the hymn ended itself, the last note going up and out through the broken roof like a bird released, and then they stood in the ringing quiet with tears on their face they had no memory of starting, and looked at what a country could be when it remembered.

Brannoc was sitting down. He didn't seem to know when he'd sat. Isolde had her hand over her mouth. Maren was crying without any change of expression at all, tears simply falling off her chin, and beside her Tansy had finally, finally stepped inside the circle, and stood in the green grass with her face turned up to the whole restored sky, drinking it.

"Well," Tansy said, when she could speak. "That's what it looks like. Write it down. Someone ought to write it down."

* * *

They camped inside the circle, which felt like blasphemy and also like the only possible thing to do. Isolde cooked properly for the first time in weeks — actual browning, actual salt — and Pip fell asleep mid-bite with his head on Brannoc's knee, and no one moved him.

Coral sat apart, at the base of the shrine, doing the arithmetic that hope demanded. The hymn had lasted perhaps ten minutes. The green had begun fading at the edges even as the last note died; the stars overhead were already thinning, going out in ones and twos like a congregation filing home. By morning, Isolde predicted, the hill would be gray again. Ten minutes of remembering, bought with everything Coral had.

A shadow fell across the grass. Sable, with two cups of Isolde's terrible tea.

He handed one over and sat, not touching, shoulder a hand-width from Coral's shoulder. For a while they drank in silence and watched the stars go out gently, one by one, and it was — Coral understood this with a strange clarity — the gentlest thing that had happened to either of them in years.

"You held the last note too long," Sable said.

"It didn't want to end."

"No. It never does." He turned the cup in his hands. Firelight moved on his face. "I testified against you, at the tribunal after you ran. Did you know that?"

Coral went very still. "I guessed."

"You didn't answer the summons. That's what I told them. That the fugitive Underhill had refused the Rite *and* ignored the summons, that they showed no remorse, that they were — I said the word *hollow*, Coral. I stood in front of the assembled Wardens and I described you as hollow, because I thought if I made you empty enough they wouldn't bother hunting you." His laugh was short and awful. "Seven years I've told myself that. That I lied you safe. And then the Gleaning started, and I understood what I'd actually done. I'd made you a story they could punish without limit. Hollow things don't suffer. Hollow things can be spent."

"Sable—"

"I'm not finished." But his voice cracked on it, and he pressed the heel of his hand briefly against his eye. "When I saw you in the crypt — heard you, before I saw you — the resonance of your voice in that crowd, I knew you in a way I have never known another living person. And I've been afraid, since we left Vell, that everything I feel building in me isn't love. It's penance. It's guilt wearing love's coat, and you deserve better than a man who can't tell the difference, and I am *telling you* this because you nearly burned yourself out tonight for ten minutes of grass, and I need you to know that whatever I am to you, I am not worth that."

The stars overhead had thinned to a dozen. The wind coming down off the remembered hill was turning cold.

Coral set down their cup. They reached over and took Sable's hand — the first time, either of them counting, that they had touched on purpose — and turned it palm-up, and looked at it, the ink stains and the rope callus and the old burn scar from a censer chain.

"When I sing," Coral said, "I can feel everything the song touches. It's not a metaphor. Tonight I felt the hill, and the trees, and Tansy standing in the grass like she was drinking light, and Pip making that sound." Their thumb moved over Sable's scarred palm. "And I felt you. At the door. Holding still the way you hold still in the crypt when you think no one can hear you pray. And whatever coat it's wearing — guilt, penance, fear — underneath it, it rang true. It rang like the hymn." They looked up at him. "I can't tell the difference either. Maybe there isn't one. Maybe love is just the debt you keep paying because you want to."

Sable made a sound that was half laugh and half something else entirely, and closed his hand around theirs, careful, careful, like holding a charm he was afraid of waking.

Above the shrine, the last star went out.

Neither of them moved. The cold crept in, and the gray began its slow reclamation at the edge of the circle, and Coral sat with Sable Fen's warm hand around theirs and thought: *ten minutes. All this, for ten minutes.* And thought, underneath, in the place where the land whispered its coordinates: *but it remembered. It can be reminded. Which means it can be saved.*

The whisper stirred, and shifted, and gave them three new notes.

These did not rise. These descended — slow, minor, deliberate — like footsteps going down a stair into the dark, and at the bottom of them Coral felt, for one vertiginous instant, something vast turning over in its sleep.

Pip, asleep by the fire, opened his eyes and did not tell anyone what he knew.
