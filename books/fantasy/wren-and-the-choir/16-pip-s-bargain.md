## 16. Pip's Bargain

Pip had three rules for ghost-markets, and he'd learned all of them the hard way.

One: never let them see your hands shake. Two: never say what you want out loud, because wanting was a coin and ghosts could bite it straight out of your mouth. Three — and this one he'd made up himself, in the drowned cellars of Vell where he'd first traded a stolen fog to a dead man with no face — never trade anything you loved.

He looked down at the storm in his pocket and broke rule three on purpose.

* * *

The Waystation had gone quiet an hour past midnight. Above them the shrine's broken dome let the starless dark pour through like water through a cracked cup, and the land outside lay gray and unfinished to every horizon. Pip couldn't sleep. He'd tried counting his bottles instead of sheep, and that was worse, because counting them meant thinking about how few there were left, and how each one held weather he'd caught with his bare hands and his teeth and six years of being small enough and quick enough to survive.

Forty-one bottles when they'd left Vell. Thirty-three now. He'd spent two buying them passage through a village of people who smiled like eggs, one bribing a ferry-ghost at the static-ford, and three — this was the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone — three just *giving away*, because Tansy had looked cold, and a bottled hearth-fire's worth of summer wind, uncorked under a blanket, was as good as a coat if you were ninety years old and fading at the edges.

Tansy slept now by the shrine's eastern wall, wrapped in Isolde's spare cloak, breathing so softly Pip had checked twice to make sure she was still there at all. She was grayer than she'd been a week ago. Not old-gray. *Un*-gray. Like someone was rubbing her out with an eraser, slow, from the fingertips inward.

That was why he was going to break rule three.

Because everyone on this crew had a job. Brannoc had his arms and his boat-knowledge and his way of humming that made frightened people calm down. The twins had their dead-star charts and their bickering, which was its own kind of navigation. Sable had his hymns, which could hold back the unraveling for a few minutes at a stretch, and Tansy had her impossible memory of a real world, and Coral —

Coral had the whole song inside them. Coral was the reason any of this worked. When Coral sang even a scrap of the Choir, the land remembered itself: grass, warmth, a sky full of stars like the ones in the bottle Pip had caught off a rooftop in Vell the night everything ended. Pip had watched it happen at the Waystation earlier that evening. He'd stood in the doorway with his fists full of useless glass and felt something he didn't have a name for yet, except that it tasted like the winter he'd spent alone under the tanner's stairs, watching other children get fed.

Useless. That was the name. It tasted like that.

So. The ghost-market.

Isolde had told him about it in her flat twin-voice, half warning, half dare: *There's a market comes to the unfinished places, little thief. Where the land's rubbed thin, things leak through. Dead traders, mostly, or things that were never properly alive. They deal in information because it's the only thing they can carry. Don't go. Or go, but don't be stupid.*

Pip was many things, but stupid wasn't one of them, whatever Maren said.

He eased out from under the shrine's sagging eave, barefoot because boots made noise and he'd stopped trusting shoes years ago, and slipped into the gray.

* * *

The land outside the Waystation's glow was wrong in ways Pip was learning to read like weather.

Roads ended mid-stride, just stopping, as if the walker had been interrupted by a thought too big to finish. A forest stood to the east that was only the *idea* of a forest — trunks sketched in pencil-gray, leaves suggested rather than grown, and when the wind moved through it, it made no sound at all. A river ran backward nearby, quietly, apologetically, like it had forgotten which way rivers went.

The market gathered where the forgetting was thickest.

Pip smelled it before he saw it: beeswax and cold iron and something sweetish underneath, like fruit going bad in a locked room. Then the lanterns — dozens of them, hanging from nothing, burning without light exactly, glowing the way memory glows behind your eyes. And between the lanterns, stalls. Stalls made of draped shrouds and coffin-lids and one entire upturned rowboat, and behind the stalls, the sellers.

Most wore shapes that almost fit. A woman who was slightly too tall and slightly too smooth, smiling with slightly too many teeth. A man whose face kept trying to be several faces at once and settling, disappointedly, on none. Things hunched under tarps. Things that floated. One seller appeared to be nothing but a long gray coat, standing.

Pip put his shoulders back and walked in like he owned a stall himself.

Nobody looked at him. That was rule one working in reverse — in a place like this, looking was a kind of touching, and touching was a kind of claiming, and nobody wanted to claim anything here. He drifted. He listened. Ghost-markets ran on whispers the same as Vell's night market had; you just had to sort the whispers that mattered from the ones that were only wind wearing voices.

He heard a dead ferryman selling directions to a house that no longer existed, and the buyer paying in the memory of her own wedding. He heard a thing in a hood offering jars of last words. He heard, drifting from the far end near a lantern the color of a bruise, a voice saying the word *Door*.

His heart went off like a dropped pan.

* * *

The Door. Everyone talked about the Door the way sailors talked about the edge of the map. Coral's whispers led toward it — Pip had heard him murmuring in his sleep, coordinates spilling out of his mouth like beads off a snapped string — but nobody knew what it *was*. A gate. An exit. The way back to the real world, where the sky stayed lit and countries didn't forget themselves overnight. Tansy spoke about it softest of anyone, the way you speak about home when you've been gone so long home might be embarrassed to see you.

If Pip found out where it was — really found out, first, before anyone else — then he wouldn't be the tag-along anymore. The stowaway. The kid they hadn't planned on. He'd be the one who'd bought them salvation with a storm he'd caught himself.

He followed the word *Door* through the market like following a smell of bread.

It came from a stall at the very edge, where the gray got so thin that the ground under Pip's feet felt less like dirt and more like the memory of dirt. The stall was small. Its wares were laid out on a cloth that was also, somehow, a moth-eaten banner with a sigil Pip didn't recognize — some old crest, worn to a shadow.

And the seller.

It was shaped like a person, roughly, the way a glove is shaped like a hand. Gray all through, like smoke that had decided to sit still. But it had eyes — two points of dim silver, and when they settled on Pip, they did not look away, and Pip understood that rule one was already failing him, because his hands wanted to shake and the ghost could see them plain as morning.

"You're living," the ghost said. Its voice was dry paper, patient. "That's rare, lately."

"I'm busy," Pip said. "You selling or chatting?"

Something moved under the gray that might have been amusement. It gestured — a sleeve-like ripple — at its cloth. On it lay scraps of knowing: a key that opened a door that had burned down two hundred years ago. The true name of a fish. The location of a grave that would matter enormously to someone, someday, though not to Pip. And one item set apart from the rest, on its own square of black velvet:

A secret, folded up small. You couldn't see what was inside it. You could only see that it was heavy — the velvet dented under it, the lantern-light bending around it like light bends near something massive.

"That," said the ghost, "is a good one."

"What is it?"

"If I told you, I'd have sold half of it." The silver eyes blinked, slowly, like pond ice shifting. "You want it. I can taste the wanting. What have you brought me?"

Pip's hand went into his pocket before he could stop it, which was rule two failing, and he hated himself a little, and kept talking anyway to cover it. "Depends what you're buying. I don't sell junk."

"Everyone sells junk. Most sell it without knowing." The ghost leaned closer, and the air went cold the way a cellar is cold, the way a crypt is cold. "But sometimes. Sometimes someone brings me something with weight."

Pip drew out the bottle.

It was a squat green thing, corked with wax, and inside it a storm turned over and over — a real one, caught whole off the Vell rooftops on the night the stars went out. Rain that fell upward when you tipped it. Wind that screamed in a voice like a kettle. Lightning, tiny and furious, flashing against the glass like a trapped wasp. He'd caught it in the first hour of the end of the world, when the wardens were burning notices and the crowd was screaming and Pip, who had been stealing since before he could write, had done the only thing he knew how to do with catastrophe.

He'd bottled it.

"It's a storm," Pip said. "Whole one. Caught it myself. There's maybe four people alive who can do that, and two of them are dead."

The ghost was silent a long moment. Around them the market whispered on, lanterns swaying over bargains no living person should make. Then the silver eyes came back to Pip, and there was something new in them, something almost like respect, and Pip hated how much it warmed him.

"Caught weather," the ghost mused. "I remember when that was common. Before. When the world was stitched tight and the Choir sang it awake every morning." It rippled closer. "This has weight, boy. This has *history*. A storm from the last starred night in Ashenmoor." The sleeve gestured at the folded secret. "For that, you can know what's in the fold before you buy. Ask me one question and I'll answer it true. Then decide."

Here was the trap, and Pip saw it clear as glass: ask *what's the secret*, and you'd learn nothing except whether to pay. The ghost would fold the answer inside the answer. He chewed the inside of his cheek. Thought about Coral asleep under the shrine, murmuring coordinates. Thought about Tansy's fingertips going transparent. Thought about the taste of useless.

"One question," Pip said. "Fine. Here it is." He made his voice bored, which took work. "The Door everybody's hunting. The way out of this country. Tell me where it actually is — not rumors, not direction-of-travel. Where does it sit?"

The ghost went very still.

Then it laughed — a sound like a book falling open — and unfolded the secret right there on the cloth, because it was a trader, and a trader honors a question well asked.

"Beneath the Conservatory," it said. "The great school of the Choir, north and east, three weeks' walk if the land holds still. The Door sits under its deepest floor, under the chamber where they taught the singing. The god sleeps below it. The founders built the school on top of the lock so the key would always be close to hand." The silver eyes glittered. "Your singer will know the place. Your singer was raised there. Everyone who matters knows that, boy — it's the worst-kept secret in this unraveling country, and it's why the road ahead of you is going to be crowded."

The market noise seemed to come back all at once, loud and far away at the same time. Pip's mouth was dry.

*Coral was raised there.* Of course he was. Pip had pieced together that much from campfire silences and the way Sable watched Coral sing — careful, guilty, hungry. But beneath it. Under the floors where Coral had learned the magic that was outlawed, that had gotten children culled and teachers burned, under all of that, the way out. Which meant the way out was guarded by everything Coral had run from. Which meant —

Which meant Coral already knew. Had to know. The whispers, the coordinates — Coral's insides had been pointing him home this whole time, and he'd said nothing, and Pip suddenly understood something enormous and awful about the tall, tired, brilliant thief who'd taken him in off the streets of Vell:

Everybody on this crew kept secrets. Even the best of them. *Especially* the best of them.

"The storm," the ghost prompted, holding out a sleeve-hand. "We have a bargain."

* * *

Pip walked back to the Waystation with empty pockets and a full head, and the fullness weighed more.

He'd handed over the storm. Watched the ghost uncork it right there and breathe the lightning in, rain spiraling up into that gray shapeless body until the lanterns flickered, until for one moment the dead trader wore weather like a crown — and then it was gone, and the bottle was just glass, and Pip set it down on the cloth because his hands had finally started shaking and he refused to let them shake while he was still holding it.

Rule three, broken. And the strange thing, walking back under the sketch-forest where the leaves made no sound, was that it didn't feel like loss. It felt like spending. Like the storm had finally gone somewhere useful after six years rattling in his pocket.

No. That wasn't the strange thing.

The strange thing was what he did next.

He reached the shrine as the gray east was deciding whether to become dawn. Inside, the embers of their fire ticked. Tansy breathed. The twins slept tangled together like rope. Sable sat propped against a pillar, half-dozing, one hand loose around a hymnal he never read from anymore.

And Coral lay on his back with his brow creased, whispering numbers to the ceiling of the ruined world.

Pip stood over him a long time. Three steps away, easy, and wake him, and say: *I know where the Door is. Under your old school. I bought it with a storm, and a ghost says the road's going to be crowded, and we should move tonight.*

Three steps. His feet wouldn't take them.

Because if he told, then he was the messenger. The errand-boy. The kid who'd carried news somebody else already had. But if he *didn't* tell — if he kept it folded up small in his chest like the ghost's secret on its black velvet — then it was *his*. Weight and all. Something he'd paid for. Something that made him matter to the shape of what was coming.

And besides — a smaller voice, a colder one, one that sounded like six winters under the tanner's stairs — besides, Coral was keeping secrets too. Coral knew where the Door was and hadn't said. Whatever Pip was about to do, he'd only be doing what the adults did. Learning late what they'd clearly known all along: that secrets have weight, and you choose what to carry them *for*.

Pip crouched, banked the fire with a stick, and told himself that was why.

He'd tell them. Soon. When it counted. When telling would make him the one who saved them instead of the one repeating what they knew.

Above the broken dome, the sky stayed black and blank, and somewhere three weeks north, under a school built on a lock, a god slept beneath a door — and a child sat down between the embers and the sleeper, guarding a secret the size of both, and called it a plan.
