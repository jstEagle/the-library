## 17. The Choir's True History

The archives of the Choir had been beautiful once. Sable knew this the way he knew his own hands — by memory, by faith, by the long habit of loving things that were already gone.

He remembered them whole: the Scriptorium beneath the Motherhouse in Vell, where light fell through honey-colored glass and forty scribes sat in perfect silence, copying hymnals whose ink was mixed with powdered starlight so that the words themselves seemed to breathe. He had been nineteen when he first descended those stairs, a farmboy with a voice like a struck bell, and the archivist — old Brother Ellery, dust in the creases of his smile — had laid a hand on his shoulder and said, *Here is where we keep everything we are afraid to forget.*

Sable had believed him. That was the whole tragedy of Sable Fen, told plainly: he had believed.

Now he stood in an archive that was eating itself. The ruin they'd sheltered in for two days lay on the road between the forgotten town and the first Waystation — some grand edifice from before the unraveling, its purpose worn off it like paint. The lower vaults had been flooded and frozen and flooded again until the stone wore a skin of gray ice, and the shelves rose out of the dark in ranks, their books swollen to twice their size, spines split, pages fused into blocks of pulp that crumbled at a touch. Somewhere above them, wind moved through a collapsed roof with a sound like slow breathing.

He had come down with a lantern and a crowbar and the last of his faith, which weighed less than either.

* * *

It had been Isolde who found the sealed door, on the second morning, while Maren slept with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest and Coral walked the perimeter with that faraway look they'd carried since the stars went out — listening, always listening, to something no one else could hear.

"The iron's cold but not rusted," Isolde had said, rapping her knuckles against a door set into the vault wall, half-buried in flood-silt. "Everything else down here is rotting. Why not this?"

"Because it matters," Tansy had said, from her chair by the brazier. She said very little lately, but what she said landed like a stone dropped down a well. "Things that matter get protected. Even by people who are ashamed of them."

Sable had felt those words go through him like a fishhook.

So now it was night again, and the others slept above, and he worked the crowbar into the seam of the door by lantern-light, and the seal broke with a sound like a held breath finally released. Not locked, he realized. Sealed from the inside. Someone had wanted to be shut in here — or wanted whatever was in here to be shut away, and had chosen the only door that could not be opened from outside without violence.

Inside: dry air, impossibly preserved, smelling of cedar and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral and vast, like the inside of a mountain. A single room, circular, shelved floor to ceiling. And in the center, on a reading table of black oak, a book the size of a paving stone, chained shut with seven locks of seven different metals.

Sable stood before it for a long time.

He knew this book. Every priest of the Choir knew its title the way children know the names of monsters — *The Concordance of Founding*, the great authorized history, compiled by the Nine Founders themselves, of how the Choir had risen and why the magic had been given to humanity and what the Rites meant. He had studied from a copy of it as a novice. He had sworn oaths on it. He had, three years ago, testified at a tribunal with it open on the bench beside him while they stripped Coral Underhill's name from the rolls.

But copies were abridgments. Everyone knew that. The Founders' original was kept under seal, accessible only to the Archprimus, because — the official reasoning went — the full founding texts contained hymns too powerful for untrained voices.

Sable broke the first lock with the crowbar. It took him an hour to break all seven, working by feel in the lantern-light, and each one snapped differently — the iron rang, the silver bent, the lead simply gave up, and the last one, made of some pale wood he didn't recognize, split with a smell like a forest burning somewhere far away.

* * *

The first hundred pages were familiar. Creation-hymns. The cosmology every chorister learned: the world sung into shape, magic as the residue of the first song still hanging in the air, the Choir as the instrument by which humanity kept the song going. He read it standing, then sitting, then kneeling on the cold stone because his knees had decided the matter without consulting him.

Then page one hundred and twelve. And everything after.

The handwriting changed. The same hand, but slower now. Pressing harder. As if whoever wrote it had stopped transcribing and started confessing.

*We did not find the god,* the Founder wrote. *We built the prison around it, and called the prison a country.*

Sable read the sentence eleven times. His lips moved with it. The lantern hissed.

*You who come after will have been taught that Ashenmoor is the world. It is not. It has never been. There is a world beyond this one — wide, lit, unbroken — and nine centuries ago it was dying of a wound, and we fled from that wound into the only shelter we could make.*

No. Not fleeing. He turned pages with hands that shook.

*The god came first. Understand this, whoever you are, however many centuries hence you read these words: the god was not our enemy. The god was a child — the youngest of its kind, born of the deep places, and it loved us the way a child loves, wholly and without understanding. When the wound came — when the Unmaking began to eat through the world — the god tried to heal it. It poured itself into the breach. And a god's love is not a gentle thing; it has the weight of mountains, and the breach could not hold it, and the god began to die.*

*A dying god does not fade. We learned this too late, watching it suffer. A dying god screams. And the scream of a god is not a sound — it is an ending. It would have unmade everything the scream touched. The world, perhaps. Certainly us.*

*So we did the only merciful terrible thing left. We stole a country.*

*Not land — there was no land to steal. We stole the memory of a country. From the minds of everyone who had ever known one, we gathered every village and river and hearth-fire, every song and grave and harvest, and we wove it into a single seamless recollection, and we sang it into being around the dying god as a swaddling-cloth. Ashenmoor is a lullaby with borders. The stars you see are notes we hung in the sky of it, the Choir's oldest hymn set burning above us, and their light does not come from any sun — it comes from the god's own dreaming, dimming as the god dims.*

*And we bound the singing of it to human voices, generation upon generation, conduits raised in conservatories, so that the lullaby would never stop. This was the mercy. This was also the theft. Forgive us. Do not forgive us. We could not forgive ourselves, which is why we locked this book behind seven metals and taught you a prettier story.*

Sable closed the book.

He did not remember deciding to close it. He found himself standing with both palms flat on its cover, head bowed, breathing the way you breathe when you've been struck in the sternum — shallow, careful, as though the next full breath might crack something.

Above him, faintly, through the collapsed roof, the starless sky. The stars had gone out six weeks ago. Every priest had preached explanations since: sorcery, judgment, the Warden-Princes' sins, a celestial eclipse beyond understanding. Sable himself had taken coin to sing funeral hymns in a flooded crypt and had told frightened strangers the stars would return, that the heavens were only resting.

*Their light comes from the god's own dreaming.*

The stars going out meant the god was waking. Or drowning. Or — worse, so much worse — beginning to die in earnest, the way the Founder described, the way gods die, screaming.

And the border wards had fallen the same night. And the countryside beyond Vell had gone to gray static. And villages were forgetting themselves. Because the lullaby was failing. Because the swaddling-cloth was coming apart at the seams. Because somewhere at the heart of everything, the child they had imprisoned in a country was stirring, terrified, in its sleep.

Sable laughed. One short, ugly bark of it, echoing wrong in the sealed room. He put his fist against his mouth to stop more.

He had spent his whole life inside a hymn and been told it was the sky.

Every Rite he had ever performed. Every doctrine he had defended. The testimony he had given against Coral — *the accused showed conduit-resonance, the accused refused the Rite of Severance, the accused endangered the stability of the Song* — he had spoken those words believing he was protecting a holy machinery, the great engine of creation, and it had been a cage. All of it a cage. The conservatories were kennels where they trained singers to rock a dying god's cradle. The Wardens were not guardians of magic; they were jailers of the lullaby, hunting anyone whose voice might wake the prisoner or tear the cloth.

Coral had refused the Rite of Severance. Coral had run rather than let them cut the resonance out of their throat. Seven years hiding in Vell, selling stolen memories, suffocating under anti-magic wards — and the whole time, the truth was that Coral had heard the god through the bars and known, in the wordless way of conduits, that the song was wrong. That the prisoner was real.

They had known. Children always know.

Sable slid down the shelf until he was sitting on the cold stone, the open book heavy on his knees, and he read on through the night because stopping would have meant feeling all of it at once.

*Some among the Nine wished to release the god,* the Founder wrote, near the end, in a hand gone nearly illegible. *To carry it back through the wound and let it die in the wide world, where its scream would fall on empty distances instead of on us. But the Door requires a price no Founder could pay, and so we chose the prison, and we chose amnesia, and we chose to call it salvation.*

*If the god wakes fully, it will find itself caged, and its terror will be the end of us. If it dies caged, its scream will be the end of us. There is a third path — the Door, and the price, and the song complete — but I leave no map to it, because I do not trust the generations after me to choose mercy over survival. Let them find this book only when the stars begin to fail. By then they will have no choice but to understand.*

By then they will have no choice but to understand.

Sable looked up, through the doorway he'd broken open, toward the frozen stairs, the sleeping crew, the unraveling country, and understood that he was living inside the Founder's prophecy like a man living inside a sentence spoken about him before he was born.

* * *

He climbed the stairs at dawn, carrying the book against his chest like a child.

The main hall was gray-blue with early light, or with what passed for light these days — the sky had a quality now, colorless and sourceless, like illumination remembered rather than seen. Brannoc was awake, feeding the brazier. Tansy sat wrapped in her blanket, watching the ruined doorway with her ancient patience. Pip was a lump of blankets near the fire that snored with astonishing authority for someone so small.

And Coral was at the broken eastern wall, facing out, absolutely still.

Sable stopped. He had rehearsed nothing on the climb, and now every opening he might use deserted him. How did you tell someone the ground under their entire life had been a lie you helped enforce? How did you say *I read the confession of the men who built your cage, and I helped guard it, and I loved you even while I did*?

Coral turned before he spoke. They always did that — some conduit-sense of him arriving, maybe, or just seven years of surviving making them alert to footsteps.

"You were down there all night," Coral said. Not a question. Their eyes went to the book. "What is it?"

"It's the founding text," Sable said. His voice came out rough. "The real one. Not the abridgment."

"And?"

He could have softened it. He could have built up to it, cushioned it, done all the careful priestly work of delivering hard truths in bearable doses. Instead he heard himself say, plainly, because Coral deserved plainness and had earned it a hundred times over:

"Ashenmoor isn't real. It's a memory — a country stitched together out of other people's remembering and sung into being around a dying god, to muffle its death-scream. The stars were part of the lullaby. Their light is the god's dreaming. And they've gone out because the god is waking up."

Silence. Wind through the broken roof. Pip's snoring hitched and resumed.

Coral did not recoil. That was the thing about Coral that Sable had never gotten used to, across all these weeks on the road — they absorbed horror the way stone absorbs cold, all the way through, and then stood there holding it. He watched them look at the book, then at the colorless sky, then at their own hands.

"I've been hearing it," Coral said quietly. "For weeks. Since the stars went out. I thought the whispers were memory-fragments. Map-pieces." A pause. "They're not coordinates, are they."

"No."

"They're directions. To the Door." Coral's jaw tightened. "And to it. The god." They looked at Sable, and their voice, when it came, was almost gentle, which was somehow the worst thing it could have been. "You knew the Choir was lying about something. You testified against me and you knew—"

"I didn't know this." It came out cracked. "I swear it on anything you want. I thought I was protecting a holy thing. I thought—" He stopped. Started again, smaller. "I thought the song was the world."

Coral held his gaze for a long moment. Whatever moved behind their eyes, they kept it there, and he would spend a long time wondering about it later.

"Then we need to move faster," Coral said finally. "If it wakes and finds itself caged—"

"Everyone dies," Sable said. "Yes. And if it dies caged, everyone dies. The Founder says there's a third path. The Door, and a price, and a song complete. But he wouldn't write down what the price was." He hugged the book tighter. "Only that no Founder could pay it."

Tansy's voice came from across the hall, dry as autumn leaves, and both of them turned to find her watching them with eyes that had seen too many countries to be shocked by one more.

"Well," she said. "Now you know what I've suspected since my hair went white. Ask me sometime how a woman remembers a sea she's never sailed." She rose, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and nodded at the book in Sable's arms. "Bring that. And bring yourself, priest. If the Door needs a singer, you'll want to have made your peace with what you're singing to."

She shuffled toward the fire, leaving them in the gray light.

Coral was still looking at the sky. Then at him. And when they spoke, their voice dropped low enough that only he could hear it — low enough that it was only for him, whatever it cost them to say it:

"Then we go to the heart of it. Together. And Sable—" a breath, "—whatever the price turns out to be, we find out standing next to each other. Not across a courtroom. Agreed?"

Sable's throat closed around every answer he had. He managed the only true one.

"Agreed."

Above them, the colorless sky pressed down like a held note, and somewhere far beneath their feet — deeper than cellars, deeper than graves, deeper than the roots of a country that had never existed — something small and vast and terrified turned over in its sleep, and dreamed, briefly, of stars.
