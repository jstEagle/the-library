## 18. Ambush at the Ford

Brannoc had ferried the Gray Country's rivers for three weeks now, and he had learned their rules the way a man learns a new wife: carefully, and with apologies.

Water here was not water. It was memory pretending to be water — it ran, it pooled, it reflected, but when you dipped your hand in it came away with someone else's chill on your skin. The river they called the Lisse had once run clear off the mountains; now it ran gray and slow as spilled milk, its surface flickering with images that were almost pictures: a fish that was only the idea of a fish, a willow sketched in charcoal, a bridge that existed for one heartbeat every hour or so before forgetting itself again.

"Ford's ahead," Isolde said from the bow, her dead-star compass open on her knee. The needle didn't point anywhere anymore; it just trembled, like a thing afraid to choose.

"I see it," Brannoc said, though what he saw made his stomach knot. The ford was where the old road crossed the river, and the river there was wide and shallow and *wrong* — the static in it moved against the current, little silver flecks swimming upstream like they knew something he didn't. "Maren, wake our passenger."

Coral slept in the stern these days more than anyone liked. They'd stopped sleeping nights after the village of blank faces; now they dozed in snatches whenever the whispers in their head went quiet enough, which apparently wasn't often. When Maren shook them, Coral came awake all at once, eyes already searching the banks the way a hunted animal searches treeline.

"Trouble?" they asked.

"No trouble," Brannoc said. "Just a river that's forgotten how to be wet."

He brought the ferry in low and slow. Behind him, Sable Fen sat with his hands folded around his stolen hymnal, lips moving without sound — praying, Brannoc supposed, though to whom, in a country whose god was drowning at the bottom of it, Brannoc couldn't say. Pip crouched beside him, bottle-storm clinking gently in the child's coat pockets, watching the far bank with the flat professional attention of a boy who'd grown up reading streets instead of skies.

The ford itself was beautiful, in the way of things about to go badly. The gray water spread out thin and bright over pale stones, and the light coming off it was the light of no particular time of day — the whole land had been losing its hours lately, mornings arriving late and leaving early, as if the sun too was being slowly erased. On the far side, the road climbed toward the hills through fields that were half-grown: wheat that stood knee-high and then simply stopped, cut off mid-blade, the rest of the field still waiting to be imagined.

"We cross single file," Brannoc said, shipping the oars. "Rope line. If the current takes anyone—" He stopped. Everyone knew what happened to people the river took. You found them downstream sometimes, standing in the shallows, smiling, unable to say their own names.

Isolde tied the rope to Maren first, because Maren insisted. That was the twins all over. Isolde navigated by stars that had gone out; Maren walked point into every dark place and dared it to close behind her. Between them they'd gotten this crew across three rivers and one forest that kept rearranging its trees, and Brannoc trusted them the way he trusted his own hands.

They were halfway across when Halloway's wardens came down out of the wheat.

* * *

Later, Brannoc would try to remember exactly how it started, and the memory would come back wrong every time — the way memories did now, in this place, fraying at the edges like everything else. What stayed true was the sound: a whistle, three notes high and clean, cutting across the water. A warden's signal. He'd heard it once before, seven years ago in Vell, the night the Gleaning carts came for the tinker family two doors down.

"Wardens!" Pip shouted it first, because children see fastest. "Bank, left, in the field—"

There were eight of them. Maybe nine. They rose out of the half-made wheat in gray cloaks that matched the gray country so well it was as if the wardens had been grown there, and they moved with the terrible unhurried confidence of people who have never once needed to hurry. Their leader carried a lantern even though it was day — or what passed for day — and inside the lantern burned something that wasn't fire. It was white, and still, and looking at it made Brannoc's teeth ache.

Wardlight. Anti-magic, distilled. Enough of it to strip the Choir out of a person's blood.

"Off the water," Brannoc barked. "Move!"

But the water was the problem. The rope line stretched them across thirty feet of shimmering gray, and the wardens fanned along the near bank with the easy spacing of trained killers, and Brannoc understood with cold clarity that they hadn't come to arrest anyone. Wardens didn't cross rivers to make arrests. Not anymore. Not since the notices in Vell changed from *surrender for questioning* to nothing at all.

"Coral Underhill," the lantern-bearer called, and Brannoc knew the voice before he placed it — he'd heard it through a door in Vell, once, years ago, giving orders in a voice that tried very hard to be gentle. Warden Halloway. She stood apart from her squad, and she did not raise a weapon, and somehow that was worse. "You are recalled. Come to me and no one else needs to be hurt."

Coral stood chest-deep in the middle of the ford, rope in both hands, water flickering around their waist. From the back Brannoc could see them shaking — actually shaking, the way they never let themselves shake in front of the crew.

"You know I can't," Coral called back.

"I know," Halloway said, and there it was again, that gentleness, that awful tenderness, "I've always known. Take them."

The wardens moved.

What happened next happened fast, and Brannoc's part in it was mostly holding on. The first bolt of wardlight crossed the river and struck the water, and the ford *screamed* — an actual sound, high and thin, like a kettle full of ghosts — and everywhere the white light touched, the river simply stopped being. Not drained. Not frozen. Stopped being. A circle of the ford became nothing at all, a hole in the world with the stones' reflections hanging in it like flies in amber.

Maren was nearest. Brannoc saw her turn, saw her calculate — he could read the twins' calculations by now, the half-second arithmetic they did before every reckless thing — and then Maren did the most Maren thing possible.

She turned *toward* the wardens.

"The rope!" she shouted to Isolde. "Hold the line, I'll hold them—"

"You'll hold *nothing*, you idiot—"

But Maren had already planted herself in the center of the ford like a fencepost, arms out, body between the wardens' light and the rest of them, and Brannoc realized what she was doing. She was making herself the target. She was spending herself the way she spent everything — completely, immediately, without counting.

The second volley came. Maren took it crossing her arms — wardlight doesn't burn, that's the horror of it, it *unmakes* — and Brannoc watched the left side of her body flicker. Flicker, like a candle in wind. Like a sketch being erased. Her arm was still there, and also it was becoming less there, edges going soft, going gray, going to the same unfinished nothing as the wheat fields beyond the hills.

"MAREN!" Isolde's scream came from somewhere very far away, or maybe it was just that the world had gone narrow and quiet, the way it does when everything is about to change.

Maren went down into the gray water.

* * *

Here is what Brannoc knew about Coral Underhill, after three weeks on the river with them: they were careful. Careful with words, careful with rations, careful above all with the magic coiled in them like a second heartbeat. They rationed it the way a starving man rations bread. They'd used fragments — a whisper to calm a frightened horse, a thread of song to steady Tansy's fading — small things, deniable things, things Brannoc could pretend not to see because pretending was kinder.

He knew what using it openly cost them. The wards of Vell had pressed on Coral for seven years like a boot on a throat, and they'd fled that pressure rather than be found by it, and everything they'd built since — the rules, the aliases, the small safe thefts — was a wall between them and exactly this moment.

So when Coral dropped the rope, turned through the gray water, and knelt over Maren's flickering body, Brannoc understood what he was about to watch, and part of him wanted to shout *don't*, and a larger part wanted to shout *finally*.

Coral began to sing.

It was not a song Brannoc had words for. It wasn't melody, exactly — it was more like hearing the river remember being a river, hearing the stones remember being mountains, hearing the light remember the sun. The Choir. He'd heard smugglers whisper about it his whole life: the old magic, the outlawed magic, the music that the founders of Ashenmoor had used to steal a country whole. Every child in Vell was taught to cover their ears if they ever heard it, though no one in seven generations had heard it.

It sounded like home. That was the trap of it. It sounded like a kitchen Brannoc had never had, a mother's voice singing a lullaby he'd never been sung, warm bread and rain on a roof and every good thing his life had skipped past — and underneath the warmth, vast and patient, something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The wardlight broke against it like frost against a window.

Coral's voice rose, and the ford answered. The gray water cleared from the center outward, running honest and brown and real, and where Maren lay half-erased, the song wrapped around her like hands, and her edges came back. Slowly. Stubbornly. Her arm remembered its outline; her outline remembered its color; her color remembered her. And Coral sang with their eyes shut and tears cutting tracks through the dust on their face, pouring something irreplaceable out of themselves into a woman they'd known three weeks, because that was what conduits did, and because she was theirs, and because some debts you pay before you count the cost.

On the bank, the wardens had stopped.

Not retreated — stopped. Brannoc watched them stand in the ruined wheat with their lanterns lowered, and he watched their faces, and what he saw there frightened him more than the wardlight had. Wonder. Two of them had taken their hoods back without seeming to notice. One had begun, softly, horribly, to hum along — a harmony she could not possibly have learned, surfacing from somewhere older than her training, and the warden beside her looked at her the way you look at a stranger wearing a friend's face.

Halloway alone did not soften. She stood at the edge of the song like a stone in a stream, and Brannoc saw her mouth move around words he was glad he couldn't hear, and he saw her grip her lantern until her knuckles went white, and he understood that for her this was not wonder. This was proof. This was the confirmation of everything she'd crossed a dying country to find: that Coral Underhill could reach inside a person and rewrite them, even now, even unwillingly, even with love.

Then the ground under the wardens' feet finished forgetting itself, and the bank gave way, and three of them went into the river, and Halloway called the retreat — one long note, ragged at the end — and they were gone into the half-made hills.

* * *

They got Maren to the far bank. All of her, more or less, though her left hand came back fainter than it left, translucent at the fingertips, like glass held up to light. Isolde held her sister and did not cry, which was worse than crying, and Maren — stubborn to the last — kept saying, "Told you I'd hold them," in a voice frayed down to thread.

Coral collapsed on the stones and was sick, and then was silent, and then lay with their face to the colorless sky, breathing like a bellows. Pip crouched next to them and did the bravest thing Brannoc saw that day: the boy said nothing. He just sat, and kept watch over the empty bank, small hands curled around a bottled storm, guarding a magician who had just shown the whole horizon exactly what they were.

Sable Fen knelt and put his coat over Coral's shoulders. Whatever he whispered, Brannoc didn't catch it. But Coral's hand found the priest's sleeve and held on, and held on, and Brannoc turned away to give them whatever privacy a riverbank allows.

Tansy settled onto the stones beside Brannoc with the sigh of old wood. For a while neither of them spoke. The river ran clean behind them — it wouldn't last, nothing here lasted, by morning the ford would be gray again — but for now it ran, and the sound of it was the first honest sound the land had made in days.

"She'll come again," Brannoc said at last. "Halloway. With more."

"Yes." Tansy's voice was dry as seed husks. "And now she knows for certain. You understand that, don't you, ferryman? Everything Coral just bought for that girl, they paid for twice. Once in themselves, and once in the hunt. There won't be warnings next time. There won't be offers."

Brannoc looked down the river, toward the Door none of them could see, buried somewhere beneath a Conservatory in the heart of a country that was unraveling a little more each night. He thought about the warden humming. He thought about Coral's face while they sang — not powerful, not terrible. *Grieving.* Like a person handing out pieces of their own heart and knowing the heart doesn't grow back.

"Aye," he said finally. "Then we'd best get used to running."

Behind them, Isolde gasped.

Brannoc turned. Maren was sitting up, Isolde gripping her shoulders, and Maren was staring at her own faded left hand — but not at the fading. At what she was saying. Her voice carried across the stones, thin and strange:

"Coral. When you sang — I was *inside* it. Inside you. I saw—" She swallowed, and Brannoc watched the color leave what color she had left. "I saw what you're carrying. All of it. Thousands of them, Coral. Memories, stacked like cordwood. And something else. Something at the bottom that was *looking back*."
