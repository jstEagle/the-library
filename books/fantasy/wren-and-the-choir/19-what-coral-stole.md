## 19. What Coral Stole

Maren woke to the smell of woodsmoke and someone humming.

The humming was wrong. Not wrong the way a sour note is wrong — wrong the way a room is wrong when you wake in it and can't remember its name. The melody moved in circles, gathering itself and unraveling, like water trying to remember downhill. She lay still under the wool blanket, her bandaged arm heavy at her side, and listened, and understood with a lurch that she was hearing the river again. The static-river. The one that had taken three fingers' width of her flesh and left it singing in her bones.

She was in the loft of some waystation shrine. Rafters dark overhead, straw beneath the blanket, rain on the shingles in long slow sheets. Below, through gaps in the floorboards, lamplight and low voices.

She had been dreaming of the ford. Of Brannoc roaring her name across the gray water. Of Coral standing knee-deep in that impossible current with their arms flung wide and their mouth open, and the song coming out of them — not sound exactly, but *weight*, a pressure that bent the world's grain — and the river remembering, just for a moment, what rivers were supposed to be. Water. Cold. Going somewhere.

Maren had been underwater when it happened. That was the thing no one else knew. When Coral's magic swept over the crossing, it had poured into the wound in her arm like light into cracked glass, and for one suspended heartbeat Maren had been *inside* it. Inside Coral. Inside whatever it was that Coral actually was.

She had seen things there.

Her sister Isolde thought she'd been delirious with pain. Maybe she had been. But pain didn't leave residue like this. Pain didn't come back to you in the night wearing other people's faces.

* * *

She found Coral two mornings later, mending nets with Brannoc by the millpond while the crew waited out weather that fell sideways from a sky the color of dirty wool. They were camped in the ruins of a drover's inn, all of them too tired and too haunted to move faster than grief allowed. Maren's arm was splinted and wrapped to the elbow; the flesh beneath the bandages had closed strangely, in pale seams, as if the wound had been *stitched* rather than healed.

Coral sat apart, as they always did, working a net needle with quick precise fingers. Watching them, you'd see nothing but a thin, sharp-faced thief with ink-stained cuffs and tired eyes. You would not see what Maren had seen: a cathedral of stolen light, vast beyond reason, humming inside a person like bees in a wall.

"Your stitching is crooked," Maren said, sitting down beside them without asking.

Coral glanced up. "Brannoc did the bandages. I only held the—"

"Not the arm." Maren nodded at the net. "The knots. Left-hand crossings. Isolde taught us both."

Something flickered behind Coral's eyes — the small, practiced blankness of a person deciding which lie fits best. Maren had grown up navigating by dead stars, reading currents no living navigator could chart. She knew false bearings when she saw one.

"Coral." She kept her voice level, conversational, pitched not to carry to Brannoc at the pond's edge. "When your magic went through me at the ford — I was awake inside it. I don't know if you know that. I was underwater, and then I wasn't. I was *in you.*"

The net needle stopped moving.

"And before you tell me I dreamed it," Maren went on, "I want you to explain why I now remember a wedding I never attended. A woman named Odile Voss, marrying a cooper's son, in a chapel in Vell's Salt Quarter, with marigolds because roses were too dear. I remember the smell of the tallow candles. I remember being *happy*, Coral, and I have never once been happy at a wedding in my life."

The silence stretched. Rain filled it. Somewhere below, Pip was shouting at Tansy about cards, his voice bright and ordinary and impossibly far away.

"You took memories," Maren said quietly. "That's how your magic works. It doesn't just pass through. It *carries*. And when it carried me back to my own body—" she flexed the stitched arm, "—it leaked. Or maybe it didn't leak. Maybe you just have so much in there that it sloshes over the sides."

"Maren—"

"How many?"

Coral looked down at their hands. For a long moment Maren thought they wouldn't answer. Then, in a voice stripped of all its usual deflection, flat as a ledger:

"I stopped counting at four thousand."

The number landed in Maren's chest like a stone dropped down a well. Four thousand. Four thousand moments that belonged to other people, hoarded inside one narrow body. Weddings and funerals and first kisses and last words, all of them pressed together in the dark behind Coral's ribs like moths in a jar.

"That's theft," she said. "That's — Coral, that's *graves*. You're walking around full of graves."

"I know what it is." The words came out rough. "You think I don't know? I've known since the first one. I was sixteen years old and a woman paid me to take her grief and I felt it go and I felt how much lighter she walked afterward, and I told myself it was mercy. And it was! Some days it was. But I kept the change, Maren. Every time. I kept everything I ever took, and I told myself it was so nothing would be lost, so I could give them back someday, and that was true, and it was also a lie, because keeping them is the only time I've ever felt like more than a hollow place where magic used to live."

Their voice cracked on the last word and they pressed their lips shut, jaw working, staring out at the rain-hammered pond.

Maren should have stood up. Should have gone to Isolde, to Sable, to anyone, and said *the conduit we've chained our lives to is a magpie, a hoarder, a thief of the dead's last possessions.* Instead she heard herself ask the question she'd been circling since the night she woke with Odile Voss's marigolds blooming behind her eyes.

"What else is in there?"

Coral went very still.

It was the stillness itself that answered her. Not denial. Recognition.

"There's something in you," Maren whispered. "Something old. Something I touched when I was inside. It wasn't human, Coral. It was like — do you know what a whale sounds like through a ship's hull? That depth-note that you feel in your teeth? There was something in your hoard that made that sound. Something that had been singing for longer than countries have existed. And it was afraid. It was *drowning*."

Coral closed their eyes.

"When the stars went out," they said at last, each word placed like a foot on ice, "everything in me started whispering. All the memories, all at once, like a market opening. And underneath the market there was a voice I didn't recognize. It knew roads I've never walked. It grieved for hills I couldn't find on any map. I thought at first it was a memory I stole off some mad pilgrim. Then I thought it was the land talking. Now—" they opened their eyes, and Maren saw that they were wet, "—now I think it's been in there longer than I have. I think pieces of it got scattered into every conduit the Choir ever trained, and I'm one of the last vessels left holding a shard of something that the whole country is built on top of."

"The god," Maren breathed. Everyone whispered about the god now, the way sailors whisper about the thing under the hull. "You're carrying pieces of the god."

"I'm carrying a prisoner's letters home," Coral said, "and I don't know who to send them to."

For a moment neither of them moved. The rain came down. Brannoc laughed at something across the yard, a big easy sound, and it hurt to hear it.

"You have to tell Sable," Maren said finally. "And Isolde. And Pip — no, not Pip, he's a child, but the others. They deserve to know what's riding with us. What we're *carrying*. If Halloway takes you, or Quill, or if the god's pieces tear loose inside you somewhere on the road—"

"And say what?" Coral turned on her, and for the first time Maren saw the fear under all that competence, naked and shaking. "'Forgive me, I'm a walking reliquary full of stolen lives and one furious fragment of the thing we're all standing on'? They'll look at me the way you're looking at me right now. Like a locked door."

"I'm looking at you like a person who lied to us."

"I lied to everyone my whole life!" It burst out of them, too loud, and Brannoc's laugh died across the yard. Coral lowered their voice to a fierce whisper. "That's what survival *is* here. Seven years I survived by being nobody. And then you people — you *idiots* — you smugglers and priests and children — you kept insisting I was somebody, and I let you believe it, because I wanted it to be true. But the truth is I am a vault with legs, and everything in me belongs to someone else, including possibly the ground under your feet, and the only thing that's ever been mine—" 

They stopped. Swallowed.

"The only thing that's mine," Coral finished, very quietly, "is what I owe."

Maren sat with that. Her arm throbbed in its strange stitched seams. She thought about the twins' code, the first law of dead-star navigation: *a false bearing trusted long enough becomes the route.*

"You could have told us after the ford," she said. "I nearly died holding that line. I bled out half my life into a river of static, and your magic sewed me up, and it never once occurred to you to mention that the thread you used came from a stolen warehouse."

"It occurred to me every hour since."

"But not enough."

"No," Coral admitted. "Not enough."

Below them, Pip's voice rose again — arguing with Tansy, laughing, alive — and Maren watched Coral flinch toward the sound like a man toward warmth, and hated how much she understood it.

"I won't tell the others," she said at last. "Not yet. You get one day, Coral. One day to tell Sable yourself, because that man has been looking at you like you're the last dry plank on this wreck, and he deserves to hear it from you and not from me. If you haven't told him by tomorrow night, I will. And Isolde. And Brannoc. And then we decide — all of us — whether the vault walks with us or whether we walk away from it."

Coral nodded slowly. "Fair."

"It isn't fair. Fair would be you having told us in Vell." Maren stood, her arm cradled against her chest, and looked down at the thief who carried four thousand graves and a god's despair in a body barely bigger than her own. "One more question. The woman. Odile Voss. The widow whose wedding you carry."

Coral's face did something complicated. "What about her?"

"Is she in Vell? Is she real, does she miss it, does she even know it's gone?"

"She sold it to me herself," Coral said. "Her happiest day, for bread money, the winter her husband died. She said she couldn't bear to keep carrying something that heavy." A pause. "I always meant to bring it back."

Maren studied them a long moment — the liar, the hoarder, the vessel, the girl-shaped locked door — and found, to her own fury, that she believed it.

"Then start with hers," she said. "Give something back before you ask any of us to trust you with anything again."

She turned to go, and had reached the top of the stairs when Coral spoke behind her, so softly she almost missed it under the rain.

"Maren. The thing inside me. When you were in there. Did it — did it say anything to you?"

Maren stood on the landing, hand on the rail, rain loud on the roof, and told the truth because she was too tired to do otherwise.

"It was counting," she said. "All that vastness, all that terror — it was counting down numbers, over and over, like a child hiding in a cellar during a bombardment. And the number it kept coming back to was one."

She heard Coral's breath catch behind her.

"One what?" Coral asked.

"I don't know." Maren started down the stairs. "But tomorrow, when you talk to Sable, I'd figure it out fast. Because I don't think it's counting down to the Door, Coral. I think it's counting down to *waking*."

Behind her, in the loft above the ruined inn, the net needle clattered to the floorboards, and did not pick itself up again.
