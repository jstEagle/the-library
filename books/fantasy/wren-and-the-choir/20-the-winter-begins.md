## 20. The Winter Begins

The snow came before the autumn had finished dying.

Coral felt it first as a wrongness in the air, a held breath, the way a room goes still just before someone speaks a name that shouldn't be spoken. They were crossing the stubble-fields north of the Ford, Maren's arm bound in a sling and Brannoc hauling the cart with the grim economy of a man who had decided not to think about what he was hauling, when the light changed. Not dimmed — *changed*, the color draining out of the afternoon as if the land had been dipped in gray water and hung up to dry.

Then the first flakes came down, fat and slow and deliberate, like ash from a fire no one had lit.

"That's not right," Isolde said. She stood in the cart bed, one hand shading eyes that navigated by stars that were no longer there. "Snow doesn't fall *sideways-then-up*. Look at it."

She was correct, of course. Some of the flakes rose. They drifted down for a while and then reconsidered, climbing back into the cloud as though they'd forgotten which direction they were supposed to go. The unraveling had reached the sky itself, Coral thought, or the sky's memory of falling.

"We need shelter," Brannoc said. "Now. Before it gets ideas."

They found the barn an hour later, half-buried already at the edge of a farmstead whose farmhouse was only half there — the front door real enough to have a latch, the windows behind it suggestions, sketches in charcoal where glass should be. The barn, though, remembered itself stubbornly. Its beams were oak-dark and honest. Hay still sweetened the dark inside it. Whoever had built it had built it well, and perhaps the land, in its forgetting, held on longest to the things people had made with their hands out of love or necessity or both.

Sable went through the motions of blessing it, then stopped halfway, his hands hovering awkwardly over the doorpost. "Old habit," he said, not looking at anyone. "The Choir used to bless thresholds."

"Nothing stops you from finishing," Tansy said mildly, from her place at the back of the cart. She had been quieter since the Ford. Quieter and smaller, somehow, though Coral couldn't have said how a person could shrink without losing weight. There was a translucence gathering at her edges, faint as breath on cold glass.

"I'm off duty," Sable said, and climbed down.

* * *

By full dark the snow had stopped pretending to be weather and become something else entirely. It fell in sheets now, and where it landed too thickly, things changed. Coral watched it happen through the barn's wide doors: a copse of birches at the field's edge thinning as they watched, the white trunks going pale, then paler, then simply *less*, until there was a gap in the world where trees had been, smooth and blank as an unwritten page.

Every night now. Whole regions, erased between one dusk and the next. Coral had begun keeping count in the margins of their thoughts the way other people kept accounts: the mill-town gone two nights past. The river road, gone last night. A hill that Pip swore had been following them, patient as a dog, gone this morning with its whole valley.

"It's speeding up," Coral said to the room at large.

The room at large was the barn's interior, firelit, hay-walled, smelling of animal warmth even though the animals themselves were long gone — taken, or unmade, or walked through some door none of them would ever find. The crew had arranged itself around the fire with the practiced geometry of people who had been traveling together long enough to know each other's shadows. Brannoc sat closest, being largest and coldest-blooded. The twins had wedged themselves together against Maren's good side, Isolde braiding Maren's hair with fierce concentration, as if neatness could hold a person together. Pip was upside down in the hay loft, legs hooked over a beam, watching them all from his inverted kingdom. Sable sat near Coral, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not quite touching. Since the chapel, since the ruin-chapel and everything said and unsaid there, this was their arrangement: nearness without claim. It was more than Coral had expected to have again. It was also less than Coral wanted, and they suspected Sable knew both those things.

Tansy sat wrapped in every blanket they owned, hands folded, watching the fire with the expression of someone listening to music only she could hear.

"It is speeding up," Tansy agreed. "The god stirs faster when it's cold. Cold is closer to nothing, and nothing is what it dreams of." She said it the way she said most things, plainly, as if reporting the price of eggs.

"And what do we dream of?" Pip called down from the loft, upside-down, hair hanging like dark straw. "Because I'll tell you mine. I dream of a house. With a *door* that opens onto a street, and the street has other houses on it, and none of them are gray, and nobody's forgotten anything."

"What kind of house?" Isolde asked, humoring him, her fingers never pausing in Maren's braid.

"A tall one. Narrow. With a crooked chimney." Pip's voice lost its performance quality; it dropped into something young and unguarded. "There was a house like that where I'm from. Before." He didn't say before *what*. None of them did, about their own befores. "My mother kept herbs on the sill. Rosemary. You could smell it from the street if the window was open."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow fell upward past the doorway in slow gray ribbons.

"Rosemary doesn't grow in Ashenmoor," Tansy said quietly. "Not properly. The soil's wrong for it. I've tried forty years." She looked up, and her old eyes caught the firelight like water catching a lamp. "It grows outside. Where I'm from. It grew along my mother's walkway, and she'd cut a sprig every Sunday and put it behind my ear, and tell me I smelled like Sunday dinner." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "That's how I knew, when I was small, that this wasn't home. No rosemary. No proper sea either — the Ashenmoor coast is a rumor of a coast. And the sun here sets in the wrong quarter. I checked every evening for a year when I was seven. Every evening. My parents thought I was mad."

"You're not from here," Maren said. Not a question. They had all known it, or half-known it, the way you know a shape under a blanket. But Tansy had never said it aloud.

"No. I was born under a whole sky." Tansy turned her hands over, palms up, as though weighing something invisible. "Stars in their seasons. Constellations with names that meant things — the Plowman, the Serpent-Bearer, the Wagon. Here—" she gestured vaguely at the ceiling, beyond which the starless dark pressed down like a lid, "—the founders copied them, but copies fade. That's why your dead-star navigation works at all, girls. You're steering by ghosts of ghosts."

Isolde's braid-stillness was the loudest silence in the room.

"My turn, then," Brannoc said gruffly, because Brannoc could not abide a silence longer than three heartbeats. He fed a stick to the fire with the delicacy of a man handling something explosive. "Home's a ferry landing. Two hundred miles south of Vell, on the Merle. Or it was." He shrugged his great shoulders. "River smell. Tar and fish and my father swearing at the current. He swore at it like it owed him money. Every morning, same oath, same river, never paid up once." He almost smiled. "I used to think I'd take the ferry over when I was old and sit in the middle of the water and just — stay. Let the current argue with somebody else." He looked into the flames. "Ferry's probably static by now. River too. Doesn't matter. I can smell it whenever I want. Tar and fish and swearing. That's home. Home's a smell you carry."

"Home's a sound," Sable said.

Everyone looked at him. He had been quiet all evening, sitting with his long priest's hands loose in his lap, and now he seemed surprised to have spoken at all.

"The crypt where I sang, in Vell. Flooded. Knee-deep water, and the acoustics were—" he shook his head. "You'd sing a note and the note would keep living after you, bouncing off the stone, and for a few seconds the crypt was fuller than any cathedral ever built. Full of echo. Full of everything the hymn left behind." His eyes found Coral's across the fire, and Coral felt the look like a hand laid flat against their sternum. "I thought that was the holiest thing I'd ever feel. Sound that stays. Then I learned what staying costs, so." He spread his hands. "Home's a sound that stays. Even when you wish it wouldn't."

Coral understood he was talking about them. About the resonance he'd heard in a night market crowd, years of guilt folded into recognition. He was offering it up plainly now, the way the chapel had taught them to: here is my wound, look, I am not hiding it anymore.

Pip slid down from the loft and landed in the hay beside Coral, close, the way he always positioned himself lately — within arm's reach, as though Coral might dissolve like a birch copse if unsupervised.

"Coral hasn't said theirs," Pip announced to the company, with the shamelessness of the very young.

"There's no law," Coral said.

"House rule," said Pip. "Barn rules. Everybody pays a story for the fire."

Coral looked around the circle: Brannoc's river-smell patience, the twins leaning together, Tansy translucent at the edges and burning anyway, Sable with his open hands, Pip with his chin up and his eyes daring them. Found family, assembled from wreckage, demanding tribute of one story.

And the truth was — the terrible, ordinary truth — that Coral did not know which memories were theirs anymore. Seven years of carrying other people's best moments, and beneath those the conservatory's careful thefts, and beneath *those* whatever the god had seeded in them without asking. When Coral reached for *home*, they got an armful of strangers' weddings, strangers' childhoods, a widow's wedding-day luck-charm sold for six coppers in a night market that might not exist anymore.

But there was one thing. Small. Possibly true. Possibly their own.

"When I was very small," Coral began, and their voice came out rough, "before the Conservatory took me, there was a kitchen. I don't know if it was our kitchen or a memory someone sold me or something I dreamed. But there was bread proving under a cloth, and rain on the window, and someone humming — low, tuneless, the same four notes over and over, like a kettle deciding." Their throat tightened. "I've spent seven years telling myself it was mine. Because if it wasn't mine, then I don't have anything, do I? No before. Just the thefts and the hiding."

The fire cracked. Nobody rushed to fill the silence, which was its own kindness.

"Then it's yours," Pip said finally, with the absolute authority of a child ruling on a matter of law. "Kitchens belong to whoever remembers the humming. That's just how it works."

"That's not how anything works," Isolde said softly.

"It's how *this* works," said Pip, gesturing at everything — the barn, the gray snow, the unraveling country, the ragged circle of them. "This whole place runs on remembering. So kitchens run on it too."

Tansy made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief. "Out of the mouths of thieves," she said. "The child's not wrong, though. Whatever else this country is — a stolen page, a prison, a god's sickbed — it runs on memory the way a body runs on blood. Which is why it's dying so fast now. The god is dreaming less. Dreaming less means remembering less. Remembering less means—" she nodded toward the doorway, toward the blankness where the birches had been, "—that."

Maren spoke for the first time, her voice still frayed from the Ford, from the wound she'd taken holding the line. "Then we should spend it well. The remembering." She leaned into Isolde. "Tell them about the lighthouse, Izzy. The real one."

Isolde's hands went still in Maren's hair.

"You've never let me—"

"I'm letting you now."

Isolde looked around the circle like a woman asked to undress in daylight. Then she began, haltingly, and the story came out of her the way water comes from a cracked jar: the lighthouse her grandmother had tended on a coast none of them could name, outside the stolen country, in the real world; the lamp she'd been allowed to polish as a girl; the ships' lights strung along the black water like a second sky. "Grandmother said the sea keeps every promise the land breaks," she finished. "I believed her. I still believe her." Her jaw tightened. "Which is why I'm going to see it again. Both of us are. Through that Door or over my own corpse."

"Over *our* corpses," Maren corrected. "Plural. We've discussed this."

Brannoc snorted. Tansy smiled her almost-smile. And Coral sat in the warmth of it, in the firelight and the hay-smell and the press of bodies who had chosen each other badly and completely, and felt the ache that had lived in their chest for seven years shift into something else. Still an ache. But warmer-edged. The ache of having something to lose.

Outside, the snow fell upward, and somewhere beyond it another region of the world forgot itself into blankness. Inside, Pip demanded Brannoc's father's exact curse word, word for word, and Brannoc refused on grounds of decency and then delivered it anyway to general delight, and Tansy taught them a round from her impossible homeland, four notes rising and falling like breathing, and Sable's voice joined Coral's on the third verse without either of them deciding it, harmonies finding each other the way they always had, the way they always would.

Warmth against the dark. It wouldn't be enough. Nothing would be enough. But tonight it was *this*: a barn that remembered itself, a fire that burned, a stolen song sung by thieves and exiles in a country that was forgetting how to exist.

* * *

Coral woke past midnight to silence of a new texture.

Not sleep-silence. Not snow-silence. A silence with edges, as though the world had been trimmed.

They lay still in the hay, listening. The others breathed around them — Brannoc's rumble, the twins' matched rhythm, Pip sprawled against Coral's side with the boneless trust of the rescued. Sable slept sitting up, head tipped back, one hand still loosely closed around a stick he'd been whittling. Only Tansy's blanket-shape showed no rise and fall, but Tansy's breathing had grown so quiet these last weeks that absence proved nothing.

Coral rose, careful as a thief, which was after all their trade.

The barn doors stood open to the night. Through them, the fields lay white and still, and the snow had stopped, and the sky above was clear — clear and utterly black, the lid of the world pressing down. And at the field's edge, where yesterday a farm lane had run toward a village called Hallowmere — Coral knew the name now, the whispers in their skull supplied it freely, map-fragments surfacing like drowned things — there was nothing. Not ruins. Not wreckage. Nothing. A smooth white sheet of un-being, edged clean as paper cut with a knife, and beyond it the dark simply continued, featureless, patient.

Hallowmere was gone. All of it. While they sang rounds and argued about curse words and slept warm, the land had reached out and unpicked an entire village, name and all.

And standing at the edge of that nothing, small against the vast blank, wrapped in a blanket with her silver hair loose down her back, stood Tansy.

She wasn't fading tonight. Tonight, at the border of the unmade ground, she was *glowing* — faintly, terribly, lit from within like a lantern of old horn, like a candle seen through skin. As if the land, having finally noticed the stranger in its midst, had begun the slow arithmetic of her dissolution and found the sum.

"Tansy," Coral said quietly.

She didn't turn. "I felt it go," she said. "Hallowmere. Like a tooth coming out. Didn't even wake the children." Now she looked back at Coral, and her old face was calm, and her glow lit the snow around her feet in a soft ring. "It knows me now, Coral. The land. It's been polite about it for forty years, but politeness ends." She said it the way she reported everything — the price of eggs, the habits of gods. "I've maybe a season in me. Less, if we hurry. Which," and here the almost-smile arrived, fully formed at last, "we should."

"Tansy—"

"Hush." She raised a glowing hand, gentle as a benediction. "I've been waiting forty years to say this part, so hush and listen. The Door needs a Conduit — yes, you knew that. But a Conduit alone burns up opening it; the Door drinks conduits like wine. Unless—" her eyes were bright and ancient and absolutely certain, "—unless the Conduit carries something from *outside* the stolen land. An anchor-memory. Grass. Ocean. An unbroken sky. Something the god never owned, so the Door can't drink it, and the Conduit can stand in the threshold and live." She stepped closer, and her glow washed over Coral like shallow water. "I have such a memory, child. Forty years of them. And I mean to give you mine before the land takes the rest."

Coral's chest hurt. "Why me?"

"Because you're the only one mad enough to try, and kind enough to mean it." Tansy took Coral's face in her luminous hands, and her palms were warm, impossibly warm, warm as a kitchen with bread under the cloth and rain on the glass. "But not tonight. Tonight you'll sleep. Tomorrow we walk. And tomorrow night, or the next, when you're ready — I'll teach you the ocean."

Behind them, from the hay, a small voice piped up, sleep-thick and scared:

"Why is Tansy glowing?"

And in the silence that followed, at the edge of the village-shaped nothing, under the lid of the starless sky, the winter wind began to rise — and it carried, faint and far and unmistakable, the sound of singing.
