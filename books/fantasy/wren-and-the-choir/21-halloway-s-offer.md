## 21. Halloway's Offer

The snow had stopped by the time Coral noticed they were being followed.

Not followed—*herded*. That was the difference, and Coral should have known it sooner. For three days the crew's trail through the white country had felt too clean: no wardens at the ford where there might have been, no ambush in the pass where the ground begged for one. Someone was letting them walk. Someone was choosing the shape of their journey the way a shepherd chooses which fold to open next.

Coral slipped away from camp on the third evening, telling Sable only that they were going to check the snares. He'd watched them a moment too long before nodding—the particular stillness he wore when he suspected a lie and loved them anyway—and Coral had carried his silence out into the dark like an extra coat.

They found her at the shrine.

It was a small one, older than the unraveling: a stone alcove with a carved lintel worn smooth by centuries of weather that had, until recently, been real weather. The god's dreaming had not bothered to erase it. Inside, someone had built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and beside it, warming her gloved hands with the unhurried patience of a woman who has waited her whole life for exactly this conversation, sat Warden Halloway.

She did not look up when Coral stopped at the mouth of the alcove. She simply said, "You always did check the snares twice. Once for rabbits, once to be alone." A pause. "I used to pretend I didn't know why."

"You shouldn't have come here," Coral said. Their voice came out flat, which surprised them. They had rehearsed rage on the long road—rage was easier to carry than the other thing, the old thing, the thing with her name wrapped around it. But standing here, with snow melting off their shoulders and her face lit from below by firelight, all the rehearsed words fell out of them like coins through a torn pocket.

"No," Halloway agreed. "I shouldn't have. And yet."

She turned then, and Coral saw what seven years and a war against reality had done to her. She had always been lean; now she was carved, as if grief were a whetstone she'd been sharpening herself against since the night she'd reported them. Her warden's gray was patched with black where new cloth had been sewn over old, and at her throat hung the signet of her office—but Coral noticed her hands most of all. They were bare. Wardens wore gloves in the field, always, because conduits could be read by touch. She had taken hers off.

For Coral, or for herself. Perhaps both.

"Sit down," she said. "You're shivering."

"I'm not."

"You are. You always shivered when you lied, too. You thought nobody noticed." Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, something older and more wounded wearing a smile's clothes. "I counted every one. Seven years, and I can still count your lies."

Coral didn't sit. They stood in the falling hush outside the firelight's reach, half in the shrine and half out of it, which felt right. Which felt like their whole life.

"Where are your wardens?" they asked.

"Two miles back, holding the ford. Brannoc's ferry-rig is clever—I'll grant you that—but my people know how to watch a crossing without being seen watching it." She fed a twig to the fire. "Your friends are safe tonight. That's not a threat. It's a courtesy. I'm asking for one hour, Coral. One hour where no one sings, no one fights, and no one pretends."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no, and I let you walk back down that hill, and tomorrow we do this again with swords." She looked at them steadily. "But I don't think you'll say no. You've wanted this conversation as long as I have. You just couldn't have it while anyone else could see what it costs you."

The worst part was that she was correct. The worst part was that she had *always* been correct—that was the whole architecture of their old life, teacher and student, Warden and novice, two people who understood each other so completely that understanding itself became a cage.

Coral sat down across the fire from her. Not close. Never close. The fire cracked between them like a small argument neither would win.

* * *

"You've seen it," Halloway began, without preamble, because she had never wasted words on ceremony when substance would do. "Out past the Waystations. The land coming apart. Tell me what it looks like from inside your head."

"You know what it looks like."

"I want to hear it in your voice."

Coral stared at the flames. "Like a book with pages going blank. Like walking through a house where the rooms behind you stop existing. Yesterday we crossed a valley that was green on one side and gray static on the other, and the border between them moved while we watched. There was a farmhouse on the green side. By morning it will be nothing. Whatever family lived there—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Whatever family lived there already doesn't remember themselves."

"And the stars," Halloway said softly. "Tell me about the stars."

"They're lanterns," Coral said, before they could stop themselves. "Dimmed ones. Hanging in a ceiling that isn't a sky. I've known that since the night they went out. I feel them when I sleep—little fires banked low, all in a row, keeping something dark and enormous asleep underneath."

Halloway nodded slowly, as though Coral had confirmed a ledger she'd been keeping for years. "So you understand what's happening better than anyone alive. Then you understand that this ends only one of two ways. Either the god wakes—and everything inside this country dies screaming, unmade by a death-throes the founders built these walls specifically to prevent—or the god dies quietly, the memory finishes unraveling at its own pace, and whatever can be salvaged of the people gets salvaged." She leaned forward. "There is no third door, Coral. However much you want one."

"There's a Door," Coral said quietly.

"Don't."

"There's a Door, and it opens, and the people walk through into the real world—"

"*Into what?*" For the first time her voice cracked its casing, and beneath the Warden was the woman who had taught Coral to read resonance, whose hands had guided theirs through their first careful theft of memory. "Into a world that has never heard of Ashenmoor? Where they arrive with nothing—not history, not names that mean anything, not even a *sky* they recognize? Do you know what happens to a mind when the country that made it turns out to be a fiction? I've seen the refugees from the forgotten villages. They don't grieve, Coral. Grieving requires something to hold. They just... thin. They smile and thin and blow away. That is what the real world does to the people of a stolen country."

"It wouldn't have to be like that—"

"It would. Because that's what it's *for*." She drew a breath, and when she spoke again her voice had returned to its terrible calm. "The founders weren't monsters. Read the archives you've been digging through—they were desperate people doing arithmetic with the survival of everything. A dying god screams, and its scream unmakes the world. So they stole a country, folded it around the god like a bandage, and dimmed the sky to keep it sleeping. Cruel? Yes. Necessary? Also yes. And now the bandage is rotting, and you want to tear it off and drag forty thousand souls through a hole in the world because you've decided you know better than the people who built the prison."

"I'm not tearing anything off," Coral said. "I'm opening a way *out*."

"The same thing, from the inside."

"The same thing," Coral repeated, and laughed—a short, bitter sound that steamed in the cold. "From inside, yes. From inside, it's called rescue."

"Is it?" Halloway asked, and the question landed like a knife laid flat on a table. "Or is it called abandonment? Think about it truly, the way I taught you to think. Every person who walks through that Door leaves behind the only home their minds were ever woven from. They become exiles in reality—refugees from a country that never existed, carrying memories of a place the world will insist was never real. You've spent seven years among thieves and liars, so tell me: what happens to people with nowhere that recognizes them?"

"They find each other," Coral said. "That's what they do. I've watched it happen. A smuggler, a priest, twins, an old woman, a feral child—none of them belong anywhere, and they've built a home out of each other on the road while the ground disappeared behind us. If that's possible on the way to the Door, it's possible after it."

Something moved behind Halloway's eyes—something that looked, painfully, like pride. She smothered it the way she must have smothered it every day for seven years.

"You were the best student I ever had," she said. "Do you know why I reported you?"

Coral went very still.

"Because you were the best student I ever had, and the Rite would have destroyed you, and there was no other way to save you from it except the one that also broke you." Her voice stayed level but her hands, bare above the fire, had curled slightly inward, as if holding something invisible and precious. "You think I betrayed you out of duty. Duty was the *shape* of it. The heart of it was that I loved you, and love, in the Choir, was the first thing they taught us to spend. I spent you. I have regretted it in every hour of every day since, and I would do it again, because the alternative was watching them hollow you out for the god, and I could not." She looked up. "So do not stand there and tell me I don't understand sacrifice. I wrote the book you're trying to read."

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang to a sky with no stars in it, and got no answer.

"That's your offer, then," Coral said finally. "Surrender. Let it fade."

"My offer is this." Halloway reached into her coat and produced a document, folded once, sealed with gray wax. She set it on the stones between them, deliberately within Coral's reach and deliberately not pushed toward them. "Full pardon. Restoration of your standing—you'd be a Conduit of the Choir again, legitimate, protected. Your friends walk free; Brannoc, the twins, even the boy, no charges, no Gleaning. And Sable Fen's crimes expunged—he testified under coercion, that's in the record, I checked it myself years ago. He never has to know what you traded for it if you don't tell him." She met Coral's eyes. "In exchange, you come with me. You stop seeking the Door. You help me ease the god toward quiet death instead of waking—and when the last of the memory unravels, you and I walk out together through the border, the slow way, the honest way, and we let this country rest."

"You'd pardon everyone," Coral said slowly. "For me."

"For the only person in this country who can still hear the Choir," Halloway corrected. "Which is you. Which has always been you." A pause, and then, quieter, with the care of someone handling a live blade: "Yes. For you."

Coral looked at the seal. Gray wax, stamped with the sigil of the Wardens—a closed hand. Their name would be written somewhere inside in a clerk's careful hand, restored, forgiven, returned. Everything they'd lost at fifteen, handed back across a fire by the woman who'd taken it.

And it wasn't even a lie. That was the horror of it. Halloway believed every word. She had done the arithmetic honestly, with real numbers: the god must die quietly, the fewest people must suffer, the scream must never come. She had loved them for seven years across a gap of law and betrayal, and she had crossed that gap to offer them everything, and none of it was false.

It was simply wrong. It was wrong the way a perfect map of a nonexistent country is wrong.

"Halloway." Coral used her name—her first name, Ede, which they hadn't spoken aloud since they were children in the conservatory corridors, and saw her flinch as though struck. "Ede. The widow in Vell. The one whose wedding-day memory I stole in chapter—whose wedding-day memory I stole, years ago, and sold as a luck-charm. When I bought it back and gave it to her daughter, do you know what the daughter said? She held it against her chest and said, *now I know my mother was young once.* That's what memories are. That's what this country is. Yes, it was stolen. Yes, it's a fiction stitched around a dying god. But the people inside it were born in it, and loved in it, and buried their dead in it, and every single one of them is as real as you are. You can't do arithmetic with them. They're not numbers. They're the sum."

"The sum of what?" Halloway asked, harsh now. "If the god wakes, they're the sum of nothing. If the god fades gently, some of them survive to be poor and haunted in a world that doesn't know them. You keep talking about rescue as though rescue were a thing instead of a trade. I'm offering you the trade with the lowest cost. That's all any ruler ever offers. That's all mercy is, Coral—mercy is just arithmetic you can live with."

"Then we don't share a mathematics," Coral said. They reached forward and picked up the sealed document. Halloway's breath caught—for one heartbeat she believed, and Coral saw the belief bloom in her face like frost-flower, seven years of waiting cracking open—and then Coral held the parchment over the fire.

"Coral—"

"You told me once," they said, "that a conduit's first lesson is knowing what a memory weighs. You said everything has a weight, and the art is paying only what you can afford." The wax seal began to soften. "Here's my answer, weighed out."

They dropped it into the flames.

It caught all at once, the way documents do, curling and browning and going to ash with a softness almost like breathing. Halloway didn't move to save it. She watched it burn with an expression that Coral would carry for the rest of their life—which, given what Coral intended at the Door, might not be long—watched it with her bare hands open on her knees and her whole future as a merciful arithmetic burning down to gray flakes that rose on the heat and vanished.

"You always did burn things rather than put them down," she said at last. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "Your mother was the same."

"Don't," Coral whispered. "Don't you dare speak of her."

"Someone has to. You won't even look at the memory anymore—I've felt it in you, every time our paths have crossed, the way you flinch away from one particular shape in your hoard. You keep her locked in the deepest drawer and call it protection." Halloway rose, brushing ash from her gray coat, and for a moment she was again the tall figure in the conservatory doorway, saying *this is the last time I can protect you, child.* "I hope the Door is everything you believe. I hope you open it and forty thousand people pour through into the light and the god sleeps sweetly and no one pays anything. I hope it so hard that I am ashamed of hoping it, because if it fails—if that god so much as stirs on its way out—there will be nothing left of anyone, and it will be because a thief with good intentions couldn't bear to choose the smaller grief."

She stepped out past Coral into the snow. Paused there, her back to them, a gray shape against the gray dark.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "the pardon was real. The restoration was real. And if you change your mind before the end—come to the ford, any hour, any night. I'll leave the watch word as it was when you were twelve. You remember it."

*I remember everything,* Coral almost said. *That's the problem.*

What they said instead was nothing, and Halloway walked away downhill, and the snow took her footprints one by one behind her, erasing her passage with the same patient thoroughness with which the country was erasing itself, until there was no proof she had ever been there at all except the smell of smoke and the absence of a document and the ache in Coral's chest where two loves had pulled in opposite directions hard enough to leave a permanent mark.

Coral sat alone by the dying fire for a long time.

Then they climbed back down toward camp, where the snares were empty, where Sable waited with that too-long look in his eyes, where Pip slept curled around his bottles of weather like a dragon around gold. Where Tansy was fading a little more each day, the land noticing her now, and Maren learning to write left-handed, and Isolde keeping watch under a ceiling of banked lanterns pretending to be stars.

Where, in the morning, the whispers in Coral's blood would begin again—the map-fragments, the coordinates, pulling them onward through the white country toward the Conservatory, toward the Door, toward the price.

Toward the arithmetic they hadn't yet learned how to do.

And as Coral crested the last rise and saw the small brave lights of the camp below, they felt, for the first time since the stars went out, another whisper threaded among the map-fragments—faint, rhythmic, unmistakably a hymn. Not the god's voice. Something older and closer, rising from the direction of the Conservatory like smoke from a chimney:

someone else was singing the Choir.

And they were singing it wrong.
