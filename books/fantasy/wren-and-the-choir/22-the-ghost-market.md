## 22. The Ghost Market

The market did not exist until you needed it.

That was the first thing Coral had to explain to the others, huddled in the lee of a ruined tollhouse where the road forgot itself in a drift of gray static. *You don't find it,* they said. *You want something badly enough, and it finds you.* Tansy, wrapped in every layer she owned, nodded along like a woman hearing news of her childhood home.

The wanting began at dusk. They stood together at the edge of a field that was only half a field — furrows drawn in careful ink that faded toward the hedgerow like an artist losing interest — and each of them wanted something so plainly that the air grew thick with it. Brannoc wanted rope and tar. The twins wanted word of any road still whole. Pip wanted everything he could see and several things he couldn't. Sable wanted — Coral didn't look too closely at what Sable wanted; looking felt like trespassing. And Coral wanted a memory back, one small stolen thing sold seven years and a lifetime ago, and their want must have burned brightest of all, because the market came for them first.

It arrived the way fog arrives over water: no footsteps, no heralding, just a slow assembling of shapes where there had been nothing. Lanterns lit themselves one by one down a lane that hadn't been there, hung from poles of pale unseasoned wood, and beneath them stalls unfolded like moths from cocoons. The vendors were the 半-forgotten — people the unraveling land had chewed halfway through. A woman whose left side was smooth as an eggshell, features worn away, selling bottled birdsong with her one remaining hand. A man made mostly of outline, tending a brazier of coals that glowed without heat. Children who flickered when they moved, as though the world were deciding whether to keep them.

"They're not ghosts," Tansy said quietly, and her voice cracked on it. "They're just people the country hasn't finished forgetting. Same as me. Only faster."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't be a lie, and lies, in this place, had a way of becoming architecture.

* * *

They split into pairs, because a crowd drew attention and attention in the Ghost Market was currency someone else might spend. Brannoc took Maren — her arm bound and splinted since the ford, her face set in the particular stubbornness of the twins, who treated injury as a personal insult — and went hunting for rope, tar, and anything that would keep a boat whole on a river of static. Isolde went with Tansy, moving slow, letting the old woman set a pace she pretended not to need. Sable took Pip, which was either wisdom or folly; the boy could pick a lock with his eyelashes, but he'd also once tried to trade a bottled thunderstorm for a horse he couldn't ride.

Coral walked alone, hood up, hands in pockets, doing what they had always done best: being nobody.

The stalls sold what you would expect a dying country to sell. Jars of last summer, still warm. Names in little paper packets — *two for three coppers, miss, good strong ones, hardly used* — and Coral had to look away from those. A stall of doors, none attached to walls, all standing open onto different weather. A woman selling sleep in colors. An old man with a hand-cart full of yesterday, going cheap.

"Memories?" hissed a voice at Coral's elbow, and there was a vendor Coral knew by instinct if not by name: the trade had a smell, a certain greasy sweetness like wax and old sugar, and this creature reeked of it. It was shaped roughly like a person, roughly like a memory of a person, its face a smear. "Buying or selling? We've got weddings, we've got funerals, we've got first kisses, slightly damp. Finest quality grief. Or—" the smear leaned close, "—if you're a collector, I've got something rare."

"I'm not buying," Coral said, which was true, and also a lie, and both halves sat in their mouth like stones.

Because they had felt it. From three stalls away they had felt it, the way a mother feels a child crying in a crowded room: the widow's wedding day, the memory Coral had lifted seven years ago in Vell and sold to a charm-monger for rent money. It had been the first memory they'd ever sold rather than merely borrowed, and they had told themselves then what thieves always tell themselves — *she'll never miss it, she has ten thousand days, this one won't leave a hole* — and they had carried the lie so long it had grown comfortable, like a stone worn smooth in the pocket.

Now it called to them across a ghost market in a country that wasn't real, singing its small bright song: candlelight, fiddle music, a young woman laughing with her mouth full of honey cake, rain beginning like applause on a canvas roof.

"How much," Coral said, and hated how fast it came out.

* * *

Across the lane, Pip was conducting business.

"You can't just take things," Sable said, for perhaps the fortieth time since the boy had stowed away in the smugglers' hold, "even here. Especially here. Everything in this market is somebody's."

"It's not *taking*, it's *trading*." Pip held up his prize: a bottle no bigger than his thumb, stoppered with wax, in which something turned and muttered and flashed green-white-green. A storm. Not a big one — a squall, really, petulant and small — but storms were storms. He'd found the stall himself, traded two bottled fogs and a jar of honest river-mud ("it's *good* mud," he insisted, wounded) plus the buttons off his coat, and he had done it fair and square, which was more than most of the vendors could say.

"What do you even want with it?"

Pip shrugged with elaborate carelessness. "A person ought to have weather," he said. "In case."

Sable looked at him — this scrap of a child with soot under his nails and a storm in his fist, guarding it like other children guarded a favorite stone — and felt something in his chest go tight and complicated. He thought about saying something priestly. He had spent years being careful never to say anything priestly; the words had gone out of him like wine from a cracked flask, or so he told himself. But the hymn still lived somewhere under his breastbone, and sometimes, watching Pip, it hummed.

"In case of what?"

"In case of whatever," Pip said firmly, and pocketed the storm, and stole an apple anyway, because trading was trading but an apple was an apple.

Nearby, Tansy stood before a stall that sold nothing at all — a bare table, a single candle, a vendor who was only a voice. She and Isolde listened while it named prices in the old fashion, the way markets worked before money came north: a story for a story. Tansy paid in tales of the real world, the one outside, and Isolde watched the flickering faces around them lean in hungrily to hear. Grass, Tansy said. Oceans. A sky with stars in it that stayed put. Each tale bought them a little: passage-rights through lanes that shifted, a warning about which vendors lied, a small tin lamp that would burn on remembered light.

"You've been here before," Isolde said, not as a question.

"Once." Tansy's hands were translucent at the edges now, when the lantern-light caught them wrong. She kept them folded so no one would notice, except everyone noticed. "When I first understood what this country was. I came looking for a door out." Her mouth quirked. "Found the market instead. Found that even ghosts need to eat."

Isolde took her arm, gently, the way you steady something you are afraid will blow away. "Maren's asking after you. She wants to know if you remember the song about the ferryman's wife. She says your version's wrong."

"My version is *right*. Maren learned it off a man in Vell who learned it off a wall."

"She'll fight you."

"Good." Tansy smiled, and for a moment the lanterns seemed to brighten around her, as though the market liked her, as though the land was loath to let her go even now. "Keeps my blood moving. Come on, girl. Buy me something foolish before I'm too thin to carry it."

* * *

Coral counted out the price in memories.

That was the market's cruelty and its honesty: coin meant less than nothing here, so everything was barter, and what did Coral have to barter? Not their own past — that was hoarded like a dragon's bed, guarded against exactly this kind of spending. No. They had thousands of *other* people's yesterdays, lifted over seven years of quiet theft, and the market took them gladly. A fisherman's dawn. A grandmother's recipe, measured in handfuls. Someone's first snow, someone's last conversation with a brother, given up like drops from a jug.

Each one hurt going out, which Coral had not expected. In Vell, selling had been clean — a touch to the temple, a silver thread drawn out, sealed in glass, done. Here, in a country built of memory, every thread pulled left a sound behind, a faint note like a plucked string, and the whole market flinched when it sounded. The vendors turned. The flickering children stopped their games. Even the lanterns guttered.

"You shouldn't spend those here," said the smear-faced vendor softly, wrapping the wedding-memory in oilcloth with strange gentleness. "Not in a place made of remembering. Every one you give away, the country hears it. Starts wondering why some folk get to keep their heads full and others get emptied." Its non-face tilted. "Starts wondering who taught them to steal in the first place."

Coral's blood went cold. "Who taught—"

But the vendor had already begun folding itself away, stall collapsing inward like a sigh, and the last thing it said was less a sentence than a smell: *the Conservatory remembers you.*

Coral stood very still in the lantern-light with the small wrapped bundle in their hands, feeling its warmth through the oilcloth, feeling the fiddle music and the honey cake and the rain like applause. Seven years of telling themselves it hadn't mattered. Seven years of competence stacked on emptiness like bricks on a wound.

Somewhere across the market, Sable was watching them, and Coral did not know it, and Sable turned away first.

* * *

They regrouped at the tollhouse near midnight, laden and lighter by turns. Brannoc had rope enough to rig a cathedral and a keg of tar that whispered when you knocked on it. The twins had passage-rights scribbled on birch bark and, thanks to Tansy's storytelling, a lamp that glowed the color of a summer afternoon. Pip had his storm, hidden, smug. Sable had bread — actual bread, traded for a hymn sung low in a corner stall while a crowd of the half-forgotten wept silently around him — and he distributed it like a man distributing absolution.

And Coral had the bundle, and a problem.

Because the daughter was there.

They had seen her an hour ago, working a stall near the market's edge, and Coral's heart had stopped like a struck clock. Mara Underhill — no, not Underhill, Mara *Vess*, married name, widowed name — the widow's daughter from Vell, from the night market, from the ordinary theft that had begun all of this. Fled the city when the wards fell, obviously. Fled with the refugees pouring in from villages that had forgotten themselves, and ended up here, in a market of ghosts, selling her mother's luck-charms to buy porridge.

She was selling charms. Coral's stolen work, resold, and every one of them a piece of some stranger's joy.

"Mara Vess," Coral said.

The woman looked up. She had her mother's brow, Coral saw now, the same deep-set patience. Recognition came slowly, the way it does for someone you've only ever met in the dark of a market stall, hooded, anonymous, taking things. Then it came all at once, and her face did something complicated.

"You." Not anger. Something worse — bewilderment. "You bought from us. Mother said you had an honest touch, for a buyer." Her laugh was short and cracked. "Honest. And now the whole sky's fallen in and I'm selling luck to ghosts, and Mother—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Mother doesn't remember her own wedding. Did you know that? When the stars went out, it went with everything else. She sits by the fire and she asks me whose dress is hanging in her chest, and I have to tell her it's hers, and she looks at me like I'm kind to lie to her."

The bundle weighed nothing in Coral's hands. It weighed everything.

"I brought something back," Coral said.

They unwrapped the oilcloth between them, and the memory rose out of it like heat-shimmer: candlelight, fiddles, rain like applause, a young bride laughing with honey cake on her tongue. Mara stared. Her breath caught in a way that Coral recognized, having heard it a thousand times from buyers touching a beloved day for the first time in years.

"This is—" Mara's voice failed. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "This is hers. This is *hers*, I was *at* this, I dropped the ring and Uncle Aldous dove under the table for it—" She looked up at Coral with wet eyes and dawning, terrible understanding. "You had it. All these years. You *sold* it to us as a luck-charm and it was her *wedding day*—"

"Yes."

"Why?"

There was no answer that wasn't monstrous. *Because I was hungry. Because I was lonely. Because carrying other people's best moments is a weight and I found a way to set some of it down.* Coral said, "I was a thief. I'm trying — I don't know what I'm trying. But this is yours to give her. Touch your thumb to it and think of her, and it will go home. It will be as if she never lost it. Almost."

Mara took the memory the way you take a sleeping child from a stranger's arms: carefully, disbelievingly, with a fierce tenderness that made Coral look away. For a long moment there was only the lantern-hiss and the distant murmur of the market, and then Mara said, thick-voiced:

"The others. The ones you sold in Vell. Are there more? Could they go home too?"

"Some," Coral said. "Some sellers are dead. Some buyers scattered when the wards fell." They heard their own voice go flat and far away, the voice of a person reading an inventory of sins. "I kept records. I'll send them home. As many as I can reach."

Mara studied them for a long moment — the hood, the hollow eyes, the hands that had stolen her mother's joy and carried it seven years like a stone — and then, astonishingly, she reached out and gripped Coral's wrist, brief and hard.

"Mother used to say luck comes back around," she said. "Go home, thief. Whatever door you're walking toward — walk through it."

Then she was gone into the lantern-dark with her mother's wedding day cupped against her chest like a flame sheltered from wind, and Coral stood alone in the Ghost Market, lighter by one stone and heavier by everything else.

* * *

Sable found them at the market's edge, where the stalls thinned and the gray static waited beyond like a tide.

"Pip says you gave something away," he said. "Something big. He says you paid for it with — Coral, he says you paid with *memories*. Other people's. Dozens of them. Into *this*." He gestured at the hungry lantern-lit lane, at the flickering children, at the whole assembled want of the place.

"It was restitution."

"It was reckless. This country hears everything you spend. That vendor you talked to — I heard what it said. *Who taught them to steal.* Coral, if the land starts pulling that thread—"

"Let it pull." Coral's voice came out harder than intended. "Maybe it should. Maybe every thread in me should come unraveled and see what's left."

Sable went quiet. Around them the market murmured, buying and selling, the half-forgotten trading pieces of themselves to stay half-remembered. Somewhere a fiddle played a tune with holes in it, notes missing like teeth.

"That's not you talking," Sable said finally, gently, and Coral almost laughed, almost said *you don't know what's me, priest, you've never known*, but the words snagged on something — on the memory of his hymns in the flooded crypt, on the way he had said *I know your voice* — and the laugh died unborn.

Behind them, unnoticed, the smallest member of their ragged company crouched in the shadow of a stalled cart, listening with all his ears. Pip heard the word *restitution*. He heard *the Conservatory remembers you* repeated in Coral's low, troubled voice, and he heard Sable say, soft as confession:

"There's something I have to tell you. About the Conservatory. About that night, seven years ago. Before we reach the Door — you deserve to hear it from me, not from the ruins."

Pip's hand closed around the bottle in his pocket. Green-white-green flashed inside it, once, like a signal.

The storm, he thought. Maybe it was time to find out what a storm was worth.
