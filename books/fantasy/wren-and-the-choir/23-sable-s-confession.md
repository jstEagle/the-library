## 23. Sable's Confession

The ruin-chapel came out of the snow like a confession waiting to be made.

Sable saw it first — a gray tooth of masonry rising from the white slope, its bell tower snapped off at the shoulder the way so many towers were now, half-finished things in a half-finished country. The land had been unraveling faster each night. Two evenings ago the river they'd followed had simply stopped believing in itself mid-bend and become a seam of pale static hissing through the pines, and Brannoc had sworn in three languages while Isolde recalculated by dead-star arithmetic that no longer matched any sky. They needed walls. They needed a roof that still remembered being a roof.

"Chapel," Maren said, when they crested the rise. Her voice was flat with cold; her arm was bound tight against her chest where the ford had taken its toll. She looked at the broken tower the way sailors look at weather. "Consecrated ground. In a country that isn't real." She spat into the snow. "That's either the safest place left or the stupidest."

"It's shelter," Brannoc said, and that was the end of the argument, because Brannoc's pronouncements always were, delivered in that ferryman's voice that expected water to hold.

Sable said nothing at all. He was looking at the chapel door, at the faded carving above the lintel — the seven-note spiral, the sigil of the Choir — and he felt the old guilt shift in his chest like ballast, the way it had shifted every day since the flooded crypt, since he'd looked up from his forbidden hymn into a pair of gray eyes that resonated against his voice like a struck bell answering a struck bell.

He had told himself it was recognition. He had told himself a great many things.

Inside, the chapel was dry. That alone made it holy as far as Tansy was concerned; she settled onto a fallen pew with the groan of an old woman who had been aging wrong for seventy years and had opinions about it. Pip went straight to the altar and started investigating the hollows behind it for anything stealable or interesting, which for Pip amounted to the same category. Isolde and Maren claimed the corner near the collapsed wall, where they could see the sky — what there was of it, that colorless unstarred nothing — and Brannoc got a fire going from the chapel's own ruined pews, which Maren called sacrilege and Brannoc called practical theology.

Coral stood in the doorway a long moment before coming in. Snow in their dark hair. Their breath hanging around them like something they were reluctant to put down.

They had barely spoken to him in four days. Since the barn, since the night of stories — no, longer than that, since the ford, since Coral had opened themselves like a wound and poured out whatever conduit-magic had saved Maren's life, and Sable had watched them do it and thought: *I know that voice. I have always known that voice.* Since before the ford, even. Since the crypt, if he was honest, and he was trying, lately, very hard, to be honest.

He had run out of road. That was the truth of it. There was only the chapel ahead, and Coral inside it, and the snow falling on a stolen country, and the thing in his chest that he had carried for seven years like a stone sewn behind his ribs.

He waited until the fire was low and the others had gone to their bedrolls. Tansy slept sitting up, hands folded, looking disconcertingly like a saint in an old painting, the kind Sable had once been trained to make. Pip was a small snoring heap of coats near Brannoc. The twins breathed in tandem across the chapel, as they did everything.

Coral sat apart, near the window, keeping watch the way they always kept watch — not because anyone asked, but because sleep was a country Coral had never trusted. The firelight moved on their face. Outside, the snow came down without wind, in that terrible patient way the winter here had, as if the sky were slowly emptying itself of everything it had ever held.

"Coral."

Their eyes came to him. Gray, watchful, tired. They didn't answer. They had learned, somewhere in seven years of Vell, not to answer to anything until they knew why it wanted them.

"I need to tell you something," Sable said. "And you're going to want me to stop. And I'm asking you — please don't let me stop. Because if you let me stop now, I'll find a reason tomorrow too, and the day after, and I'll die with it still in my mouth."

Coral's head tilted, very slightly. In the firelight their face was all planes and shadows, a face made for keeping secrets. "You've been rehearsing this," they said quietly. Not a question.

"Since the barn." He swallowed. "Since the ford. Since—" He laughed, a short ugly sound. "Since the crypt, if I'm honest, and I promised myself honesty tonight, so. Since the crypt. Maybe since the conservatory. Seven years, Coral. I have been rehearsing this for seven years."

The word landed between them. *Conservatory.* Coral went very still — that particular stillness Sable had learned to read, the stillness of someone making sure their body wouldn't betray them before their mind decided what to show.

"Say it," Coral said.

* * *

He should tell it properly. He owed it that much. But stories, Sable had learned in his years as a priest and then as a liar — which had been the same years — are how cowards hide. So he would not tell it as a story. He would say it plain, in pieces, and let the pieces be sharp.

"You remember the Rite you refused," he said. "Of course you do. You remember the hearing after — the inquiry. Eleven witnesses. Choir elders, your teachers, your— your friends. People who stood up one by one and swore that you had been corrupted, that you were unstable, that you had conspired to take the Rite's power for yourself rather than give it back to the god as the liturgy demanded." His voice was steady. He made it be steady. "Ten of those witnesses were summoned. Pressured. Told what to say."

Coral had not moved. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the walls, the old wood settled, remembering its weight.

"And the eleventh?" Coral asked. Their voice was almost gentle, and that gentleness was worse than anger, because Sable recognized it — it was the kindness Coral showed to strangers they were about to pick clean. Distance disguised as mercy.

"The eleventh volunteered." Sable's hands were shaking. He looked at them, at his own traitor hands, and folded them in his lap like a man at prayer, which was the oldest lie he knew. "I was a deacon then. Twenty-two years old. I had just been elevated to the Cantorate — the youngest in a generation — and the elders came to me and said, Fen, we need someone whose ear is beyond question. Someone who can testify to the resonance. Because the accusation wasn't just about what you did, it was about what you *are*, and only another singer could confirm the signature." He closed his eyes. "They played me a recording. A memory-vessel from the night you refused the Rite. Your voice, caught in the stone. And I listened to it, and I heard what they told me to hear — the corruption, the selfishness, the fracture — and I stood up in front of the whole Consistory and I sang the counter-tone, the formal testimony, and my voice fit against yours perfectly. That's how they knew I was telling the truth, Coral. That's how testimony works. You can't fake resonance." He opened his eyes. "It fit because it was true. Not the lies. The *fit*. My voice has known yours since the first hour we sang together in the novice gallery, and I used that. I took the truest thing in me and I aimed it like a weapon, and you ran, and I was promoted, and I told myself for seven years that I had done my duty to the god."

Coral rose. It was not sudden. That was the horror of it — they unfolded from the window seat with the unhurried grace of someone leaving a room they had already left long ago, and the firelight slid off them, and Sable's testimony hung in the air between them, complete, useless, damning.

"The flooded crypt," Coral said. "In Vell. When you sang the hymns. When you looked at me in the crowd."

"I knew you," Sable said. "Not your face — your face I might never have known, you've remade yourself so thoroughly. But you began to sing along, under your breath, below hearing, and your note found mine across that room like—" His throat closed. "Like it had been waiting. And I knew. God forgive me, Coral, I *knew*, and I followed you out into the market, and I introduced myself as a stranger, a runaway priest, a fellow exile, and I let you believe it was chance. I built our whole — everything — on top of the same lie I told the Consistory. That coincidence was covering for guilt. It always does. Guilt is the best disguise there is, because it makes you helpful, it makes you devoted, it makes you stand watch all night and carry the wounded and never ask for anything back."

"Why?" The word came out of Coral stripped bare. No performance in it at all, and somehow that was the cruelest sound Sable had ever heard from them. "Why did you volunteer? What did I ever do to you?"

"Nothing." The word broke in half on the way out. "Nothing, that's the whole of it, that's the poison of it. You did *nothing*. We shared a gallery bench for two years and you never learned my name and I loved you anyway, silently, the way novices love — badly, at distance, in secret — and when the elders asked for a witness I said yes because I thought, if I am close to this, if I am *useful* in this, then I will matter to the story. To you. Some rotten corner of me believed that testifying would bind us together, that being part of your fall was better than being nothing in your life." He laughed again, and it came out wet. "And then you fled, and the story went on without me, and I discovered there is no worse hell than being important to a tragedy that has forgotten you. So I drank. I was defrocked. I ended up singing hymns to smugglers in a flooded crypt for copper, and every night I sang, some part of me was listening for you. For seven years. And then you walked into my audience wearing someone else's name, and my voice reached for yours before my mind could stop it, and I thought: *at last. A chance to fix it.*"

"So everything," Coral said slowly, "the crew, the road, the Waystation, the chapel — you arranged to be near me."

"No." He stood up too, desperate now, the words tumbling. "No — yes — Coral, I swear to you on what's left of my god, I never planned a single step of this. I followed you because following you was the only prayer I had left. Everything else — Brannoc, the twins, Tansy, the Door, all of it — that happened *to* us, the way real things happen. That part was true. I need you to believe that part was true."

"How can I?" Coral's voice didn't rise. It went quieter, which was so much worse. "How can I believe any of it? Every time you looked at me on this road, every time you sang beside me at the Waystation, every time—" They stopped. Something crossed their face, fast, and was gone, and Sable understood with a physical pain what had almost been said and would now never be said, not tonight, maybe not ever: *every time you touched my hand.*

"I know," he whispered. "I know what I've done. I've taken the one true thing I ever built and made it impossible to trust. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to know it. That's all confession is, in the end — not absolution. Just putting the weight down where someone else can see it." He spread his empty hands. "There. Now you've seen it. Seven years of it. Do what you want with me."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The snow ticked softly against the broken window. The fire ate the last of the pew-wood. And Coral looked at him with those gray eyes that had seen through every ward in Vell, and Sable waited to be condemned or forgiven or struck, and found that all three would have been easier than what actually happened, which was:

Nothing. Coral simply turned and walked out the chapel door, into the snow, without a word, without even taking their coat.

* * *

Sable stood in the doorway and did not follow.

Every instinct screamed at him to go — into the cold, after them, into the white silence where their footprints were already filling. But he had spent seven years inserting himself where he was not wanted, shaping outcomes, arranging coincidences, and he understood with terrible clarity that following Coral into that snow would be one more act of the same sin: refusing to let them have a single feeling that didn't include him.

So he stood in the doorway, letting the cold pour over him, watching the small dark figure move away down the white slope toward the treeline, and he prayed. Not to the dying god under the world, and not to the absent saints of the Choir. Just the raw wordless pleading of a man who had finally told the truth and discovered that truth, unaccompanied, is not music at all. It is just noise. It needs two voices to become a hymn.

Behind him, a floorboard creaked. Tansy, awake, watching him from her pew with eyes that had seen a real sky.

"She'll come back," the old woman said. "Or she won't. Either way, lad, you did the harder thing tonight, and the land knows it." She nodded past him, at the snow, and Sable turned —

— and saw it. Far off, where Coral had vanished into the trees, the darkness between the trunks had begun to shimmer. Not static. Not unraveling.

Light. A faint, pulsing, unmistakably golden light, rising from the frozen forest like something answering a call — or like something that had finally noticed the last Conduit walking alone beneath the stars that weren't there.

Tansy's hand closed hard on his arm, her grip shockingly strong for a woman who was fading out of the world.

"That," she said, "is no place she should be alone."
