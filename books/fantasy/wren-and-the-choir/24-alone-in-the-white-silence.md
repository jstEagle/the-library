## 24. Alone in the White Silence

The snow did not fall so much as arrive.

Coral had walked out of the ruin-chapel without a coat, without a pack, without looking back at Sable's face — because if they had looked back, they would have seen him standing there with his hands open like a man offering bread to someone who had just learned it was poisoned, and they would have gone to him, and they could not go to him. Not yet. Maybe not ever. So instead they had walked into the white, and the white had swallowed them gently, the way a mother swallows a child's cry by humming.

Three days ago the world had ended in green fire and Tansy's light. Now it ended again in snow.

They walked because walking was the only prayer they knew that required no words. Their boots left prints that filled behind them almost before they were made, as though the land were embarrassed by evidence of passage, as though even footprints were memories it could no longer afford to keep. The trees here were wrong — Coral noticed it distantly, the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn sung by someone you love. They were birches, but their bark bore no marks at all, smooth as candle-wax from crown to root. Unwritten trees. Trees that had never been touched, never carved, never climbed, never burned. The unraveling had eaten the history right out of them and left only the shape.

*Keep walking*, Coral told themselves. *The cold will make the decision for you eventually.*

That was the truth of why they had come out here, and they knew it, and they let themselves know it: they had walked into the white silence to die, or to stop being the person who had been lied to for seven years and loved anyway, or simply to be somewhere no one could find them — not Halloway, not Pip, not the crew, not Sable Fen with his beautiful ruined voice and his beautiful ruined honesty. Somewhere the word *testimony* could not follow them.

The wind found the seam of their collar and slipped in like a knife learning its work. Coral pulled their stolen coat tighter — one of the few things they'd carried from Vell, bought with a memory of a harvest festival — and thought, absurdly, of the widow Underhill's wedding day, the charm they'd lifted in chapter one of their own long crime. *The smell of orange peel and beeswax. His hand shaking on mine during the vows.* They still carried it, wrapped in oilcloth against their heart, bought back at the Ghost Market for three charms' worth of another woman's first frost. Restitution was a strange currency. It didn't buy forgiveness; it only bought the right to keep walking.

*You are very far from anything*, said something that was not the wind.

Coral stopped.

The snow kept arriving. The birches stood in their unwritten rows. And beneath everything — beneath the wind, beneath the creak of frozen branches, beneath Coral's own heartbeat hammering its small urgent telegraph against their ribs — there was a voice. Not heard, exactly. More remembered than heard, the way you remember a room you dreamed once: the shape of it present, the details already going.

"Who's there?" Their breath came out in a plume that hung too long in the air, refusing to disperse, as though even their exhale wanted to stay and listen.

*You know who,* said the voice. *You have been carrying pieces of me since you were nine years old.*

* * *

Coral had felt the god before. Everyone in Ashenmoor had, whether they knew it or not — in the way the stars had hung overhead like lanterns in a sickroom, dimmed and patient; in the way the land sometimes felt *watched* rather than merely inhabited. Since the stars went out, Coral had heard whispers in their stolen memories, map-fragments murmuring coordinates, the god's mind leaking through the hoard they'd spent seven years building inside their own skull. But always at a distance. Always through glass. Always mediated by other people's best moments, the god's presence diluted across ten thousand borrowed joys like ink in a flooded river.

This was not through glass.

This was a child sitting down beside them in the snow.

*I'm sorry about the cold,* the voice said, and it sounded genuinely sorry, and it sounded genuinely young. *I don't control the winter anymore. I used to be able to make it spring whenever I wanted. I used to be able to do anything. That was the whole problem.*

Coral's knees gave out — not from fear, they realized, but from exhaustion, from three days of walking on grief and guilt, from the sheer relief of a voice that asked nothing of them. They sat down hard in the drift, and the snow received them like a pew.

"You're supposed to be monstrous," they said aloud. Their voice cracked in the middle, embarrassingly human. "Sable's archives said— the founders wrote that your death-scream would unmake the world. That you had to be locked away. That the whole country—" They stopped. Started again. "That Ashenmoor is your prison."

*Yes,* the god said. *And also it is my blanket. Those are both true. Nobody who wrote about me ever held both truths at once. It's very heavy, holding two truths. I don't recommend it.*

Something in Coral's chest, wound tight since the chapel, loosened by one impossible degree. "You're joking," they said. "You're a dying god and you're making jokes."

*What else is there to do?* The voice rippled, and for a moment the snowfall around Coral shimmered — not stopping, but stuttering, like a singer catching breath between verses. In the stutter, Coral saw the field around them as it had been: summer, tall grass, a stone wall, sheep like scattered clouds. One heartbeat of remembered green. Then the winter closed over it again like water over a dropped coin. *I have been alone in here for longer than your country has had a name. Humor is the last thing I built that hasn't unraveled. Please don't take it away from me too.*

"What do you want from me?"

There was a long silence. Snow arrived. Somewhere far off, a branch cracked under its own accumulating weight, and Coral flinched, and hated themselves for flinching.

*Nobody has asked me that,* the god said at last, wonderingly. *Do you know that? The founders asked what I could do. The wardens ask where I am. The priest asks how to reach me. You are the first person in all this time to ask what I want.* Another pause, and when the voice came again it was smaller, the way voices get smaller when they are about to say the true thing. *I want out. But not the way Quill wanted out — she wanted to use me as a door handle. Not the way the founders feared — I don't want to scream. I have been holding my scream in my cupped hands for centuries like a bird I'm afraid to crush. I am so tired, Conduit. I want to be free, and I cannot be free, and those are the same sentence, and I don't know how to live inside a sentence like that.*

Coral stared at the snow. "Why me? Why now?"

*Because you are full of holes,* the god said, with terrible gentleness. *Every conduit is. That's what the gift is — we pour ourselves into the empty places. But you, Coral Underhill, you have been pouring yourself out for seven years and filling the holes with strangers' weddings and strangers' harvests and strangers' mothers' lullabies. You are the leakiest vessel in my prison. Which means you are also the most porous wall in it. I can reach you the way water reaches a shore — not through strength. Through seepage.*

"And the others? Sable hears the Choir. Halloway trained me. Why aren't you whispering to them?"

*Sable I cannot reach. He sealed himself too well — guilt makes excellent mortar, did you know that? He built his penance brick by brick and it keeps the cold out and me out with it. And the Warden—* The voice shuddered, and the temperature seemed to drop with it. *The Warden hears me perfectly. She has always heard me. She hears me every night and calls it doubt, and prays it away, and prays it away, and prays it away. Do not pity her. Pity is what she would hate most. But understand her. She loves you, and she believes love is a debt she pays in cruelty. I have watched her carry that arithmetic since she was younger than you are now.*

Coral's throat tightened. "Don't talk about her. Don't talk about any of them." The words came out harder than intended, edged with chapel-ruin and snow and Sable's open hands. "Everyone I've trusted lately turns out to be someone else underneath."

*I know,* said the god. *I testified against no one. I only want to say: I know.*

* * *

The light began to fail — although, Coral reflected, watching the gray deepen toward violet, it hardly mattered in a country where the stars had gone out. Dusk without stars was just a longer kind of dark, unbroken, undecorated. They should build a fire. They knew this. Hypothermia did not care about heartbreak; it would take them as efficiently as it took anyone, and then the crew would find their body frozen mid-stride somewhere north of nowhere, and Pip —

Pip.

Coral pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. Pip, who had followed them into the sewers and out of the city and across the gray country, who bottled weather in stolen jars and called it magic with the fierce defiance of a boy who'd been told he had none. Pip, who had cried himself silent in the barn during the long winter nights and pretended he hadn't. If Coral lay down in this snow and let the white take them, Pip would lose another person. Pip, who had already lost whatever family came before the streets of Vell, whose losses predated Coral and would outlast them unless —

Unless Coral got up. Unless Coral built the fire. Unless Coral walked back tomorrow, or the day after, and faced Sable, or didn't face him but stood in the same ruin-chapel breathing the same air, which was its own kind of terrible beginning.

"Damn you," Coral said to the god, and meant it as gratitude. "You could have let me lie here."

*And miss the conversation?* A ripple of something that might have been laughter moved through the snowfield, soft as wind over wheat. Then it faded, and the voice that returned was serious again, and young, and afraid. *Conduit. There is something I must show you, and I need you to promise you won't run. Running is what everyone does when they see it. Even the founders ran, and they were the ones who built the cage.*

"Show me what?"

*Me.*

The snow stopped.

Not slowed — stopped, each flake hanging motionless in the violet dusk like a held breath, thousands upon thousands of suspended stars in miniature, and Coral understood suddenly, with a lurch, why the sky above Ashenmoor had gone dark. The stars hadn't gone out. They had come *here*. Every light the stolen country had ever owned was falling inward, drifting down through the layers of the unraveling memory like sediment sinking through still water, gathering toward the center, gathering toward—

Toward the prison-heart under the Conservatory, where the dimmed stars hung like lanterns in a chamber of suspended memory. Where the Door waited.

And in the midst of the frozen snowfall, something appeared. Not a figure, exactly. More an absence shaped like a child — a silhouette cut out of the world, through which Coral could see the summer field that wasn't there anymore, the grass, the stone wall, the sheep like clouds. A child-shaped window onto everything that had been lost. When it spoke, its voice no longer came from everywhere. It came from very close, the way a voice comes when its owner is kneeling beside you in the snow, close enough to touch.

*This is what I actually look like,* the god said. *A frightened thing in a locked room. The founders saw a monster because they needed a monster — you cannot steal a country from the world to imprison something innocent. The guilt would eat them alive. So they made me monstrous in the telling, and the telling became the wardens' doctrine, and the doctrine became the Rites, and the Rites became your childhood, Coral. You were raised on a lie with my name on it.*

"I refused the Rite," Coral whispered. It was the oldest fact of their life, load-bearing, foundational. "Seven years ago. I wouldn't help feed you. I ran."

*I remember.* The absence-child tilted its head. *Do you know what I remember about that day? Everyone assumed I raged. The masters spoke of my wrath for years. But I remember a terrified child at a window, watching another child walk out the gates into the snow, thinking: go, go, get out, get anywhere that isn't here. I cheered for you, Conduit. I have been cheering for seven years.*

Coral broke.

It was not dignified. It was not lyrical. They folded forward over their knees in the stopped snow and sobbed like a child themselves, seven years of vigilance and loneliness and stolen joy and stolen grief coming up out of them all at once, and the god did not tell them to be quiet, and the god did not tell them to be brave. It only knelt beside them in its window-shape, close enough that its almost-presence warmed them like a hand hovering just above a burn.

When Coral could speak again, the words came out raw and small. "Sable testified against me. At the Conservatory. He was there, and he spoke against me, and he let me believe for months that he found me by accident." They wiped their face with a sleeve already stiff with ice. "How am I supposed to forgive that?"

*Are you asking me?* The god sounded faintly amused and entirely gentle. *I am the creature everyone forgave by forgetting. My opinion on forgiveness is worthless. But I will tell you what I know, which is this: a prison gets smaller every year, until the walls are the same size as the prisoner. He has been in his. You have been in yours. The Warden lives in hers and calls it the law.* A pause. *The Door is open now, Conduit. All of you could walk out. None of you can carry your walls through it.*

The snow began to fall again, slowly, each flake remembering its purpose. The violet dark deepened. Far to the south, Coral thought they could see — or imagined they could see, or the god let them see — a smudge of orange against the horizon where a ruin-chapel fire might be burning, where a crew might be worrying, where a boy with jars of bottled weather might be standing watch and pretending not to cry.

"He begged me to hear him out," Coral said. "In the chapel. Before I left. He said he'd testify again — against the Choir, against the Conservatory, in front of anyone — if I'd just listen to why."

*Will you?*

"I don't know." Coral laughed, wet and cracked. "You're a dying god imprisoned under a school and you're giving me relationship advice."

*Someone has to,* said the god. *Everyone else in this country is busy stealing, warden-ing, or unraveling.*

But the joke faded quickly, and what remained in the god's voice was the other thing, the heavier thing, the thing it had knelt down here to say. *Coral. Listen to me now, because the winter is coming faster than your friends know, and the unraveling is eating miles every night, and soon there will be no more conversations, mine or anyone's. What I showed you tonight — the child in the locked room — hold onto it. When you stand before me at last, and you will stand before me, at the Door, with the whole choir of your stolen life in your hands — remember that I am not the monster from the founders' telling. Remember that I am afraid. Because the choice that is coming will look different, depending on which of us you see when you look at me. Monster, or child.*

"Choice," Coral repeated. The word landed in their stomach like a stone dropped down a well. "What choice?"

The god rose — the absence-shape lengthening, unfolding, becoming less like a child and more like a doorway itself, a door-shaped window onto the lost summer field. Through it, impossibly, Coral smelled cut grass and warm stone and sheep-dust, the whole buried country exhaling one last breath of itself.

*Soon,* the god said. *Rest tonight. Walk south tomorrow. And Conduit—* the voice softened to almost nothing, a child's whisper at the edge of sleep, *—when you go back to them, to the priest and the boy and the ferryman and the twins — go back. That's the first choice. Everything else is only arithmetic.*

Then the field was gone, and the snow fell ordinary and cold, and Coral Underhill sat alone in the vast white silence with a fire to build and a road to walk and, for the first time in their life, the exact weight of the god on whose grave-country they had been born.

They got up. They gathered wood from the unwritten trees — deadfall, mercifully, branches that had never been carved and therefore had nothing left to forget. The fire caught on the second strike of their flint, and its warmth was real, and real things, Coral was learning, were worth walking back for.

South, then. Toward the smudge of orange. Toward the open hands.

Behind them, unnoticed, the first of the falling stars struck the snow at the edge of the firelight — a bead of dim gold, cooling, waiting — and began, very softly, to sing.
