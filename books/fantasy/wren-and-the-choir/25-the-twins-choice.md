## 25. The Twins' Choice

### ISOLDE

The snow came in the night without sound, the way everything came now — no wind to carry it, no clouds to birth it. It simply arrived, as if the sky had remembered snow and decided to practice.

Isolde woke with her sister's name half-spoken and found the barn rafters furred white with frost, and Maren awake beside her, staring at her own bandaged hand where it lay on the blanket like something that had been left there by mistake.

"It's early," Maren said. "Winter."

"I know."

"Early even for a country that's coming apart."

Isolde said nothing, because there was nothing true to say that wasn't cruel. Outside, the gray pressed against the barn's one window like a face at glass. The land beyond had been a valley of farms when they'd made camp; this morning it was a valley of suggestions, hedgerows drawn in pencil, a river that ran only when you weren't looking directly at it. Somewhere out there a rooster crowed at intervals too regular to be alive.

Maren flexed the fingers of her ruined hand. The wardens' blades at the ford had not cut clean — nothing here cut clean anymore — and what the surgeon among the refugees had saved, he'd saved crooked. Three fingers curled like question marks. The other two were wrapped and still, and Isolde knew without asking that they would never move again.

Maren had been their navigator. Had steered by dead stars through channels no living chart remembered, because the twins had learned their trade running contraband along coasts that only existed at certain tides, under stars that had burned out ten thousand years before anyone thought to sail beneath them. You didn't need living light if you memorized where the dead light used to fall. That was the whole secret of the twins' craft: the past was reliable in a way the present had never managed to be.

And now Maren couldn't hold a tiller, let alone a sextant.

"We should talk about it," Isolde said.

"I know we should."

"The wintering. There's a town back east of the ford — half-remembered, but the granary was solid. Brannoc could run us downriver before the water forgets itself. We shelter, we wait out the cold, we—"

"Maren," she almost said. She caught herself. It was an old habit, speaking for them both, one self split into a convenient plural. But Maren was right here, watching her, waiting to be allowed her own sentence.

"—we come back in spring," Maren finished quietly. "When the thaw comes. If it comes."

"If."

"You think I can't do it." Not bitter. Just tired, the way stone is tired.

Isolde took her sister's good hand. "I think winter kills people who are whole. I think you bled half a river into static three weeks ago. I think Tansy is dying in the next room and Coral walks around talking to a god inside their own skull, and Sable prays to a Choir that doesn't exist, and Pip—" she stopped. "I love them all. I also want my sister alive more than I want any of them to succeed."

"That's honest."

"One of us has to be."

Maren laughed — a small, cracked thing, but real. Then her eyes went past Isolde's shoulder, to the window, and the laugh died in her throat.

"Tansy's outside," she said. "In the snow. Barefoot."

* * *

### MAREN

Maren felt the cold as a rumor rather than a fact. The fever that had lived in her since the ford had rearranged her relationship with temperature; heat and cold were both just weather happening to someone nearby. So when she stood in the barn doorway with Isolde's arm under hers and watched old Tansy walk out into the fresh snow, what struck her wasn't the woman's bare feet going blue.

It was how the snow behaved around her.

It fell everywhere else. Around Tansy, it hesitated — flakes hanging a half-breath longer than they should, drifting wide of her shoulders as though the storm itself wasn't sure she was there. And where her bare feet pressed the ground, the prints filled in too fast. Gray creeping up from below, smooth as poured milk, erasing each step before the next one landed.

"She's fading," Maren said. Her voice came out flat. She hated that. Some things deserved to be said shaking.

"She's what?" Isolde whispered.

"Not dying. Fading. Look at her edges."

Tansy reached the fence line at the field's edge — a fence that was only a fence on its western posts; the eastern ones trailed off into sketch-lines, rails dissolving into suggestion — and stopped. Stood very straight. Raised her face to the starless dark the way other old women raised theirs to a sunrise.

Then she began to sing.

Not the Choir. Maren had heard Coral's magic, had felt it crawl through her blood at the ford like a second heartbeat, and this wasn't that. This was smaller. A work-song, maybe, or a lullaby, in a language none of them spoke — round vowels, a rhythm like oars. Tansy's voice was thin and rusted and absolutely unashamed.

The land listened. Maren felt it listening, the way you feel a held breath. For the length of the song the fence sharpened. The eastern posts remembered themselves into wood. Snow fell honestly, stupidly, beautifully, all around the old woman who did not belong to this world and never had.

When the song ended, the sharpening faded like a tide going out. Tansy turned, saw them both in the doorway, and smiled as if caught doing nothing worse than stealing jam.

"Cold out," she said. "Come warm an old woman's hands."

* * *

### ISOLDE

They sat her by the stove and wrapped her feet and pretended not to notice that the wool blankets seemed reluctant to touch her, settling over her legs the way cloth settles over a chair. Tansy accepted the tea Brannoc brewed and held it in both palms without drinking, warming herself on its warmth.

"You shouldn't have gone out like that," Isolde said, gentler than she meant to. "Barefoot. In the cold."

"The cold doesn't trouble me much lately." Tansy said it the way you'd say *the rain doesn't trouble me much lately*, a note in passing. "I wanted to see the snow. Where I come from — the real place, the one outside — we had snow like this. First snow of the year. My mother used to stand me in it barefoot, one minute only, so I'd remember what winter tasted like." She smiled at some private distance. "I've been tasting it again these last days. Everything real comes back to me now, sharper and sharper. Like the land is finally looking at me properly and I'm remembering myself to meet its eye."

"What happens," Maren asked from her cot, "when it finishes looking?"

Tansy turned her cup slowly. Steam rose from it and bent strangely, toward her, as though even warmth was being drawn home.

"I go somewhere else," she said simply. "I don't think it hurts. I think it's like the moment between songs, when the last note is still in the room but the singer's already breathing in. I'll be a memory of a stranger, in a country made of memories. Honestly, dear, I've been a stranger here seventy years. This is just the land catching up to what everyone always knew."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, the rooster crowed its dead regular crow.

"You knew," Isolde said. "You've known for weeks. Since before the ford. You walked into a battle line at eighty years old knowing the land was eating you alive, and you said nothing."

"I said plenty. Nobody listens to old women until they're useful or dead. I was hoping to manage both at once."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in grief. In the corner, Sable looked up from his broken hymnal with wet eyes and said nothing, because there was no rite in any of his books for this.

"And the Door," Maren pressed, sitting up despite the fever-slick shine on her forehead. "The key. Your memory of the real world. If you fade before we reach the Conservatory—"

"Then Coral sings without it, and the Door takes them instead of taking nothing." Tansy said it without flinching, which was somehow worse. "Or the whole errand dies, and everyone in Ashenmoor goes out with it, gentle as a candle. There's no version of this story, child, where I make it to spring. There may not be a version where I make it to the next village. What there is, is tonight, and this stove, and you two sitting there deciding whether you're leaving."

Isolde went hot with shame. "We never said—"

"You didn't have to. I've raised sailors." Tansy's eyes, milky at the rims, moved from one twin to the other with terrible kindness. "A ship takes on damage, the crew starts pricing the lifeboats. It's not cowardice. It's arithmetic. So do your sums out loud, girls. Let's hear them."

* * *

### MAREN

So Maren did the sums. Because Tansy had asked, and because lying to the dying was a debt you paid interest on forever.

"If we leave," she said, "we live. Probably. The east country's holding its shape better — the unraveling runs westward, mostly, following whatever the unraveling follows. Brannoc knows the river. Even one-handed, I can read the dead stars well enough to keep us off the shoals. By midwinter we're in a granary town with stores, and by spring—" she faltered. "By spring either this is over, one way or another, or it isn't, and we've lost nothing but our fare."

"And if you stay?" Tansy asked softly.

"If we stay, we follow Coral to the Conservatory. Through whatever's left of the map. With a helmswoman who can't hold a line and a guide who's—" Maren looked at her sister. "With what we have. And we probably die. Not gloriously. Coldly, stupidly, somewhere between here and there, in a country that won't even leave our bodies behind for anyone to bury."

She hadn't meant to say the last part aloud. The barn went quiet around it.

"That's the sum," Isolde said hoarsely. "That's the honest sum."

"Yes," Tansy agreed. "Now here's mine."

She set down the cup. She leaned forward, and Maren watched the lamplight pass through the edge of the old woman's shoulder — actually pass through, faintly, like sun through smoke — and understood with a lurch that they were having this conversation on borrowed time measured in hours, maybe days, and that Tansy had spent those hours not on fear or bargaining but on getting two stubborn smugglers to look at what they loved.

"When I was young — truly young, over there, in the real world — I ran away from an arranged marriage on a fishing boat out of a harbor whose name I'd give a great deal to say correctly one more time." Her voice was steady. "The captain took me on knowing he'd hang for it. No reason. No debt. He said: *everyone in this world is somebody else's stranger, girl, and the only sin is leaving them to be strangers alone.*"

She looked at her hands, translucent at the fingertips now, and Maren's chest hurt.

"I've been in this stolen country seventy years. Every soul in it is a stranger to me and I to them, and I stayed anyway, because staying is the only prayer I ever learned. And now the land is closing its ledger on me, and I find I mind only one thing — that I taught all of you to navigate by dead stars and never once taught you the difference between surviving and *staying*. They're not the same lesson. One keeps you alive. The other makes the alive worth the keeping."

Her eyes found Maren's bandaged hand, then Isolde's face, then the corner where Pip slept curled against Brannoc's coat like a burr.

"That child stowed away to follow a woman he calls family into a country that's forgetting itself. That priest sings to a god he no longer believes in because the singing is the only honesty he has left. And you two — you crossed the ford under warden fire and Maren held the line with her body until her hand broke doing it, and you have the gall to sit here telling me you don't know whether you belong on this voyage."

"We know we belong," Isolde whispered. "We're afraid."

"Good," said Tansy. "Fear's the ballast. Sail without it and you capsize on the first honest wave." She settled back, suddenly frail again, suddenly eighty years old and fading at every edge. "But decide tonight, loves. Because tomorrow we walk north, and I'd take it as a kindness to spend my last miles surrounded by my family — all of it, even the parts that price the lifeboats."

* * *

### ISOLDE

Isolde didn't sleep. Neither did Maren; she could tell by the breathing. After midnight they lay in the dark of the loft, shoulder to shoulder the way they'd slept in a fishing skiff at nine years old, and the conversation happened the way it always happened between them — in fragments, in silence, in the language they'd built before either could write.

*She's got days,* Maren's silence said.

*I know.*

*If we stay and she fades on the road, we carry that.*

*If we leave, we carry worse.*

*You don't know that.*

*No,* Isolde answered aloud, because some things had to be said in the shared air. "I don't. But I know what I saw tonight. The land forgot her fence and she sang it back. Seventy years a stranger and she's still teaching this country how to be real, one song at a time, while it eats her for the privilege." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I don't want to be the kind of person who does the sums and leaves. I've met that kind of person. I've *been* that kind of person. I don't want to die having been her."

Below them, in the dark, Maren was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice was rough with fever and something older.

"My hand doesn't hurt tonight," she said. "First time since the ford. Do you know why? Because I was so busy watching her that I forgot to hurt." A breath. "All right. North. All of us. But Isolde — when she goes, wherever she goes — we're the ones who sing at it. Both of us. The way she sang at that fence. Nobody else gets to."

"Nobody else would dare."

"True." A ghost of the twins' old grin in the dark. "We set an outrageous standard."

Isolde laughed despite everything — a wet, broken laugh, but a laugh — and reached across the dark and found her sister's good hand and held it, and above the barn where no stars had hung for months, the starless dark went on being starless, patient, and vast.

* * *

### MAREN

They told the others at dawn, and Maren watched what the decision did to the room. Brannoc nodded once, hard, like a man accepting a load he'd already rigged for. Sable closed his eyes and mouthed something that was either gratitude or prayer. Pip whooped and then, catching the mood, converted the whoop into a cough with the practiced dishonesty of a born street-thief. Coral only looked at the twins for a long moment and then said, quietly, "Thank you," in the voice of someone keeping a ledger of debts they intended to pay in full.

Tansy was already at the door, wrapped in every coat the crew owned, her feet in two pairs of borrowed socks, humming her foreign lullaby under her breath. The snow around her still hesitated, still drifted wide, still erased her footprints almost as fast as she made them. But this morning, Maren noticed, the hesitation had changed quality. Less like uncertainty now.

More like reverence.

"Roads won't hold long past noon," Brannoc rumbled, hauling the sled. "Gray's moving faster every night."

"Then we walk fast," Tansy said cheerfully, "and sing slow," and she stepped out first, barefoot-hearted into the white, and the crew fell in behind her —

— every head turning at once when the sound rolled down out of the starless north: distant, vast, and unmistakable, a single deep bell-note from the direction of the Conservatory, as if something impossibly far away had just opened one eye.
