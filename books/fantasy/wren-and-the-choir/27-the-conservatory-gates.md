## 27. The Conservatory Gates

They saw it from the ridge while the light was still the color of old pewter — that hour when the unraveling country could almost pass for evening.

Coral had known it was coming for three days. The memory-whispers in their skull had changed pitch, the way a tuning string changes when you turn the peg: not coordinates anymore but *recognition*, a hum that rose in their back teeth and behind their eyes whenever they faced east. By the second day they had stopped trying to sleep. The whispers sang to them in the dark hours, patient as water working at stone, and the song was made of corridors.

Now the Conservatory stood in the middle of a dead valley like a lie told beautifully.

Everything around it was ruin in the way of unfinished things. The hillsides were gray sketches of themselves — hills drawn by someone who had never seen hills, all suggestion and no weight. A riverbed ran through the valley floor holding nothing but a faint silver shimmer, static given the shape of water, making a sound like a whisper repeated until it lost its meaning. Trees stood in clumps with their bark unrendered, pale as bone, their leaves flat gray ovals that did not stir even when the wind moved through them and moved nothing else.

And the Conservatory rose out of all this intact. Not merely standing — *tended*. Its white walls were clean. Its copper roofs had not gone green. The great glass galleries caught what little light remained and threw it back warm, gold-edged, as if the building kept its own private afternoon inside. Gardens spread below the terraces in ordered beds, and the flowers were in bloom, impossibly, riotously in bloom, roses and night-scented stock and something blue that Coral's body knew before their mind did — *forget-me-nots*, planted along the cloister walk every spring by the youngest novices, because the Choir loved a joke it didn't understand.

"It's whole," Maren said. She said it the way other people said *it's burning*.

Isolde had her spyglass up, though there was hardly anything left in this country worth the name of distance. "Walls are sound. Gates are closed. I can see movement on the gallery walks." A pause. "Or I can see something. It flickers."

"Everything here flickers," Brannoc muttered. He had his hands full with the cart and with Maren's crutches and with the general business of being the only one among them who still expected the world to make sense. "Whole buildings don't just sit in the middle of an empty country waiting politely."

"This one does," Coral said.

They hadn't meant to speak. But the words came out of them with the flat certainty of a memory surfacing, and everyone turned, and Coral looked at the Conservatory and felt seven years fall away like a coat dropped from the shoulders.

"The Choir built things to last," they said. "That was the whole doctrine. Memory lasts. We were taught that before we were taught our letters. What is sung into the deep memory cannot be unwritten." Their voice sounded strange to them — younger, or older, or both. "The god is dreaming this place. It's the last thing it has left to hold on to. Of course it's intact. It's the only thing in Ashenmoor the god can't bear to let go."

Sable stood close beside them — closer than he'd stood two weeks ago, before the ruin-chapel, before the plain speaking, before whatever it was they had decided to continue instead of forgiving. His shoulder nearly touched theirs. He didn't say anything. That was one of the things about Sable Fen: he knew when a silence was load-bearing.

Pip was not so restrained. "It looks like a palace," he said, hanging off the side of the cart, eyes enormous. "You *lived* there?"

"I was raised there."

"Was there treasure?"

"Pip," Isolde said warningly.

"There's always treasure in palaces."

"There's a library," Coral said, and heard how their voice went quiet. "There's an archive underneath that holds more memories than any city holds people. And there's—" They stopped. There was a room on the third floor with a window facing west where a girl named Coral Underhill had once sat and practiced stealing the taste of plums out of her own mouth, so she would know what taking felt like before anyone ever taught her the rest. There was a practice hall with forty marks worn into the floor where forty students had stood for morning resonance. There was a door in the sublevels that Coral had run from, seven years ago, with someone's hand reaching after hers through the dark.

"There's everything," they finished. "Let's go down before the light goes."

No one argued. In the gray country, no one argued anymore about moving toward shelter; the nights took regions now, whole valleys erased between one dusk and the next, and everyone had learned to treat distance as a debt that came due at sundown.

* * *

The road down through the gardens was worse than the valley had been, because the gardens were *trying*.

Coral understood, walking between the beds, that these were not real plants. They were remembered plants — rendered from the god's long dream of them, each rose assembled out of some choir-novice's summer, some gardener's pride, some child's stolen handful of petals. And because memory is never complete, the flowers were wrong in small, terrible ways. Roses without thorns. Lilies whose scent arrived a half-second before you reached them and vanished when you did. A bed of marigolds that all faced Coral as they passed, tracking them gently, the way sunflowers track the sun.

"They're watching," Pip whispered, delighted and horrified.

"They're *remembering*," Coral said. "There's a difference. Barely."

Brannoc kept close to the cart wheels. Tansy rode in the cart now — she had stopped pretending she could walk the distances three days ago — and she looked at the Conservatory rising above them with an expression Coral couldn't read, an expression like a woman reading a letter written in a language she'd almost forgotten.

"You hate this place," Tansy said. Not asking.

Coral walked a few steps more before answering. The gravel under their feet sounded right, and that was the worst part. Someone had dreamed the exact crunch of it.

"I loved it," they said finally. "That's worse. You can't grieve a place you only hated. You'd be free." They touched one of the marigold heads absently as they passed; it was warm, like a living thing, like a brow. "I loved it for eleven years, and then in one night I learned what it was for, and I ran, and I have spent seven years telling myself I never looked back."

"And did you?"

"Every time I slept," Coral said. "It turns out you can't steal your own dreams away. I tried."

Tansy made a low sound, not quite laughter. "No," she said. "You can't. Believe me, girl. I've tried with bigger countries than yours."

* * *

The gates were iron and white wood, twenty feet high, worked over with the Choir's knot-script — the notation system in which hymns were written directly into metal, so that a gate, a bell, a coffin-lid could hold a chord forever. Coral had copied that script as a child, tongue between teeth, ink to the wrist. Standing before it now, they found their fingers moving without permission, tracing the counterpoint in the air.

"It's a warding-song," they said. "An old one. *Abide, abide, the door remembers.*" They laughed, short and ugly. "We sang that to keep intruders out. All this time it's been keeping the god in."

"So how do we open it?" Isolde asked. Practical as ever. She and Maren had navigated them across half a dead country by stars that no longer existed; she did not tolerate rhetorical questions.

Coral looked at the gates. The whisper in their skull had gone very loud and very clear, the way it always did near places thick with stored memory, and beneath the whisper was the other sound — the one they had been hearing since the white silence, the child-voice of the god, thin and vast at once, like wind through a keyhole the size of a cathedral.

*I know you*, the sound said. Or the sound said something that meant that. *I held you when you were small. I remember your weight.*

"I knock," Coral said.

They set their palm against the iron, closed their eyes, and let the first note out.

It had been weeks since the ford, since they had used the magic openly in front of all of them, and it had not gotten easier. Conduit-work felt like this, always: reaching down into yourself and finding that the bottom of you opens onto somewhere else, somewhere with a floor made of other people's lives. Coral had spent seven years selling off the top layer of what they carried, the widow's wedding day, the fisherman's last summer, a thousand small bright things traded for bread — but the deep hoard was still there, thousands of memories sunk in them like ballast, and when they sang, the ballast sang back.

The note left their throat and entered the iron, and the knot-script lit.

Not glowed — *lit*, each character kindling gold-white in sequence, the way candles take flame from one another down the length of an altar rail. The chord the gates held woke and answered Coral's note, harmonized with it, and for a moment the whole gatefront was singing, a sound like forty voices in a stone room, like morning resonance in the practice hall, and Coral's knees nearly went because they *knew this song*. They had stood in the second row from the left, age nine, barefoot on cold flags, singing this exact line while Warden Halloway walked the rows with her hand hovering over each novice's crown, listening for the ones who resonated true.

*Coral.* Halloway's voice, from seven years and a hundred miles away. *Again. From the top. The rest of you, listen to her — this is what the Choir is for.*

The gates opened.

They opened slowly, without any grinding a gate that size should have made, swinging inward onto a courtyard of white flagstones where the fountain still played — actually played, water arcing and breaking, the only real-seeming water in the whole gray valley — and beyond the courtyard rose the Conservatory itself, galleries and towers and the long west window of the refectory, all of it lit from within by a warm lamplight that had no source.

The crew came through the gates in a huddle, the way people enter cold water. Even Brannoc, who feared nothing on any water anywhere, kept his hand on his knife. Only Tansy looked around herself with something like recognition, and only Pip looked around with something like joy.

"It's warm," Pip said, astonished. He was right. Inside the walls the air was soft and smelled of cut grass and lamp oil and bread — bread, actual baking bread, though the kitchens had been abandoned for longer than Coral had been alive. "Why is it warm? Everything else out there is dead."

"Because something is dreaming hard," Maren said quietly. She leaned on her crutch and stared up at the towers. Her hand went to her ruined leg, the one the wardens' blades had opened at the ford, and Coral saw her jaw tighten. "Dreams are warm. Ask anyone who sleeps through winter."

"Where do we even start?" Isolde asked. "This place is a warren."

"The archive," Sable said.

Everyone turned to him. He had been silent through the whole descent, and now he stood in the courtyard with the fountain-light moving on his face, and he looked — Coral searched for the word — *called*. It was a priest's look. It was the look he must have worn in the flooded crypt, singing forbidden hymns to strangers for coins, before any of them ever met him.

"The founders wrote everything down," Sable said. "Doctrine, rites, the true history — Coral told us what the archives hold. If we want to understand what's happening to this country, what the god is, why the stars went out — the answers are under our feet. And we're running out of country to be ignorant in."

He was right, and Coral hated him a little for being right, because the archive was down. Down past the practice halls, down past the dormitories, down past the door they had fled through seven years ago. Down meant through.

"We should eat first," Brannoc said firmly, in the voice of a man who had learned that the best way to manage scholars and priests and thieves was to get between them and their obsessions at least once a day. "And see to the horses, such as horses are. And Tansy needs—"

"I need to sit in that garden," Tansy said, pointing her chin at the cloister walk, the row of blue forget-me-nots. "Go do your digging. I'll watch the sky."

"You can't watch the sky," Maren said gently. "There isn't one anymore."

"Then I'll watch it not be there," Tansy said. "Someone ought to keep an eye on it. Go on. Old women are the safest thing in any country; even dying lands leave us till last."

So it was decided the way most things in their family were decided: quickly, imperfectly, by whoever spoke loudest about food. Brannoc and the twins made camp under the gallery eaves, in the lee of a wall that had stood for centuries against weather that had never fallen. Pip appointed himself explorer of the fountain and was forbidden, in one voice, from going more than ten feet from it.

Which left Coral and Sable standing at the head of the stairs that led down.

* * *

The interior of the Conservatory was lit everywhere and lit by nothing. Warmth pooled in the corridors like water finding its level. Their footsteps rang on the flags in a way footsteps shouldn't ring indoors, a chapel-echo, as though the building considered every visitor a kind of congregation.

Coral had meant to lead them straight down. Straight through, eyes forward, the wound walked past rather than entered.

But the corridor to the main stair passed the practice hall, and the doors of the practice hall stood open, and the sound coming out of them was forty children singing morning resonance.

Coral stopped as if they'd hit glass.

"Sable," they said. Their voice came out cracked. "Do you hear—"

"I hear it," Sable said softly. "I've been hearing it since the gates. Haven't you realized? This whole place is a memory being replayed. It's not a haunting. It's a rehearsal."

The practice hall lay open before them, and it was full.

Not solid-full — flicker-full, presence-full, the way the twins had described the figures on the gallery walks. Children stood in their rows on the worn flags, forty of them, ages six to sixteen, robes gray-white, mouths open on a chord that rose and hung and rose again. Light came through the tall windows and fell through them too, passing through the children as if they were panes of colored glass, laying their shadows on the far wall in colors that shifted and reformed. At the front of the room a teacher stood with her hand raised, and her face was turned away, and her silhouette was so exactly, unbearably familiar that Coral's whole body clenched around a name.

*Mistress Vael.* Who had taught them breath. Who had told them, age eleven, *your voice is a door, child; be careful what you let through it.* Who had died — no. Coral didn't know. Had Mistress Vael died? Had she passed through the Door already, in the exodus, or was she one of the ones who had forgotten herself in some village, smiling blankly at a sky with no stars? There was no way to know. The land had eaten its own ledgers.

One of the flickering children turned its head.

It was a small figure in the third row, second from the left. A girl of perhaps nine, barefoot, dark-haired, with a smudge of ink still on her wrist from morning notation. Her face was Coral's face. Her mouth was open on the note Coral had been singing a moment ago at the gates — the same note, held, sustained, the warding-chord's opening line — and as Coral watched, the flickering child smiled around the note, the way you smile when the music is good and you are safe and the morning is cold and someone you trust is walking the rows behind you.

Coral could not breathe.

Seven years. Seven years of rules and aliases and sold-off Sundays, seven years of being no one with a market stall and a bag of charms, and it had all come apart in the end not at the point of a warden's blade but here, at a door, looking at a child who was themselves and who was happy, who was *happy*, who had not yet been taken down the night-corridors to be shown what the Rite was, who had not yet stood in front of a tribunal and refused, who had not yet run, who had not yet spent a decade learning that the price of surviving your own childhood is carrying it everywhere you go, forever, in a body that will not put it down.

"That's you," Pip said. He had followed them despite the ten-foot rule, of course he had; he was standing at Coral's elbow with the fearless tactlessness of the young. "That little one. That's you."

"Yes," Coral said.

"She looks nice," Pip said. "She looks like she likes it here."

"She does," Coral said. "She loves it here. That's the whole horror of it, Pip. Nobody had to hurt me to make me loyal. They just had to be kind, and sing well, and mean it. The Choir wasn't a prison. It was a home with a cellar, and none of us were ever allowed to ask what was kept in the cellar." They swallowed. "I'm not sure I've forgiven this place for being beautiful."

Sable's hand settled between their shoulder blades. Not a grip. Just weight. Just *here*.

"We don't have to go down today," he said.

"Yes, we do," Coral said. "Tansy is fading. Maren can't walk much farther. Every night another valley goes. And somewhere under this floor is the reason for all of it." They straightened. The flickering choir swelled toward some cadence, and Coral found their own lips shaping the resolution before they could stop themselves — muscle memory, soul memory, whatever lived deeper than either. "But thank you. For offering. For knowing I needed the offer even if I couldn't take it."

"That's what I'm for," Sable said. And then, quieter, with the guilt he still carried like a stone sewn into his coat: "It's the least I owe you."

"You don't keep accounts anymore," Coral reminded him. "We agreed. In the chapel."

"We agreed you'd stop keeping them," Sable said. "Nobody agreed about me."

Before Coral could answer, the cadence resolved. The forty voices came down together onto the final chord — and held it. Held it past all natural length, past comfort, the chord stretching and stretching like a held breath, like a heart pausing between beats, and the light through the windows dimmed, and the warmth in the corridors pulled backward toward the center of the building like tide going out, and every flickering child turned its head at once.

Forty faces. Forty pairs of eyes. All of them Coral's eyes, at every age they had ever been, looking at her from the rows.

The smallest one — nine years old, ink on her wrist, barefoot on the cold flags — lifted one small hand and pointed, not at Coral, but down. Through the floor. Toward the archive. Toward the cellar.

Then the hall emptied. Not faded — *emptied*, between one blink and the next, children and light and chord all gone, leaving only the worn marks on the flags where forty singers had stood for two hundred years, and the silence afterward, which was not an empty silence at all but a listening one.

"Well," said Pip, into it. He had gone pale, and had also, visibly, decided not to show it. "Your school's a bit intense."

"It's showing us the way," Coral said. Their heart was hammering and their mouth was dry and beneath all of it, beneath everything, the whisper in their skull had gone quiet for the first time in weeks — quiet the way a room goes quiet when the person you've been talking about enters it. "Down. It wants us to go down. It's been waiting for me to come home."

They took the stair.

The steps were white stone worn hollow in the centers by generations of feet — Coral's own among them, some seven-years-ago version of her descending to lessons with her satchel banging her hip — and the lampless lamps burned warmer as they went, and the air grew older, damper, full of the smell of vellum and cold iron and something else, something underneath, vast and slow and breathing.

At the bottom of the stair, the corridor forked. To the left lay the archive galleries, the shelves, the answers Sable had promised.

To the right lay a plain door of black wood, banded in iron, with a lock shaped like an open mouth.

Coral stopped. Sable stopped beside them. From somewhere behind the black door — muffled, distant, unmistakable — came a sound neither of them had heard since the white silence.

A child, crying.
