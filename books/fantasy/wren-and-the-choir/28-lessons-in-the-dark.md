## 28. Lessons in the Dark

The Conservatory did not creak. That was the first wrong thing Coral noticed.

Every old building creaks — settles and sighs in the night like a sleeper turning over. Coral had spent seven years sleeping in Vell's crooked tenements, in smugglers' holds, in a barn while winter gnawed at the doors, and every one of them had talked to itself after dark. But the Conservatory was silent as a held breath, silent as a room where someone has just stopped singing, and its silence had a texture to it, a velvet pressure against the ears. The god was dreaming this place. Dreaming it whole, down to the polish on the banisters, down to the smell of lamp-oil and chalk dust that rose to meet them in the entrance hall like a greeting from something that did not know it was dead.

"It's too clean," Isolde said behind them, and her voice came out flat and strange, swallowed by all that quiet. "Seven years of ruin outside, and the floors look swept."

"Because they are," Coral said. "Someone sweeps them. Something does." They did not say *the god*. They did not have to. Everyone on the crew had learned by now what an impossibly intact thing meant in this country: it meant the land loved it too much to let it go.

Sable walked close at Coral's shoulder, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech between them now. Since the ruin-chapel they had developed a language of small silences — the silence that meant *I am here*, the silence that meant *I remember what you cannot forgive*, and this one, the longest silence, which meant simply *keep walking; I will keep walking with you*.

Pip pressed against Coral's other side, his small hand hooked in the hem of their coat like a burr. He had been strange since the ford, since Maren, stranger still since the ghost market — jumpy, watchful, carrying some secret the way he carried his bottled storms, tucked against his ribs where no one could see. Coral felt it off him like heat off a stone. But there was no time for children's secrets tonight. Tonight the Conservatory wanted something from them.

The corridors knew their name.

Coral understood that within the first hundred steps. The lamps lit ahead of them — not all at once, not theatrically, but one by one, politely, the way they had lit when Coral was nine years old and running late for morning drills. The walls bore the same hairline crack above the third archway that Coral had watched a groundskeeper fail to repair for six years. Their feet found the worn hollows in the flagstones without being told. It was like walking through a memory of walking, and the memory had been kept so faithfully, so lovingly, that Coral's body began to believe it was eleven again, twelve, fourteen, and the dread of that belief sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

"You don't have to do this alone," Sable murmured.

"Yes I do." Coral heard how cruel it sounded and could not soften it. "That's the lesson. That's always been the lesson."

* * *

The dormitory wing opened off the east corridor, and Coral went there because grief is a compass and points wherever it hurts most.

The room they had shared stood exactly as they had left it seven years ago. Two narrow beds. Two trunks. A window with a view of the practice courts, where the sky beyond was painted a soft, impossible blue — the god's best guess at a sky, or perhaps its fondest memory of one. On the left bed someone had turned down the covers. On the right bed the covers were thrown back in a tangle, mid-flight, the way a child leaves them when she wakes in the dark and runs.

Coral stood in the doorway a long time.

Then the memory took them. Not metaphorically. The Choir-magic lived in the walls here, saturated the plaster and the floorboards, and it reached up into Coral the way water reaches into dry cloth, and the stored memories stirred and rose and *opened*.

Their mother's face.

It came whole, the way it never came in Vell, where Coral had kept it locked in the deepest vault of themselves, rationed out in fragments because a face remembered too often wears smooth as a coin. Here it arrived complete: the broad brown face, the laugh-lines cut deep as irrigation channels, the gap in her teeth she covered with her hand when she laughed and never bothered to cover when she sang. Her hands smelled of tallow and river clay. She had worked the memory-farms outside Corriehold before the Choir took Coral — had sung the seed-barley awake each spring, unlicensed, untrained, the way farm-folk had done for generations before the conservatories made singing a crime of bloodline.

*Mother,* Coral thought, into the memory, *your face — I'd been losing your face.*

And the memory, being generous the way all deep memories are generous, gave them more. Her voice, low and rough: *"You'll sing for them, little wren, and they'll think you're theirs. But you listen to me. You know whose song it is. You know who taught you the first note."* Her thumb, rough as bark, pressing Coral's palm open. *"A song belongs to the singer. Never let anyone tell you different."*

Coral came back to themselves kneeling on the cold flags, both hands pressed over their mouth, and Sable was beside them — not touching, just near, radiating warmth like a banked fire — and Pip was watching from the corridor with wide eyes.

"I forgot her face," Coral said. It came out wrecked. "Seven years, and I was — I was selling other people's weddings and funerals and first kisses, and my own mother's face was going smooth as a—"

"No." Sable's voice was very steady. "You weren't forgetting. You were keeping it safe. There's a difference, and you just proved it. Look how whole it came back."

But Coral shook their head, because Sable didn't understand yet, couldn't, and Coral could not explain that the wholeness wasn't theirs. The Conservatory had kept her face. The god had kept it, dreaming, all these years, and had handed it back like a teacher returning confiscated property. Even their grief here was on loan.

* * *

The Rite chamber was on the lower level, past the archive stairs, and Coral's feet tried to refuse the descent. They made them go anyway. Grief as curriculum — that had been the Conservatory's true doctrine, beneath all the hymns and harmony drills. *You cannot conduct what you have not survived.* Master Ellery-Vane had said it a hundred times, gentle as snowfall, terrible as snowfall too, the kind of gentleness that buries things.

The chamber was round, domed, acoustically perfect. Nine chairs in a crescent for the assessing masters. One chair in the center, smaller, for the candidate.

Coral sat in it.

They hadn't meant to. Their legs simply folded, the way a body folds into a pew it has sat in ten thousand times, and the memory rose around them like floodwater.

They were sixteen. The chamber smelled of beeswax and lilies — the Choir loved lilies, said their white trumpets were shaped like listening ears. The nine masters sat in their crescent, and Master Ellery-Vane stood at the center with her hands folded, and she was smiling the smile Coral had trusted more than any face in the world.

"The Rite of Deep Harmonic," she said. "You know what it asks."

A dying man lay on a bier at the chamber's edge — a volunteer, they said, a faithful servant of the Choir whose pain had grown past bearing. The Rite would take his last hour, his final memories, and pour them through the candidate's voice, teaching the young throat what death sounded like from the inside. Only then, the doctrine said, could a Conduit carry the great memories safely. Only someone who had held a dying mind could hold a country's.

Except Coral had already touched the man's mind. That was the crime. In the preparation, in the anointing, Coral's gift had slipped its leash — it did that, always had, hungry and untrained — and Coral had felt what waited in the volunteer's last hour, and it was not peace. It was terror. He was not a faithful servant. He was a debtor, a coerced man, a father of three who had been told his family's debts would die with his consent, and his last hour was full of his children's names, repeated like a prayer, repeated like a scream.

"I refuse," sixteen-year-old Coral said, and the word fell into the perfect acoustics and rang there, and rang, and would not stop ringing.

The memory showed Coral what came after, and Coral let it, because they had decided on the walk down that they would finally look at all of it. The cell. The week of fasting called *clarification*. Master Ellery-Vane's visits, her voice never rising, explaining that refusal was a wound in the Choir's body and wounds must be cauterized. And then the fourth night, and the loose bar in the window that someone had sawn nearly through — someone with small hands, someone who had whispered through the door, *third window, east wall, go at the change of the watch, don't wake anyone, don't look back* —

Wren.

The memory gave Coral Wren's face too, and that was the cruelest mercy of the night. Fifteen years old, sharp-boned, gap-toothed in a way that echoed Coral's mother as if the world enjoyed making jokes with knives in them. Wren — named for the same bird, which was why they'd found each other, two wrens in a cage of hymns. Wren who had sawed the bar over three nights with a stolen file, who had planned the escape down to the change of the watch, who had said *we go together, obviously, don't be stupid.*

And the memory played the rest. The wall. The drop into the frost-killed garden. Coral over first, landing badly, ankle wrenching. Voices behind — a bell, the night-watch waking early, everything unraveling. And Wren on the wall, halfway over, saying *go, go, I'm right behind you* —

And Coral going. Running. Not looking back, because Wren had said not to, and because the wards were waking, and because Coral was sixteen and terrified and the running felt like the only true note left in their body.

They had told themselves for seven years that Wren was caught, punished, alive somewhere. They had built the story carefully, brick by merciful brick, because the alternative was unbearable.

Here, in the god's dream, the story collapsed. Because the Conservatory remembered too, and it remembered honestly.

Coral surfaced from the memory gasping, and Sable caught their shoulders, and Coral heard themselves say it aloud, the thing they had never said:

"There was a girl. Wren. She got me out and I ran and—" Their voice broke apart. "The records. The archives are here. Sable, the archives will say what happened to her. I have to know. I've been a coward about it for seven years and I have to know."

* * *

The archive was a long vaulted hall lined with memory-cases, thousands of them, each one a small glass reliquary holding a coiled thread of light. The founders' collection. The Choir's hoard. Coral walked the aisles reading labels in a daze — *Harvest rites, Corriehold district. Lullabies, the drowned valley. Last words, compiled 311.* Whole lives filed like specimens. Their own theft in Vell suddenly looked like what it had always been: a child picking crumbs under a table where monsters dined.

It was Pip who broke the silence, and he broke it sideways, the way he did everything.

"You said the Door's under here," Pip said. Too loud. Too casual. His voice had a crack running through it.

Coral turned slowly. "I said we hoped it was."

"No." Pip's hand had gone to his ribs again, to the hidden thing. His eyes were wet and furious, furious at being wet. "No, you didn't hope nothing. You *knew*. You've known since the stars went out, since the whispers started — you've been following them like a map, and the map ends here, and you knew it ended here before we ever left Vell, didn't you? Before the sewers. Before any of it."

"Pip—"

"A ghost told me." The words tore out of him all at once, a bottled storm breaking. "At the ghost market, when I traded the storm. I asked about the Door because I wanted to be useful, because everyone's got a job except me, and the ghost said — it said *ask the wren, the Door sleeps under the place that raised her, under the singing school, under the oldest root* — and I didn't tell you because—" His breath hitched. "Because if you knew we were going *here*, to this place, you'd have gone alone. I seen how you look at these walls. I seen you in that bedroom upstairs. You'd have left us behind to keep us safe, same as you left that girl on the wall, and I wasn't going to let you, so I shut up, and it's been eating me, it's been *eating* me—"

He ran out of breath and stood there shaking, small and blazing in the lamplight, and the truth of him settled over the archive like ash.

Coral knelt. It took effort; their knees were still shaking from the Rite chamber. They took Pip's face in both hands, the way their mother had once taken theirs.

"You protected me," Coral said. "Badly, secretly, at cost to yourself — you protected me. That's the most stupid, brave, mule-headed thing anyone's ever done for me, and I include a girl with a stolen file in that count." Pip hiccuped something that was half laugh, half sob. "Listen to me. I'm not leaving anyone on any walls. Not you. Not Sable. Not Isolde and Maren and Brannoc. Whatever's under this place, we face it together or not at all. That's the whole lesson of my entire miserable life, and I learned it late, and I'm done failing the examination."

Pip nodded against their palms. Then, muffled: "Your face is doing the crying thing."

"So is yours."

"We don't talk about mine."

* * *

They found the record an hour later, in the disciplinary rolls, in Sable's careful priest's hands, because Sable read old script the way other men read weather.

Coral had asked him to read it aloud. He hesitated — she hesitated, Coral corrected themselves, remembering again that Halloway had been a woman once, before duty sanded her edges — and then she read it in a low, level voice, standing among the memory-cases with the lamplight pooling at her feet.

*Candidate Wren of Corriehold. Assisting escape of condemned candidate, night of the Frost Vigil. Sentence: Gleaning of acquired resonance, remanded to the Rite of Deep Harmonic as substitute vessel.*

Substitute vessel. The words went through Coral like a blade finding the gap in armor. They had refused to pour a dying stranger's terror through their own voice — and the Choir had simply poured it through Wren instead. Through a fifteen-year-old. As punishment. As *curriculum*.

"And after?" Coral's voice came from very far away. "What does it say after?"

Sable/Halloway's throat moved. "Nothing after. The roll ends. But Coral—" She closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the care of someone handling something explosive. "The Rite of Deep Harmonic doesn't have survivors. Not vessels. The doctrine says the vessel's voice becomes part of the Choir's permanent collection." She looked up, and her eyes — Halloway's eyes, Warden's eyes, but underneath them, for one unguarded moment, the eyes of the woman who had trained a frightened child to control a dangerous gift — were bright with something she would never permit herself to call grief.

"Coral. If she died in that chamber, her last hour is in one of these cases. They kept everything. They kept *everything*."

The archive stretched away into the dark, aisle after aisle, thousands of glass reliquaries, thousands of coiled threads of light. Somewhere in those shelves was a girl named Wren, and her final terrified hour, and whatever she had thought at the end — whether she had cursed the friend who ran, or forgiven her, or both.

Coral stood very still, listening to the god's silence press in around them, and far below — so far below it might have been imagination, might have been the whisper-map stirring in their blood, might have been something else entirely, something *waiting* — they heard a single note begin.

Not the god's note. Smaller. Younger. A voice practicing scales in the dark, patient as roots, seven years deep.

Someone was singing under the Conservatory. And they were singing Coral's mother's lullaby.
