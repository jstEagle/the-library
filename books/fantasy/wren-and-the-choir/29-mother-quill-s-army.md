## 29. Mother Quill's Army

Brannoc had ferried enough desperate people across enough bad water to know the sound an army makes before you see it. It wasn't drums. It wasn't marching. It was quieter than that — a wrongness in the ground itself, a hush that pressed up through the soles of his boots like the river did through a hull. He'd been standing watch on the Conservatory's outer wall since before dawn, and when the hush came, he set down his mug of chicory, spat over the parapet, and said, to no one, "Well. There she is."

The gray country had been kind to them for exactly one night. The land around the Conservatory still held its impossible wholeness — the god's dreaming keeping the grounds green, the gravel drives swept, the hedges clipped by no hand — and Brannoc had let himself, for a few hours, believe they might reach the Door without anyone else knowing it existed. That was the trouble with hope. It made a man slow.

He counted the torches coming up the western road. Lost count at two hundred. Started again and gave up somewhere past four.

"Isolde," he called down the stairwell, and his voice came out flatter than he meant it to. "Wake them all. Wake Coral first."

* * *

The Conservatory rose out of the unraveling country like a tooth out of a ruined gum. Brannoc had seen it first three days ago, cresting a hill of grass that ended mid-slope in gray static, and his first thought — the honest one, the one he'd never say aloud to the others — was *I know this place*. Not from memory. From the water. He'd been a ferryman his whole life, first on the Mirren, then on the coast runs, and there was a feeling a building gave you when it had been built to last: a settledness, a weight in the way it met the ground. The Conservatory met the ground the way a cathedral did. Whatever else the Choir had been, whatever crimes its founders had committed, they had built to outlast the world they'd stolen.

Now he stood on its wall and watched an army come to pick the lock.

They moved without discipline and without disorder, which was the worst combination — a mob that had organized itself out of pure want. Brannoc picked them apart the way he'd pick apart a fleet: here, a banner of the Saltmarsh clans, their colors bleached to near-white because the dye had stopped being real; there, a company in Vell warden-gray but no badges, deserters or worse; a knot of river-people with their boats abandoned on a shore that had forgotten how to be a shore. Farmers. Dockhands. Two whole families with carts and children, which was the part that sat worst in his chest, because you didn't bring children to a fight unless you believed there was nothing left to lose.

And at the head of it, riding a palanquin that eight men carried through the mud, Mother Quill.

He'd sold her passage twice, years back, before he'd known what she was. She'd paid in old coin, real coin, and she'd sat in his ferry with her hands folded and asked him questions about the tides in a voice like warm oil, and he'd answered, because she was just a rich woman then, and rich women liked to hear about tides. He'd learned better since.

"She's stopped," Isolde said beside him. She'd come up the wall quiet as her sister always didn't. Her eyes were on the army's van, where the palanquin had halted at the edge of the Conservatory grounds, where the gravel began. "Why's she stopped?"

Brannoc looked. The army had stopped too — the whole mass of them, hundreds deep on the road, standing in the gray morning light and staring at the grounds. And he understood, because he'd felt it himself three days ago. The land here was *whole*. Green grass. Living hedges. A sky overhead that, for a mile in every direction, still held the faint ghost of color, like a bruise healing. After weeks in the static, after villages that forgot their own names and roads that ended mid-stride, the sight of an unbroken world was its own kind of wall.

"She's letting them look," he said. "Letting them want it. That's the first thing she ever sold me, too. Not the passage. The wanting."

Below them, a horn sounded — three notes, low, almost gentle. And Mother Quill's palanquin began to move again, up the drive, alone.

* * *

They gathered in the entrance hall, under windows of colored glass that depicted the founding of the Choir in scenes no one had yet had the stomach to study closely. Coral stood in the middle with that look Brannoc had learned to read and hate — the look of someone listening to something no one else could hear. The whispers, Pip called them. The map in their head. Sable stood at their shoulder, close enough to catch them if the listening pulled too hard, which it had done twice already.

"They're not all hers," Coral said, when Brannoc told them the number. Their voice had that thin quality it got when the god's dreaming pressed close. "The army. I can feel them at the edge of — at the edge of everything. They're not angry. That's the terrible part. They're *hopeful*."

"Hopeful people burn down more than angry ones," Brannoc said. "Angry people want something to stop. Hopeful people want something to start, and they don't much care what."

Halloway stood apart, by the doors, in her warden-gray with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman at a funeral. Her wardens — the forty-odd who'd turned with her after the ford, after Maren, after whatever it was she'd seen in Coral's shared memory — were already moving along the walls with the brisk economy of people who'd done this before and didn't like doing it again. She hadn't spoken to Brannoc directly in two days. He hadn't taken it personal. Some debts you paid by standing next to a person and not mentioning them.

"She'll ask for parley," Halloway said. "Quill doesn't waste soldiers when words are cheaper. And words are very cheap for her."

"She sold the location," Sable said. His voice was careful, the way it always was now, since the chapel. "Pip said — the ghost market. She sold it to everyone. That's why they're all here. Every desperate faction in what's left of the country, all bought the same map."

"She didn't sell them a map," Brannoc said, and they all looked at him. He shrugged. "I told you. I ferried her twice. She doesn't sell things. She sells *debts*. Every one of those people out there paid her something — coin, favor, a name, a secret — for the location of the Door. And every one of them knows that if they take the Door for themselves, they stop owing her. And every one of them knows the others know it too." He looked out the tall windows, at the army on the road, at the torches guttering in the gray light. "She didn't build an army. She built a knife-fight and sold tickets."

Coral made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter hadn't been rationed for weeks. "And she's coming up the drive alone because—"

"Because she wants us to see her come alone. Wants us to think: here's a reasonable woman. Here's someone we can deal with." Brannoc rubbed his jaw. "She's the most dangerous thing on that road, and she's wearing the shape of the least."

* * *

They met her at the gates.

Brannoc insisted on standing in the parley, over Halloway's objection and Isolde's silence, which from Isolde was an argument. "I know her," he said. "Not her heart — nobody knows that — but her habits. You'll want a man who knows her habits." And Halloway, after a long look at him, had nodded once, which he understood to be the closest thing to trust she was currently issuing.

Mother Quill's palanquin set down ten paces from the gate, and she stepped out of it herself, declining the chair her bearers tried to bring. That was a habit too. She liked to be underestimated. She was a small woman, broad and soft, with a grandmother's face and hands that looked like they'd kneaded a lot of bread, and she wore plain wool, and she smiled at the four of them — Brannoc, Halloway, Coral, Sable — like a woman arriving at a family dinner.

"Brannoc," she said warmly. "You've gotten old. The river does that, or the not-river, I suppose, now." Her eyes moved on, and Brannoc watched them find Coral and stop there, the way a hand stops on a purse. "And you. The Conduit. I've been so curious about you."

"You sold my location," Coral said. Flat. No question in it.

"I sold *a* location," Quill said, "to a great many people, for a great many things. Whether it was *your* location depends entirely on what you choose to do next, doesn't it?" She spread her hands. "I'm not your enemy. I want you to hear that from my own mouth. I have never once been anyone's enemy. I'm a woman who keeps a market running in a country that's forgetting how to have markets. Do you know what I've done since the stars went out? I've fed people. I've moved them. I've kept coin moving when coin was the only thing that still behaved like it used to. And now I've found the one door out of this —" she gestured, elegantly, at the gray horizon, the starless sky, the whole unraveling theft of a country — "and I'm told it needs a Conduit to open. So here I am. Not to threaten. To *negotiate*."

"Open it for what?" Sable asked quietly.

Quill's smile didn't move but something behind it did, a shutter, a ledger-line drawn. "For whoever pays best, priest. For whoever can hold it. You've all seen what's out there." She turned, slowly, to take in the army on the road behind her — the Saltmarsh banners, the deserters, the families with their carts. "Do you know what they believe? I told them, and they believed it, because it's what they were already telling themselves. They believe the Door leads to a country where the stars shine. Where their villages are villages again. Where their dead are dead in the ordinary way, with graves, and their children can have names that stay named." She looked back at Coral, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Maybe they're right. Maybe that's exactly what's through it. And maybe you four, and a handful of wardens, and a dying old woman, mean to walk through it yourselves and close it behind you. Keep salvation like a purse. I've met that kind before. I was married to that kind."

Brannoc watched Coral's face and saw the exact moment the words found the crack in them. Coral had spent seven years selling other people's best moments. Quill had spent the same seven years selling other people's best *hopes*, and she knew, as Brannoc knew, that hope was heavier.

"You'd open it for all of them?" Coral said. "Every faction you sold it to? They'd tear each other apart at the threshold."

"Probably," Quill agreed pleasantly. "But they'd tear each other apart *outside*, in a country with stars. That's more than you're offering."

* * *

The parley ended the way Brannoc had known it would, which was nowhere. Quill had given them until nightfall — "a reasonable deadline, from a reasonable woman" — and rolled her palanquin back down the drive, and the army had made no move, only settled into camps with a terrible patience, cooking fires dotting the western road like a fallen constellation. Brannoc stood on the wall again as the light went thin and watched them, and Isolde came to stand with him, and for a while neither spoke.

"My sister would've had something to say," Isolde said finally. Maren was below, in the infirmary Halloway's healers had set up, her ruined arm bound and her fever holding steady, which the healers called winning. "About all those fires. She'd have counted them and told us exactly how many men each fire meant and which ones were bluffing." Isolde's jaw worked. "I can't count them. I just see fires."

"I see them too," Brannoc said. "Come here." He pointed, down the road, past the camps, to the eastern dark. "Now look there."

Isolde looked. And Brannoc watched her go still, the way the water goes still before a squall.

Torches. A column of them, moving with the order that Quill's mob lacked — paired, spaced, disciplined. And ahead of the column, on foot, a banner that Brannoc had last seen in Vell, hanging from the warden barracks before the world ended: the gray and silver of the Wardenate, whole and official and not Halloway's.

"That's not hers," Isolde whispered.

"No," Brannoc said. "That's the Vell garrison. What's left of it. Sent after Halloway when she turned, I'd guess, or sent for the Door on their own authority, or both." He felt something in his chest that was too tired to be fear and too old to be surprise. "Quill sold the location to everyone, remember. Everyone's coming. That's the thing about greed, Isolde. It's the one prayer that always gets answered. You just never like the answer."

Below them, in the entrance hall, a bell began to ring — the Conservatory's own bell, which no one had rung, which no one *could* have rung, which was ringing anyway, low and slow and mournful, as the god's dreaming registered what was coming and began, Brannoc thought, to be afraid.

Down in the camps, Mother Quill's fires burned on. Down the eastern road, the wardens' column raised its banner higher. And somewhere beneath their feet, beneath the green and dreaming grounds, the Door waited in the dark, and the child-god waited with it, and the night came down on all of them at once like a lid.

Brannoc turned from the wall. "Get Coral," he said. "Whatever they're listening to — tell them it's time to stop listening and start deciding."
