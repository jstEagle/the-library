## 30. The Siege of the Conservatory

Brannoc had fought in three wars before he'd decided they were all just arguments with worse manners, and so he knew the shape of a battle coming the way sailors know weather: not by looking at it but by feeling it in his teeth.

The wardens came first.

They came up the eastern road in a column of gray lantern-light, ranks of four, boots striking the frozen ground in unison, and Brannoc thought — with the detached admiration of one professional watching another — that Halloway marched them beautifully. No shouting. No wasted motion. The banner of the Wardens of Vell rose above them like a blade held very still.

Behind them, from the south, came Quill's mercenaries, and they came like a spill: no ranks, no banner, only a loose flood of armed bodies wearing every color of the gray market, torches bobbing, voices carrying across the dead fields in whoops and jeers. Mother Quill rode at their center in a palanquin of black lacquer borne by six men, and even at this distance Brannoc could see the gold glinting at her throat — the same gold she'd once offered him for a cargo he'd been too honest to carry.

"Two armies," Isolde said beside him at the wall. She was counting under her breath, lips moving, her fingers tapping out numbers on the stone. "Wardens, two hundred, maybe more. Mercenaries — hard to say. Four hundred? Five?"

"And us," Maren said. Her arm was bound tight against her chest where the ford had taken its toll, but her voice was steady as a keel. "Seven. Eight if Pip counts."

"Pip counts," said Pip, appearing between them as if summoned, slingshot already loaded with a bottle of captured fog.

Brannoc looked out over the grounds of the Conservatory — those impossible green lawns holding their color while the world around them went to ash and static — and felt the old arithmetic settle in his chest. He had carried people through rivers and storms and once, memorably, through a customs inspection, and every time the sums came out the same: too few of us, too many of them, and the water rising anyway.

"Then we don't fight them," he said. "We hold the gates until Coral gets below."

* * *

Coral stood in the Conservatory's great entry hall, and the hall remembered them.

That was the worst of it. Not the armies outside, not the wards cracking like river ice along the horizon — but the way the marble floor warmed beneath their feet in exactly the pattern it had warmed when they were nine years old, running late for morning canticle. The way the tall windows threw colored light in bars across the stone, violet and gold, the same bars they had stepped over a thousand times. The building was dreaming, and in its dream, Coral had never left.

"Don't listen to it," Sable said quietly. He stood close enough that his shoulder nearly touched theirs. He had been standing close since the ruin-chapel, since the plain speaking, since *I will not forgive you and I will not leave you*, both sentences true at once. "It wants you comfortable. Comfortable conduits sing slower."

"I'm not going to sing slow." Coral pressed their palm flat against the doorframe, and beneath the wood felt the hum — the god below, vast and small at once, turning its thousand stolen stars like a child turning stones in a pocket. "I'm going to sing the way you taught me. Like drowning."

"That's my best lesson," Sable said, and almost smiled.

The first horn sounded from the east road. Then, a breath later, a second horn answered from the south, lower, uglier, and the two sounds met over the lawns and tangled, and somewhere in that tangle the morning broke open.

* * *

Halloway gave the order to halt two hundred paces from the gates, because she was not a butcher, whatever else she was.

She stood at the head of her column and looked at the Conservatory rising against the starless sky — intact, luminous, obscene in its wholeness — and felt the old ache in her chest that had lived there seven years, the ache shaped exactly like a student who used to hum off-key in the practice halls and look at her as though she were worth learning from.

"Coral Underhill is inside," she said to Captain Verel. "And whatever else happens today, they are taken alive. Do you understand me? Alive."

"And the others?"

"The smugglers are obstacles. The priest is an obstacle. The child—" She paused. Somewhere behind her eyes, a memory stirred: a boy on a Vell rooftop, years ago, selling bottled rain to children who couldn't afford real weather. She had let him go then. She had told herself it was mercy. "The child is an obstacle too," she said, and hated how it sounded.

Verel saluted and turned to give the order, and that was when the southern line hit.

Quill's mercenaries did not wait for parley. They came screaming across the lawn in a wave of torchlight, and Halloway watched them come and understood, with cold clarity, that Mother Quill had never intended to negotiate. Why would she? The Door beneath the Conservatory was power — the kind of power that outlasted countries — and power did not share.

"Wardens!" Halloway drew her sword, and the gray lanterns flared in answer, anti-magic light rolling down the column like a tide. "Shields! Hold the center!"

The lines collided where the lawn began, and the lawn screamed.

Not the people. The *lawn*. Because this was stolen country, memory-country, and every wound dealt to it now was a wound dealt to the remembering itself, and the grass tore like skin and bled light instead of red. Where a mercenary's boot crushed the turf, gray static bloomed. Where a warden's warding-spell struck a tree, the tree forgot its own rings and came apart into pale sketches of branches.

And high above, through the torn clouds, the empty places where stars should have been seemed to lean closer.

* * *

Isolde fought the way she navigated — by dead reckoning, by instinct, by the positions of things that were no longer there.

She and Maren held the left gate together, backs to the stone, and the twins had always been two halves of one instinct, so when Isolde ducked, Maren's knife had already found the throat of the man ducking toward her; when Maren stumbled on broken ground, Isolde's shoulder was there to catch her weight. They moved like one person written twice.

"They're not trying to take the gate," Maren shouted over the din. A torch spun past her ear and burst against the wall in a fountain of sparks. "Look! They're driving toward the *east wing* — they know about the undercroft—"

Isolde looked, and saw it: the mercenaries weren't pressing the gates at all. Quill's whole assault was a fist aimed past them, at the low service entrance where the stairs down began, and the wardens were the anvil and Quill was the hammer and the crew of the ferry-*Marginalia* were simply what lay between.

"We have to collapse the arch," Isolde said. "Bring the lintel down."

"With what? We've got rope, two knives, and Pip's weather."

"You have me," said Tansy.

* * *

Tansy had been fading for weeks, and everyone had pretended not to see.

It was in her hands first — the way they'd gone translucent at the fingertips, so that candlelight passed through them like light through early ice. Then her shadow had grown unreliable, arriving late or not at all. The land had finally noticed her, the stranger in its blood, the body that aged wrong because it had been born under a real sun in a real world that Ashenmoor had been stolen away from — and the land was correcting its mistake the way a body rejects a splinter, slowly, patiently, completely.

But this morning she had walked the walls with her spine straight and her old bones singing, because she had known, the way she'd known since the Gray Country, that she would not be spending what was left of herself on growing old.

Now she stood beneath the east wing's archway, where the undercroft stairs descended toward the Door, and she put her hand against the stone, and she said, in the tone she used for telling the twins to eat their vegetables:

"Stand back, loves. And don't you dare look away. I want witnesses."

"Tansy—" Isolde started forward.

"*Back.*"

The fighting reached them then — a knot of mercenaries breaking through the warden flank, torches high, Quill's own lieutenants in the lead, close enough that Isolde could see the gold chains they wore over their armor, Quill's promise made metal. Three of them. Five. Too many for the space of seconds available.

Tansy turned to face them, and she began to remember out loud.

That was the only word for it. She spoke — low at first, then rising, a voice like a hearthfire catching — and what she spoke was the world outside: *grass*, she said, *real grass, ankle-high and tickling, and the smell of it after rain, cut hay and clover and the warm dust of summer roads*. She spoke of oceans — *gray-green and enormous, salt on your lips from a mile away, gulls screaming like unpaid debts*. She spoke of a sky with no seams in it, no edges, no starless nights, a sky that went on being sky whether anyone watched or not.

And the land listened.

Because this was stolen country, and it was starving, and here was a woman offering it the one thing it had never tasted: a memory of the world it had been taken from. The unraveling around the east wing stopped. The static hung trembling in the air like a held breath. Even the fighting stuttered — mercenaries and wardens alike turning, weapons dropping, faces slackening, because everyone in Ashenmoor was made of this place's memory, and the place itself was leaning in to drink.

The breach in the world — the raw tear where the east wing's foundations met the nothing beyond — began to pull inward, drawn toward Tansy's voice like water finding a drain.

"No," Coral breathed, from the doorway of the hall. They had come out onto the steps without deciding to, Sable's hand half-raised behind them, and they could feel it happening, could feel the land gathering Tansy up the way it gathered everything — except this time it wasn't taking her memory. It was taking *her*, all of her, the last outsider, folding her into itself to patch the hole she was sealing. "No — Tansy, stop, we can find another way—"

"There isn't another way, dear." She didn't turn around. Her outline had begun to shine at the edges, a soft golden light, the light of remembered things. "There never was. That's what I've been trying to teach all of you — everything costs. I'm just paying with money I was going to lose anyway."

The mercenaries nearest her dropped their torches and fled, weeping without knowing why. One fell to his knees and stayed there.

"Tansy." Maren was crying, her good arm outstretched, her maimed arm forgotten. "Tansy, please—"

"My girls." Now she did turn, and her face was mostly light already, her eyes still warm and sharp and entirely her own. "You hold each other. That's the whole trick of it, do you understand? Nobody survives alone. Not countries. Not gods." Her gaze found Coral, and held it, and in it was something being handed over, something heavy and green and salt-bright. "Conduit. When you get down there — remember what I showed you. Grass first. Always start with the grass."

The tear in the world closed around her like a mouth.

For one heartbeat the east wing stood in a column of pure remembered light — summer grass and ocean-water and an unbroken sky, blazing upward, and every soul on the battlefield, warden and mercenary and smuggler alike, stood blinded and silent in the glow of a real world most of them had never seen.

Then the light sank into the ground, gentle as rain soaking in, and was gone.

Where Tansy had stood, the earth was whole. The static was sealed. The archway held, and the stairs down waited in shadow.

On the stone where she had last set her hand, something remained: a small pressed flower, blue as evening, that did not grow anywhere in Ashenmoor.

* * *

Halloway lowered her sword.

Around her, her column had stopped fighting — not broken, not routed, only *stopped*, two hundred wardens staring at the sealed breach and the impossible flower and the place where an old woman had been. She heard Verel beside her breathing hard. She heard, from the direction of Quill's shattered assault, the sound of mercenaries retreating in ragged clumps, their gold chains dull in the gray light.

She had trained Coral Underhill. She had reported them anyway. She had spent seven years telling herself that duty was love with its eyes open.

And she had just watched a dying stranger buy a hundred lives with the last coin of a world none of these people would ever know — watched her do it laughing, almost, calling the twins *my girls* — and Halloway's certainty, that load-bearing wall of her life, cracked straight down the middle.

"Captain," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "Sound the retreat."

"Ma'am?"

"*Retreat.* Fall back to the road. Hold position. Nothing else." She was already walking — not toward the Conservatory, but toward the gates, toward the small huddled shapes of the smugglers, toward the conduit standing on the steps with an old woman's absence hanging around them like smoke.

Somewhere below her feet, faint as a pulse, she felt the Door begin to wake.

* * *

Coral knelt and picked up the flower.

It was cool and impossibly alive in their palm, and through their conduit's senses they could feel where it had gone — not destroyed, not dead, but *everywhere*: a thread of real-world green woven now through the fabric of Ashenmoor itself, holding the seam shut, part of the country forever.

"She's in the walls now," Pip said. He had appeared at Coral's side, soot-faced, slingshot hanging forgotten in his hand, and his voice was very small. "Like the song says. The land remembers its keepers."

"Yes," Coral said. And then, because grief needed somewhere to go and there was no time for anywhere else: "Pip — go find Brannoc. Tell him the stairs are clear and I'm going down. Tell him—" Their voice caught. They started again. "Tell him Tansy paid our passage, and I intend to arrive."

Below, beneath the dreaming Conservatory, beneath the lawns where armies had broken against a memory of grass, the god turned over in its sleep — and every starless point in the sky flickered, once, like a lamp deciding whether to go out or come home.

Coral stood, tucked the blue flower against their heart, and started down the stairs.
