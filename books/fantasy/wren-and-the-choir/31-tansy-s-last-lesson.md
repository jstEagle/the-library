## 31. Tansy's Last Lesson

Tansy had always known she would die twice.

The first death had happened long ago, in a country that no longer existed except inside her — the real one, the wide one, the one with an ocean you could walk beside for a week and never hear the end of. She had been a young woman then, crossing into Ashenmoor on a smuggler's road through the hills, carrying nothing but a satchel of dried fish and her grandmother's ring, and when she stepped over some invisible line in the grass the world had gone quiet around her in a way she couldn't name. Not silence. Quiet. The difference mattered. Silence was absence. Quiet was attention — as if the land itself had turned its head to look at her and decided, for reasons of its own, to let her pass.

She had lived fifty years inside that decision. And now, lying in the wrecked infirmary beneath the Conservatory with the ceiling breathing overhead like the flank of some enormous sleeping animal, she could feel the land finally finishing what it had started. It was noticing her. All the way through, this time.

The unraveling came for outsiders slowly at first, like frost finding the cracks in a wall. Tansy's hair had gone gray at thirty and white at forty, though her mother's people kept their color into their eighties. Her bones ached in weather that hadn't happened yet. And lately — these last weeks, since the ford, since Maren, since the siege above them where old Brannoc still paced the broken cloisters with a splinted arm and Isolde hadn't slept in two days — lately the forgetting had begun to move through her like water through sand. Names first. Then faces. Then the order of things: which came before which, morning before evening, seed before harvest, the small domestic grammar by which a life holds its shape.

She was losing the wrong memories, though. That was the cruelty of it. She was losing Ashenmoor — the market towns, the river names, the face of the ferryman she'd loved and buried — while the other country stayed bright in her. The real one. Grass to the horizon under a sky that went on past all arguing. Salt wind. Gulls hanging motionless over a harbor town whose name she would not lose, because it was not part of this place and so could not be taken from her by the same slow tide.

*Harrow Point*, she thought, holding it the way you hold a coal in cupped hands. *My grandmother's house at Harrow Point. The stairs down to the shingle. The smell of bladderwrack at low tide.*

The door opened. She knew the footsteps — Coral walked like someone perpetually about to apologize for taking up space, weight rolled to the outside of the feet, quick and soft. A thief's walk, even now. Even here.

"Tansy." Coral came and knelt by the cot, and their face did the thing it always did when they looked at her, the thing that had taken Tansy months to learn to read: nothing moved, but everything tightened. Grief held behind glass. "Isolde sent for me. She said—" Their voice caught and steadied. "She said you were asking for me."

"I'm always asking for you," Tansy said. "You just don't listen half the time. Sit down, love. I haven't got the breath for shouting."

Coral sat. Up close they smelled of ash and cold stone and, underneath it, that faint resonance Tansy had learned to feel rather than hear — the hum of a conduit, of a living instrument, of someone the Choir had tuned and never quite let go. She reached out and took their hand. Their fingers were cold. They were always cold; the boy hoarded warmth the way he hoarded everything else, against some imagined winter of the soul.

"I'm dying," Tansy said, because she had never seen the use of wrapping bad news in ribbon. "Not the way you're all frightened of. Not unraveling — not yet. Just dying. Plainly. Like a person. My heart is seventy years old and it has carried two countries and it's tired, and no magic in any world is going to argue with it. So we won't waste the time I've got left on pretending otherwise."

"Don't." Coral's voice cracked clean through. "Don't say it like that."

"Like what? Like a true thing?" She squeezed their hand. "Child. I have watched you carry more grief than any one spine should hold, and every hour of it alone, because you learned somewhere that asking for help costs more than bleeding. I'm not going to spend my last lesson teaching you how to lose me. I'm going to teach you how to keep me."

Coral looked at her then, really looked, and Tansy saw the question form behind their eyes — the thief's question, the merchant's question, the question that had kept them alive seven years in Vell: *what does it cost, and who else pays it?*

"That," Tansy said, "is exactly the look I needed. Good. Listen."

* * *

She made him bring the lamp closer first. Old habit — her grandmother had said you didn't teach anything worth knowing in the dark, because the dark kept what it was given.

"The Door needs a Conduit," she said. "That much everyone knows, or guesses. What nobody knows — what maybe only I know, because I'm the only fool alive who came in from outside — is why."

Coral frowned. "Because it takes power to open—"

"No. Power it has. It's been sitting on top of a god's dream for a thousand years; there's enough power under this floor to crack the sky. What it doesn't have is *proof*. Think, love. Use that clever thieving head of yours. The founders built the Door as an escape, yes, but also as a seal — a way out that could only ever be opened from inside by something that remembered outside. Because if anyone could open it, the god's death-scream gets out with them, and the world ends. So they keyed it. To memory. Real memory. Outside-memory. A Conduit can turn the lock, but only if the Conduit carries the key — and the key is a life lived under an unbroken sky."

Tansy watched the understanding arrive in Coral's face the way dawn arrives over water — slowly, and then all at once, and then too much.

"And if the Conduit doesn't carry it," Coral said quietly, "it pays instead. With everything. Its whole remembered life. Equal weight." Their hand had gone rigid in hers. "That's the arithmetic. That's what I figured out down there. That's why I was going to—"

"I know what you were going to do." Tansy's grip tightened, surprising them both; her hands had been bird-bones for years. "I heard you saying goodbye to that priest without saying it. I watched you memorize Pip's face like a woman memorizing a room she's leaving. Don't you dare look ashamed of it. It was the bravest stupid thing I've ever seen, and I once saw Brannoc fight a customs cutter with a frying pan." She laughed, and the laugh cost her, and she paid it gladly. "But you don't have to. Because I have a key, child. I've been carrying it for fifty years without knowing what it was for. Isn't that the way of keys?"

"The real world," Coral whispered. "Your memory of it. But Tansy — taking memories is what *I* do. Giving one — can you even—"

"Any thief worth salt knows the truth about locks, love: everything that opens can be made to close, and everything that closes can be picked. You taught me that yourself, back on the barge, when you showed me how your charms worked. You take a memory out whole or you take it in pieces. Well. I'm giving you mine whole. Every stitch of it. Harrow Point and the harbor and the ocean and the sky that doesn't stop. Fifty years I've kept it safe from this dying place, and now I know why. Nothing keeps a thing that long unless it's meant to be given."

"You'll lose it," Coral said. "You'll lose the last—"

"Love." Tansy pulled herself up on one elbow, and her voice came out with the full weight of seventy years behind it, the voice she'd used to talk crews through storms and children through fevers. "I am dying in a country that was never real, of a life that was spent keeping a stranger's secret. Everything I have of Ashenmoor is already going — the forgetting takes it nightly, like rent. But *that*" — she tapped her own sternum, hard — "*that* stays. Bright as the day I crossed the border. Do you know why? Because it isn't part of the theft. It's the one thing in me this place can't touch. And I will not let it be buried in me. A key buried in a dead woman is just a lump of iron. Give it to the lock."

Coral was crying. Silently, the way they did everything — tears sliding down without a single sob, without permission, as if even their grief had learned to steal. Tansy lifted her free hand and wiped their cheek with her thumb, rough as sailcloth.

"There's my girl," she said — using the old word deliberately, the one that fit no one neatly, the one she'd used for Pip and for Maren and for every stray the crew had gathered. "Now hush and hold still. This will feel like standing in a doorway with the sea wind coming through."

* * *

She closed her eyes and went looking for it, the way you go down stairs in a familiar house in the dark.

Harrow Point. Grandmother's kitchen with the blue smoke-stain above the hearth. The path down through the sea-pinks to the shingle, stones shifting underfoot with that particular deep clatter, each one wet and glossy as a licked sweet. The harbor at dawn, boats knocking gently against the quay, tar and fish and rope. And beyond the breakwater — the sea. Not this gray sketch-water of the unraveling country, not the static river at the ford, but the *sea*: moving in long green shoulders, folding over itself, endless, patient, older than any god's nightmare. And above it the sky, unbroken, running on and on, blue going to pale gold at the rim of the world, with clouds in it that cast shadows on the water and gulls crying in it like nails drawn across heaven.

*Take it,* she told the memory, the way you'd tell a child to jump into waiting arms. *All of it. Take the lot.*

And Coral — she felt it happen from the inside, felt the boy's gift open like a hand, felt her one true country lift out of her and pour across the gap between them, warm and vast and smelling of salt. It didn't hurt. That was the miracle she hadn't expected. It felt like being set down after carrying something so long you'd forgotten it had weight. For one shining moment she stood in the kitchen at Harrow Point again, both countries hers at once, grandmother's ring on her finger and the kettle singing—

—and then it was in Coral, and she was here, in the dim infirmary under the breathing ceiling, and the space where the sea had been was quiet. Empty. Light.

She opened her eyes. Coral was staring at nothing, lips parted, and the lamp-flame leaned toward them as if drawn, and for three heartbeats the air in the room smelled unmistakably of low tide.

"Oh," Coral breathed. "Oh, Tansy. The *sky*—"

"Aye." She settled back against the pillow, lighter than she had been in decades, drowsy as a child after a long swim. "It goes on, doesn't it. No edges. First thing I ever loved that I couldn't see the whole of." She smiled at them, and found her smile came easily, easier than it had in years. "It's yours now. Guard it better than you guarded your charms. And when you open that Door — and you *will* open it, none of your moping, I've seen your stubbornness up close — you give the land my regards. Tell it an outsider says it deserved better than its founders."

"It's not fair," Coral said, fierce and wet-eyed, gripping her hand. "Fifty years, and you don't even get to see it again—"

"Fair?" Tansy laughed, a real laugh, rolling up from somewhere the forgetting hadn't reached. "Child, I got to *give* it. Do you know how few people get to be useful at the exact moment they're most tired? I got to hand the sea to a conduit and watch them smell salt in a cellar under a dying god. That's not a tragedy. That's a *good death*, and I've earned the right to know the difference." Her eyelids were heavy now, and the heaviness was kind. "Besides. Who says I won't see it? You're going to carry me through, aren't you. In the only way that matters. When you stand on a real shore someday — and you will, all of you, even that great soft ferryman, even the twins — you look at the water and think of me. That's seeing. Better than eyes."

"Pip will want—"

"Pip gets my ring." She fumbled at her finger; Coral helped her, their hands steady when hers wouldn't be. "Grandmother's. Silver, worn thin on the inside. Tell him it's for holding weather, and he'll believe you, and he'll be right, because that boy bottles storms the way I bottled secrets. And tell Isolde—" Her breath caught, and she rode it out. "Tell Isolde she was right to stay. Tell Maren the ford wasn't her fault. Tell Brannoc he owes me a drink and can pay it at any shore in the real world. And Sable—" She stopped. Smiled, slow and sly, the last of her mischief surfacing like a gull over a wave. "Sable you'll tell yourself. He's yours to tell things to. That's rather the point of him."

Coral bent their head over her hand, and she felt them shaking, and she laid her other hand on their hair, on the soft unruly crown of them, and held on.

"Last lesson," she murmured. "Listen well, because I'm nearly done talking and I intend to enjoy the quiet. Love isn't a treasure, love. Everyone treats it like one — bury it, guard it, die for it. It's not a treasure. It's a *trade*. You give it away and it comes back changed, heavier, and you give it away again. That's the whole of the craft. Fifty years I traded with this crew of strays and I die richer than any queen of Vell. Now you've got the sea in you and a family at your back and a god downstairs waiting to be sung to sleep. Go and make the trade."

Her eyes were closing. The ceiling breathed. Somewhere far above, muffled through stone and ruin, she thought she heard singing — Sable's voice, or the Choir's, or the land remembering a hymn it had tried to forget — and it sounded, to her fading ears, wonderfully like gulls.

"Coral," she said, from very far away, from the shingle at Harrow Point where the stones clattered and the tide came in. "The key's in the lock. Turn it."

* * *

They buried her at dawn in the cloister garden, where the Conservatory's impossible green still held — roses blooming out of season, fed by the god's dreaming, the only corner of Ashenmoor that refused to unravel. Brannoc dug the grave himself, one-armed and weeping without shame. Isolde laid sprigs of rosemary that Maren, propped on a crutch, had insisted on cutting. Pip placed the silver ring in her folded hands, then took it back out, considered, and put it on his own thumb, where it spun loose and gleaming.

"She'd have wanted it used," he said defiantly, daring anyone to argue.

No one did.

Coral stood at the grave's edge with the sea inside them — the whole unbroken weight of it, gulls and shingle and the sky going on forever — and felt it settle into place among their stolen thousands like a keel laid in a ship. Around them the others waited, red-eyed, watching, and beyond the garden walls the gray static gnawed at the horizon, closer than it had been yesterday, eating another mile of the unfinished world.

Sable came to stand beside them, shoulder brushing theirs, and said nothing, because Tansy had taught him too, in her way, when to keep quiet.

Coral looked east, toward the heart of the Conservatory, where the stairs went down and down to the chamber of hanging stars and the Door that was waiting for its key.

"Tonight," they said.
