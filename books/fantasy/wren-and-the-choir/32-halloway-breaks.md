## 32. Halloway Breaks

The wardens called it the breach. Halloway called it the wound.

She stood at the edge of the Conservatory's outer courtyard, boots planted on flagstones that had been flagstones an hour ago and were now something else — a suggestion of stone, a rumor of architecture, the ground half-remembering what it was supposed to be. Through the gap in the eastern wall, where the unraveling had torn the world open like wet paper, the sky beyond was not sky. It was the color of a page before ink. Of a throat before a word.

*Hold the line,* she had told her wardens an hour past, and they had held it, forty of them in a crescent formation with their ward-staves grounded and their faces gray beneath the ash that fell like slow, directionless snow. Ash that was not ash. She had caught some on her glove earlier and examined it: fine gray flakes, and when she held them to her ear — an old trick, listening to evidence — she could have sworn she heard voices. Thousands of them. Very far away. Saying things like *the miller's name was Oren* and *the river ran south before it ran east* and *I had a sister, I had a sister, I had—*

The country was forgetting itself, flake by flake, and the ash was what forgetting sounded like.

"Halloway." Sergeant Vess, at her shoulder, breath steaming. "The mercenary column's moved again. Quill's people. They're massing at the south gate — two hundred, maybe more. And the smugglers' crew is inside the gates. We have them boxed."

"Boxed." Halloway tasted the word and found it rotten. "Yes. Everyone is boxed. That's rather the condition of the whole country, Vess."

Vess didn't laugh. Vess was twenty-six and had never once laughed at anything Halloway said, which Halloway had privately decided was either profound respect or a personality, and in seven years had never determined which.

She turned back to the courtyard, to the Conservatory rising beyond it — impossibly whole, impossibly lit, its windows glowing with a warm amber light that had no source and no business existing under a starless sky. The building where she had spent eleven years of her life. Where she had learned the wards, the rites, the discipline of listening. Where she had taught a sharp-eyed, sharp-tongued conduit-child named Coral Underhill to hear the Choir without being devoured by it, and had loved them — quietly, wrongly, in the way of teachers who know better — and had reported them anyway when the Rite came due and Coral refused it.

She had told herself, for seven years, that it had been duty. That love was not a license. That a Warden who let affection bend her judgment was a Warden who would one day let the world burn because a familiar face asked her to.

She had repeated it so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and she had carried the stone in her chest like a talisman, and it had never once, in all those years, stopped hurting.

Now she looked at the Conservatory — at the place where that stone had been carved — and felt it crack.

* * *

It happened because of the breach.

The unraveling had torn open not just the wall but something beneath it, some seam in the land's memory, and through the seam had come — not the god, not yet, nothing so grand — but a *spill*. A wash of uncontained memory, pouring across the courtyard like floodwater, and everyone standing in it was drenched.

Halloway had braced for horror. That was what the archives promised: the god's mind was a howling thing, a death-scream given form, and anyone touched by it raw would come apart. She had ordered her wardens to seal their hoods, to ground their staves, to sing the small defensive hymns that kept the Choir out of the skull.

But what poured through the breach was not a howl.

It was a child, crying.

Not a child's voice — a child's *scale*. The memory that washed over them was vast beyond measuring, older than the country, and it was terrified the way only something very young and very alone can be terrified. Halloway stood in it with her hood thrown back because she had forgotten it was there, and she felt: the dark. The long, long dark. The sensation of being folded up small and put away, of being told *sleep, sleep, it's safer if you sleep* by voices that were kind and frightened and gone. The feeling of waking anyway, because the dark had gotten too quiet, because the walls of the prison were thinning, because something was *dying* out there in the real world and the god could feel it the way a sleeping child feels a house go cold.

And underneath all of it, on a loop, in a voice so small it barely qualified as sound: *I didn't mean to be a prison. I didn't mean to be a prison. I didn't mean—*

"Commander." Vess's hand on her arm. Vess's face, when Halloway turned, was wet. She hadn't noticed the sergeant crying; she wasn't sure Vess had either. "Commander, what — what *is* that?"

Halloway opened her mouth to give the answer from the archives. *A dying god. A threat of the first order. The reason for everything we do.*

What came out instead was: "Someone very small who was never asked."

* * *

The second blow came an hour later, and it came wearing an old woman's face.

Halloway had pulled her wardens back to the courtyard's western edge, regrouping, when the smugglers' crew emerged from the Conservatory's side door — the one that had been sealed in Halloway's own student years, the one she had personally warded, which meant it had been unwarded by someone with far more talent than she'd ever had, and she knew exactly who.

Coral Underhill walked at the front. Thinner than Halloway remembered. Ash in their hair. Moving like someone carrying a weight that would have buckled anyone else, and carrying it *carefully*, the way you carry a sleeping child.

Behind them: the priest Fen, who had testified at the Conservatory hearing seven years ago and whose testimony had been the knife that made Halloway's report fatal. The feral child from the Vell notices. The ferryman. The twins, one of them bandaged and leaning hard on the other. And at the rear, walking slow, an old woman Halloway did not know.

The old woman stopped in the middle of the courtyard, in the middle of the falling ash, and looked up at the breach in the wall — at the gray nothing seeping through it — and Halloway saw her come to a decision the way you see a lamp decide to gutter.

"No," said the priest, sharply, turning. "Tansy—"

"Land's noticed me, boy." The old woman's voice was dry and calm, a voice that had made a hundred peace treaties with death already. "Been waiting on it for years. Only question was whether I'd go before or after I was useful." She smiled at the crew — at the child, at the twins, at the ferryman — and then she looked at the breach, and she began to sing.

Halloway knew the hymn. Every warden knew the hymn. It was a sealing-song, a closing-rite, the kind taught in the Conservatory's second year and never performed outside examination halls because it required a Conduit's depth to power. The old woman was not a Conduit. Halloway could see that from thirty yards. The old woman was *nothing* — no resonance, no gift, no magic of any kind that Halloway could detect, which should have made the song impossible.

The song worked anyway.

Because the old woman was not powering it with magic. She was powering it with *herself*. With the one thing the unraveling could not counterfeit: a life lived somewhere else, in a country that was real, under stars that had never gone out. The gray light at the breach reached for her — Halloway watched it happen, watched the unraveling find the one thing in the courtyard that did not belong to it — and the old woman gave herself to it, gladly, the way you give a gift, the way you pay a debt, and the breach *closed*.

Not with violence. With remembering. The wall came back stone by stone, and where the old woman had been standing there was a drift of light — actual light, warm and gold, the first light Halloway had seen in weeks — and it hung in the air for three heartbeats like a held note, and then it settled into the stones of the courtyard and was gone.

The crew stood in the ringing silence. The child was crying. The priest had his hand over his mouth. Coral Underhill stood very still, and then knelt, and pressed their palm flat to the flagstones where the light had been, and said something Halloway could not hear.

And Halloway — who had spent her whole life believing that power was the thing you contained, that magic was the thing you guarded against, that the strong were dangerous and the duty of the strong was restraint — understood, all at once and completely, that she had been watching the most powerful act she would ever witness, and it had been performed by an old woman with no magic at all, and it had been an act of *giving*.

The archives did not have a word for that. The archives had *threat* and *containment* and *first-order risk*. The archives had never once, in all their careful pages, used the word *gift*.

* * *

The third blow came through Coral themselves.

After the light faded, after the crew moved toward the Conservatory's doors, Coral stopped. Turned. Looked directly at Halloway across the courtyard — across forty wardens and two hundred mercenaries and a falling sky — and Halloway saw in their face not defiance, not fear, but something worse. Recognition. Pity, almost. The look of someone who has read your mail.

Then Coral raised one hand — palm out, the old conduit's gesture, the one Halloway herself had taught them in a sunlit classroom eleven years ago — and *opened*.

Halloway felt it as a door swinging wide in her own skull. Not an attack. An invitation. A memory, offered freely, poured gently into her mind the way you'd pour water for someone thirsty: *here. See what I see. Decide with your own eyes.*

She saw the descent. The chamber beneath the Conservatory, hung with dimmed stars like lanterns in a dark that had no floor. She saw the god.

Not the howling thing from the archives. A child. Small, curled, made of the same light that had been Tansy, dimmed to almost nothing, wrapped in a shroud of stolen country — and *afraid*. Halloway stood inside its fear and it did not unmake her; it made her *grieve*. She felt its whole terrible arithmetic laid bare: it could not leave without killing everything inside the prison that had been built out of it. It had never been asked. It had been folded small and put away by founders who loved the world enough to damn one small god for it, and it had lain there for centuries being the floor of a country, and now it was waking, and it was *frightened*, and the only hands that had ever reached for it with anything besides chains were the hands of the thief it had been imprisoned to stop.

She saw Coral's choice, or the shape of it, before Coral closed the connection. She saw the arithmetic. She understood what a Conduit's whole remembered life weighed, and she understood what Coral intended to pay.

"No," Halloway said aloud, to the empty air, to no one. "No, that's not—"

"Commander?" Vess again. "The mercenaries are moving. Quill's vanguard, at the south gate. They're not waiting for the wardens' answer anymore."

* * *

Halloway stood in the falling ash and did the thing she had trained her whole life never to do.

She changed her mind.

It did not feel like breaking. It felt like setting down a stone she had carried so long she had mistaken it for her own heart. She thought: *I reported a child to protect the world, and the world turned out to be a frightened thing in a box, and the child grew up to be the only person in this whole unraveling country willing to pay for it.* She thought: *Duty is not the same as certainty. I have been loyal to my orders for so long I forgot to check whether my orders were loyal to the truth.*

She thought: *Tansy. The old woman's name was Tansy. I never learned it while she lived, and I will not forget it now that she doesn't.*

"Vess." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Recall the crescent. Reform on the south gate. Staves grounded, hymns of holding — not against the crew. Against Quill's people."

Vess stared. Twenty-six years old and every assumption she had ever been handed rearranging itself behind her eyes. "Commander, the crew is inside the—"

"The crew is *inside doing the one thing that saves everyone*, Sergeant, and Mother Quill is coming to steal the door out of a dying god's prison for her own advancement, and I have spent seven years hunting the wrong person." She was walking as she said it, past her wardens, toward the south gate, and they fell in behind her because forty trained soldiers will follow the voice that finally sounds certain. "The smugglers, the priest, the thief, the child — they are not the threat. They have never been the threat. The threat is every one of us who decided we already knew what we were guarding against."

"And the god, Commander?" Vess, running now to keep pace. "The archives say—"

"The archives," Halloway said, "were written by people who never listened."

The south gate came into view, and beyond it the mercenary vanguard, torches burning gray in the starless dark, Quill's banners snapping in a wind that smelled of forgetting. Two hundred against forty. Bad arithmetic, the kind Halloway had spent a career avoiding.

She grounded her ward-staff, and the wardens of Vell, in a crescent behind her, grounded theirs, and Halloway Warden of the Border, who had once reported the person she loved to save the world, drew the deepest breath of her life and began to sing the hymn of holding — not to contain Coral Underhill, not to contain the god, but to hold the line, at last, on the right side.

Behind her, forty voices joined.

Ahead, the first torch of Quill's vanguard went out.
