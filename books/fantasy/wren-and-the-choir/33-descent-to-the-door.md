## 33. Descent to the Door

The stair went down farther than the world had room for.

Coral counted the steps at first — a habit from childhood, when the novices were made to climb the bell tower in silence and counting was the only permitted prayer. They lost the number somewhere past four hundred, somewhere past the point where the walls stopped being stone and started being something older and more patient, a substance like packed memory itself, dark and faintly warm to the touch. When they pressed their palm against it, they felt a pulse. Slow. Enormous. The heartbeat of something that had been sleeping so long its dreams had grown continents.

"Stop doing that," Sable said quietly behind them. "Every time you touch it, it stirs."

"Maybe I want it to stir."

"No," Sable said. "You don't. Not yet."

The others came after in single file, their lantern-light stretched thin along the spiral. Brannoc carried Maren on his back where the steps grew steep, her bandaged arm cradled against his shoulder; she had refused to stay above with the wardens' wounded and no one had been fool enough to argue with her twice. Isolde walked with one hand on the wall and her dead-star charts folded into her coat like scripture. Pip clung to the back of Coral's belt, small fingers hooked into the fabric, breathing too fast.

And Tansy was not there at all, and she was everywhere. Her last gift sat behind Coral's ribs like a second heart: grass under an open sky, the smell of a real ocean, a horizon that did not end in gray static but simply went on, blue into bluer blue, until the eye gave up before the land did. Whenever the dark pressed close, Coral reached for that memory the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. *This is what we are walking toward,* they told themselves. *This is what is real.*

The stair ended without warning.

One moment there were steps beneath their boots; the next, the floor opened outward into a vastness that swallowed the lanterns whole. Coral stood at the lip of a gallery of black glass, curved like the inside of a throat, and looked down into the prison-heart of Ashenmoor.

They had expected darkness. What they found was worse: light, everywhere, all of it dimmed.

Memories hung in the air below them — thousands upon thousands of them, suspended in the deep like fish in a frozen sea. Some were small and bright as sparks: a child's first snow, a loaf pulled from an oven, a dog shaking rain from its fur. Some were vast and slow, turning like whales: a harvest festival seen from a hilltop by four hundred people at once; a wedding that lasted three days; a funeral in which an entire town wept in unison. And threaded among them, rising higher than any of them, hanging in ranks that receded up into the unseen vault of the chamber —

Stars.

The stars of Ashenmoor. The ones that had gone out on the night everything began to unravel. They hung here, hundreds of them, each one a captured memory so old and so enormous that it had become a light unto itself — and every one of them was guttering. Not dark. Dying. Each star pulsed with a feeble, arrhythmic glow, like lanterns running out of oil, like breath drawn shallow in a sickroom.

"Oh," said Isolde, very softly. She had navigated by these stars her whole life, by their absence, by charts drawn from a sky that no longer answered. She took out one of her instruments, held it up, and put it away again without measuring anything. "Oh, you poor things."

"They're not stars," Coral said. Their voice came out strange, resonant, the chamber drinking it and giving it back changed. "They're memories. The founders took the sky too. Every star anyone in this country ever wished on — it's down here. It always was." They had known this, in the way one knows a thing in a dream and forgets it waking. Seeing it was different. Seeing it was like being shown your own skeleton. "We've been living under someone's recollection of night."

Pip's grip tightened on Coral's belt. "Which one's ours?"

Coral looked. It should not have been possible to find a single star among so many, but the Choir-magic rose in them unbidden, that old forbidden resonance humming up through their chest, and suddenly the chamber organized itself around their attention the way a crowd parts for a king. There — low, near the center, still burning a stubborn orange-gold — hung a small bright knot of memory: a rooftop in Vell, two children passing a stolen bottle of rain back and forth, laughing at nothing, the whole city glowing below them like spilled jewelry.

"That one," Coral said, and could not say more for a moment.

Pip looked for a long time. "It's still lit," he said finally, with the fierce practicality of a child who has learned that some things must be defended. "Good."

* * *

They made camp on the gallery because there was nowhere else and because no one could bear to go further without resting, though rest seemed absurd here, profane, like sleeping in a cathedral. Brannoc laid Maren down gently against the curved wall. Isolde and Sable divided the last of the waybread. The light of the dying stars did not reach them properly — it came to the gallery as a kind of twilight, sourceless and sorrowful — but it was enough to see each other's faces by, and that was almost too much.

Coral caught Sable watching them across the little circle of supplies. He had not spoken much since the gates. Since the chapel. Since the word *forgive* had been set down between them like a coin neither would pick up. But he had not left either, and he had walked every step of the descent at Coral's back, close enough that Coral could hear him breathe, and there are confessions that live entirely in proximity.

"The hymns my mother sang," Coral said, to the chamber as much as to him. "I used to think they were lullabies. Just sounds to make a child sleep. Now I know she was singing the god down. Every night. All those years." They turned a piece of waybread over in their fingers without eating it. "She knew. She must have known what this place was. She sang to it the way you sing to a frightened animal in a locked room."

"And then they took her voice anyway," Sable said. "When you were nine."

"When I was nine." The Rite of Quieting. The conservatory's great mercy, they had called it — silencing the conduits so the god could not hear them and wake. Coral had watched through a door as the cantors worked their knives of sound on a woman who never once cried out, because crying out was singing, and singing was forbidden. "I used to hate her for not fighting. I understand now. She wasn't surrendering. She was keeping the rhythm. Someone had to keep the rhythm or the thing in the dark would startle awake."

Below them, a star flickered hard, twice, and steadied. Somewhere in the chamber, a memory was being eaten. The unraveling had come underground too. Nothing was safe. Nothing had ever been safe.

Maren spoke from against the wall, her voice thinned by pain but level. "How much longer does this place have? Honestly."

Coral let themselves feel for the answer rather than think it — let the conduit-sense spread out through the chamber, touching the hanging lights, reading their burn. It was like putting a hand into cold water and feeling the current pulling everything toward a drain. "Days," they said. "Perhaps less. When the last star goes out, the country doesn't fade. It stops having ever been. Everyone in it stops having ever been. The Door won't matter anymore because there will be no one left to walk through it."

Silence, except for the vast slow pulse in the walls.

"Then we don't sleep long," Brannoc said, and began repacking the waybread.

* * *

Coral slept anyway — or fell into something like sleep, there at the edge of the gallery with Sable sitting watch an arm's length away — and dreamed the way they had not dreamed since childhood: in Choir.

In the dream they stood in the middle of the chamber, down among the hanging memories, and the memories drifted toward them like curious fish. A baker's hands kneading dough. A soldier's letter home, half-finished. A girl running through a wheat field that had never existed outside this room. Each one brushed against Coral's mind and poured itself in a little, and Coral understood, with the terrible clarity of dreams, that these were not prisoners. They were offerings. Seven hundred years ago, the founders had not only stolen a country — they had asked it to remember itself into this room, generation after generation, every life pouring its brightness down into the dark to feed the sleeping thing, to keep it dreaming gentle instead of waking screaming. The people of Ashenmoor had been tithing their joy to a god for seven centuries without knowing it.

That was why the memories felt warm. That was why stealing them had always felt like communion.

*Coral.*

The voice did not come through the ears. It came up through the floor, through the glass, through the bones.

*I know why you've come.* The child-voice, the drowning voice, the voice Coral had heard alone in the white silence — but closer now, unarmored, unashamed. *You've come to pay. Or you've come to refuse. Either way, you've come all this way just to sit with me awhile first, and I want you to know that I noticed. Nobody sits with me. They sing past me. They sing at me. You sat.*

"I'm not sitting yet," Coral said in the dream. "I'm looking."

*Does it frighten you?*

"Yes."

*Good. Then you're honest.* A pause, and the hanging lights swayed as if in wind. *The woman who walks above — the one who is fading — she showed you the real sky. I felt it when she gave it to you. Blue all the way up. Do you know what I miss most? Not freedom. Not worship. Color. Everything down here is remembered, and memory fades at the edges first. My edges are going. Soon I won't remember what color is, and then I'll be afraid, and then—*

"Then the scream," Coral said. "The one that unmakes the world."

*The one I've been holding in for seven hundred years.* The dream trembled. *Do you understand what that costs? To hold in a scream that long? I am so tired, little thief. I am so tired of being everyone's secret.*

Coral woke with salt on their face and did not remember crying. Sable was beside them instantly, hand hovering near their shoulder without landing, asking permission even now.

"It talked to me," Coral said.

"I know. We all felt it. The whole chamber rang like a struck bell." His eyes were hollow in the dying starlight. "What did it say?"

Coral looked down at the hanging lights, at the small gold one that was a rooftop and a bottle of rain and a boy named Pip, and lied, gently, the way you lie to someone when the truth is a door that only opens once.

"It asked for a song," they said. "That's all. It asked for a song."

Sable held their gaze a moment too long — he had always been able to tell when Coral stole something, even a truth — but the chamber shuddered faintly, dust sifting from the unseen heights, and there was no time left for calling lies by their names.

* * *

They heard Quill before they saw her.

Not footsteps — Mother Quill had been a smuggler before she was a queen of the gray market, and she moved like smoke moving uphill. What they heard was her voice, drifting down the stairwell, pitched to carry: warm, reasonable, absolutely merciless.

"—no need for violence, Warden. Your wardens hold the door above; my people hold the stair below; and the little songbird holds the key to all of it. Three parties, one prize. I've always said the best bargains are triangular." A pause, and then, silkier: "Besides. You want the Door closed forever, and I want it open, and only one of us can afford to be wrong. I suggest we let the bird decide, and both of us stand ready to thank or bury them accordingly."

Halloway's answer was too low to catch, but Coral knew its shape. They had grown up hearing that shape. Duty, spoken quietly, like a blade being sheathed.

"They're coming together," Isolde breathed, flattening against the wall. "Quill and Halloway. Together."

"Then we have minutes," Brannoc said. He lifted Maren onto his back again; she bit down on a strap and said nothing, which from Maren was a scream.

Coral stood at the edge of the gallery and looked down one last time at the prison-heart: the sea of suspended memories, the ranked and guttering stars, the small gold light of a rooftop and a bottle of rain. Below it all, barely visible in the deepest dark, was the thing the founders had built the whole country to hide — a shape like a door standing free in the void, tall as a cathedral gate, carved over every inch with the same hymn written ten thousand times in a script older than Ashenmoor. The Door. It did not glow. It *waited*, the way a held breath waits.

Tansy's memory stirred behind Coral's ribs — real grass, real ocean, real sky — and Coral understood, with a calm that felt like falling, that the old woman had known exactly what she was handing them, and why, and what it would cost. She had given them the key and let them walk down here carrying it, because if she had told them the price at the top of the stairs, they might have found the courage to refuse.

"Coral." Pip's voice, small and steady. "Whatever happens next. I bottled that rain myself. First thing I ever got right."

Coral knelt and pressed their forehead to the boy's, and the chamber's pulse beat through them both, slow and enormous, like the heart of a frightened child pretending to be asleep.

Then the light at the top of the stairwell bloomed — lanterns, wardens' white cloaks, the glint of Quill's rings — and the quiet before the choice came to its end.

Mother Quill stepped out onto the gallery, smiling, arms open, merciful as winter.

"There you are, songbird," she said. "Let's talk about doors."
