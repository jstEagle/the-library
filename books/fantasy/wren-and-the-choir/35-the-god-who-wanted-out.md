## 35. The God Who Wanted Out

The stair went down longer than the Conservatory was tall.

Coral counted the steps at first — a thief's habit, measuring escape routes even in the belly of the world — but lost the number somewhere past four hundred, when the walls began to change. The stone gave way to something older and softer, a substance like packed memory itself: gray at the edges, brightening toward the center of each wall as if every surface remembered being something else. Doors that had once been doors. Windows that had once been windows. A corridor in some other country's house, folded down here beneath the roots of Ashenmoor like a keepsake pressed between pages.

Sable walked behind them. He had not spoken since the gates. Neither had Pip, which frightened Coral more; Pip's silence had the specific gravity of a child carrying something heavy.

*He knows,* Coral thought. *He's known since the ghost market.* There would be time — there had to be time — for that reckoning later.

The air grew warm. That was the first wrongness. Everything above had been winter and unraveling, roads dissolving into gray static, villages forgetting their own names overnight. But here, deep beneath the ruin, the air was warm the way a sickroom is warm, close and breathing, and it smelled of cut grass and candle smoke and — Coral stopped on the stairs — of Vell's night market, of frying honey-bread and wet wool and the particular iron tang of coins passed hand to hand in the dark.

It smelled like everything they had ever stolen.

"It knows we're coming," Sable said quietly. His voice did not echo. The walls drank sound the way sand drank rain.

"It always knew," Coral said. "I think it's been listening to me my whole life."

* * *

The chamber at the bottom was round, and vast, and full of stars.

Not real stars — Coral understood that now, had understood it since the night the sky over Vell went black like a snuffed lamp — but the stars themselves, or the memory of them, hanging suspended in the dark at every height, dim lanterns on invisible threads. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one burned low and tired, guttering like candles at the end of a vigil, and between them drifted shapes that might have been clouds and might have been thoughts, slow as whales, turning over and over in the dark.

And in the center, sitting cross-legged on nothing at all, was the god.

Coral had expected terror. They had carried pieces of its mind inside their own skull for years without knowing — fragments hoarded among the wedding days and first kisses, shards of something enormous that had leaked into their thefts like blood into water. They had felt its rage in nightmares, its grief in fevers. They had braced themselves all the way down the stair for a monster.

What sat in the starlight was a child.

It looked perhaps eight years old. It wore a robe that kept changing — linen, then wool, then something like woven light — because, Coral realized, no one had ever dressed it, and it was wearing what it imagined robes should be. Its hair was dark and uncombed. Its feet were bare. It had been sitting in the dark for a very long time, in the way that a child sits when it has learned that no one is coming, spine curved, hands loose in its lap, eyes too old for the face around them.

"Hello," the god said.

Its voice was every voice. It was the widow in Vell laughing at her own wedding. It was Tansy describing an ocean she'd seen before the world was folded away. It was Coral's mother, saying their name in a kitchen that existed only in a stolen memory now — and Coral flinched, hearing that, feeling the theft of it land fresh all over again.

"Hello," Coral managed.

"You came." The child unfolded itself and stood, and did not fall, though there was nothing beneath its feet. "They never come. They send wardens. They send priests with knives made of doctrine. Once they sent a whole choir, and I sang back so loud the mountains forgot how to stand up." It tilted its head, studying Coral with an attention that pressed against their skin like deep water. "But you came yourself. You brought your little lanterns." Its gaze moved past Coral to Sable, to Pip, to Brannoc and the twins crowding the stair behind them. "You brought people who love you. That's clever. That's the strongest thing you could have carried."

"We didn't come to fight you," Sable said. His priest's steadiness held, mostly. His hand found Coral's shoulder. "We came to talk."

"Talking." The god smiled, and the smile cracked something in Coral's chest, because it was such a small, hopeful, human smile, worn by something that had eaten a country. "I haven't talked in six hundred years. I've only *been*. Do you know what that's like? To be awake inside your own prison, watching them build a world on top of you? They took a whole country from the earth — fields, rivers, cities, children — and laid it over me like a blanket, like a shroud, and told everyone inside it that it had always been there. And I lay still. I lay so still. Because if I moved, the blanket would tear, and everyone under it would fall out of the world."

Pip made a small sound. The god's eyes found him, gentle and terrible.

"You bottled a storm once," it said to him. "That was mine. A little piece of me, leaking out through a crack in the sky. You caught it in glass and sold it for bread. It was the kindest thing anyone ever did with a piece of me."

Pip stared. "I just wanted bread," he whispered.

"I know," the god said. "That's what makes it kind."

* * *

They spoke for what might have been hours. Time moved strangely in the star-chamber; Coral's sense of it stretched and pooled like spilled water.

The god wanted, more than anything, simply to be heard. It told them about the founding — the dying scream building in its chest six centuries ago, the scream that would have unmade the world's remembering, and the founders of the Choir who came not to kill it but to *hide* it, stealing an entire country from reality and wrapping it around their god like bandages around a wound. It told them about the Rites, the hymns, the conduits bred and trained to siphon off its terror in manageable doses, generation after generation, until the Choir grew proud and then afraid and then outlawed itself, leaving the machinery running with no one at the levers.

"And now the blanket is wearing through," it said. It lifted one small hand, and overhead, a star flickered and went out — and somewhere far above, Coral knew, a village was forgetting its own name. "Every year I get weaker and the stitches get looser. When I die — truly die, not sleep, *die* — the scream comes anyway. All of it. Every year of it, all at once. And everything inside this country ends. Not painfully. Just — ends. Like a word forgotten mid-sentence."

"There has to be another way," Maren said from the stair. Her crutch clicked against the stone. "There's always another way."

"There are three ways," the god said. It counted them on its fingers like a child reciting a lesson, and Coral understood suddenly that it had rehearsed this, had lain in the dark for centuries composing these sentences for whoever finally came. "One: I die screaming, and everyone dies with me. Two: you open the Door and drag me out into the real world — and I am too big for the real world now, little thieves. Six hundred years of growing in the dark. I would come out like a flood out of a broken dam, and the world outside would drown in me. Your oceans would forget their shores."

"What's the third?" Coral asked, though their heart already knew. Had known since the Waystation, since the fragment of song that had made the land remember itself green and starred for one impossible minute. Had known, maybe, since the first memory they'd ever stolen, since they'd learned the oldest law of their trade: *nothing is given. Everything is paid for.*

The god looked at them for a long moment.

"The third," it said softly, "is that someone sings me to sleep. The whole song. The complete Choir, the lullaby my mother-singers wrote and no one has ever finished, because finishing it takes everything the singer has. A lullaby is a trade, do you understand? You pour yourself into the sleeping one until they can hold the weight of it. Memory for memory. Weight for weight. I am made of six hundred years and an entire stolen country, and I cannot rest unless someone pays me in equal weight." Its voice frayed. For a moment it sounded exactly like what it was: a child, exhausted, asking for someone to stay until it fell asleep. "You would have to give me your whole remembered life. All of it. Every moment you have ever been. And then you would walk through the Door with everyone else, alive, breathing, whole — and empty. A person with no past. A cup with nothing in it."

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the drifting thought-clouds seemed to stop turning.

Coral stood very still.

They were a thief. They had spent seven years taking other people's best moments and selling them in the dark, telling themselves it was survival, telling themselves it was mercy — *she wasn't using that memory anymore; I gave it a better home* — and all the while carrying a hoard of borrowed joy that kept their own life hollow. They had built their entire self out of other people's remembering.

And now the arithmetic stood in front of them, plain as a ledger.

*Everything you have ever been,* the god had said. Coral turned the phrase over and found, with a strange calm, that it weighed less than they'd expected. Their memories of their mother — already half-stolen, half-sold. Their years in Vell — loneliness stitched through with small brightnesses. The crew. Pip asleep against Brannoc's side. Tansy's laugh, and Tansy gone now, dissolved into remembered light holding a breach closed above them. Sable.

Sable.

The flooded crypt. His voice rising through candle-smoke, finding the shape of theirs across a crowded dark. The chapel in the snow where they had chosen each other without forgiving, which was somehow more than forgiving. The way his hand fit at the back of their neck. Every single evening. Every single morning after.

*Coral understood the price.*

That was the terrible thing — not that the price was high, but that they understood it completely, instantly, the way a thief understands a lock. Equal weight for equal weight. A life poured out. And underneath the horror, in a place they would never say aloud, a small and shameful relief: they had spent seven years feeling like a hollow vessel full of other people's moments. Now the hollow had a purpose. Now the emptiness was a door.

"No," Sable said.

Coral hadn't spoken. No one had. But Sable was looking at them with a priest's old fluency in reading souls, and his face had gone white.

"Coral. Whatever you're doing in there — stop it."

"You don't know what I'm—"

"I know *you*." He crossed the starlit floor to them, and his voice dropped low, urgent, meant only for them, though every word landed in the god's hearing like a struck bell. "I know how you think. I watched you give away your last coin in Vell and call it business. I watched you buy back a widow's wedding day and return it to her daughter and pretend it cost you nothing. You are already standing at the scale, Coral. I can see it. Don't you dare decide alone."

"They'll all die otherwise." Coral's voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Everyone under the blanket. Every village that forgot its name. Everyone in the refugee columns. Halloway's wardens. Quill's mercenaries — well. Most of them." A breath. "Pip."

"Don't." Sable's voice cracked on it. "Don't you dare put Pip on the scale like that."

"He's already on it. Everyone's on it. That's the whole point of scales." Coral reached up and touched his face, memorizing it by touch, the way they'd once memorized locks — the stubble, the scar at his brow, the wet at the corner of his eye that he would deny later. "Six hundred years, Sable. It's been alone in the dark for six hundred years, and it's a *child*, and it's terrified, and nobody — not the founders, not the Choir, not the wardens, not one single soul in this whole stolen country — ever once asked it what it wanted."

"What does it want?" Sable whispered.

"To not be alone when it goes to sleep," Coral said. "Same as anyone."

Overhead, the dimmed stars burned on, patient as vigil-candles. The god watched them with its too-old eyes, and said nothing, because it had made its offer, and the offer was honest, and honesty was all it had left to give.

Behind them, at the top of the long stair, faint as a heartbeat, came the sound of the Door beginning to open — and the sound of many, many feet, descending.
