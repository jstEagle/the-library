## 36. The Choice

The Door did not look like a door.

That was the first thing Coral understood, standing at the edge of the god's prison-heart with the dust of the siege still sifting down through the dark like gray snow. It looked like a wound in the shape of a doorway — a tall rectangle of absence cut into the memory-stuff of the world, through which, if you leaned close and let yourself believe, you could see grass. Real grass. Not the sketched, half-finished fields of unraveling Ashenmoor but true green, wet with dew that had fallen from an actual sky, under stars that burned because they were made of burning and not because someone remembered them so.

The light that came through it was morning-colored. Coral had forgotten — no, they hadn't forgotten, they had never known — what morning smelled like when it belonged to a whole world.

Behind them, dimmed to lanterns on their chains, the stolen stars hung in the vast chamber like a nursery mobile above a sleeping child. And below, coiled through the floor of the place, breathing so slowly that one breath took a season, lay the god.

It was smaller than Coral had expected. Smaller than the terror of it, smaller than the archives' warnings, smaller than Halloway's lifetime of dread. It was the size of a child curled against cold, knees drawn up, its long pale hands over its face as though it could not bear to be seen — or as though it were playing a game whose only rule was *if I can't see you, you can't see me*. Its breath stirred the hanging memories around it: snowfall in a village square, a wedding feast, a mother's hands kneading bread, all of them drifting like leaves on the slow tide of its dreaming.

*You came back,* it said. It did not speak aloud. It never had. Its voice arrived the way warmth arrives, the way grief arrives — already inside you before you notice the door it used. *Nobody comes back.*

"I said I would."

*People say things.* The child-god shifted. One eye appeared between its fingers, enormous and gray as a winter sea, and fixed on Coral with a patience that was worse than hunger. *You brought them. The ones who smell of outside.*

"Some of them." Tansy's absence stood among the crew like a person-shaped gap in a fence. Coral had stopped trying not to look at it. "And there are more coming. Thousands. Everyone the land can gather. They're walking toward this place even now."

*Through the Door.* The god's voice went strange, thin, a wire drawn tight. *Out of me. Into the world I was taken from.*

"That's why I've come. To ask what it costs."

The eye blinked. When it opened again something had changed behind it — the child-thing receding, the ancient thing underneath rising like a stone surfacing in a pond.

*You know what it costs,* it said. *You have always known. You have been paying installments of it your whole life, little thief. You take memories out of people and put them in glass. What do you think I am?*

Coral's mouth was dry. "Say it anyway."

The god uncurled, slow as a season turning. Around it the suspended memories shivered.

*A Conduit opens the Door by singing. That part is true, and simple, and a lie by omission. The Door is not locked. The Door is held shut — by me, from my side, with everything I have left, because if it opens while I am awake I will pour through it, and what I am will drown the world the way my death-scream would have. My founders were right about that much. Cowards, thieves, right.* A sound moved through the chamber that might once have been laughter. *So the singer must give me something to sleep on. Something heavy enough to hold me under while they pass. Not a lullaby. A life.*

"How large a life?"

*All of it.* The word fell like a gate dropping. *Every remembered thing you carry. The ones you stole and never sold — the thousands in you, the widow's wedding, the baker's first love, all of it, mine to dream instead of yours. And yours too. The ones that were yours before anyone taught you to steal. Your mother's face. The Conservatory corridors. The Rite you refused. Your name — do you understand? Not the name people call you. The name underneath, the one you were before you were Coral Underhill. Everything. You will walk through that Door as a stranger to yourself, and you will not grieve it, because grief is also a memory, and I will be holding it for you.*

The silence afterward was very long. Somewhere far above, muffled through a thousand fathoms of unraveling country, Coral could hear it — the sound they had been hearing for days now, growing nightly: the soft continuous slither of the world forgetting itself. Regions going out like lamps. Rivers running backward into their own names.

"And if no one pays?" Coral asked. "If we just wait? If the land fades gently, like Halloway wanted—"

*Then I wake as it fades, because fading is pain, and pain wakes me. And I come through after them, screaming, into a world with nowhere left to hide me.* The god's hands closed again over its face. *I do not want to scream. Do you hear me? I have been listening to my own death for ten thousand years and I do not want it. Give me a life to live instead. Any life. Yours. Let me dream being you, just for a little while, until the Door closes behind the last of them and I can sleep the honest sleep.*

Coral thought: *the arithmetic is terrible and it is also simple.* One remembered life against a nation's. Against the world's, maybe — they still didn't truly know what the god's rage would do beyond the borders of its prison, and Sable's white face in the archive-lamplight, three nights ago, saying *unmake, the word was unmake*, had told them everything estimation could not.

They thought of Sable's voice in the flooded crypt, the hymn rising off black water like heat off summer stone. Of Pip's laugh, sharp and startling as a bottle breaking. Of Brannoc's broad hand on their shoulder the night after the ford. Of Isolde navigating by dead stars, Maren learning to write with her ruined arm, Tansy dissolving into remembered light and smiling as she went.

They thought: *I cannot tell them. If I tell them, they will find another way, or refuse one, or break themselves trying to spare me, and there is no other way, and there is nothing to spare.*

"Show me how the song goes," Coral said.

* * *

They climbed back up through the conservatory's bones as the false dawn came — Ashenmoor's dawn now, starless, a bruise-colored graying at the edges of the ruin. The crew had made camp in the great shattered atrium where the god's dreaming kept the stones whole. Someone — Brannoc, judging by the scale of it — had gotten a fire going in a hearth the size of a chapel door, and the smell of tea and toasted grain hung in the air like an argument against the end of the world.

Beyond the broken gates, visible across the frost-gray plain, the refugees had begun to arrive. Torches in ragged lines, miles of them, converging on the Conservatory the way iron filings converge on a lodestone. Coral watched them and felt the map-whispers in their skull hum agreement: *this way, this way, the Door, the Door.* The land itself was herding its children home.

"You look," said Sable, falling into step beside them, "like someone who has been negotiating with something enormous."

"I have."

"And?"

"It agreed to my terms." Which was even true.

He studied them with those gray eyes that had been reading Coral since a crypt full of candle-smoke and forbidden hymns, and Coral braced for him to push — he was a priest, pushing was his whole vocation — but the camp needed tending and Maren needed her bandages changed and the moment drowned in work, the way merciful moments do.

The day went by in preparations, and Coral moved through it like a thief through a rich man's house, lifting small treasures with both hands.

* * *

In the afternoon they found Pip on the roof of the collapsed refectory, legs dangling over the drop, shaking a bottled wind next to his ear the way other children shook presents.

"You're supposed to be resting," Coral said.

"You're supposed to be dead seven times over by now, and here we both are." He scooted sideways to make room. From him it was practically an embrace. Coral sat.

Below, the refugee lines crawled. Pip followed their gaze. "Is it true they can all go through? Out? To real?"

"So I'm told."

"And us? The crew?" He turned the bottle in his fingers. "Brannoc says he'll open a ferry on the other side. A real river, with real water that goes somewhere. Isolde says she wants to see stars she didn't memorize. Maren says—" His voice snagged. He covered it with bravado, badly. "Maren says she'll learn to swim. With two arms or none."

"What do you say?"

Pip was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, the bravado had drained out and left behind the actual child, the one he showed perhaps twice a year, in the dark, when he thought no one could see.

"I say I don't want to go anywhere you're not."

The words landed in Coral's chest like a knife laid flat — no edge yet, only weight. *Not yet,* they reminded themselves. *Not until the last minute. You owe him the last minute.*

"Wherever I end up," Coral said, "I expect letters. Long ones. Full of weather you've bottled and trouble you've caused. I want to be able to say I knew you when."

"You'll always know me when." Pip bumped his shoulder against theirs, fierce and brief. Then, because feelings of that size frightened him worse than wardens did, he thrust the bottle at them. "Here. Wind off the Gray Sea. First storm I ever caught. It's rubbish, honestly, mostly it just smells like salt and my own sweat, but—" He shrugged. "For luck."

Coral closed their hand around the little glass storm and thought: *this is the last gift I will ever receive that I will remember receiving.* They made themselves look at Pip's face — the chapped lips, the soot in his eyebrows, the gap in his teeth — and carved it deep enough to hold even if the holding wouldn't last.

"Thank you," they said, and meant six other sentences besides.

* * *

Evening. Isolde and Maren sat by the fire, Isolde mending a sail-cloth cloak with stitches neat as notation, Maren watching the flames with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest. They made room without looking up; twins made room for you with their whole bodies before their eyes ever agreed to.

Coral sat. For a while nobody talked. The fire cracked. Across the plain, ten thousand cookfires answered it.

"We were talking," Isolde said finally, "about what we'll do first. Outside."

"Maren wants to see the sea."

"Maren has always wanted to see the sea. She read about it in a book when she was nine and has been insufferable ever since."

"I want to *hear* it," Maren said. "Everyone forgets the hearing. Waves don't stop. All day, all night. Imagine sleeping inside a sound like that."

"Imagine sleeping at all with that racket," said Isolde, but softly, the way you contradict someone you'd die for.

Coral listened to them build the future plank by plank, and understood that they were doing it on purpose — that the twins, who navigated by dead stars and therefore knew everything about reading light that wasn't there anymore, had looked at Coral tonight and seen whatever it was that shows in a face carrying a secret of this weight. They weren't asking. They were laying planks within earshot, so that Coral could stand on them.

"We'll need a fourth for the house we're going to buy," Maren said, eyes still on the fire. "Somewhere coastal. Isolde insists on somewhere with decent markets."

"Someone has to keep us supplied."

"Someone has to keep you fed, is what she means." Isolde bit her thread. "You eat like a heron, Coral. All angles and no appetite."

"I'll write," Coral said. "When I'm settled. Wherever I end up. I'll write you both, and you'll complain about my handwriting, and it will be exactly like this."

"Exactly like this," Maren agreed, and reached over with her good arm, and gripped Coral's wrist once, hard, the way sailors grip — meaning *hold fast*, meaning *I see you*, meaning everything the words couldn't be trusted with.

* * *

Later, Brannoc found them at the edge of the camp, looking out at the torch-lines. He stood beside them the way he did everything: solidly, without hurry, like terrain.

"Ferryman's blessing," he said at last, "is a short one. We're not a wordy trade." He cleared his throat. "Safe crossing. Fair current. May the far bank be worth the water."

"Amen," Coral said.

He huffed — almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "Tansy told me, near the end. Not everything. Just that the Door would want feeding, and that I shouldn't argue with whoever offered to feed it, because arguing with Conduits is like arguing with rivers." His big hands folded, unfolded. "I didn't understand her. I'm telling you I didn't understand her. In case it matters later, that I tried."

Coral's throat closed. "It matters."

"She also said—" He stopped. Started again, gentler than Coral had ever heard him. "She said you'd try to make it look like goodbye instead of what it was. And she said to let you. Said it was a kindness, letting a brave person choose the shape of their own leaving." He put his hand on Coral's shoulder then, briefly, enormously. "So. Safe crossing, Coral Underhill. Whatever bank you land on."

He walked away before they could answer, which was the greatest mercy the night contained.

* * *

Sable found them at midnight, or the hour that would have been midnight if the stars existed to mark it. He came quietly, the way he did everything that mattered, and sat beside them on the fallen lintel of the Conservatory gate with his knees drawn up and his hands loose between them, priest's hands, singer's hands, scarred from the ford.

"The lines are settling for the night," he said. "Halloway's wardens are running the queues. She's organized. I'll grant her that till my dying day." He paused. "Which I intend to be a long one. Ours, I mean. Yours and mine. I've decided we're getting old together somewhere damp and unfashionable, and you're going to hate it, and I'm going to sing anyway."

"Sable."

"Hm?"

"There's a hymn," Coral said. "The one you sang in the crypt. The first night. The one that found me across a room full of strangers." Their voice held. They were almost proud of it. "Sing it tomorrow. At the Door. When everyone crosses — sing it loud enough to reach the far side. Promise me."

"That's an odd request for a farewell—"

"It's not a farewell. It's a commission." *Lie beautifully, thief. It's the last craft you have.* "I want to hear it in a place with acoustics worthy of it. Consider it my fee. I never did pay you for that night in Vell."

His smile started slow. "You're buying my singing?"

"Desperate times."

He laughed — really laughed, head tipped back toward the starless sky — and then he caught himself and looked at Coral, and the laughter drained out of his face by degrees, the way color drains at dusk, and what was left underneath was the thing he had been carrying since the ruins of the archive: the look of a man watching a door close that he cannot name.

"Coral. Look at me and tell me there isn't something you're not saying."

And here was the trap of it: the truth was the one thing they could not tell, and lying to Sable Fen was the one thing they had sworn — privately, in a hundred unspoken moments — never to do again. So they gave him the third thing, the truest lie available.

"I'm saying that whatever happens tomorrow," Coral said, taking his hand, fitting their fingers between his scarred ones, "I chose this. All of it. Every road from Vell to here. Given the choice a hundred times, I make the same one every time, and it's always the one that leads to you. Don't let tomorrow complicate that."

He searched their face for a long, terrible moment. Then he bowed his head and pressed his brow to theirs, and his breath came out shaky, and he said, "Whatever you're carrying in there — you don't have to carry it alone. That's the whole sermon. That's the only one I've got."

"I know," Coral whispered. *Forgive me. Forgive me. This alone is the one thing you cannot do for me.* "Sing tomorrow. Promise."

"On my life," he said, meaning it as rhetoric.

Coral meant it as arithmetic.

* * *

They did not sleep. Toward the coldest hour they went back down alone, past the sentries, past the fire, down the stairs the god's dreaming kept whole, into the chamber of hanging memories and lantern-stars.

The child-god was waiting. It always would be, Coral understood now, until someone gave it something better to wait with.

"Tomorrow," Coral said. "At midmorning, when the last of them are through. You'll feel the song start. Don't fight it. It's not a weapon. It's — think of it as a story. The best one I know. It's about a thief who spent their whole life carrying other people's brightest moments, and never once got to keep any, and found out at the end that giving them away was the keeping."

The god lowered its hands from its face. Both eyes now, gray and vast and terribly young.

*Will it hurt you?*

"No," said Coral, and was surprised to find it nearly true. "It will un-hurt me. All of it at once."

*I'll be careful with them.* The voice had gone very small. *Your moments. I'll keep them the way you kept the widow's wedding day — perfectly, like glass, like something holy that belongs to someone else.* A pause, and then, with the nakedness only the very young and the very ancient can manage: *Will some of them be about the singing priest?*

The question broke something open in Coral's chest, painlessly, like ice going out of a river in spring.

"Yes," they said. "Almost all of them. Be careful with those most of all."

*I will.* The god curled back down among its chains of stars, drawing the suspended memories close around itself like blankets. *Little thief. Little conduit. Little choir-of-one. Thank you for coming back.*

Coral climbed toward the surface and the gray false dawn, and did not look back, because they had learned from the best — from a widow in Vell who had sold her wedding day and walked home lighter — that some things are carried better forward than behind.

Above, the first of the refugees were already moving. Below, the Door stood open on real morning. And in Coral's throat, rising like water finding its level, quiet as a promise and total as a grave, the first note of the last song waited to be sung.
