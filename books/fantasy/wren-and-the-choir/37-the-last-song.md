## 37. The Last Song

**Coral**

The chamber beneath the Conservatory was not a room. It was a held breath.

Memories hung in the dark around Coral like fruit on unseen branches — thousands of them, glimmering faintly, the stored harvest of seven years of theft and a lifetime before that. The dimmed stars drifted among them, close now, close enough to touch, each one a lantern gone gray, each one humming a note too low for ears and too deep for bones. And at the center, where the floor should have been, the Door.

It was smaller than Coral had imagined. A door should be. It was made of ordinary wood, scarred and iron-banded, the kind found in any kitchen in any village in Ashenmoor, and it stood alone in the void with nothing behind it but the slow pulse of a god's sleeping heart.

*You came back,* said the god, in the voice of everything at once — in the creak of the Door, in the drip of memory-light, in the small wet sound of Coral's own breathing. *Nobody comes back.*

"I came to pay," Coral said.

Their hands did not shake. That surprised them. Seven years of hiding, of selling other people's joy by the charm-weight, of flinching from every warden's boot on Vell's cobblestones — and now, at the end, their hands were steady as a singer's on the first note.

Behind them, up the long stair, they could hear the crowd. The people of Ashenmoor had come — all of them who could still walk, still remember their own names, streaming out of the unraveling country toward the one place the land had kept whole. They stood in the corridors above, in the shattered courtyards, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the snow, waiting for a door that most of them did not understand and all of them needed.

Sable was up there. Pip was up there. Brannoc was carrying Maren on his back because she could no longer walk, and Isolde walked beside them with her sister's hand gripped in hers, and somewhere in that press of bodies Halloway stood with her wardens turned outward, holding back Mother Quill's routed mercenaries, holding open a corridor of steel so that strangers could pass.

Coral had said goodbye without saying it. That had been the plan. Feed the god, sing the Choir complete, let the weight of a whole remembered life settle the child-god into sleep, and do it quietly, while the Door stood open, while the people filed through, while nobody looked too closely at the Conduit standing at the threshold.

They had not counted on how loud silence could be.

*What will you give me?* the god asked. It sounded young when it asked. It sounded like Pip, once, haggling over a bottled storm. *I know what you carry. I have felt it all my life, pressing against the walls of me. Thousands of moments that were never yours.*

"Every one," Coral said. "And mine besides."

The hum changed pitch. Somewhere far above, through leagues of stone and snow and failing country, the last stars flickered.

*You don't understand what you're offering.* For the first time, the god's voice cracked. *I have been drowning in memory since before your country had a name. I know its weight. I would not wish it on —*

"You've been drowning alone." Coral knelt at the threshold of the little wooden door, the way they had knelt at their mother's bedside, the way they had never knelt for anyone else. "That's the difference. I won't be alone. You'll hold it with me. Or I'll hold it with you. Whichever way you want to say it."

The memories stirred. All of them. The widow's wedding day, returned and re-stolen and finally given back. The taste of rain in Vell's night market. Tansy's real ocean, green-gray and endless, bequeathed in dying light. Sable singing in the flooded crypt, candlelight on water, the first time Coral had heard their own magic answered. Pip asleep against their shoulder on the ferry, smelling of river mud and stolen apples. Their mother's face — her actual face, recovered from the Conservatory's vaults, worn smooth from handling.

And underneath all of it, oldest and heaviest: their name. Coral Underhill. The small bright coin of self that every other memory had been minted from.

*I can't take only some,* the god whispered. *You know that. Equal weight or nothing. That was always the arithmetic.*

"I know," Coral said. "I've known since the ford, when I saved Maren and felt how much it cost. I've known since the Waystation. I just needed to walk here slowly enough to stop being afraid of it."

They stood. They rolled their shoulders the way Sable did before a hymn, an unconscious theft, the last thing they would ever steal. And they began to sing.

Not a fragment this time. Not a smuggled verse sold by candlelight. The Choir complete — the whole forbidden architecture of it, rising out of them like water finding its level, filling the prison-chamber wall to wall.

The first thing to go was Vell.

**Pip**

Pip had run. That was the whole of it, later — that when Coral had told them all to stay above, stay with the crowds, help the old ones through the Door, Pip had waited exactly as long as it took for the grown-ups to start crying and hugging and arguing, and then done what Pip did best, which was go where they weren't wanted.

The stair down was longer than any stair had a right to be. Pip took it two at a time, bare feet silent on stone, past wards that didn't spark at them anymore because the whole country's magic was awake now and busy with bigger things. The air got warmer. The dark got full. And then Pip rounded the last turning and stopped, flattened against the wall, mouth open.

Coral was singing.

Pip had heard Coral hum. Once, drunk on victory after the ford, half a bar of something before they'd caught themselves and gone quiet as a locked box. This was not that. This was a flood. The song came out of Coral and filled the chamber and the hanging memories caught fire with it, every stolen moment lighting up like the lamps of the night market, and Pip understood, in the way children understand things adults spend lifetimes missing, exactly what was happening.

Coral was giving it all away.

"No," Pip said. Out loud. Stupidly. Loudly.

Coral's eyes opened. They found Pip across the chamber without the song faltering, and they smiled — that crooked, guilty, market-day smile, the one that meant *caught you, little thief* — and shook their head, once, gently.

*Keep singing,* said something enormous and sad and young, from everywhere. *He shouldn't watch. But he came. Let him come.*

Pip ran. Pip crossed the chamber faster than they had ever run from any warden, and threw both arms around Coral's middle, and Coral sang on over the top of Pip's head, one hand coming down to rest there, warm and steady, while the memories began to leave them.

Pip felt them go. Felt them pass through Coral's body like fish through a net — Vell first, the whole gray city, the sewers, the market stalls, the smell of frying onions at midnight — and each one left Coral a little lighter, a little more transparent, a little more like paper held up to a flame.

"Don't," Pip said into Coral's coat. "Don't. I'll give mine instead. I've got loads. I've got the storm, I've got the ferry, I've got —"

"You've got a whole life ahead of you," Coral said, and their voice was going strange, going thin, going beautiful, harmonizing with itself. "That's worth more. Keep it. That's an order, street-thief."

"I don't take orders."

"I know." The hand on Pip's head ruffled their hair, clumsy with fading. "That's why I'm keeping you."

Above them, muffled by leagues of stone, the first star reignited.

**Sable**

Sable felt it before anyone told him. He was at the head of the column, holding the Door-line open with Halloway's wardens, passing refugees through into a dawn that smelled of grass and salt — *real* grass, *real* salt, the world outside the stolen country pouring its ordinary miracles through the little wooden door — when the song reached him.

It came up through the soles of his boots. Through the priest-marks on his palms, which had been cold and dead for eleven years and now burned like brands. He knew the melody. Of course he knew it; he had sung its fragments in flooded crypts for copper coins, he had testified against a child for singing it, he had built his whole penance around its absence. But this was the Choir entire, unbroken, the way it must have sounded in the founders' age, the way it had sounded the day they stole a country out of the world — and it was being sung by one person, alone, paying for it syllable by syllable.

"Sable." Halloway's hand on his arm. Her face was gray. "The resonance — it's coming from below. From the Door itself. Who's down there?"

And Sable thought: *the stair is long, and Pip runs fast, and Coral told us all to stay above, and Coral has been saying goodbye for three days without saying a word of it.* He thought about the way Coral had looked at him that morning in the ruin-chapel, after everything, after the testimony and the snow and the choosing-anyway — had looked at him like a woman memorizing a room she was leaving.

He thought about the widow's wedding day, and how Coral had carried it for years rather than lose it, and what it must cost to set down a thing like that willingly.

"No," Sable said, aloud, to a god, to a country, to himself. "No, no —"

He broke from the line. Halloway shouted something behind him — *the column, the column needs you* — and he heard her take his place, heard her voice steadying frightened people with a warden's flat calm, and then he was running down the long stair with his dead marks blazing, taking the steps three at a time, too late, knowing already that he was too late, running anyway because some part of love is just refusing arithmetic.

The chamber opened around him and he saw it all at once: Coral ablaze with song, translucent as sea-glass, the memories streaming out of them in rivers of light. Pip clinging to them, screaming things Sable couldn't hear over the music. The stars overhead kindling one by one, gray lanterns catching flame, until the prison's ceiling was a sky again — a real sky, starred, the first sky Ashenmoor had seen since the night everything went dark.

And the Door, standing open, spilling morning.

"Coral!" His voice tore out of him. "Coral, stop, I'll pay it — I'm a priest, I was consecrated, I can carry —"

Coral turned their head. Just their head; the rest of them was mostly music now. And they looked at him with eyes that were already forgetting how to be Coral's eyes, and they smiled, and they sang a line just for him, pitched under the great chord where only he could hear it:

*Two liars, recognizing each other's true names.*

"That's not fair," Sable sobbed. "That was ours. You can't give that away, that one was *ours* —"

But it was already going. He watched it go. Watched the crypt go — candlelight on black water, his voice, their face in the audience, the first evening, the whole seed of them — watched it lift out of Coral like smoke and pour into the vast young dark of the god, and the god received it the way a starving creature receives bread, tenderly, reverently, and the stars above blazed brighter for it.

Vell went. The conservatory childhood went. The mother's face went last of the faces, and Coral sang her name — sang *their* name, *Coral Underhill*, the final coin, spending it — and then there was no one singing. There was only the Choir, complete, sustained a moment longer by momentum and grace, hanging in the chamber like light in water.

Then silence.

The starless country ended. The stars burned. The Door stood open on a real morning, and through it, in the world outside, ten thousand exiles stood under an unbroken sky watching constellations they had never seen ignite over a sea they had never smelled, and wept, and did not know yet what the stars had cost.

Coral fell.

Sable caught them. He was fast enough for that — fast enough for that one thing. He gathered them off the cold stone with the marks on his hands still burning, and Coral weighed almost nothing, emptied of everything, a purse turned out, a bell after the ringing. Their eyes were open. Gray as the country had been. Looking at him.

Looking *through* him.

"Hello," Coral said politely. Their voice was their own and entirely strange, a stranger's voice, a voice with no history in it at all. "Are you — were we singing? I feel like someone was singing."

Pip was howling. Sable held on. Above them, through the open Door, the dawn came down the long stair like a tide, and the last note of the last song faded into it, and did not echo, and was gone.

Somewhere in Sable's coat pocket, unnoticed, a small glass charm — bought in a ghost market, carried without knowing why — grew suddenly, impossibly warm.
