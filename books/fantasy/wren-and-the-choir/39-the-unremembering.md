## 39. The Unremembering

*Wake.*

That is the first word. Not a word, exactly — more a shape the dark makes when it decides to let go of you. Wake, says the shape, and Coral wakes.

There is sand under their cheek. They know this because they can feel it, grain by grain, each one a small cold fact pressed against their skin. Sand. Salt. The sound of water that does not end, that goes on and on past any border any ward ever drew, and beneath it a wind carrying something they have no name for except *outside*.

They open their eyes.

The sky is enormous. That is the second thing. It goes up forever, pale gray-blue at the edges of morning, and threaded through it — thin as thread, faint as breath on glass — are stars. Stars that have not gone out. Stars that burn the way stars are supposed to burn, patient and indifferent and impossibly far away, and Coral lies in the sand looking at them and feels nothing at all.

That should frighten them. They understand, distantly, that it should. A person looks at the sky after everything and feels *something*. But there is only the looking, and the cold coming up through the sand, and the long hush of the sea.

*Who am I?*

The question arrives the way questions do, and then it keeps going, walking off the edge of them into empty air, and nothing answers. Not silence, even. Silence would be an answer of a kind. This is just — absence. A room where furniture used to be. They reach inward the way they have reached inward ten thousand times, into the deep shelves of themselves where memories hang like lanterns, and their hands close on nothing.

Not nothing. Worse than nothing. The *shape* of things. Hooks without coats. Doorways without rooms. They know there was a mother; they cannot find her face. They know there was a city called Vell; they cannot find its smell. They know — this comes with a physical ache, a hand-shaped bruise over the heart — that there was someone. Someone who sang. The ache is specific. The ache has a name-shaped hole in the middle of it, and the hole will not be filled, and Coral lies on the strange sand under the unbroken stars and grieves for a person they cannot remember grieving for.

A gull cries somewhere. The tide comes in. The tide goes out.

Coral breathes.

* * *

Later — minutes or hours, time moves strangely here, moving like water instead of like wards — a woman brings them soup.

She is wrapped in three shawls against the sea wind, and she crouches beside them on the sand with the careful manner of someone who has learned not to startle the broken ones. There are many broken ones here. Coral can see that now that they are sitting up: the beach behind them rises to dunes, and beyond the dunes stand tents — hundreds of them, thousands, canvas and tarpaulin and sailcloth, a city made of whatever could be carried. Smoke from cookfires. Children running between the guy-ropes. People standing at the tideline staring out at the water as if the sea might explain itself.

"Real," the woman says, following their gaze to the water. "It's real, love. First thing everyone asks." She presses the bowl into Coral's hands. It is fish broth, too hot, too salty, and it is the best thing Coral has ever tasted, though they could not tell you why, since they cannot remember tasting anything else. "You came through last night. Near the end. There was light on the water, all colors, and then—" she makes a gesture, opening her hands, "—and then people. Just people, walking out of nowhere onto the shore. You were lying right at the front of them. Like you'd been carrying everyone."

"I don't remember," Coral says. Their voice is rough, unused, a stranger's voice.

"No," the woman says gently. "Most of you don't. Or you do and it's wrong — half a village here, half a childhood there. The healers say give it time. Some of it comes back." She hesitates, and her kindness falters into something more careful. "Some doesn't."

"What happened?" Coral asks. "Before the beach. I know there was a before. I can feel it. Like stairs going down into the dark, but the stairs aren't there anymore."

The woman looks at them for a long moment. Wind pulls at her shawls. Behind her, a child shrieks happily at a gull, and the sound is so ordinary, so *alive*, that Coral's eyes sting without permission.

"You're asking the wrong person," she says finally. "I'm from Hollowmere. We forgot our own names, near the end — you'd have heard about that, if you remembered anything." She smiles, and it is a tired smile, but it holds. "We're remembering now, though. Slowly. Like ice melting. Whatever you did — whoever did it — we got ourselves back. So." She touches Coral's shoulder, once, lightly. "Whatever the stairs cost you, love, they bought a great deal."

She walks away up the sand toward the fires, and Coral sits holding the warm bowl and watches the stars fade one by one into the brightening sky, and thinks: *I paid for this.* Not remembers. *Knows*, the way you know the sun rose, the way you know your own hands. Somewhere below those vanished stairs they gave away everything they had, and what is left sitting here in the sand is what it purchased: a woman named — she said her name, they missed it — from a village called Hollowmere, eating breakfast by a real sea, under real stars, free.

It seems a fair trade. It seems an unbearable one. Both things are true, and Coral holds them together like two halves of a charm, and does not cry, because they cannot find the memory that crying belongs to.

* * *

In their pocket is a charm.

They find it when they finally stand, patting themselves down with the automatic thoroughness of a body that remembers habits the mind has lost — pockets, sleeves, the hollow of the collarbone where a thief keeps the smallest valuables. Fingers close around something small and hard and smooth. They draw it out into the morning light.

It is a luck-charm. They know this the way they knew the stairs: by feel, by craft, by a competence that survived the drowning of everything else. Carved bone, whale-white, strung on waxed cord, sealed with a drop of amber resin in which a single hair is caught — dark hair, curling. Their own work. No doubt about it. The knotwork is theirs; the way the resin pools is theirs. Some other life's hands made this, and those hands knew what they were doing.

But they do not remember making it. And charms are not supposed to work for their maker anyway — that was the first rule, the oldest rule, the rule they built seven years of survival on. A thief cannot pickpocket herself. A conduit cannot drink from her own well.

And yet.

The charm is *warm*. Warm as a held hand. And inside it — they can feel it pressing against the seal like a moth against a window, patient, gentle — is a memory.

Their breath stops. Because they know what a stored memory feels like from the outside; they sold a thousand of them, they wrapped strangers' wedding days and first snowfalls and mothers' lullabies in resin and cord and handed them to buyers with steady eyes. This is that. This is unmistakably that. A memory, whole and living, folded into bone.

Someone put a memory in a charm and left it *on them*. In the pocket of a person who woke with nothing. As if whoever it was knew exactly what they had lost, and knew exactly how to wrap a replacement, and had done it fast, before the light on the water faded — a seed pressed into ash by someone who refused to leave the ground entirely bare.

Coral sits back down in the sand. Their hands are shaking. They hold the charm up and look at it until the shaking slows, and then, with the care of a thief handling the one treasure worth dying for, they crack the amber seal.

The memory opens like an eye.

*Darkness first. Stone underfoot, wet — a crypt, flooded to the ankles, black water still as a mirror. Candle-smell. Then a voice, and the voice is singing, low, in a mode the body knows the way it knows its own heartbeat, a hymn that should be forbidden and is being sung anyway in defiance of every law in a stolen country. The singer stands in the dark with his collar torn off and his faith in ruins and his voice steady anyway, and he sings to a handful of coin-poor listeners in a drowned church, and he is beautiful. He turns his head — candlelight along a jaw, dark eyes catching flame — and looks directly at Coral, at whoever-Coral-was, standing in the crowd with stolen memories whispering coordinates behind their ribs. And he sees them. Not their face. Something deeper. His song stumbles, catches, continues — changed. Aimed. He sings the next verse as if it were a letter, and the address is Coral, and Coral's whole body knows it, and the feeling that floods through the memory is so large and so terrified and so hopeful that it barely fits inside the charm at all—*

The vision closes.

Coral is kneeling in the sand. They do not remember kneeling. The broth has spilled, a dark stain soaking away into the wet grains, and the tide is coming in around their knees and they do not care, because they are holding the charm against their sternum with both hands like a splint for a broken rib, and they are saying a name they did not know five minutes ago:

"Sable."

The name tastes like candle smoke and cold water and the exact pitch of a voice in the dark. Sable Fen. They know it. They know *him* — no. They know one evening of him. One evening out of — how many? The stairs went down further than one night. The stairs went down years. And all of it is gone, all of it is paid out into the dark beneath a conservatory, into the keeping of a frightened god, and what remains is this: a single evening, bottled, returned. Mercy, the charm says, in the language of its warmth. Not restoration. Mercy. One seed, left in the ash, so that Coral would know the garden existed.

They laugh. It comes out cracked, halfway to a sob, entirely involuntary. Of course. Of course the god kept this one. Of course the one thing given back is the beginning, not the middle, not the end — the moment two liars recognized each other's true names across a drowned chapel floor. Whoever chose it understood theft perfectly. Understood that the first memory is the load-bearing one. Build on that, and maybe — maybe — the rest can grow again. Not restored. Grown. The way you cannot give a burned field back its harvest, but you can leave it one seed, and wait for spring.

Spring. Is it spring? They do not know what season it is. They do not know what year. They know the sea smells honest and the stars came back and a man sang in the dark once, beautifully, to them specifically, and loved them — the memory says *loved*, it is written all through the feeling like a watermark — and they cannot remember his face beyond candlelight on a jaw.

"Sable Fen," they say again, testing it, learning it the way you learn a chord. The gulls wheel overhead. The refugee camp murmurs and smokes behind the dunes. Somewhere out there in the tent-city is a man who knew them — knew them wholly, knew them at their worst and best and final — waking to a Coral who is a stranger wearing a familiar face.

What does he see when he looks at me? they wonder. Does he see the thief? The conduit? The person who walked into the dark and did not come back the same? Do I want to?

Yes. And no. And yes again. The wanting itself is new and raw and entirely their own — the first possession of the new life. *I want to know him.* The charm pulses warm against their chest, agreeing.

* * *

Toward evening they walk the camp.

They cannot help it. The charm hangs at their throat now, under their shirt, against the skin, and every stranger they pass is a question. Did you know me? Were you on the boat, the road, the shore, the stair? An old ferryman with rope-scarred hands nods to them by the cookfire line and Coral's heart leaps absurdly — *Brannoc?* — but the man's face means nothing, and he passes on, and Coral learns the cruelest grammar of their condition: recognition will ambush them without warning or withhold itself forever, and they cannot tell which until it happens.

They pass a girl mending a net with one hand and a carved wooden crutch leaning against her knee, and Coral's step hitches — *Maren* — but the girl looks up with a stranger's polite blankness and returns to her knots, and Coral walks on, shaken. Or maybe not Maren. Maybe every limping sailor is Maren now. Maybe none. The dead and the scattered and the saved are all mixed together in this camp like flotsam after a flood, and Coral has no map of who washed ashore where.

Near the northern edge of the tents, where the dunes give way to scrubby grass and the smell of woodsmoke thins, they hear it.

Singing.

Low. Half-lost under the wind. A hymn — and their body reacts before their mind can object, the way a struck bell reacts, the whole skeleton ringing. They know that mode. They know that *phrasing*, that particular way of leaning into a line as if handing it to someone across a dark room. It is not the memory replaying. It is the same voice. Alive. Present. Fifty yards away, past the cookfires, singing to children gathered on a dune — a man with a torn-off collar and a ruined faith and a steadiness that survives both, teaching a round to a circle of refugee kids who are laughing when they miss their notes.

Sable Fen.

Coral stands at the edge of the firelight and does not move. Every instinct they have left screams at them to walk forward, to say the name aloud, to hold out the charm like proof of purchase. But what would they even say? *I am the person you loved. Here is the one hour of it I have left. Introduce yourself to me. Tell me who I was.*

He hasn't seen them yet. The children are giggling; the sun is going down over the sea in colors no stolen country ever managed, orange and gold and an honest, ordinary red. He looks tired. He looks *unbearably* familiar, the way the charm looked familiar — known by the hands, unknown by the mind. He looks like a man who has been searching a beach for days.

And beside him, tugging his sleeve, scowling at the ocean with the ferocity of a very small storm cloud — a boy. Ten years old, maybe. Wild-haired. With a stoppered bottle hanging at his belt that glimmers faintly, impossibly, with weather.

The boy looks up. Looks straight at Coral, across the firelight, across fifty feet of sand and everything that has been forgotten. His eyes go wide. He grabs the man's sleeve harder.

"Sable," the boy says. "Sable. *Look.*"

And the singing stops.
