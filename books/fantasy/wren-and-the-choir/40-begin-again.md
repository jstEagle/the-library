## 40. Begin Again

*Pip tells it first, because Pip was there.*

The house sits on a cliff above a real sea — not the gray static sea that ate the horizon of Ashenmoor, but a sea with weather in it, with gulls and salt and a temper. In the mornings the light comes up gold off the water and pours through the kitchen window across three mismatched chairs, a table scarred with candle burns, and a shelf of charms that nobody sells anymore.

Pip counts the household like this: one priest who isn't a priest anymore, one thief who isn't a thief anymore, one ex-Warden who visits on Thursdays and pretends she's only passing through, one old ferryman who built the porch and then rebuilt it when the storm took half of it, two twins who come and go like cats, and one boy who bottles rain for the pleasure of it, because out here the rain is real and no one has ever told him he can't.

And one more. The most important one. The one the house is really built around.

Coral Underhill wakes early, every day, before the sun. Nobody knows why. Maybe some habit survived the emptying out — some bone-deep instinct from seven years of night markets and stolen sleep. Coral rises in the dark and stands at the window watching the sea until the light comes, hands flat against the sill, breathing slow, like someone listening for a song they can't quite catch.

Pip watches them do it sometimes, from the hallway, and doesn't say anything. Some things you don't interrupt.

* * *

Sable had made himself a rule in those first weeks, after the Door, after the camp, after the terrible morning when Coral looked up at him from the cot with eyes like cleared water and asked, politely, *who are you?*

The rule was this: **he would never ask Coral to remember.**

It was harder than any fast the Choir had ever set him, harder than the flooded crypt winters, harder than standing in the crowd while his testimony was read aloud and watching Coral's face go still as a locked door. Because Sable remembered everything. He remembered Vell's night market and the smell of fried dough and the first time he'd seen Coral's mouth move along with a hymn they couldn't have known. He remembered the Waystation, when the land remembered itself green for one impossible hour and Coral's hand had found his in the grass. He remembered the snow, and the ruin-chapel, and the fight, and the forgiveness-that-wasn't-forgiveness-but-was-better-than-it-sounded.

He remembered a whole life with this person, and the person beside him remembered none of it.

So instead, one bright morning in the refugee camp, with the real sea thundering beyond the tents and gulls screaming their idiot joy, Sable Fen had walked over to where Coral sat mending a net badly, and introduced himself.

"My name is Sable," he said. "I used to sing. I'm told I'm good company, though the evidence is mixed."

Coral had looked at him — wary, curious, the way a bird looks at an offered hand — and said, "Coral. I don't know what I used to do."

"Neither do I," Sable said, which was the kindest lie he ever told. "Would you like to find out together?"

* * *

Spring comes to the cliff house like a rumor confirmed.

First the gorse blooms, outrageous yellow against the green. Then the swallows come back to the eaves, and Pip spends a whole week trying to bottle their song before admitting that some things refuse to be kept. The garden Tansy would have planted — the garden they planted for her, in her memory, with seeds Brannoc carried out through the Door in his coat pockets — comes up ragged and triumphant: beans, onions, a row of flowers nobody can name that Isolde insists grew outside the stolen country, real-world flowers, stubborn strangers like Tansy herself.

Maren walks the garden path every morning now, her cane tapping stone, her balance better than the healers promised. On bad days she doesn't walk it. On bad days Isolde sits with her and they look at the sea together and don't talk, which is its own kind of navigation.

Halloway arrives on Thursdays. She always brings something small — bread, a jar of honey, once a book of star-charts for Pip, because the stars over the real world are different, and Pip is determined to learn all of them. She never stays past dusk. She always looks at Coral too long when she thinks no one sees, and whatever is in her face, she folds it away before she leaves, the way she folded away everything else her whole life. Duty, love, grief — all of it put carefully in pockets.

But last Thursday she stayed past dusk. She sat on the porch while Coral played a tuneless wooden flute they'd bought from a peddler, badly, happily. And Halloway listened with her eyes closed, and when Coral finished and laughed at themselves, Halloway said, quietly, "You were always better than you believed," and Coral said, "You've heard me play before?" and Halloway said, "No," and looked at the sea, and stayed until the stars came out.

Pip saw the whole thing. Pip tells no one. Pip keeps it in a bottle inside his chest, unlabeled, unpriced.

* * *

Here is how the house works:

Sable cooks, badly, with enormous enthusiasm. His bread could stop a door. His stew could strip paint. Coral eats every bite of both and asks for seconds, and Sable glows like a lantern, and Pip gags theatrically and eats the stew anyway because it's actually getting better, week by week, the way everything in this house is getting better — slowly, unevenly, without anyone demanding it be otherwise.

Coral sings while doing chores. Not the Choir — never the Choir; the magic went out of them through the Door like water through open fingers, and what's left is just a voice, an ordinary human voice, a little rough, a little true. They sing work songs from Vell they shouldn't remember and don't know where they learned. They sing lullabies in a language nobody in the house speaks. Once, washing dishes, they hummed seventeen bars of a hymn that the Choir outlawed two hundred years ago, and Sable stood in the doorway with a dish towel in his hands and let the water run cold rather than make a sound and break it.

Afterward Coral said, "That one felt important. Do you know it?"

"No," Sable said. "But I'd love to learn it."

He taught it back to them the next week, note by note, pretending he was working it out fresh. Coral never noticed the way his voice shook on the third verse. Or maybe Coral noticed, and understood, in the wordless way they understand things now — not with memories but with something older than memory, some part of them the god's hunger couldn't reach. Some part that recognized him anyway.

Two liars, recognizing each other's true names. Again. Even with the names wiped clean.

* * *

Pip keeps his bottles on the windowsill in his room: rain from the first spring storm, fog off the headland, a scrap of rainbow caught in spray, one swallow's song (he got it eventually; it took four days and a great deal of dignity sacrificed). And on the shelf above them, wrapped in cloth and touched rarely, a single vial of gray nothing — static, the last of the unraveling country, saved from the far side of the Door. He doesn't know why he keeps it. He does know that throwing it away would feel like saying Ashenmoor never happened, and it did happen, and the people it happened to are downstairs eating terrible stew.

Sometimes at night he hears Coral and Sable talking low in the next room, the murmur of voices through the wall like a tide. He hears Coral laugh — a sound Pip has catalogued more carefully than any weather. He heard Coral ask once, very softly: "Did I have people? Before? Did someone love me?"

And Sable, without a heartbeat's hesitation: "Yes. Someone loved you very much."

"You?" Coral said, and Pip held his breath.

"I'm here, aren't I?" Sable said. "People don't stay for nothing."

There was a long quiet. Then Coral: "I think I must have been hard to love."

"I know you were," Sable said warmly, and Coral laughed, startled, and Pip grinned into his pillow and bottled the moment the only way he knew how — by staying awake until he was sure he'd never forget it.

Because that's the thing Pip figured out that the grown-ups keep dancing around: memory isn't the only way to keep a thing. You can also just — decide. Every morning. Decide again. Sable decides Coral. Coral decides the house. Pip decides all of it, fiercely, the way he once decided to follow a stranger into a sewer because she'd fed him and hadn't hit him and that was enough reason in Vell.

Some things get restored. And some things get relearned, from scratch, by hand, like bread, like trust, like love. And Pip privately believes — though he's only eleven and a half and would deny it under oath — that the relearned kind might hold better. Things you build yourself fit the shape of your life. Things you're given might not.

* * *

In June, Coral starts having the dreams.

They don't speak of them at first. But Pip sleeps lightly, street-habit, and he hears Coral rise before dawn more often, and stand at the window longer, and once — only once — he hears them humming in the dark, and the humming is not a hymn or a lullaby or anything with words. It is something vast slowed down to the size of a throat. It sounds like the dimmed stars hanging in their chamber beneath the Conservatory, like lanterns waiting. It sounds, Pip thinks with his skin prickling, like the child-god turning over in its sleep, dreaming kindly, somewhere on the other side of everything.

In the morning Coral remembers none of it. But Coral hums more, those weeks. Touches the charms on the shelf. Stands at the cliff edge with the wind pulling at their clothes, looking east — inland, toward where the Door stood, toward wherever the country of Ashenmoor now lies folded up safe inside its own closing song — and their face does something complicated and peaceful, like a person reading a letter they can't read but recognize the handwriting of.

One evening Coral comes down from the cliffs holding something: a smooth gray stone with a hairline seam of silver running through it, sea-tumbled, ordinary. Except that when Coral holds it up to the last light, the seam glimmers like a thread of song.

"Look what the tide gave me," Coral says, pleased, puzzled. "It almost sounds like something. If you hold it to your ear."

Nobody in the house says what they are all thinking. That the sea gives gifts. That songs leave echoes. That maybe — just maybe — a god who was sung to rest by a thief who paid everything might have kept back one small coin of the payment, tucked it somewhere safe, and spent a year teaching the ocean how to carry it home.

Sable takes the stone in both hands, careful as communion. "May I?"

"It's yours if you want it."

"It's yours," Sable says. "It was always yours. I only — " He stops. Starts again, the way he has learned to, patiently, honestly, a man building a bridge plank by plank in full view. "I think we should sing together. You and I. Just us. There's a song I've been wanting to try, and it needs two voices, and I think — I think it might be yours as much as mine. I think it might be ours."

Coral looks at him for a long moment. The wind moves through the garden, through the flowers nobody can name, through the beans Tansy will never taste and somehow planted anyway. Somewhere below, the sea breathes in, and holds, and lets go.

"All right," Coral says. "Stranger. Teach me."

And Sable laughs — the whole laugh, the real one, the one that cracked him open in a flooded crypt years ago when a thief's mouth moved along with a forbidden hymn — and takes Coral's hands, and begins.

* * *

They sing at dusk, on the porch, with the whole household gathered and pretending not to listen. It's a simple song at first, almost foolish — a round, a walking tune, something with nonsense syllables that Sable claims is very old and Coral claims is being invented on the spot. Then Coral's voice finds the harmony underneath, the one they shouldn't know, the one that fits Sable's melody the way a hand fits a hand, and the song opens up like a flower, like a door, like a country remembering itself green.

The gorse burns yellow in the last light. Maren's cane taps time on the boards. Isolde cries and blames the wind. Brannoc's big hands rest easy on his knees. Pip sits on the steps with his bottles around him, catching what he can, keeping what he can't.

And high overhead, right at the seam between day and night, one star comes out before any of the others — brighter than it should be, brighter than charts allow, hanging in the west like a lantern left burning in a window for someone expected home.

Coral sees it. Stops singing. Smiles — a smile with nothing missing in it, nothing owed, nothing priced.

"Huh," Coral says softly, to the sky, to the stone in their lap, to whatever patient enormous dream listens on the far side of the world. "Hello to you too."

Then Coral turns back to Sable, and asks him to teach them the second verse.

And the sea goes on, and the house holds, and the song — begun again, chosen again, theirs again — carries on into the blue.
