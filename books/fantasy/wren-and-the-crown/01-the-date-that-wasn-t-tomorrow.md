## 1. The Date That Wasn't Tomorrow

There are three certainties in the life of a ledger-keeper of the Meridian Guild: the ink dries faster than you'd like, the columns never balance on the first pass, and Master Aldous Vane will find the one error you missed, no matter how small, and say nothing about it — which is worse than shouting, because the silence means he's filed your failure somewhere in that vast, dusty archive he keeps behind his eyes.

I had been a ledger-keeper for four years, the youngest in the guild's history, and I had learned to love the work the way you love a wall that keeps the flood out. Numbers are honest in a way people are not. A column of figures will never tell you it wasn't at the tavern when it was. A sum will never smile at you while reaching for your purse. I liked that about them. I liked that about my life, which was mostly numbers, and mostly safe.

It was the ninth of Embersfall when the wall cracked.

* * *

The guild's counting room occupied the third floor of the Meridian Hall, a long chamber with windows of green glass that turned the afternoon light the color of pond water. Forty desks, most of them empty now. The guild had been dying for years — everyone said so, the way you say a grandparent is fading, with a shrug that hides the grief. We were down to eleven keepers, and Master Vane, and the ledgers. Always the ledgers.

That evening I was balancing the autumn quarter: the usual impossible trades. Sixteen barrels of shadow, received from a vintner in Lowmark, entered against a payment of one song, sung twice. A debt of three hours' sleep, purchased by a midwife and repaid in full by a stranger who would never know what he'd given. A tithe of first frost, collected from an orchard that had no business yielding anything in Embersfall at all.

If you're wondering how one barrels shadow, or sells a song, or collects frost like coin — I stopped wondering years ago. That's the first thing they teach you. *Record, don't ask.* The guild's trades had been strange since before my grandmother's grandmother, and the strangeness was simply the water we swam in. A guild that traded in impossible things required keepers who could write down the impossible with a steady hand and a clean margin.

I was on the fourth page, cross-checking the frost tithe against last year's entry, when I noticed the discrepancy.

The orchard's payment was short. Not by much — a fraction, the kind of shortfall a careless keeper would round away. But I am not careless, and I had last year's ledger open beside me, and last year the same orchard had paid a fraction *more* than its tithe, and the year before that a fraction more again. I went back. Five years. Ten. Twenty. The line of entries marched backward through the books like a tide going out, each year's payment a hair smaller, a hair thinner, a hair less bright — and I know how that sounds, that I could tell a number was less bright, but ledger-keepers will understand. Some entries you feel in your fingers.

The tithe was failing. Had been failing, quietly, for as long as I'd been alive.

I did what any good keeper does with an anomaly. I followed it.

* * *

The discrepancy led me to the vault ledger — the one book even senior keepers weren't meant to open without Master Vane present — and the vault ledger pointed, in a hand two centuries dead, to a shelf in the restricted archive that I had walked past four hundred times without seeing. That's the trick of the Meridian Guild. Nothing is hidden. Things are simply filed where no one thinks to look.

The book I pulled down was bound in dragonhide — real dragonhide, which should have told me something right there, because dragons were the stuff of nursery stories and the Emberlands beyond the mountains, and no one traded with the Emberlands. No one had traded with the Emberlands in a hundred years. That was history, that was settled, that was the kind of fact that sat in your head unexamined like a coin at the bottom of a well.

The book was sealed with a wax the color of cooling iron, stamped with the guild's mark: a circle bisected by a line. The covenant seal. I'd seen it on the guild's charter, on the door of the Hall, on the signet Master Vane wore and never explained.

The seal broke like an eggshell.

The pages inside were not ledgers. They were prophecies — or what the guild had called prophecies, in the old tongue, in a script that shifted when I wasn't looking directly at it. Dates. Hundreds of dates, each with a line of text beside it, each line a trade. *On this day, a debt comes due. On this day, a fire is fed. On this day, the price is paid.* It read like a ledger. That was the first thing that frightened me: how much it read like a ledger.

I turned pages. The early dates were a century old, and each had been struck through in red — *paid, paid, paid* — the way I struck through settled accounts at the end of a quarter. Then the red ink stopped. Forty years ago. Thirty. The entries went unmarked. Unpaid.

And then tonight's date. The ninth of Embersfall. Inked in a hand I did not know, a hundred years before I was born.

And beside it, in the same ink, the same century-dead hand:

*Jude Hartwell, keeper, balances the books. The covenant fails.*

* * *

I want to tell you I did something sensible. I want to tell you I closed the book and walked out of the archive and reported the anomaly through proper channels and went home to my narrow bed above the chandler's shop and slept the dreamless sleep of the gainfully employed.

I stood there in the dark of the archive with a hundred-year-old book open in my hands and my own name looking up at me, and I read the entry again. And again. As though the words might rearrange themselves if I gave them enough opportunities. As though a ledger had ever once, in the entire history of ink, changed its mind out of embarrassment.

*The covenant fails.* I didn't know what covenant. The guild's charter used the word — *the Meridian Covenant, sealed and perpetual* — the way charters use grand words, as decoration. I had never once wondered what it sealed. I had never once wondered what the guild was *for*, beyond the trading of impossible things, beyond the ledgers, beyond the wall that kept the flood out.

Flood. I remember thinking that, standing in the dark. I remember thinking: *I've been keeping the books for a flood, and no one told me there was water.*

The lamp at the end of the aisle guttered. The archive had no drafts. I know the archive had no drafts, because knowing the behavior of every room in the Meridian Hall was part of my work, the way knowing the behavior of every entry was part of my work, and I was good at my work.

"Put it down, child."

Master Vane stood at the end of the aisle. I don't know how long he'd been there. He had a talent for arriving without the arrival — seventy years old, thin as a ledger's spine, with eyes the gray of old paper. He was not holding a lamp. The lamp had guttered for him, I understood that much, the way a dog goes quiet for its master.

"Master Vane. I was cross-checking the frost tithe, and the — the shortfall led me —"

"I know where it led you." He came down the aisle at his unhurried pace, the one that made juniors feel like furniture. He held out his hand. I gave him the book. Of course I gave him the book. Four years of habit put it in his palm before my mind caught up and asked what I was doing.

He looked at the open page. He looked at my name in century-old ink. His face did nothing at all, which was the most frightening thing it could have done.

"You were not to find this," he said. Not *you weren't supposed to.* *You were not to.* As if it had been arranged. As if my not-finding had been an entry in some ledger of its own, and I had defaulted.

"The tithe is failing," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "The orchard payments. Forty years of shortfalls. Master, what are the tithes *for*?"

He closed the book. Gently. That was the worst part — the gentleness. Master Vane handled everything gently, as if the whole world were a page he was afraid of tearing.

"Jude." He said my name the way he said it when he was about to correct an entry. "What you have read does not exist. What you have surmised does not exist. The frost tithe is in order. The archive was never opened. You will go home, you will sleep, and tomorrow you will balance the autumn quarter as though this evening were a blank page."

"The book has my name in it."

"Yes," he said. "It does."

And then he took the lamp from the end of the aisle, and held it to the book, and burned it.

I don't mean he lit a fire in a brazier and fed the book to it with ceremony. I mean he held a hundred years of sealed prophecy to a counting-room lamp and let it catch, and stood there in the pond-green dark watching it burn with the same expression he wore reconciling accounts, and the dragonhide cover went last, curling like a living thing, and the light of it turned his face the color of the wax seal, the color of cooling iron, and neither of us said a word until there was nothing left but a smell like a struck match and a small drift of ash on the archive floor.

"You'll want to know why," he said.

"No," I said. And then, because I am a keeper, because I record, because the truth comes out of me the way entries come out of a pen: "Yes."

He brushed the ash from his sleeves with two precise strokes, the way he did every evening before leaving, and looked at me for a long moment. Whatever he saw in my face, it made him tired. It made him look, for the first time in four years, like an old man.

"The guild has kept a fire burning for two hundred years, child," he said. "The tithes are its fuel. The books are its bellows. And the fire is going out, and there is nothing eleven keepers and one old man can do about it — except what we have always done. Record. Don't ask. Balance the quarter."

"And my name? In a book written before my birth?"

"Go home, Jude."

I went home. I walked the long way, through streets smelling of woodsmoke and the first frost the orchards had sold us, past the guild's sign — the circle bisected by the line — hanging crooked above the Hall door, and I thought: *a circle is a covenant, and a line is a blade, and I have walked under that sign every day of my working life without once asking which side of the line I stood on.*

Above the chandler's shop, I lit my lamp and pulled out my personal ledger — every keeper keeps one, the record of their own accounts — and I uncapped my ink, and I wrote the date. The ninth of Embersfall. And beneath it, because a record is a record, and because some part of me had already understood that Master Vane burning a book does not burn what it says:

*Found my name in a sealed book. The covenant fails tonight.*

I sat back and looked at the entry, and my hand was steady, and that frightened me more than the burning had. Because a keeper who can write down her own doom with a clean margin is either very brave or already lost, and I did not know which.

Outside, far to the east, past the mountains, past the border no one crossed, I would have sworn the sky glowed — faint and red, like a fire seen through a closing door.

Like something guttering.
