## 2. Tithes and Impossible Things

Aldous Vane burned the page, but he did not burn the book.

That was my first clue. You don't leave the body behind if you're only worried about the murder. He fed that single leaf to the hearth in his office and watched it curl like a dying moth, and then he locked the prophecy book back in its iron case, and then he looked at me with those pale eyes of his and said, "Ledgers before breakfast, Jude. The Meridian account is three marks short."

Three marks. As if the world hadn't just been scheduled to end.

I did what I was told, because I always do what I'm told — it's the one talent the Guild ever noticed in me — but a thing had come loose in me overnight, some small pin holding up a great deal of machinery, and while my hands copied figures my mind kept returning to the same question: *why keep the book if the page was the danger?*

You can't answer questions like that sitting still. So at half past nine, when Master Vane went down to the counting floor to argue with the silk factors, I let myself into his office with the copy-key I'd cut two years ago for exactly this sort of occasion and had never, until now, had the nerve to use.

The iron case opened on the second try. The prophecy book sat inside like something sleeping, its leather cover gone soft and dark as old blood. And beneath it, wedged into the false bottom of the case where a sensible man keeps his deeds and his blackmail letters, lay a ledger no entry had ever mentioned.

It was older than the building. Older than the Guild's charter, which hung downstairs and claimed our founding in the reign of King Alderic the Second. This ledger's first page was dated forty years before Alderic took the throne, written in a hand so fine it might have been engraved, on paper that had gone the color of weak tea.

The heading across the top read: **TITHE LEDGER — FIRST HEARTH ACCOUNT**.

*First Hearth.* I turned the page because turning pages is what I do. It's practically a religious act with me.

The columns were wrong. That was my second thought, right after the title — wrong in a way that made the back of my neck prickle, because I know ledgers the way other people know their own pulse, and these entries were structured like trades and recorded like prayers.

Take the entry dated 14th of Thawmonth, year whatever-it-was:

> *Received: one memory of the sea, freely given.*
> *Rendered to the Hearth: same.*
> *Balance: maintained.*

No price. No counterparty beyond "the Hearth." No profit, which in a ledger is like a prayer with no god at the top. And the handwriting — I checked, because checking is also a religious act — matched, stroke for stroke, the founding hand of our own charter documents. The Meridian Guild's very first keeper had kept two sets of books.

Every guild-keeper does, you'll say. Every merchant since money was invented has kept a clean set for the tax men and a true set for himself. But this wasn't that. This was the opposite. The public ledgers — the ones I balanced every day of my life — were the fiction. This was the truth, and the truth said we weren't merchants at all.

Page by page, year by year, the pattern held. A memory of the sea. A name, freely surrendered. Three drops of blood from a woman who signed herself *Kestrel*. A song nobody else remembered anymore. A lock of hair from a child born under a red sky. Each received by the Guild through some trade or tithe or debt, each rendered onward to an account called the First Hearth, each marked *balance: maintained* in that engraver-fine hand.

And then, further along — this was where my stomach started doing arithmetic without my permission — the balances stopped being maintained.

> *Received: one memory of the sea.* (the hand here had changed; later, cruder, hurried)
> *Rendered to the Hearth:* —
> *Balance: SHORT.*

Short. Then short again. Then short by increasing amounts, year over year, like a merchant bleeding coin from a hole in his purse. The last full page was dated eleven years ago. After that, the entries grew sparse as winter leaves, until the final line, undated, in a shaking hand I recognized — God help me, I recognized it — as Aldous Vane's:

> *We are taking more than we give. The fire knows. May whoever finds this forgive us, and be quick about it.*

I closed the book. I opened it again, because closing books doesn't unwrite them, and read the whole thing twice more, front to back, the way I'd read any ledger I meant to audit.

Here is what I understood by lamplight in my master's office while the guild went about its honest dishonest business below: for a hundred years and more, the Meridian Guild had collected impossible things — memories, names, years of life, songs, blood — and passed them downward, past the counting floors, past the vaults, down to a fire beneath the world. The strange trades everyone joked about, the ones that made the Guild famous and faintly disreputable — the man who sold his grief, the widow who bought back one hour of her husband's voice — they were never commerce. They were tithes. We were not a bank. We were a wick.

And we had stopped paying in full.

Eleven years ago the balance went short. Eleven years ago, I realized, with the particular cold that comes when a number finally means something — eleven years ago was when old Keeper Brandt died, and half the senior members left or were lost, and the Guild began its long slow shrinking from a hundred strong to the nineteen of us rattling around a building built for ten times that. Everyone said the Guild was dying of irrelevance. The ledgers said it was dying because it had stopped feeding the fire, and the fire was what kept — what? I didn't have the word yet. I had a glowing eastern sky and a sealed prophecy and a column of *SHORT*s, and somewhere beneath all of it, a word I could feel but not yet read.

*Covenant,* whispered the part of my brain that reads ahead in books.

I put the tithe ledger back in the false bottom. I locked the case. I went downstairs and balanced the Meridian account to the mark and found the missing three marks in a transcription error I'd made myself, and I corrected it, and my pen did not shake even once, and I was proud of that for approximately four seconds before I wanted to be sick.

Because here's the thing about bookkeeping that nobody tells you until it's too late: the numbers are never just numbers. Every figure is a person who paid. That memory of the sea belonged to someone who woke up one morning unable to remember why they'd become a sailor, and gave it gladly, or thought they did. The name that was surrendered — someone walked around after that being called whatever the clerk wrote down instead, and smiled, because the ledger said so. I had spent four years believing I kept accounts of *things*. I kept accounts of people. And the fire beneath the world had been running on them, and now it was starving, and tonight —

Tonight. The date in the prophecy book. Her own name beside it — mine. *Jude Hartwell.* Not a warning about some far-off doom. An appointment.

* * *

They came at midday, which I suppose shows a certain professional courtesy. Inquisitors never arrive at dawn; dawn is for bandits and bailiffs. The Crown's people respect the working hours.

There were three of them, gray-cloaked, with the sunburst-and-scale badge of the Office of Inquiry worked small on their collars — deliberately small, the way you'd wear a weapon you hoped not to need. The one in front was tall and dry-voiced and introduced himself as Inquisitor Hale, and he asked for Master Vane by name, and Vane met him in the front hall wearing the face he wore for tax assessors, which is to say pleasant, opaque, and slightly bored.

"We're conducting a survey of historical records," Hale said. His voice was mild. Mild voices frighten me more than loud ones; loud voices have already shown you their shape. "Certain texts of prophetic character have gone missing from Crown archives. We're tracing copies."

"Prophetic character," Vane repeated. "How exciting. We deal in silk contracts and salt debts, Inquisitor. The most prophetic document we own is a weather-promise from the herring fleet."

"Of course." Hale smiled, and it reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window. "Then you won't mind if we look."

And this is the part I will be ashamed of until I die, so I may as well write it plainly: they looked, and I helped.

Not openly. Nothing so dramatic. But when Hale's clerks came through the scriptorium, and one of them paused over the iron case — because iron holds a cold that draws the eye, and clerks are trained to notice — I heard myself say, "Master Vane's deed box. Boring as gravel," in a bright helpful voice, and the clerk moved on, and the prophecy book slept on untouched, and I stood there with the tithe ledger's contents burning a hole behind my ribs and lied to the Crown to protect the very institution whose books had just told me it was killing the world.

Why? Because Vane had ordered silence? Because I feared him? No. Worse. Because for four years the Guild had been my ledger, my columns, my clean margins — the one account I knew how to keep — and some traitor part of me still believed that protecting the books was the highest calling there was. Even books that fed people into a fire. Even books that ran *short*.

Hale paused at the door on his way out. He looked at me — not past me, not through me, but *at* me, the way an auditor looks at a figure that doesn't reconcile — and said, "Keeper Hartwell, isn't it? Youngest in Guild history." He consulted a small notebook. "Your name came up."

"In what connection?"

"A list." He closed the notebook. "Old lists have a way of becoming current. If anything... unusual presents itself to you — a text, a dream, a date that shouldn't be inked anywhere — the Office pays well for early notice. Better than the Guild does, I'd wager."

"I'm well paid," I said.

"No one is," said Inquisitor Hale, "who works with fire," and left.

* * *

That evening I sat alone in the scriptorium with the lamps trimmed low and did what I should have done at dawn: I pulled the public ledgers, all forty years of them, and set them beside my memory of the hidden book, and reconciled.

It took hours. It was, I am not ashamed to say, beautiful work — the kind of reconciliation you dream of, where two corrupted records snap together and the whole history of an institution stands revealed in the differences between them. Every "impossible trade" in the public books mapped to a tithe in the hidden one. Every shortfall mapped too. And the shortfalls had a shape, once I charted them — a curve, gentle at first, then steepening, the classic signature of a debt compounding against a dwindling asset.

The asset being, apparently, everything.

I drew the curve out to its end and looked at where it crossed zero, and the date it crossed was not exact — curves like that never are — but it fell within a week of the date in the prophecy book.

Within a week. Possibly within a night.

I sat back in the dark and listened to the building settle around me, nineteen souls' worth of footsteps and coughs and closing doors, and understood that I was listening to the last members of an order of priests who had forgotten they were priests, tending by rote a flame they no longer believed in, feeding it less and less each year, while beneath the mountains something enormous grew hungry.

*I have been bookkeeping an apocalypse,* I thought. *In triplicate. With footnotes.*

And then, because I am who I am, I did the only reasonable thing. I took a fresh sheet of paper and began a third ledger — my own — titled it honestly (*What I Know, and How Badly It Reconciles*), and started writing down everything: the prophecy, the tithe columns, the shortfalls, the curve, Hale's visit, Vane's burning page, the red glow east. Evidence wants recording. Whatever else was true, that much would stay true, and a record kept true is the only kind of power I've ever trusted.

I was three pages in when the window above the scriptorium desk shattered inward.

Not broken — *opened*, cleanly, inward, though it had been latched, and through it came the smell of woodsmoke and oiled steel and a voice I hadn't heard in six years, low and dry as a drawn whetstone:

"Don't scream, little keeper. Scream brings the gray cloaks back, and you've got maybe an hour before they return anyway, and I've ridden two hundred miles to talk to you about a debt."

I turned, pen still in hand, ink still wet on the words *Inquisitor Hale — dangerous, observant, offered to buy me* — and found a woman standing on the sill of a fourth-floor window in full mail under a road-stained cloak, with a knight's sword slung across her back and Ser Maren Kestrel's name in the Guild's oldest tithe ledger written in her own blood.

"You're supposed to be dead," I said.

"Frequently alleged." She dropped lightly to the floor and looked at my open third ledger, at the curve I'd charted, and her face did something complicated. "So you've seen the books."

"The books have seen me."

"Good," said Ser Maren Kestrel, disgraced, exiled, and very much alive, and drew the latch shut behind her. "Then we haven't time to start from nothing. The covenant fails tonight, keeper — and I need to know if you're the one who lets it, or the one who refuses."
