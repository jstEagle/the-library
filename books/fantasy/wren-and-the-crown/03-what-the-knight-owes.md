## 3. What the Knight Owes

I had been drinking in the Saltcellar for three days when the news reached me that Vane's ledger-girl was about to be arrested.

Not that anyone in the Saltcellar said *arrested*. They said *the Inquisitors are at the Guildhall*, and then they said it quieter, and then they stopped saying it at all and went back to their cups with the particular diligence of people who have decided not to know things. I've always admired that in civilians. It's a discipline. I lost mine somewhere between my oath and my disgrace, which is why I was drinking alone in a dockside tavern with a sword I wasn't supposed to own and a debt I'd been avoiding for eleven years.

The debt had a name. It was *Jude Hartwell*.

Here is what I knew about Jude Hartwell before that night: she was Aldous Vane's youngest clerk, precise as a compass needle, and she had once — at fourteen, ink on her fingers, utterly unimpressed by me — caught an error in my escort roster that would have left the guild's west archive unguarded for a night. She'd pointed it out without a flicker of deference. Vane had laughed. I had not. That was the whole of our acquaintance, and yet the guild's master-at-records owed her protection under the old terms, and Vane had signed that obligation over to me the day he dismissed me from his service, along with three months' back pay and a look of profound disappointment I have never entirely scrubbed off.

*A knight's word is collateral,* he'd said. *Yours has depreciated, Ser Maren, but it retains some value.*

Aldous Vane could make an insult sound like an appraisal. It's why everyone trusted him with money and no one trusted him with feelings.

So: the Inquisitors were at the Guildhall, and the debt was there too, and my cup was empty, and the rain outside had that particular Meridian quality of being both constant and somehow insufficient to wash anything clean.

I paid my tab. This deserves mention because I don't always.

* * *

The Guildhall square was lit like a festival nobody wanted. Inquisitorial lanterns — the pale blue ones that burn without heat and make everything look embalmed — ringed the building, and white-cloaked figures moved between them with the unhurried confidence of people who own the law the way other people own boots.

There were six Inquisitors visible. There are always more than six. That's not pessimism; it's arithmetic. The Crown sends its Inquisitors in pairs because two can watch each other, and it sends them in sixes because six can watch each other lie.

The front doors were barred, which meant someone inside had locked them, which meant someone inside still believed doors worked. I gave them perhaps four minutes. Inquisitors carry a thing called a writ-spike — a length of cold iron they drive through a lock like a nail through a promise — and after that, doors are a metaphor.

I went around the back, where the guild kept its coal delivery and its dignity equally neglected. The service door was unlocked. Of course it was. The Meridian Guild had spent a century trading in secrets and had never once learned that secrets want locks, not ledgers.

Inside, the lower corridors smelled of tallow and panic. Somewhere above, boots on stone, measured, patient. And somewhere closer, a small light moving — a girl-shaped shadow with a lamp, coming toward me at a dead run, which is how I met Jude Hartwell properly at last.

She rounded the corner, saw me, and did something I have since learned is characteristic: she stopped. Assessed. Decided I was probably a problem but possibly a solvable one.

"You're not guild," she said. "You're armed, but the scabbard's wrong for a city watchman, and you came through the coal door."

"Ser Maren Kestrel," I said. "Formerly of the guild's guard. I'm here to collect—"

"The debt." She didn't blink. "Vane told me about you once. He called you 'a lesson in depreciation.'"

"He would." I jerked my head toward the stairwell, where the boot-steps had paused in that listening way. "We can trade biographies later or we can leave. Those are the options. Choose fast."

Above us, something went through a door that had been locked. Not forced — *opened*, with the terrible courtesy of a writ-spike doing its work. A voice followed it down the stairwell, pleasant as a bank clerk's: "Ledger-keeper Hartwell. We only wish to ask you questions. You may answer them here or downstairs. Downstairs has better light."

Inquisitor Hale. Even then, before I knew his name, I knew the voice — the Crown trains them all to sound like forgiveness with a knife behind it.

Jude looked up the stairs. Then at me. Then — this is the part I think about — she tucked the book under her arm more tightly. A slim black thing, older than either of us, held against her ribs like a child.

"The back way," she said. "Through the counting room. There's a window over the canal."

"There's a window over a thirty-foot drop into a canal."

"I know," she said. "That's why they won't be watching it. Nobody watches the option that requires courage."

I have served kings' officers, merchant princes, and one bandit queen who paid in teeth, and I have never once heard a better tactical argument delivered so flatly. We ran.

* * *

The window was exactly as advertised, except colder. The canal took us both without comment — Meridian's waterways have seen everything and judge nothing — and we came up coughing behind a rope-walk where the smell of tar covered the smell of fear, if you're generous, and doesn't quite, if you're honest.

We walked. Fast, but walking. Fleeing draws eyes; two soaked women striding purposefully draw nothing.

Three streets on, in the shadow of the Tallowbridge, she finally asked it. "Why? Why collect me? Vane dismissed you years ago."

"He signed the debt to me when he did. Protection of the guild's record-keepers, in perpetuity, transferable." I wrung out my cloak, which helped neither the cloak nor my mood. "It's the only part of my oath the guild ever let me keep."

"That's a strange reason to save someone."

"It's the best kind," I said. "Strange reasons hold. Sensible ones get renegotiated."

She studied me with those ledger-keeper's eyes, the ones that total you up and find a remainder you didn't know you were carrying. "You knew the Inquisitors would come tonight."

"I knew they were asking after the prophecy book in every port from here to Greyharbor. I came because of the date." I hesitated. Wished I hadn't. Continued anyway, because a debt half-paid is a debt still owed. "Hartwell. What did the book say?"

For a moment I thought she wouldn't tell me. She was, I would learn, very good at silence — it runs in the guild's blood like a family resemblance. But the rain was cold, and shame is heavier wet, and she said:

"My name. Tonight's date. And five words: *the covenant fails; the fire dies.*"

Well. I had drunk enough in the Saltcellar to hear worse, but not much, and not sober. I sat down on a bollard, which was undignified, and let the truth sit in my stomach like swallowed riverwater.

"Then you should know what I know," I said. "Since apparently we're colleagues now."

"Are we?"

"I owe you. You need someone who owes you. That's a colleague." I looked up at her, dripping, and gave her the rest of it, the part I'd carried for eleven years like a stone sewn into my coat lining.

"When I guarded the guild — before my disgrace, before Vane handed me off like spoiled goods — I stood post in the deep archive. The tithe vaults. You'll have found the columns by now, the ones that don't balance, the payments that go nowhere." Her face flickered; she had. Good. "Every impossible trade the guild makes — the memory-brokerage, the weather-debts, the years sold in bundles — none of it is commerce. It's fuel. It goes *down*, Hartwell. Into the fire beneath the world. The fire the Emberlands gave us and we pay to keep burning, because if it dies—"

"If it dies, the covenant fails." She said it like a woman reading her own death warrant aloud to check for typos.

"And the payments have been short for years. I saw it before I was dismissed. Vane called my concern 'an accounting matter beyond my station.' Then he made it beyond my station permanently." I stood, joints complaining, an old knight's percussion section. "The guild is dying from the top down, Hartwell. Vane knows. The Inquisitors know, and they want it — they want the fire out and the Emberlands sealed, a tidy world with nothing in it that breathes flame. And the only people alive who might do anything about it are scattered across three kingdoms pretending to be dead."

She was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked on the bridge stones. Somewhere a night-watch bell counted the hour with the smugness of institutions.

"Names," she said finally. "Give me names."

"Osk Marrow," I said. "Canal city of Veilport. Memory-forger. Erased more pasts than the Crown has rewritten, including, rumor says, his own. He'll refuse you. Refusing is most of what he does now."

"Who else?"

"A man called Ryn. Half-dragon, raised human, thrown out of both worlds for the crime of existing. Last seen near the Ashfall Marches, and if you approach him wrong he will treat you as a Crown agent, which is a reasonable mistake given how many of them hunt him." I pulled my cloak straight, as if order in wool could stand in for order anywhere else. "And that's nearly all. Nearly everyone else who understood what the guild truly was is ash or exile or Vane."

"Not you?"

"I'm disgraced, not dead. There's a difference, but it's narrower than I'd like."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face — gone before it landed, filed away. "Then we start with Osk. Tomorrow. There's a barge at dawn."

"We?" I raised an eyebrow. "Bold of you to assume I'm invited."

"You owe me," she said simply. "And I need someone who owes me. That's a colleague."

Gods help me, she'd quoted me back at myself within the hour. I told myself then — and tell myself still, some nights, when the fire dreams come — that I went along out of obligation. Debts are comfortable. They have terms and boundaries and an end.

But I looked at her standing there in the rain, seventeen years old and carrying a prophecy under her arm like a parcel she refused to sign for, and I thought: *Vane, you fool. You tried to dismiss the one person in your guild who reads everything. Including you.*

"Dawn barge," I agreed. "But I pick the taverns."

Behind us, far off across the rooftops, a column of pale blue light rose briefly into the clouds — an Inquisitorial beacon, calling in the hunt. They knew who'd gone out the window. They'd know by morning where barges sailed.

Jude watched it fade, and her jaw set in a way I'd see again later, at the First Hearth, when everything hung on it.

"Then we'd better be on it," she said.
