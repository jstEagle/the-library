## 4. The Forger of Memories

The canal city of Vessengard smelled like a promise someone had made and immediately regretted — brine and lamp oil and old fish doing their best impression of perfume. Ser Maren hated it on sight.

"Every city built on water is a city that expects to drown," she said, pulling her hood lower as we crossed the first of a hundred arched bridges. "They just haven't told the water yet."

"That's very grim for a Tuesday," I said.

"I'm a disgraced knight, ledger-keeper. Grim is the uniform."

I'd learned three things about Maren in the week since the Inquisitors burned half the Meridian Guild's records office with us barely outside it: she could sleep sitting upright against any wall, she counted her sword's maintenance like a rosary, and she did not, under any circumstances, discuss what exactly had gotten her disgraced. I respected that last one. I had my own list of things not discussed, and lately the list was getting longer than the ledgers back home.

Which was the problem, really. Home was a smoking hole in the ground, and the only copy of the First Ledger's hidden columns lived in my head now, transcribed in ink that hadn't dried yet onto pages I kept sewn into my coat like a madwoman's diary. Because that's what I'd become. A madwoman carrying apocalypse arithmetic through canal country.

"You're sure he's here?" Maren asked.

"The guild's own records say Oskarin Vell retired to Vessengard eleven years ago." We ducked under a line of drying eel-nets strung between two leaning houses. "Retired being a generous word. The record notes he 'removed himself from all correspondence and requested his entry be struck.'"

"Struck?"

"As in erased. From the books." I tapped my temple. "You don't ask to be erased from a guild that trades in secrets unless you've done something worth erasing. Or unless you *are* something worth erasing. Either way, he knows where the bodies are buried. Possibly literally."

"And if he refuses? Your plan for that?"

"I have a ledger full of leverage and a face people find trustworthy."

"People find your face *nervous*, Hartwell. You look like a rabbit negotiating with a hawk."

"Trustworthy rabbits get fed, though."

She made a sound that in a better mood might have been a laugh.

* * *

We found him above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, which felt like the kind of joke the universe tells when it's bored. The door was unlocked. That should have been our first warning.

Inside, the room was small and obsessively tidy. Jars of pigment lined one wall — but not artist's colors. These were memory-tints: forget-me-blue, half-remembered amber, that particular grey of things you know happened but can't quite picture. A memory-forger's palette. On a workbench sat a single unfinished object: a locket, open, holding not a portrait but a folded scrap of paper inscribed with a name I couldn't read from the doorway.

And at the bench sat a man who looked like forgetting had been done *to* him rather than by him. Grey at the temples, hands steady, eyes the flat calm of a pond that has decided not to reflect anything.

"If you're selling indulgences," he said without turning around, "the Church of the Tides handles those. If you're Crown, the door was unlocked because I want the walk to the gallows to be scenic. And if you're guild—" now he turned, and his gaze landed on me and went strange, like a man seeing a ghost wearing sensible shoes, "—then the guild is deader than I left it, and you're nobody I know."

"Jude Hartwell," I said. "Ledger-keeper, third class. Was. The office is currently ash."

Something moved behind the flatness of his eyes. Grief, maybe. Or relief. With forgers, I suspected, even they didn't know which was which.

"Ser Maren Kestrel," Maren said, and let her hood fall.

Osk looked at her a long moment. "They gave you a knight and a child clerk to come collect me. The guild must be truly desperate."

"The guild is dead," I said. "That's rather the point. The covenant keeping the Emberlands' fire alive is failing, the tithes stopped working years ago, and tonight's date appears in a century-sealed prophecy book next to my name. We need someone who understands how memories were forged into the original covenant. Which means we need you."

"No." He said it the way other people said *good morning*. Complete. Final. He picked up his locket again and bent over it like we were weather.

"We haven't even offered anything yet," I said.

"There's nothing to offer. Whatever you think I took when I left, I didn't take it. Whatever you think I know, I don't. Eleven years ago Oskarin Vell died in a boat accident, tragically, no body recovered, and the man you're speaking to is a chandler's assistant with an interest in pigments." He didn't look up. "You should go before I remember how to call the watch."

Maren's hand drifted toward her sword out of pure professional habit. I put my own hand on her wrist. Then I did the thing I'd been rehearsing on the boat down, the thing I hated myself for planning and loved myself for knowing would work.

"You forged yourself once already," I said quietly. "Let me show you the forgery."

His brush stopped moving.

"It's in the ledgers, Osk. Everything. The Meridian Guild wrote down every trade it ever made, including the ones that were never trades at all. Including payments. Including *receipts*." I unbuttoned my coat, careful, slow, and drew out the transcription pages. "Including a receipt dated forty-one years ago, signed with a mark I traced to a journeyman memory-forger named Oskarin Vell, for services rendered to the founding circle. Services involving, and I quote the ledger's euphemism, 'the softening of recollections pertaining to the Hearth.'"

The silence in that little room got very heavy, very fast. Somewhere outside, a barge bell clanged twice, indifferent.

"You're lying," he said. His voice had lost its pond-calm. Good. Ponds are boring.

"I'm a ledger-keeper. Lying is the one thing I'm professionally incapable of. It's why they never liked me." I laid the page on his workbench, next to his jars of forgetting. "Read it. Read what you were paid to do. Read what they paid you to make yourself *forget doing it*. Because that's the elegant part, isn't it? They didn't just buy silence. They bought amnesia with a receipt. The guild kept proof of its crime in the same book where it erased the criminal. Someone wanted it recoverable. Someone always does."

He didn't touch the page. His hands hovered over it like it was hot iron.

"I don't remember," he whispered. "You understand that? I don't *remember*. There's a shape where something used to be and it aches like a tooth. Forty-one years I've lived around that hole, telling myself it was grief, it was guilt, it was nothing, and you're telling me—"

"You paid to have it removed. And then, I think, you paid again to have the payment forgotten too. Double-blind. The guild's finest work." I softened my voice, which for me takes effort; I'm told I have the natural warmth of an audit. "Osk. I'm not here to punish you. I'm here because whatever's in that hole in your head is the last piece of the truth about the covenant, and the covenant is dying, and when it dies it won't just be the Emberlands that burn. Every impossible thing this realm runs on — every mercy-trade, every debt-forgiveness, every quiet miracle the guild brokered for a hundred years — goes out with it. I've seen the columns. I've done the sums. I am *very* good at sums, and the sum says everyone drowns. Even cities built on water."

Maren snorted softly at that. Traitor.

Osk finally touched the page. He read it once, fast, like tearing off a bandage. Then again, slow. Then he sat back and laughed — one dry, terrible note with nothing in it.

"*Softening recollections.* They made me complicit and then they made me innocent. Do you know what that is?" He looked up at me, and the flatness was gone entirely, replaced by something rawer and more honest. "That's the cruelest forgery there is. Anyone can steal a memory. They stole my *guilt*. My one honest possession. I've spent eleven years feeling vaguely haunted and calling it temperament."

"Then take it back," I said. "Not the memory. The guilt. Come with us. When we reach the First Hearth, you'll be the only person alive who can tell true memory from forged, and we are going to need that, because I'm beginning to suspect the entire founding of my guild was written by someone with your skill set and fewer scruples."

"Why would you want me?" he asked. "A man who erased his own sins?"

"Because I keep books," I said. "And a bookkeeper knows the difference between a debt that's been paid and a debt that's been *hidden*. Hidden debts accrue interest, Osk. Yours has been accruing for forty-one years. I'm offering you the chance to pay it."

He stood. Walked to the window. Below, the canal slid past, green-black and patient, carrying its cargo of reflections and drowned things.

"The prophecy," he said, not turning around. "Your name beside tonight's date. What does the book actually say happens?"

"That the covenant fails," I said. "That the fire beneath the world gutters out. That the realms are severed." I hesitated. I'm bad at hesitation; it looks wrong on me. "It doesn't say anyone stops it. It doesn't say anyone tries."

"Prophecies love an omission," Osk said. "It's how they get you." He turned back, and began, methodically, to pack his jars — the blues, the ambers, the greys. "Give me a moment. I'm not leaving these for the Crown to find. Some forgetting should stay forgotten until it's needed."

Maren leaned close to me. "Was that persuasion or blackmail?" she murmured.

"Yes," I said.

* * *

He traveled light, for a man smuggling forty-one years of stolen guilt. One satchel of pigments, one coat with suspicious pockets, and the locket, which he wore inside his shirt and touched at intervals like a man checking a wound.

We took the evening barge east, toward the Ashfen road and the long climb toward the First Hearth. The canal city fell away behind us in tiers of lamplit windows, each reflection wobbling on the black water, each one pretending to be solid.

Osk sat across from me, watching me transcribe the day's events into my coat-pages with what I chose to interpret as professional admiration rather than alarm.

"You write everything down," he observed.

"Everything true. It's the only currency I trust."

"Hm." He touched the locket through his shirt. "Then write this down, ledger-keeper. Tonight, on the barge, I remembered something. Not the big thing — the hole's still there. But an edge of it. A smell." His jaw worked. "Sulfur and milk. Sulfur and *milk*, Jude. Do you know what smells like sulfur and milk?"

I shook my head.

"A hatchling," he said. "A dragon egg, freshly opened. Which means whatever the guild did at the founding, whatever they paid me to soften — it wasn't a ritual." He looked out at the darkening water, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "It was a birth. Someone stole something that was being *born*, and the whole covenant has been standing on that theft ever since."

The barge bell rang the hour. Somewhere behind us, far down the canal, a lantern flared that wasn't a lamplighter's — white and cold and moving too steadily.

Inquisitor-light.

"They followed us out of Vessengard," Maren said, already rising, hand on her sword. "How?"

I was already flipping to my newest page, already doing the sums, and the sums came out wrong in the worst way. Not the Crown following us.

Someone aboard had been leaving a trail.

I looked up. Osk met my eyes — and smiled apologetically, and held up one small jar of forget-me-blue.

"Sorry, ledger-keeper," he said. "Habit. I needed to be sure you were who you claimed. Now pour us both some tea, wake the knight gently, and start writing *very* fast — because in about ten minutes, neither of us is going to remember why we're on this barge."
