## 5. Ash and Exile

I had been living in the ruins of Cinderhold for three years when the Crown finally sent someone to kill me.

You'd think they'd have done it sooner. I'd made a nuisance of myself — stealing from supply caravans, burning toll-gates in the night, leaving claw-marks on the doors of every magistrate who'd ever signed an expulsion order against my mother's people. The Emberlands wanted nothing to do with me because I was half human and smelled like it. The human realm wanted nothing to do with me because I was half dragon and *was* it. So I lived between, in the ash-fields where neither kingdom bothered to patrol, and I told myself I preferred it that way.

Three years is a long time to tell yourself a lie. You start believing it around year two. By year three you've decorated it.

So when I heard hoofbeats on the old pilgrim road — four riders, moving quiet, the way soldiers move when they don't want to be heard — I did what any sensible exile does. I climbed into the burnt shell of the watchtower, found a comfortable patch of char, and waited to see who I was going to murder.

Four of them dismounted below me. That gave me pause. Crown patrols travel in eights at minimum, and these four moved wrong for soldiers — one walked with a knight's roll in her shoulders even though she wore no colors, one kept touching his own temples like a man checking his head was still screwed on, and the other two were small, hooded, and carrying satchels that clinked like a chandelier falling downstairs.

Satchels. Ledgers. Guild-bought metal corners on guild-made books.

My blood went hot under my skin, the way it does, the way I hate. Scales prickled up my forearms before I could stop them. Because I knew what the Meridian Guild was. My mother had told me stories — the guild that traded in impossible things, that had bargained with the Emberlands a hundred years ago and then, when the bargaining was done, had helped the humans forget the dragons entirely. Helped them *unremember* us. Every human child who grew up thinking dragons were myth, every magistrate who signed my family's exile with the bored expression of a man filing paperwork — somewhere behind all of it stood the guild, keeping its tidy books.

And now here were four of them, sneaking toward the Emberlands' border with ledgers clinking like stolen bells.

I dropped out of the tower onto the road behind them, wings half-spread for the landing, and put a wall of fire across their escape route. Not a big fire. A *polite* fire. I'm not a monster.

"Turn around," I said, "slowly, and tell me why the Meridian Guild is crawling toward the First Hearth."

They turned around slowly. Good. Obedient. The tall one — gray-haired, sword at her hip despite the lack of colors, hands open and empty — looked at me the way people look at a storm coming in off the sea. Assessing. The temple-touching one made a small sound like a kettle giving up.

But the smallest one, the hooded one with ink-stained fingers, stepped *forward*. In front of the others. Toward me.

"You're Ryn," she said.

That was the wrong thing to say. People who know your name are people who've read files about you, and files mean the Crown, and the Crown means the men who burned my mother's roost while she was still in it. Fire rolled up my throat. "Who told you my name?"

"A ledger," she said. And then — and this is the part I still can't quite believe — she sat down. Cross-legged, on the ash, in front of a half-dragon with smoke coming out of her nose, and opened a book on her knees. "It's a long history. It'll take a few minutes. You can burn me after, if you still want to."

"Jude," the gray-haired woman said, in a tone that suggested this was not the first time this girl had volunteered herself as kindling.

"It's fine, Maren." She licked her thumb, found a page. "Ryn. Born in the border fens. Mother, Vessaneth of the Ashwing line, exiled from the Emberlands for — " she paused, and something crossed her face, some flicker of disgust aimed inward — "for refusing to surrender her clutch to the Emberlords' census. Father, Tomas Rell, a fen-warden, killed in the same raid that — "

"Stop." My voice came out cracked, half-scaled throat doing things throats shouldn't do. "Where did you get that?"

"The guild's records." She didn't flinch. Didn't look away. Ink stains on her fingers, ash settling on her shoulders, and eyes that held steady like she'd decided being afraid of me was a luxury she couldn't afford. "The guild recorded everything, Ryn. Everything anyone paid them to hide. Your mother's exile. Your father's death. Every name the Emberlands struck from their rolls and every name the human registries refused to write down. All of it's in these books, filed under tithes nobody understood."

"And you came all this way to read me my mother's death warrant?"

"No." She turned pages. Found another. "I came to read you the rest of it. Listen."

And she read.

She read about the covenant — the real one, the one beneath the guild's pretty fiction of commerce. About the tithe-fires and the payments that fed a flame under the world, and how the flame wasn't metaphor, wasn't ritual, but *actual*, a hearth older than either realm. She read about the years the payments failed, and the ledger-columns that showed the guild quietly cooking its numbers rather than admit the fire was guttering. She read names — dozens of them — half-bloods like me, born in the cracks between kingdoms, listed in the guild's margins under a column headed *ASSETS UNCLAIMED*.

That one stopped me cold. "Assets unclaimed."

"The guild tracked you," she said quietly. "All of you. The children of the covenant's casualties. They never did anything about it. They just — recorded. Kept the ledger current. Like bookkeeping was the same thing as caring."

Something in my chest did a complicated maneuver that I chose, in the moment, to interpret as indigestion.

"I spent three years hating the guild," I said.

"You should probably keep doing that," said the temple-touching man, who apparently felt the silence required contribution. "We do. We're just — hating it productively. With documents."

"Hush, Osk," said Maren.

"He's not wrong," Jude said. "That's basically our whole plan."

*

Here is what nobody tells you about fury: it needs somewhere to go, and if you don't aim it, it aims itself, usually at whatever's closest. For three years my fury had been aimed at everything — the Crown, the Emberlands, the guild, the weather. Especially the weather. Ash gets into everything and I refuse to apologize for holding grudges against precipitation.

But sitting there on the road, listening to a human girl read my mother's name aloud like it mattered — like it was worth *ink* — I felt the fury doing something it hadn't done since the raid. Losing its grip.

Not gone. Don't misunderstand. But loosening. Because hatred of everything is very heavy, and it turns out a specific grievance with a specific target is much lighter to carry, and this girl was handing me specifics like a merchant handing out free samples.

"The Emberlords took my mother's clutch for the census," I said, when she reached that page again. "What happened to the eggs?"

Jude's jaw tightened. She glanced at Maren, who gave the smallest possible nod.

"We don't know yet," Jude said. "There's a gap in the founding records. A hundred-year gap, right at the covenant's sealing. Something was traded that the guild never wrote down, and I think — " she hesitated, and I watched her decide to say the next part out loud, watched it cost her — "I think it might have been an egg. I think the whole covenant might be built on one theft. And I think the fire is failing because the debt was never honest."

The wind moved through the burnt watchtower above us, whistling through arrow-slits like the building itself was inhaling.

"My mother said," I heard myself say, "that the Emberlands' fire had been strange since before I hatched. Dimmer. That the elders blamed the half-breeds. Blamed *me*." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "They exiled her for hiding her clutch, and then they blamed the children she hid for the fire going cold. And everyone believed it. Both realms believed it. *I* almost believed it."

"That's what lies do," Jude said. "They need somewhere to land. And the people in the margins are always soft ground." She closed the ledger on her knees, but kept her hand flat on it, like an oath. "I'm not asking you to forgive the guild. I'm asking you to help me burn its founding lie down and rebuild the record true. Starting with your mother's name written correctly. Starting with all of them."

Maren spoke for the first time since the fire. Her voice was gravel and old discipline. "The Inquisitors are a day behind us, maybe less. Hale himself. If he finds us scattered, we die separately and uselessly. If we stand together — " she shrugged, a soldier's shrug that admitted the odds without surrendering to them — "we probably still die. But slower, and louder."

"She's very encouraging," Osk murmured.

"I'm honest," Maren said. "It's a recent affliction."

I looked at them. All four of them. A disgraced knight pretending she hadn't already chosen a side. A forger wearing his guilt like a coat two sizes too small. An ink-stained girl who had sat down in front of my fire and offered me her life as a reading fee. And behind them, the ember-glow of my polite wall of flame, still burning neatly along the roadside like a good host's candle.

Three years alone in the ash, telling myself I preferred it. Three years of decorating the lie.

"I know a way through the fens," I said, "that avoids Hale's scouts. And I know where the Emberlands' wardens keep their old patrol schedules — useless now, but Hale won't know that." I let my wings fold down, slow, deliberate. A choice, visible. "And if you're lying to me — any of you — I will find out, because I can smell fear and I can smell forgery, and Osk here reeks of both."

Osk put a hand over his heart. "Flattered. Honestly."

"One condition." I held up a finger, taloned, unapologetic. "When you rewrite those ledgers — my mother goes in as what she was. Not an exile. Not an asset. Vessaneth of the Ashwing line, who defied two kingdoms to keep her children. First entry. Best ink."

Jude smiled — small, tired, real. "First entry. Best ink. I swear it on the record."

And that — that ridiculous, clerical little oath, sworn on a *book* of all things — did more to settle the fire in my chest than three years of solitude ever had. Kinship, it turns out, doesn't arrive like a sunrise. It arrives like a ledger entry: someone writes your name down, in full, correctly, and refuses to cross it out.

We doused the polite fire. I led them off the pilgrim road into the fens, and I was three steps ahead of them, mapping the route in my head, when Jude caught up to walk beside me and asked, carefully, casually, the way you ask when you already dread the answer:

"Ryn — the census. Do you know what the Emberlords wanted the eggs *counted* for? Or..." she swallowed. "Where they kept them?"

I stopped walking. Somewhere far to the east, under the mountains, under a century of careful, dishonest bookkeeping, something answered the question before I could.

The horizon flickered gold — once, twice — like a heartbeat deciding whether to continue.

"No," I said slowly, staring at that light, feeling my scales prickle with recognition I didn't yet understand. "But I think it just told us where to look."
