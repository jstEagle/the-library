## 6. The Inquisitor's Mercy

I have burned forty-one heretics in my eleven years of service to the Crown's Inquisition, and I want you to know that I remember every one of their names.

This is not a confession. It is a professional habit. The guild taught me it — the real guild, the one before the rot — *a ledger is only as honest as the hand that keeps it.* I was eight years old when Aldous Vane wrote that above the chalkboard in the counting room, and I copied it into my practice book in careful strokes, and then I watched him erase it a year later when the ledgers started lying and he decided honesty was bad for business.

The Crown taught me fire. The guild taught me arithmetic. Between the two of them, they made me exactly what I am: a man who can count the cost of everything and has never once been permitted to weep about it.

* * *

The canal city had been a dead end. Osk Ferrow was gone from his loft above the dye-house before my agents arrived, which meant either he'd been warned or he'd finally developed the survival instincts everyone always said he lacked. His workbench was still warm. On it, someone had left a memory-wax seal, half-melted — the kind of forgery Osk could do in his sleep, pressing a false hour into a man's mind like a thumbprint in tallow.

I held the seal for a long time. Then I put it in my evidence case, because that is what an Inquisitor does with things, even things that used to be pieces of people.

"They went south," said Agent Corvel, at my shoulder, breathing through her mouth because the dye-houses stank. "The ferryman says two women and a man who argued about the price of everything."

"That narrows it to half the population of Meridian."

"The woman with the sword argued about the price of *everything*, sir."

"Ah," I said. "Ser Maren Kestrel." Some names you carry like coins in a boot, jangling at every step. "Then the ledger-keeper is with them. Good."

Corvel gave me the look agents give superiors who say unsettling things in pleasant voices. I have cultivated that voice the way other men cultivate gardens. "Good, sir?"

"It means they're following the plan," I said, which was true, though not my plan, and not theirs either, if any of them had the wit to know it. "South. Toward the Emberlands road. Toward the First Hearth."

"And when we take them?"

"When," I said, "we will be merciful. That is the whole point of me, Corvel. Write it down somewhere."

* * *

Here is what the Crown believes, and pays me handsomely to believe, or at least to enforce: the covenant between the human realm and the Emberlands is an old and necessary thing, and old necessary things fail, and when they fail the wise course is to seal the door behind them rather than go looking for whatever broke them. The dragons are dying. Let them die privately. Seal the Emberlands, wall off the ash and the fire, and humanity gets to keep its sunrises without paying rent on them.

It is a clean doctrine. I admire clean doctrines the way I admire well-balanced columns. They are almost never true, but they balance, and there are mornings when balancing is all a man has.

Here is what I actually believe, which I have told no one, not even my mirror: I believe the guild's impossible trades were tithes. I have believed it since I was twelve years old and found a column in the Third Ledger that didn't sum — payments going out to nothing, to nowhere, entries marked only with a small flame-symbol that Vane himself struck through whenever he saw it. I asked him about it once. He looked at me for a long moment with those eyes like wet slate, and then he had me reassigned to the salt warehouses for a season, where the most dangerous thing was gulls.

That was the season the guild stopped feeding its foundlings properly. Budgetary necessity, they called it. There were nine of us children in the guild hall that winter, and by spring there were four, and nobody sums those columns anymore because the answer would be too simple: the money went to the fire, and we were cheaper than the fire.

I chose the Crown because the Crown, whatever else it is, does not abandon its children quietly. It abandons them loudly, with banners, and calls it service. At fourteen I could tell the difference only in volume. Now I can't tell it at all. That is either wisdom or scar tissue; the Inquisition does not test which.

* * *

We caught them at the crossroads shrine outside Vell's Ferry, three days south of the canal city, and I will grant them this: they nearly didn't get caught. Ser Maren had posted watches. The half-dragon — Ryn, the records call her, exile, unregistered, the kind of name that exists only in margins — smelled my agents from a mile out, apparently, because when we came up the shrine hill at dawn there she stood on the shrine wall like a gargoyle with opinions, and the others were awake and armed behind her.

Which left us in a standoff at sunrise, eleven Inquisitors against four fugitives, and me doing the thing I do best, which is talking.

"Inquisitor Hale," I called up. "Crown Writ the Fourth. Jude Hartwell — I'm given to understand you're keeping company with knights of dubious pension history, forgers of dubious memory, and exiles of dubious existence. I've come to offer you a way out of all three dubious categories."

"You've come to burn us," Ryn said. Her voice had a rasp of smoke under it, like a page held too near a candle.

"I have never burned anyone," I said. "I sign the order and stand well back and let the Crown do it. It's an important distinction. The entire legal architecture of the realm rests on important distinctions."

"Hale." That was Hartwell herself now, stepping forward past Maren's outflung arm. She looked worse than the reports suggested — ink-stained, sleep-starved, wearing the particular expression of a person who has read something that rearranged the furniture of her soul and hasn't finished dusting. Ledger-keepers get that look. I remembered it from the glass-dark windows of the counting room, from my own face, young and certain that numbers were a form of safety. "Why you? You're not their hound. Everyone knows Hale doesn't hunt."

"Everyone knows correctly," I said. "I don't hunt. I offer terms. Hunting implies uncertainty about the outcome, and I find uncertainty vulgar."

Maren's sword hadn't wavered a finger-width in all this, which was professionally admirable and personally exhausting. "Say your terms, Inquisitor, and be quick. My arm tires of pointing at you."

"Your arm has pointed at better men than me and missed," I said. "But gladly. Jude Hartwell. Full pardon. Not just for you — reinstatement. A place in the Crown's own archive, keeping records that actually matter, under my personal authority. Your companions walk free. All three. Even the one who shouldn't exist." I let that sit a moment, because generosity lands harder when you make them wait for the invoice. "In exchange, you tell me where the prophecy book is, and you stop this pilgrimage to the First Hearth, and you let the covenant die the quiet death the Crown has planned for it."

Silence. Wind off the low fields. Somewhere below, a lark doing its idiotic morning mathematics.

"And if I refuse?" Hartwell said.

"Then in roughly six hours, the Emberlands' last fire gutters out, both realms begin to starve for warmth in ways your grandchildren will invent new words for, and I arrest you for conspiracy against the Crown. Also possibly the realm. The writ is vague on realms."

"Why?" And there it was — not the question of terms, but the question underneath, the one she'd been carrying since I rode into view. "You were guild. Everyone says so. Why would you *want* the covenant to fail?"

I had prepared for many questions. I had speeches drafted, contingencies mapped, exits planned. I had not prepared for being seen, and so for a moment I did what I never do, which was answer honestly.

"Because the guild took me in when I was seven and threw me away when I was thirteen," I said, "and the Crown picked me up and made me useful, and useful is the only love I've ever been able to verify. The covenant failed *me* long before it fails the world, ledger-keeper. I am simply returning the favor."

The words came out level. They always come out level. But I felt them leave a mark on the way out, the way a blade marks the scabbard.

Hartwell looked at me for a long time. And here is the thing I will not write in my official report, the entry I keep only in the honest ledger I maintain behind my sternum: she looked at me with *recognition*. Not pity — pity I could have survived, pity I have armor for. Recognition. As if she'd read a column that didn't sum and understood exactly why.

"You were one of Vane's foundlings," she said quietly. "The nine. You're one of the nine."

"The five," I corrected. "Accuracy matters."

"And the other four?"

"Dead, scattered, or worse than dead. One of them is standing next to you, incidentally, though I doubt she's mentioned it." I watched Maren's face do something complicated and private. Oh yes. Ser Kestrel and I went back further than her disgrace. The guild discarded children in more ways than one. "So you see, Hartwell, I know precisely what the guild costs the people it claims to keep. I'm offering you the chance to stop paying it."

* * *

She thought about it. I want to be clear about that, because the ballads will inevitably decide otherwise — that she refused instantly, heroically, without a flicker. Nonsense. She stood there in the dawn wind and I watched her weigh it, really weigh it, the way you weigh anything heavy: pardon, safety, her friends' lives on one side; a dying fire and a stolen book and a truth she'd barely begun to speak aloud on the other.

And I confess — privately, silently, in the ledger behind my sternum — I hoped she'd take it.

Not because I wanted the covenant sealed. Because if she'd taken it, I'd have known the world worked the way the Crown says it works: that everyone has a price, that loyalty is just arithmetic done slowly, that no one ever refuses. I have built eleven years on that axiom. It is load-bearing. If she refused, the whole structure might come down, and I genuinely do not know what stands behind it.

"I refuse," Jude Hartwell said.

Of course she did. They always refuse, in the end, the ones worth remembering. It's why we burn them instead of promoting them; promotion requires a flexibility of principle that refusal disqualifies you for.

"The egg," she said, and my spine went cold as a warehouse floor. "You know, don't you? About the founding. About what's under the First Hearth. You were *guild*, Hale — you must have seen the First Ledger."

"I was thirteen," I said. "I was allowed to see the salt inventories."

"Then you don't know," she said, and something in her voice was almost gentle, and gentleness from a target is intolerable, absolutely intolerable, I would rather she'd drawn steel. "The covenant wasn't a bargain. It was a hostage-taking. Every tithe, every impossible trade, every coin the guild ever moved — it was ransom. We've been paying a thief to keep stolen goods. And the Crown wants to let the hostage die so no one ever has to admit it."

"Stop," I said, and my voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough.

"You feel it, don't you. The column that won't sum."

"*Enough.*" I raised my hand, and my agents leveled their crossbows, and the moment folded shut around us like a ledger closing. "Take them or kill them, Corvel — no. Take them. The Crown prefers its heretics breathing until the paperwork clears."

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do. Ryn went off the shrine wall in a spread of wings she should not legally have possessed. Maren Kestrel hit my front line like a debt coming due. Osk Ferrow — the little rat had been *under the altar the whole time* — pressed a memory-seal against Agent Duvane's neck and suddenly Duvane was fighting Corvel over some imagined insult from a decade ago, and the dawn filled with shouting and smoke and the shriek of a half-dragon who had decided, quite reasonably, that we were the problem.

And through all of it, Jude Hartwell looked back at me once — once — across the chaos, ink-stained and impossible, and called out:

"Come with us, Hale! Come and *read*!"

And I stood in the smoking grass of the shrine hill, Writ in hand, agents bleeding around me, and did not give the order to shoot her in the back.

I am entering that omission in my report as tactical mercy.

I am entering it here as what it is: the first column in eleven years that will not sum.

By nightfall I had dismissed my surviving agents, forged my own transfer orders — Osk's craft, learned from watching, forgive me, or don't — and turned my horse south along the Emberlands road, riding alone toward the First Hearth, telling myself I was pursuing the fugitives.

A ledger is only as honest as the hand that keeps it.

Mine is about to be tested at the source of the fire.
