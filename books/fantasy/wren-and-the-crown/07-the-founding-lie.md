## 7. The Founding Lie

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in old books. I'd always thought it was the silence of waiting — of pages patient for a reader. Standing in the guild's deepest vault with the First Ledger open before me, I learned it was something else entirely.

It was the silence of a held breath. Of a secret that had been keeping itself for a hundred years, wondering when someone would finally be stupid enough to come looking.

"You're going to want to sit down," Osk said behind me. He hadn't touched the ledger. He wouldn't. He'd stood at the vault door since we descended, arms crossed like a man guarding against his own shadow. "Or don't. You won't listen to me either way."

"I listen fine."

"You listen the way ledgers listen," he said. "You wait until the numbers confess."

I didn't answer, because that was fair, and because the First Ledger deserved my whole attention. It was nothing like the working books upstairs — no neat columns of tithes, no Aldous Vane's precise hand marching across the years. This was older. The vellum was the color of old ivory, soft as cloth at the edges, and the ink had gone brown with age except where later hands had tried to correct it. Correction was, I would learn, the founding activity of the Meridian Guild.

The covenant text ran down the left page in a script I half-recognized — the same angular hand that appeared in the margins of our oldest trade records, the one the catalog called *Founder's Script* and treated as decorative. Decorative. We had filed away the actual terms of the world's survival as *decoration*.

I read it aloud, slowly, translating as I went, because some things need to be spoken to be believed.

"'Let there be one fire beneath all fires,'" I said. "'And let it burn so long as the debt is honored.'" A standard covenant opening. Every guild contract began with a consideration clause. "And let the realms be joined at the Hearth, and let no wall stand between ember and flesh.'"

Then came the sealing clause. And everything stopped being standard.

"*'And let the seal be the egg of Vaelith, last-laid of the Mother's line, kept in trust by the first signatories, that its warmth may feed the Hearth and its loss may end all burning.'*"

I read it again. Then a third time, moving my finger under each word like a child, as if the sentence might behave differently if I slowed down enough.

Kept in trust.

That was the phrase. That was the little knife hidden in the wool of it. *In trust.* As if the egg had been handed over. As if there had been a transaction, a signature, a dragon mother who signed something.

"Osk," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat. Ledger-flat. "What does 'kept in trust' mean in Founder's Script?"

He was quiet for long enough that I turned around. He had gone gray. Not pale — gray, the way metal goes before it rusts.

"It means stolen," he said. "It means stolen, but the person writing it couldn't make themselves write *stolen*, so they wrote the most respectable lie available." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Gods. A hundred years, and the euphemism still works. You read it three times before you heard it."

"The egg was taken from the Emberlands."

"The egg was taken from Vaelith's nest while she burned on the front lines of the last war," Osk said. "Yes."

The vault felt colder. That's not possible — vaults keep steady temperature; I'd checked the climate logs myself, twice, during an inventory dispute — but cold crawled up my arms anyway.

"Say the rest."

"You've read it. You don't need me to—"

"Osk. Say the rest. If you make me assemble it alone, I'll assemble it wrong, and then Maren will have to fix me, and she'll be insufferable about it."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might live. He came closer. He still didn't touch the book.

"There were eleven founders," he said. "Merchants, mostly. Smugglers, honestly, if we're being accurate, and since accuracy is apparently the theme of the week — smugglers who got rich running goods through the borderlands during the war. When the war ended and the Crown moved to seal the Emberlands, their livelihood died with it. So they went looking for a way to keep the border *open*. Profitable. Permanently profitable."

"And the egg."

"They found the nest in the ash fields after the battle of Cindral Vale. One egg, unhatched. The mother dead or dying — accounts differ, and the accounts are theirs, so believe neither." His voice had gone very quiet and very even, the way voices go when they're carrying more weight than they want to show. "They took it. They discovered almost immediately what they had — not treasure, exactly. Leverage. A dragon egg isn't just an egg, Jude. It's a fire that hasn't decided where to burn yet. The Emberlands' whole covenant magic, the joining between their realm and ours — it flows through unborn fire. An unhatched egg is a *door*."

"And they built a guild on the door."

"They built a guild on the hostage." He said it plainly, and I watched him pay for the plainness; his hands had started shaking, so he shoved them into his coat. "Every tithe, every impossible trade, every ledger line you've spent your life balancing — it all feeds the Hearth, and the Hearth is warmed by the egg, and the egg sits in the dark under this building because eleven smugglers decided a stolen future made better collateral than a stolen shipment ever had."

"The dragons agreed to this?"

"The dragons agreed to *nothing*. That's the part the histories got wrong — deliberately wrong, corrected generation after generation until the lie wore smooth." He pointed at the right-hand page. "Read the marginalia. The early ones. Before the corrections start."

I read them. The earliest note was in the founder's own hand, cramped and hurried:

*V. came to the wall at moonrise. Would not cross. Spoke the old tongue at us for an hour. Asher wept. We do not return the egg. Without it there is no guild, and without the guild there is nothing between the realms but soldiers.*

Below it, another hand, decades later:

*Mother-fire seen above the Hearth vents again. Third time this century. She has not forgotten. We must strengthen the tithes.*

And below that, in the newest ink, the freshest entry in the book — dated four years ago, in handwriting I knew. Neat. Precise. Marching.

*Aldous Vane.*

*The flame gutters. The egg weakens. Recommend we begin winding down operations and prepare sealed statements. The covenant will fail on schedule. Let it be recorded that the Guild met its obligations.*

*Let it be recorded.* Four words. The whole rotten architecture of my life, balanced neatly on top of them. I had spent six years believing the ledgers were honest because the columns added up. But you can make anything add up if you're willing to decide what counts.

"He knew," I said. "Aldous knew it was failing and he started pre-writing the apology."

"Aldous knew what all the masters knew," Osk said. "And here's the thing you actually need to hear, so put your finger back in the book and read the tithe clause again. The original one. What does it say the tithes are *for*?"

I flipped forward, found it. "*'And let the signatories render up one-tenth of all gain, in gold and in memory and in name, that the Hearth may be fed and the egg kept warm—'*"

"In name," I said slowly. "Tithes paid in names."

"That's why guild members give up their family names on induction. Everyone thinks it's tradition. Symbolic humility." Osk's jaw tightened. "It's fuel. The egg doesn't just burn gold. It burns identity. Belonging. The Hearth eats the thing that makes a person *someone*, and it converts it into covenant-fire. That's why the old members go strange. That's why so many die forgetful, smiling, hollowed out. We haven't been paying a tax, Jude. We've been feeding a furnace with ourselves."

Hale, I thought. The boy the guild raised and discarded, who chose the Crown rather than be spent. He'd known. Maybe not the details — but he'd felt the truth of it in his bones, the way you feel a draft from a door everyone else insists is shut.

"And the payments have been failing," I said. "Because the membership is dying. Fewer people, less name to burn."

"Fewer people, less *everything*. The egg is starving. Which means the Hearth is guttering. Which means—" 

"—the prophecy," I finished. "Tonight. My name. The date the covenant fails." I closed the First Ledger, gently, the way you close a coffin. "It wasn't a prediction. It was arithmetic. Someone did the math on how much fuel was left."

Osk nodded. And then, quietly: "There's one more thing. And I need you to understand that I only know it because I dug it out of my own head, piece by piece, the way you dig shrapnel out."

I waited.

"I didn't just erase my memory of the founding documents," he said. "I erased my memory of the *vault*. I was the keeper of the First Ledger for nine years. And in my last month, before I took the knife to my own past, I wrote myself a letter and hid it somewhere I'd never think to look." He reached into his coat and drew out a strip of vellum, cracked with age, folded small. "Found it two days ago. I've been working up the courage to give it to you since."

"Osk—"

"It's in my hand. Old hand. I checked." He set it on the closed ledger, between us, like a man laying down a card he'd been palming for years. "Read it."

I unfolded it. The writing was mine to decipher now — his younger self's cramped script, the letters of a man writing fast because he was afraid of being interrupted, possibly by himself.

*I, Oskar Vell, keeper of the First Ledger, being of sound mind and unsound conscience, do record the following: the egg lives. It is not dormant. It dreams. On quiet nights the Hearth hums and I have heard, through the stone, something like a heartbeat. And I have begun to suspect the thing the founders feared most — that the egg cannot be kept forever. That it will hatch, or die, or call, and every debt we have ever written against it will come due at once.*

*Find the mother's line. Find what remains of it. There is a child of ash in the borderlands whom the Crown could not kill and the Emberlands would not claim. Whatever else happens — find Ryn before the flame goes out.*

I looked up. Osk was watching me with the expression of a man who has finally set down something heavy and isn't yet sure whether his arms remember how to be empty.

"Ryn," I said. "You wrote this four years ago. Why didn't you say anything when we found her?"

"Because I didn't remember writing it," he said. "Because I made certain I wouldn't. Because I was a coward with excellent penmanship." He exhaled. "But you asked me once why I erased myself. Here's your answer. I found out what the guild really was, Jude. I found the egg. I stood under the Hearth and listened to it dream. And then I went back upstairs and kept the books, because that was my job, and I told myself the numbers weren't mine to question." His voice cracked, just barely, on the last word. "I lasted nine days. Then I took everything out of my head and walked into a canal city and became nobody, because nobody was the only honest thing left to be."

The vault was silent around us. Above, faintly, through a hundred feet of stone, I thought I could hear it — the Hearth, humming. Or maybe a heartbeat. Or maybe just my own pulse, hammering the same terrible arithmetic into my ears:

One theft. One egg. One hundred years of tithes, trades, names, debts, prophecies, Inquisitors, dying members, guttering flame — every column in every book I had ever balanced traced back to eleven smugglers and a stolen door.

We hadn't been keeping the covenant. We'd been collecting ransom.

"We have to tell Maren," I said.

"She'll want to renew it," Osk said. "Patch the old terms, refill the furnace, protect what's left. She'll tell you the truth can wait until the realms are safe."

"I know."

"And you're going to tell her anyway."

I picked up the First Ledger. It was heavier than it had been an hour ago, though it weighed exactly the same.

"Yes," I said. "And then we're going to open the vault under the Hearth — because if that egg is dreaming, it deserves to wake up to an honest world. And if it's dying—"

The lamps guttered. All of them, at once, every flame in the vault leaning sideways toward the stairwell as though called by a voice too deep for ears.

Osk went white. "That's not a draft."

"No," I said, already running for the stairs. "That's a heartbeat. And I think it just skipped."
