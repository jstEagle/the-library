## 8. Where Loyalty Ends

I have stood watch through sieges, through plague winters, through the long ugly night when Ser Halvard's company broke and ran and I was the only fool who held the line at Corren's Ford. I know how to wait for a battle.

What I do not know how to do is sit across a campfire from a twenty-year-old ledger-keeper who has just told me she intends to burn down everything I spent my life guarding — and mean it kindly.

"You're not listening," Jude said.

"I'm listening fine." I fed the fire a stick I did not need to feed it, because my hands wanted work and my mouth had been ordered out of the argument. "You're going to stand in front of the last living dragon-fire in creation, open the guild's oldest book, and read aloud that we are thieves. That the covenant our grandfathers bled for, that knights died holding the trade roads for, is built on an egg we stole like common smugglers."

"Not *we*," Jude said. "People who've been dead a hundred years."

"Then it's dead sin, girl. Let it lie with them."

"It isn't lying." She pulled the First Ledger into her lap, that great slab of vellum and iron clasps, and rested her hands on it the way other people rest their hands on a wounded friend. "It's in every column. Every impossible trade, every tithe that never made sense — I can trace all of it back to one payment, one entry, one night. The fire beneath the world isn't a gift we maintain. It's a ransom we pay. And ransoms end, Maren. Either you pay until you're empty, or someone comes to collect the hostage."

"And your answer is to hand over the hostage."

"My answer is to give back what was stolen." Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it. I'd have known better what to do with a shaking voice. "The egg belongs to the Emberlands. It always has. We reforge the covenant on honest terms or we don't reforge it at all."

"And if the honest terms fail?" I leaned forward. "If the dragons look at the egg, then look at us, and decide a century of theft deserves more than an apology? You'll have traded a dying covenant for no covenant. Both realms go dark, and the last thing anyone ever writes in a guild ledger is *she meant well*."

Osk, who had been whittling something at the edge of the firelight — he whittles when he's nervous, which is to say always — cleared his throat. "For what it's worth, I could forge her a memory. Make her believe she never found the entry. Very clean work. She'd sleep beautifully."

"Don't you dare," said Jude.

"He's joking," I said.

"I'm mostly joking," said Osk.

Ryn said nothing. Ryn had said nothing since the First Ledger gave up its secret, which for Ryn was a kind of screaming. The half-dragon sat apart from our little circle, wings folded tight, tail curled around those clawed feet, watching the fire as though it were a relative they weren't sure they liked. When I glanced over, they caught me looking and looked away first.

That decided me more than any argument. Because here is what I knew that Jude didn't: what happens to exiles when the old powers renegotiate. I'd seen treaties signed before. The people on the margins — the half-bloods, the deserters, the ones whose existence embarrassed both sides — they don't get seats at the table. They get footnotes. And footnotes burn.

"Jude." I kept my voice level, knight-to-recruit, the tone I hadn't used since the guild stripped my spurs. "Renew the covenant. Seal it the way it's always been sealed. Take the tithes, keep the fire fed, and write the truth in the ledgers after — quietly, properly, where it can't hurt anyone alive. Let history judge us gently. That's not a lie. That's mercy."

"That's a lie with a schedule," she said.

"*Girl*—"

"No. You want to hear it plainly? Here it is." She closed the Ledger. "My master burned a page in front of me rather than let me read my own name. The Inquisitors would rather see both realms sealed than admit what the Crown's peace is built on. Osk carved up his own mind because the truth was unbearable. Ryn has been thrown away by two worlds that needed a scapegoat more than they needed a child." She looked around the fire, at each of us, and I will swear on whatever's left of my honor that her eyes were wet and furious about it. "Everyone in this story has already paid for the founding lie except the people who told it. I'm done collecting their debts."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the wind moved through the dead pines of the Ashvale ridge, and I thought: this is what the Emberlands must sound like now. A forest holding its breath.

"The guild raised me," I said finally. "After Corren's Ford, after the disgrace, when the Crown wanted my head and every order in the kingdom had my name blacked through — the guild took me in. Gave me walls to guard again. A purpose." My thumb found the old scar along my palm, the one from the oath I technically still owed. "I don't have a realm, Jude. I have an institution. You're asking me to help you break it."

"I'm asking you to help me save what it was supposed to be."

"And if I think those aren't the same thing?"

She didn't flinch. "Then I guess we've found where your loyalty ends."

There it was. Spoken by a child in secondhand boots, and true, and I hated her for approximately four seconds, which is a personal record for hating anyone.

I stood. Picked up my sword, because a knight without her sword is just a woman with opinions, and I'd had quite enough opinions for one night. "I'm taking first watch," I said. "Sleep. If Inquisitors come, you'll want your wits, whatever you intend to spend them on."

"Maren—"

"Watch," I said. "First. Mine."

And I walked out beyond the firelight into the cold, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you lose arguments you've already lost.

* * *

Here is what I did on first watch, which stretched — by my own cowardice — into second and third:

I counted reasons.

Reason one: she's young. Twenty, maybe twenty-one, and grief makes prophets of the young. Give her a winter and she'd soften. (Counter-reason: I'd thought that about myself at twenty, and I hadn't softened. I'd just gotten better at aiming.)

Reason two: she might be wrong about the terms. Renewal might hold another century. Another century is a lot of lives. (Counter-reason: a century bought with a stolen child — because that's what an egg is, a child not yet argued into being — buys exactly nothing. It only borrows.)

Reason three, the big one, the one I circled all night like a hawk on a rope: the guild was the last thing that ever chose me back.

Understand what the guild was to the likes of me. The Crown has room for its heroes and its traitors and precious little between. The guild had room for everyone the ledgers couldn't balance — disgraced knights, forgers, bastards, exiles. It was a roof. It was the only roof. And roofs, I told myself, are not asked to be honest. Roofs are asked to hold.

But somewhere past midnight, watching frost form on the leather of my bracer, an older memory surfaced, uninvited, the way the true ones do.

I was newly sworn to the guild, twenty-three and raw, standing guard outside Aldous Vane's study while he met with a delegation from the Emberlands. Through the door I heard a dragon-envoy — old Smaragdine, voice like gravel poured slow — ask him one question: *"When does the hostage go home?"*

And Vane laughed. Warm, easy, the laugh that made merchants trust him with fortunes. *"When it stops being useful,"* he said. *"You know how these things work."*

I had heard it and filed it away as commerce. That was the word we used. Everything was commerce. The guild taught us that word the way the Crown teaches its soldiers the word *necessary*, and for the same reason: so you can stop hearing what's actually being said.

Twenty years I carried that door between me and the sound of it.

Jude hadn't even been born. And she'd walked through it in an afternoon, because nobody had taught her yet to call a theft a trade.

* * *

Dawn came up grey over the Ashvale ridge, the color of old ash, which felt less like weather than commentary.

They were breaking camp when I came back down. Osk saw me first and did his best impression of a man who hadn't spent the night listening to me pace. Ryn watched me with those furnace-gold eyes and gave nothing away, which from Ryn is diplomacy. Jude stood by the cold fire pit, rolling the ledgers into their oilcloth, and she didn't look up until my shadow crossed her hands.

"If you're here to argue again—" she started.

"I'm not." I unbuckled my sword belt and held it out to her, scabbard and all.

She stared at it. "...What am I supposed to do with that?"

"Nothing. It's not a surrender. It's a deposit." I almost smiled. "You're rebuilding the guild as keepers of the record. Fine. Then record this: Ser Maren Kestrel, disgraced of Corren's Ford, formerly sworn to the Meridian Guild, hereby swears herself instead to Jude Hartwell — not to the institution, not to the ledgers, not to the covenant. To you. Because somebody in this mad enterprise has to know which end of a sword to hold, and because—" and here my voice did shake, damn it, twenty years too late to be dignified about anything— "because you're right. And I'd rather be right with you than loyal to a lie."

Jude took the sword the way you take something fragile, which no one had ever accused my sword of being. "Maren. What Vane did — what the founders did — that's not on you."

"It is if I knew and stayed quiet. That's the whole arithmetic of it." I looked at the others. Osk had gone very still; Ryn's tail had loosened, just slightly, the way a fist loosens. "We move for the First Hearth today?"

"Today," Jude said.

"Then there's something you should know before we march." I took the sword back and belted it on, because if we were doing this, we were doing it armed. "Hale won't meet us in the field. He was raised guild — he knows how we travel, where we'll shelter, what we'll need. He'll be ahead of us, not behind. And he won't come alone."

"How many?" Ryn asked, the first words they'd spoken since the night before, rough as struck flint.

I looked east, toward the pass, toward the thin thread of smoke rising against the dawn that was either a campfire or the world's patience finally burning through.

"All of them," I said. "He's brought the entire Inquisition to the Hearth. He means for tonight to be the last night either realm keeps secrets."
