## 9. The Last Dragon-Fire

**Jude**

The First Hearth was not what I had imagined, which was fitting, because nothing about the guild had ever been what it claimed to be.

I had pictured a temple. A cathedral of black iron and dragon-bone, incense and choirs. What I got instead was a cave — a vast one, sure, with a ceiling lost in shadow like the underside of the world — but at its center there was only a hearthstone the size of a dinner table, and on that hearthstone, a flame no bigger than a candle.

A hundred years of tithes. A thousand impossible trades. Every strange payment in every ledger I'd ever balanced — the memories, the years of life, the names given up willingly by guild members who never knew why — all of it funneled down to keep alive something you could snuff out by breathing wrong.

"It's dying," Ryn said quietly. She could feel it, I think, in a way the rest of us couldn't. Her scales had gone dull since we entered the Emberlands' threshold, the copper of them fading to ash-gray, as if the land itself were draining her by way of apology. "The fire remembers what it is. It remembers it isn't supposed to be fed."

"Fed?" Osk shifted beside me, arms wrapped around himself. He'd been quiet since the First Ledger told us the truth — since his own erased memory came back to him in a flood and he'd spent two days being sick about it. "What do you mean, *fed*?"

"It was never meant to burn like this," Ryn said. "It was meant to *live*."

And then Inquisitor Hale stepped out of the dark, and the conversation ended the way conversations do when crossbows end them.

There were six of them, fanned out along the cavern wall, white-and-silver livery catching the guttering light. Hale stood front and center, hands empty, which from him was somehow worse than a weapon. He looked older than he had in the canal city. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with marching.

"Jude Hartwell," he said. "You've made this very difficult."

"I've been told I have that effect on ledgers too."

He almost smiled. Almost. "I'm going to offer you something one last time. Walk away from them. Walk away from all of it. The Crown will seal the Emberlands whether the fire dies naturally or not — the difference is whether you're standing here when it does. There's a place for you in Meridian. A real place. You're the best ledger-keeper they've produced in a century, and everyone knows the guild wasted you."

Behind me, I heard Ser Maren's armor shift — the small sound of a woman deciding where to put her weight.

"You were one of us once," I said to Hale. "The ledgers say so. Entry four thousand and eleven. A child traded to the guild for a debt his father couldn't pay, apprenticed to the record-keepers, then—" I hesitated, because the next part still made my throat tight. "Then abandoned. When the tithe quotas changed. When children stopped counting as assets worth feeding."

"Don't." The word cracked in half.

"The guild did that to you. Not me. And the Crown picked up the pieces and taught you to call it loyalty."

"The Crown gave me purpose." His voice was flat now, controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut against a flood. "The guild gave me a number. So yes, Jude. I chose. And tomorrow, when the Emberlands are sealed and the dragons are a story again, I'll sleep fine. Can you say the same? Because I've read your file. You balance things. It's what you *do*. And if you walk through that fire with them, everything you've ever balanced comes due at once."

I looked back at my people. Maren, scarred and steady. Osk, hollowed out but standing. Ryn, gray as old pewter, watching the little flame with an expression I couldn't read and was afraid to try.

Then I looked at the egg.

We had carried it three hundred miles in a padded case strapped to Osk's back, and it had never once felt heavy, which should have been my first clue. It sat on the hearthstone now, beside the dying fire, its shell the color of storm clouds with veins of gold running through it like handwriting.

Every debt traces back to one theft. I had written those words myself, in the margin of the First Ledger, the night we understood. The first covenant hadn't been a bargain. It had been a ransom note. The founders stole an egg, held the future of the dragons hostage, and called the payments *trade*. The fire beneath the world wasn't a gift they maintained. It was a prisoner they kept alive just healthy enough to be useful.

And prisoners, eventually, stop eating.

"Hale," I said. "Do you know what happens when the last dragon-fire goes out?"

"The realms seal. The Emberlands fold in on themselves. Humanity stops paying for its sins."

"No. The realms *were* joined once, before the covenant — did you ever wonder why the maps show old roads running under the mountains? Roads nobody built? The fire didn't divide us. It connected us. One flame, burning on both sides of the border, and everything the light touched belonged to both worlds. Your sealing doesn't protect humanity. It amputates it." I stepped forward, past the point where the crossbows tracked me, and put my hand flat on the hearthstone. The stone was cold. Cold as a grave, cold as a closed book. "The guild kept a hostage for a hundred years and billed both realms for the room and board. I'm not renewing that contract. I'm voiding it."

I turned to the others.

"It has to be returned," I said. "Not destroyed. Not traded. *Returned.* Freely, with the debt named out loud, so the record is true. That's the only thing that reforges a covenant — not a signature. An honest entry."

"And who carries it?" Osk asked softly. He already knew.

* * *

**Ryn**

They all looked at me, and I hated them for it, and I loved them for it, and mostly I understood.

I was the only one the fire would not kill. Half of me was Emberlands; the other half had been raised on bread and canal water and human kindness that always came with conditions. My mother's people had rejected me at the border when I was nine — *too much man*, the warden said. My father's people had rejected me long before that — *too much dragon*, the neighbors said, though they used other words. I had spent thirty-one years belonging nowhere, and here, at the end of everything, was the first job in history that required exactly that: someone who belonged to neither realm and therefore, possibly, to both.

"The fire will take what you are," Jude said. "That's the sacrifice. You'll carry it in, and it will read you, all of you, the human part included, and either it accepts the return or—"

"Or I'm ash," I said. "Yes. I did the arithmetic too. I'm told I'm good at arithmetic now. Terrible influence, Hartwell."

Osk unstrapped the case with hands that shook. "I stole the memory of this," he said. "Of the theft. I wrote over it so I could live with what my masters did, and living with it became doing it, and doing it became — this. A hundred years of it. If you come back out of that fire, I want you to know the ledger records my name next to the founding sin. All of our names. We didn't hide anymore."

"Good," I said. "Shame you can carry is shame that can be paid."

Maren clasped my forearm, gauntlet and scale, knight and exile. "I held a line once for an institution that lied to me," she said. "This time it's for something true. Try to survive it anyway. I've grown accustomed to your insubordination."

And Jude — Jude took my face in her two small bookkeeper's hands, ink still in the creases of her knuckles, and said, "Whatever happens in there, I'm writing it down. Exactly as it happens. Even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly. That's the whole point of us now."

"That," I said, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and I need you to know how depressing that is."

I lifted the egg. Warm. Alive. For a hundred years it had been a hostage, and in my arms it weighed less than a sleeping cat, because the truly terrible things never weigh anything at all until it's time to put them down.

I walked toward the flame.

* * *

**Ser Maren**

The Inquisitors moved the moment Ryn moved. Of course they did. Orders are orders, and men with crossbows are rarely invited to think.

"Hold!" I stepped into their line of fire, alone, sword drawn but low. Six bolts aimed at my chest, and behind me the soft glow of the hearth, and beyond that a half-dragon walking into legend with a stolen future in her arms. "Inquisitor Hale. Your quarrel is with the guild. I am the last of the guild's sworn guard. Whatever judgment falls tonight, it passes through me first."

"Maren Kestrel," Hale said. "Deserter. Forger's accomplice. Traitor to the Crown." Each title landed like a coin counted out. "Step aside. This is your one act of mercy on offer, and I am not a merciful man, so it expires quickly."

"I stood aside for twenty years while that institution fed children to a lie," I said. "I am done standing aside. Draw your lines, Inquisitor. Hold your fire or spend your bolts. But know that the woman behind me has already won, whatever happens next — because she's telling the truth, and truth is the only thing your seals and your fires and your white cloaks have never once been able to counterfeiting."

"Poetic," Hale said. "For a disgraced knight."

"For a knight," I said, "there is no such thing as disgrace. Only debts. And I intend to pay mine in full view of witnesses."

Hale raised his hand. The crossbows drew. Behind me, Ryn reached the flame, knelt, and set the egg into the fire like a mother laying a child in a cradle, and the whole world held its breath—

—and the little candle-flame went out.

* * *

**Jude**

Darkness. Total, absolute, the kind of dark that has texture, that presses on your eyes like a hand.

Someone screamed — an Inquisitor, young, terrified, the sound of a man whose certainty just died. Maren shouted something about holding. Osk's hand found mine in the black and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

Then the hearthstone warmed beneath my palm.

Warm, then hot, then blazing, and light came up out of the stone itself — not the thin yellow candlelight of the hostage-fire but something deeper, red-gold and enormous, pouring upward through cracks in the rock, through the ceiling, through the world. The egg's shell split with a sound like a bell struck underwater, and what rose out of it was not a creature but a *color*, a heat, a presence that looked at each of us — at Ryn standing unburnt in its heart, at Maren with her drawn sword, at Hale with his lowered hand, at me — and *knew*. Everything. Every tithe, every theft, every name in every ledger, weighed and found legible.

The mountains trembled. Far above, I knew without knowing how, the borders between the realms were dissolving like ink in rain, the old roads waking, the two worlds remembering they had once been one country with one fire.

And in the middle of the light, Ryn turned to look back at me. Her scales burned copper again — brighter than I'd ever seen them, bright as a struck coin — and she was laughing, actually laughing, ash and gold and alive.

"It accepted the return!" she called. "Jude — it *accepted*—"

Hale sank slowly to his knees in the firelight, six crossbows forgotten around him, staring at the reborn Hearth with the expression of a man reading the last page of a book he'd burned the first edition of.

"Well," I said, mostly to myself, already reaching for the satchel, the pen, the blank page. "Somebody has to write this down."

And then the light spoke my name.
