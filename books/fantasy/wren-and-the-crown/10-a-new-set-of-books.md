## 10. A New Set of Books

The ash took eleven days to settle out of the sky over the Meridian Quarter, and I spent every one of them sweeping.

Not because the guildhall needed it — though it did, spectacularly; the roof over the eastern stacks had partially collapsed during the Inquisitors' retreat, and there was a fine grey silt on everything, in everything, ground into the ledgers like a confession nobody wanted read aloud. I swept because sweeping was a task with edges. You started at one wall. You ended at another. Nothing about it asked me to think about the fact that I had held a dragon egg in my arms while an empire's faith cracked down its middle.

On the twelfth day, Maren found me with the broom and took it away from me without a word, the way she takes away most things she thinks are bad for me — swords, second helpings of guilt, brooms.

"You're the guildmaster now," she said. "Guildmasters don't sweep."

"Guildmasters absolutely sweep. That's the whole point of what we're doing. Nobody's above the work anymore." I held out my hand for the broom. She did not return it. Ser Maren Kestrel has many virtues; flexibility is not among them.

"The guildmaster," she said, "writes the first entry. That's the tradition you're keeping, isn't it? So write it."

* * *

The guildhall looked different with honest light in it.

That sounds like a metaphor, but I mean it literally. For a century, the Meridian Guild's windows had been shuttered with smoked glass — a privacy measure, Aldous Vane called it, though I suspect now it was shame wearing a practical coat. We'd pried the shutters off three days after the reforging, and morning came in like something that had been waiting a long time at the door. It fell across the long tables where the impossible trades had been counted, across the empty niches where the sealed prophecy books had stood, across the great hearth at the hall's center, which — this is the part I still can't say plainly, even to myself — was warm.

Not fire-warm. There was no fire in it. Warm the way a hand is warm. The First Hearth's flame had gone into the world when Ryn carried the egg home, but the stone remembered, the way stone does, and the Emberlands remembered harder. On quiet mornings you could stand at that hearth and feel something on the other side of it, vast and patient and *awake*, leaning against the wall between realms like a neighbor listening through plaster.

Osk claimed he could hear it humming. Osk claims a lot of things. He is a forger of memories who erased his own role in the guild's founding sin and then spent forty years being a charming stranger to himself, so his relationship with verifiable fact is, at best, collegial.

But he was there when I sat down at the master's desk with a fresh ledger — new paper, new binding, no hidden columns, nothing sewn into the spine but thread — and he watched me open the cover with the expression of a man watching someone defuse the bomb he built.

"Nervous?" I asked.

"I don't get nervous. I get *editorial*." He pulled up a stool. "You're going to write it all down, aren't you. The egg. Vane. Hale. The whole ugly century."

"That's rather the point."

"It's also rather the point that the old guild survived by *not* doing that." He tapped the blank page. "Every ledger before this one had two sets of columns. The true ones and the ones that kept people comfortable. You're holding a book with only one kind of column in it. Do you know how heavy that is?"

I did, actually. My hands had learned the weight of true columns over the past months, and they were heavier than any false set I'd ever balanced. But heavy isn't the same as wrong.

"Yes," I said. "And I'm going to write it anyway."

He studied me for a moment — the scarred face, the eyes that had seen too much of both worlds to belong comfortably to either — and then he smiled, crooked and real, which from Osk is practically a signed affidavit.

"Well," he said. "Then I suppose I'll want my name spelled correctly."

* * *

They came, one by one, in the order I'd hoped and feared they would.

Maren first, because Maren is always first through any door that might be dangerous. She knelt — actually knelt, which I forbade immediately and she ignored, because she ignores most of what I forbid — and swore herself to the guild anew. Not the old oath. The old oath had been to the institution, to the secret, to the fire that burned whether or not anyone knew why. Her new oath was four sentences long, and I made her say them slowly:

"To keep the record true. To tell the truth even when loyalty begs otherwise. To guard both realms, and neither above the other. And to serve the guild only as long as the guild deserves it."

"That last clause is insubordinate," she said, rising.

"It's the most important clause in the book."

She almost smiled. With Maren, that's a parade.

Osk came next, and made a joke of his oath, and then didn't — halfway through, his voice went quiet and rough, and he said the line about truth twice, the second time looking at his own hands, which had forged so many false pasts for other people. When he finished, he said, "There. Now it's on record. Now I can't erase it." And he laughed, but it was the laugh of a man setting down something very heavy, and I let him have it without comment, because some weights you can only honor by letting the person carrying them pretend, briefly, that it was easy.

Ryn came last.

Ryn does everything last, on principle — arrives last, eats last, sleeps last, patrols the perimeter of every room they're in like the room might be a trap. Raised human, rejected by humans. Half-dragon, unwelcome among dragons. A creature of both realms and neither, until eleven days ago, when they had carried the stolen egg into the failing fire of the First Hearth and given it back, and the fire had taken it — taken *them*, for one terrible minute — and then given them back changed, marked, their skin laced through with lines of faint ember-glow like cracks in cooling bronze.

The Emberlands had accepted them in that moment. I'd felt it through the hearth-stone, a vast slow recognition rolling outward like a bell. *Ours,* the fire had said. *Ours.*

Ryn still wasn't sure they believed it.

"You don't have to do this," I said, when they stopped at the desk and looked at the open ledger like it might bite. "You've paid more than anyone. You could walk out that door and both realms would owe you—"

"Don't." Their voice cracked like heat on cold stone. "Don't you dare make me a debt. I've spent my whole life as somebody's debt or somebody's mistake. I'm standing here because I choose the record too. Write my name."

So I wrote it. Ryn Ashvane — the surname was Maren's idea, offered gruffly two nights ago over terrible stew, and Ryn had gone so still and silent that Maren had briefly looked alarmed, until Ryn said, quietly, "No one's ever put their name next to mine before," and then left the table abruptly, and Maren and I pretended not to notice that they'd gone to the well in the dark to cry where the dark couldn't testify.

Four names. That was the whole new guild. Four names, one hearth that remembered fire, a half-collapsed roof, and a founding document that began, unflinchingly, with the word *We stole*.

* * *

Here is what I wrote. I'll set it down here exactly, because if this chronicle ever falls into other hands, I want the first entry preserved word for word, in my own poor hand, mistakes included:

*In the year since the reforging of the covenant, let the record show:*

*The Meridian Guild was founded on theft. Its founders stole a dragon egg and called the ransom a covenant. Every trade, every tithe, every impossible bargain struck in these halls for one hundred years was payment on a crime, dressed in commerce so that decent people could sleep.*

*We are done dressing it.*

*The egg has been returned. The fire burns steady in both realms. The debt of the founders cannot be repaid — debts like that don't close, they only get carried — so we will carry it openly, in these pages, forever, where anyone may read it.*

*This guild reopens today as keepers of the record. We will trade in truth: verified histories, witnessed accounts, honest testimony between realm and realm. Our fees will be fair. Our columns will have no shadows in them. And when we are wrong, the record will say so.*

*Signed, Jude Hartwell, guildmaster, former ledger-keeper, former liar by omission, current sweeper of floors.*

Maren read it and said, "Strike 'current sweeper of floors.'"

"No."

"It undermines the dignity of the office."

"So does the hole in the roof. Dignity will have to queue."

* * *

Hale came on the thirtieth day.

I saw him from the window before anyone else did — Inquisitor Hale, former guild child, the boy the old guild had abandoned to the Crown's charity and who had grown into the Crown's sharpest knife. He stood across the square in plain clothes, no badge, no escort, hands folded behind his back like a man visiting a grave he wasn't sure he had the right to mourn at.

The others tensed when I pointed him out. Maren's hand went to her sword out of pure reflex. Ryn's ember-lines brightened along their forearms, a slow tide of gold.

"He's alone," I said. "Let him be alone."

I went down and opened the door myself.

For a long moment neither of us spoke. He looked older than he had at the Hearth — the pardon he'd offered me, and the refusal I'd given him, and everything after, seemed to have settled onto him like snow that wouldn't melt. The Inquisition had been formally dissolved by a Crown suddenly very interested in appearing reasonable; Hale had refused the pension, refused the transfer, refused everything except, apparently, the habit of standing watch outside buildings he wasn't welcome in.

"The records are public now," he said finally. "I read them. All of them." A pause. "Including mine."

"Then you know we got it right."

"I know you got it *exact*," he said, and there was something raw under the words. "That's the worst part. I spent ten years telling myself the guild abandoned me because I was expendable, because children are always expendable, because that's simply how institutions are. And your ledger says: yes. Exactly that. Page after page. No excuse. No version where someone meant well." His jaw worked. "Do you understand what it does to a person, to be told the truth about the worst thing that ever happened to them?"

I thought of Osk, reading his own forged history. I thought of Ryn at the well in the dark. I thought of myself, one year ago, watching Aldous Vane burn a page with my name on it and calling the ash *routine*.

"Yes," I said. "It frees them. Eventually. Usually after it breaks them a little first."

A ghost of a smile crossed his face, there and gone. "The entry about me. The one that says the guild failed you — that's what it says, your own words, 'the guild failed him, and we record our failure'—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Nobody's ever written that down for me before. Not once. Not even as an apology. Just... facts. On a page. Where anyone can see."

"Facts are sturdier than apologies. They hold weight."

He nodded slowly. Then, before I could invite him in — and I was going to; I'd decided it somewhere around *page after page* — he stepped back.

"Not yet. I don't... I need to learn how to be a person who reads records instead of burning them. Give me time." He turned, then paused. "The northern provinces are saying the fire under the world sings now. Have you heard? Farmers, shepherds. They say on quiet nights you can hear it through the stones, and it sounds glad."

"We've heard," I said. "We're fairly sure it's thanking us. Or laughing at us. With dragons it's difficult to tell."

That earned a real laugh, short and startled out of him, and then he walked away across the square, unhurried, a man with nowhere official to be for the first time in his life, and I stood in the doorway and watched him go and did not write his name in the guild book.

Yet.

* * *

That evening we closed the shutters — ordinary ones, wooden, bought from a carpenter who charged us a fair price and gave us a discount anyway because, as he put it, "my grandmother always said you lot were thieves, and I like being wrong" — and shared a meal at the master's table, which was just the long counting table with a cloth thrown over it. Stew. Bread. Maren ate three servings and denied eating two of them. Osk told a story about his own childhood that was either entirely true or entirely invented, and for the first time in forty years, even he couldn't tell which, and he laughed until he cried, and Ryn put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

Later, when the others had gone up to the rooms we'd cleared for them — theirs now, permanently, though none of them had said so aloud and none of them needed to — I sat alone at the master's desk with the new ledger open to its second page.

The first entry was finished. Honest. Heavy. Done.

I dipped my pen and wrote:

*Day thirty. The fire sings in the north. The roof still leaks. Maren swept the eastern stacks unprompted, which proves guildmasters do sweep, whatever she says. Osk laughed today like a man with nothing left to forge. Ryn stayed at the table through the whole meal.*

*Hale came. He is not ready. Neither was I, once. The door stays unlocked.*

*Balance carried forward: four members, one debt we can never repay and will never stop carrying, and the truth, entire.*

*I find the books balance better than they ever have.*

I blotted the page, closed the ledger, and sat for a while in the warm dark, listening to the hearth hum its low, glad note — and to footsteps on the stair behind me, light and deliberate, someone coming back down who had forgotten to say goodnight, or perhaps had come down to say it after all.

I did not turn around. Some entries, you let arrive in their own time.
