## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The dead woman's name was Maren Voss, and she had died holding a grudge the size of a cathedral.

Elodie knew this because she was holding Maren's knucklebone, and the knucklebone was talking.

*Tell my sister she was right about the house. Tell her I'm sorry about the rest of it. And tell her the silver isn't buried under the hearth like everyone thinks—*

"Under the fig tree," Elodie murmured, chisel poised. "I know. You've said it eleven times."

*Twelve,* said the bone, with the particular satisfaction of the recently dead, who had finally found an audience that couldn't walk away. *And tell her I kept the blue shawl. She never asked for it back, but she wanted it. I could tell.*

Elodie set the chisel against the bone and began to carve. This was the work, and the work was good: taking what the dead could no longer say and putting it somewhere the living could read. Bone held a voice better than paper. Paper rotted. Bone remembered. She carved in her small tight script, the one she'd invented at fourteen when she realized no one else could read it—*the sister was right, the silver is under the fig tree, I kept the blue shawl and I am sorry*—and as she carved, the voice in the bone grew quieter, the way a kettle goes quiet when you take it off the fire. By the time she finished the last stroke, the knucklebone was just a knucklebone. Smooth. Warm from her hand. Silent.

She set it down among the others on her workbench: a rib that had carried a farmer's apology to his estranged son, a finger bone holding a recipe for plum wine that its owner had guarded jealously for sixty years and finally, in death, decided to share, a small carved button that contained nothing at all except the memory of a song, hummed by a girl who had died too young to leave anything else behind. Elodie kept that one on the corner of the bench where she could see it. She didn't know the girl. She'd never known the girl. The button had come in with a lot sale from a house clear-out in Corvane, and when Elodie had picked it up, the song had risen out of it like steam, thin and sweet and unbearably lonely, and she had carved it into a strip of whalebone so the girl's mother—somewhere, if she was still alive—might someday hear it again.

The song was the reason Elodie worked with the shutters half-closed. Not because the work was illegal, though it was, in the way that breathing was legal but breathing *fire* was not. Because if the neighbors knew that the quiet woman on Candle Lane could hear their dead grandmothers, she would never again have a quiet hour, a quiet thought, or a quiet anything. Grief made people bold. Grief made people desperate. And desperate people talked.

The Ember Guild had survived three hundred years on the principle that nobody talked.

"Elodie." Tam's head appeared around the doorframe, all elbows and cowlick and the particular gravity of a boy who had recently discovered he was hungry. "Quill says the ledger. He says it's been three days and he says—" Tam paused, visibly reconstructing Quill's exact phrasing, "—he says that if I have to come down there again he's going to dock my supper, and he says you should know that was a joke, mostly."

"The ledger," Elodie said. "Right."

She capped her ink, wrapped Maren Voss's knucklebone in linen, and followed Tam down the narrow stairs into the counting room, which was not a room for counting so much as a room for pretending that impossible things could be counted. It was long and low-beamed and smelled of tallow and old paper and the faint mineral tang of the bottled storms that lined the eastern shelves—each jar sealed with wax and wire, each one humming faintly against its neighbors like restless bees. There were forty-one storms in inventory. The guild had sold nine that year. Nobody asked who bought bottled storms, in the same way nobody asked who bought traded memories or a night of borrowed luck or the last words of a dead woman carved into bone. The Ember Guild's clients paid well precisely because they didn't ask, and the guild survived because it didn't tell.

Quill sat at the great table with his spectacles pushed up into his white hair and the day's correspondence fanned out before him like a losing hand. He was a narrow man, Quill, all sharp angles and sharper eyes, with the kind of face that suggested he had been disappointed by the world early and had made his peace with it through bookkeeping. He had founded—no. He had *inherited* the guild from his predecessor, who had inherited it from hers, and so on back through three centuries of quiet, careful, unremarkable people who had made a remarkable living from the remarkable.

"You're late," Quill said, without looking up.

"I'm early," Elodie said. "The appointment was for tomorrow."

"The Voss woman's sister came by at dawn. She stood in the alley and cried at our door for an hour. I gave her tea. She gave me a look that suggested she'd have preferred the silver under the hearth, since that's where her husband is planning to dig tomorrow." Quill finally looked up, and his eyes crinkled. "Fig tree?"

"Fig tree."

"Good. I've always liked fig trees. They're honest. They grow where they're planted and they don't pretend to be anything else." He slid the ledger across the table. "Balance it. We're short."

"We're always short."

"We're short by a *memory* this time. Lot forty. A first kiss, traded by a cooper's apprentice in spring, sold to a widow in the summer. The widow says it never took. She says she paid for a first kiss and what she got was a second kiss, and she knows the difference, and she wants her money back or the real one, and she is *not* a woman one argues with."

Elodie pulled the ledger toward her and opened it. The pages were her favorite thing in the world, after the bones. She had designed them herself: columns for the item, the provenance, the price, the buyer's cipher, and—a column no other guild in the world had, so far as she knew—a column for *weight*. Because impossible things had weight. Not the kind you could put on a scale. The kind you could feel in a room. A bottled storm weighed like an argument. A traded memory weighed like a debt. A dead woman's last words weighed like a held breath.

She ran her finger down Lot Forty's entry. Cooper's apprentice, first kiss, traded to pay a debt of honor, sold to the Widow Asche, summer, fourteen crowns. And there, in the provenance column, in her own handwriting, a note she'd made at the time and now read with dawning horror: *Seller advises the memory is "mostly accurate."*

"Mostly accurate," Elodie said.

"He was nineteen," Quill said. "He wanted the crowns more than the truth."

"Then we owe her either a refund or a first kiss, and I'm not trading mine."

"God forbid. You'd charge too much." Quill rubbed his eyes. "Refund it. Take it out of my share. And write in the ledger that the guild apologizes, in full, with the apology column filled in, because if we're going to run a dishonest trade we're at least going to be honest about the dishonesty. That's the whole difference between us and the crown, Elodie. They steal and write it down as law. We steal and write it down as theft."

It was, Elodie reflected, the closest thing to a moral philosophy the guild had, and it was written in her handwriting, in her ledger, in her columns, and she loved it the way you loved a scar: not because it was beautiful, but because it was yours.

* * *

Sera arrived at noon, the way she arrived everywhere: already there.

She came through the counting room's back door without knocking, because knocking was for people who might be refused, and Sera had never in her life been refused anywhere except by her own better judgment, which she ignored professionally. She was small and dark and quick, with a fence's smile and a smuggler's hands, and she carried a market basket that smelled of bread and conceals.

"Delivery," she announced. "Two loaves, one for the table and one for Tam, who I am told has been eating his feelings again. Also—" she set the basket down and began unpacking it with the ceremony of a priest, "—I have acquired, at considerable personal risk and no small expense, *this*."

She laid a jar on the table. It was about the size of a fist, sealed in grey wax, and inside it something moved—not water, not smoke, but the *idea* of weather. The light in the room bent toward it slightly, the way a conversation bends toward the person who's about to say something unforgivable.

"That's not ours," Quill said.

"It is now."

"Sera."

"It's a *small* storm, Quill. Look at it. It's barely a squall. It wouldn't frighten a shepherd."

"It wouldn't frighten a shepherd because it's *bottled*. The last time one of those got loose in this house, Tam cried for a week and I found hailstones in my boots in *spring*."

"That was a big storm," Sera said. "This is a small one. And it's not for sale. It's for *trade*. There's a man in Corvane who deals in luck, and he's been sitting on a night of borrowed luck for two months because he can't find anyone who wants it, and I know for a fact that the Widow Asche—" Elodie looked up sharply, "—yes, *that* Widow Asche, don't make the face, Elodie, everyone in this city knows that woman—has been asking after a squall to settle a dispute with her neighbor about a fence line. So we trade her the squall, we take the luck, we sell the luck to the Corvane man's rival, and everyone is happy and nobody is honest, which is the natural condition of the world."

Elodie opened the ledger to a fresh page and began to write. *One squall, acquired by Sera, provenance undisclosed, to be traded against one night of borrowed luck, provenance undisclosed.* In the weight column she wrote: *heavier than it looks.*

"That's not a real weight," Sera said, reading over her shoulder.

"Everything's a real weight. That's the whole point of the column."

"You know what your problem is, Elodie Winterbourne? You believe in the ledger more than you believe in the things in the ledger."

"And your problem," Elodie said, not looking up, "is that you believe in the things and not the ledger, which is why you owe the guild eleven crowns from the spring and have paid back three."

"Four," Sera said. "I paid back four."

"You paid back three and a *story*, and the story was about you, and it wasn't even flattering."

Tam, who had been eating his loaf in the corner with the focused intensity of a boy who had learned never to interrupt the adults when they were doing accounting, snorted so hard he choked on the crust. Sera cuffed him gently on the ear and stole a piece of his bread as tax, and Quill sighed the sigh of a man who had been sighing at the same three people for years and would go on sighing at them until the ledger closed on all of them, and the counting room settled into its midday rhythm: Quill drafting the apology to the Widow Asche with the gravity of a treaty, Sera inventorying the storm shelf and arguing with the jars, Tam doing his letters at the small desk by the window, his tongue between his teeth.

Elodie balanced the accounts.

This was the hour she loved most, though she'd have bitten anyone who said so. The guild was quiet. The light came slantwise through the shutters and lay in gold bars across the table. The ledger opened under her hands like a living thing, and she moved through it column by column, line by line, and for a little while the world made sense: every impossible thing in its place, every debt and credit balanced, every weight recorded. Somewhere in the walls the house creaked and settled. Somewhere in the shelves the storms hummed. Somewhere in the city above them, the living went about their living, and the dead went about their silence, and Elodie Winterbourne sat in the middle of it with her pen moving, keeping the accounts of a world that didn't know it owed her anything.

* * *

It was Tam who broke the quiet, because it was always Tam.

He had finished his letters—he'd been practicing the word *provenance*, which he spelled *provenants*, which was honestly better—and he'd come to stand at her elbow the way he did when he wanted something and hadn't yet figured out how to ask. Elodie felt him there the way she felt weather coming. She kept writing.

"You heard her," Tam said finally. "The dead lady. This morning. You heard her the whole time she was talking."

"I did."

"What's it like?"

Elodie set down her pen. This was the question, the one he circled every few weeks and never landed on, and she'd never figured out whether he wanted the answer or just wanted to stand near someone who had one. Tam had a gift too—small, quiet, the kind you could hide in a pocket. He could find lost things. Keys, buttons, a ring Sera had lost down a drain two years ago. He'd never lost a person. He'd never lost anything that mattered enough to teach him what the gift cost.

"It's like," Elodie said, and stopped, because the true answer was *it's like being the only person in a city who can hear the rain, and the rain never stops, and half of it is grief*, and that was not an answer you gave a twelve-year-old over bread. "It's like being a door," she said instead. "Everyone else gets to be a wall. Walls get to rest."

"Does it ever stop?"

"No."

"Not even at night?"

"Especially at night. The dead keep evening hours. They're very traditional."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, chewing on the inside of his cheek. "Do they ever say anything *good*? Not sorry-things and where's-the-silver things. Good things."

Elodie thought of the button on her workbench, and the girl's song, and the way the song had filled the whole room for a moment with something that wasn't grief at all, that was just *gladness*, a gladness so old and small it had outlived everything around it. She thought of Maren Voss, who had died holding a grudge and had let it go the moment someone carved it down, and whose last words, in the end, had been *I kept the blue shawl*—which was not an apology or a confession but a gift, a thing given across the gap between living and dead because it was too lovely to keep.

"Sometimes," Elodie said. "More than you'd think. The sorry-things are just loud. The good things are quiet. You have to lean in."

Tam nodded slowly, filing this away in whatever place he kept the things that mattered, and Elodie turned back to the ledger, and the afternoon went on, and it was warm, and it was ordinary, and she was surrounded by people who knew what she was and had never once made her feel like a door instead of a wall.

She was lonely anyway. She'd stopped trying to explain that to herself years ago—the way you could be held by a family and still be the only one of your kind in the room, the way the ledger could balance to the copper and still leave a column empty, the way a gift that let you hear every last word ever left unsaid could not, apparently, let you hear the one voice you actually wanted. Her mother's voice. Eleven years gone, and Elodie had carved a hundred messages for a hundred strangers' dead, and had never once, not in a hundred nights of leaning in, heard her.

The light was going amber when Quill came down the stairs two at a time, which Quill never did, and he was holding a letter in both hands the way you held something that might be a snake, and his face had the look of a man who has just read his own name in a place where names go to die.

"Elodie," he said. "We have a client."

"It's nearly supper."

"This one keeps royal hours." He set the letter on the table between them, and Elodie read the seal on it twice, because the first time she was sure she'd imagined it. It was pressed in black wax, and it was a king's seal, and the letter was a request—courteous, brief, and signed in a hand that trembled—for an audience regarding *a matter of inheritance*.

"Whose inheritance?" Sera asked from the storm shelf, very quietly.

Quill looked at Elodie, and Elodie looked at the seal, and somewhere in the back of her skull, faint as a song from a button, she heard—though the letter said nothing of the kind, though it was only wax and paper—the first thin edge of a voice she didn't yet know, waiting.

"A king's," Quill said.
