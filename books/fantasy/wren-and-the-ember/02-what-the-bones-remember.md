## 2. What the Bones Remember

The client came at dusk, which Elodie would later understand was deliberate. Dusk was when the Ember Guild's shutters closed and its lamps went amber, when the shop stopped being a place of business and became a place of family—and a stranger arriving then was a stranger who wanted to be remembered as an interruption.

She was old, wrapped in grey wool gone silver with road dust, and she carried her parcel against her chest the way Tam had once carried a wounded sparrow. Quill admitted her himself, which he almost never did; usually Sera vetted them through the grate and turned away anyone whose grief smelled like trouble.

"Name?" Sera asked, not unkindly.

"Maren," the woman said. "Of Harrow Fen."

Sera's pen paused over the ledger. There was no Harrow Fen anymore—everyone knew that, the way everyone knew the old king was dead and the new one had simply *always been*, more or less. Some places drowned in the wars. Some places were made to drown. You didn't ask which.

"I've something for the carver," Maren said. Her eyes found Elodie in the lamplight, in the back room doorway, bone dust still on her apron. "Not a message to send. A message already kept."

Quill's chair creaked. "We don't buy curiosities."

"It isn't a curiosity." The woman unwrapped the parcel on the counter with hands that shook only a little, and there it lay: a fingerbone, yellowed, heavy-looking for its size, with a ring of tarnished gold fused around the second knuckle like a scar.

Elodie felt it before she touched it.

Her gift had always been quiet things. A mother's last word in her son's rib, worn soft by years of being held. A soldier's joke in a tooth, repeated so often it had gone smooth. The dead spoke the way water speaks—by wearing grooves into what they'd touched most. She'd spent eleven years listening to the dead, and the dead were all the same in one essential way: they were finished. Their voices had a bottom to them, a floor. Grief, love, spite—it all settled eventually, like silt.

This voice had no floor.

The moment she stepped close enough, it rose through the floorboards, through the ledger shelf, through her own teeth: *I am here. I am here. I am HERE.* Not the faded echo of a man long buried. A voice pressed flat under something enormous, still struggling, still burning, and the pressure of it made Elodie's vision swim so that the lamp flames doubled.

"Carver," Quill said sharply, and she realized she'd gripped the counter.

"It's alive," she said, before she could stop herself. "The voice. It isn't dead."

The room went very still. Tam, who had been pretending to sweep near the storm bottles, stopped mid-stroke. Sera set down her pen with the deliberate care of a woman deciding where to put a knife.

"That's not possible," Sera said.

"I know what dead sounds like." Elodie's mouth was dry. "I've heard four hundred of them. Dead is—" she searched for it— "dead is *finished*. This is a man screaming into a pillow. Except the pillow has been screaming for—" she looked at the bone, at the gold fused around it, and the knowledge arrived whole, the way her gift's knowledge always did, unwelcome and certain— "for twenty-two years."

Twenty-two years. Since before Tam was born. Since the year the old king died, officially, of a fever, and the young queen regent had been sent to a convent, and a new crown had appeared on the throne that everyone agreed had been there forever.

Nobody said any of this out loud. Nobody needed to.

*Please,* said the voice in the bone, and now that Elodie had opened the door to it even a crack, it poured through like floodwater through a cracked sill. *Please. Whoever hears me. Twenty-two years I have watched him wear my face. Twenty-two years I have felt my gifts go out of me one by one, and each one he takes makes him stronger and makes me less. I do not know how much of me is left. Please.*

Elodie put both hands flat on the counter to keep the world steady.

"How did you come by this?" Quill asked Maren, and his voice was the merchant's voice, mild and pleasant, and Elodie had known him six years and had never until that moment understood that the mildness was armor.

"My husband was a warden," Maren said. "Before Harrow Fen drowned. He guarded a thing he wasn't told the name of. When the levy came for the fen, he took what he was guarding and swam, and got sick from the swimming, and died slow. He told me: take it to the Ember Guild. He said the guild remembers what the crown forgets." Her chin lifted. "He also said you'd try to refuse me. So let me say plainly: I'm not selling it. I'm asking you to listen. That's all. Listen, and tell me if what he told me about it is true."

"And what did he tell you?"

Maren looked directly at Elodie. "That the king isn't dead. That something else is king instead, and it's been eating the gifted for a generation, and that someday someone with ears good enough would hear him in this bone." She folded her hands. "So. Carver. Is it true?"

Every eye in the room was on Elodie, but the only voice she could hear was the one pouring out of the fingerbone, and it had stopped begging now. It was saying something else, quieter, in the tone of a man who has had twenty-two years to choose his words:

*I know your name, Elodie Winterbourne. I have known it since before you were born. Your gift did not come to you by accident. Nothing in your line ever came by accident.*

The lamps guttered, though there was no draft.

"Yes," Elodie heard herself say. "It's true."

* * *

They didn't decide anything that night, because Quill forbade decisions after dusk—a guild rule older than any of them—but nobody slept either, and by the third bell they had all migrated to the kitchen, which was what the Ember Guild actually used for a heart.

Tam sat cross-legged on the table itself, which Sera permitted because arguing about it took more energy than ignoring it. He had the look he got when adults were frightened and trying not to show it: too-wide eyes, thumbs hooked in his belt, absolutely silent.

"So we refuse it," Sera said. She stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the street. "Client takes her bone, client goes home. We never opened the parcel. That's the ledger entry: *no transaction.*"

"You can't unhear a thing," Elodie said.

"You can sure as hells pretend to."

"For how long? Sera, it knows my name. It knew my *mother's* craft. Whatever's in that bone has been waiting for—" she gestured helplessly at herself— "*me*. Specifically. Do you think refusing stops it?"

"I think," Sera said, turning from the window, "that everything the crown has done for two years—the registries, the informants, the new assay offices that weigh 'unusual goods'—has been a net closing slowly, and the one thing guaranteed to spring it is the guild taking custody of a piece of the royal dead. Royal bones aren't grief, Elodie. They're *bait*. They have always been bait."

"She's right," Quill said quietly, and the kitchen went still, because Quill rarely agreed with Sera in front of them, and never that quickly.

He sat heavily at the head of the table, and Elodie saw, really saw, how old he'd gotten while she wasn't looking—the liver-spotted hands, the tremor he hid in his left one by keeping it in his pocket.

"When I was younger than Tam," Quill said, "my master told me three rules. Never deal in living voices. Never hold what the crown wants found. And never, ever touch the regalia." He rubbed his face. "I asked him, the way apprentices ask, *what regalia?* And he hit me. Only time he ever struck me. And he said: *the regalia that isn't missing.*"

"The crown," Sera said slowly. "The scepter. The—" 

"The ring," Tam said.

Everyone looked at him.

"The ring on the bone," Tam said. "I noticed it earlier. It's not jewelry. I've held it—I helped carry the parcel. Things that are lost pull at me a little, you all know that, it's why you keep me around." He swallowed. "That ring doesn't pull like lost. It pulls like *hidden*. Like it wants to be found by the right person and eaten by the wrong one. I've only felt that twice. The other time was the seal on the letter that started all this."

The silence that followed had texture. Elodie could feel it pressing on her eardrums alongside the distant, muffled roar of the bound king, who had not stopped speaking since dusk—not words she could parse anymore, just the ocean-sound of a man insisting on existing.

"We should burn it," Sera said at last. "Fire kills voices. Everyone knows that. We burn the bone, the voice goes out, whatever's hunting us loses its hook."

"Burn it," Elodie repeated.

"Yes."

"And the king stays bound. And the thing wearing his crown keeps harvesting people like me for another twenty years, another fifty, and every single one of them ends up in a ledger of mine or a grave, and we sit here warm and safe and *complicit*." She heard her own voice rise and couldn't stop it. "You want to burn the only witness in the world who can testify to what the throne actually is."

"I want us alive," Sera snapped. "I have buried friends, Elodie. I have burned their names out of ledgers with my own hands so the crown couldn't follow them past death. Warmth and safety are not nothing. They are the *whole prize*. Most people never get them and die stupid about that fact."

"And I spent eleven years listening to the dead because the living wouldn't tell the truth," Elodie said. "Every client, every message, every last word carved into rib and femur—they all come to me because somewhere along the way, the living agreed to lie to each other so well that only bones remember honestly." She pulled the ledger toward her across the table, her ledger, the Ledger of Impossible Things, with its coded entries and its careful columns. "I am a record-keeper. If the truest record in the kingdom is sitting on our counter asking for help, then my whole trade is a hypocrisy unless I listen to it."

*Then listen,* said the voice, suddenly clear again, close as a mouth against her ear, though the bone was locked in the strongbox downstairs. *Then listen, daughter of Ishikawa's line, and I will tell you what your gift is for.*

Elodie gasped aloud. Across the table, Tam's head snapped up. "What did it say? What did it say just now? Your face—"

"It knows my grandmother's name."

"Your grandmother," Quill said, very carefully, "was a bone-carver in Vail Hollow."

"My grandmother," Elodie said, "was a founder of something. That's what it called her. *Founder's line.* Quill—" and she turned on him, and the dread in the room changed shape, curdled into something worse— "you knew. Look at your face. You've known since the letter."

Quill didn't answer. He looked, for a long moment, like a man standing at a door he'd nailed shut decades ago and hearing knocking on the other side.

"Royal bones are bait," he finally said, to the table rather than to her. "That's the rule. That's what my master died knowing, and what his master before him died knowing, and why the guild has survived sixty years under three different crowns by staying small and staying gray and staying *quiet*. Because somewhere under all of it, there is a debt this house owes, and the interest has been compounding since before I was born." He lifted his head and met Elodie's eyes, and she saw fear in him—not for himself. For her. "You asked me once why I took you in. An orphan carver with a dangerous gift, into a hidden house. You asked me and I told you it was because you were talented."

"I remember."

"I lied," Quill said. "And tomorrow I will tell you the truth, and you will wish I hadn't. But tonight—tonight we do nothing. We sleep on it. Those are the rules." He pushed himself up from the table, and at the doorway he paused, his back to all of them. "For what it's worth, Elodie. The voice in that bone has been calling this house for twenty-two years. Tonight is just the first night anyone could hear it."

He left. Sera followed, but at the stair she stopped and said, without turning around: "If we do this—if we're fools enough to do this—I need you to know I'll fight beside you anyway. Someone has to keep the ledgers honest. Even the impossible ones." Then she climbed the stairs, and her door closed with terrible gentleness.

Tam stayed. He slid off the table and came to stand beside Elodie's chair, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the banked fire ticked, and far below them in the strongbox the king went on insisting that he existed.

"Elodie," Tam said finally. "When I lose things—when things are lost and I find them—it feels like being *chosen* by them. Like they picked me out of everybody." His voice dropped. "Is that what this is like? Being chosen?"

Elodie thought about lying to him. She was getting good at lying lately; it frightened her how good.

"Yes," she said. "Exactly like that."

"Does it ever stop feeling like that?"

She reached over and ruffled his hair, the way she'd done since he was nine. "No," she said. "But you get to choose back. That's the part nobody tells you."

Upstairs, the guild settled into its uneasy sleep. Downstairs, behind iron, the fingerbone waited in the dark, and the old king waited inside it, patient as only the imprisoned are, and in the space between waking and dreams Elodie heard him speak one last time—not begging now, not commanding, just telling her, the way you tell a friend a hard truth:

*Tomorrow they will offer you a way out. Take it or don't. But understand what you'd be refusing, daughter of founders: you would be refusing to know what you are. And that, of all the bindings in this kingdom, is the only one I cannot break for you.*
