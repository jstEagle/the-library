## 3. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, as criers always did, because misery in this city kept respectable hours.

Sera heard the first bell from the guild's kitchen, where she was doing what she did every morning: counting. Coins in the false bottom of the flour tin. Debts owed to them, debts they owed to others, favors banked with three harbor captains and a widow who ran the tannery and never asked why Sera paid her in secrets instead of silver. She had the whole ledger of the city's hidden kindnesses in her head, and it was a good morning until the bell changed its rhythm.

Three strikes, pause. Three strikes, pause.

That was not a market bell. That was not anything.

She set down the coin and went to the window, and below her, in the grey light, the street was already filling. People came out of doorways with their shirts half-buttoned, their children trailing, drawn the way people are always drawn to official noise—the same instinct that makes crowds gather at executions. A crier stood on the fountain steps in crown livery, red and black, flanked by two soldiers whose spears caught the early light like needles.

"By decree of the Crown," the crier called, and his voice was too loud for so small a square, "a general accounting shall be made of all persons possessed of unnatural gifts."

Sera's hands went still on the sill.

"Registration offices open at every district hall within seven days. All such persons shall present themselves, declare their gift, and be entered upon the rolls. Registration is mandatory. Registration is protection." He paused for breath, and the square held its breath with him. "Those who fail to register within thirty days shall be remanded."

Remanded. The word dropped into the crowd and nobody picked it up. It just lay there between the cobblestones.

An old woman near the fountain—Marta, who sold fish and gossiped about everyone including Sera—raised her voice in that way old women have, fearless because they've already outlived everything that could frighten them. "Remanded where?"

"To the care of the Crown," the crier said.

"Cared for how? Cared for *where*?"

"The Crown," said the crier, in a voice that suggested he'd been given one answer for every question, "shall determine all particulars."

A man beside Marta laughed—one short, ugly bark—and then seemed surprised by himself, and looked around to see who else had laughed, and no one had. The soldiers' spears did not move. The crier rolled his scroll with great ceremony and read the whole thing again from the top, because that was the law now: twice at dawn, once at noon, once at dusk, in every square, until every gifted soul in the city was either registered or gone.

Sera counted the crowd without meaning to. Forty-one heads. She knew eleven of them by name and by what they'd bought from the Ember Guild and what they'd paid. The lamplighter who traded a memory of his wedding to forget his wife's face after she left him. The midwife who bought a bottled calm to hold steady through hard births. The cooper's daughter who sold her dreams to pay her father's debts and now slept like the dead, which was its own kind of tragedy Sera had helped broker and never felt clean about.

Eleven people in that square who would hear the word *remanded* and know, the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop falls, that it meant *taken*.

And forty-one people total, which meant thirty who didn't know yet but would learn, the way cities always learned—by watching neighbors disappear and asking careful questions and getting no answers.

*Registration is protection.*

Sera had been a fence for nineteen years. She knew what protective language sounded like when it was being sharpened into something else. She'd used it herself, God help her—*this is for your safety*, she'd told clients signing over memories they couldn't afford to lose, and she'd watched them sign anyway because fear makes everyone agreeable.

She closed the shutter. Her hands were steady, which was its own small horror. Nineteen years of trading in other people's disasters had taught her body to go calm while everything behind it screamed, and right now everything behind it was screaming.

* * *

Quill was already at the long table when she came downstairs, and Elodie was already carving, which meant both of them had heard the bells and both of them had decided, in their separate ways, that work was the only sane response. Tam was not there yet, which was unusual—he was usually up before anyone, hungry as a gull.

"We need to talk," Sera said.

"After this entry," Quill said. His pen scratched. "The Alderman's payment cleared."

"*After this entry?*"

"Sera." He didn't look up. That was Quill all over: the world could end and he'd finish the line first, because a ledger with an unfinished line was a debt unpaid, and debts unpaid were the one sin the Ember Guild recognized. "I heard the criers. I heard them twice. Finishing the entry doesn't change what they said."

"It changes how much time we have to decide things!"

He did look up then, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down the pen. Across the table, Elodie had stopped carving too. The bone knife rested against the rib-slat they were working—a commission from a dying ferryman who wanted his apology to his brother carved somewhere it would last—and Elodie's hands hovered over it the way hands hover when they've forgotten what they were doing.

"They listed the gifts," Elodie said quietly.

Sera turned. "What?"

"The proclamation. You were listening to the words. I was listening to the list." Elodie's voice was flat, reciting. "*Bone-speaking, weather-working, memory-trading, dream-selling, finding, mending, unmaking.* Seven gifts named. Seven." They swallowed. "That's us. That's the whole guild, itemized. Someone wrote that list, Sera. Someone sat down with a pen and wrote down exactly what we do."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the house creaked the way old houses do, settling into the morning, unaware it was sheltering criminals now.

"That's not possible," Sera said, and hated how thin it came out. "We're careful. We've been careful for—"

"For how long?" Quill's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. Gentle Quill meant he'd already thought past your objection and was waiting for you to catch up. "How long have we been telling ourselves we're invisible, Sera? Because I've been running this house for twenty-six years, and I'll tell you my honest count. We were never invisible. We were *unnoticed*. There's a difference, and the difference has a price, and I think the bill just arrived."

Elodie set down the bone knife very carefully, aligning it with the edge of the table, and Sera recognized the gesture—it was what Elodie did instead of wringing their hands. Order as armor. "The ferryman's commission," Elodie said. "It's evidence. If they come here—"

"No one's coming today," Quill said.

"You don't know that."

"I don't. But I know this: panic burns more than fire does, and we are not burning anything until we've decided what needs deciding." He folded his hands. "First question. How many names are in our ledgers?"

Sera closed her eyes and let the numbers surface. "Active clients, four hundred and six. Historical entries going back to the founding—two thousand, if you count the coded ones. Of the active, maybe sixty are gifted themselves rather than buyers. The rest just... purchased. Memories, storms, calms, luck."

"And of the sixty?"

"At least twenty live within the walls of this city." She opened her eyes. "The rest are scattered along the trade roads. Fishers up the coast. A family of finders in the hill towns. Two bone-speakers I know of besides Elodie, though they're far north and careful."

"Careful," Quill repeated, tasting the word. "Right. Second question, then, and think before you answer. If the registration rolls get filled—if sixty gifted folk walk into those offices and declare themselves—what happens to the guild?"

Sera didn't answer, because the answer was obvious and horrible. Sixty declarations meant sixty interrogations, and sixty interrogations meant the guild's name surfacing like a drowned body, eventually, inevitably. Every client they'd ever served was a thread, and someone with patience and a rack could pull threads all day.

"So we burn the ledgers," Elodie said.

"No," said Quill and Sera together.

Quill raised an eyebrow at her. She shrugged. "You first."

"Those ledgers are promises," Quill said. "Every entry is a promise kept or a promise outstanding. We burn them, and the ferryman's brother never learns why his brother died cold to him for ten years. The cooper's daughter's family never learns where her dreams went or how to buy them back. We don't get to erase other people's histories because our own got dangerous."

"Then we *move* them—"

"Which brings me," Quill said, "to the third question. Where does Tam stand in all this?"

And there it was, the question under the questions, the one they'd all been circling since the bells started.

Because Tam was fourteen, and Tam had a gift, a small one, a harmless-seeming one—he found things. Lost keys, lost rings, lost letters, lost people, though none of them had ever said that last part out loud. He'd been found himself, four years ago, half-starved in the winter market, and he'd never known his parents, and he'd grown up in this house believing—because they'd all worked very hard to help him believe it—that a boy who found lost buttons was not the sort of person proclamations were written about.

But the proclamation had said *finding*. Itemized. Written down by someone with a pen and a purpose.

"I'll register him," Elodie said suddenly.

Sera stared. "What?"

"If it comes to that. If hiding fails. I'll take him in myself and say his gift is mine—that I found things through him, that he's just a servant. Registration is per person, isn't it? One declaration covers—" They stopped, hearing themselves. "No. No, that's stupid, they'd see through it, they'd—"

"It's not stupid," Sera said slowly. "It's exactly the kind of lie that works, actually, which is what worries me. But it puts you on the rolls, Elodie. And the rolls aren't a list. They're a net. You don't put your name in a net unless you plan to stay out of the water, and we can't leave. This house, the stock, the clients—"

"The clients will scatter on their own," Quill said. "By tonight, half the city's gifted will be on the salt roads heading anywhere else. That's what proclamations are *for*, Sera. Not registration. Flight. The crown wants us gone or gathered, and either way it wants us counted." He rubbed his eyes, and for a moment he looked every year of his age, an old man in a young man's habit of authority. "Burn nothing yet. Move nothing yet. We decide at dusk, together, all four of us. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Sera said, because there was nothing else to say, and because dusk was twelve hours away and twelve hours was a currency and she intended to spend every minute of it.

* * *

Tam came home at noon, breathless, cheeks bright with cold, holding a paper like it was treasure.

"They gave them out! At the market, free, everyone gets one!" He spread it on the table with the pride of a boy delivering important news. "Look, they printed hundreds, there's a picture of the crown and everything, and it says—" His finger moved along the lines, lips shaping the harder words. "It says all persons of unnatural gift must present themselves for registration. For *protection*." He looked up, glowing. "Isn't that good? Someone finally wrote it down proper. Like a rule. Rules mean they can't just do whatever."

The kitchen went very quiet.

Sera watched it land on him—not all at once, but in stages, the way cold water lands. First confusion, at their faces. Then a flicker of doubt. Then the slow, terrible arithmetic of a clever boy re-reading the paper in his hands and seeing, for the first time, the word *finding* sitting in a list of dangerous things.

"But that's not..." He smoothed the paper on the table, pressing out wrinkles that weren't there. "Finding isn't unnatural. It's just—I'm *good at it*. Like Marta's good at fish. Nobody registers Marta."

"No," Sera said gently. "Nobody registers Marta."

"Is it because I take money for it?" His voice climbed. "Because I can stop taking money, I don't care about the coins, Quill keeps them anyway mostly, I just like—the looking. I like knowing where things went. Is it because I'm a child? Do they only want adults? Because I'm nearly fifteen, I'm practically—"

"Tam." Elodie's voice, low and even. "Come sit down."

"I don't want to sit down, I want someone to tell me the rule so I can follow the rule, because if there's a rule and I follow it then I'm safe, isn't that how it works?" He was gripping the paper now, crumpling it, and his eyes had gone glassy in the way of boys who refuse to cry on principle. "If I register, they protect me. It says so. It says it right *here*—"

Sera took the paper from his hands. He let her. And she looked at him—at this boy they'd pulled out of a winter market four years ago, this boy who still left his shoes by the door pointing outward because he'd once been a child nobody came home to—and she understood that the proclamation had done its worst damage already, right here at their own table, in the space between a boy and his belief that rules kept you safe.

"Sit down, love," she said. "Let me tell you what that paper actually says."

She told him. Not all of it—she was not so cruel as to give a fourteen-year-old the whole weight of it—but enough. Enough that he sat, very still, with his hands flat on the table like a child in a boat keeping it balanced. Enough that when she finished, he asked the question she'd been dreading, in a voice gone small and careful:

"When they remand people. Where do they take them?"

"No one knows," Elodie said.

"Has *anyone* come back? From registering?"

Silence again. Sera realized she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to hurt her palms.

"Tam," Quill said, from the doorway where he'd been standing long enough to hear everything, "no one has registered yet. The offices open in seven days. Whatever comes next, it comes to all of us together, and no one in this house goes anywhere alone. Do you understand me?"

Tam nodded. Then, in a smaller voice: "My gift's small, though. Right? It's small. It's not like Elodie's. Nobody would really want it."

And Elodie—who heard the dead, who carried other people's last words around inside them like stones in a riverbed—looked at the boy and lied to him with their whole face, warm and certain, and said: "Nobody wants anybody's gift, Tam. They want to know who has them. That's all this is. A census. Censuses end."

Sera met Elodie's eyes across the table and saw that they both knew it wasn't true, and that they'd both decided, in the same silent instant, that some lies were load-bearing and this was one of them, and they would carry it together for as long as carrying was possible.

* * *

At dusk, while Quill drew up lists and Elodie sat motionless over unfinished carvings, Sera built a small fire in the washhouse grate and fed it pages.

Not the main ledgers—Quill had won that argument, or postponed it. Just the index. The master key she'd kept in her own head for nineteen years, finally committed to paper during the lean years when she'd feared her memory might go the way of her mother's. Client names matched to codes. Codes matched to gifts. Four hundred and six active souls, reduced to ash in under an hour, and she burned them one page at a time, reading each name aloud as it went, because someone should.

"...Vess, the lamplighter. Odile, the midwife. Bren and his daughter, the cooper's..."

The fire ate them all equally, the guilty purchases and the innocent ones, the desperate and the merely curious. She watched the light dance on the wet stone wall and thought: *we were never invisible. We were unnoticed, and unnoticed was a story we sold ourselves at a discount.*

Behind her, the floorboard creaked.

Tam stood in the doorway in his nightshirt, barefoot, holding his shoes. His eyes went to the fire, to the curling pages, to her face.

"You're burning names," he said.

"I am."

"Whose?"

"People who trusted us."

He considered this with the terrible directness of children. Then he stepped forward and sat down on the cold flagstones beside her, shoes still in his lap, and held out his hand toward the fire the way you warm yourself at any ordinary flame.

"Read me the next one," he said. "Before it burns. So somebody remembers them besides you."

So she did. And the fire burned low, and outside, far off across the rooftops, a third bell began to ring—the dusk criers, calling it again, twice at dawn, once at noon, once at night, the civic weather rolling in over the city like a front that would not break for years.

*Registration is protection,* the bell seemed to say.

In the washhouse, Sera fed the last page to the flames and thought about salt roads, and harbors, and which of her banked favors would buy passage south for four people and a crate of bones—and whether any of it would matter, because the offices opened in seven days, and she had already begun, deep in the place where she kept her worst knowledge, to wonder who would walk through the guild's door before then wearing gloves the color of court business.

Someone would come. There was always a buyer, before the hunt. There had always been a buyer.

She just didn't know yet that he'd arrive tomorrow, with grey gloves and gold, smiling.
