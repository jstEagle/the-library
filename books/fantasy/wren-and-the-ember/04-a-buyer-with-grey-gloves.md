## 4. A Buyer with Grey Gloves

The bell above the guild door rang wrong.

I'd been keeping the Ember Guild's accounts for six years, and I knew every sound the shop made the way a sailor knows creaks in his own hull—the groan of the third stair when Tam took them two at a time, the tick of Quill's abacus through the floorboards like rain on tin, the bell's bright double-note when Sera shouldered in with her coat full of other people's secrets. This was not that. This was one clean note, unhurried, struck by a hand that had never once needed to hurry.

I looked up from the ledger and saw grey gloves resting on the counter.

They were good gloves. That was my first thought—carver's instinct, reading material before anything else. Kidskin dyed the color of river fog, stitched fine enough that the seams were nearly invisible, and not a scuff on them despite the mud outside. Whoever wore them had come to us without touching a single dirty thing between the palace district and our door. Either luck or servants. Both cost money.

The man inside the gloves was unremarkable by design. Medium height, medium build, a face like a door someone had forgotten to paint. His clothes were expensive in the way that court clothes are expensive—quietly, expensively, so that only the right people would notice. He smiled at me the way a fisherman smiles at water.

"Good morning," he said. "I'm told you deal in unusual goods."

"We deal in bone," I said. "Carvings, keepsakes, reliquary work. My master does restoration for two temples." All true. All beside the point, and I watched him hear it as beside the point.

"And memories," he said pleasantly. "Storms in bottles, I've heard. A woman's last breath kept warm in wax." He turned his head slowly, cataloguing the shelves the way Quill taught Tam to inventory stock—one pass, nothing missed. "The crown has developed an interest in unusual goods."

"The crown buys from us?" I asked. "We're honored."

His smile didn't move but something behind it did, a small recalibration, like a merchant finding the scale tipped. "Not yet. But the crown is broadening its acquisitions." From somewhere inside his coat he produced a purse and set it on the counter. It didn't clink when it landed. Gold doesn't clink when there's enough of it; it just sits there being heavy. "Names first. Then terms."

"I'm afraid I don't follow."

"No," he agreed. "You do. You followed perfectly. But we'll play it as if you didn't, since it costs nothing." He peeled one glove off, finger by finger, and laid his bare hand flat on the counter between us. An agent's courtesy, or a threat dressed as one: *look how little you frighten me.* "Recent proclamations have made certain trades complicated. Registration, remandment—all very tedious. The court prefers to work quietly where quiet work will serve. So. The crown wishes to acquire bones that speak."

The word went through me like a needle finding a seam.

*Speak.* Not *relics.* Not *curiosities.* Speak.

I had spent six years learning to keep my face the way Quill kept his ledgers: balanced, legible to no one, every entry in its place. Six years of hearing dead women ask their daughters to forgive them and carving the words into rib-bone with steady hands. Six years of listening to the old king's whisper rise out of that fingerbone in its locked box downstairs—*help me, carver, help me*—and smiling at customers while it happened. My face was a craft. I had believed it finished work.

The grey-gloved man looked at me the way I look at bone before I cut.

"There's a story," he said, conversational now, settling in, "about a carver in the capital, thirty years back. Before your time. She could hold a skull and tell you the name of the person it used to be. The court found her useful for a while. Inheritance disputes, mostly. Then someone decided she was more useful registered than free, and then someone decided she was more useful *gone*, and her workshop burned with her in it. Officially, an accident." He examined his bare hand, flexing the fingers. "Unofficially, the court learned something important. Gifts like hers don't die with the gifted. They pass down. Bloodlines. Apprenticeships. Sometimes they simply—settle, like silt, into whoever's nearby when the vessel breaks." His eyes came up. "So the crown watches the trade houses. The bone-sellers. The memory-fences. Because if you wanted to find a speaking bone, or the hands that make them speak, this is the sort of shop you'd visit."

"And what would the crown pay," I said, "for such a thing?"

There it was. I heard myself say it and felt the strangest sensation—not fear, though fear was there, cold under everything—but a kind of horrified curiosity, like watching my own hands move without instruction. He hadn't threatened me. He'd told me a story about a burned woman, and then he'd opened a purse, and some part of me that I had never met before leaned forward and asked about the price.

"That," he said warmly, "is the correct question."

* * *

Quill was upstairs with a client—a widow who wanted her husband's knucklebone carved into the shape of a lark, because he'd whistled to her every morning for forty years—and Tam was supposed to be sorting the delivery crates but was almost certainly asleep among them. Sera was out. Which meant the shop was mine, and the man in grey gloves knew it. Of course he knew it. He'd waited for the bell of Sera's arrival all morning the way a spider waits with its web already spun.

He introduced himself as Master Corven, agent to the Office of Acquisitions, and I understood immediately that Master Corven was a name worn like his gloves—well-made, fitted, removable. He walked the aisles while I stood behind the counter and let him. What else could I do? Refuse a court agent the run of your stock and you might as well hang a sign.

He handled things correctly, I'll give him that. Two fingers, never squeezing, setting each piece back exactly as he'd found it. He paused over the bottled storm Sera had fenced from a sea-witch off the Salt Coast—turned it once in the light, watched the little thunder roll inside the glass—and set it down with the faint disappointment of a collector confirming a forgery. He lingered longer at the shelf of traded memories: stoppered vials of first kisses and funeral days, a soldier's whole summer of umber light, a baker's memory of her mother's hands. He read the labels. He read the prices. He was building a picture of us, and I could see the picture assembling itself behind his forgettable face.

"You have excellent provenance records," he said.

"We're a trade house. Provenance is everything."

"Mm." He lifted a vial labeled *MARGIT'S LAST WALK HOME, AGED NINE* and held it up to the window. "And these are all... voluntary trades?"

"Every one. Fair price, witnessed consent. Guild law."

"Guild law." He said it the way a man tastes an unfamiliar wine. "Fascinating institution, your guild. No charter. No registration. A master who appears in no census. And yet here you are, six years established, paying no duties, answering to no livery company. Almost as if you were designed to be invisible." He set Margit's childhood back on the shelf, precisely aligned. "Invisibility is a service the crown can also provide, Mistress Winterbourne. For valued partners."

My name. I had not given it to him.

I want to be honest in this account, because honesty is the whole discipline of a ledger-keeper's life, even now—even after everything, especially now. So: in that moment, standing behind my own counter with a court agent's attention resting on me like weather, I was not thinking of the dead king in his box downstairs. I was not thinking of Tam asleep in the crate straw, or of Sera walking home through streets where criers had spent three days shouting the Gift-Hunt proclamation. I was thinking about the purse.

That's the confession. The gold sat there between us doing its patient work, and I caught myself calculating. If I gave him nothing—if I gave him *something*, a trifle, a bone that murmured the way old bone sometimes seems to murmur, nothing true—how much would it weigh? Enough to buy Tam an apprenticeship somewhere far away? Enough to move the guild? Enough to buy the kind of invisibility Corven was selling? The hunter's logic is a warm room, and I was cold, and I hated myself for noticing how warm.

Because here is what no one tells you about being hunted: the trap isn't made of iron. It's made of sense. Register and be safe, said the proclamation. Cooperate and be valued, said the gloves. Every rung of the ladder down is reasonable. Every one of them is taken one sensible step at a time. I stood at the top rung and felt the whole staircase below me, solid and well-lit, leading somewhere warm.

"Master Corven," I said. "Let me save us both some theater. We sell carvings and curiosities. Bone that 'speaks' is a fairground trick—a carver with a good ear for a client's grief, telling them what they've paid to hear. If the crown wants fairground tricks, I can source them by the dozen. If it wants the real article—" I shrugged, and the shrug cost me more than any lie ever had. "Then the crown is hunting a ghost, and ghosts are outside my inventory."

He studied me for a long moment. Somewhere upstairs the abacus ticked, oblivious, faithful.

"You lie beautifully," he said at last, and there was something in his voice that wasn't mockery. It sounded almost like respect, and it was the worst thing I'd heard all day. "Did you know that? Most people, when they lie to me, overbuild. Too many details. Too smooth. Yours had exactly one flaw in it, placed where I'd find it, which means either you're a natural—which happens—or someone trained you, which interests me more." He drew the glove back onto his bare hand, finger by finger, unhurried. "A carver who lies like a professional. Where would a bone-carver learn that, I wonder?"

"My mother sold fish," I said. "You learn to lie at market."

Something flickered across his face at *mother*—a note made, filed away—and I understood I'd given him a thread to pull, and hated myself for that too. Everything I said became evidence. Silence became evidence. I was learning the arithmetic of the hunt in real time: whatever you spend, they take.

He left the purse on the counter.

"For anything that comes to you," he said. "Bones that murmur. Clients with strange requests. Apprentices who find lost things." My blood went still at that—Tam, he'd said *apprentices*, had he seen Tam, had someone watched the shop—but his face was painted-smooth again, a door with nothing behind it. "The Office of Acquisitions pays better than grief does, Mistress Winterbourne. Think about it. Everyone thinks about it, eventually." At the door he paused, one gloved hand on the frame, and gave me the fisherman's smile again. "Oh—and the proclamation lists registration offices opening Monday. For traders dealing in anomalous goods. Bone-carvers, specifically named." The bell rang its clean single note as the door shut behind him. "Lovely shop. So easy to find."

* * *

I stood there for I don't know how long, the purse sitting on the counter like a small sleeping animal I couldn't decide whether to feed or drown.

Then I picked it up, carried it to the hearth, and held it over the fire long enough to feel the heat through the leather. I couldn't burn it. Burning a court agent's payment was a message, and messages get delivered both ways. Instead I took it down to the cellar, past the locked case where the king's fingerbone lay in its velvet dark—*help me, carver*—and buried the purse in the flour barrel, because it seemed fitting that gold should sit next to something honest until I decided what it was.

When I came up, Quill was standing at the counter with the widow's lark half-carved in his hands, and he was looking at the spot where the purse had been, and then at me, and his face had gone the color of old ivory.

"He was here," Quill said. Not a question. The abacus upstairs ticked on, faithful and blind, counting a world that had already changed.

"A buyer with grey gloves," I said. "He wanted bones that speak. He left gold." My voice held level, and I noticed that too, noticed myself noticing, the way you watch your own hands work. "Quill—I lied to him. About the trade, about the gift, about all of it. And it was—" I stopped, because the true word was *easy*, and saying it aloud felt like showing a wound in front of a child.

But Quill was already shaking his head, and the look on his face wasn't comfort. It was the look of a man doing sums he'd hoped never to finish.

"Elodie," he said quietly, setting down the unfinished lark. "Grey gloves. Are you certain?"

"Certain as I'm standing here."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, he was looking at me the way he looks at a ledger line that won't balance—like the error is his own.

"Then we have three days at most," he said. "And there's something I should have told you six years ago. Something about why this guild exists at all." He glanced toward the cellar stairs, toward the locked case and the whispering bone, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Sit down, child. It's a long inheritance, and none of it is clean."

Outside, in the square, the criers had begun again—Monday, Monday, registration Monday—and their voices carried through the glass like weather coming in off a cold sea.
