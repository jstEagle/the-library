## 5. Tam Runs

The gift woke in Tam's hands the way it always did, small and warm and traitorous.

He'd been sorting the day's intake in the back room of the Ember Guild, a crate of odds that Sera had bought off a river barge: cracked compasses, a wedding ring with no inscription, a child's tin soldier missing one arm. Ordinary junk. Except his fingers knew better. They always knew. The compass wanted to point at a grave outside Vell; the ring had been thrown into the canal on purpose by a woman named Ansa who regretted it every morning since; the tin soldier's owner had lost it under a floorboard six years ago and still cried about it sometimes, though he was nearly grown now.

Tam set them all down carefully, like they were sleeping animals, and pressed his palms flat against the table until the knowing faded to a hum.

*Small,* he told himself. *It's small. Nobody notices small.*

But that was before the proclamation. Before the criers came through Ashfall with the crown's seal stamped on their broadsheets, before the word *remanded* started appearing in sentences that used to be about weather and bread prices. Before Quill had stood in the doorway last night with the ledger open under his arm and said, quietly, "They're not asking anymore, lad. They're counting."

Tam had heard Elodie and Sera arguing through the wall after that. He hadn't meant to listen. He'd listened anyway.

"—registration is not safety," Sera had said, her voice flat as a coin on a counter. "Registration is a list. Lists are how you find people when you've decided what to do with them."

"And if we don't register?" That was Elodie, low and tight. "Then we're criminals by morning."

"We were always criminals, love. We just used to be paid for it."

He'd lain awake after that, staring at the ceiling beam where he'd carved his initials three years ago, the night Quill took him in off the street with frostbitten fingers and a hunger so deep it had made him stupid. Three years. He had a bed now. He had a stool with his name burned into the leg. He had a family that argued about him in low voices through walls because they were scared for him, which was its own kind of unbearable.

And underneath all of it, humming in his hands like a trapped moth: *they will ask you what you can do, and your hands will answer before your mouth does.*

That was the thing nobody understood about his gift. It wasn't something he did. It was something that happened to him, the way falling happens. He could stand in front of a magistrate and swear he had nothing, and then some clerk would drop a keyring, and Tam's whole body would lean toward it like a compass needle, and everyone in the room would see.

So in the grey hour before dawn, while the guild house slept, Tam dressed in the dark, took only the coins from the loose brick—his own coins, earned fair—and left a note on the table because even running away felt wrong without paperwork. Quill would appreciate that. He hoped Quill would appreciate that.

*Gone to sort it out myself. Don't come after me. I'm doing this FOR the family, not against it.*

He read it back and winced. The capital letters were too much. But there was no time to be an artist. He folded it, weighted it with the tin soldier—the one-armed one, because someone should have it who wasn't ashamed of broken things—and slipped out the kitchen door into the cold.

The registration office opened at eight. It was a long walk across the city, and Tam had planned to use every minute of it to feel brave.

* * *

Ashfall's night market didn't know it was ending. That was the terrible thing about it, Tam thought as he cut down Tanner's Row toward the fish stalls—the market was still roaring along at full volume, lanterns strung between awnings, eel smoke and frying batter and a fiddler murdering a popular song two streets over. People were buying. People were haggling. A woman laughed so hard she had to grab her sister's arm.

In four days, maybe five, the criers would reach this street too. But tonight the market went on believing in itself, and Tam moved through it fast with his collar up, trying to be furniture.

He was three stalls past the pickle-barrel man when the feeling hit him.

It came the way it always came—a tug behind his sternum, gentle as a hook through cloth. Something lost, nearby. Something that wanted finding. His feet turned before his mind agreed to it, and he found himself drifting toward a pile of secondhand boots, and there, wedged in the heel of the left boot on top, was a rolled scrap of paper. A prayer, he realized without touching it. Someone had written a prayer to Saint Verity and hidden it in their boot sole years ago and sold the boots without remembering, and now the prayer was lost, and it *ached*, it ached like a sore tooth, and Tam stood over a stranger's boots in the middle of a market wanting to cry because even a prayer could be lost, even a prayer needed somebody—

*Stop.* He clenched his fists. *Stop it. You're a lamp in the dark and they are hunting lamps.*

He walked faster. He kept his eyes on the cobbles. And that was how he almost missed the voice behind him saying, "—third table today, all of them registered, clean as you like—"

Two men at a wine stall, cups in hand, wearing the particular boredom of clerks off shift. "My cousin works the office on Cordwainer Street," one said. "Says the gifted ones mostly come in quiet. Says it's the ones who don't come in that get the knock on the door." A shrug. "Better a list than a knock, I say."

*THERE.* That was the sentence Tam had been walking toward all night, the one he'd built out of fear and hope like a little boat to carry him. *Better a list than a knock.* He held onto it. If he surrendered first—if he walked in with his hands open and his head bowed and said *I'm one of the small ones, barely anything, hardly worth the ink*—then there would be no knock on the Ember Guild's door. No knock for Quill, who was old and slow and couldn't run. No knock for Elodie, whose secret Tam had never been told but had accidentally known for two years, because bones whispered around Elodie the way secrets whispered around Sera, and Tam's gift didn't just find objects. Sometimes it found truths lying around loose, and he'd learned to pretend he hadn't picked them up.

If anyone was going to be on the crown's list, let it be him alone. Let his name be the shield he laid down in front of theirs.

It was a good plan. It was the best plan he'd ever had, and it was garbage, and he knew it was garbage, and he walked toward Cordwainer Street anyway, because fear doesn't take arguments. Fear takes directions.

* * *

Elodie caught up with him at the mouth of Cordwainer Street, where the market thinned and the lanterns gave out and the registration office sat ahead like a held breath—lit windows, a queue already forming even at this hour, grey-coated clerks at a trestle table with a ledger as big as a tombstone.

Tam heard the footsteps and knew before he turned. He'd have known anywhere. Elodie walked like someone carrying water they refused to spill.

"Don't," Tam said, without turning around. "Please don't. You'll just make it worse."

"Make what worse?" Elodie stopped beside him, breathing hard, hair escaping its braid, still in yesterday's coat. "Tam. Look at me."

"I have to do this." His voice cracked on *this*, which ruined everything, so he pushed on louder. "I'm the smallest one. I'm the safest one to lose. If I go in there and give them my name, they stop looking. They stop knocking on doors. You and Quill and Sera—"

"You think they'll stop?" Elodie's voice wasn't angry. That was the worst part. It was soft, the way you talk to someone standing on a ledge. "Tam, look at the queue. Look at their faces."

He looked. He'd been avoiding it, but he looked. The line of people shuffling toward the trestle table weren't relieved. They weren't men unburdening themselves of a legal duty. They were quiet the way people are quiet at funerals—holding themselves very still, watching the clerks' pens move, flinching when a name was read back aloud for confirmation. At the end of the table, a side door stood open, and through it Tam could see a courtyard and a wagon with barred sides, and two men in grey gloves standing beside it, not loading anything, not unloading anything. Just standing. Just waiting to see what the day brought them.

"They're not registering gifts," Elodie said. "They're harvesting them. One name at a time, polite as taxes. And you want to walk into that yard and hand them the only thing about you that's ever been yours?"

"It's not *mine*," Tam burst out, and immediately wished he could eat the words, because they were true and true things shouldn't be shouted in the street. "It's not mine, it's a— it's a leash, it's a hook in me, it finds things whether I want it to or not, it found YOUR secret, Elodie, I never asked—" He was crying now, furiously, hating it. "I never asked for any of it. All I asked for was a stool with my name on it and a family that doesn't send me away, and if being on their list keeps that family safe, then the list can HAVE me—"

"Hey." Elodie's hands came up to his face, cold from the night air, steady as a carver's. "Look at me. Nobody sends anybody away. Not for this. Not ever. Do you hear me? Quill would burn the guild down before he signed your name to that wagon. Sera would forge the entire kingdom's paperwork first. And I—" Elodie's breath caught, just once. "I would carve every bone in that office into whistles before I let them take you."

"That's treason," Tam said wetly.

"That's Tuesday," Elodie said, and Tam laughed, horribly, snot and all, right there in the street.

Behind them, the queue shuffled forward. A woman reached the table. Tam heard the clerk ask, in a bored voice, "Nature of the gift?" and heard the woman say, "I dream true, sir, only little things," and heard the scratch of the pen, and heard—one beat later, faint, from the courtyard—the rattle of the wagon's bar being lifted.

Tam's stomach dropped through the cobbles.

"They're taking people *now*," he whispered. "Off the queue. Right there."

"Now you understand." Elodie didn't let go of his face. "So here's what happens instead. We turn around. We walk home. You tell Quill yourself, tonight, everything—your choice, your words, your timing. And tomorrow we figure out together what a boy who finds lost things is worth to people who hunt them. Because I promise you, Tam, whatever it's worth to them, it's worth more to us."

"The wagon—"

"—is not your debt to pay. Your life isn't currency, and I won't watch you spend it."

For a moment Tam let himself lean into Elodie's hands. Just a moment. Then he nodded, and Elodie exhaled like they'd been holding that breath since the kitchen door.

They turned. They got perhaps ten steps.

"TAM WINTERBOURNE'S FRIEND!" a voice barked behind them, and Tam's blood went to ice, because he'd never given anyone his surname here—but the clerk wasn't calling to him. The clerk was calling past him, to the queue, to a boy of maybe twelve clutching a dead bird in both hands. "Boy! You'll state your gift or move along!"

"I find things," the boy said, chin up, terrified, proud. "I find *anything*."

And Tam felt it happen—felt the boy's gift brush against his own across twenty feet of open street, two hooks catching, a resonance like struck bells. The clerk's pen paused. One of the grey-gloved men turned his head, slow, like a weathertop turning to wind.

Toward the boy. Toward Tam. Toward the space between them where something invisible had just sung.

"Walk," Elodie breathed. "Don't run. Walk."

They walked. Behind them, Tam heard the clerk say, "Step aside, boy. There's someone who'd like a word," and heard the crowd's murmur change texture, and heard—faintly, terribly—the wagon bars lift again.

At the corner, Elodie's hand closed around his wrist, and they broke into the dark of Tanner's Row together, running now, running hard, and Tam thought with a kind of wild, shattered clarity: *the note. The note is on the table. They'll know I tried to leave. They'll know I tried to sell myself.*

And worse, colder, arriving like winter:

*The grey gloves saw us. The grey gloves will remember our faces. Home isn't home anymore, and I led them straight to it.*

Ahead, the guild house windows were dark. Behind them, the market roared on, believing in itself.

Between the two, Elodie ran like someone carrying water they refused to spill, and Tam ran alongside, and neither of them said the thing they were both thinking:

*We have until morning.*
