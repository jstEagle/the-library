## 6. The First Bonfire

Quill had watched the city burn things before. He was sixty-one years old, and a man his age in Corvale had seen the plague-carts, the tax riots, the year they torched the harbor warehouses to stop the fever spreading. Fire was civic weather. Fire came and went and left a smell.

But he had never watched the city burn *gifts*.

They built the pyre in Founder's Square at dawn, which Quill thought was either stupid or deliberate — probably both. Dawn light made everything look ceremonial. The soldiers stacked confiscated relics in a lattice like cordwood: singing stones wrapped in chains so they couldn't be heard, prayer-flags that moved against the wind, a crate stenciled with the crown's own customs mark because some clerk hadn't known what he was inventorying. And bones. Quill could see them even from the guild house roof, three streets away — pale lengths of bone laid across the top of the pile like the whole thing had been built around a skeleton, which, in a way, it had.

He stood on the roof with his hands flat on the cold tiles and counted what he recognized.

The storm-bottle from the Marrow coast, the one with the lightning still circling inside it like a fish in a jar. Sold four years ago to a merchant's widow who paid triple and cried when she held it. The memory-beads, seven strings of them, bought back off a debtor last spring — Quill had told himself returning them to stock was mercy, because the man couldn't feed his children and remember his mother's face at the same time. The little carved wren, oak instead of bone, that Tam had made as an apprentice piece and Sera had sold to a collector without asking anyone, because that was her job.

Tam didn't know yet. Quill had checked twice this morning that the boy was downstairs, minding the ledger with Elodie, keeping his hands busy.

Down in the square, a priest said something over the pile. A herald read something else. The crowd was thin — dawn crowds always were — but there were faces Quill knew. The baker from Tanner's Row. Two of the dock clerks. People who'd traded with the guild once or twice, or wanted to, or simply liked knowing the Ember existed the way you like knowing there's a cellar under your house even if you never go down into it.

A soldier put the torch to the south corner, where the wind would carry it through the lattice fastest. That was craftsmanship, at least. Whoever built that pyre knew stacking.

Quill watched the storm-bottle catch.

Lightning doesn't burn like wood burns. When the bottle went, it went all at once, a white flash inside the fire, and for one heartbeat the whole square lit blue-white and every shadow fell the wrong direction. Then the glass burst and the lightning came out with nowhere to go and grounded itself through the nearest thing, which was a soldier's pike, and the man dropped screaming, and the crowd made a sound Quill had never heard a crowd make before — not horror exactly, and not delight, but the sound of people learning something about their neighbors all at once.

*It's real,* the crowd was thinking. You could hear the thought move through them like wind through wheat. *All those stories. It's real, and we've been standing next to it our whole lives.*

That was the mistake, Quill thought. Not the hunt, not the proclamation, not the grey gloves. The mistake was the bonfire itself. For thirty years the crown had pretended the gifted were rumor, and rumors can't be hunted. Now the crown had put magic on public display and burned it, and every soul in that square had just learned the world was wider than they'd been told. Fear would keep most of them quiet. But not all. Somewhere in that crowd was a child who saw lightning jump out of a bottle and felt not terror but *recognition*, and somewhere behind that child was a family deciding whether to report the strange thing their daughter did with dead birds' feathers or hide it.

The crown had just taught the whole city to ask the question. Quill had spent thirty years teaching the same people never to ask it.

He went downstairs.

* * *

Elodie was at the ledger desk with the shutters closed, working by lamplight, which meant they were doing two ledgers: the real one and the one for showing. Tam sat across from them grinding ink, moving slow and careful the way he did when he was scared and didn't want anyone to see him being scared.

"Numbers," Quill said.

"Numbers," Elodie agreed, not looking up.

He sat down across the desk and looked at the open ledger upside down. Elodie had turned the page to the dullest month of the previous year. Good instincts. He'd trained them well, or they'd trained themselves, and either way it made what he had to do next no easier.

"Tam," Quill said. "There's a delivery coming through the coal chute around noon. Salt pork and a crate of lamp oil. I need someone I trust to sign for it."

Tam looked up. "We have a coal chute?"

"We have a coal chute now." Quill kept his voice easy, kept the old shopkeeper warmth in it, the voice that had talked a hundred frightened clients down off a hundred terrible decisions. "Back door, cellar, noon. Don't open for anyone who isn't wearing the flour-mark."

Tam nodded and went, and Quill listened to his footsteps cross the cellar and stop, and stay stopped. The boy would wait by the chute for hours rather than risk missing it. That was Tam. That was the whole problem with Tam.

When the cellar door clicked, Quill turned the ledger around to face himself, then closed it.

"I sold that storm-bottle," he said.

Elodie's pen stopped.

"Four years ago. Widow Anselm, Marrow coast. She used to sit by it in the evenings and watch the lightning circle, and she told me it was the first time since her husband died that a room didn't feel empty." He turned the ledger over in his hands, feeling its weight, its terrible ordinary weight. "It's ash in Founder's Square as of an hour ago. Along with the memory-beads, and the wren Tam carved, and eleven other pieces we moved over the years that ended up in crown hands through seizures and dead men's estates and one auction I should never have let Sera run."

"You couldn't have known," Elodie said. It wasn't comfort. It was assessment. They were checking whether the statement was true before letting him have it.

"No," Quill said. "I couldn't have known. And that's not the confession, Elodie, so let me get to it, because my nerve is going to fail if I take the long road."

He got up and went to the hearth. There was a brick above it, third from the left, looser than the others, and behind the brick a cavity, and in the cavity a box of black lacquer that he had not opened in nine years. He brought it back to the desk and set it down between them, and his hands were shaking, and he let Elodie see them shake because hiding it now would cost more than showing it.

"When I founded the Ember Guild," he said, "I was twenty-nine years old, and I was not founding it alone. There was a man named Aldous Vey. You'll find his name in none of our records, because I took it out of all of them. He was the one with the idea, and the money, and — this is the part that matters — the *inventory*. He came to me with a list of objects. Regalia, he called them. Pieces of royal regalia, broken up and scattered, each one carrying something that shouldn't fit inside it. A fingerbone. A clasp. A third piece he never showed me. And he asked me to do one thing with my life: keep them apart. Never let them be in the same room. Never let them be found together. Build a trade house around them, a real one, a loud one, so the world looks at your impossible things and never thinks to look for his."

Elodie had gone very still. Quill had seen them still before — over a difficult carving, over bad news — but this was different. This was stillness like deep water.

"The fingerbone," Elodie said quietly.

"Aldous carried it himself until he died. Then it passed to me, and I kept it nine years, and then—" Quill stopped. Started again. "Then a client came with too much gold and the right questions, and I panicked, and I moved it, and I have hated myself for nine years for how easily I moved it. Like it was stock. Like it was ever mine to move."

"Quill." Elodie's voice was careful. "Where is it now?"

"That's not the confession either." He opened the box. Inside, on faded velvet, lay a folded paper gone soft at the creases, and a key of dark iron, and nothing else. "This is the confession. Aldous didn't find those pieces. He didn't inherit them, didn't steal them. He *made a bargain*, thirty years before I met him, with something that wore a crown — or claimed to. He told me once, drunk, only once, that the binding holding that thing on the throne was woven *out of people*. Gifted people. Taken one at a time, generation after generation, and that the regalia pieces weren't trophies. They were *anchors*. Each one pinned part of the binding into the world, and as long as they stayed scattered, the thing upstairs stayed scattered too." He pushed the box toward Elodie. "And the hunt that started this week? The registration, the remanding? Aldous described it to me forty years ago like he was reading tomorrow's weather. *When it gets hungry again, it will make the country help it harvest.*"

The lamp flame leaned. Neither of them touched the box.

"So here it is," Quill said. "Here is the property I inherited and never explained to any of you. I built this guild as a lockbox and told you it was a family. Both things were true, and I let you believe the second one so I wouldn't have to say the first. Every year I didn't tell you, the lie compounded interest. And now the crown is burning gifts in the square, and the thing on the throne is gathering what it planted, and the debt has come due, and I am an old man sitting at my own desk telling my own—"

His voice broke. He let it.

"—telling my own children that I'm the reason the wolf knows where we sleep."

For a long moment Elodie didn't move. Then they reached out — not for the box, but for Quill's hand, and their grip was dry and steady and stronger than he expected.

"You kept us safe for thirty years," Elodie said. "However wrong the reasons started, the keeping was real. I've heard enough last words carved into bone to know the difference between a life badly begun and a life badly lived. Yours is the first kind."

"It doesn't feel like the first kind."

"No," Elodie agreed. "It wouldn't. That's what guilt is — it inherits like property. You hold it whether you earned it or not. The only question is what you do with it before you pass it on." They turned the box toward themselves and lifted out the paper, unfolding it with the care they gave to thousand-year bone. "What is this?"

"The location of the clasp. The second anchor. Aldous hid it somewhere no finder could stumble on it — he told me it was hidden *inside a riddle only a certain kind of gift could solve*." Quill shook his head. "I never understood what he meant. I was never meant to understand. He said if the day ever came, I'd know who to give it to."

Elodie looked at the paper. Then at him. Something moved behind their eyes — that listening look, the one they got when the voices were talking, except Quill had never before wondered what it cost them to carry that look every hour of every day.

"And the key?" they asked.

"The third piece," Quill said. "Or the way to it. Aldous never said which. He said the third piece was in the worst possible place, and the key was for the person brave enough or foolish enough to go and get it." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I always assumed that person would never be me."

From the cellar, faintly, came the sound of the coal chute scraping open — hours early, because of course it was, because Tam had been waiting there all morning in the dark, keeping faith with a door. And beneath that, far away, a bell in Founder's Square began to ring the alarm cadence, though the fire had been out for hours.

Quill looked at Elodie, and Elodie looked at the paper in their hands, and between them the box sat open like a mouth.

"They're ringing it wrong," Elodie said softly. "Listen. Three and one. Three and one." Their face had gone the color of the ledger paper. "Quill — that's not an alarm. That's a summons. They're calling names."
