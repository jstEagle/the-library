## 7. Quill's Bargain

The bonfire smoke still hung in the city like a curtain nobody had thought to take down. Two days after the burning, and every time Elodie stepped into the guild courtyard they could taste it—char and something sweeter underneath, the way relics went when they gave up whatever they'd been holding. Memory had a smell. So did grief.

Quill hadn't left the counting room.

That was wrong in itself, because Quill was a man who moved the way water moved—always toward some task, always filling the space in front of him. For two days he had sat behind his desk with the shutters closed, and when Sera brought him tea he drank it cold, and when Tam tried to talk to him about anything at all Quill would look up with an expression Elodie couldn't name, an expression like a man listening to a door he expected to open.

On the second evening, he called for Elodie by name. Not *bone-carver*. Not *keeper of ledgers*. Just: "Elodie. A word."

They climbed the stairs to the counting room together, past the shelves where the guild's honest stock slept in velvet—teeth that remembered their owners' sins, a storm in a bottle, three generations of traded courage—and Elodie noticed, for the first time, that Quill walked slowly. Not old-slow. Careful-slow. Like a man carrying something fragile down a long flight of stairs.

He lit one lamp. Then, after a moment, a second, so the light fell on both of them evenly, without shadows either could hide in.

"Do you know why I named this place the Ember Guild?" he said.

"Because embers keep," Elodie said. "You told me that when you took me in. Fire goes out; embers wait. You said we were in the business of waiting."

"I did tell you that." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, worn thin as old paper. "And it was true, as far as it went. But there's a longer version, and I've kept it from you for eleven years, and I find I can't keep it another night. Not after what you heard. Not after the fingerbone."

Elodie's hand went, without permission, to the satchel at their hip. Inside it lay the king's fingerbone wrapped in linen, and inside the bone—if bone could be said to have an inside—the voice still waited. It had not spoken since the night it first begged them. But Elodie could feel it the way you feel weather coming. Pressure behind the ears. A hush with weight to it.

"You're going to tell me royal bones are bait again," Elodie said. "You already did. You were very eloquent."

"No." Quill folded his hands on the desk. His knuckles were scarred from decades of handling things that bit back. "I'm going to tell you why I know they're bait. Because I've known about the hook for forty years. I helped bait it."

The lamp flames stood very still. The house creaked around them, settling into evening, ordinary sounds, and Elodie felt the ordinary sounds begin to recede, the way the market noise used to recede when a dead voice came close.

"Sit down," Quill said. "This is a story with a bottom to it, but it's deep."

Elodie sat.

* * *

"The guild wasn't founded by me," Quill began. "I inherited it. The way you inherit a debt, or a fever. Before me there was Master Aldous Vane, who ran this house through the last years of the old king's reign and the whole of the usurper's rise. And before Vane—" Quill tapped the desk twice, "—there was a woman called Maren Ember, and she is the reason for the name, and she is the reason for everything else too, though she never wrote her reasons down. She carved them instead. Into bone."

"Maren Ember," Elodie repeated. The name landed somewhere under their ribs and stayed there, warm and strange, like a stone held too long against the skin.

"A carver. The finest anyone ever saw, and I say that knowing what you can do, because what you do, she did first. She heard the dead through bone. Not echoes, not residue—voices. Whole voices. And the dead talked to her, and she listened, and she made a trade of it until the day a dying man put something in her hands that changed the shape of her life."

Quill rose, crossed to the wall of small drawers behind him, and opened one Elodie had never seen him touch—a drawer set low, near the floor, unmarked. From it he lifted a flat wooden case, and from the case, a bone.

Not a fingerbone. A knuckle joint, yellowed with age, carved all over with lines so fine they looked like cracks until you got close. Elodi leaned forward despite themselves. The carving was a script, but not any script they knew—not ledger-hand, not court cipher, nothing taught in any school.

"What is it?"

"A piece of instructions," Quill said. "Written by Maren Ember's own hand, in a code only a hearing-gifted carver can read—because the words aren't just cut into the bone. They're sung into it. The pattern carries the meaning the way a riverbed carries water. To anyone else it's decoration. To someone like you..." He turned it over. "To someone like you, it should sound like a person talking."

Elodie reached out before they knew they were doing it. Their fingertips touched the knuckle-bone.

Nothing.

No—that wasn't right. Not nothing. A silence shaped like waiting. The same pressure they felt from the king's fingerbone, but older, fainter, a voice speaking from very far away through many walls.

"It's quiet," Elodie whispered. "But it's not empty."

"She died two hundred years ago," Quill said. "Whatever she put into that bone has been fading ever since. But the bones remember, Elodie. That's the whole of our trade, isn't it? Bones remember. So does wood, if you treat it right. So does paper, and ink, and the smell of a room where something important happened." He set the knuckle-bone down between them on the desk, deliberately, like a card played face-up. "Here is what Maren Ember learned, in the last year of her life, from a voice no other soul in the kingdom could hear."

* * *

"There was a king," Quill said. "Before the usurper. A good enough king—not a saint, kings rarely are—but a man who ruled, and aged, and feared death the way men in crowns fear it more than most, because a crown looks like immortality from the outside and feels like a countdown from within. And in his fear, he went looking for a cure. Or an extension. Or whatever you want to call the thing that was offered to him."

"Offered by whom?"

"That's the part Maren couldn't learn, and neither have I. Some pact-maker, some binder, something old enough to speak the language of oaths in its sleep. What Maren did learn—from the king himself, or rather from what was left of the king—is that the bargain was struck. The king would not die. The crown would not pass. And the price—" Quill's voice went flat and careful, the way it did when he quoted figures he hated, "—the price was paid in installments. Every gifted soul the binding could reach. Their gifts didn't fade when they died, Elodie. They were taken. Fed into the thing wearing the crown. Generation after generation. The hunt isn't new. The hunt is the harvest, and the harvest has been running since before your grandmother's grandmother."

Elodie thought of the bonfire. Of confiscated relics going up in flame while the crowd cheered, and how some of those relics had been gifts the guild itself had sold, years ago, to clients who'd seemed harmless. Thought of how the criers had read the proclamation: *all gifted persons to be registered, then remanded.* Remanded. A tidy word. Nobody asked what happened after remanding, because the answer was standing on the throne wearing a face.

"And the king?" Elodie managed. "The real one. In the fingerbone. He said—he said he's not dead. He said he's bound."

"He's the anchor," Quill said. "Or the lock, depending on how you look at it. The binding needs a living core—a will to wrap itself around, a life to wear. So it took the king and it never let him die. Two hundred years, Elodie. Can you imagine? Every gift harvested feeding the thing that holds him. He's been conscious the whole time, or enough of him has. Screaming into the only medium that could carry it. Bone."

"And nobody heard him."

"One person per generation might," Quill said. "A hearing-carver. And here is the cruel arithmetic of it: the binding knows when it's been heard. Maren Ember heard the king. Within a year, she was dead—quietly, officially of fever, though her own bones, which she'd prepared in advance, tell a different story. Her apprentice heard next, thirty years on. Dead within the decade. Vane himself had the tail end of the gift, enough to hear the king once, badly, and Vane spent the rest of his life terrified, and built this guild as much to hide himself as to hide anything else."

Elodie sat very still. The lamp flames had begun, finally, to move again—or perhaps Elodie had simply stopped noticing them. "So it kills us. The ones who hear."

"It finds us," Quill corrected gently. "It doesn't need to kill us. It needs to collect us. Death is just its preferred method of delivery." He paused. "Until now. Because now it's grown bold. Now it wears a crown openly and calls the hunt a proclamation, and registration a kindness. It's stopped being patient because it thinks it's won."

"And my mother." The words came out of Elodie before they could weigh them, and once out, they wouldn't go back. "My mother had the gift. You told me she died of marsh-fever. You told me—"

"I told you what was written," Quill said quietly. "I wrote it myself. And I have regretted the lie for sixteen years, and I am done carrying it. Your mother heard the king, Elodie. She came to Vane's successor—came to me—pregnant, frightened, asking what the voice in her bones wanted. And I told her the truth, or part of it, because I owed her family that much. And then she died, and you arrived on my doorstep with her eyes and her hands, and I looked at those hands and I knew exactly what you were, and I swore I would never let you hear what she heard."

"But I did anyway."

"You did anyway. Because the gift doesn't ask permission. Because it's in your blood, and the blood came from her, and hers came from—" Quill exhaled. "From Maren Ember. Direct line. Maren's daughter carried it south for safety; her daughter's daughter carried it back; and somewhere in that long chain of women keeping secrets and staying alive, your grandmother married a Winterbourne, and your mother was born, and then you. Eleven generations, Elodie. You are not a bone-carver who happens to hear the dead. You are the eleventh keeper of Maren Ember's charge. Whether you wanted it or not."

* * *

The silence afterward was the kind Elodie usually dreaded—the kind where the dead pressed close. But this time the silence was only human, and heavy, and theirs to sit in.

Eleven years. Eleven years of ledgers and lessons, of Quill teaching them to balance accounts of bottled storms, of being told they were valuable, chosen, family—all of it true, and all of it resting, apparently, on top of a truth Quill had buried beneath it like a body under floorboards.

"Why tell me now?" Elodie asked finally. "Why not six years ago? Why not last month, before any of this?"

"Because telling you made you a target sooner," Quill said. "Every day you didn't know was a day the binding couldn't use you. Knowledge is a handle, Elodie. Things grab handles." His jaw tightened. "And because I was a coward. There it is. Forty years of running a house built on a dead woman's secret, telling myself I was protecting everyone, when the truth is I was protecting myself from the conversation we're having right now."

"And the regalia." Elodie latched onto it because latching onto facts was how they survived hard rooms. "You said hide the regalia. The fingerbone is part of it?"

"Not part of it. The key to it." Quill drew a slow breath, and Elodie watched him gather himself the way he gathered himself before opening the strongbox. "The binding was made with three anchors. Three pieces of regalia, each one worked into the oath—a crown-piece, a scepter-stone, and the king's own fingerbone, which the binder took as surety. Break or scatter all three, and the pact comes apart. But the binding protects its anchors, and it hunts anyone who learns where they are. Maren Ember spent her last year doing one thing: taking the pieces she could reach and hiding them, one by one, in places only a hearing-carver could ever find—because she hid them with her gift, and they can only be located with it. Then she scattered the knowledge the way you'd scatter seed. One thread of it into her daughter. One into her apprentices. One into a bone she sang her instructions into and sent out into the world."

"The knuckle-bone."

"Which came back to me thirty years ago, by ways I still don't fully understand, along with the guild itself. And I have spent those thirty years failing to act on it, because acting meant becoming visible, and I chose the ember over the fire. I chose waiting." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he did not wipe them. "Waiting is over. The crown is hunting the gifted openly. Sera is burning names. Tam is frightened half to death. And the king's voice found you anyway, because it was always going to find you—it found your mother, and hers, and Maren's. Eleven generations, Elodie, and every single one of them had to decide what to do with the hearing. And now it's yours to decide."

Elodie looked at the knuckle-bone on the desk. At the king's fingerbone in their satchel, silent and patient. At Quill's ruined, honest face.

It should have felt like a cage closing. That was the strange thing—it didn't. It felt like a door they'd been leaning against all their life finally swinging open onto a corridor they'd always, in some bone-deep way, known was there. The loneliness they'd carried even inside the guild's warmth—the sense of hearing a conversation no one else could—suddenly had a name, a lineage, a purpose. They weren't broken. They were addressed. Somewhere across two hundred years, a woman who carved songs into bone had written them a letter, and the letter said: *you are not the first, and if you're brave, you needn't be the last.*

"The council of free cities won't believe a voice in a bone," Elodie said slowly. "Unless there's proof."

"Proof is the third piece," Quill said. "Or rather, finding the pieces proves the story—because only the true inheritance could find them. But that work starts tomorrow, and it starts dangerous, and I'll not pretend otherwise." He slid the knuckle-bone across the desk. "Tonight, I want you to try something. Hold both bones together. Maren's instructions and the king's voice. If the blood in you is what I think it is, they'll recognize each other."

Elodie picked up the knuckle-bone. Unwrapped the fingerbone from its linen. Set the two side by side on the desk, fingertip to fingertip, the way you'd introduce strangers at a funeral.

The pressure behind their ears spiked. The lamplight bent—just slightly, just at the edges, like heat-haze in winter. And from the meeting point of the two bones, faint as breath on a window, Elodie heard singing.

Two voices. An old man's and a woman's, two hundred years apart, finding each other in the space between Elodie's hands, harmonizing on a note that had been waiting since before the guild had a name.

Quill's chair scraped back. "Gods," he breathed. "It's real. After all this time, it's—"

The singing cut off.

Not faded. Cut. The way a throat closes around a scream. And into the ringing silence that followed, Elodie heard—clearly, unmistakably, in a voice like grey gloves flexing—the words:

*I hear you too, little carver.*

Downstairs, the front bell of the guild rang. Once. Twice. Then kept ringing, though no one stood at the door.

Sera's footsteps pounded up the stairs. "Someone's in the street," she said, breathless. "Watching the house. Grey coat. And Elodie—" she hesitated, and Elodie watched the news land on her face like a stone into still water, "—Tam's gone. His bed's empty. There's a note."

She held it out. In Tam's careful, childish handwriting, four words:

*Gone to register. Sorry.*
