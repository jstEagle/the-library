## 8. Salt Road South

Sera had stolen a great many things in her life—a bishop's signet, three years of another woman's memories, a storm out of a bottle once—but she had never stolen a goodbye. She'd always assumed it couldn't be done. Goodbye was the one commodity that lost all value the moment it changed hands.

She learned otherwise on the morning they burned the Ember Guild's books.

Not all of them. Quill kept the founding ledgers, the ones written in his own cramped hand, and those went into a lead-lined case that Tam carried slung across his back like a schoolboy's satchel. The rest—the trade records, the client lists, forty years of debts and favors and names that could hang every gifted soul from here to the northern passes—went into the courtyard brazier while Sera stood over it with an iron poker, stirring the pages down to ash so nothing legible survived even by accident.

The paper burned green at first. Alchemical treatments, preservation spells, the small clever magics of a house that dealt in impossible things. Then it burned ordinary, which was somehow worse. All of it reduced to the same dumb orange flame as any fishmonger's receipt.

"Page," she said, without turning.

Tam, who had been hovering at her shoulder for an hour, handed her another page. His hands were steady. His eyes were not.

"You don't have to help," she said.

"You said every name we burn is a name they can't find."

"I did say that." She fed the page into the coals. "It doesn't make it less of a funeral."

Behind them, Elodie was saying their goodbyes to Quill in the doorway of the counting room, voices low enough that Sera caught only shapes of words: *salt road*, *Meridian*, *the boy first*. Quill would not be coming. He had said it plainly at dawn—*someone stays to hold the house, someone has to answer the door if the crown comes asking questions, and no one lies better than I do when I'm standing on my own ground*—and Sera had heard under the plainness everything he wasn't saying. That he was old. That he was slow on a road. That he intended, if it came to it, to be the thing the grey gloves found instead of the children.

She had told him he was a sentimental old fraud. He had thanked her.

The last page went in. It was the ledger of traded favors, her own ledger, the one she maintained because Quill understood that a fence's memory is only as good as her records and a fence's records are only as safe as her fire. Her own name appeared in it more than anyone's. Favors owed to her in seven cities. Favors she owed in four. She watched the ink bubble and blacken and thought, with a professional's detachment, that she had never in her life destroyed an asset this valuable without being paid.

Then Elodie touched her elbow, and the detachment failed.

"Road's empty till the tannery," Elodie said. "Tam's checked twice."

"Then we go."

They went out through the salt cellar, the way smugglers had gone out of that building for two hundred years before it was ever a guild hall, and came up in the alley behind the chandler's where a cart waited, loaded with barrels of the chandler's honest worst-grade tallow. Three fugitives hidden among forty barrels of candle fat. It was, Sera reflected as she took up the traces, almost insultingly simple. Invisibility had always been the Ember Guild's finest product. They sold it by the yard.

She just no longer believed in the stock.

* * *

The salt roads were not roads, properly. They were the routes the smugglers ran when the king's tolls got too greedy or the harbormaster too honest: cart tracks that followed dry watercourses, drovers' paths that wandered across three parishes without once touching a tollgate, fords unmarked on any map printed for respectable use. Sera had spent eleven years learning them the way other women learned psalms. She had never expected to need them for herself.

By the second day she had taught them the rhythm. Move at dusk and dawn, sleep through full light in the hollows where the gorse grew thick. Tam walked between them, always, though nobody had said so aloud—it had simply arranged itself that way on the first night and stayed arranged. Elodie carried the lead case now, and carried it badly, favoring the left shoulder, and Sera said nothing about it because Elodie needed to feel useful more than Sera needed a comfortable porter.

On the third evening, in the blue hour before dark, Elodie sat on a fallen wall turning a knucklebone over and over in their fingers and not talking.

"That's new bone," Sera said. "You carved it yourself?"

"Mm. Blank." Elodie turned it again. "I keep trying to hear something in it and there's nothing. It's just—" A small, terrible laugh. "It's just a bone. Do you know how long it's been since anything was just what it was? I've been listening to objects talk since I was six. Silence used to be restful."

"And now?"

"Now every silence sounds like a held breath." They put the bone away. "Quill's alive. I'd know if he weren't. There's a—weight, in the direction of the city. Like a voice I'm not close enough to make out but I know it's still speaking." They looked at Sera sideways, in the way they had, checking whether the truth had landed too hard. "That's how I know he stayed on purpose. He's holding the line where I can feel it."

Sera looked north, at the smudge of the country they'd come from, and let herself feel exactly none of it. That was her gift—not magic, just discipline, the trained ability to put a feeling in a drawer and deal with the inventory later.

"Eat," she said. "We cross the Erle at the weir ford tomorrow, and after that we're in the open hills where you can see a rider coming an hour off. You'll want your legs."

Tam, curled against his own satchel of ledgers, said into the firelight: "Do you think the free cities will actually take us?"

"The free cities take anyone with money or skills."

"We don't have money."

"You have skills, and you're traveling with me, and I have—" She stopped. Recalculated. "I have friends. Of a sort. Meridian's ruled by the guilds, and guilds understand each other. We'll be vouched for."

"What does 'vouched for' cost?"

Gods, but the boy asked precise questions these days. He'd come to the Ember Guild eight months ago, a foundling with a talent for finding things, all elbows and appetite. Somewhere along the salt road he'd turned into a person who knew that safety had a price.

"It costs whatever it costs," Sera said, "and I'll be the one paying it, and you'll stop worrying your face about it. That's final."

He worried his face about it anyway, all night, in the way of apprentices, and she pretended not to see, in the way of fences.

* * *

The checkpoint was not on her map because it had never existed before.

They came down out of the gorse hills at midmorning on the fifth day to find the coast road barred. Two wagons broadside across it, a crown banner nailed to a plank, and around the barricade maybe a dozen soldiers doing the unglamorous work of tyranny—checking carts, pulling off hats, making farmers unload flour they had no intention of confiscating just to watch them sweat. And apart, leaning against the wagon with the patience of a man being paid by the day, a figure in grey gloves.

Sera took them off the road within the minute, back into the rocks, and crouched with the other two behind a shelf of granite while her mind ran the arithmetic.

"Checkpoint for the ferry road," she murmured. "Every cart searched. Every traveler questioned."

"They're looking for us specifically?" Tam whispered.

"They're looking for everyone specifically. That's worse." She chewed the inside of her cheek. "There's no crossing within two days' walk that isn't watched or drowned. The weir ford's a lie in summer; in autumn it'll have you to the chest. With the boy—no."

"I can swim," Tam said.

"You can flounder," Sera said, with the authority of someone who had watched people drown for profit, "and the current at the weir takes bodies thirty miles down to a fishing village that sells them back to the crown for bounty. No. We go through the checkpoint."

"With what faces?" Elodie said. "I'm a registered carver in the Guild of Bonecraft. My papers are real. My papers are also flagged—I filed a commission renewal the week before the proclamation. If they check—"

"Then they won't check. Give me an hour."

She left them in the rocks and walked, unhurried, hat low, around the long flank of the hill until she came to the hamlet that fed the checkpoint—one street, a shrine, and a tavern called the Drowned Ox whose keeper she had last seen nine years ago, on a night involving a customs officer, a bribe, and a favor rendered that had never yet been called in.

Nine years. In her experience a favor older than five was a dead asset; people died, forgot, or grew consciences. But Maren Osgood did not forget, and Sera was fairly sure she hadn't died, because Sera kept track of such things professionally.

Maren was behind her bar, gray now to the roots, and when she saw Sera her face went through surprise, recognition, and dread in the space of a breath.

"No," Maren said, before Sera had cleared the doorway. "Whatever it is. No."

"It's good to see you too."

"Sera. Nine years, and you walk in wearing a road-dirty cloak two hundred yards from a Gift-Hunt checkpoint. I know why you're here. Everybody with your kind of luck knows why you're here."

"Two travelers and a boy need to pass the barrier without their papers read." Sera sat down on a stool, making herself heavy, permanent. "No violence, no mess, nothing traced to you. I need a word in the right ear and a distraction at the right time, and then you will never see me again."

Maren polished a mug that didn't need polishing. "I heard you were a buyer of secrets," she said at last. "I always wondered what happened when a secrets-seller needed one."

"She pays," Sera said. "That's what happens."

"And what does she pay?"

Sera had rehearsed this in the rocks, running the exchange rates of her own ruin. She had assets left: coin, contacts, three favors owed to her in towns they'd already passed forever out of reach. Any of them might have bought what she needed. Any of them, spent, left her poorer on the far side of the checkpoint, and she had decided in the night that she could live poor.

But Maren was frightened, and frightened people didn't want payment—they wanted insurance.

"I'll tell you the true name of the man who runs the grey gloves' southern network," Sera said quietly. "Not the title. The name. The one he answers to at home. The thing you can hold over him for the rest of your life, so that no matter what happens at that checkpoint today, no matter who signs what order in some office, you have a knife in your hand for as long as you live. That's the price. That's what nine-year-old credit buys in this market."

The mug stopped moving. Sera watched Maren understand it—that she was being offered not gold but leverage, the rarest commodity on the coast, and that accepting it made her a player rather than a bystander, which was its own kind of danger and its own kind of shelter both.

"And it's true?" Maren said. "Verifiable?"

"It's true enough to kill him with, if you ever need to. Which you won't. Because he'll never know you hold it, and neither will anyone else." Sera leaned in and gave her the name, and watched it land like a stone down a well.

When she straightened, Maren's face had settled into the particular calm of a woman who has just become dangerous.

"Cart leaves for the checkpoint at noon," Maren said. "Ale resupply. The driver's my nephew and deaf as a post and the soldiers know him. Your people ride in the false bottom—he thinks he's hauling kegs. At the barrier, Corporal Vess will wave it through because Vess owes me for a matter I won't describe, and the grey-gloved gentleman doesn't search ale carts because he considers them beneath his dignity. I've watched him three days." She paused. "The boy. Is he—"

"He's mine," Sera said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't correct it.

* * *

The false bottom of an ale cart, it turned out, was built for kegs, not people, and the journey from the Drowned Ox to the barrier took the better part of an hour over rutted road, in the dark, pressed shoulder to shoulder in a space you couldn't sit up in. Sera lay with her cheek against rough boards and felt Elodie trembling beside her—not fear, she realized. Laughter, strangled to nothing.

"What?" she breathed.

"Nothing. It's just—we spent eight years building a guild with wards and warded wards and a vault door that weighs four hundred pounds." A thread of air escaped, half laugh, half sob. "And now we're smuggling ourselves in a box meant for beer."

"Everything worth having ships in something undignified. Memories ship in bottles. Storms ship in jars. Kings—" She stopped herself. Too close to the fingerbone, too close to the thing Elodie carried wrapped in wool against their ribs. "Hold still. We're near."

The cart slowed. Voices outside, muffled by planks—Corporal Vess's bored drawl, the driver's cheerful deafness, boots on gravel. Then a third voice, silk over wire, the grey gloves' owner, and Sera's whole body went to iron.

"Papers are in order," Vess said.

"Everyone has papers in order lately. Open a keg."

A pause with weight in it. Sera counted her own heartbeats and got to nine.

"As you like," Vess said. "Mind you tap it right, sir, that's good stock, spilled ale comes out of wages."

Wood knocked against wood somewhere above their feet. Then the silky voice said, with the weary contempt of a man declining to grovel in front of subordinates, "Leave it. I've searched forty carts today and found forty grandmothers. The ones we want aren't riding beer." Boots receding. "Move it along."

The cart lurched forward. Rolled. Rolled on. A mile passed before anyone dared breathe fully, and two more before the driver halted and the false bottom slid open onto blinding noon and Tam's white face and Elodie blinking like something newly hatched.

They climbed out onto a lane above the sea, and there it was below them—the harbor town, the ferry, and beyond the gray-green water, faint on the horizon, a suggestion of hills that belonged to no crown Sera answered to. Free shores. Days away still. But visible. Believable.

Tam was looking at her strangely.

"You bought that," he said. "The whole thing. What did it cost?"

"A favor," Sera said. "Old one. Nearly worthless."

"Liar," said Elodie mildly, shaking straw from their hair.

"Apprentice carver," Sera said, "when you can lie half as well as I just did, you'll be qualified to have opinions about it."

But Tam was still watching her with those finding-eyes of his, the ones that could locate a lost coin in a riverbed, and she understood that he had located something in her and was too polite to dig it up. So she turned to the sea instead, to the wind coming off it smelling of salt and kelp and elsewhere, and allowed herself, briefly, in private, behind her face, to grieve. For the guild hall with its warm counting room. For the ledger she'd burned with her own name in it. For the woman she had been nine days ago, who had a home and keys to it and thought that was what home was.

Home, it turned out, was two people on a cliff, straw in their hair, waiting on her word.

"South," she said, and started walking, and they fell in beside her, Tam on her right and Elodie on her left, the formation they'd fallen into without discussion and would keep, she suspected, all the way to the water and past it.

They made eight miles before dark, sleeping in a shepherd's ruined bothy with a roof half gone, and it was there, over a fire of gorse roots, sharing hard bread and harder cheese, that Elodie went suddenly still, head tilting, the way they did when the dead spoke.

"Sera." Their voice had gone thin. "Something's wrong in the north. Quill's weight—it just—" Their hands closed around the blank knucklebone hard enough to whiten. "It changed."

Sera set down her bread.

"How," she said, "changed?"
