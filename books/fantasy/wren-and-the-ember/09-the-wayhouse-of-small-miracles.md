## 9. The Wayhouse of Small Miracles

The salt road ran out of hills on the third day, and that was when Sera finally let them stop walking.

"Not because we're safe," she said, dropping the pack against a fencepost gone grey with weather. "Because if I make you two take one more step, you'll fall over and then I'll have to carry you, and I'm not built for carrying."

Tam sat down where he stood, right in the road, and didn't care about the dust. His feet had stopped being feet somewhere around noon. They were just two aching ideas at the ends of his legs.

Ahead of them, tucked into the elbow of a hillside where the road bent toward the sea, stood a building that looked like nothing much—stone walls, a slate roof, a sign hanging from an iron bracket with no name painted on it at all. Just a blank oval of wood, sanded smooth.

"No name," Tam said.

"Good," said Sera. "Named places can be found."

* * *

The door opened before anyone knocked.

A woman filled it—broad as a doorway herself, with flour to her elbows and grey hair pinned up with what looked like a bone knitting needle. She looked at Sera first, then Elodie, then Tam, and her eyes did something odd: they went soft in a way that made Tam want to check over his shoulder for whoever she was actually looking at.

"You'll be hungry," she said. "And tired. And you'd rather not say why. Come in, then."

"We don't have money," Sera said flatly. "We have almost nothing."

"I didn't ask." The woman stepped back to let them through. "I'm Marta. This is my house. The rules are on the wall; read them or don't. Nobody here asks anybody anything they don't offer freely."

Inside, the wayhouse smelled like bread and woodsmoke and something else underneath—something clean and green, like the air after rain, though no window was open. A fire burned in a hearth big enough to roast an ox, and the moment Tam stepped into its light he felt it do something no fire should do.

It remembered him.

Not his face—he could see that the flames didn't flicker toward him like eyes. But the warmth went into him and found all the cold places, and instead of warming them evenly the way ordinary fires do, it warmed them *specifically*. The ache behind his knees from three days of marching. The tightness in his chest he hadn't known was there since the night market, since he'd run and Elodie had chased him and caught him by the sleeve and not let go. Even the small bruise on his palm from when he'd fallen on the salt road. The heat sank into each one like fingers pressing exactly where it hurt.

"Oh," Tam said, before he could stop himself.

"First time?" Marta said, without turning from her bread. "It does that. Hearth's older than me, older than this house probably. It remembers every hurt that's ever come inside and it tends to those first. Don't ask me how. I've kept this place thirty years and I've stopped asking how."

Elodie stood at the threshold still, one hand on the doorframe. Tam watched their face—the careful face, the ledger-keeper's face—and saw something move under it. Elodie had been listening to something. Tam couldn't hear what Elodie heard; nobody could. But he'd learned, these last weeks, to read the signs. The slight tilt of the head. The way their breath caught and held.

"The hearth," Elodie said quietly. "It's not just warm. There's—" They stopped themselves, glanced at Marta.

"There's what," said Marta, "love? Yes. Somebody loved this house so hard it soaked into the stone. That's all most magic is, when you get down to it. Just love that didn't know where else to go." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Sit down before you fall down, both of you. Bread's twenty minutes."

* * *

The bread never molded.

Tam learned this the way he learned most things, which was by accident. He'd been given a heel of loaf wrapped in cloth for the morning, and three days later, digging in his pack for a strap, he'd found it again—still soft, still smelling faintly of rosemary, not a spot of green on it anywhere.

"It keeps," Marta said, when he asked. "That's its gift. Every loaf out of this oven keeps until it's eaten. My mother's recipe, my mother's gift, baked into the dough same as the salt." She said it lightly, but Tam noticed she checked the shutters were closed first.

That was the other thing about the wayhouse. Everybody checked the shutters.

There were maybe a dozen people living there or passing through when Tam arrived, and in a week he'd met most of them, and not one of them had told him plainly what they were. He didn't need telling. He'd started to notice things. The old man who swept the yard whose broom never missed a leaf, even in wind—because the leaves came to him. The twin girls who carried water up from the well and never spilled a drop, because the water liked them. A thin, silent man who slept by the hearth and woke screaming sometimes, and whom nobody asked about, and who was slowly learning to sleep again because the hearth remembered his hurts too, whatever they were.

They traded, Tam realized. Not coin—nobody had coin—but kindnesses. Small ones. The sweeping man swept extra. The water-girls watered Marta's garden past their share. A tinker mended everyone's shoes and never once mentioned it. And Marta fed them all, and the hearth held them, and somehow the whole thing balanced, like a set of scales that weighed things no scale could hold.

It reminded him of the guild. Of Quill's ledgers and Sera's fences and Elodie's bone-dust workroom. Except the guild had always been a secret inside a secret, a handful of people huddled around impossible things, and this—

This was a whole house of it. Maybe a whole road of it. Maybe more.

"How many places like this are there?" he asked Sera one evening, on the bench outside while the twins raced buckets of water up the hill behind them.

Sera chewed her bread a long time before answering. "More than the crown thinks," she said. "Fewer than we need." She tossed the crust to the yard, where it vanished promptly, some hen having opinions. "People think hiding means being alone. It doesn't. Hiding is a trade too. You hide me, I hide you. Whole networks of it, all down the salt roads. The guild was one node. This is another." She nodded at the glowing windows. "The crown hunts gifts like they're treasure buried in separate chests. One digger per chest. They've never understood it's all one vein."

Tam turned that over. "Like the roots under a forest," he said. "You cut one tree, looks like one tree. But underneath—"

"Underneath," Sera agreed, "it's all holding hands."

* * *

It happened on the eighth night, in the common room, with the rain coming down hard enough to sound like applause on the slate roof.

The thin man by the hearth was screaming again.

He'd been better all week—sleeping longer, eating more, letting Marta put a blanket on him without flinching. But that night the storm had rolled in off the strait, thunder close enough to rattle the glass, and something in it dragged him back wherever he'd been. He thrashed and cried out words in no language Tam knew, and the hearth threw its warmth at him and it wasn't enough, and Marta knelt beside him saying his name—which was Aldric, though he'd never offered it—and it wasn't enough either.

And Tam felt it happen. Felt the pull.

It started low, in the same place his gift usually lived—that quiet sense of *lostness*, like a compass needle behind his breastbone that pointed at missing things. All his life it had pointed at objects. Lost keys. A dropped coin in a gutter, glinting. Once, horribly, a lost child's shoe in the reeds by the canal, and the memory of what came after that discovery was part of why he'd spent years pretending he had no gift at all.

But now the needle swung. Now it pointed at a person.

At Aldric, thrashing, lost—not lost *somewhere*, but lost *inside himself*, gone down into some dark cellar of his own history with the door slammed behind him. And Tam could feel the shape of that darkness the way he could feel the shape of a key under floorboards. Could feel, more than feel, *where* Aldric was, in the way you know where your own hand is in the dark.

Tam crossed the room before he'd decided to.

He knelt on the other side of Aldric from Marta and put his hand on the man's shoulder, and he did the only thing his gift knew how to do. He reached along the thread of lostness and he called, silently, the way you call to something you've found: *Here. You're here. This is where you are.*

Aldric stopped screaming.

His eyes opened—grey, unfocused, swimming—and for a moment they fixed on Tam's face with terrible clarity, and Tam thought: *he sees where he is now, and he wishes he didn't.* Then the clarity softened. Aldric's breathing slowed. His hand came up and gripped Tam's wrist, hard, and held it the way a drowning man holds a rope, and after a while the grip gentled into something that was just holding.

"There you are," Tam whispered, without knowing he was going to say it. "Found you."

Aldric slept. Really slept, deep and even, his face slack and young-looking, decades younger than it had been all week. The hearth-fire settled into a low, contented roar. Outside the rain kept applauding.

Marta looked at Tam across the sleeping man for a long moment.

"That's new," she said.

"He was lost," Tam said. His voice came out strange, thin. "I find lost things. I didn't—I didn't know people counted."

Marta reached over and put her flour-dusted hand briefly on his head, like a blessing, like a weight. "Everything counts," she said. "Everything that's lost is somebody. That's what the crown will never understand. They think gifts are tools. They're not tools. They're ways of loving."

* * *

He told Elodie the next morning, out by the well, because Elodie was the only one he wanted to tell first.

Elodie listened the whole way through without interrupting, hands busy with the whetstone and a carving knife they'd taken to maintaining obsessively since the flight south—a nervous habit, keeping the blade sharp for reasons nobody said aloud. When Tam finished, Elodie was quiet for a long moment, stone rasping against steel.

"People," Elodie said finally.

"People," Tam confirmed.

"And it worked. You pulled him back."

"He pulled himself back. I just—I showed him where the rope was." Tam kicked at a pebble. "Elodie. What if it's not finding objects? What if it was never finding objects? What if objects were just the easy thing, the practice thing, and the real gift is—" He gestured helplessly at the whole world, at the shutters and the hidden folk and the burning cities somewhere north of them. "Finding *people*. People who are lost."

Elodie set down the whetstone.

Something moved across their face that Tam had never seen there before—hope, actual hope, naked and unguarded, and it looked almost like pain because Elodie had clearly gotten out of the habit.

"Do you understand what that means?" Elodie said. "Do you understand what you might be able to do? Tam. There are gifted people being 'remanded' all over the kingdom right now. Carted off to places nobody names. Families that don't know where they went. If you can find a lost person—if you can *feel* them—"

"Then I could find them," Tam said slowly. The idea landing in him piece by piece, heavy and enormous. "The taken ones. Wherever they're kept."

"If you got strong enough," Elodie said. "If you learned to listen deeper." They caught themselves, and Tam watched the ledger-keeper come back into their face, cautious, weighing. "I'm getting ahead of myself. You're fifteen and exhausted and we're fugitives. But Tam—write this day down somewhere in your head. Mark it. Because I think this is why you're in this story. I think the gift found you for a reason, and I don't think the reason was keys."

Tam laughed, shaky. Then, because the thought wouldn't leave: "Do you think the king heard about it? The bound king, in the bone. Do you think that's why he—"

He didn't finish, because Elodie had gone very still.

"What," Tam said.

"Nothing." Elodie picked the whetstone back up, too quickly. "It's nothing. Eat your breakfast."

But Tam had lived with Elodie long enough to know what *nothing* meant, and he spent the rest of the morning chewing on it along with his never-molding bread, turning the thought over and over: that the dead king's voice, whispering through fingerbone and firelight, had chosen its listeners carefully. That maybe it hadn't just needed someone who could hear the dead.

Maybe it had needed someone who could find the lost. And maybe the lost it most wanted found weren't people at all.

Maybe they were pieces.

* * *

The knock came on the ninth evening, just past dark, while Marta was ladling out stew and the twins were arguing about whether rain counted as weather or as water's opinion.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Polite as a tax collector.

Marta's ladle stopped mid-air. The sweeping man appeared in the yard doorway with his broom held like a spear. And down the table, Sera went quiet in a way Tam had learned to fear—she set down her spoon, touched Elodie's knee under the board, and rose without a word, moving toward the shuttered window with the soundless steps of a woman who had spent her whole life being somewhere else a moment ago.

Through the gap in the slats, Sera looked. Tam watched her shoulders change.

"Well?" Marta breathed.

Sera turned back. Her face was composed, which was how Tam knew it was bad. "Two riders," she said. "Grey cloaks. Court grey." Her eyes found Elodie's, then Tam's, then Marta's. "They're asking after travelers from the north. And they have a list."

The hearth-fire, behind them, dimmed—just slightly, just at the edges, like a held breath.

Marta set down the ladle. She untied her apron, folded it once, and laid it on the table with the deliberateness of a woman putting down a tool she would need again very soon.

"Aldric," she said quietly. "Take the children down the root-cellar. Sweeper—yard door, bolt it. And somebody wake the tinker; we'll be wanting his back gate." She looked around the room, at every hidden face, and Tam saw her do the arithmetic of love, quick and merciless, weighing who could be saved and how.

Then her eyes landed on Tam, and stayed there.

"You," she said. "The boy who finds people." Something kind and terrible moved through her expression. "I have a sister in the crown's custody. Three years now. No body, no grave, no answer. If you get out of this—if you live, and learn, and grow strong in that gift of yours—you find her. That's my price for tonight. That's what this house costs."

Outside, the knocking came again. Patient. Grey.

Tam's gift stirred behind his breastbone like a compass needle waking—and pointed, faint and far and unmistakable, toward the capital.

"Yes," he said.
