## 10. Grey Gloves at the Door

The wayhouse smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and bread that would never mold, and for three days I let myself believe that was the smell of safety.

We had come in out of the rain on a Tuesday—Tam soaked to the ribs, Sera's face grey with road-weariness, my hands still shaking from the checkpoint—and Maren, the keeper, had taken one look at us and asked no questions. That was the first miracle of the place. The second was the hearth.

I noticed it that first night, sitting closest to the fire because I was coldest. The flames leaned toward me when I shivered. When Tam dropped his spoon, the fire flicked a tongue of heat across the floor toward it like a dog fetching. I looked up and found Maren watching me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of someone deciding whether you're family or threat.

"Hearth remembers," she said simply, when she caught me staring. "My mother's gift. She fed it into these stones before the hunt came north. Now it keeps whoever's cold." She shrugged, as if she'd described a leaky roof. "It's not much."

"It's everything," Tam said, and meant it, and Maren looked at him the way Quill used to look at me over the ledger—like something long-buried had surfaced and was breathing.

That was the wayhouse of small miracles. A woman whose sourdough never spoiled. A boy who slept without nightmares because the straw in his pallet had been charmed by some grandmother two generations dead. A cellar where bottled light glowed soft as candlefat. People who had hidden their gifts so long they'd nearly forgotten them, trading tiny kindnesses like smuggled coin—here, take this; it does nothing much; keep warm; sleep well.

I kept the ledger of it in my head. Not on paper—Sera had burned enough paper to last us all a lifetime—but in the bone-deep habit of record-keeping. *Maren, keeper. Gift: none of her own, but custodian of her mother's hearth. Hedda, baker. Ossik, stableman, can quiet any animal. Seven souls under this roof, four of them gifted, all of them pretending otherwise.*

I should have known better than anyone: ledgers get found.

* * *

It was the smoke, later, that told me. But before the smoke there was the dream.

I dreamed of the chorus—the dead voices I carried, the ones that lived in the marrow I carved and sometimes, uninvited, in my own skull. They had been quieter since we fled the city, muffled by distance from anything they'd touched. But that night they came back all at once, loud as a market bell, and every one of them said the same word:

*South. South. South.*

I woke in the dark with my heart slamming. The hearth in the common room below had gone strange—not its usual steady amber but a hard white, guttering sideways against a draft that shouldn't exist, because every shutter was closed.

I went down the stairs quiet as I could, which is very quiet, bone-carver's hands. Sera was already in the common room, dressed, knife out, standing in the doorway with her ear to the night.

"Riders," she breathed. "Four. Maybe five. They've doused their lanterns."

"The grey gloves?"

"I didn't see gloves." Her jaw tightened. "I saw the way they ride. Like men who've never been cold or wet or hungry in their lives and resent the road for reminding them."

Above us, floorboards creaked. Tam, awake. Good—I didn't have time to decide whether that was good.

"Maren," I called softly, and the keeper came out of her kitchen wiping flour from her hands, calm as Sunday. When Sera told her what was in the yard, Maren didn't panic. She nodded once, the way a woman nods at weather.

"Cellar," she said. "There's a door behind the ale racks. Goes out under the pigsty. Take the south field, not the lane—lane's got sightlines." She was already moving as she talked, banking the hearth, waking Hedda, rousing the others. "You three especially. You're the ones they want."

"We're not leaving you to answer for us," Sera said.

"You're not answering for yourselves either, girl, you're running, which is different." Maren smiled, and it was a good smile, crooked and unhurried. "Fifty years I've kept this house. Fifty years the crown never knocked. Whatever's coming through that door tonight, it isn't looking for me." She pressed a cloth bundle into my arms—bread, cheese, a flask. "Go. The hearth will cover your tracks. It remembers how."

I want to write here that I hesitated nobly, that I argued. What I actually did was obey, because Sera was already herding me toward the cellar and obedience was faster than courage. We went down into the cold dark among the ale racks, Tam's hand fisted in my sleeve, and Maren pulled the door shut above us, and the last thing I heard from the world above was her voice, easy and ordinary, calling out: "Coming! Who knocks at this hour?"

Then the trapdoor, then earth between us and whatever happened next.

* * *

What happened next, I pieced together from sound.

The cellar was black and close and smelled of hops and stone. Above us, muffled, the front door opened. Boots on floorboards. A voice I knew—that smooth court-agent voice, the one from the shop in the city, the man with the grey gloves who had smiled while asking after bones that speak.

"Well, mistress. A fine establishment. Warm, even. Unusually warm."

Maren answered him with a yokel's slowness I could have kissed her for. "Fire's been lit since my mother's day, lordship. Doesn't go out."

"No. I imagine it doesn't." Paper rustled. "We're looking for travelers. Three of them. A woman, a youth, and—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—a bone-carver. Perhaps you've heard the proclamation? All gifted persons are to be registered. There is a reward for information leading to registration." He pronounced *registration* the way other men pronounce *execution*, and perhaps he knew it, and perhaps he enjoyed it.

"Travelers pass through," Maren said. "Don't mark their faces."

"No," the agent agreed pleasantly. "But hearths remember, don't they? I've read about such things. Fire fed a gift keeps a record of everyone it has warmed. Faces. Names, if it was given them." His boots crossed the room—I tracked him by sound, each step a nail in my chest. "Show me your fire, mistress."

And Maren—brave, foolish, magnificent Maren—said: "It's just a fire."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Then something heavy hit the floorboards, and Hedda screamed, and Tam lunged for the ladder and I caught him around the chest and held him with everything I had, my hand clamped over his mouth, his whole body shaking with the effort not to run back up into the noise.

Because the noise was fire. Real fire now, not the hearth—the agent's men had done something, oil or torch or worse, and the wayhouse of small miracles began to die above us in crackling increments. Through the seams of the trapdoor came orange light. Smoke crawled down the cellar stairs like something living, feeling for us.

"The records," the agent's voice said, raised now over the roar, still infuriatingly level. "Burn it all. And bring the keeper. She'll remember for us, one way or another."

* * *

We came out through the pigsty door into rain and ran.

No discussion, no plan—we ran because staying was dying, south across the plowed field with the burning wayhouse lighting our backs like a second dawn. Tam sobbed as he ran, not quietly, and I couldn't blame him; I wanted to sob too. Behind us the hearth-stones, even as the house collapsed around them, did the last thing they knew how to do: the fire bent away from our path, leaning north, drawing the smoke and the searchers' eyes backward while we stumbled south into the dark.

Hedda made it out. I saw her shape ahead of us, and Ossik's, and two others. Five souls from seven, running blind through wet barley.

Maren did not make it out.

* * *

We walked until the fire was a smudge and the smudge was nothing, until dawn came up thin and grey over the salt marshes, and then Sera called a halt in the lee of a ruined windmill and we stood there dripping and shaking and counting ourselves like coins.

"They took her," Tam said. It wasn't a question. He'd seen it too—hearths remember, and so did he, the way children remember, totally and forever.

"They took her because of us," I said, and my voice came out wrong, flat and hot at once. "Because we slept under her roof. Because hiding near her was safer than hiding anywhere else, and we knew it, and we did it anyway."

"Elodie." Sera's voice cut clean. "Say the true thing or say nothing."

I looked at her. Rain ran off her chin. "The cost of hiding is the cost of fighting," I said. "Quill told me that once, about debts. I thought it was bookkeeping. It's not. Every day we run, someone pays for us. First the checkpoint guard's favor. Now Maren. How many more roofs burn before we stop being the thing that burns them?"

Nobody answered. The windmill groaned.

And then—because grief in me has always opened doors I'd rather stay shut—the chorus spoke.

It came up through my feet, through the marsh ground itself, faint as a voice through a wall: an old woman's tone, dry and flinty, unhurried even now. *Maren.* Not dead—I would know, I thought, I would feel the difference—but taken, bound somewhere with iron between her gift and the air, and yet some residue of her had soaked into the stones of her own hearth, into the bread-flour dust of her kitchen, into the fifty years of warmth this region of earth remembered, and from that residue her voice reached me like light from a star already moved on.

*"South,"* Maren's echo said. *"You're going the wrong direction to stand there crying, girl. South. Two miles, there's a drainage cut crosses the marsh—the grey gloves won't ford it, their boots are too good. Follow the cut till you smell peat smoke. There's a fen-man named Brack owes me for forty years of bread that doesn't mold. Tell him the hearth sent you."*

A pause, and then, softer, with something that might have been laughter in it:

*"And tell them they didn't burn it all. Stones don't burn. My mother's gift is in the stones, and stones scatter, and wherever a piece of that hearth ends up, it will still keep whoever's cold. You can't harvest what gets given away."*

I told them. My voice shook and I let it. Tam stared at me, then at the southern horizon, and something in his tear-swollen face hardened into a shape I hadn't seen before—something with edges.

"She's alive," he said. "In a cart or a cell or a collar, but alive. Which means we can find her." He looked at his own hands. "I found the lost spoon. I found Sera's ring when she dropped it in the harbor. Maybe I can find people. Maybe that's the real gift, and the lost things were just practice."

Sera and I exchanged a look over his head—the look of two people realizing simultaneously that the child had just volunteered for war.

"After Meridian," Sera said. "First sanctuary, then rescue. In that order. I don't spend lives I can't afford."

"That's not—" Tam started.

"That's exactly how it is," Sera said, and for once her pragmatism sounded like tenderness, because it was. "She stayed so we could run. The best way to honor that is to arrive somewhere worth having run to. Then we come back with more than knives."

* * *

We followed the drainage cut south. The grey gloves did not follow—they were too busy sifting ashes for bone, I imagine, and finding only hearthstones, which do not burn and cannot be registered and hold nothing the crown can read.

By midmorning we smelled peat smoke and found Brack, a broad silent man in a coracle, who heard the words *the hearth sent you* and looked at each of us for a long moment before pushing off from the bank and jerking his head aboard. No questions. Small miracles recognize each other.

As the marsh slid past, I sat in the bottom of the coracle with my palms flat against the gunwale, and Maren's echo murmured in the wood and water, patient as bread rising, keeping me company the way she'd kept strangers warm for fifty years.

They think gifts are things you can confiscate, that echo said, or maybe I only thought it and lent it her voice because I needed to hear it in someone's voice but mine. Register them, collar them, burn the houses they sleep in. But a gift spent freely doesn't stay where you left it. It moves. It multiplies. It's in the bread and the stones and the boy who wants to find people instead of spoons.

You can't harvest what gets given away.

I watched the south open ahead of us—grey water, then green shore, then, days distant, the towers of the free cities—and felt something settle in my chest that hadn't been there since the proclamation: not peace, nothing like peace. Purpose. The rage had stopped being a fire consuming me and become a forge.

They had taken Maren. Fine. We would take back a king.

"Meridian by week's end," Sera said at the tiller, eyes on the horizon. "If the council there won't shelter us, no one will."

"And if half of them want to sell us to the crown?" I asked.

"Then we sell them something first," Sera said, "and we make sure ours is worth more."

Behind us, far to the north, the wayhouse was ash. Ahead of us, everything else.

The chorus murmured southward, and for the first time, I murmured with it.
