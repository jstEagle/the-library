## 11. The Free Cities

The sea announced Meridian an hour before we saw it.

I smelled it first—tar and brine and woodsmoke, the particular stink of ten thousand people who had decided that no king would ever tell them what to do again. Then the gulls, screaming over our heads like they were paid to. Then, rounding the last headland of the salt road, the city itself: white walls stacked on white walls climbing a hill that fell straight into the harbor, tier upon tier, every rooftop tiled in colors that would have gotten a man arrested in the capital. Red roofs. Blue roofs. One entire district roofed in gold leaf that caught the morning sun and threw it back like an insult.

"They'll tax you for looking at that," I said.

Tam laughed. Elodie didn't. They'd been quiet for three days, the two of them, walking close together the way people do after a fire—checking each other's hands, each other's faces, making sure nothing else was missing.

The keeper's name was still written on the inside of my wrist. I'd copied it off the wayhouse registry before we ran, because Sera Voss does not leave names behind. *Maren Ostrey.* Somewhere north of here, in a crown dungeon or a crown grave, a woman who fed us bread and lies about the safety of small towns was paying for her kindness. Every step toward Meridian was a step away from her. I carried both facts the way I carry everything: quietly, in a place where they couldn't spill.

"Rules," I said, stopping them on the last stretch of road, where pilgrims and carters and smugglers funneled together toward the Southgate. "We are merchants out of Corvale. Cloth trade, failed, come to buy dyes. Tam, you're my nephew. Elodie—"

"Your clerk," Elodie said. "I know. You've said it four times."

"I've said it four times because the fourth time is when it sticks." I looked at them both. "This isn't the capital. Nobody here will ask about gifts, because half this city has one and the other half doesn't care. But nobody here will protect you either, not unless we can afford it. Everything costs. Remember that."

"Everything costs," Tam repeated solemnly. He was fourteen and carrying everything he owned in a flour sack, and he still managed to make my own words sound like wisdom coming home to roost.

"One more thing." I hesitated. This was the part I'd been rehearsing for three days, and rehearsal hadn't made it easier. "When we get inside—if anyone asks why we left Corvale—we left because of debts. Mine. Not the Gift-Hunt. Not the proclamations. Debts."

"Why lie about that?" Tam asked. "The Hunt's worse than debts."

"The Hunt's a reason to pity us," I said. "Debts are a reason to be careful around us. Pity gets noticed. Carefulness gets ignored. In a city full of eyes, I want to be ignored."

Elodie studied me with those ledger-keeper's eyes that saw columns where other people saw clouds. I could feel the question forming—*what aren't you telling us*—and I started walking before it could get out into the open air.

* * *

Meridian's Southgate was a throat, and we went down it in a crowd of fishwives and dye merchants and one very unhappy camel. The gate guards didn't check papers. They checked purses—a copper apiece to walk through, a silver if you wanted to be remembered kindly, which was the most honest piece of civic philosophy I'd ever encountered.

Inside, the city hit me the way cities do when you've spent weeks in hills and salt marshes: too loud, too fast, too alive. Streets wide enough for six carts, then alleys narrow enough that Tam and I had to walk single file with our shoulders brushing the walls. Guild banners hung from every third window—dyers' guild, chandlers' guild, the great chain-and-anchor of the Harborwrights—and beneath them, chalked on doorposts in a dozen hands, little marks I recognized from my old life. Smuggler's signs. *Safe house. Fair prices. Friend of friends.*

And underneath all of it, everywhere, the strait. You felt the water before you saw it, a cold breath between buildings, and when the streets opened up at last onto the harborfront there it was: three miles of grey-green sea, and beyond it, hazy with distance, the far shore.

The usurper's shore.

They'd built watchtowers along the breakwater, and bonfire platforms above those, and beyond the platforms the whole waterfront bristled with the masts of war galleys under construction—the knock of caulking mallets so constant it had become the city's heartbeat. Across the water, faint as smoke, I could see the campfires of the army gathering. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands.

"They're really going to come," Tam said quietly.

"Armies don't cross water for fun," I said. "Somebody down here insulted somebody up there, or thinks they did. Or—" and I didn't finish it, because the rest was obvious. The usurper had finished with the north. The gifted there were registered, remanded, burned. There was only one direction left for a hunt to grow.

Elodie stood at the harbor rail a long time, watching the far shore. I watched them watch it, and I thought about the fingerbone, and the dead-not-dead king whose voice lived in it now somewhere against Elodie's skin, wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of their pack. Three fugitives and a royal ghost, come to ask a city for sanctuary.

"We need the Ledger Council," I said. "And I know exactly how much that's going to cost me."

* * *

The Council of Ledgers met in a hall built like a strongbox—no windows at street level, one door of black ironwood, guards who wore no livery because in Meridian the guilds were the state and states don't advertise. We waited three hours on a bench in an antechamber that smelled of ink and tallow, long enough for Tam to fall asleep against Elodie's shoulder and for me to memorize the antechamber's exits, of which there were two, one of them behind a tapestry, because I am who I am.

Then a clerk with ink-stained cuffs called us in, and I got my first look at power, Meridian style.

Nine people sat at a crescent of tables. No throne, no canopy, no crown—not even a ceremonial chain of office. Just nine chairs, nine ledgers, and nine pairs of eyes that had spent decades pricing things. A shipwright with forearms like dock rope. A woman in dyer's red with rings on every finger. An old man whose bald pate bore the tattooed sigils of the Chandlers' Compact. And at the center, neither oldest nor grandest but somehow obviously first among them, a spare, grey woman with a face like a closed account.

"Sera Voss," she read from a slip. "Of the Ember Guild, Corvale. Fence, appraiser, and"—her eyes came up—"keeper of certain accounts of unusual character."

"You have my file," I said.

"We have everyone's file. That's rather the point of a ledger city." She folded the slip away. "You're asking for letters of sanctuary. Protection under guild law. Residence, work rights, non-extradition."

"I'm asking for all of that for three people. Two of whom are children by your law."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange I offer services. Appraisal. Authentication. I can tell a forged relic from a true one faster than any expert on your payroll, and I can move goods through channels your own factors can't find."

The shipwright snorted. "Every fence who washes up on our docks offers the same. We're not short of fences, Mistress Voss. We're short of reasons."

"Then let me give you one." I'd known this moment would come since the checkpoint, since before the checkpoint, since the night I burned names out of Quill's ledger while Tam asked his terrible innocent questions. I'd hoped it wouldn't come. Hope is a luxury good and I stopped buying it years ago.

But the council sat silent, waiting, and across the strait an army was gathering, and Elodie was standing beside me holding a dead king's bone, and there was only one thing in my possession rare enough to buy what we needed.

"My real name," I said.

The dyer-woman's rings clicked against the table as she leaned forward.

"Not Sera Voss—that's the third name I wore, and I wore it well, but it's bought and paid for and worth exactly what it cost. I mean the first one. The one I was born under, in the capital, twenty-nine years ago. The one I buried so thoroughly that even I sometimes forget it existed." I kept my voice level, the voice I used for quoting prices. "I'm the daughter of Aldric Vane."

The silence changed quality. Even Tam, waking groggy against Elodie's shoulder, felt it and sat up straight.

Aldric Vane. Lord Chancellor to the old king. The man whose signature was on the founding charter of the Gift-Hunt's predecessor laws. The man who, according to every history taught in the capital, died the same week the old king died—traitor's death, traitor's grave, his family stripped of name and lands. The usurper's first victim, the histories said. The usurper's first *proof*, the whisperers said. The man who knew too much about how the new king came to wear the crown.

"That's a claim," the grey woman said carefully, "that would be extremely valuable. To several parties. Including parties across the strait."

"I know."

"If it's true, you're not a refugee. You're a weapon. Blood testimony, living memory, direct knowledge of the succession—you could stand before this council, or any court in the free cities, and testify to what happened in the palace the night the old king died. If you *saw* something. If you *know* something."

Here it was, then. The exact shape of the trap I'd walked myself into, priced to the penny.

"I know what my father knew," I said. "I know what he told me the night he sent me away with a purse of gold and a new name and instructions never to come home. I know why the histories call it a traitor's death when there was no trial. And I will give all of it to this council—name, testimony, bloodline, everything—in exchange for sanctuary for these three, sworn and sealed under guild law."

"Sera." Elodie's voice, low, beside me. Not a protest, exactly. A question shaped like a name.

"It's fine," I said, which was a lie, and Elodie—who had heard the dead speak and knew what lies sounded like coming out of living mouths—let it go anyway, which was its own kind of mercy.

The council conferred. Actually conferred, heads together, voices dropped, for a long enough time that Tam whispered, "Is that good or bad?" and I said, "With committees, there's no difference."

When the grey woman turned back, her face was as closed as ever, but she'd taken out a fresh sheet of paper.

"Terms," she said. "Sanctuary, sealed, for the three of you. Non-extradition to the crown, per guild law, which means they'd have to take this city wall by wall to reach you. Residence and work rights within the Dyer's Quarter, initially. In exchange: your full testimony, recorded under seal, delivered when and as this council requires. Your bloodline entered into the Great Ledger. And your continued availability." Her eyes were flat and unblinking. "You understand that once your name is in our books, Mistress Vane, you become an asset of the Ledger Council. Assets don't leave without settlement of accounts."

"I've never once left without settling accounts," I said. "It's practically my religion."

She wrote. The clerk brought forward the great book—I got a glimpse of it, chained to its stand, pages soft as cloth from a century of hands—and the pen scratched, and just like that I was someone else again. Third name buried. Fourth name entered. *Sera Vane.* It sounded strange even in my own head, like a coat that fit better and weighed more.

The wax was green, guild-green, and when it set on the page I felt something in my chest loosen and ache at the same time, the way a fist feels when you finally open it.

Twenty-nine years keeping my father's secret. Twenty-nine minutes to sell it.

Freedom, it turns out, is also a price. I paid it quietly, the way I pay for everything.

* * *

They gave us a room in the Dyer's Quarter above a shop that sold, of all things, pigments ground from imported stone—a little cave of saffron and lapis smell, two beds and a pallet, a window overlooking a courtyard where someone was always washing something. Tam claimed the bed by the window immediately, on the principle that the fastest person chooses first, and was asleep before his boots were fully off.

Elodie sat on the other bed, unwrapping the oilcloth bundle just enough to confirm the fingerbone was safe, then wrapping it again. Ritual, not necessity. I'd seen them do it every night since the wayhouse.

"Tell me what it cost," Elodie said. Not looking up. "Exactly. I keep accounts too."

I almost smiled. "You wouldn't believe the number."

"Try me. I balance trades in bottled storms."

So I sat down across from them and told it plainly, the way I'd told the council. My father. The chancellor. The night he put me on a horse. What he'd said—or part of what he'd said; some of it I gave to the council sealed, and some of it I've never given to anyone, and won't, until I understand it myself. He'd been afraid. That's the heart of it, the part I keep turning over. Aldric Vane, the most powerful man in the kingdom after the king himself, had held my face in his cold chancellor's hands and been *afraid*, and said, *It wears him, Sera. It wears the crown and it wears him, and someday it will look for me, and when it looks for me it will look for you.*

I'd thought he was mad. I was eleven. Mad fathers were easier than true ones.

"And now the council owns that," Elodie said quietly.

"The council rents it," I corrected. "Ownership implies I could get it back." I rubbed the inside of my wrist, where Maren Ostrey's name was written in fading ink. "Listen. Whatever happens next—they'll call the testimony soon, maybe days. When they do, everything my father told me becomes public property, and the usurper learns that Aldric Vane's daughter is alive and talking. After that, there's no more hiding anywhere. Not in Meridian, not past it."

"So why sell it?" Elodie asked. Genuine question, no judgment. They'd never learned judgment; Quill raised them too gentle for it.

Because Maren Ostrey burned. Because Tam ran toward the registration office and I will spend the rest of my life making sure nothing like that night ever catches him again. Because hiding cost a wayhouse its keeper and it would eventually cost us everything anyway, slowly, in installments.

"Because we're done paying rent on fear," I said. "If freedom has a price, I'd rather buy it once, outright, and own the deed."

Elodie nodded slowly, and for a moment we just sat in the pigment-smelling dark, two people good at prices pretending the arithmetic balanced.

Then Tam stirred in his sleep and said, clearly, the way he says things his gift sends up from wherever it lives in him:

"There's a piece of it under the tide-temple. It's been singing all day. I thought it was the harbor bells."

He rolled over and kept sleeping.

Elodie and I looked at each other across the lamplight, and outside, far off across the strait, the army fires were coming up like a second dawn.
