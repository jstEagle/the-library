## 12. Council of Ledgers

The council chamber of Meridian had been a tide-temple once, before the guilds bought the harbor and the gods went out of fashion. You could still see it in the bones of the building—columns carved with waves, a floor of pale stone that the morning light turned to shallow water. Nine seats stood in a crescent at the far end, and nine people sat in them wearing the faces of merchants who had never lost an argument in their lives.

Elodie counted them the way they'd taught her to count stock: quickly, by value. Four wore silk cut in the southern style, loose and practical, salt-stained at the cuffs—ship owners. Two wore grey, which meant law. One was a soldier past her prime with a scar that ran ear to collar like a seam someone had given up mending. The last two were old men with the soft hands of men who had never carried anything heavier than a pen.

Nine votes. That was what freedom looked like here: nine people deciding whether strangers deserved to keep their heads.

"They're waiting on us," Sera murmured. She stood at Elodie's shoulder, dressed in her best—which was to say her most forgettable—clothes, hair pinned flat, every ring removed but one. Tam fidgeted at her other side, scrubbed raw for the occasion, his collar scratching his neck the way collars always scratched him when it mattered.

"The herald said mid-bell," Elodie said.

"The herald said mid-bell yesterday too." Sera's mouth did the thing it did when she was pricing a room full of people. "They're making us stand. It's a tactic."

"Everything is a tactic with you."

"Yes," Sera said pleasantly. "That's why I'm rich."

A door opened behind the crescent of seats—not the great doors they'd entered through, but a small one, a servant's door—and a clerk came out with an armful of ledgers that he stacked on a side table without ceremony. Elodie watched the stack the way she might have watched a stranger handle a child. Those were council records. Those were *kept* records. She could tell from across the room that the binding was cheap string and the ink would fade within ten years, and something in her chest tightened with a contempt so familiar she had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a stone in your shoe.

*Focus*, she told herself. *You're not here to judge their filing.*

The center seat—the ninth, set slightly forward, the seat of the Speaker—was empty.

* * *

They waited through two bells. Nobody offered chairs. A servant brought water in cups so small the gesture was an insult dressed as courtesy, and Tam drank three of his and Sera pretended not to notice, and Elodie held hers untouched because her hands wanted something to do and a cup could be put down slowly instead of dropped.

It was during the third bell that she heard the dead.

She had been hearing them since the fingerbone—since the night the old king's voice first came up through bone like heat through a floor—but in the weeks since Meridian the chorus had grown. Not louder, exactly. *Closer*. As if the dead of the free cities had been standing at a distance, curious, and had begun, one by one, to drift toward her like gulls toward a boat that might feed them.

Now, in the council chamber, they were everywhere. The tide-temple columns held centuries of prayers pressed into the stone by hands that had never owned chisels—a beggar's plea for rain, a mother's tally of children, a name repeated so many times it had worn smooth. Under the floor, older still: the temple's founders, arguing about drainage, absurdly, eternally. And near the small servant's door, fresher, sharper—a man who had died there not five years ago, a clerk by the smell of the ink still clinging to his ghost, muttering numbers into the dark because even dead he could not stop reconciling.

Elodie closed her eyes for half a breath and let it all wash over her, cataloguing the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how she kept the voices from becoming drowning.

Then the small door opened again, and the Speaker came in, and the dead nearest Elodie went quiet, the way birds go quiet before weather.

He was younger than she expected. Forty, perhaps, with the careful tan of a man who sailed for pleasure rather than need, and eyes that moved across the three of them the way Sera's eyes moved across a market—pricing, sorting, dismissing. He carried no ledger himself. Someone else carried his.

"Counsel Winterbourne." He pronounced it correctly, which meant he'd been briefed. "Of the Ember Guild."

"The Ember Guild doesn't exist," Sera said smoothly. "You're thinking of a rumor."

"I'm thinking of a rumor with a very good reputation among people whose reputations are also rumors." He sat, and the other eight sat with him, a ripple down the crescent. "You've asked for this audience. The hour is yours. Spend it well."

Sera stepped forward, and Elodie watched her do the thing only Sera could do: make surrender look like strategy. "Honored council. You know why we've come. Across the strait, the crown has finished its harvest in the north. Every gifted soul registered, remanded, and—" she paused precisely, letting the word land where everyone already knew it lived, "—spent. Their armies gather at the shore. Their ships are being counted in hundreds. We bring testimony as to *why*. What the crown is. What it eats. What it will eat next."

"We know what it eats," said one of the silk-cuffs, a woman with rings worth more than the guild hall. "It eats the north. The north is not our problem until it swims."

"And when it swims?" Tam blurted.

Every eye found him. Elodie's stomach dropped, but Tam, red-eared, held his ground, chin up, hands jammed in his pockets where he always hid them when frightened—as if his gift might leak out through his fingers.

"When it swims," the woman repeated, "we will negotiate. Cities have negotiated with crowns before. Crowns want taxes. We have taxes."

"It doesn't want taxes," Elodie said.

The words came out harder than she intended, and the chamber's attention swung to her, and she felt it—the old, awful pressure of rooms full of people who had decided what she was before she spoke. She had spent her life behind ledgers, behind counters, behind the polite fiction of the guild's invisibility. She was good at being unheard. She was not good at being heard.

But the king's voice was in her ears now, patient, weary, *tell them, carver, tell them what I could not*, and she stepped forward beside Sera and did not look at any of the nine. She looked at the space above the empty tenth chair, the one that had never been filled, the one left vacant—she'd learned this from the clerk's ghost—in honor of a compact none of them fully remembered.

"My name is Elodie Winterbourne," she said. "I am a bone-carver by trade. I hear the dead. That makes me, under your new treaty language with the crown, exactly the kind of person you are considering selling."

Silence. Good. Silence was a ledger too; you could read what people wrote in it.

"I'm not asking for your pity. I'm asking you to listen to testimony. Because three nights ago, in the tide-vaults beneath this city, my apprentice found something. A piece of regalia. Bone, ancient, bound. And inside it—"

"You'll forgive me." The Speaker raised one hand, mild as milk. "Inside a bone. Voices."

"Voices. Yes."

"You understand," he said, and his voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't, "that this is the entire difficulty. The crown claims the gifted can read minds. Forge memories. Whisper lies into honest ears. And now a self-declared gifted woman stands before this council asking it to go to war on the strength of what a bone told her."

* * *

What followed was an hour of the worst kind of arithmetic.

They questioned her politely, which was to say they flayed her politely. They wanted sources; she could not give them, because the source was a voice only she could hear. They wanted corroboration; Tam testified about the flooded vaults, the singing bone, the grey gloves that had chased them through the dark, and one of the law-greys noted, gently, that a frightened boy's recollection in a submerged cellar was not evidence. They wanted the bone itself; Sera produced it wrapped in oilcloth, and the council's own scholars turned it over and pronounced it *old*, which everyone agreed proved nothing except its age.

"It's a forgery," said the second old man at last, with the satisfaction of a man closing a balanced book. "An elaborate one. The northern refugees are desperate and desperate people forge desperately. This 'binding,' this ancient pact wearing the crown—it's a story. A good one. I almost admire it."

"It's not a story." Tam's voice cracked on it. "I *found* it. I heard it sing—"

"A boy heard singing in a cave," the old man said, not unkindly, which was its own cruelty. "Children hear songs in caves."

And Elodie felt it rise in her—not anger exactly, or not only anger, but the specific fury of a keeper of records watching people burn an archive. Because she had spent her whole life understanding that truth was not a thing you *felt*. Truth was a thing you *kept*. Ink and string and witness. And here was the trap she had walked into with open eyes: the truest thing she knew was the one thing that could not be written down. The king's testimony lived only in her skull, in the chorus, in the place these nine reasonable people had already ruled inadmissible.

She thought of Quill then, suddenly, sharply—Quill somewhere on the northern roads with his guilt and his plan, Quill who had once told her, drunk on festival wine, *the difference between a merchant and a liar is a signature, girl*. She had laughed then. She didn't laugh now.

"May I try one more thing?" she said.

The Speaker gestured: *spend freely*.

She unwrapped the regalia piece herself. Up close it was ugly—yellowed bone banded with metal gone black, carved all over with script so fine it looked at a glance like grain. The council scholars leaned back from it as one, and Elodie noted that too, filed it away: *they fear it, whatever they say.*

"This is a record," she said. "Not mine. His." She laid her palm flat against the bone, and let herself listen, really listen, for the first time not to hide the hearing but to *use* it, to turn it outward like a lamp.

The chorus surged. And she spoke—not her words, never her words, but his, the old king's, the ones he had pressed into the bone across centuries of captivity, the ones she had heard fragment by fragment since the fingerbone and assembled like a carver assembling a hand from scattered knuckles:

*"I was Anselm. I was crowned in the Hall of Reeds with my brother holding the sword. I ruled nineteen winters. On the twentieth, my chancellor brought me a gift—a circlet, forged of pact and hunger—and I was fool enough to wear it. It wore me longer. I have watched my kingdom from behind my own eyes. I have felt my people's gifts poured into the thing that sits my throne. I am not dead. I am collateral. And I am asking—"*

Her voice broke. Not from grief. From effort. Holding a dead king's voice steady in a living throat while nine pairs of skeptical eyes watched was like carrying water in cupped hands across a courtyard.

"—I am asking the free cities not to save *me*. I am beyond saving. I am asking you to believe that the enemy across the strait cannot be taxed, cannot be negotiated with, because it is not a man. It is a debt. And debts," she finished, her own voice now, ragged, "only ever come due."

Silence again. But it was a different silence. She could feel the difference in the room the way she could feel the difference in a ledger—one balanced, one not.

The ringed ship-owner spoke first. "Impressive. Genuinely." She turned to her colleagues. "And entirely unprovable. She speaks in a dead king's cadence—I've heard players do as much. For all we know, she believes it. Believers are worse than liars; liars can be bought."

"Everything can be bought," Sera said lightly, and got a few grim smiles, and spent the smile like coin, buying a breath of goodwill.

The scarred soldier had not spoken all morning. Now she leaned forward, elbows on knees, and studied Elodie with the flat professional interest of one craftsman examining another's work.

"The vaults," she said. "Under the tide-temple. Your boy found the bone there."

"Yes."

"Sealed since the founding compacts. My predecessors sealed them." She tapped the arm of her chair—the tenth chair, the vacant one. "Nobody alive knows what's in those vaults. Nobody living could have planted a forgery there. So either your bone is genuine—" she shrugged. "Or your boy can walk through stone."

Tam went white. Elodie saw him calculate, saw him look at her, *asking permission* with his whole face, and something in her chest turned over, warm and terrible, because he was learning the family trade: choosing each other.

"He finds things," Elodie said quietly. "Lost things. It's a small gift. He's a child."

"He's an asset," the soldier said. "As are you. Which brings us to the actual question, counsel, and I'll thank the rest of you to stop admiring the scenery." She rose, and the scar made her shadow strange on the wall. "Half this council wants to send envoys and gold and pretend the strait will hold. The other half wants to fight but wants a reason that will hold in front of *their* councils, their fleets, their frightened sailors. What you've brought us today isn't proof. But it's a *story*—and stories win wars when proofs fail. So here is my proposal: we don't vote war. We vote *inquiry*. You get one season, one ship, and one chance to bring back something a skeptic can hold in both hands. Fail, and we sell you to the crown with our compliments. Succeed—"

"Succeed," the Speaker echoed softly, "and we'll consider believing you."

It was not victory. Elodie knew exactly what it was: it was a receipt marked *pending*, and pending receipts had a way of becoming losses. But the soldier caught her eye as the council dissolved into procedural murmur, and gave her a single nod—the nod of one keeper passing custody of something dangerous to another.

* * *

Outside, the harbor wind hit them like a wall, and Tam finally pulled his hands out of his pockets and shook them as if flicking off water.

"I hated that," he announced. "I hated all of it. They looked at me like I was a *thing*."

"They looked at you like you were valuable," Sera said. "Learn the difference. It'll keep you alive."

"It's the same look."

Elodie said nothing. She was looking at the regalia piece in its oilcloth bundle, tucked under her arm, and listening—to the harbor, to the gulls, to the dead of Meridian drifting near her again now that the doors were shut behind them. The chorus had grown again overnight. New voices. Fresher. And beneath them, threading through everything like wire through wax, a whisper she recognized, patient and vast and *closer than it should be*:

*Carver. The third piece wakes. It knows your name now.*

She stopped walking.

"What," Sera said. "What is it."

Elodie looked south, toward the strait, where the horizon held a smudge of smoke that might have been weather and might have been armies. Her arms, her ordinary, ungifted, record-keeper's arms, ached as though she had carried something much heavier than a bundle all day.

"It knows we have the second piece," she said. "And it's coming for it. Not with envoys." She met Sera's eyes, and heard her own voice go flat and certain, the way it did when a ledger finally balanced. "With grey gloves. Tonight, if I'm the reading right. We need to move the bone. And Sera—we need to find the third piece before they do. Because the king just showed me where it is."

Sera's face did its pricing look, fast, thorough, and settled on resolve. "Where?"

"Across the strait," Elodie said. "In the usurper's camp."

Tam made a small sound. The gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their one note, and the dead of Meridian pressed close around Elodie like a crowd at a crossing, whispering, *hurry, hurry*, in a hundred borrowed voices.

The season clock had started. One ship. One chance. And somewhere in the north, on a road she couldn't see, Quill was already walking toward them with a plan, an apology, and a decoy's grave already dug in his mind.
