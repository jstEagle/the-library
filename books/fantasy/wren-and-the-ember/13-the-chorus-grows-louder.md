## 13. The Chorus Grows Louder

Sleep had become a country I visited reluctantly.

I lay in the narrow bed the tide-temple's custodians had given us—a cell that smelled of salt and old incense, with a window slit that framed a sliver of Meridian's harbor lights—and I told myself, as I told myself every night, that tonight would be different. Tonight the dead king would let me rest.

He never did.

It began with the whisper, always the whisper, thin as thread pulled through bone: *Elodie. Elodie. There is something you must see.*

"I've seen it," I murmured to the dark. "I've seen it every night for a week."

*You have seen fragments,* he said. *A hand here. A mouth there. You have seen what I could bear to show you. But you are going to stand in front of the council of ledgers tomorrow and speak for me, and they will ask you what the binding is, and you will say it eats gifts, and they will nod, because that is an answer that costs them nothing.* A pause, and in the pause I felt him gathering himself, the way a carver gathers breath before the first cut. *Tonight I will show you the whole of it. And I will not be gentle, because gentleness now would be a lie, and I have been lied about enough.*

I should have refused. I want to be honest about that, even now—especially now. There was a version of me, the version who kept ledgers and balanced impossible accounts, who understood that some knowledge is a debt you cannot discharge once incurred. That version said: *No. Let me sleep. Let me keep my small ignorance like a coin in my pocket.*

But the other version—the one who had chased Tam through the night market, who had lied to grey gloves, who had watched a wayhouse burn—already knew the truth about ignorance. It is not shelter. It is just a smaller room with the same fire coming.

"Show me," I said.

The cell dissolved.

* * *

I stood on a hillside under a sky the color of unpolished silver, and I knew—I knew the way you know things in dreams—that this was three hundred years ago, and that the grass beneath my feet was real grass, long dead, on a hill whose name had been deliberately scraped from every map and record in the kingdom. The binding had begun by erasing the place where it was made. Even its cradle had been unwritten.

Below me, in a hollow ringed with standing stones, people were singing.

There were nine of them. I counted without meaning to; counting is the one gift I have never been able to put down. Nine figures in robes of undyed wool, standing in a circle around a bier, and on the bier lay a king—not the usurper, not yet, but a dying man, old and spent, his chest barely moving. His true name was spoken then. I heard it. It slid off my memory like water off wax, but I felt its shape: heavy, four syllables, ending in a sound like a door closing.

The nine sang, and their song was beautiful, and that was the first horror of it. I had expected the making of the thing that wore the crown to be ugly—screaming, blood, black rites. Instead it was music. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever heard, and I have heard the dead sing lullabies to children who died in the same fever that took them.

*They believed they were saving him,* the dead king said. He stood beside me now, or the shape of him did—a tall man rendered in the grey-gold light of remembered things, his face still wearing the patience of someone who has had three hundred years to stop being angry and failed completely. *That is how it begins. Not with ambition. With grief. Their king was dying, and they loved him, and love is the raw material of every terrible pact ever struck.*

"What were they doing?" I asked. My voice came out strange in the dream, flat as carved bone.

*Extending him,* he said. *A king is not a man, in those days. A king was a continuity. Borders, laws, the peace that lets farmers plant. If he died, the realm might fracture, and fracture meant war, and war meant famine, and they had all seen famine. So they found another way. They wove his death into a knot and tied the knot to living souls, so that when his body ended, the pattern of him would persist, held together by borrowed light.*

"And the nine?"

*Were the first payment.* He turned to look at me, and his eyes were the worst part—not cruel, not cold, just *tired*, tired down to the sediment of centuries. *Watch. You asked to see the whole of it. Watch what happens when the knot needs tightening.*

The song changed key. I felt it in my teeth, in the marrow the way only a bone-hearer can feel anything. One of the nine—a woman at the northern point of the circle, her voice the highest and clearest—began to shine. There is no other word. Light came up out of her the way heat comes off a stone at dusk, rising through her skin, streaming from her open mouth along with the song, and it flowed across the circle and into the dying king and did not stop flowing.

She sang herself empty. I watched it happen. Her gift—whatever it was, some bright thing of voice or weather or mending, I could not tell—poured out of her until she was a husk holding a shape, and then the shape folded, gently, the way cloth folds, and she went down onto the grass, and the other eight stepped over her without pausing, because the song could not stop, because if the song stopped the king died and everything they loved burned.

*They told themselves she had given her life willingly,* the king said. *And perhaps she had. Willingness does not change the arithmetic.*

"The arithmetic," I repeated.

*One soul buys a season. That was the original price. Nine singers bought nine seasons, and the king lived nine years past his death, and in the ninth year the eight survivors faced the same choice again, and this time they did not need to volunteer, because by then the knot had learned to pull.* His voice dropped, and though he was only memory given voice, the drop in it made the hillside shudder. *Do you understand what I am showing you, bone-carver? The binding was not built. It was *fed*. It is still being fed. Every generation since, it has taken, and taken, and each time it takes, it grows more able to take, and less willing to ask.*

The dream shifted under me like a floorboard.

* * *

Now I stood in a corridor, and the corridor was made of faces.

Not decorated with them—made of them. Walls of pressed souls, thousands deep, each one dimly luminous, each one turned inward toward the center of the structure as if listening to a command none of them could refuse. They were arranged by era. I could read them the way Sera reads a ledger: the oldest strata wore the clothing of three centuries past; above them, generations layered upward, fashions changing, faces changing, the same expression never changing. Hunger behind the eyes. Not their hunger. The hunger of the thing they were holding up.

*This is the crown,* the king said quietly. *You wanted to know what wears it. This. All of this. The usurper is not a demon and not a god. It is a debt that learned to walk. It is every stolen gift braided into a single appetite, wearing the shape of the man who was king, because the shape of the man who was king was the first thing it stole.*

I walked—I floated, dreamed, whatever the dead call walking—down the corridor of faces, and the faces began to notice me.

They could not speak. That was the cruelty of it. Their mouths moved and no sound came, because their voices were among the things that had been harvested, poured into the knot along with everything else they were. But their eyes tracked me, and their hands pressed against the inner surface of the wall, palms out, and I understood suddenly, viscerally, why my own gift had always felt less like hearing voices and more like being *listened to*—because these were my kin, every gifted soul the binding had ever swallowed, and some buried animal part of me had always known the chorus existed, had been waiting for it my whole life the way an orphan waits at a window.

One face pressed closer than the others. A young man, maybe Tam's age, with a farmer's sunburn and a finding-gift—I recognized the flavor of it instantly, the same warm compass-hum that lived in Tam. His mouth shaped words over and over, silent, urgent, and I leaned close and read his lips because reading lips is just another kind of listening:

*Tell them we are not gone. Tell them we can still be pulled back. The knot holds us, but it does not digest us. As long as the regalia is broken, we are recoverable. As long as—*

The wall between eras shuddered, and his face sank back into the press of souls, and the king's voice came again, lower now, grim as a closing ledger.

*You see why the hunt matters. You see it now.*

"The hunt," I said. My own voice sounded very far away. "The registration. The remandings."

*Ask yourself what the crown does with the gifted it takes,* he said. *The criers say remanded. The criers do not say where. Grey gloves do not escort prisoners to dungeons, bone-carver. Dungeons hold bodies, and bodies are not what the binding eats.* He let that sit in the silence for a moment, and the silence was the worst room I had ever stood in. *Every gifted soul the hunt delivers is another strand in the braid. Another face in the wall. The usurper is not hunting the gifted out of fear. Fear does not organize itself this efficiently. The hunt is a harvest, and the harvest has been running for three hundred years, and your generation is simply the crop that ripened late.*

I thought of Tam. Small Tam, with his lost-and-found hum, who had run toward the registration office believing surrender meant safety, and I felt something in my chest crack along a grain line, clean and terrible.

"He almost gave himself to it," I whispered. "He walked right toward the harvest."

*Yes. And you caught him. Remember that, when the horror gets loud. You caught him.* The king's shape moved ahead of me down the corridor, and the walls of faces turned to follow him, and I saw—for the first time—that the faces nearest him were brighter than the rest. Fainter, worn thinner than cloth, but brighter, and I understood: proximity to their king hurt them and fed them at once. He was the wound they were nailed across, and they loved him anyway, or hated him anyway, or both, and after three hundred years both looked identical.

*There is one more thing,* he said. *The last thing. Then I will let you sleep, such as it is.*

* * *

We stood at the crown of the structure—the topmost layer of the wall of souls, where the newest harvests hung, and above them, woven through and out of them like a root system grown upside down, ran the threads. Threads of light, hundreds of them, passing up through the ceiling of the vision and away into a darkness I somehow knew contained a palace, a throne, a thing sitting on it wearing a dead man's posture.

Each thread ended in a living person. I could see them, faint and far: a woman in a coastal town whose hands healed bruises; a boy in the capital who could charm locks open; an old ferryman who could smell storms two days out. Ordinary lives, ordinary gifts, each one anchored to the binding by a thread of pale light, each thread pulsing slowly, rhythmically, like something breathing.

*Not everyone is taken at once,* the king said. *That was the second lesson the nine learned, and the binding learned better. A fully drained soul is a spent candle. But a living gifted person, unaware, threaded—you give a little light every day, forever, and never miss it. You feel slightly tired. You feel slightly less than you were. You blame the weather, your age, your heart. Three hundred years of slow sipping, from every gifted soul in the realm, and the cup never empties because the well refills.*

My skin crawled. Because I knew—I knew before he said it—what he was going to say next, and I said it with him, aloud, in the dark of my cell in the tide-temple, my voice cracking against the salt-stained stones:

"I'm threaded."

*You are threaded,* he agreed, gently, horribly. *You have been threaded since your gift first woke. We all were. Every gifted soul in the kingdom pays tithe without knowing it, and the tax collector wears a crown, and calls himself a king, and the ledger of what he owes you has never once been balanced.* Something moved through his voice that in a living man I would have called fury. In him it was colder and older: arithmetic. *But hear the other half, Elodie, because the other half is why I chose you. A thread runs both directions. What flows out can be followed back. And what is bound can be unbound—but only by someone who can hear the knot, and carve it, and pay the toll.*

"The toll," I said. Everything came back to accounts. Even salvation was bookkeeping.

*The regalia, broken and reunited, is the blade. Your gift is the hand. And the cut must be paid for with the thing closest to the wound.* He paused, and for the first time in all our conversations, the dead king hesitated. *I will not tell you what that means tonight. You will work it out, or you will not, and either way you will choose with open eyes or not at all. I have earned the right to one cowardice in three hundred years, and hoarding this truth is mine.*

"You're a coward about exactly one thing," I said, "and it's the thing that decides everything."

*Welcome to kingship,* he said dryly, and for one absurd moment we were almost friends.

Then the corridor of faces flared—all of them at once, thousands of mouths opening in a soundless shout, and the threads above us thrummed like plucked strings, and somewhere far away in the darkness a thing on a throne lifted its head, because it had felt me looking. I felt it look back. It was like being regarded by a hole in the world.

*It knows you now,* the king said, urgency sharpening him to a blade's edge. *It has known you since the fingerbone. Sleep fast, bone-carver. Wake faster. Tomorrow you speak to the council, and half of them will call me a forgery, and you must make them listen anyway, because the harvest wagons are already rolling, and every day the council debates is a day the walls grow thicker—*

The vision tore.

* * *

I woke in the cell with salt on my lips and my hands aching, and when I looked down at them in the grey pre-dawn light, I saw why: I had been carving in my sleep. My knife lay on the blanket, and beside it, a scrap of whalebone I didn't remember taking from my kit, and cut into it in my own cramped ledger-hand, over and over, until the bone was more cuts than surface:

**NOT GONE. CAN BE PULLED BACK. THE KNOT HOLDS BUT DOES NOT DIGEST.**

Proof. Not of the king—he needed no proof from me, and the council would believe what it chose to believe regardless. Proof for myself, on the mornings when the horror got loud. The dead did not dictate. They had only ever been able to *ask*, and I had carried their asks in my memory like water in cupped hands, spillable, deniable, dissolvable in doubt. Now their testimony sat outside my skull, in the oldest material humans have ever trusted with words.

I dressed in the dark. Below my window, Meridian was waking—gulls arguing over the fish-market scraps, the tide-temple bells ringing the hour, and beyond the harbor, across the grey glitter of the strait, the faint smudge of the usurper's campfires, patient as stars.

Tam was already awake. Of course he was; his gift woke him when anything important was near, and lately everything was. He sat cross-legged in the temple corridor with his back against a pillar, and when he saw my face he stood up too fast, the way he did when he was frightened for someone else.

"You saw it again," he said. "All of it."

"All of it."

"The faces." His voice was very small. "The one who looked like—"

"Like you. Yes." I crouched to his height, which was most of my height, and took his shoulders. "Listen to me, Tam. The knot holds them, but it doesn't digest them. As long as the regalia stays broken, everyone it ever took can be pulled back. Everyone. Do you understand what that means?"

He was quiet for a moment, working it through, and I watched the understanding arrive in him the way dawn arrives—slow, then all at once.

"It means the ones the hunt takes aren't dead," he said. "It means there's still time."

"It means there's still time," I said. "But not much. And today the council decides whether we get any of it."

Down the corridor, Sera's door opened, and she emerged already armed with her ledger and her worst expression, and Quill's voice drifted up from the temple courtyard below, low and level, talking to the custodians about boats. Our strange, scattered, stubborn family, assembling itself around me in a building full of small miracles, on the morning I would stand in front of the Council of Ledgers and testify about a corridor built of souls.

Somewhere across the strait, a thing wearing a crown felt me looking, and looked back.

Let it look. I had testimony to deliver, and for the first time in my life, I finally understood what my gift had been training me for all along.

I picked up my knife, my bone, and my family, and went down to speak for the dead.
