## 14. What Tam Found

The tide-temple of Meridian smelled of salt and old incense, and Tam hated it immediately, which was how he knew he was in the right place.

That was the thing about his gift. It didn't feel like power. It felt like a splinter. Like a fishhook set under the skin of the world, tugging him by something no one else could see. Lost things pulled at him the way a drowning man pulls at a rope, and right now the pull was coming from beneath the temple floor, down where the sea had gotten into everything.

"Stop making that face," Sera said. She was dressed as a fishwife, sleeves rolled, hands red from the cold water she'd been pretending to wash in for the last hour. "You look like a boy about to be sick."

"I am a boy about to be sick."

"Good. Sick boys don't get questioned." She glanced at Elodie, who stood at the temple's edge with a bundle of carved bone trinkets spread on a cloth, playing the street-seller to perfection—hunched, humble, invisible. "Elodie. Anything?"

Elodie's eyes were closed. They'd been closed most of the morning. When they spoke, their voice came out flat and distant, the voice they used when the dead were talking through them instead of to them. "The keeper's chorus is quiet here. The temple dead are... old. Sea-dead. They don't know our names." A pause. "But there's something below us that isn't dead. Something *bound*. It's humming."

Tam felt it too. He'd felt it since they'd crossed the strait, a low sweet note under the city's noise, like a tuning fork struck inside his ribs. He'd assumed it was fear. It wasn't. Fear lived in the throat. This lived lower, deeper, patient.

"It knows we're coming," he said.

"Everything knows we're coming," Sera said. "That's why we're fishwives."

* * *

They'd been in Meridian nine days. Nine days of council chambers and closed doors, of Elodie standing before men in merchant silks with a king's fingerbone cupped in their palms like a candle flame, saying *listen, listen* while half the room looked for the forgery in it. Tam had watched from the back benches, holding the ledger case, learning what it looked like when truth wasn't enough.

It was Tam who'd found the second piece. That was the part nobody quite believed yet—not even Tam.

Three nights ago, unable to sleep in the safehouse above the chandler's shop, he'd let the hook in him off its leash. He'd lain in the dark and opened himself to the city, all its lost things, and Meridian was *full* of lost things—a wedding ring down a storm drain, a child's shoe in a temple wall, forty years of letters unsent in a dead postman's attic—and underneath it all, one note louder than the rest. One thing so lost it had stopped being an object and become a wound in the world.

He'd followed it through the dark streets half-asleep, walking like a somnambulist, until he stood before the tide-temple gates. Then the note had gone silent, the way a bird goes silent when a hawk crosses the sun.

He told Elodie first. He always told Elodie first. Elodie had listened without blinking, then said, "The king said the regalia was scattered in threes. Crown, ring, and—" They'd stopped. Looked at Tam. "Tam. What did it feel like?"

"Like a bell," Tam said. "Buried. Still ringing."

"*Ring*," Elodie breathed. "The king's signet. The Ring of the Ember Vow." And then they'd laughed, actually laughed, the wild exhausted laugh of people who have been carrying a war on their backs and just found out someone else can hold one end.

Now, standing in the temple forecourt among fishwives and beggars and the ordinary morning traffic of a free city pretending it wasn't at war, Tam tried to be brave the way Sera was brave: quietly, as a matter of logistics.

"The vault entrance is under the high altar," he said. "I can feel it. The stairs go down past the cisterns—the sea broke into them years ago, that's why they sealed the undercroft. The ring is below the waterline."

"You've never seen these stairs," Sera said.

"I don't need to see them. I need to find them. Finding is—" He stopped, because the sentence scared him. "Finding is what I'm for."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something she almost never did: she smiled with her whole face, quick and crooked, gone again like a coin palmed. "All right, apprentice. You're the map. Elodie sells trinkets until dusk. I go in as a penitent. We move when the tide turns."

"And if the grey gloves come?"

"They won't know faster than you do." She squeezed his shoulder once, hard, the way she did everything. "That's the whole plan. You're faster than they are. So be faster."

* * *

The tide turned at sundown, and Tam went down into the dark ahead of everyone, because that was the plan and because, secretly, he wanted to be first. He wanted to be the one who found it. He had spent his whole life being the smallest thing in every room—the apprentice who swept up, who carried cases, whose gift was a party trick, *find my keys, Tam, find my mother's brooch*—and now the fate of the crown itself hung on his nose for lost things, and some starving animal in his chest wanted to *devour* that.

The stairs were exactly where he knew they'd be, behind the altar, under a flagstone the priests had chalked with a warding mark nobody alive remembered the meaning of. The chalk was fresh. That was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was the water. It should have been knee-deep at worst, the spring tide seeping up through old stone. It was waist-deep and rising, and it was *warm*, warm as blood, and Tam stood chest-deep in the flooded stairwell with his heart slamming and understood: someone had broken the seals from below. Someone had opened the vault on purpose.

He turned to shout and the lamp at the top of the stairs went out.

Not blew out. Went out, all at once, like a held breath released.

"Elodie?" His voice cracked. Water swallowed the echo. In the black he could hear nothing but drip and surge and his own pulse, and then—faintly, from far above, muffled by stone—screaming. The temple screaming.

He should have gone up. Every sensible instinct said go up, find Elodie, find Sera, get out. But the hook in him wrenched *down*, hard enough to make him gasp, and in the dark he heard it for the first time not as a feeling but as a sound: a bell-note, clear and furious, ringing up through the flooded vault like a voice calling a name.

His name.

*Tam.*

"Oh," he whispered. "Oh, you know me."

He went down.

* * *

The undercroft was a drowned cathedral. His fingers found the wall and he walked it hand over hand, chin tipped back to keep his mouth above the rising water, following the note as it swelled from everywhere to somewhere—from the left, no, ahead, down, *down*—and the water climbed his ribs and his shoulders and still the bell rang, closer now, and he thought absurdly of Quill, of the old man's voice saying *you're more than you think you are, boy*, said once, drunk, never repeated, and Tam clung to the memory like a handhold.

His foot caught on a step. Steps. He hauled himself up out of the water onto a ledge, gasping, and crawled forward on stone that was dry—dry here, a pocket of air, a chamber the flood hadn't reached—and struck flint to the spare lamp in his coat with hands that would not stop shaking.

Light bloomed.

The chamber was small and round and very old, older than the temple above it, older maybe than the city. In the center, on a pillar of fused glass—sand melted by something hot beyond fire—sat a ring.

It was huge, a signet for a giant or a god, wrought of dull grey metal that drank his lamplight and gave none back. And it was singing. Not loudly. The way a mother sings to a child she thinks is asleep.

Tam knelt in front of it, dripping, and forgot to breathe.

"There you are," he said. His voice sounded strange in the little round room. Grown-up. "We've been looking for you. Well. I've been looking for you. Everyone else has been looking for a king."

The ring's song changed key. Warmer. He could have sworn it leaned toward him.

And that was when the light behind him changed—lamplight, a second lamp, coming up the steps he'd just climbed—and a voice said, pleasantly, in the accent of the capital court:

"Well. The gift of finding. The reports undersold you, Master Tam."

Tam turned.

The man in the doorway wore grey gloves. Behind him, filling the flooded stairwell, stood six more, wading, unhurried, lamps held high, water streaming off their coats. Their leader smiled the way a ledger balances: everything accounted for, nothing alive in it.

"Now," the grey-gloved man said. "Step away from the regalia, and we'll only take your hands."

* * *

Later—much later, in the telling—Tam would understand that what happened next happened in less time than a heartbeat, and that it happened because of three things: the ring, the water, and eleven years of sweeping floors in a guild house full of people who taught him that family fights dirty.

The ring sang *louder*.

It wasn't sound anymore. It was command, ancient and furious, the vow it had been forged to seal roaring up through the drowned dark, and Tam—who had spent his life listening to lost things—understood it the way he understood rain: instantly, completely, in his bones. *The vow remembers. The vow holds. The vow does not surrender what it keeps.*

The grey gloves took one step forward, and the sea took the room.

Whether the ring called it or the tide simply chose that moment, Tam never learned. The ledge cracked; the chamber walls burst along seams a thousand years old; the sea came through like judgment, black and total, and Tam grabbed the ring off its pillar—it burned cold, cold as deep water, cold as a star—and dove into the flood with the whole vault collapsing behind him.

The current took him sideways, slammed him into stone, tore the lamp from his hand. He didn't scream. Screaming meant breathing, and breathing meant drowning, and somewhere above him—through fathoms of black churn—he could hear Elodie's voice, thin and impossible and real, calling his name down through the broken temple, and the dead keeper's chorus rising around it like gulls over a wreck.

He kicked. He followed the voices. That was all finding was, he realized, in the last long moment before his lungs gave out: following the voices of things that refused to stay lost.

His hand broke the surface. Another hand—Sera's, iron-hard, fishwife-rough—closed around his wrist and hauled.

* * *

They lay on the temple steps coughing seawater while the priests shouted and the night filled with lanterns, and Tam opened his fist with the last of his strength.

The ring sat in his palm, dull and silent now, drinking the lantern light. Cold as the deep. Whole as a vow.

Sera looked at it. Then at Tam. Then she started laughing—that rare, whole-faced laugh—and pulled him against her shoulder hard enough to crack a rib, and Elodie dropped to their knees beside them both, soaked to the skin, saying his name over and over like a prayer they'd finally gotten answered.

"You went *down*," Elodie said. "We heard the vault go and you weren't in the temple and you went *down*—"

"It called me," Tam said. His teeth were chattering. He found he was smiling anyway, wide and helpless. "Nobody ever calls me. Things get lost, and I find them. But it called *me*."

Elodie took his face in their wet hands. Their eyes were shining, and behind the shine was something Tam had never seen directed at him before: awe.

"The king said the regalia chooses its keepers," they said. "Tam. I think it chose you a long time ago."

Behind them, at the edge of the lantern light, a temple priest was speaking urgently to a city watchman, and the watchman was looking at the collapsed gates, and the watchman's gloves, Tam saw with a lurch, were grey.

Two pieces now. One to go. And somewhere across the strait, in the usurper's camp, the last piece waited—and the thing wearing the crown had just learned, Tam was certain, exactly where its stolen regalia slept.

Because as he closed his fist around the ring, it sang one note more—soft, and terrible, and addressed across the water:

*He knows.*
