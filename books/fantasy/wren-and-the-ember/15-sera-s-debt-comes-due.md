## 15. Sera's Debt Comes Due

The knock came at dawn, and Sera had been awake for it.

She'd known it was coming the way you know weather — not from any gift, because she had none, but from thirty years of reading rooms and ledgers and the faces of men who wanted something they couldn't name yet. The sold secret had surfaced. Of course it had. Secrets were like salt: you could dissolve them in a whole sea of water and still taste them on everything.

She dressed carefully. Not her running clothes — the grey gloves would expect flight, and flight would confirm everything. She put on her good coat, the one with the deep pockets, the one that said *I am a merchant of Meridian and I pay my tariffs*. She counted what went into the pockets: a folding knife, small; a purse of coin, not large enough to look like a bribe, not small enough to look like poverty; and a strip of bone with three words carved into it that Elodie had made for her years ago, back when the guild was young and foolish enough to think itself invisible.

*For when it's dark.*

Sera had never asked whose voice lived in it. Some inventory you don't check.

Downstairs, the guild house they'd been given in the Salt Quarter smelled of bread and ink. Tam was asleep at the table over his maps, cheek on the tide-temple charts, the second regalia piece wrapped in oilcloth beneath his folded arms like a child guarding a drum. Quill had arrived two days ago smelling of road dust and apology he hadn't finished making. And Elodie —

Elodie stood at the window, watching the street.

"They're here," Elodie said quietly. "Six of them. Watchmen, and two men in grey. They're not hurrying."

"Good," Sera said. "Hurrying means they're afraid of us. Walking means they've already won the argument upstairs." She lifted the kettle off the fire and poured four cups, then thought better of it and poured five, out of habit, out of the old arithmetic of the guild — always one more cup than people, for whoever might come through the door. She set the fifth cup aside. It seemed suddenly like an artifact from a dead country.

Quill came down the stairs tying his belt, saw her face, and stopped on the last step like a man who has walked into a doorway he built himself. "How long have you known?"

"Since I paid the toll." Sera kept her voice level, the fence's voice, the voice that had talked smugglers down from knives and magistrates up from suspicion. "Meridian's council doesn't sell sanctuary, Quill. They lease it. And the collateral is always the same thing."

"What did you give them?"

"My name," Sera said. "My real one. Sera Vane, of Vane & Daughters, licensed broker, formerly of the crown's own customs house. The woman who ran contraband for eleven years under a false ledger before she ever heard of the Ember Guild." She turned the fourth cup in her hands. "They didn't want my secrets about the guild. They wanted proof I could be trusted to keep theirs. So I gave them the one thing that proved I'd already betrayed somebody once."

The knock came again, louder, and this time it wasn't a knock but a fist, and a voice through the wood: *"Open in the name of the Council of Nine."*

Tam woke with a start, hand going to the oilcloth bundle. Elodie moved — Sera watched it happen, watched the carver's shoulders square, watched their hand drift toward the satchel where the king's fingerbone slept in its velvet — and Sera put out one hand, flat, calm, the hand she used on spooked buyers.

"No," she said. "Nobody moves. Nobody runs. That's the whole trick now. We are respectable merchants who have nothing to hide, and we are going to be so boring that it saves our lives."

She opened the door herself.

* * *

The council men wore blue sashes. There were six watchmen behind them and two grey-gloved court agents standing slightly apart, the way vultures stand slightly apart from the thing they're waiting to become relevant to. The elder of the councilmen was a narrow woman with a magistrate's patience, Magistrate Corren, whom Sera had sold information to twice and bought silence from once, and whose face now held the specific blankness of someone performing a duty they've already regretted.

"Sera Vane," Corren said.

"That's me."

"You are accused of collaboration with agents of the usurper's court. Specifically: the sale, to this council's enemies, of intelligence concerning the movements and holdings of gifted persons within Meridian." Corren read it from a paper without inflection, which Sera recognized as mercy. A man would have performed it. "The evidence was presented by envoys of the crown this morning, along with a demand for your remand into custody pending — "

"Pending what?" Tam burst out from behind her. He was sixteen and furious and holding a map like a shield. "Pending *what*? She's the reason we got here at all, she spent her — she gave them her — "

"TAM." Sera said it the way you say a word into a room full of powder. Then, gentler: "Let the nice magistrate finish. It's rude to interrupt a woman while she ruins your morning."

Corren's mouth twitched, almost. Almost. "Pending transfer to the crown's jurisdiction," she finished. "Which, I will note for the record, this council does not wish to grant. But the crown's envoys hold letters of marque against every harbor master on this coast, and Meridian's fleets are not yet ready for war. So." She folded the paper. "I'm sorry, Vane. You knew the arithmetic when you made the trade."

"I wrote the arithmetic," Sera said. "You just carried the one."

One of the grey gloves stepped forward. Sera knew him — or knew his type, which amounted to the same thing; they all had the same face eventually, smooth and patient as river stones. "We'll take her now," he said. "And we'll search the premises."

"The premises belong to the Council of Nine," Corren said flatly, "leased to a licensed broker, and you'll search it over my writ or not at all. Take her. Touch nothing else. Those are the terms your own envoy signed yesterday, and I keep copies." She looked at Sera, and for half a heartbeat the magistrate's mask slipped and something older looked out of it — something tired, something that had buried friends. "Walk out with dignity, Vane. It helps the ones left behind more than you'd think."

Sera nodded. Then she turned around and looked at her family, and everything she needed to say, she said fast and plain, because there was no time for anything else and because she had never in her life wasted words on sentiment when logistics would do.

"Elodie. The third piece is across the strait and the thing wearing the crown knows where two of them sleep, which means speed matters more than safety now. Don't wait for me. Don't trade for me. Don't spend a single favor, coin, or kindness on getting me back — do you hear me? Every resource you waste on my rescue is a resource the usurper doesn't have to burn. I'm worth more to you free in a cell than dead in a ditch trying to get me out."

"I'm not leaving you," Elodie said. Their voice cracked straight down the middle.

"Yes, you are, because I'm telling you to, and because it's the only order I ever gave you that I'll enforce from inside a cell if I have to." Sera took Elodie's face in both hands. Her apprentice, her partner, her ledger-keeper, the person she'd pulled out of a gutter in Kell's Crossing twelve years ago because the kid could hear the dead and couldn't stop crying about it, and look at them now, look at what they'd grown into. "Listen to me. Love isn't rescue. Love is finishing the job. You finish the job."

"Quill." She turned before Elodie could answer, because if she waited for an answer she'd stay, and she could not stay. "You owe me eleven years of explanations and you'll give them to me someday over terrible wine, but today you owe me one thing only: keep them alive. Not safe. Alive and moving. You're the decoy, you're the plan, you're the guilt with legs — use all three."

Quill's jaw worked. "Sera —"

"And Tam." Last, because hardest. Tam was crying and pretending not to, sixteen years old and holding a piece of a dead king's regalia like it was a hot coal, and she crossed to him and pressed something into his hand: the strip of bone. "When it's dark," she said. "That's all. When it's dark, and not before, and only once."

"What does it —"

"It says what it needs to say. It always has." She kissed the top of his head, quick, businesslike, a fence sealing a deal. "Now everyone pick up a pack and look boring."

The watchmen flanked her at the door. The grey gloves watched her go with those patient stone faces, and she gave them her best professional smile, the one that had closed a hundred deals, and said, "Gentlemen. I hope your cells are warmer than your manners."

Magistrate Corren walked her out. At the threshold, quietly, under the sound of boots, she said: "There's a hearing in nine days. Council procedure. Slow procedure, if anyone were to make it slow — petitions, appeals, procedural motions. A busy clerk could stretch nine days into twenty."

"A very persuasive client," Sera said, "might find such a clerk."

"One exists," Corren said. "He owes a broker named Vane a great deal. Don't thank me. It makes it bribery instead of bookkeeping."

And then the door of the guild house closed behind Sera Vane, and the street took her, and the last thing she saw looking back — because of course she looked back, she was a professional, professionals verify — was Elodie standing in the doorway with their hand raised, and Tam clutching the bone, and Quill already turning away toward the maps because she'd told him to move and Quill, whatever else he was, finally knew how to follow orders.

* * *

The cell was exactly as warm as she'd promised them it wouldn't be.

Stone floor, straw pallet, a slit window with a view of the strait — the council wasn't cruel, merely practical, and practical meant keeping a valuable prisoner alive and miserable in equal measure. Sera sat with her back to the wall and did what she had done in every bad hour of her life since she was nineteen years old and the crown's customs house burned her first identity to cover its own corruption: she made lists.

Assets: one sympathetic magistrate, one bribable clerk, nine days of procedure. Liabilities: two grey gloves who reported directly to the usurper's camp, which meant everything she said in this building was already across the water. Unknowns: whether the crown's envoys would try to interrogate her before the hearing, and what they'd use.

She found she could answer that one already. She'd spent her life selling other people's secrets. She knew exactly what hers weighed.

They'd come for the regalia. Where it slept, who carried it, how it sang. And when persuasion failed they would offer her the thing the usurper offered everyone, the thing it had offered Elodie in visions, the currency the binding minted: the dead, returned. Sera had no dead she wanted back. That was the strange mercy of her life — she'd lost clients and coin and countries, but everyone she loved was still breathing, which meant they could threaten her only with her own future, and her own future had been forfeit since the day she signed it over to buy a boat south.

So. Lists.

Day one: eat everything they brought, however bad. Strength was an asset. Day two: request writing materials, citing her right as a licensed broker to prepare her defense. Paper was a weapon and she intended to file so many procedural motions that Corren's clerk would need a second desk. Day three through eight: be tedious. Be magnificent. Be the most boring prisoner in the history of Meridian, a woman so relentlessly procedural that even the grey gloves would rather sail home than sit through another of her appeals.

And day nine — well. Day nine depended on Elodie, and Tam, and a dead king, and a strait full of blockades, and whether love really was finishing the job.

Sera leaned her head back against the stone and allowed herself, briefly, privately, between one list and the next, to feel it. Not fear. Grief's smaller cousin — the ache of a table set for five with only four chairs filled, the weight of the fifth teacup she'd poured out of habit that morning and never drunk. She had built the guild's escape routes for fifteen years. She had never once planned on being the one left behind the door.

It was fine. It was, in fact, the best deal she'd ever closed: her freedom, traded for their mission, with a margin of error measured in days. Any fence in Meridian would call it a fair price.

On the second night, the slot in the door slid open, and a tray came through, and under the bread — tucked flat, greased with butter so it wouldn't crackle — was a scrap of paper in a clerk's cramped hand:

*Hearing moved to the twelfth. The grey gloves sail Friday. Something is happening across the strait. The city can feel it in its teeth.*

Sera read it twice, then ate the bread, then laughed alone in the dark of her cell — a short, sharp, unlovely sound, the laugh of a woman whose family was about to do something catastrophically reckless without her, and who had never in her life been prouder of anyone.

Friday, then. Across the water. Finish the job.

She started on her next list.
