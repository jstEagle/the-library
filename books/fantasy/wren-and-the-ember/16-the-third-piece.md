## 16. The Third Piece

The map of Meridian's war room smelled of tallow and salt. Someone had pinned the strait in blue thread, the usurper's camp on the far shore in black, and between them a hundred little knots of red marking ships that had no business belonging to a kingdom that called itself at peace.

Elodie stood over it with their arms crossed, counting knots they could not untie.

"Sixty-one," said Tam, who had counted too. He was taller than he'd been on the salt road. Grief did that, or purpose, or both. "Maybe sixty-two. That one might be two ships overlapping."

"Two ships overlapping," Elodie murmured, "is how most disasters begin."

They'd been in Meridian eleven days. Eleven days since Sera had been taken to the council cells with her wrists bound in silk—because the council was polite about its betrayals—and her last instruction still ringing in Elodie's ears like a struck coin: *Don't waste time getting me out. Waste it winning.* The council envoys from the crown had smiled through their teeth at the arrangement, satisfied that the free cities were serious about cooperation. And across the strait, the thing wearing the crown sat in its camp of sixty-one ships, holding the third piece of the regalia somewhere among all those tents and soldiers, wearing it perhaps, feeding on it nightly.

The first two pieces lay wrapped in oilcloth under Elodie's cot: the fingerbone that had started everything, and the tide-temple piece Tam had found in flooded vaults while grey gloves splashed behind them. Two pieces that hummed when held together. Two pieces that made the dead king's voice clearer, sharper, more himself—a man named Aldric who had been bound for three hundred years into his own regalia, eaten slowly by the pact that wore his shape.

They needed the third piece. It was across the strait. And every plan the council's strategists proposed ended the same way: a fleet, an assault, ten thousand men against a binding that ate gifts for breakfast.

"We're going to need something smaller than an army," Elodie said to the map.

"That's what I've been saying," Tam said. "Nobody listens to apprentices."

"Nobody listens to apprentices until the apprentices are right."

He grinned, briefly. It didn't reach his eyes. Sera's cell was four streets away and none of them had slept properly since.

* * *

Quill arrived at dusk, unannounced, the way he arrived everywhere—like weather.

Elodie heard the commotion before they understood it: voices in the guildhouse courtyard below the rooms the free cities had lent them, and then Tam's shout, high and cracked with disbelief, and then the sound of a door hitting a wall because someone had thrown it open without ceremony.

Master Quill stood in the courtyard looking exactly as he always had and completely different. The same grey beard, trimmed badly. The same ink-stained fingers. But he'd walked out of the north with nothing, apparently—no ledger satchel, no coat of many pockets, just a road-dusted cloak and a face full of something Elodie had never seen on him before.

Fear, maybe. Or the end of pretending not to feel it.

"Tam." His voice caught on the name. He put both hands on the boy's shoulders and looked at him the way you look at something you'd decided never to see again so that losing it wouldn't hurt. "You got taller."

"You got old," Tam said, and then hugged him hard enough to knock the wind out of the apology Quill had clearly rehearsed the whole way down the salt road.

Elodie stayed in the doorway. Some reconciliations needed to happen without them; they'd learned that much from watching families trade grief in their parlor, back when the guild took commissions from the living too.

But Quill looked up over Tam's shoulder, and found them, and everything in his face rearranged itself around guilt.

"Elodie."

"Master Quill."

"Don't do that." He released Tam gently and crossed the courtyard, stopping a respectful arm's length away—the distance of a man who knows he has forfeited closeness. "I owe you words. I've owed them since the bonfire. Since before the bonfire, if we're honest, since before you were born, but I'll start where I can."

"The third piece is in the usurper's camp," Elodie said. Not cruelly. Just refusing to let sentiment eat the hours.

"I know. I came from the north. I've seen what the hunt looks like when it stops pretending to be law." He pulled back his hood fully. There was a scar along his jaw Elodie didn't remember, fresh, poorly stitched. "I also came because I know how to get it. And because I'm the only one who can."

* * *

They sat in the small room with the map, all three of them, and Quill laid it out the way he used to lay out inventory—methodically, lovingly, each item placed with care.

"The regalia was made in pieces," he said. "You know this. Crown, ring, and the gorget—the throat-piece. The crown sits on the usurper's head; that's the binding's anchor. The ring we have. The gorget went north three hundred years ago, into the keeping of the first founders' sister-line, my line, though we didn't know what we kept until my grandfather's grandfather forgot why the box was heavy." He laughed, short and bitter. "That's how inheritances die. Not stolen. Forgotten."

"And the crown?" Tam asked. "It's on his head. In camp. How does knowing where it is help us?"

"Because I know which tent it sleeps in." Quill tapped the black mass across the strait. "Before I left the north I spent two months doing what the Ember Guild has always done—trading. Selling. Listening. The usurper's quartermasters buy relics the way the crown burns them; they don't trust their own master, whatever they tell their priests. I sold a fence in the baggage train three forged saint-bones and bought a map of the command encampment with the change." His mouth twisted. "Everything I ever taught you, turned toward this. It felt good. That frightened me."

"The gorget," Elodie said. "Where is it now?"

"In the camp. They took it from my family's house six weeks ago. Burned the house." Flatly. A report, not a wound—but Elodie heard the wound underneath, because hearing things underneath things was the gift that had defined their whole life. "The grey gloves keep it in the reliquary wagon, near the crown's tent. They think proximity strengthens their master. They may even be right."

"So we steal it back."

"So we steal it back," Quill agreed. "And here is the part where you tell me I'm mad, and here is the part where I tell you it doesn't matter, because there's no other version of this plan that ends with anyone alive."

* * *

The plan was simple in the way that plans are simple right up until they kill you.

The council would make noise—a feint, a landing of fireships on the northern rocks where the coast was indefensible. Every commander across the strait would look there. And while they looked, one boat would cross dark water to a cove south of the camp, and one man would walk into that camp openly, carrying the thing the grey gloves wanted most in the world.

Himself.

"They've been looking for me since the proclamation," Quill said. "The founder's line. The keeper of the gorget's hiding place. There are warrants with my description in every garrison office from here to the capital—I checked, it's a flattering likeness. If Master Quill of the Ember Guild walks into the usurper's camp and offers to trade—himself, his knowledge, every hiding place the guild ever used—for his life, they will believe it. Because it's plausible. Because men do break. And because the binding wants exactly what I'm offering." He met Elodie's eyes. "It wants to finish eating my family. We were the ones who hid the pieces. Our blood knew the secrets. When they take me, they'll want me to talk, and I will talk, and while they listen to me, someone else lifts the gorget from the wagon."

"No," Elodie said.

"It wasn't a question."

"It's still no." Elodie stood. Their chair scraped. Somewhere in their chest the dead king's whisper stirred, patient, listening—he always listened when Quill spoke, with some old sorrow Elodie couldn't parse. "They won't just question you. You know what remanded means. You've read the ledgers. They'll feed you to it. Your gift—your *memory*, Quill, your whole life—it eats lives, that's what the binding *is*—"

"Yes," Quill said quietly. "I know what it is. I've known since I was nineteen years old, when my mother finally told me what the heavy box in our cellar was and why the Ember Guild existed. Three hundred years of my family keeping a secret so heavy we bent around it like trees growing against wind. I built the guild on that bargain, Elodie. I recruited you, I sheltered Tam, I let Sera run her fences through my ledgers—all of it, every warmth we ever had, was scaffolding around a duty I was too cowardly to explain." His hands were flat on the table, steady, but Elodie saw the tremor in his forearms. "This isn't sacrifice. This is payment. There's a difference, and I've had a long road to learn it."

"The decoy doesn't have to be you—"

"It has to be me. They'll believe me breaking. No one else is worth breaking." He almost smiled. "Besides, I'm the only one who can lie to a binding's servants with a straight face. I've been lying to everyone I love for thirty years. Consider it my one area of genuine expertise."

Silence. Tam had gone very pale and very still, the way he did when he was finding something—not an object anymore, but a truth—and didn't like its weight.

"You're coming back," Tam said.

Quill looked at him for a long moment. "Tam—"

"You're coming *back*. That's not a question either." The boy's voice cracked and he let it. "Sera told us not to waste time getting her out because she thought she was expendable and she wasn't. Now you're doing the same thing. Everyone in this family keeps deciding they're the one who gets to be spent, like we're *inventory*, like—" He stopped, jaw working. "Like entries in a ledger. Well, I'm the apprentice, and I'm telling you: I am writing you down as coming back. Both of you. That's the entry. It's in ink. Ink doesn't come out."

For the first time since he'd walked through the door, Quill's composure broke—not dramatically, just a fissure, the way stone cracks before it becomes sand. He reached out and gripped Tam's wrist, the old guild gesture, the one that meant *held, counted, kept*.

"Ink doesn't come out," he repeated. "All right. Then I suppose I'd better be worth the page."

* * *

Elodie found him at midnight on the seawall, where the harbor lights bled into black water and the usurper's camp was a smear of distant orange across the strait. They sat down beside him without asking. He didn't tell them to go away, which was its own kind of answer.

"When I was young," Quill said, after a while, "I asked my mother why we kept the box. She said, *because someone has to hold what the powerful want buried, or they dig it up and use it.* I thought she meant the gorget. She meant the whole guild. Every impossible thing we ever traded, every memory bottled, every storm sold—she meant that someone has to be the place where dangerous things go to be *kept* instead of *used*. I think I lost sight of that. I think I started using people the way the crown uses gifts. Slowly. Without noticing."

"I know," Elodie said.

"You should hate me longer than this."

"I did hate you. On the night of the bonfire I hated you so completely it felt architectural. Like a room I lived in." Elodie watched the far shore. "But then I heard a dead king beg for help inside a fingerbone, and I learned that being chosen by something larger doesn't feel like glory. It feels like dread with better posture. And I thought—if I can forgive myself for the lies I told the grey-gloved man, for how easy lying came, for how much I *liked* it—then I can probably manage forgiving you. Eventually. Starting now, because we don't have eventually."

Quill exhaled. It shuddered on the way out. "Your mother would have forgiven me faster. She always said I apologized like a man settling accounts." He glanced sideways. "She'd have been proud of you. Of all of this. I hope you know that."

In their chest, the chorus stirred—and for once it wasn't the king. It was a woman's voice, warm and half-remembered, humming three bars of a lullaby Elodie hadn't heard since childhood, and gone again before they could catch it.

Elodie didn't say anything. Some messages deliver themselves.

"The crossing is tomorrow night," Quill said. "Tide turns at moonset. Tam's already found the boat—we don't ask how, anymore, we just accept that things are lost until he looks at them. And the gorget..." He reached into his cloak and produced a scrap of paper, folded small, worn soft at the creases. "If I don't come back, this goes to the free cities' archive. It's the founder's oath. My family's copy. Read it when you can bear to. It explains why your gift descends from the first founder, and why the binding fears bone-carvers specifically, and—" his voice frayed, steadied, "—and why I think, in the end, it will be you who finishes this. Not an army. You."

Elodie took the paper. It weighed nothing. It weighed three hundred years.

"Come back," they said. "Or the entry's wrong, and Tam will be insufferable about it forever."

Quill laughed—really laughed, once, the sound surprising both of them—and stood, and looked out at the enemy fires across the water with the expression of a man setting down a load carried so long his spine had grown around it.

"Tomorrow, then," he said. "Tell the council to light their fires. And Elodie—" he paused at the top of the seawall steps, silhouetted against the harbor lamps, an old man-shaped debt finally coming due. "When you carve the unbinding—carve it clean. My family kept the pieces apart for three hundred years. Let's not make them wait any longer to be whole."

Then he was gone into the dark, and across the strait the usurper's fires burned on, and beneath Elodie's ribs the dead king whispered one word, clear as a struck bell:

*Soon.*

* * *

Dawn came grey. Elodie stood on the quay with Tam, watching the smuggler's boat rock against the pilings, watching the council's fireship crews load pitch by lantern-light. Two pieces of the regalia slept under their coat, humming faintly against each other like birds in a box.

Across the strait, in a reliquary wagon beside a crowned thing that ate souls, the third piece waited.

Tonight they crossed.
