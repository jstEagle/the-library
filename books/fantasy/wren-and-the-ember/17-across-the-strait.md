## 17. Across the Strait

The boat was called the Kindly Widow, which Tam decided was either a very good omen or a very bad one, and there was no way to know which until you were already too far from shore to matter.

He sat in the bilge between two crates of salted eels and tried to breathe like Sera had taught him — in for four, hold for four, out for four — except that every time he got to three, the strait reminded him it existed. The water out here wasn't water the way the harbor at Meridian was water. It was black and heavy and it moved like something breathing under a blanket, and across its whole width, from the cliffs of Meridian to the grey shore of the usurper's country, warships sat anchored in a chain.

"Blockade," the smuggler had said, back when Tam could still hear her voice without his heart hammering. Her name was Vesh. She had a scar through one eyebrow and hands like knotted rope, and she'd taken Sera's gold with the flat expression of someone who had been paid for worse. "They watch the deep channel because that's where the fat merchants come. They don't watch the shallows, because nothing with sense goes into the shallows." She'd grinned then, and the grin hadn't reached anywhere near her eyes. "We have no sense."

Now they were in the shallows. The Widow's hull scraped something below — sand? rock? a drowned god's shoulder? — and Tam's whole body clenched around the bundle in his arms.

The regalia pieces. The second piece they'd won in the flooded vaults beneath the tide-temple, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. And beside it, in a longer case, the fingerbone of the old king, which Elodie carried everywhere now, even sleeping, even eating, the way other people carried worry-stones or saints' medals.

"Talk to me," Elodie whispered. In the dark of the bilge their face was just an arrangement of shadows, but Tam could hear the strain in it. "Anything. What did you eat on the Salt Road that you never want to eat again?"

"The eel soup," Tam said. "At the wayhouse. The one that tasted like the inside of a boot."

"You drank three bowls."

"Because I was polite."

Elodie laughed — a small, cracked sound, quickly smothered, but real. Above them, boots crossed the deck. Sera's voice came low through the boards: "Vesh says we make the crossing at slack tide, when the patrol lanterns swing widest apart. Two hours."

Two hours. Tam lay back against the eel crates and stared up into the dark where the deck should be, and thought about Quill.

Quill, who was not on this boat. Quill, who had arrived in Meridian six days ago looking ten years older than the last time Tam had seen him, with salt in his beard and an apology in his mouth that he still hadn't managed to say all the way through. *I built this guild as a hiding place,* he'd told them, sitting in the safehouse above the chandler's shop, his big hands hanging empty between his knees. *And I let it become a warehouse. I hid the king's bones so long I forgot they were waiting for something.* Then he'd laid out the plan — his plan, the only plan anyone had — and the plan required a fire.

*The usurper knows the guild exists,* Quill had said. *Knows the name Ember, has known for years — that's why I kept us small, why I moved us, why I never wrote anything down. So we give him what he's always wanted. We give him the guild, caught red-handed, running south with relics. A decoy convoy. And I'll be on it.*

Tam had argued. He remembered arguing — remembered his voice going high and stupid, saying *you can't, you're the guild, you're — you're Quill.* And Quill had looked at him with that expression he got when Tam asked a question that was smarter than intended, and said, gently, "That's exactly why it has to be me, lad. Nobody plays me better than I do. And nobody else is expendable enough."

Nobody else is expendable enough. Tam turned the words over in the dark and hated them, and hated more that they were true, and hated most of all the part of himself that understood strategy now, that could see the shape of a sacrifice and call it necessary instead of simply screaming.

"Elodie," he whispered. "Do you think he'll make it?"

The silence went on long enough that Tam thought Elodie hadn't heard. Then: "The dead tell me a lot of things, Tam. They've never once told me the future. I think that's on purpose." A pause. The strait breathed against the hull. "But I heard him the night before he sailed. He was singing. Badly. Some old dock song about a widow who drowns her husbands." Elodie's voice wobbled. "I don't think a man sings like that if he means to die."

"He sings badly all the time."

"Yes," Elodie said. "That's what I'm holding onto."

Above, Sera's boots stopped directly over their heads, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Lanterns moving. Everyone down and quiet. We go dark."

The Kindly Widow killed her lamps.

* * *

Crossing a blockaded strait in the dark, Tam discovered, was mostly waiting punctuated by moments of absolute certainty that you were about to die.

The patrol ships showed themselves as lanterns — swinging, slow, patient, like pendulums counting out the night. When one swung near, Vesh killed even the sound of the boat: oars muffled in wool, sails luffed, the crew frozen at their posts. Once, a lantern passed so close that Tam could hear voices on the deck above them — soldiers complaining about the cold, about the rations, about a sergeant named Brakk who made everyone scrub the deck twice. Ordinary men. Men with sore backs and boring superiors, doing a monstrous thing because it was their job.

Tam pressed his palm flat against the oilcloth bundle over his heart and listened.

His gift had changed since the vaults. It had always been a finding-thing, a lost-button thing, a where-did-mama's-ring-go thing — small and domestic and, until the hunt began, nearly useless. But in the flooded dark beneath the tide-temple, with the second regalia piece singing to him through thirty feet of stone and seawater, he'd learned that the gift had range. That lost things called to lost things. That he could stand in one place and feel, faint as a hearth through a wall, wherever the missing pieces of the binding lay scattered in the world.

And there were three. He knew that now the way you knew your own fingers. The fingerbone rode against Elodie's chest. The second piece — a knucklebone, huge as a fist, carved with the unbinding-script half-finished — was wrapped beside it. And the third was across the water, in the usurper's own keeping, in the treasury beneath the palace where the thing wearing the crown slept among everything it had ever stolen.

It sang to him even now. Faintly. Across the whole black width of the strait, through the hull and the water and the distance, Tam felt it the way you felt a bell after the ringing stopped — a vibration living in the bones of his ear. It didn't sound like the others. The others had sung grief. This one sang hunger.

A lantern swung toward them. Closer than any before. Tam held his breath and counted the creaks of the Widow's timbers and thought, absurdly, of the wayhouse keeper Marta, dead eleven days now, whose voice had joined Elodie's chorus somewhere on the road south. He wondered if the dead could hear bells. He wondered if Marta was listening now.

The lantern swung away.

And then, behind them — far behind, back at the northern shore, back where Quill had gone — the sky lit up.

It started as a glow, low on the horizon, orange as a coal. Then a column of smoke stood up against the stars, and the glow spread, and the wind brought them the smell of burning pitch, and Tam understood: the decoy convoy. The false guild fleet, loaded with Quill and a handful of volunteers and enough flammables to be visible from a warship's deck, making its run along the coast exactly where the crown would expect the Ember Guild to run.

Shouting drifted across the water. Distant, thin, wordless — but unmistakable. Alarm. Somewhere in the blockade's long chain, anchors rose. Lanterns that had hung patient all night began to move, converging, sliding north toward the fire like iron filings toward a magnet.

"They're taking the bait," Vesh breathed at the rail. "Saints preserve fools. They're taking it." She turned and shouted, soft but fierce, down into the hold: "Slack tide! Oars out! We run for the far shore while every eye in the navy watches a burning man pretend to be us!"

Tam scrambled up to the gunwale despite Sera's hissed objection, and looked back.

The fire was beautiful. That was the terrible part. It stood on the water like a festival, like midsummer bonfires on the beach at home, back before home was a word that meant something you'd lost. And Quill was in there somewhere, or near it, playing his last great role — the master fence, the guilty founder, the expendable man — blazing so bright that the usurper's entire armada turned to look at him.

"Don't look away," Elodie said quietly, beside him. Their eyes were wet and reflecting firelight they shouldn't have been able to see at this distance. "He deserves witnesses."

"I'm not looking away," Tam said. His voice came out steady, and surprised him. "I'm memorizing it. So I can tell him he owes me a lecture about it later. When he shows up."

Sera put a hand on each of their shoulders — hers, always, the hand that steered — and said nothing at all, which from Sera was a whole speech.

The Kindly Widow ran east into the dark, small and lampless and quick, while behind her the decoy burned and the warships chased a lie, and Tam felt the third regalia piece singing to him across the shrinking water, hungry and close, and for the first time in his life the fear in his chest didn't feel like an obstacle.

It felt like fuel.

* * *

The far shore was a country Tam had never seen and recognized immediately anyway, because the crown's reach looked the same everywhere: checkpoints, proclamations pasted to walls, the grey-glove sigil stenciled on granary doors and chapel lintels. But Vesh knew these waters, and she knew a fisherman's cove forty miles south of the capital where a cart could be had for gold and questions weren't asked, and by the second dawn they were rolling inland disguised as exactly what Tam had been his whole life before all this — nobody in particular. A clerk's boy. A carter's nephew. A person too dull to stop.

The capital announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and horse and river mud, and underneath it all, threaded thin, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room. Tam felt it before he smelled it, though. The closer they came, the louder the third piece sang, and the song had a flavor now, and the flavor was *taking.*

They entered the city in a grain convoy, folded into a wagon bed between sacks, and emerged into a maze of streets that Sera had memorized from maps bought in Meridian's underworld. The palace stood on its hill above everything, white-walled and crowned in gold that caught the morning sun and threw it back like a boast.

"That treasury," Sera murmured, hunched beside Tam at a spice stall, ostensibly examining saffron, actually watching the palace gates. "Quill's contact drew it. Underneath the east wing, behind the old mint vaults. One entrance for carts, one stair for clerks, and a garrison of forty between us and either."

"Forty," Tam repeated.

"On a coronation week? Forty is the compliment they pay us. There will be more inside." She set down the saffron. For a moment her merchant mask slipped and Tam saw underneath it — tired, frightened, ferocious. "Once we're in, we don't come out the same way. You understand? Whatever happens in there, the plan ends with us walking out the front as prisoners' escort, or not walking out at all."

"Then let's make sure it's the front," Tam said.

Sera stared at him. Then she smiled — the rare one, the real one, the one that made her look twenty years younger and infinitely more dangerous. "Look at you. When did you become someone who says things like that?"

"Somewhere around the boot-flavored soup."

They spent the day becoming invisible. Elodie grayed their hair with ash and bent their back and became a bone-seller's widow; Sera wore the grey gloves themselves, stolen in Meridian, and discovered that the usurper's own livery opened more doors than gold did; Tam pushed a cart of bone-carver blanks — Elodie's work, ordinary craft, no voices in any of them — and learned that no one in the history of the world had ever looked twice at a delivery boy.

By dusk they stood in the shadow of the mint wall, and the treasury hummed above them like a struck bell that wouldn't stop.

Tam closed his eyes and let the gift stretch. This was the part only he could do. Elodie could carve the unbinding, but first the carving had to fit the bone — and the third piece was not shaped like the others. He could feel that much even through the walls, through the stone, through forty guards and a century of stolen silence. The fingerbone was a needle. The knucklebone was a thread-eye. And the third piece —

He followed the song down. Past the garrison floor, past the old mint vaults with their rusted coin-presses, down into a chamber that the gift showed him in impressions rather than images: cold, dry, airless, ringed with wards that tasted of ash. And at the center of it, on a stand or a throne or an altar, something curved. Something that had once been part of a jaw, maybe, or a crown-band worn at the brow — carved all around its circumference with script, but the script was wrong, reversed, the binding written backwards so that it *took* instead of gave. It had been drinking for a thousand years.

And it knew he was listening.

The song changed. The hunger sharpened into attention, into something almost like delight, and Tam felt — distinctly, undeniably — the sensation of being *found*. Of something ancient turning its regard upon a small boy with a small gift standing outside its walls, and finding him interesting.

His eyes snapped open. His breath came ragged.

"It saw me," he whispered.

Sera's head turned fast. "What do you mean, saw—"

"The piece. The binding. It — I was listening and it listened back." Tam's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against his thighs. "It knows we're here. Maybe not names, maybe not faces, but it knows *someone* is hunting it, and—" He swallowed. "Elodie. When you heard the dead king say *hurry.* Did he mean hurry like a warning? Or hurry like a countdown?"

Elodie had gone very pale. Their hand found the fingerbone at their chest and gripped it, and whatever the chorus told them made them close their eyes for three long seconds before answering.

"He said," Elodie read aloud, voice thin in the evening air, "*the crown dreams of harvest tonight. Come before it wakes full.*"

Above them, on the palace hill, every bell began to ring at once.

Not alarm-bells. Not the staggered clangor of fire or invasion, but something worse — all the bells of the capital tolling in perfect unison, a single vast note rolling down the hill and through the streets and into the stones under Tam's feet, and beneath the bronze, beneath the noise, he heard the third piece singing louder than it had ever sung, exultant, awake, *hungry*, and he understood with total clarity that the thing wearing the crown had just decided to stop waiting for them.

Sera pulled the grey gloves tight over her fingers. "Change of plan," she said. "We go in *now.*"

The bells tolled on. Tam picked up his cart, and followed his family into the dark.
