## 18. The Throne Room of Wearing Things

The palace of Vhalor had been built to be looked up at. Every arch stretched, every stair widened as it climbed, so that by the time a visitor reached the throne room they had already spent an hour learning their own smallness. Elodie had read the ledgers of three kings' coronations—gold weight, feast cost, the price of the doves—and none of it had prepared them for the way the building made you feel like an apology.

They came in through the cisterns, because Quill's plan said the old waterways remembered older doors. Tam went first, his gift humming like a struck tuning fork, pulling them left when the passages forked and right when the dark got too thick to argue with. Sera was two days gone from a cell in Meridian, her last order still ringing in Elodie's ears: *Finish it. Don't you dare come back for me.* The dead king's chorus rode along beneath Elodie's ribs, a low pressure like weather about to break.

"Here," Tam whispered, and pressed his palm flat against wet stone.

Nothing happened. Then something did—a seam of light, thin as a knife cut, and the wall swung inward on hinges that hadn't screamed in three hundred years.

"The masons who built this were ours," Tam said, wonder making his voice young again. "It wanted finding."

*Everything hidden wants finding,* said the chorus, in a voice Elodie didn't recognize—a woman, dead four centuries, amused. *That's why hiding is holy work.*

Elodie stepped through, and the wall closed behind them, and there was no way back that wasn't forward.

* * *

The throne room at night was worse than the throne room by day would have been, because at night it was honest. No courtiers, no ceremony, no crowd to perform smallness for. Just the long floor of black marble veined with gold—the veins ran upward, Elodie noticed, toward the dais, like roots feeding a tree—and the throne itself, and the thing sitting in it.

It wore the shape of King Aldous the Second, the usurper, the man whose face was stamped on every coin and every proclamation. But the shape was wrong the way a glove is wrong before the hand fills it. It sat too still. Its breathing, if it breathed, kept no rhythm Elodie's ear could trust. And above its brow, where the crown sat, the air bent slightly, the way heat bends over a forge.

The regalia pieces hung against Elodie's chest under their coat: the fingerbone in its lead case, the tide-temple circlet wrapped in wool. They were warm. They had been getting warmer since the cisterns, and now they were nearly hot, and Elode understood that they were not warming on their own. Something was calling to them.

"You brought my bones home," the thing on the throne said.

Elodie's whole body flinched. Because the voice was the dead king's—not the chorus's echo of him, but him, the living-bound voice Elodie had first heard inside a fingerbone in a guild workroom a lifetime ago. *Please,* that voice had begged. *Find the pieces. Cut me loose.*

"It's not your voice," Elodie said. "I've heard the real one. I'd know it anywhere."

"Would you?" The thing rose from the throne, unhurried, and the crown's bend of air moved with it. "I have worn so many voices. Yours included, eventually."

Tam's hand found Elodie's sleeve. His gift was singing so loud now Elodie could almost hear it too—a high, desperate note, pointing not at the throne but past it, down some stair behind the dais. The third piece was close. Close enough that the two pieces over Elodie's heart pulled toward it like compass needles.

"We're not here to talk," Elodie said, and walked forward.

That was the mistake. Or maybe it wasn't a mistake at all; maybe the thing on the throne had simply been waiting for someone foolish or brave or grieving enough to walk forward, and grief was the one coin it never ran out of ways to spend.

* * *

The second voice stopped Elodie mid-stride like a rope across the throat.

"Ellie."

Their mother. Not the chorus's version, not the memory-version that lived in Elodie's own head, smoothed and softened by eleven years of missing her. This was the specific voice, the one with the faint rasp on the left side from the winter she'd nearly drowned, the one that said *Ellie* instead of *Elodie* because Elodie's father had died before choosing a formal name and she'd chosen one herself, casually, over breakfast, and it had stuck.

Elodie turned around despite everything. The throne room was empty except for the thing, which had not moved, but the voice came again anyway, from everywhere, the way voices came from everywhere when Elodie heard the dead—but the dead weren't supposed to be here, the palace wards should have—

"I hear the ovens," the voice said, warm and tired and real. "I can smell the rye going brown at the edges. Your father used to burn it, remember? Every single loaf. I told him it was character."

"She's dead," Tam said quietly, beside Elodie. "She's been dead since—" He stopped, because Elodie's look must have been terrible.

"She is," the thing agreed, in the mother-voice, gently, horribly. "Eleven years, four months. She fell ill in autumn and was gone by the first snow, and her child held her hand and heard her go quiet, and has heard nothing from her since, because death is death, little carver, even for you." A pause, delicately measured. "But I am not death. I am what was gathered before death could have her. I am what the pact took, piece by piece, gift by gift, from every gifted soul for nine hundred years. And some of what I hold still remembers being loved."

"Don't listen," Tam said. "Ellie. Don't."

But Elodie had spent their whole life listening—that was the whole of them, the whole architecture of their soul, a person built out of other people's voices—and the thing knew it, and aimed accordingly.

"Ask me something," the mother-voice offered. "Something only she would know. Test me. I want you to test me."

And the cruelest part—the part Elodie would carry afterward, into the next chapter of everything—was that Elodie wanted to. Not because they believed. Because the question was already in their mouth, had been in their mouth for eleven years, the question they'd have asked any bone, any ghost, any door between worlds if one had ever opened:

*Did it hurt? Were you afraid, at the end? Did you know I was there?*

"What do you want," Elodie said instead, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect.

"To give her back."

The thing finally moved. It descended the steps of the dais one at a time, and with each step it changed—it rippled through shapes the way water changes when you stir it. A soldier. An old woman. A child. A king with a kind face whom Elodie recognized from the chorus's oldest memories: Aldous the First, the true king, nine hundred years younger than his bones.

"That is what I am offering," it said. "Not a trick. Not a shade. The binding holds ten thousand souls, and souls are not fuel, they are *material*. I can shape material. I can take what remembers being your mother and build it a body of the same craft that built mine. Warm hands. Her laugh. The rasp on the left side. She will make bread, and it will be good bread, and she will call you Ellie, and she will mean it, because the parts of her that meant things—I kept those. I keep everything."

It stopped six feet away. Close enough to touch. The crown bent the air above it like a wound in the world.

"All I ask," it said, "is the pieces in your coat. Set them on the floor, walk out the way you came, and go home to your guild. Take the boy. Take the fence. Live your small warm life. In exchange, I will send her to you within the year, and no one will ever know she was anything but a woman who survived her illness and moved south to be near her child."

The silence after was enormous.

Elodie became aware, distantly, of their own body: the sweat cold between their shoulder blades, the two regalia pieces burning against their sternum like coals, the chorus pressing up against the inside of their skull—not shouting, the dead never shouted, but leaning, all of them, ten thousand strong, leaning toward Elodie the way a crowd leans toward a ledge.

*He lies,* said the dead king. *Or rather—he doesn't. That's the horror of him. Everything he says is true except the price.*

*Take it,* said another voice, newer, rawer—the keeper of the Wayhouse of Small Miracles, burned out of her own hearth three weeks ago. *Gods forgive me, carver, if it were my daughter asking, I'd tell you to take it.*

*Don't,* said a third, and a fourth, and a fifth, and the chorus split down the middle like a family arguing at a sickbed, because every dead soul in the binding had been someone's mother once, or someone's child.

"Your friends are talking to you," the thing said kindly. "I can feel them leaning. Ask them how many of them I've made this offer to. Ask them how many refused."

"How many?" Elodie asked.

"One," said the thing. "You."

* * *

Tam's fingers closed around Elodie's wrist. Not pulling. Just holding. The way you hold someone standing at the edge of ice.

"My mom left," Tam said, conversationally, to the thing wearing the crown, in the fearless flat voice he used when he was most frightened. "When I was seven. Walked out with one bag and never wrote. You know what I'd give to hear her say my name again?" He shrugged. "Nothing. Because it wouldn't be her saying it. It'd be you, doing an impression." His grip tightened on Elodie's wrist. "Ellie carved her mother's last words into a piece of whalebone when she was nineteen. I've seen it. It's in the guild hall. You know what it says?"

The thing said nothing. For the first time, it seemed to be listening.

"It says: *Tell our story true, or don't tell it.*" Tam looked up at Elodie. "That's the whole message. Eleven words. She trusted you with the truth, not with a copy."

The mother-voice came one more time, and this time it cracked, and Elodie would never know whether the crack was crafted or real, and that not-knowing was itself the wound:

"Ellie. Sweetheart. Don't let them use me as a weapon."

"There she is," Elodie said softly. "There's the weapon."

They reached into their coat.

The thing's whole posture changed—leaned forward, hunger visible through the borrowed face—and Elodie let it lean, let it think it had won, and drew out not the lead cases but the carving knife, Quill's knife, the one tool they had carried through fire and flood and a blockaded strait.

"No," Elodie said. "You don't get her. You don't get any of them. You've been wearing my dead like a coat for nine hundred years, and tonight the coat comes off."

"You cannot unmake me," the thing said, and all the voices came at once now—mother, king, keeper, soldier, child, ten thousand throats in one mouth, a hurricane shaped like grief. "I am the pact. I am the crown. Every gifted soul for nine centuries feeds me, carver. I am the sum of everyone you have ever lost, and you are one small bone-cutter with a knife."

"Yeah," Elodie said. Behind the dais, through the arch Tam's gift had found, something was beginning to sing—the third piece, answering the two against Elodie's chest, and the song had edges, and the edges were sharp. "But I'm the one who keeps the ledgers. And I know exactly what you're worth."

They set the two regalia pieces on the black marble floor between themselves and the throne, and began, with a steady hand, to carve.
