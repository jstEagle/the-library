## 19. Unbinding

The throne room of the usurper smelled of cold iron and old incense, and beneath both, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, of rot.

Elodie knelt on the marble with the three regalia pieces laid out before them: the fingerbone ring, the tide-temple crown-band, and the treasury scepter-tip, still warm from Tam's hands. Tam crouched beside them, one palm flat against Elodie's shoulder blade, breathing in the slow rhythm they'd practiced in the smuggler's hold. Behind them, filling the vast room like weather, the chorus pressed close — a hundred dead voices braided into one long note, and threaded through it all, patient and cracked and terribly human, the voice of the true king.

*Now,* he said. *Before it gathers itself again.*

Above them, on the dais, the thing wearing the crown was screaming.

It had stopped pretending to be a man. The court agent's body hung slack in its own grey gloves like a coat on a hook, and what moved inside it now was larger than the body could hold — a shape made of stolen light, of gifts eaten whole, of every generation the pact had swallowed since the first founders signed their names in blood and bone. It wore faces. It wore Elodie's mother's face for a moment, then Sera's, then Tam's young and terrified, cycling through them like a merchant showing wares.

"You could have everything back," it said in his mother's voice, in *her* voice, warm and low the way she'd sung to him before the fever took her. "Every voice you've ever lost. I keep them all, little carver. I am the only ledger that never loses an entry."

"I know," Elodie said, and picked up the graver.

Their hands did not shake. That surprised them most. Twenty-two years of carving messages for the grieving, and their hands had learned a discipline deeper than fear.

"Tam," they said quietly. "Anchor me."

"I'm here." His fingers tightened on their shoulder. His small gift hummed against their spine like a plucked string — the gift of finding, turned now to its truest use: finding *them*, holding them in place against whatever current the thing on the dais might drag them into. "I can feel where you are. I won't lose you."

The chorus rose behind them. The dead king's voice led it, and Elodie finally understood what the chorus had been trying to tell them across all those nights of visions: the binding was not a chain. It was a *contract*. And every contract has terms. Every contract has a cut line — the place where, if you carve precisely enough, the whole agreement comes apart.

The terms were written in the regalia. They had always been written in the regalia. Three pieces, three oaths, three generations of gifted souls fed into the joining. Quill's ancestors had hidden the pieces not to destroy the king but because destroying him would have meant unmaking the pact without severing it — leaving a hundred bound souls loose and starving. There was only one clean way out, and it required a price no founder had been willing to pay.

A gift, freely given, poured into the cut.

Elodie set the graver against the fingerbone ring and felt the chorus lean in.

They began to carve.

The first stroke went deep. Bone is honest material — it takes a blade the way truth takes a witness — and this bone had waited four hundred years for someone to write on it honestly. Elodie carved the first term of the unbinding: *what was taken returns*. As the graver moved, they felt something move in answer, a great slow shifting in the dark under the world, like ice breaking up in spring.

The thing on the dais stopped wearing faces.

"No," it said, in a voice suddenly its own — vast, hollow, ancient, frightened. "You don't understand what you're unmaking. The pact holds the kingdom together. Without it—"

"The kingdom will do what kingdoms do," Elodie said. "Carve its own way."

Second piece. The crown-band, cool and heavy, humming with tide-magic. Elodie pulled it close and carved the second term: *what was borrowed is forgiven*. Their gift surged toward the cut without being asked — eager, hungry, wanting to pour itself into the work the way water wants downhill — and Elodie held it back with gritted teeth. Not yet. Not until all three cuts were made. A contract half-severed was worse than none.

The usurper came off the dais.

It didn't walk; it *poured*, shadow and stolen light flooding down the steps toward them, and Tam shouted and gripped harder, and Elodie felt his anchor hold — felt himself fixed to the marble, to the moment, to his own name, while the darkness broke around them like surf around a stone. Voices came with it, dozens, hundreds, the dead it had eaten screaming through it:

*Liar. Traitor. Carver. You are one of us. You belong to us. Your mother is here, your mother is HERE—*

"I know she isn't," Elody said, and hated the crack in their own voice, and kept carving anyway. "You're wearing her like you wear everything. She'd be ashamed."

The third piece. The scepter-tip. Elodie took it up and found the last term waiting, the one the dead king had shown them in the visions, the one that made their stomach turn even now: *what is given freely cannot be reclaimed*.

The price.

"Elodie." Tam's voice, very small. "What does that one say?"

"What it says." They looked back at him once — his dirty face, his chapped lips bitten white, his eyes so full of stubborn love it hurt to look at. "Listen. When this is done, I'm going to be ordinary. No voices. No hearing. Just... quiet."

"That's awful," Tam said.

"It's fair," Elodie said. "I spent my whole life listening to the dead. Time I listened to the living instead." They smiled at him, and meant it, mostly. "Besides. You'll talk enough for both of us."

And then, because there was nothing left to say that mattered, Elodie turned back to the bone and made the final cut.

The graver bit deep. The words went home. And Elodie opened themselves the way they'd never opened for any client, any ghost, any grief — opened the door in their chest where the gift lived, the hearing, the listening, the twenty-two years of other people's dead whispering through their skull — and gave it all away into the cut.

It went like blood goes, like breath goes, like warmth leaves a body into winter air. For one endless moment Elodie heard everything at once: the whole chorus of the bound and the dead rising in a single chord, the dead king weeping openly, the usurper howling as its stolen sustenance turned to smoke, the pact's four hundred years of terms unraveling clause by clause, thread by thread, soul by soul—

Then silence.

Not quiet. *Silence.* The difference between a room with no one in it and a room where everyone has just left.

The usurper came apart like ash in wind. There was no dramatic end, no last curse. It simply lost its argument for existing — a contract dissolved, a debtor with no debts left to enforce — and unraveled into motes of grey light that drifted down over the throne, over the empty gloves, over the slack face of the court agent who had been, once, just a man doing terrible work for terrible wages.

On the dais, something small remained. A figure of bones, seated on the steps where the crown had fallen — the true king, freed at last, mortal again for exactly as long as it took him to die.

Tam let go of Elodie's shoulder and ran to him, but Elodie couldn't move. Couldn't hear. Sat in the wreckage of their own silence and breathed, and breathed, and breathed.

The king's voice reached them one last time — not through the gift, which was gone, but through the plain old miracle of ears and air, the way the living speak to each other. Faint. Human. Grateful.

"Carver," the dead king said, with his real mouth, in his real voice, for the first time in four hundred years. "Thank you."

He died smiling, in a ruined throne room, free.

* * *

Afterward there was dust and daylight and the sound of Tam crying, and Elodie sat in the middle of it all and felt nothing where the chorus used to be.

It wasn't grief, exactly. Or it was, but grief the way you grieve a limb — not the pain of losing something outside you, but the strangeness of reaching for something that had been part of you and finding only air. All day, since childhood, there had been the murmur. The dead, murmuring. Now there was just Elodie, alone in their own head for the first time in memory, and the aloneness was so vast it echoed.

"Hey." Tam knelt in front of them, face blotchy, hands steady. He held out the dead king's bones, wrapped in his own cloak. "We should take him home. Bury him right. You said — you always said the dead deserve their names said over them properly."

"I can't say them anymore," Elodie said. "Not the way I used to."

Tam considered this with the fierce practicality he brought to everything. "So we say them the regular way. Loud. Out loud. Like people do."

Elodie laughed — a wet, cracked sound, surprised out of them — and took the bundle, and stood. Their legs held. Outside, somewhere beyond the broken doors of the palace, bells were beginning to ring: not alarm bells, not yet, but the old bells, the ones that rang for coronations and festivals, rung now by whoever had noticed the sky change color over the palace, by whoever had felt the weight lift. Word would spread. Armies would stand down or wouldn't. Cities would rise or wouldn't. None of that was theirs to carry tonight.

Tonight there was only this: a silent carver, a crying apprentice, a bundle of freed bones, and a long road home.

"Come on," Elodie said, and put their arm around Tam, and walked out of the throne room into the noise of the living, which was — they were astonished to find — more than enough.

Behind them, on the empty throne, the crown lay in pieces, and did not sing.
