## 20. Silence

The first thing I understood was that the dust had stopped singing.

Not the dust itself—the throne room was full of it, hanging in the light that came through the shattered rose window, gold and grey and slow as snowfall. The dust was fine. The dust was only dust. But for twenty-nine years, everything in my hearing had carried a second voice beneath it: the hum of old wood remembering the tree, the murmur of stone that had been quarried by men whose names nobody wrote down. I had lived my whole life inside a chorus so constant I had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat until it stutters.

Now it had stopped.

The silence was not empty. That was the strange thing. It had *weight*, the way deep water has weight. It pressed against my ears from the inside. I sat on the steps of the dais with Tam's hand on my shoulder and I listened as hard as I had ever listened in my life, and what I heard was Tam breathing, and the wind moving through the broken window, and somewhere far below, water finding its way through the palace's ruined veins.

Only that. Only the living world.

"Elodie." Tam's voice sounded strange without the dead underneath it. Everything did. "Elodie, say something."

"I'm deciding what to say," I said, and my own voice startled me—thin, ordinary, a voice that belonged to exactly one person.

* * *

We had done it, I think. It was hard to be sure, in the way that it is hard to be sure of anything you watched happen from the inside of a storm. The thing that had worn the crown for four hundred years was gone. I had felt it go—not heard it go, that was the cruelty of it, I had felt the unbinding travel up my arm like cold water in reverse, and then there had been a moment of *absence* so total that my knees had gone out from under me, and when I looked up, the throne was only a throne.

Old, ugly, gilded. A chair with pretensions. The kind of thing a vain man commissions and a vain man's grandson gilds and a vain man's grandson's grandson dies in.

And on the steps beside it, arranged with a care I did not remember arranging, the king's bones.

I had carried them out of the binding itself. That part I remembered clearly, because it was the last thing I ever heard. When the pact unraveled—when the four hundred years of stolen gifts came apart like a knot picked loose—the souls inside it had not scattered. They had *left*, one by one, and each of them had paused, on their way out of the world, to say something. Not to me. Most of them didn't know I was there. They said it to the air, to their own long-dead children, to the god or the dark or whatever they had been promised and finally received. *I'm coming. I'm sorry. It's over. It's over. It's over.*

And the king—Aldric, the true king, the one whose fingerbone a frightened client had brought to my workbench a lifetime ago, which was four months ago—Aldric had said, in a voice like a door opening after centuries: *Thank you, carver. Now let me be quiet.*

I had let him. I had let all of them. And the gift had gone out of me through the same door they left by, and it had not come back, and I understood, sitting on the cold steps, that it was never going to.

"Is he—" Tam crouched beside the bones. My apprentice. Not my apprentice, not anymore, but the word had roots. He was sixteen and his face was grey with exhaustion and there was soot in his curls and he was the reason I was alive, because when the unbinding had started to pull me under with it, he had held on. Anchored, the chorus had called him. The dead had chosen their word well. "Is he free?"

"He's free." I picked up the skull. It was lighter than I expected. All my life I had handled bones that *meant* something, bones that hummed with what they'd witnessed, and I had forgotten that bones were also just bones. Small. Fragile. The architecture of a man who had once been a boy who had once been afraid of the dark, probably. "He's just a man now. That was the whole point."

Tam nodded, and then his face crumpled, and he sat down hard on the step next to me and cried the way sixteen-year-olds cry, all at once and without dignity, and I put my arm around him and let him, because there was no one left in the world for either of us to perform composure for.

The dead king did not thank me again. The dust did not murmur. The silence held.

* * *

Sera found us at dawn.

She came up through the ruined gates with a company of Meridian's free-swords at her back and her ledgers under her arm, because Sera carried her ledgers the way other people carried weapons, and when she saw us sitting on the dais steps among the wreckage of four centuries of tyranny, she stopped. She looked at the empty throne. She looked at the bones in my lap. She looked at my face, and whatever she saw there made her close her eyes for a moment, the way you do when a bill arrives that you knew was coming and had hoped to defer.

"Oh, Elodie," she said.

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. If you say it out loud I'll have to have feelings about it, and I'm rationing."

That got a wet, exhausted laugh out of Tam. Sera climbed the steps and sat down on my other side, which was the Sera way of saying everything—she didn't touch me, didn't fuss, just *stayed*, arranging herself into my silence like furniture that belonged there.

"Quill's decoy worked," she said after a while, because someone had to say the report out loud or it would stay a dream. "The usurper's whole western host broke for the coast when they saw the palace burn. Half of them have thrown down their arms. The council's sending criers to every city by nightfall." She paused. "The council is also asking, with tremendous politeness, who is going to tell them what happens now."

"The council," I said, "can read a ledger like anyone else."

"The council," said Sera, "is terrified of you. You walked into a throne room that has eaten every soul who touched it for four hundred years, and you walked out. They're calling you the Unbinder in the streets already. It's got a ballad. The ballad is terrible."

"I heard the ballad," Tam said. "There's a verse where I do a backflip."

"There's a verse where we *all* do a backflip," Sera said. "Poetic license."

I should have laughed. Some part of me did, distantly, the way you hear a festival in the next street. But mostly I was looking at the king's skull in my hands and thinking about the voice I would never hear in it again, and Sera, who had spent thirty years reading people the way I read bone, went quiet and let me.

* * *

We buried him in the old royal necropolis, because Sera said the politics demanded it, and because Tam said the man had waited four hundred years and deserved to be put down properly, and because I said nothing, and my nothing was the loudest vote.

The necropolis sat on a hill above the city, a honeycomb of tombs cut into white stone, older than the binding, older than the crown. The kings buried there had been buried by people who believed a dead king could still hear. I kept thinking about that as we walked up through the cypress trees. Whole generations, talking to their dead, certain the dead were listening. I had been the only proof, in all the free cities, that they were right—and now I was nothing but another mourner, speaking into the dark on faith.

The council wanted a state funeral. Sera negotiated them down to a quiet one, which in council-speak meant only two hundred mourners and a choir. The choir sang. The priests of six traditions argued over the rites and compromised on all of them at once, which produced a ceremony that was either very inclusive or very confused. Tam carried the casket on one shoulder, and a Meridian free-sword carried it on the other, and I walked behind with my hands empty, which was the strangest part. My hands were always full of bone. My hands had been full of bone since I was nine years old, since the day I picked up my grandmother's carving knife and heard my grandmother's voice come out of the handle, telling me where she'd hidden the money and that she was proud of me and that the knife was sharper than it looked.

I kept waiting for the skull to say something. Even now. Even knowing. When Tam set the casket down in the tomb and the masons began sealing it, some animal part of me strained toward it the way you strain toward a sound in another room—*say something, say anything, tell me it was worth it*—and the tomb said nothing, because tombs say nothing, because they always had, and everything I had ever heard come out of one came from the gift, and the gift was spent.

I had known that was the price. I had carved the unbinding myself, into the regalia bones, in the old founder's script, and I had written the cost into the design the way you write a figure into a ledger: *one gift, whole and irrevocable, to feed the cut.* The dead king had warned me. The chorus had warned me. I had fed my hearing into the severance like a coin into a slot and I would do it again, I would do it a thousand times, and I still stood at that tomb with my empty hands and grieved like a widow.

Because it hadn't just been a tool. It had been *me*. I had been the girl who heard the dead before I had been anything else. It was the first fact of me, before my name. And I had spent it gladly, and now I had to find out who was left.

* * *

The others gave me the night after the funeral. Sera understood without being told—she took Tam back to the embassy house the council had lent us, and she didn't say *are you all right* or *you did the right thing*, which were the two sentences I could least have borne. She just squeezed my shoulder once, hard, and said, "There's soup if you want it. It's bad soup. Tam made it."

"I'll want it," I said. "Later."

I walked the city instead.

Meridian at night, after a war, is a city holding its breath between grief and celebration. Fires burned in the streets—not the grey gloves' fires, not this time, but cook-fires and bonfires and candles set in windows, because the Gift-Hunt was dead, the proclamation was ash, and people who had hidden their whole lives were standing in doorways looking at the sky like men let out of a cellar. I passed a woman teaching her daughter a small cantrip, a little light in the palm, right there on a public street, unhidden, and the girl's laugh was the loudest thing I'd heard all day, and I stood in the shadows and watched until my chest hurt.

They'll never know, I thought. What it cost. What it was like to carry a gift when carrying one was a death sentence, and what it was like to be the one who could hear all the others carrying theirs. The whole hidden country of the gifted, whispering through bone and ash and candle-smoke, and I had been its post office, its confessor, its ear. And now the country was free and its post office was closed.

A man was selling roasted chestnuts near the fountain square. I bought some because I needed to do a thing with my hands and my money, and while he wrapped them he talked at me—the way people talk at strangers after a victory, giddy, unguarded—and he said, "They say the Unbinder's a carver. A bone-carver, from the north. They say she heard the dead king himself."

"She did," I said.

"Is it true?" he asked, leaning in, delighted, terrible. "Is it true the dead talk? My mother's been gone two years, and I—"

And I looked at his open, hopeful face, and I had to decide, in the space of a heartbeat, what to do with the first lie of my new life.

"They talk," I said, because it was true, because it had been true, because the least I owed my gift was to let it keep its dignity in the retelling. "They talk. And they mostly say the same thing. That they're all right. That they want you to eat properly and stop worrying."

He laughed and pressed an extra handful of chestnuts into my hands and would not take payment, and I walked on with hot chestnuts in my cold fingers and tears running down my face, and no one in the celebrating city noticed, because grief and joy look the same from a distance, and everyone was crying that night anyway.

* * *

I went back to the embassy house near midnight. The soup was bad. Tam had made it with too much salt and not enough of anything else, and I ate two bowls of it at the long table while he sat across from me pretending to read and actually watching me with the anxious, loyal face of a dog who has decided a person is his.

"You're different," he said finally.

"I'm exactly the same. That's the problem."

"No," Tam said, and he said it with the flat certainty he saved for the things his gift told him, except his gift was for finding lost things, and I wondered—not for the first time, and not for the last—what he'd found, sitting in that throne room, holding on to me. "You're different. Quieter. But not smaller." He turned a page he hadn't read. "Before, you were always listening to two things at once. Us, and them. Now you're just—" he gestured at me, at the table, at the bad soup, "—*here*. All the way here."

I wanted to tell him he was wrong. I looked for the argument and couldn't find it.

Sera came in from the balcony with the night air on her and a sheaf of council correspondence under her arm, and she dropped it on the table with a thump that said *business*, and I loved her for it, because business was a thing I still knew how to do.

"Three items," she said, sitting. "One: the council has formally voted to pardon every name in the Ember Guild's ledgers, which means I have to reconstruct those ledgers, which means, Elodie, I need your codes, and yes, I know you encrypted them out of spite, and yes, I'm going to decode every one of them if it takes a year. Two: Quill sent a bird from the north. He's alive, he's insufferable about it, and he says—" she squinted at the scrap, "—he says, and I quote, 'Tell the carver the guild is hers if she wants it, and if she doesn't want it, it's hers anyway, that's how inheritance works, I checked.'"

I looked at the scrap of paper. Quill's handwriting, cramped and unrepentant. Twenty years of ledgers, of impossible things, of a family assembled out of runaways and orphans and one bone-carver who heard too much. Mine. The word sat in my chest like a coal.

"And three?" Tam asked.

"Three." Sera's voice changed, and I looked up, and she was wearing the expression she wore when a deal turned strange in her hands. "A bird also came from the tide-temple. From the keepers. They say that since the unbinding, the second regalia piece—the one Tam pulled out of the flooded vault—" she paused, choosing the words like a woman walking on ice, "—it's *humming*. Every morning at dawn. And this morning, for the first time, a keeper's daughter put her hand on it, a girl with no gift anyone ever found, no gift at all, and the humming got louder. And she says—" Sera looked at me across the table, and the candle flame stood very still between us, "—she says it sounds like singing. And the keepers want to know, from the Unbinder herself, what they should do with a relic that has started to sing."

I sat back. Outside, Meridian's fires burned, and the free cities stretched away into the dark, full of people who had spent their whole lives hiding and were only now learning what their hands could do. And in my chest, where the chorus had lived for twenty-nine years, the silence was still vast, and still strange, and—new, this, as of tonight, as of the bad soup and the chestnut-seller's face—not entirely empty.

Because a hum, I thought, is not a voice. And a girl with no gift, touching a singing bone, is not a dead woman's message. It is something else. Something that doesn't need me to hear it.

"Tell the keepers," I said slowly, feeling my way, "not to bury it. And tell them to bring the girl to Meridian." I looked at Tam, and Tam was already grinning, because he'd gotten there first, because he always got there first when something lost was about to be found. "I think," I said, "we should open a new ledger."
