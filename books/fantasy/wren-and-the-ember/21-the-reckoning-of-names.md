## 21. The Reckoning of Names

The ledgers came back to me in crates.

Three months after the unbinding, a cart rolled up to the guild hall on Meridian's Lantern Street, and two council clerks unloaded eleven wooden boxes, each one sealed with the grey wax of the old crown's archive. Confiscated records. Everything the Gift-Hunt had taken from the Ember Guild's northern house before it burned—every page Sera Voss had ever coded, ciphered, or disguised.

I stood in the doorway with my arms crossed and watched them stack the crates in our entry hall like they were delivering firewood.

"You'll want to sign for these," said the elder clerk, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and the particular patience of someone who has spent thirty years being shouted at by officials.

"I know what's in them," I said. "I wrote most of it."

"Then you'll understand why we're glad to be rid of them." He held out the receipt. "The council has no use for coded trade records. Half the archivists tried to crack your ciphers and gave up. The other half requested reassignment."

"What did you do with the ones you did crack?"

He had the grace not to smile. "Burned them, madam. By order of the new charter. Names given under coercion are not evidence. They're wounds." He tucked his pen away. "We were told you'd want them anyway."

"We were told right."

After the cart rattled off down Lantern Street, I stood a long while looking at the crates. Eleven boxes of everything I'd spent twenty years hiding. Every client entered as *the Grey Glove*, every gifted child recorded as *a parcel of wool*, every desperate mother logged as *a shipment of salt*. Twenty years of making people invisible so well that even I sometimes forgot they'd existed.

Tam found me there, of course. He always found things.

"Are we burning them?" he asked hopefully. Tam had developed an enthusiasm for bonfires since the war that Quill said was unhealthy and I said was earned.

"No," I said. "We're reading them."

* * *

It took four weeks.

Elodie did the deciphering—I'd built my codes on their bone-carving conventions, glyph-stacks only a carver could read at speed—and I sat across the table doing the harder work: putting names back where codes had been.

That was the part nobody warns you about, when you spend a career erasing people. Somebody has to un-erase them. And the somebody is you, line by line, with a pen, remembering.

*Parcel of wool, autumn, 14 lbs.* That was Maren Ostell, who traded three years of her own memories of her dead husband because she couldn't afford bread for her daughters and thought forgetting would hurt less than hunger. It hadn't. She'd come back twice asking us to find the memories again. We never could.

*Shipment of salt, spring, one crate.* That was Jem Corrow, age nine, whose gift was that broken things mended themselves near him. His mother had sold him to a tinker caravan for passage south during the first purge, and the caravan had sold him onward, and by the time the Hunt ended he was somewhere in the free cities, if he was anywhere. Tam put out word through every finder's network from here to Saltmere. As of this writing, no answer. I keep the entry open. I will keep it open until I die.

*A parcel of wool, winter, 6 lbs.* That was a girl called Wren.

I stopped when I got to that one. Elodie looked up from the glyphs.

"You knew her?" they asked.

"I placed her," I said. "Eight years ago. Fleeing a village that decided her hearing-voices gift meant she was cursed. I moved her through three safehouses to a family in the hill towns who owed me a favor. Then she went into the ledger as wool and out of my head entirely, because that was how I survived this work—one name at a time, packed away where I didn't have to carry it."

Elodie was quiet a moment. They don't hear voices anymore—they've told me the silence is like weather now, something they live inside rather than something that happens to them—but I think sometimes they still listen out of habit, the way an amputee's hand closes around a cup.

"Wren," Elodie said. "That's a good name for a book."

"It's a good name for a girl," I said. "Go find out if she's still alive. You're better at doors than I am."

They went. They came back three days later with mud on their boots and a letter written in a careful adult hand, and inside the letter was eight years of a life—a family that kept her, a trade learned, two younger brothers who adored her, and one line at the bottom that made me leave the room so neither of them would see my face:

*Tell the fence who sent the wool that I remember her kindness, and that I have named my daughter after the road she took me down. Her name is Ember.*

* * *

The Council of Ledgers convened on the first of Thawmonth to formalize what the war had already made true.

I attended as the guild's representative, which still struck me as absurd—I'd spent two decades cultivating a reputation as a woman who did not exist, and now there was a chair with a little brass plaque reading VOSS, EMBER GUILD, and clerks brought me water without being asked. Power, I have learned, is mostly furniture.

The Charter of Sanctuary ran to forty-one clauses. Most of them were the usual legal gristle—definitions, jurisdictions, appeals. But clause nine was mine. I'd fought for it through six drafting sessions and one genuinely vicious argument with the Meridian harbormaster, and it read, in full:

*No person shall be recorded in any register of gifts against their will. Where a gifted person consents to registration, the record shall bear their true name, given freely, and no such record may be used as evidence in any proceeding, nor transferred, sold, or surrendered to any foreign power. A name given in fear is theft; a name given freely is citizenship.*

The harbormaster had wanted a central registry. Of course he had. Men like him always want lists—it makes the world feel like cargo. I told him, in open session, that the last government that kept a registry of the gifted had used it as a harvest schedule, and that if the free cities wanted to become the thing they'd just finished bleeding to destroy, I could recommend several excellent clerks who were very good at lists. The chamber had gone very quiet. Clause nine passed unanimous.

Afterward, a young delegate from Saltmere caught me on the steps. She had the look of someone who'd rehearsed her question the whole way over and lost it anyway.

"Madam Voss—the old registers. The crown's registers. What happens to them? The ones that were kept before."

"Every copy we can find gets burned," I said. "Publicly, in the squares, with the names read aloud first."

"Read aloud?"

"That's the point of a reckoning," I told her. "You can't repair what you won't say out loud."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her write something down, and I understood that I had just become someone's precedent, and that someday some stubborn old woman in Saltmere would be fighting her own harbormaster with my words in her mouth. Good. Let the words do the work. Words are cheaper than wars and last longer.

* * *

Quill retired in the spring, in the manner of all great men retiring: badly, then suddenly.

For weeks he wandered the guild hall rearranging stock that hadn't needed rearranging since the war, re-grading inventory that was no longer inventory (the impossible trade having been formally dissolved by clause fourteen—no more bottled storms, no more traded years, though the council grandfathered in the healing bones, because even legislators aren't fools), and telling anyone who'd stand still that he intended to travel north and see what remained of the old house.

He did go north. He came back with a handful of ash from the foundation stones in a pouch around his neck and a look on his face I'd never seen on him in twenty-five years, which was peace.

"It's gone," he told us at supper, turning his cup. "All of it. The cellars, the vaults, the wall where I hid the first regalia piece when I was younger than Tam. There's a goat paddock on top of it now." He laughed—a real laugh, rusty from disuse. "A goat paddock. Forty years of the most dangerous secret in the kingdom, and goats."

"You could rebuild," Tam said.

"No." Quill set the cup down. "That's the whole lesson, boy. Some things should stay burned. The guild I built was a hiding place. Hiding places are for when the world is hunting you." He looked around the table—at Elodie, at Tam, at me, at the hall itself with its open windows and its lanterns lit at dusk without fear. "This isn't a hiding place anymore. Whatever it is now, it needs building forward, not backward. Which means it needs young hands."

"I'm twenty-two," Tam protested.

"And you've been running this place de facto since the strait," I said. "We've all just been pretending otherwise to spare your feelings."

Tam turned red. Elodie smiled into their soup. Quill signed over the deed the next morning with a lawyer, two witnesses, and a flask of the good brandy he'd been saving for a occasion worth ruining himself for, and left on the southbound coach before noon so none of us could make a scene.

He sent a letter from the coast. It said, in its entirety:

*Sera—you were the best lie I ever told and the worst friend I deserved. Keep the books honest. —Q*

I keep it in the front of the new ledger. Some debts you repay by carrying them forward.

* * *

Which brings me to the ledger itself.

The old ledgers were coded because honesty was lethal. The new ledger—the one Elodie proposed the night the humming bone arrived, the one we opened on the first page of the new year—is honest on purpose. One name per entry. True trades, true goods, true histories. If a gifted person wants their work listed—bone-craft, finding, hedge-healing, weather-sense—we list it the way any guild lists any craft, openly, with rates and references. If they want nothing listed, nothing is listed, and clause nine stands behind their silence.

The first page reads:

*Ember Guild, established in hiding in the year of the Grey Regency, reborn in freedom in the year of the Unbinding. Our first trade was impossibility. Our second was silence. Our third, and lasting trade, is this: that no one who comes through our door need be anything other than what they are.*

Below it, the entries begin. Real names. Real work.

Maren Ostell's daughters came to us in early summer—not for magic, just for work. The elder one has a steady hand and no gift at all and doesn't want one, and she's learning ledger-keeping at the desk by the window, and when she asked me why the guild bothered keeping records of ordinary folk too, I told her that the fastest way to make a sanctuary fail is to make it a special place. Special places get raided. Ordinary places just get visited.

Jem Corrow's entry stays open. Tam checks the finder-networks every week. Last month a caravan master in the eastern hills swore he'd once known a boy who could mend things by holding them, but the boy had moved on years ago, heading south, heading anywhere. South. I marked the entry *active, southern routes*. Hope, I've learned, is a logistics problem. You route it. You resupply it. You refuse to let it lapse for want of follow-up.

And last week, a woman arrived on Lantern Street with a child of maybe six, shy as a wren, and asked—carefully, testing the word in her mouth like ice—if this was the place where you could tell someone what you were and have it written down somewhere safe instead of somewhere dangerous.

"Yes," I said.

"She hears things," the mother whispered. "Dead things, living things. She's frightened everyone her whole life. Everyone but us."

The girl looked up at me with eyes that reminded me of someone, and I knelt down on the guild hall floor, an old fence with bad knees, in front of witnesses, and I said:

"Then let's give her a name in a book. What's yours?"

She told me. And I wrote it down—true name, freely given, witnessed—in a fair hand, in permanent ink, in a ledger that will sit behind no lock that a child can't ask to have opened, and I felt something settle in my chest that I'd been carrying crooked for twenty years.

Belonging, it turns out, can be written down. It just can't be written *for* someone. It has to be written *with* them, their hand on the pen beside yours, or it's just another kind of cage with nicer calligraphy.

* * *

Tonight the hall is loud. Tam's apprentices—he has three now, gods help us all—are arguing about inventory in the kitchen. Elodie is teaching carving to a circle of students who will never hear a voice in bone and don't need to, because Elodie says the craft teaches patience whether or not it teaches listening. The hearth remembers warmth the way it always has. The bread won't mold until tomorrow, and tomorrow it will be eaten anyway.

Upstairs, the new ledger lies open on my desk to the last written page. Below the final entry there is blank space, and blank space, in a ledger, is not emptiness. In a ledger, blank space is a promise that more is coming.

I dip the pen. I leave it resting in its groove, ready for morning, ready for whoever walks through the door next with a truth they've been carrying like contraband.

Because the hunt is over. But the reckoning—that goes on as long as there are names to give back.

And outside, on Lantern Street, someone has just knocked.
