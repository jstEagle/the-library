## 22. Wren and the Ember

Three years, and Elodie still reached for the silence the way a tongue finds a missing tooth.

It happened most mornings, in the hour before the apprentices arrived. They would stand at the workbench in the old ledger room—now a classroom, the ledgers themselves moved to Sera's rebuilt archive upstairs—and their hands would settle on a fresh piece of bone, and for half a heartbeat they would wait. Listening.

Nothing came. Nothing ever came. The dead had gone quiet as shut doors, all of them at once, the night Elodie fed the last of the gift into the cut. The king's chorus, the drowned keeper of the wayhouse, the woman whose last message had been the first thing Elodie ever carved—all of it folded into that single severing, spent like a coin too large for any purse.

Elodie had expected to mourn it forever. What they had not expected was how the mourning would soften into something almost like rest. A workshop full of children shouting about grain lines was its own kind of voices.

"Master Ishikawa." Pell, the youngest, stood in the doorway with sawdust in her hair and an accusation in her posture. "Tam says I have to redo the whole spoon."

"Did you carve the bowl too thin?"

"I carved it *artistically* thin."

Elodie bit down on a smile. "Then Tam is right, and so am I, and you'll do it again. Thin spoons snap. Snap spoons don't feed anyone." They held out their hand; she surrendered the ruin of birchwood with theatrical grief. "Artistry comes *after* it works. That's the first law of craft."

"And the second?"

"The second law is that your master also once carved something too thin, and his master made him do it twice."

Pell brightened at this—the eternal comfort of a master's confessed failures—and went off to suffer productively. Elodie turned back to the bench, to the bone blank waiting there: a practice piece, nothing more, a rib of long-dead ox for a student's first attempt at lettering. Ordinary bone. Ordinary work. They had promised themselves ordinary, and mostly they kept it.

The guild hall had changed less than Elodie feared it would. Quill's old counting room still smelled of tallow and cedar. The great hearth in the common room still remembered warmth longer than any fire had a right to—one small miracle the free cities' surveyors had catalogued and, finding it harmless, left alone. Sera's archive occupied the second floor now, its shelves heavy with ledgers written in real names: *Marta Voss, bone-carver, gifted-hearing, registered freely under the Sanctuary Act.* Names instead of codes. It had been Sera's condition for coming back, and the council, chastened by war and by Sera herself, had agreed to everything.

Quill lived above the stables these days, officially retired, unofficially present at every meal. He had stopped apologizing somewhere in the second year—not because the guilt was spent but because Elodie had finally told him that watching him flinch every time he entered a room was worse than the thing he was flinching about. He taught the older students trade arithmetic now. He was terrible at patience and magnificent at honesty, and the children loved him for both.

And Tam—

Tam ran the finding-house three streets over, where people brought lost things: wedding rings, runaway dogs, brothers who hadn't written from the coast in a year. His gift had grown the way a sapling grows when someone finally stops standing on it. He could find people now. Sometimes, when the loss was fresh enough, he said he could feel which way their hearts were leaning. He had found forty-one souls in three years, and he kept a tally chalked inside his doorframe, and he added to it only when someone came back to tell him the finding had mattered.

He was due for supper tonight. All of them were. It was the anniversary—the third since the throne room, the third since the king died free on a slab of stone while Elodie knelt in the wreckage of a crown, deaf for the first time in their life.

The city would light lamps along the strait road after dark. That had started spontaneously the first year and become ordinance the second: *the Ember Night*, the criers called it, though everyone just said the anniversary. In Meridian they burned no bonfires. Bonfires belonged to the usurper's harvest. Instead they lit small flames in windows—a candle for each name in Sera's ledgers, a light for every gift that had been taken before the taking stopped.

Elodie set the ox rib aside and went up to help Sera close the archive.

* * *

Sera counted by lamplight the way other people prayed.

She stood at the shelf of that year's register, running her finger down entries, lips moving. Three hundred and eleven names. Two hundred and ninety-four living within city walls. Seventeen scattered to other free cities. None remanded. She had fought for the word *none* the way she'd once fought over a smuggler's debt, and the council had learned that Sera Winterbourne did not repeat herself.

"Seventeen letters went out this morning," she said without turning. "Invitations. If they can't come home for the anniversary, they can at least know the door's open."

"You sent invitations to people who already know the door's open."

"I sent invitations to people who need to see it *written down*," Sera said. "There's a difference. You of all people should know it. Half your life you heard the dead because nobody wrote them down alive."

That landed truer than she probably intended. Elodie leaned against the doorframe. "Any trouble at the council?"

"Envoys from the eastern kingdoms want our registers 'shared for mutual security.' I told them our registers are written in ink that fades when read by unfriendly eyes." She closed the book. "It isn't true. But they believe it, because powerful men always believe magic serves whoever holds the leash. Let them waste a decade trying to buy fading ink."

"Sera."

"Mm."

"Thank you. For staying. After—" Elodie gestured vaguely, at the arrest, the cell, the pardon, all of it compressed into one wave of the hand.

Sera looked at them then, and her face did the rare thing it did maybe twice a year, which was show itself entirely. "You fed your gift into a binding older than the kingdoms so my name could be written in a book instead of scratched on a warrant. If you thank me for staying, I'll start charging you rent on the sentiment." She picked up the lamp. "Supper. Quill will burn the bread out of spite if we're late."

* * *

They burned the bread out of spite, or possibly out of being Quill, and ate the unburnt halves anyway around a table crowded with apprentices who had invited themselves, as apprentices do. Tam told the story of the flooded vaults for perhaps the hundredth time—he told it better every year, and every year the eel grew larger—and Pell demanded the part about the singing bone twice.

"It didn't sing," Tam said, patient, for the hundredth time. "It hummed. Like a struck bell after the sound's mostly gone. I felt it here." He touched his sternum. "Like something saying *here, here, here.*"

"What happened to it?" asked one of the smaller ones, a boy named Osk who still flinched at loud noises. "The regalia pieces."

"Buried," Quill said, and the table quieted, because Quill rarely spoke of it. "Deep, in salt ground, under stone no charter allows anyone to dig. The council knows. Three of us know the place. When we die, the knowledge dies with us, and the pieces stay buried until the world has forgotten why anyone would want them."

"And the king?" Osk again, small voice.

Elodie answered this one, because it was theirs to answer. "We carried him home. He lies under an unmarked stone in the orchard behind the hall, with the founders. No crown, no name. Just a man, resting." They paused. "He waited four hundred years to be free. The least we owed him was a quiet grave."

Osk nodded gravely, the way children nod at things they understand better than adults suspect, and returned to his bread.

Afterward, dishes done and apprentices herded home, the four of them sat in the common room the way they had sat through a hundred ordinary nights—Quill dozing upright, Tam whittling something he'd never finish, Sera reading, Elodie simply present, which was still, some nights, the strangest luxury of all. To be in a room with no task. To be wanted without function.

"Third year," Tam said eventually. "Does it get easier? The anniversary."

"Yes," Elodie said. "No. Both. It gets quieter, and then you miss the noise, and then you're ashamed of missing it, and then you make peace with the shame. Round and round." They looked into the banked fire. "I used to think the gift was what made me worth keeping. The guild took me in because I could hear the dead. It took me years to learn they'd have kept me anyway."

"We'd have kept you anyway," Sera said, not looking up from her page, and Tam nodded, and even Quill stirred enough to grunt agreement, and Elodie let the words settle into the place where the silence lived.

* * *

The child came at dusk, on the anniversary itself, while Elodie was banking the classroom stove.

They heard the footsteps on the stairs—small, hesitant, the particular rhythm of someone who has rehearsed a speech and is losing nerve with every stair—and turned to find Osk in the doorway, holding something wrapped in a scrap of cloth against his chest like a bird he was afraid would fly.

"Master Ishikawa. I—I found something. On the way to lessons yesterday. By the river." He crossed the room slowly. "I didn't tell anyone else. Tam says things that are lost should come to the finding-house, but—" He stopped. Unwrapped the cloth with fingers that shook only a little.

A bone. Small, old, yellowed with time—part of a bird's wing, Elodie thought, or the foot of something long dead. River-tumbled smooth.

"It hums," Osk whispered. "When I hold it. Not out loud. *Inside.* Like it's saying something, but I can't— I'm not gifted. Am I? I checked. The testers said I'm not. But it hums, Master Ishikawa. And you used to hear bones, before. Before the silence. So I thought—" He thrust it out. "Maybe you could hear it. Even a little."

The classroom was very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, the family's voices drifted up, preparing the evening's lamps.

Elodie looked at the bone and did not reach for it right away. Because they knew what it was, or suspected—they had known since the moment the boy said *hums*. A fragment. A seed. Perhaps a splinter of the old binding itself, washed loose and worn harmless by centuries of river; perhaps something older and stranger, a piece of the deep gift that had passed through Elodie like water through a channel and pooled somewhere new. Perhaps simply a bird bone, and a child's longing making music of nothing.

It didn't matter which. What mattered was the boy's face, and the choice standing in front of them both.

Elodie took it.

Turned it in their fingers. Held it to their ear the way they had held ten thousand bones across two lifetimes of listening, and opened whatever door remained where the gift used to live.

Nothing. The vast familiar silence.

But underneath the silence—so faint it might have been blood in their own ears, might have been memory, might have been the ghost of a ghost—something moved. Not a voice. Not words. A hum, low and patient, like a struck bell after the sound has mostly gone. Like something saying, in the only language it had left:

*Here. Here. Here.*

Elodie's breath caught. Their eyes burned. They lowered the bone slowly and found Osk watching them with an expression no tester had ever put on a child's face: hope, terrified of itself.

"Well?" he breathed.

"It hums," Elodie said.

The boy's whole body lit like a lamp.

"It hums," Elodie repeated, and crouched to his level, and closed the boy's fingers carefully back around the bone. "Listen to me, Osk. The testers measure loud gifts. Fire and finding and voices from the dark. They don't measure *this*—quiet things, patient things, things that wait inside small bones for the right ears. Maybe you have those ears. Maybe you don't. Either way—" they tapped the cloth-wrapped bundle gently, "—this doesn't belong in a finding-house or a vault or a drawer. This belongs in hands. Yours, I think. For now."

"Mine," Osk whispered.

"Yours. And when you've learned enough craft—and you will, because you'll be at lessons every day starting tomorrow, spoon and all—you and I are going to study it together. Properly. Slowly. The way my master taught me." Elodie straightened, and found their voice had gone rough, and decided not to mind. "Not because anything is hunting us anymore. Because some things are worth passing on."

Downstairs, the first anniversary lamps were being lit, one flame for every name in Sera's ledgers, and the light climbed the stairs soft and gold. Osk clutched the humming bone to his chest and grinned up at Elodie with wet eyes, and outside, across the rooftops of Meridian, the windows began to bloom.

"Come on," Elodie said, offering their hand. "The others will want to see."

And hand in hand, the bone-carver and the boy went down into the lamplight—to Quill dozing by the fire, to Sera with her ledgers of true names, to Tam with his chalk tally of the found—to the family that had chosen them, and been chosen, and stayed.

Somewhere under salt ground, a buried king slept unbound and dreamless. Somewhere in a boy's small hands, a gift that would not die waited patiently to begin again.

The ember held.
