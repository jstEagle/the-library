## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with selling charms in Greyhollow was that half of them actually worked.

Quentin Eastwick had learned this early, at seventeen, when old Marta Corvey bought a luck-knot for her fish stall and promptly hauled in a catch so fat the harbor master accused her of poaching his private waters. Quentin had smiled, taken the coin, and said nothing about how she'd whispered into the knotwork while braiding it — nothing about how she'd asked the river to be generous, and the river, obliging thing, had listened. In Greyhollow you did not say such things aloud. You did not even think them too loudly, if you could help it. The Magistrate's wardens wore grey cloaks and patient expressions, and they were very good at listening.

So on market day, under a sky gone the color of unwashed wool, Quentin arranged her wares on the same scarred trestle table she'd used for fifteen years: copper bells against nightmares, knotted thread against fever, little clay birds that were supposed to bring safe travel and mostly just brought compliments. Harmless. Almost all of them harmless. She priced them low and smiled her market smile, the one that made her forgettable, and she did not look at the ground, because the ground had been doing something it should not do.

It had started three days ago. A shiver under the cobbles, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Yesterday it had come again, longer, and the well water in her courtyard had swirled briefly counterclockwise for no reason wind or plumbing could explain. Today, standing behind her stall, she felt it through the soles of her boots like a drum struck somewhere far below the city — below the city's roots, below its ward-stones, below anything that ought to be down there at all.

*There it is again,* said a voice near her ankle. *Did you feel it? Don't step back. Don't make a face.*

Quentin kept her eyes on a customer, an apron-flecked baker's boy counting out coppers for a fever-thread. "Two bits," she said pleasantly. "Tie it on your left wrist, not the right."

Under the stall, wedged in the shadow between a crate of dried lavender and Quentin's boot, sat the shop-cat from the chandler's across the row — an enormous grey tom with one torn ear and the air of a magistrate himself. His name, as he told everyone who could hear him, which was no one but Quentin, was Duke of Ashes.

"Feel what?" she murmured, straightening a bell so no one would mark her lips moving.

*The knocking,* the Duke said, tail flicking. *Rats have gone strange about it. They say the deep ways are singing. My girl caught one yesterday — plump fellow, half-mad — and all he'd say was 'it opens, it opens.' Rats don't rhyme, Eastwick. Something down there is teaching them poetry.*

"That's grim," she said lightly, to the baker's boy, who nodded as though fever-threads often were, and moved off.

*I'm serious.* The Duke rose, stretched until every claw expressed an opinion, and fixed her with his yellow stare. *My human says the wardens doubled their rounds last night. She heard them talking. The border stones at Hollowmere went quiet for a full minute at moonset. Quiet, Eastwick. Wards aren't supposed to be quiet. Wards are supposed to hum like a hive.*

"They're always adjusting things," Quentin said. "New Magistrate's been in office six months. He likes to leave his fingerprints on everything."

*He can fingerprint a wall all he likes,* the Duke said. *That wasn't adjustment I felt through the floor this morning. That was strain. Like a rope taking weight it was never braided for.* He paused, and for once the cat's eternal irony dropped away. *You of all people know what happens when a rope parts.*

She knew. Her mother had died telling her, more or less — died fifteen years ago with blood on her hands and a word half-spoken, in a house that smelled of scorched feathers, leaving Quentin a locked box, a warning (*never let them hear you, never let them see*), and a silence Quentin had built her whole life inside of, brick by careful invisible brick.

"I sell charms," she said quietly. "I mind my own business. That's the whole trade."

*And yet,* said the Duke, *here you are, talking to a cat in the middle of a market square.*

Before she could answer, the bell above the chandler's door rang, and a warden came around the corner of the row.

Grey cloak. Patient expression. He wasn't looking at her; he was looking at the cobbles, crouching, running one gloved finger along the seam between stones as though reading a letter written there. Then he stood, and spoke to another warden at the far end of the row, and even over the market noise Quentin caught the shape of the words, if not the words themselves: *again — deeper this time — send for—*

The sentence ended where wardens' sentences always ended, in someone else's name being taken.

Quentin sold charms until dusk. She smiled her market smile. She let the Duke of Ashes drowse under her stall, and when the tremor came again — long, rolling, unmistakable, enough that a fruit cart jolted and a woman laughed nervously and said *earthquake weather* — Quentin laughed too, and inside she was counting. Not the coins. The hours.

* * *

Her rooms above the dye-shop were small and deliberate: one bed, one stove, shelves of thread and copper wire, a window that faced away from the ward-tower so she would never have to see the grey cloaks passing beneath it. She lit a single candle. She took out the locked box her mother had left — she took it out perhaps twice a year, turned it in her hands, put it away — and tonight her hands were unsteady enough that the key rattled against the lock like teeth chattering.

She did not open it. She was still deciding whether to when the wards broke.

There was no sound, at first. That was the horror of it. Fifteen years of living inside Greyhollow's hum, that faint hive-note in the stones that every soul in the city had stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own pulse — and then it simply stopped, and the silence rang louder than any bell. Quentin's candle flame stood perfectly straight, unnaturally still, as if the whole world were holding its breath.

Then the light outside changed. It didn't darken — darkening would have meant something. Instead every color drained toward grey, the way a bruise blooms backward, and Quentin felt the hair rise on her arms because she understood, in the animal way one understands falling, that something enormous had just been severed.

She went to the window. Below, the street had gone quiet. Doors opening. People stepping out and staring upward, and Quentin followed their faces up past the rooftops, past the ward-towers whose etched sigils had gone dark as closed eyes, to the sky.

The stars were gone.

Not clouded. Not hidden. Gone — the whole spilled scatter of them, wiped clean as a slate, so that the night overhead was one seamless black, a lid, a held breath. Somewhere down the lane a child began to cry, and a man's voice said, steady and terrible, *"The wards. The wards are dead."*

Quentin's legs took her to the door before her mind agreed to it. She was down the stairs and into the street with no coat and no plan, and the cold that met her there was wrong — not autumn cold but the cold of a place that had lost its fire, and she thought absurdly of her mother's word, half-spoken, fifteen years dead: *the covenant, the covenant—*

That was when she saw the fox.

It came up the middle of the lane the way nothing healthy comes anywhere: limping, low-slung, one foreleg dark and dragging, its red pelt dulled to rust and ash. It should have fled from the crowd of doors and lanterns. Instead it walked through them as though they weren't there, straight as a drawn line, and stopped at Quentin's feet.

Every neighbor was watching. Every neighbor would remember. None of that mattered, because the fox lifted its head, and its eyes caught her candlelit window like embers catching, and it spoke.

*"Quentin Eastwick."* Its voice was cracked, wet, worn down to the quick. *"Daughter of Maren."*

Her mother's name. Spoken aloud, in the open street, in the dead of the wards — spoken by a bleeding fox while half of Greyhollow stood frozen at its windows.

*"Keeper's daughter,"* the fox said, swaying, holding itself upright by sheer refusal to fall. *"I've run three days without stopping. Listen to me. The Garden is dying. The last dragon is guttering out. And the stars—"*

Its legs buckled. Quentin caught it before she knew she'd moved, its blood soaking hot through her shift sleeve, its heartbeat fluttering against her palms like a moth in a jar.

Down the lane, a bell began to ring — not the market bell, not any hour-bell, but the warden's alarm, thin and rising. Doors slammed. Somewhere, boots.

The fox pressed its muzzle against her wrist and whispered, *"Your mother's letter. The box. You have to open it now."*
