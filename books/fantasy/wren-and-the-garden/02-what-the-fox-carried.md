## 2. What the Fox Carried

The fox led her out through the tannery district, where the smell of lye and rotting hides covered their passage and the wardens' lanterns could not easily follow. Bramble moved like a rumor—now a shadow along a wall, now a flicker between rain barrels—and Quentin followed because the alternative was standing still in a city that had stopped being safe.

The stars were still gone. She kept checking, the way you probe a tooth that's come loose. The sky above Greyhollow's broken wards was a flat, starless black, like a page with the words scraped off. Below it the city seethed. Bells clanged in the temple quarter. Somewhere a mob had already decided the Magistrate's wardens were to blame, and somewhere else the wardens had already decided the mob was.

"Left," Bramble said, low. "Then down the cistern stair. Your mother's letter is waiting, and letters do not read themselves."

"You said that already."

"I will say it again. Repetition is how you train humans."

Quentin hauled her satchel higher on her shoulder—charms she'd never sell now, dried lavender, a knife, her mother's thimble, which she had carried for fifteen years without knowing why. Her lungs burned. They had been running for two hours, and grief ran beside her the whole way, keeping pace, patient as a debt collector.

Her mother. Keeper. The word kept turning over in her mind like a coin she couldn't spend. Fifteen years of hiding, of mumbling nonsense at cats and horses so no one would look twice at her, of building a small gray life in a small gray city—and her mother had known. Had known and said nothing, had died and left nothing but a fox and a letter and a word that weighed as much as the vanished sky.

The cistern stair was where Bramble said it would be: an iron door behind a curtain of dead ivy, rusted shut until the fox pressed his scarred shoulder against it and it groaned open on a darkness that smelled of old water and older stone.

* * *

Warden Hale crouched at the mouth of Tannery Row and held up a closed fist. Behind him, six wardens stopped, lanterns hooded, crossbows half-raised.

"Tracks end here," said Warden Vess.

"They end where the fox wants them to end." Hale straightened. Rain dripped off the brim of his helm. "Beasts don't cover trail like that. Something's guiding her."

"Something you know?"

"I know the woman." He said it flatly, and hated how it sounded. Fifteen years since he'd last spoken to Quentin Eastwick, and the order in his pocket had turned her into a line of ink: *the Eastwick woman. Terminate on contact. By authority of the Magistrate.*

Terminate. Not arrest. Not question. He had read the order three times by lantern light and each time the word had sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Sir?" Vess was watching him. "The cisterns run under the whole quarter. She could surface anywhere."

"Then we split. Two at each stair. I'll take the old tannery access myself." He heard his own voice give the orders and marveled distantly at how steady it was. *You can still refuse,* said a small voice that sounded like her, from a night fifteen years ago when she'd laughed at him under a sky that still had stars in it. *You can always refuse.*

He had never yet refused anything in his life. That was the whole of his career, the whole of his character, and he was beginning to suspect it might be the whole of his damnation.

"Move," he said.

* * *

Inside the cistern, Quentin struck flint to a stub of candle while Bramble nosed open a loose stone at the base of the far wall. Behind the stone: an oilskin packet, dry as bone after fifteen years in the dark.

"She hid it here before you were born," Bramble said. "No. Before the wards. The night the covenant first cracked, she came down here with wet eyes and dry hands, and she asked me to guard it until you asked."

"I never asked. I didn't know there was anything to ask."

"Then it waited. Things of blood wait well."

Quentin's fingers shook as she unwrapped the oilskin. Inside: a letter, sealed with a smear of red wax pressed with a thumbprint instead of a signet. And beneath the letter, wrapped in a scrap of silk, a ring of black iron set with a stone that was not a stone—it was a sliver of ember, and it glowed faintly, the only warm light in all the cold dark.

She broke the seal. Her mother's hand, slantwise and hurried:

*My Quentin. If you are reading this, the sky has failed and the fox has found you, and I am sorry for every year I let you believe we were ordinary.*

*The fire under the world is going out. It has been going out since before you could walk. The covenant that feeds it—the covenant between the mortal realm and the dragon-fire that fuels all magic—demands a Keeper, and I am the last, and a Keeper's part is to give. Not to rule. To give. My gift was small; I gave what I had, and it bought us fifteen years of dim stars and failing wards. It will buy no more.*

*But your gift, my love. Your gift is not small. The beasts speak to you plainly, and they listen, and that has not happened in the bloodline for nine generations. The old law says a Keeper surrenders the gift entire to seal the pact. I could not ask that of you. I hid this instead, and I hid you, and I am sorry that hiding was the only inheritance I could give.*

*Go to the Garden. Where the fire sleeps. Bramble knows the way. Whatever they told you about the covenant, whatever the Magistrate's law says—remember that a law which demands a child's whole self is a law that has forgotten what it was for.*

*The ring is the key. The ember remembers the first oath. Take it, and go, and forgive me.*

*—Your mother, who loved you badly and well.*

Quentin read it twice. Then she sat down on the wet stone floor of the cistern with her back against the wall and did not move for a long time. The candle guttered. The black ring glowed in her palm like a held breath.

"She knew," Quentin said at last. "All those years. She knew what I was and she let me hide."

"She let you *live*," Bramble said. He came and sat close, his scarred flank warm against her knee. "There is a difference, and it is the whole difference. Your mother traded her life for your hiding, and called it a fair price, and never told you the bill existed."

"That's not fair."

"No," the fox agreed. "It is not. Fairness is a young word. Grief is an old one. Feel the old one first; the young one will keep."

Quentin laughed—a cracked, wet sound that echoed down the cistern and came back sounding like someone else. She wiped her face with her sleeve. When she spoke again her voice had changed, had dropped into something harder and steadier, a stone settling into a wall.

"The Magistrate wants me dead for a gift I never chose. The old law wants me hollow for a covenant I never swore." She closed her fist around the ring. "Then I'll go to the Garden. And I'll decide for myself what I give and what I keep. That's the only law I'm answering to."

Bramble's ears tipped forward. "Now you sound like your mother."

"Good."

"Now," the fox said, rising, "we run again. The tannery stair has wardens on it. I heard them through the grate—six, maybe seven, one of them quiet the way quiet men are when they'd rather be anywhere else."

Quentin stood. She tucked the letter against her heart and the ring on her finger, where the ember-sliver pulsed once, twice, like a second pulse learning to live inside hers.

* * *

Hale found the loose stone twenty minutes after they'd gone.

He knelt in the dark of the cistern with his lantern turned low and read the letter by its light, because it lay there open where she'd left it, and because some part of him had already decided to disobey and wanted evidence, wanted reasons, wanted anything to hold between himself and the word *terminate*.

He read it twice. He thought of the Magistrate in his high tower, feeding the failing wards with confiscated charms, with seized beasts, with whatever small magics his wardens dragged in off the streets—and calling it order. He thought of the sky with no stars in it, and of what the city would be in a month, in a week, when the last of the borrowed fire burned through.

Then he heard the grate above shift, and he was on his feet with his lantern out and his blade drawn before his mind caught up, and through the dark came the sound of footsteps—too heavy for a fox, too light for a man.

A voice out of the black, low and level: "Warden. You've read the letter."

Hale turned. Vess stood at the stair's head, crossbow lowered, lantern hooded, and his face in the half-light was the face of a man who had also read the letter, or had read enough of Hale's face to need no letter at all.

"Sir," Vess said quietly. "The kill order. I want to hear you say it's right. Out loud. Because I've been listening to you all night, and I don't think you can."

The ember-ring pulsed against Quentin's finger, miles away and closing on the Garden road, and in the dark of the cistern, Hale opened his mouth—and found, to his own surprise, that he could not.
