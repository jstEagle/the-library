## 3. The Garden Where Fire Sleeps

The Garden had no gate left. There was only a gap in a wall of blackened stone, wide as a barn door, and through it Quentin stepped into a country that had forgotten how to be alive.

Bramble went ahead of her, nose down, tail low, moving the way foxes move through places where something large once died. The path underfoot was ash over ash, and where there should have been roses—or so her mother's letter promised, *the Garden is roses, Quentin, all of it, every hedge and border*—there were instead gray skeletons of trees, their branches curled like fingers pulled back from a flame.

The sky above the Garden was wrong in a way she hadn't seen even when the stars went out. Over Greyhollow, the dark had been an absence, a blankness. Here it was a pressure, a low ceiling of nothing that leaned on the land, and Quentin felt her gift straining against it like a voice shouting into wool.

"It's here," Bramble said. Not a question. The fox's ears were flat. "It's been calling for three days. Can't you feel it? Like a bell rung until the metal thins."

Quentin could. Since crossing into the ruined grounds, the ember-ring her mother had left her had warmed against her finger—not painfully, not yet, but with the insistence of a hand tightening its grip. She walked toward the heat.

The heart of the Garden opened before her without warning: one last clearing where the blight stopped as if drawn by a compass, a perfect circle of bare earth at whose center lay a dragon.

She had imagined dragons the way everyone did—wings like ship sails, a body long as a river barge. What lay in the circle was the memory of such a thing. The dragon was vast still, but vast the way a burned cathedral is vast; its scales were the red of coals going out, dull along the flanks, and along the ridge of its spine they were cracked, and through the cracks showed not flesh but light, thin and guttering, like embers seen through a dying grate. Its breathing moved its whole body slowly, a tide going out and out and out.

"Emberfall," Quentin said. Her mother's letter had given her the name, and saying it aloud felt like stepping onto ice.

One eye opened. It was the size of a cartwheel and gold as the last coin of a bankrupt kingdom, and it found her across forty feet of dead ground with terrible precision.

"A Keeper," the dragon said. Its voice was not sound so much as warmth—she felt it in her sternum, in her teeth, in the ring on her finger, which flared bright enough to cast shadows. "Small. Late. And frightened." A pause that might have been amusement. "You are your mother's daughter. She was also frightened, right up until the moment it mattered."

"I'm not a Keeper," Quentin said. "I'm a charm-seller. I talk to cats. I've spent fifteen years making sure nobody—" She stopped, because the eye had not blinked, and because lying to a dying creature the size of a chapel felt like lying in church. "I didn't ask for this."

"No," Emberfall said. "Keepers never do. That is rather the point of us."

* * *

They spoke while the light failed, or while what passed for light failed, which in the Garden amounted to the same thing. Emberfall could not rise—the great wings lay folded and would not unfold, the joints locked like rusted hinges—and so the conversation happened with Quentin sitting against the dragon's foreleg, close enough to feel the heat coming off it in waves, each one fainter than the last.

"The covenant is simple," Emberfall said. "That is why it has held so long, and why it cannot hold much longer. Mortal hands keep the wards. Dragon-fire fuels them. A Keeper binds the two—with blood, with oath, and with a gift surrendered. When a Keeper dies, the covenant frays. When a Keeper dies without an heir sworn in time, it tears." The great head turned; the gold eye regarded her. "Your mother tore it. Not from carelessness. From love. She would not bind you to a life of servitude, so she let the clock run out, and here we are."

"She knew I'd come anyway," Quentin said quietly.

"She hoped. Hope and cowardice look alike from inside, little charm-seller. Do not judge her too fast—you will want the same mercy someday."

Quentin looked down at her hands. They were a charmer's hands, ink-stained, small, good at knotting thread and palming coins. "Tell me what renewing it costs. All of it. Don't soften it."

The dragon did not soften it. "The old terms are fixed. To reforge the covenant, a Keeper must surrender the gift entire—the speaking, the hearing, all commerce between your kind and mine—into the fire. It becomes fuel. Your gift burns, and the wards stand, and the stars return, and you live out your days deaf to every creature that breathes, including yourself, in a way. Silence forever, traded for light." The eye closed briefly. "Your mother took that bargain for thirty years. I watched her pay it daily. She never once complained, and she never once stopped grieving."

The ring burned. Quentin thought of the shop-cat in Greyhollow, of the horses she'd quieted at the toll-gate, of fifteen years of a world that talked to her in a hundred voices she'd pretended were nothing. She thought of silence. Of waking tomorrow and hearing only wind.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the fire gutters out within the season. The wards are already down; what remains is the deep magic underneath everything, and that goes too. Magic does not vanish, Quentin Eastwick—it goes feral. You have seen what the world looks like when it is merely lawless." The dragon's breath rattled. "But hear me. The old terms were written when dragons were many and mortals were afraid. Fear writes cruel contracts. If you are asking whether there is another way—" the guttering light along its spine pulsed, once, like a failing heart finding rhythm, "—then you are asking the right question, and I am too old and too empty to answer it. The covenant is written in fire. But fire can be rewritten by whoever stands in it and does not burn."

* * *

Hale came at moonrise, or at the place where the moon would have been if the sky had anything left to give.

She heard him first through Bramble—a low warning growl, the fox rising with hackles up—and then heard his boots on the ash, unhurried, the walk of a man who had stopped pretending to sneak. He came out of the black trees with his sword drawn but lowered, and behind him, three paces back, came Vess with a lantern hooded to a slit and the crossbow pointed at the ground, which was its own kind of message.

Quentin stood. She put herself between Hale and the dragon without deciding to, the way you flinch.

"Hale." His name came out rougher than she meant it to. Fifteen years since Greyhollow, since the night he'd reported a rumor about her mother and then spent a decade pretending he hadn't, and still his face did to her what faces do.

"Quentin." He stopped ten feet away. In the slit of lantern-light his eyes went past her to the dragon, and she watched him take it in—the scale, the ruin, the dying light—and watched the kill order die behind his eyes in real time. "I have orders," he said. "From the Magistrate himself. Bring back the beast-speaker alive if possible. The heir to the covenant." His jaw worked. "He doesn't want the covenant renewed, Quentin. He wants a Keeper on a leash. Someone who can open the wards for him and close them against everyone else. You're not a heir to him. You're a key."

"I know," Quentin said. "Vess told you. Or you read the letter. Either way, you knew when you crossed that wall."

"I knew," Hale said. "Knowing isn't the same as—" He looked at the dragon again. At the cracked spine and the light bleeding out of it, at the thing the Magistrate's wardens had been ordered to extinguish like a lamp. Something in his face finished breaking and began, quietly, to mend into something else. "Fifteen years ago I gave your mother's name to a man who told me it was for her protection. I was twenty-two and I believed him because believing was easier than checking." He turned, then, fully away from Quentin, and raised his voice, and it carried across the dead Garden like a struck bell. "Magistrate's warrant names the speaker and the beast both for seizure. I find them already dying of his neglect, and the law he serves built on a covenant he means to enslave. Vess. Witness me."

"Witnessed," Vess said steadily.

Hale drove his sword point-down into the ash at his own feet—the blade of the Magistrate's own forge, the blade he'd carried fifteen years—and stepped back from it, and stood unarmed between Quentin and the road home.

"That's treason," Quentin said. Her voice shook.

"It's testimony," Hale said. "There's a difference. Treason serves the man who wins. Testimony just tells the truth and lets the chips land." He looked at her at last, and it was the old look, the young look, the one from before everything. "I can't give you back the years. But I'll stand in front of whatever comes down that road, for as long as I'm standing."

Behind her, the light along Emberfall's spine surged—faint, flickering, but rising, like a tide answering a moon that shouldn't exist anymore—and the dragon's voice rolled warm through Quentin's bones:

"Well then, Keeper. The law has arrived to watch you break it. Ask your question of the fire."

Quentin looked at the burning crack in the world, at the silence waiting to swallow her gift whole, at the man who had chosen her side over his own—and she stepped forward, off the ash, toward the fire, and did not stop.
