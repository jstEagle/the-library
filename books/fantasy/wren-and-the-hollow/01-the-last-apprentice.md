## 1. The Last Apprentice

The Thornfells did not forgive, but they remembered, and that was worth more.

Tamsin Kavanagh had learned that lesson at nine years old, flat on his back on a scree slope with the wind trying to peel him off the mountain like old paint, while old Master Corwin stood over him and said, *The mountain forgets no one, boy. Your job is to be someone worth remembering.* Twenty years on, the mountains still knew his name. It was the world below that had begun to forget his guild's.

He stood at the head of the caravan on the last morning of the crossing, watching dawn come up grey and slow over the Widow's Teeth, those three jagged peaks that guarded the high pass like old women gossiping over a fence. Behind him, eleven wagons and forty-one souls and three hundred head of trade goods waited on his word. Wool and salt and dyed leather, a merchant's fortune in oiled canvas, and every coin of it resting on the fact that Tamsin Kavanagh knew which of the two roads ahead would still exist by noon.

The low road was shorter. The low road was also, as of the spring melt, a canyon full of moving water and the occasional corpse. Any fool could see that. But the high road—the Grey Stair, the old-timers called it—was a different kind of treachery, the kind that looked sound and wasn't, and that was where the guild's knowledge lived. Not in maps, though the guildhall's archive held maps older than the kingdom. Not in books. In the walking of it. In the calluses and the counting and the small, unglamorous litany of things a Wayfarer carried in his skull: *the Stair's third switchback shifts after hard frost; don't trust the cairn at the Widow's left hand, boys built it drunk; if the wind comes off the Teeth smelling of iron, get under stone before the hour turns.*

"Wayfarer." The caravan master, a fat, anxious man named Odell with rings on every finger and fear in every crease, came puffing up the trail. "The drivers are asking. Low road or high?"

"High," Tamsin said.

"It's longer."

"It's there."

Odell's mouth worked. "The other masters said—forgive me, but they said the guild's roads were... old. That there are surveyors now. Crown engineers. They've mapped the Thornfells, they say. All of them."

Tamsin looked at the man for a long moment. He didn't blame him. You couldn't blame a man for trusting the new thing, the printed thing, the thing with the Crown's stamp on it and the promise that the world had been made safe and flat and known. But he thought of the Crown surveyors' map he'd once seen, the fine engraved one, and how it showed the Grey Stair as a clean ribbon of a road, and how the ribbon passed directly across a slope that had slid three winters ago and taken a mule train with it, and how no one drawing that map had stood on the ground and smelled the mountain and known.

"Your surveyors," Tamsin said, "map the world as it was when they walked it. I map it as it is. There's a difference, and the difference has a price, and you're paying it. Mount up. We cross the Stair before the wind turns."

The wind turned at midday, as he'd said it would. They were under stone by then, all forty-one of them, packed into the hollow beneath a granite shelf while the storm came down the pass like a living thing, shrieking, hurling gravel, and Odell clutched Tamsin's sleeve and stared out at the white fury and said, hoarsely, "How did you know?"

"I didn't," Tamsin said. "The mountain told me. You just have to speak its language."

* * *

The guildhall stood at the foot of the Thornfells where the last farm road gave out and the real roads began: a long, low building of grey stone, roofed in slate the color of old bruises, with the guild's sign above the door—a staff crossed with a star, carved so long ago that weather had worn it nearly smooth. There had been forty masters once. There had been a hundred apprentices in the yard, and the sound of them, and the smell of bread from the kitchen, and the great map room with its hundred lamps.

Now there were four.

Tamsin came up the track in the last light and saw the smoke rising thin from the kitchen chimney—good, at least one person cooking—and then he saw the other thing, the wrong thing, and stopped as if he'd walked into a wall.

There were men on the roof. Strangers. Strangers with hammers, prying up slate.

He dropped the caravan's fee-purse in his pocket without thinking about it and took the yard at a run, and by the time he reached the door his hand had already found the staff-bracket by the frame out of twenty years of habit, though he hadn't taken up a staff in anger since he was a boy. Inside, the entry hall was cold. The great map room's doors stood open, and lamplight spilled out, and he could hear voices.

Guildmaster Ede Voss sat at the long table with a stranger in a merchant's good coat, and between them lay a document, and the stranger was signing it.

Ede Voss was seventy years old and had walked every road in the Thornfells and most of the roads out of the world besides, and her hands were the hands of a woman who had held rope in every kind of weather. She did not look up when Tamsin came in. She looked like a woman who had decided not to look up, which was worse.

"Master Voss," Tamsin said. "There are men on our roof."

"They're surveying it," she said. "For the buyers. Sit down, Tamsin."

"Buyers."

"Sit down."

He sat. The stranger gave him a pleasant, pitying nod and finished his signature with a flourish, and Tamsin understood, in a slow cold way, that he was watching the end of something and that it had been arranged without him.

"The guildhall," Ede said, when the man had gone, "is sold. The deed transfers at month's end. The archive goes to the Crown surveyors' office in Marrowgate—they'll pay for the paper, at least. The four of us get a share, and the guild dissolves."

"The guild doesn't dissolve." His voice came out strange. "The guild is the roads. The guild is—"

"The guild is four old people and a leaking roof, Tamsin." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. Ede Voss had never been cruel; she had only ever been true, the way the mountains were true. "I've sold it because the alternative is watching it rot around us and then burying it. There's no one left to teach. There's no one left who wants to learn."

"I learned."

"You did." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were tired in a way he had never seen, and it frightened him more than the men on the roof. "And look what the world gave you for it. Caravans and coin, and every year fewer of them, and men with printed maps who call what you do superstition. You're the last apprentice, Tamsin. There will be no more. That's what *last* means."

He walked out. He didn't mean to; his legs simply took him, down the hall, past the map room where the hundred lamps had burned once, out into the yard where the strangers' ladders leaned against the guildhall like siege engines. Above him, slate came up under their pry-bars with a sound like a tooth being pulled.

He stood there a long time, looking up at the roof of the only home he'd ever had, and the wind came down off the Thornfells and it smelled of iron, and he thought, absurdly, *get under stone before the hour turns,* and there was no stone to get under. The hour had turned. The storm was already here.

* * *

He didn't sleep in the hall that night. He told himself it was because the strangers had taken the east rooms and their noise carried, but that was a lie, and he was an honest man, and lies sat poorly on him. He slept in the old waystation hut a mile up the farm road, the one the guild had kept for walkers coming off the mountain late, with its iron stove and its single cot and the carved names of a hundred years of Wayfarers on its doorframe.

He sat up most of the night feeding the stove and reading, by lamplight, one of the archive's maps—not a Crown map, a guild map, drawn on hide by a master dead four hundred years, the ink gone brown, the roads rendered not as lines but as *instructions*. *Where the river forks, take the left bank though it seem the worse, for the right bank lies.* *The stone that stands alone is not alone; dig at its north face and you will find the walker's cache.* Roads as promises. Roads as a covenant between the dead and the living: *we walked this, and we tell you how, and one day you will tell another.*

One day you will tell another.

He laid his palm flat on the hide map, on the faded mountains, and felt the weight of the covenant, and for the first time in his life it felt less like a gift and more like a debt being called in.

Near dawn he heard footsteps on the frost outside, and knew them—Ede walked with a slight drag of the left heel, an old break badly set—and he didn't turn when she came in and stood warming her hands at his stove.

"You'll take the fee-purse share and go to Marrowgate," she said. "You're young. You can hire out to the caravans direct, cut the guild out. Odell would sign you on permanent. It's a good living."

"I don't want a living."

"I know what you want. I wanted it too, at your age. I wanted the roads to need me forever." She was quiet a moment. The stove ticked. "Tamsin. There's a man coming to see me tomorrow. A Crown man. An Inquisitor's office clerk, doing the inventory of the archive before the transfer. Be polite to him. Sign nothing you don't have to. And—" she hesitated, and he turned, and saw something in her face he had never seen there before, something that in a lesser woman he would have called fear. "And whatever he asks you about the deep archive, the old holdings, the ones we don't show to visitors—you've never heard of them. Do you understand me?"

"The deep archive?"

"Good," Ede Voss said. "You learn fast. That was always your trouble, boy. You learn fast, and the world punishes fast learners."

She left before full light, dragging her heel a little on the frost, and Tamsin sat with the hide map in his lap and the stove going cold and the word *Inquisitor* sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

He went back up the Grey Stair three days later, alone, without a caravan, because he couldn't help it—because the mountain had been in his head all week like a song he couldn't stop humming, and because he wanted, one more time, to stand on the high pass and be the man the mountain remembered.

The Stair was empty in the way that only high places are empty, the sky enormous, the world below folded up small and blue with distance. He walked it slow, touching the cairns as he passed, an old guild habit—*counting the neighbors*, Corwin used to call it—and at the third switchback he checked the slope the way you check a sleeping animal, and it was sound, and he felt a fool's relief anyway.

It was at the fifth switchback, where the path narrowed to a ledge above a long fall, that he found the man.

He was lying against the rock face as if he'd lain down to rest, which no one did on the fifth switchback, and at first Tamsin took him for dead. But as Tamsin knelt the man's eyes opened, and they were grey and clear and terribly focused, and his hand came up and caught Tamsin's wrist with a strength that had nothing left in it but will.

"Wounded," the man said. "Coming down. From the north."

Tamsin saw the wound then, and his stomach turned. The man's side was soaked dark, but the blood had dried wrong—not crusted, but *set*, like wax, like something poured and hardened—and beneath the blood, along the edge of the wound, the skin was marked. Faint lines. Fine as spider silk. Silver, or near-silver, catching the high light.

Script. It looked like script.

"Hold on," Tamsin said, and reached for his pack, for the needle and gut-thread every Wayfarer carried, for anything. "I can—there's a waystation, half a day—"

"No." The man's grip tightened. His other hand came up, shaking, holding something folded tight and small, and pressed it into Tamsin's hands and then held Tamsin's hands closed over it, both of his own around them, cold as the rock he leaned against. "Take it to the Hollow."

"What? What hollow—there's no hollow on the Stair, there's a ravine, if you mean the ravine—"

"You'll know it," the man said. "You'll know it when—" and something moved behind his eyes, some vast tired recognition, and he looked at Tamsin the way you look at a face in an old crowd, someone you'd half forgotten and suddenly can't imagine how you ever forgot, and he said, softly, almost gently: "Oh. It's you."

Then the light went out of him, all at once, like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, and his hands slid from Tamsin's, and Tamsin Kavanagh knelt on the fifth switchback of the Grey Stair holding a folded map he had not accepted, over a dead man whose blood had dried like wax, with silver script pressed into his skin like a brand, and the wind came off the Widow's Teeth and it smelled of iron.

Below him, the world went on without knowing. Above him, the peaks stood in their old silence, keeping whatever they kept.

And in his closed hands, the folded thing was warm.
