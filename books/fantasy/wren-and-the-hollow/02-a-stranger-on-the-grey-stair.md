## 2. A Stranger on the Grey Stair

The Grey Stair did not forgive, and it did not forget. That was what old Maren had told him on his first crossing, when he was nine and terrified of the drop, and she had laughed at his white knuckles and said, *It remembers everyone who falls, boy. It just never says their names.*

Tamsin Kavanagh had crossed the Stair forty-one times since, and he had come to think of it as a colleague: difficult, honest, and utterly indifferent to whether you lived.

The morning was doing its best to kill him anyway. Ice glazed the fifth switchback where the path narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for two boots abreast, and beyond the lip the mountain fell away in a long grey throat of cloud. Wind came off the Widow's Teeth in gusts that seemed personally offended by anyone upright. He had sent the caravan's drovers down the low road with a purse of his own coin to pay for the detour—merchants always grumbled about the toll of extra days, but they grumbled louder about widows—and so he climbed alone, rope coiled at his hip, testing each hold twice because no one was watching and the mountain didn't care about pride.

That was how he heard the sound. Or rather, how he heard the wrongness of no sound.

The Grey Stair had birds. Rock wrens, mostly, foolish little brown things that hopped along the ledges and scolded the wind. He hadn't seen one since the third switchback. He hadn't thought much of it until now, standing in a silence so complete he could hear his own pulse in his ears, and realized the mountain itself was holding its breath.

He found the man forty paces farther on, tucked into the hollow beneath an overhang of granite, as if the rock had tried to shelter him and failed.

He was lying on his side, knees drawn up, dressed in travel-stained wool the color of wet ash. At first glance Tamsin took him for dead—but then the man's hand moved, a slow crawl of fingers against stone, and Tamsin forgot the ice and the drop and everything else and went down beside him.

"Hey. Hey." He pulled off a glove and pressed bare fingers to the man's throat. A pulse, thready and fast, like a bird trapped under cloth. "You're on the Grey Stair. You fell? Were you attacked?"

The man's eyes opened.

They were grey eyes, pale as the pass itself, and they found Tamsin's face with terrible precision—not the wandering, glassy look of the half-conscious, but a fixed and deliberate focus, as if he had been saving the last of himself for exactly this moment and knew he had very little left to spend.

"You're a Wayfarer," the man said. His voice was cracked and thin, but the words came out whole. "The clasp. Guild-clasp."

"Last one, near enough," Tamsin said. "Can you move? There's a waystation two hours down, there's a stove, I can—"

"No." The word stopped Tamsin's hands where they hovered. The man's mouth bent into something that might have been a smile on a healthier face. "No waystation. No stove. Listen to me. We don't have time, and you're going to want to argue, and we don't have time for that either."

Blood was pooling beneath him. That was the first wrong thing, though it took Tamsin a moment to understand why it was wrong: there was too much of it. A dark spread of it, soaked into the grit of the ledge, blackening in the cold—and yet the man lay there talking, clear-eyed, steady, as if his body were bleeding out somewhere slightly to the left of where he actually was.

Then Tamsin saw the wound.

It was high on the man's chest, above the collar of his shirt, where the wool had been cut or torn away. It was not a large wound. It was a puncture, neat and round, the kind a stiletto makes or a long nail driven true—and from it, the blood came slowly, thickly, in a way blood does not come. It welled and then stopped. Welled and then stopped. As if the wound were breathing.

And around the wound, pressed into the skin like ink into wet paper, was writing.

Silver script, fine as spider silk, curving out from the puncture in lines that followed the anatomy beneath—the tendons of the neck, the ridge of the collarbone—as if the writing were not on the man but in him, showing through, the way a watermark shows through paper when you hold it to the light. It moved. Tamsin would swear afterward, before any god who'd have him, that it moved: a slow drift of those silver lines, rearranging, settling, like letters finding their order in a sentence being written very far away.

"What happened to you?" Tamsin heard himself ask, and hated the shake in his voice.

"I ran out of road," the man said simply. "Doesn't matter. What matters—" He tried to rise, failed, and Tamsin caught his shoulder without thinking, easing him back against the granite. The touch of him was wrong too: cold everywhere except over his heart, where heat came off him like a hearthstone. "What matters is this."

His hand went inside his coat, moving with the clumsy determination of a man spending his final strength on a single errand, and came out holding a folded thing.

A map. Folded small, folded tight, in the way cartographers fold—along printed creases worn soft with use. It was bound with a plain cord, and even closed, even dark, Tamsin felt it the way you feel a struck bell through a wall.

"No," Tamsin said. The refusal came out of him faster than thought, faster than courtesy. "Whatever this is, whoever's after you—I guide people over mountains. That's all. I carry letters, I carry pilgrims, I carry merchants who haggle over every copper. I don't carry whatever that is."

The man looked at him. And in that look was such a weight of exhaustion and grief and grim, stubborn hope that Tamsin's next words died unspoken.

"You think I wanted this?" the man said softly. "I've run with it for eleven years. Through four countries. I buried two friends. I watched them take my brother in Marrowgate, in the fish market, in broad daylight, and nobody screamed because nobody could see it happen. Do you know what it is to be hunted by men who leave no scent and cast no shadow the dogs will mark?" He coughed, and the cough cost him; when he spoke again his voice had gone thinner. "I didn't choose it. It chose where to stop, and it stopped here. With you. On your mountain. Because your guild built these roads, Wayfarer, and some roads remember who made them."

"That's not—" Tamsin started, and could not finish, because he did not know what it was not.

"Take it." The man pressed the folded map against Tamsin's chest, hard enough that Tamsin felt its edges through his coat. "Not for me. For her."

"For who?"

But the man's eyes had gone distant, fixed on something past Tamsin's shoulder, past the cloud-throat of the pass, at some country only he could see. "She waited," he murmured. "All of them waited, and I was the one who got out, and I swore—" His hand spasmed. Blood welled at the neat round wound and this time did not stop.

"Tamsin," the man said.

Tamsin went cold from the scalp down. "I didn't tell you my name."

"No," the man agreed, and there was that ghost of a smile again, terrible and tender. "You didn't."

The wind rose off the Widow's Teeth with a long, keening note, and the silver script on the dying man's skin flared bright enough to throw light up into both their faces—a cold, clean radiance, like moonlight through ice—and Tamsin, kneeling there with his bare hand still braced on the stranger's shoulder, felt the script answer something in his own blood. Not pain. Recognition. The awful, vertiginous feeling of hearing your own name called in a voice you've never heard, in a room you've never entered, and knowing somehow that you belong there.

"Take it to the Hollow," the stranger said.

Each word came separately now, spaced like footsteps on a narrow ledge.

"Take it—to the Hollow—and when you get there—tell them—" His breath hitched. Held. "Tell them the door was kept. Tell them someone kept the door."

His grey eyes stayed open. The wind moved his hair. The blood beneath him had gone still, spreading no further, and the silver script began, slowly, to fade—not vanishing but sinking, sinking below the skin like lights going out floor by floor in a tall house, until the last faint line slipped beneath his collarbone and was gone, and he was only a dead man on a mountain, ordinary and cold, with a small round wound and no story left to tell it.

Tamsin knelt there for a long time.

Long enough for the ice to creep across the puddle of blood and turn it dull. Long enough for his knees to ache through both layers of wool. Long enough for the rock wrens to come back, one of them landing bold as brass on the overhang above and regarding the two of them with a bright, incurious eye.

He said the rites over the body because someone had to. The Wayfarers' words for the dead were older than the guild itself, older than the roads: *The path is walked. The walker rests. Let the road behind him close like water.* His voice cracked on the last line, and he wasn't sure why. He had spoken it over fallen drovers and frozen pilgrims without faltering. But this time the words felt less like a blessing than a question, asked of a man who could no longer answer, and the silence after them felt less like peace than like listening.

*Take it to the Hollow.*

There was no Hollow. Tamsin knew the Thornfells the way a shepherd knows his flock—every col, every scree-run, every hidden spring and hanging valley—and there was no place in them called the Hollow. He turned the phrase over looking for another meaning. A hollow tree? A hollow way, one of the sunken lanes in the low country? Some smuggler's cant he hadn't learned?

Nothing fit. Nothing wanted to fit.

He should leave the body. That was procedure, guild procedure drilled into him since childhood: mark the location, note the effects, report at the nearest town so the coroner's men could come up with mules. The mountain kept its dead well; nothing up here scavenged but crows, and the cold preserved what grief couldn't.

He reached to close the man's eyes, and that was when he saw the hands.

Both of them were stained to the wrist with silver. Not blood-silver, not paint—the same fine script, faded almost to nothing now, ghost-lines across the knuckles and fingers, as if the man had spent years handling whatever he had carried until it had written itself into him. One hand was curled loosely open. The other was closed in a fist, and even in death, even with the strength gone out of him entirely, the fist held.

Tamsin pried the fingers back. They gave with the reluctance of winter branches.

Inside the fist, folded small, was the map.

He stared at it. Then he stared at the space beside the dead man, at the flattened grass-grit where the stranger had pressed the folded thing against Tamsin's chest—where Tamsin had felt it, unmistakably, edge and corner and pull of cord. Where he had refused it.

Where it had not, apparently, cared to be refused.

"That's not possible," he said aloud, and the mountain declined to comment.

He sat back on his heels. His heart was doing something arrhythmic and unpleasant. There was a version of him—a smarter version, a more sensible version, the version his Guildmaster constantly lamented did not exist—who stood up right now, walked down the Stair, reported a dead man with strange marks on the nearest Crown authority, and washed his hands of the whole affair. The Inquisition handled odd deaths. That was practically their charter. Let the men in grey coats climb the Grey Stair and puzzle over silver script; Tamsin Kavanagh had a caravan waiting at the waystation and a guildhall with a leaking roof and three elderly colleagues who needed firewood split before the week was out.

But the stranger had said his name.

Had known his name, on a mountain pass, miles from anywhere, with his last breath. And had looked at him—with those pale grey eyes, steady and certain—and spoken as if delivering a message long addressed, long delayed, to exactly the right pair of hands.

*Because your guild built these roads, Wayfarer, and some roads remember who made them.*

Tamsin looked at the map lying in the dead man's palm. Even folded, even corded shut, it seemed to sit differently than an object ought to sit—present, attentive, the way a dog sits when it has decided whose door it lives at.

"This is a bad idea," he told it.

It did not disagree.

He took it. The cord was cold; the paper within was warm, absurdly warm, warm as the stranger's chest had been over his heart, and when the folded thing settled into Tamsin's gloved hand the recognition he'd felt in his blood stirred once, gently, like something turning over in its sleep, and was still.

He wrapped the body as best he could with his spare rope and a groundsheet, dragged it deep into the hollow beneath the overhang where the crows wouldn't easily find it, and stacked stones over it with the quick economy of a man who has done this work before. Forty-one crossings. He had never once had to bury anyone on the Grey Stair. He marked the cairn with a guild-sign scratched into the largest stone—an old sign, a sign for *one who carried faithfully*—though why he marked a stranger with a Wayfarer's honor he could not have said, and did not try.

Then he stood, and shouldered his pack, and turned down the mountain, and made it exactly seven steps before he stopped.

The map was gone from his pocket.

He checked the other pocket. His pack. The loops where he kept his tools. Seven steps back up to the cairn, heart hammering, half convinced he'd see the stranger sitting up and smiling—and the body lay as he'd left it, roped and sheeted and silent, empty-handed.

He was bending to check beneath the groundsheet when he felt it: a slight warmth against his ribs, on the left side, over the heart. He put his hand inside his coat with the slow dread of a man probing his own wound, and drew out the folded map, corded and creased and patient, resting precisely where the stranger had pressed it against his chest, as if the intervening minute had been a formality it had seen no need to observe.

The wind came off the Widow's Teeth, keening down the long throat of the pass, and Tamsin Kavanagh stood alone on the fifth switchback with a dead man at his back and a living thing against his heart, and understood, in the wordless way one understands falling, that the choice he had refused on this ledge had not been refused at all—that the map had not asked, had merely informed, and that somewhere below him in the world were men with stilettos and silver script and no shadows, who would want to know exactly where that information had landed.

He thought about the waystation. The caravan. The leaking roof of the guildhall, and old Coll asleep in his chair, and the firewood that needed splitting.

He thought: *they can't be part of this.*

He thought it the way you think a wish, knowing wishes are free precisely because they are worthless.

Then the light went out of the day, all at once—or seemed to, a flicker at the edge of vision, like a lamp pinched between wet fingers—and Tamsin looked down at the map in his hands, at the corded folds, and for one heartbeat he would deny ever after, the paper shifted under his fingers. Not moved. Shifted. Settled. The way a sleeping animal settles closer to the fire.

Below him, the world went on without knowing. Above him, the peaks stood in their old silence, keeping whatever they kept.

And in his closed hands, the folded thing was warm.
