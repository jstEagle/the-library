## 3. What the Map Wants

The fire took the first corner of the map at midnight, and by dawn the map had taken it back.

Tamsin knew this because he had watched it happen, or thought he had. He had held the folded paper over the hearth in the guildhall's common room with the tongs, the way his master Corvin had taught him to hold anything he meant to destroy—firmly, without sentiment—and he had seen the flame crawl along the edge, browning the parchment, licking up toward that impossible silver ink. He had smelled it scorch. He had felt the heat through the iron.

Then Corvin had called him away to mend a shutter latch, and when Tamsin came back the map lay on the mantel, unfolded, whole. The scorched corner was gone. Not faded—gone, as if it had never burned, the parchment smooth and pale and patient.

He told himself he'd imagined the burning. Grief did strange things; he was grieving a stranger he'd never met, a man whose blood had cooled in his arms on the Grey Stair while the wind read the silver writing off his skin. Grief made men see things. The Wayfarers' Guild had a saying for that: *the mountain shows you what you bring.*

But the mountain had never shown anyone a map that mended itself.

* * *

He tried again the next night, properly this time. No interruptions, no half-measures. He built the fire hot—guild fires were always hot, the Thornfells saw to that—and he weighted the map open with two flat stones so it couldn't fold itself shut and hide from the flame. He stood over it and made himself watch every second.

The paper browned. Curled. Blackened along its edges, the way paper does, ordinary as bread toasting. And then, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, the blackening reversed. It crawled backward like water running uphill, char becoming scorch becoming tan becoming clean white parchment, and the silver lines beneath—the roads, the rivers, the coastline of a country Tamsin could not name because no one alive could name it—shimmered once, faintly pleased, and settled.

The stones sat on empty air for a moment before the map reappeared beneath them, unburnt.

Tamsin sat down hard on the hearthstone. His hands were shaking. Outside, wind worried the loose tiles of the roof they could not afford to fix, and the four remaining members of the Wayfarers' Guild slept their tired sleep above stairs, and Tamsin Kavanagh, who had walked every pass and ford and smuggler's stair from Marrowgate to the sea and never once been lost, understood that he was lost now, thoroughly, in a country of one.

"You're still here," he said to the map. His voice sounded foolish in the empty room. "Why are you still *here?*"

The ink didn't answer. But as he leaned close, breath fogging in the cold, he could have sworn the silver lines were brighter than they'd been on the Grey Stair. Brighter, and denser. As if the map were filling itself in, road by road, the way a wound fills with blood.

As if it were drinking something to do it.

He looked down at his hands. At the bandage wrapped around his left palm, where the dead man's grip had pressed the map into his flesh hard enough to bruise. The bruise had faded. The bandage was clean.

Mostly clean.

There was a mark there he hadn't put there. A single curl of something pale, fine as a hair, lying just under the skin of his wrist where the bandage ended. He told himself it was a scar. He told himself it was thread. He wrapped the bandage tighter and did not look again.

* * *

In the morning he tried to be rid of it the honest way: he gave it away. Or tried to.

Corvin was in the yard behind the guildhall, splitting kindling with the slow economy of a man who has done nothing else for fifty years and expects to do it for fifty more. He was seventy-one, broad as a door, and he had been Guildmaster back when there had been a guild worth mastering—eleven members, three halls, contracts from the coast lords and the river barons both. Now there were four of them and a leaking roof and a buyer coming Thursday to look at the property.

Tamsin laid the map on the chopping block, beside the split wood.

"Burn it yourself," he said. "Or keep it. I don't care which. I just want it out of my sight."

Corvin wiped his hands on his apron and looked at the map for a long moment. He didn't touch it.

"Where was it?"

"In my pocket." Tamsin heard how it sounded even as he said it. "I put it in the hearth last night. Twice."

"Twice."

"It doesn't burn, Corvin."

The old man's jaw worked. He picked up the map—carefully, by one corner, the way you pick up something that might be sleeping—and turned it toward the grey morning light. The silver script caught it and threw it back, and for an instant Tamsin saw his master's face go strange: not afraid, exactly. *Recognizing.* Like a man hearing a song from his childhood in a language he'd forgotten he spoke.

"I saw this once before," Corvin said quietly. "Forty years ago. My own master had it out of a dead courier's satchel on the Highmoor road. Silver writing. Roads that weren't roads." He set it down on the block as if it were hot. "He gave it to the Inquisition himself. Carried it to their house in Vael with his own hands, and came back grey as ash, and never said why, and never took another contract north of the Thornfells as long as he lived. That's what your map is, boy. That's what it's *from.* And you've had it in your pocket for three days."

"The man who carried it died telling me to take it somewhere. To the Hollow. Whatever that is."

"I know what it is." Corvin said it flatly, and would not say more.

Wind came off the fells and rattled the yard gate. Somewhere beyond the wall, the town of Dunmarrow was waking—carts, gulls, a smith already hammering. Ordinary sounds. Tamsin clung to them.

"So we give it to the Inquisition too," he said. "Like your master did. Let them deal with it."

"And how do you suppose that conversation goes?" Corvin gestured at him with the splitting axe, not unkindly. "A stranger dies on our stairs with silver written into his wounds, and three days later the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild walks into an Inquisitor's house carrying the thing the man bled out protecting? You think they'll thank us? Ask polite questions?" He shook his head. "Whatever the Crown erased badly enough to need maps like this, they erased it *completely*, Tamsin. There's no version of this where you hand it over and walk home."

"So we burn it."

"We burn it," Corvin agreed. "Tonight. All of us together, witnesses to it, so no one can say different afterward. And then you take the south road for a month—the Ellsford contracts, the salt run—and by the time you come home this will be a story we tell young wayfarers, if we ever have young wayfarers again." He paused. "If we have a hall to tell them in."

That landed where he meant it to land. Thursday. The buyer. Tamsin looked at the guildhall behind them—at the shingles slipping like loose teeth, at the sign above the door where the guild's old emblem had weathered to a ghost of itself, a winding road inside a ring—and felt the weary pride of it all settle back onto his shoulders like a pack. He loved these roads. He loved this rotten, leaking, dying house. The world had simply stopped needing either, and there was no contract in existence for fixing that.

"Tonight," he said. "Together. Fine."

It was the first promise he'd ever made his Guildmaster intending to break.

* * *

Because here was the thing Tamsin had not told Corvin, could not find words for, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone:

The map wouldn't leave.

Not wouldn't be destroyed—wouldn't be *put down*. He had tested it, quietly, methodically, the way he tested any route before trusting it. He'd left it on the mantel and walked to the well at the end of the lane: it was in his pocket by the time he drew water. He'd locked it in the contract chest in Corvin's study, a chest with an iron lock older than the town: it was under his pillow when he woke. He'd handed it to Sorrel, their oldest member, half-deaf and incorruptible, and asked her to hold it and not give it back no matter what he said.

She'd held it for nearly an hour. Then she'd blinked down at her empty hands, gone white as milk, and said, "Tam, love, what did I have?" She didn't remember holding it at all.

And there was the other thing. The dreams.

Every night since the Grey Stair, Tamsin dreamed of walking. Not nightmares—worse, because they weren't frightening. He dreamed of roads in perfect clarity: a causeway of white stone crossing a flooded valley, the water on either side so still it doubled the stars. A stair cut into a cliff face, each step carved with a word in that same silver script, and his feet knowing the order of them. A door of grey stone in the roots of a mountain, tall as a church, and around its frame a line of writing that moved, slowly, the way light moves on water.

He woke each morning knowing these roads the way he knew the Grey Stair. He could have walked them blindfolded. He had never seen them in his life.

Except that some part of him, some part deeper than his life, clearly had.

On the third night—the night before the burning—he fell asleep at his desk over the open map and dreamed without walking at all. He dreamed of a kitchen. Low beams, copper pots, a woman singing in a language he didn't know and somehow knew perfectly, her voice rising and falling like the lullaby his mother used to sing—or the lullaby he'd always believed his mother made up, because no one else had ever known it. In the dream the woman turned, flour on her hands, and smiled at him with an expression of such particular, unguarded love that he woke with tears freezing on his face and the taste of woodsmoke and rosemary in his mouth.

The map lay open on his desk. Its ink had shifted.

He was sure of it the way he was sure of a changed trail—scuffed earth, a broken twig, the small wrongnesses a wayfarer reads without thinking. Three days ago the map's roads had clustered in its western half, dense as veins, trailing off into blankness eastward. Now the blankness was filling. New roads crept across the eastern quarter in silver threads finer than spider silk, and at the center of them, growing clearer every night, a shape: a circle within a circle, and inside that, a single word in the silver script.

He couldn't read it awake. But in the dream-kitchen, the woman had sung a word that fit it exactly, and when he thought of the two together—ink and voice, map and song—something behind his ribs turned over like a key.

The map wasn't showing him roads. The map was teaching him. Patiently, nightly, the way you teach a child a trade. And it wasn't asking permission.

*Which meant it hadn't fallen into his hands on the Grey Stair at all,* he thought, sitting in the dark with his heart going like a hammer. *A dying man doesn't press a map on whoever happens to be standing there. He'd pressed it on Tamsin specifically. Maybe he'd carried it across half the mountains looking for him specifically.*

The map had chosen him. Or something behind the map had. And he did not know which frightened him more: that he might be able to refuse, or that he might not want to.

* * *

They gathered in the common room at dusk, all four of them, as promised. Corvin. Sorrel, with her knitting abandoned in her lap for the first time in thirty years. Young Piet, seventeen, all elbows and earnestness, who had joined the guild eighteen months ago because it was the only future on offer and who watched Tamsin now with the naked faith of a boy who believes his elders know what they're doing.

The fire was built high. The map lay on the table between them, silver lines drinking the firelight.

"Tamsin found it on a dead man," Corvin said, for Piet's and Sorrel's benefit, giving the plain version. "It's not ours and it's not safe, and whatever it belongs to, it belongs to the past. We're a guild of the present. So we end it, tonight, together, and tomorrow we speak of it to no one." He looked around the room. "Anyone against?"

Sorrel's needles were very still. Piet glanced at Tamsin—waiting for him, trusting him—and Tamsin realized with a lurch that he was about to watch the boy's face while he lied, or while he confessed, and he could not do either.

"Before we burn it," Tamsin said, and his voice came out steadier than he deserved, "look at it. Properly. All of you. Because I don't think you understand what we're burning."

He opened the map flat and turned it so the firelight struck the silver. The new eastern roads gleamed. The circled word at the center seemed to sit just beneath the surface of the parchment, like a coin at the bottom of clear water.

Sorrel leaned close. Her lips moved, tracing the script without sound, and then stopped, and her hand went to her mouth.

"That's—" she whispered. "I know that lettering. From my grandmother's psalter. The old rite, the one the priests called—" She stopped herself, glancing at the window, at the dark, as though the dark might be listening. Everyone in the border towns knew what you didn't say aloud. Everyone knew the shape of the prohibition without ever being taught its content.

"What does it say?" Piet asked.

"I don't know," Sorrel said. "But I know it's *old*, Tam. Older than the Crown. Older than anything." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet. "What is this place?"

And Tamsin opened his mouth to answer—I don't know, I swear it, I don't know—and found that he did know. The knowledge rose in him like water into a well shaft, cool and complete, arriving from somewhere below his own life: a name for the coastline, a name for the white-stone causeway, a name for the country entire, and the name rang through him with the terrible rightness of a bell struck in an empty church.

He clamped his jaw shut on it. Sweat broke cold along his spine.

"No," he said hoarsely. "Don't ask me that. Don't ever ask me that."

Corvin studied him for a long moment. Fifty years of reading trails, of reading weather, of reading men; Tamsin watched all of it arrive at its conclusion in the old man's face, and watched the conclusion grieve him.

"It's in you already," Corvin said quietly. "Isn't it. The map isn't in your pocket, boy. It's in your *blood.*"

The fire cracked. Piet looked from face to face, understanding nothing, understanding everything.

"Then burning the paper changes nothing," Tamsin said. It wasn't defiance. It was surrender wearing defiance's coat. "You must see that now. Burn it, don't burn it—it's already done its choosing." He pulled off the bandage on his left wrist and held it to the firelight, and showed them the fine pale curl of script lying beneath his skin, and watched Sorrel cover her eyes and Piet step back and Corvin go very still.

"Gods keep us," Sorrel whispered. "Boy. What have you walked into?"

"I don't know." And this time it was true, and it was worse than any lie. "But a man died getting this to me, and whatever it wants, it wanted *me*, and I keep dreaming of a door." He looked at the map, at the circled word, at the silver thread vanishing off the map's northern edge, up into the blank paper where the Thornfells would be, where the passes began, where everything he knew ended and everything he was beginning to remember started. "It goes north. Whatever the Hollow is—it goes north, and it goes through Marrowgate."

"Marrowgate's a border town full of thieves and spies and Inquisitor informants," Corvin said flatly.

"I know," Tamsin said. "I'm leaving tonight."

"You're leaving—" Piet started.

"Alone," Tamsin said, and hated how much it cost to say it, and said it anyway. "No one else carries this. No one else bleeds for it. That's the whole point of what I'm showing you." He took up the map, folded it—it came to his hand eagerly, like a bird to a glove—and slid it inside his coat, over his heart, where it apparently intended to live regardless of his opinion.

Corvin rose. For a moment Tamsin thought the old man would forbid him, would invoke the guild, would stand in the door with fifty years of authority—and some traitorous part of Tamsin hoped he would, hoped to be stopped, hoped to be saved from the road he was already walking in his sleep.

Instead Corvin took down the heavy traveling cloak from its peg—the Guildmaster's own cloak, worn by every master of the hall for a hundred years—and put it around Tamsin's shoulders.

"Then you carry the guild with you," he said gruffly. "Go north. Find out what this Hollow wants. And come home, Tamsin Kavanagh. However long it takes, however far you walk—" his voice roughened— "*come home.*"

Tamsin hugged him, hard, and could not speak, and did not try.

An hour later he was on the north road alone, frost smoking off the fields, the guildhall's single lit window shrinking behind him. In his pocket, the map lay quiet and warm, its silver roads brightening mile by mile like a pulse quickening. And ahead, far off in the dark where the border lamps of Marrowgate burned like dropped embers, someone was watching the same road—someone with quick fingers and a stolen childhood and a grandmother's songs—who would reach for his coat before the week was out, and change everything.

But Tamsin didn't know that yet. He only knew he was finally moving, after three days of being moved, and that—for the first time since the Grey Stair—the road ahead felt like his own choice.

He would learn, soon enough, how little of it was.
