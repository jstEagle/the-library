## 4. Wren of Nowhere

Marrowgate smelled the way all border towns smelled: wet stone, tallow smoke, and the sour breath of a hundred people pretending not to watch each other. Wren had lived in that smell for three years now, and she'd learned to read it like her grandmother read weather. Today it said rain coming off the Thornfells by dusk, and a rich crowd at the fish-market because a caravan had come down from the passes.

She worked the market's edge with her hood up and her hands loose, the way she'd been taught—not by anyone who loved her, but by necessity, which was a stricter teacher. The trick was never to want anything. Wanting made your eyes stick. You let your gaze slide over purses and satchels and jeweled clasps the way other people slid their gaze over puddles, and then, only when you'd already decided not to look, your fingers did the work.

The caravan had come down from the Grey Stair, which meant mountain men, which meant coin sewn into coat linings rather than carried in purses. Harder work. She drifted along the stalls anyway, buying nothing, spending a copper on roasted chestnuts so she had somewhere to stand, and that was when she saw the map.

It shouldn't have caught her eye. It was just a folded square of paper poking from a young man's breast pocket as he haggled over rope, unremarkable except that it was the wrong color for paper—more the color of old milk, or bone—and it sat against his chest with the particular stubbornness of a thing that did not want to be there.

But it was the fold that caught her. Three folds, the last one back over the top, tucked corner-in. Her grandmother had folded every map she ever drew that way. *So the wind can't open it,* she used to say. *Maps are shy.*

*Maps are shy.* Nobody else in the whole world folded them like that. Nobody else in the whole world—

Wren's chest went tight. She ate a chestnut without tasting it and followed the young man at a distance, telling herself she was being stupid, telling herself that a fold was a fold, that half the world folded things corner-in because it kept the corners from fraying, that wanting things was how pickpockets got caught.

He was tall, this one, with the sunburned neck and easy stride of someone who'd spent his life on roads. A wayfinder, maybe—a guild badge glinting dull silver at his collar, though the guild was supposed to be dead. He moved through the market the way water moves downhill: without hurry, around obstacles, always knowing where he was going even when he clearly didn't. And he kept touching the pocket where the map sat. Not checking it. Touching it. The way you touch a bruise, or a wound that's started talking to you.

Wren trailed him past the fish-stalls, past the money-changers with their scales and their suspicious faces, down toward the harbor steps where the fog came in off the grey water. Good ground for her. Fewer eyes.

She told herself one more lie: *I'll just see it. Just the top edge. If it's Aldermere I'll know in a heartbeat, and if it isn't I'll walk away.*

Her grandmother had sung about Aldermere the way other grandmothers sang about heaven—somewhere you couldn't get to, full of things you'd lost. *Aldermere the apple-gold, Aldermere the deep,* she'd hummed while carding wool, while dying slow in a rented room that smelled of camphor and other people's cooking. *Where the rivers run both ways and the hills keep sheep that sing.* Wren had thought it was a fairy tale until she was seven, when she'd asked a schoolmistress where Aldermere was on the map, and the woman had looked at her with such flat, cold blankness—not confusion, not ignorance, but *blankness*, like a door painted on a wall—that Wren had understood, in the wordless way children understand some things, that she'd asked the wrong question and would do well never to ask it again.

There was no Aldermere. There had never been an Aldermere. Every map in Marrowgate agreed on this, and maps were the one scripture everyone trusted.

Except her grandmother's songs, sung low, after dark, with the shutters closed.

The young man stopped at the harbor rail and stood looking out at the fog like it owed him money. Wren drifted close behind him, close enough to smell woodsmoke and road dust, close enough to hear his breathing—steady, tired, the breathing of a man carrying something heavy. She let a pair of dockhands jostle past between them, used their shoulders as cover, and lifted the map out of his pocket with two fingers and a whisper of motion so smooth it was almost polite.

*I'll just see the top edge.*

She stepped into the shadow of a net-drying rack and opened the folds.

The paper was warm. That was the first wrong thing—it was warm like a held hand, warm like something alive—and the second wrong thing was the ink, which wasn't ink at all but script, fine silver lines running through the drawing like veins through a leaf. And the third wrong thing—

The third wrong thing was that Wren knew it.

Not the way you know a picture. The way you know a face. The coastline curved under her thumb exactly the way her thumb had always known it would curve; the mountains ran north-south in a spine like a sleeping animal's back; the little marks for towns—she could *read* them, though the letters were in no alphabet she knew, and she knew what each one was called before the name reached her eyes. Vael. Bellharbour. The Hollow, marked at the center with a symbol like a door seen edge-on.

*Aldermere the apple-gold. Aldermere the deep.*

The harbor fog pressed close. Somewhere behind her, gulls screamed over fish guts, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, and Wren stood holding a country in her hands.

It was real. It had been real the whole time. Every flat-eyed schoolmistress, every map that agreed with every other map, every adult who had smiled the small patient smile reserved for children who invented countries—they had all been lying, or lied-to, and her grandmother had died singing true songs into a world that called them lullabies.

Something hot rose up Wren's throat. She wanted to laugh and she wanted to weep and mostly she wanted to run, to take this warm impossible page and run until the fog swallowed her, and that wanting—that *sticking* of the eyes and heart—was exactly why the next moment happened the way it did.

"You're standing very still," said a voice at her shoulder, "for someone who's just stolen from me."

* *

He didn't grab her. That was the first surprise. In Marrowgate, catching a cutpurse meant grabbing, shouting, summoning the harbor-watch, maybe a broken finger as tuition. This man just stood there, arms crossed, watching her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—not anger, not triumph. Something closer to curiosity, worn thin over exhaustion.

"Give it back," he said, "or tell me why I should let you keep it."

Wren's mind ran its usual ledgers. Run? The dock was hers; she could lose him in the net-sheds in ten heartbeats. But the map was *warm*, and running with it felt like running with a lit lamp in a powder store. Lie? She was good at lies. But he'd already seen her face do whatever it had done when she opened the folds, and her face had never once learned to hide what her grandmother's songs made her feel.

So she did neither. She turned around, held the map out flat on her palms—the way you'd hold something holy, or wounded—and said, "What is it?"

"That's my question."

"It's mine too." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "That's the problem. It's both our questions, and I don't think either of us gets to just walk away with it." She nodded at the silver-veined paper. "You've been touching that pocket all morning like it bites. So either you know what it is, in which case you can tell me, or you don't, in which case we're both idiots standing in fog."

His eyebrows went up slightly. Up close he was younger than the road-weariness suggested—twenty, maybe twenty-one—with dark hair going unstylishly long and the kind of honest gray eyes that made Wren deeply suspicious, because in her experience honest eyes belonged to people who hadn't yet learned what the world did to honesty.

"Tamsin," he said at last. "Tamsin Kavanagh. Wayfarers' Guild."

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren." She smiled with all the warmth of a fishhook. "Of nowhere. It's on none of the maps, you'll find."

Something flickered across his face at that. He glanced down at the map between her palms, and she watched him decide something—watched the decision move through him like weather.

"You read it," he said. "When you opened it. I saw your face. You read those marks."

"I read three names," Wren said. "Vael. Bellharbour. Something called the Hollow." She swallowed. "And I know the shape of the coast, and I know the mountains make a spine like a sleeping animal, and I know all of that because my grandmother sang me a country that doesn't exist, and this is it. This is Aldermere." The word came out of her mouth and the fog seemed to lean closer to listen. "Which means either I'm mad, or you're carrying the one map in the world that tells the truth, and I need to know which."

For a long moment Tamsin Kavanagh said nothing. A wave slapped the harbor pilings below. Then, slowly, he took the map from her palms—but gently, taking care that their hands didn't quite touch, taking care with it, the way you take care with a stranger's grief—and refolded it, corner-in, three folds, the way her grandmother would have.

"My guildmaster wants it burned," he said quietly. "It won't burn. I've tried tearing it. It comes back to my pocket. Last night it showed me a road I've never walked, and this morning my feet wanted to walk it." He laughed, short and humorless. "I found the man who gave it to me dying on the Grey Stair. His wound didn't bleed right. There was writing in it. Silver, like—" he tapped the folded square through the cloth— "like this. His last words were *take it to the Hollow*. I don't know what the Hollow is. But I think you might."

"I know it's on the map," Wren said. "Center of the country. Marked like a door."

"A door to what?"

"To Aldermere?" She spread her hands. "To the country that isn't there? How would I know? I'm a pickpocket, not a—" She stopped. Breathed. Made herself say the rest, because saying it out loud in fog to a stranger felt safer than it had ever felt alone in the dark. "My grandmother sang the door-song. I didn't know that's what it was until right now, standing here, but I know it. She was teaching me a key my whole childhood and calling it a lullaby."

The fog beaded on Tamsin's eyelashes. He looked at her the way she imagined scholars looked at books in languages they'd only dreamed existed.

"There are men looking for this map," he said. "Crown men. An Inquisitor came to my guildhall asking after it. Serle Halvane. Do you know the name?"

Everyone knew the name. Halvane was a story mothers used, the way other mothers used wolves. Wren nodded once.

"Then here's what I know," Tamsin said. "I can't burn it, I can't lose it, and I can't give it to the Crown, because a man bled out in my arms telling me to carry it somewhere, and I don't leave debts unpaid. I was going north alone. Which now seems—" his mouth twisted— "possibly stupid. You know things about this map I don't. Songs. Names. What it's for." He hesitated, and she watched pride and prudence fight a brief ugly duel behind his honest gray eyes before prudence lost. "Come with me. At least as far as the next town. I can't pay much."

"I don't want pay." The words came out fast and fierce and surprised her. She slowed down, made herself sound like a professional again. "I want in. Whatever this map opens, whoever erased my grandmother's country off the face of the earth and made everyone forget to ask—I want to be there when it gets un-erased. That's my price. That and you never call me *girl* or *lass* or *little miss*."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Tamsin said gravely, and then ruined it by almost smiling. "Wren of Nowhere. It has a ring."

"It's accurate."

They stood a moment longer in the fog, two strangers with one country between them, and Wren felt the strangest sensation—not trust, nothing so soft as trust, but the click of two lock-parts aligning. She'd spent three years learning that everyone was alone and everyone was for sale and everyone would eventually reach for your pocket. Here was a man who'd been robbed an hour ago, offering partnership to the thief because she could read a song.

Either he was the biggest fool in Marrowgate, or—

Or he was the first person since her grandmother died who wanted the same truth she wanted, and was willing to walk toward it instead of away.

"The caravan leaves at dusk," Tamsin said. "Smugglers' gate, north wall. Bring nothing you can't run with."

"I own nothing I can't run with." She was already moving backward into the fog, because that was how she left conversations, leaving them unsure whether they'd ever really had her at all. "One thing, Wayfarer."

"Yes?"

"If the Inquisitor's men ask—you never saw me. I was never here. I am a rumor."

"Fitting," Tamsin called after her, "for a girl from a country that doesn't exist."

She grinned into the fog—and stopped, three steps later, because a cold thread slid down her spine that had nothing to do with the sea air. Across the harbor square, beneath the customs-house lamp, a man stood watching the spot where she'd been standing. Big. Still. Too still for the crowd churning around him, the way a stone is still in a river. And as the lamplight swung, she saw his collar, and on the collar a sigil she knew from stories mothers used: the crowned eye of the Inquisition.

The man was not watching her. He was watching Tamsin. And as she watched, unmoving, breath held, he raised one gloved hand—not in threat, not in greeting, but in a small, courteous, terrible salute—and turned, and walked away into the fog like a man who had all the time in the world.

Wren stood very still, the way she had when she first opened the map.

Then she went, quickly and quietly, to pack the nothing she owned.
