## 5. The Inquisitor's Summons

The knock came at dawn, and it was the politeness of it that woke Tamsin properly.

Three raps, evenly spaced, soft as a knuckle on a chapel door. Nobody knocked that way in Marrowgate's lower quarter. Debt collectors hammered. Drunks pounded. The Crown's men didn't knock at all; they came through doors with their boots and their writs and left them hanging by one hinge.

Tamsin lay in his cot under the eaves and listened to the sound again in memory, the way he'd listen to a strange birdsong, trying to place it. Then he heard Master Corvin's cane on the stair below—slow, deliberate, the old man putting each step down like a coin he couldn't afford to lose—and Tamsin knew, with the cold certainty of a wayfarer who has felt a mountain shift before the avalanche comes, that the day had already turned against him.

He dressed in the dark. He took the map from the loose stone behind the hearth where he'd hidden it—though *hidden* was a generous word for what the thing allowed, since it had appeared twice now inside his coat without his asking, folded neat as a love letter, warm as a living hand—and then, after a moment's argument with himself, he put it back beneath the stone. If this was what he feared it was, carrying it would be like carrying lit pitch into a powder store.

He went down.

* * *

The man standing in the guildhall's common room did not look like an Inquisitor. That was the first trap, and Tamsin walked straight up to its edge before he understood it was there.

Inquisitors, in the stories the guild told over winter fires, were gaunt zealots in ash-grey robes with censer-chains and eyes like nails. This man wore a plain travelling coat of good brown wool, road-muddied to the knee, and carried himself with the easy bearing of a surveyor or a wine merchant. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a face built for being believed: open, lined, faintly amused at some private joke the world had told him years ago. His hands were clasped behind his back as he studied the guildhall's great map wall—the master chart of the Thornfells that four generations of Wayfarers had inked and corrected—and he stood looking at it the way other men looked at altars.

"Master Corvin." He inclined his head. "And you'll be the last apprentice. Tamsin Kavanagh."

"Tamsin," Corvin said, before Tamsin could speak. The old man's voice was level, but his knuckles were white on the cane. "Kavanagh is my apprentice. What can the Crown do for the Wayfarers' Guild this morning? Or—" a dry pause, "—what can we do for the Crown?"

"The second, I'm afraid." The Inquisitor smiled as though the apology cost him something genuine. "Serle Halvane. Office of Roads and Records, by my commission, though we both know what that office means." He said it lightly, almost apologetically, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "I've come about a dead man."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere above them a beam settled with a groan; the roof had leaked for three winters and the guild could no longer afford the slate. Four members, Tamsin thought. Four members and a leaking roof, and now this.

"We found no dead man," Corvin said.

"No," Halvane agreed pleasantly. "You found him on the Grey Stair. I'm not here to accuse anyone of anything, Master Corvin. A traveller came down out of the pass nine days ago—a peddler, half-frozen, babbling—and he spoke of a body on the Stair, and of a young guide who'd gone up after a lost mule and come back changed. Changed how, the peddler couldn't say. But he remembered the boy's face, because the boy gave him his own cloak against the cold and wouldn't take payment." Halvane turned from the map wall at last, and his eyes found Tamsin with the unhurried precision of a man laying a card face-up. "That sounds like guild training. Courtesy to strangers on high roads. It also sounds like you, if you're the Kavanagh the peddler described."

Tamsin's mouth was dry. Nine days ago. He had buried the stranger himself, up past the third cairn, under stones he'd carried one at a time because the ground was frozen iron. He had taken the man's pack, his papers—or what passed for papers, all of it burned except the map, which would not burn—and he had told himself the lie that wayfarers tell: *the mountain keeps its dead, and the mountain tells no tales.*

But mountains don't talk. Peddlers do.

"There are bodies on the Grey Stair every season," Tamsin said. His voice came out steadier than he'd expected. "Fools who think the pass is open after first snow. We bury what we find when we can reach them. It's part of the work."

"It is indeed." Halvane nodded slowly, approvingly, and somehow that nod was worse than an accusation. "Part of the work. And did this particular body have a name?"

"He had frost in his throat and a wound no blade makes cleanly. He had nothing else. No name."

"No papers?"

"Burned with his pack. There was little enough left worth taking."

"That was honest of you," Halvane said, and smiled, and Tamsin felt the smile pass over him like the shadow of a hawk over a hare—weightless, and full of death. "Most people keep something. A ring. A purse. Guilt keeps better than grief, in my experience." He unclasped his hands and produced, from inside his coat, a small leather folio, but he did not open it. He simply held it, letting everyone look at it. "I have a description, of course. Tall. Dark-haired. Grey-eyed, unusual grey, like weather coming. A scar through the left eyebrow. And he was carrying something, our peddler thought—a satchel, flat, strapped across the chest. Did you find a satchel, Master Kavanagh?"

"I found a dead man," Tamsin said. "I buried a dead man. If the Crown wants him dug up for its records, send men with shovels and I'll show you the cairn."

It was too far. He knew it even as the words left him—he heard Corvin's breath catch, heard the offer become a door swinging open—and Halvane's smile deepened by a single degree, the way ice deepens on a pond overnight.

"Oh, we may well do that," the Inquisitor said softly. "Thank you. You've been more helpful than you know."

* * *

Corvin got rid of him with tea and forty minutes of guild history, the old man performing weariness and poverty so convincingly that Tamsin, watching from the corner, almost believed they were poor. Halvane drank the tea, complimented the map wall, asked three questions about the northern passes that sounded like courtesy and were not, and left his card on the table: cream stock, a crown stamped in grey foil, *S. HALVANE, OFFICE OF ROADS AND RECORDS*, and beneath it, in smaller type, a motto Tamsin had never seen on any Crown document:

*All roads are known.*

When the door had closed behind him, Corvin stood a long time without moving, leaning on his cane, looking at nothing.

"You offered him the grave," the old man said at last.

"I panicked."

"You offered him the grave." Corvin turned, and his eyes—clouded now, going milky at the edges the way eighty-year eyes do—were still sharp enough to cut. "Sixty years I've kept this guild alive by giving the Crown exactly nothing it asks for and making it feel generously received. And in one morning you invite soldiers up my mountain with shovels."

"They'll come whether I invite them or not."

"Yes. That's what frightens me." Corvin crossed to the hearth and lowered himself into his chair with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "Sit down, boy. Tell me what you actually buried on the Grey Stair. All of it. And then tell me why you hid a map from me."

Tamsin sat. The word *map* landed in his chest like a dropped stone.

"How did you—"

"You sleep like a corpse and talk in your sleep like a living one. You've been saying names in a language I haven't heard since I was younger than you. Also," Corvin added, "you've been keeping your coat within arm's reach at meals, and you flinch when the wind moves the shutters. I sold secrets to no one and I raised four apprentices to competence, Kavanagh. I notice things."

So Tamsin told him. The wound that didn't bleed right—the blood too bright, too thin, running off the stones like water off oiled leather. The script pressed into the flesh around the wound, fine as spider silk, silver-grey, moving. Not writing, exactly—more like writing remembering itself. The last words: *Take it to the Hollow.* The map that would not burn, would not tear, would not stay lost; that reappeared in his pocket warm as a held hand; that showed him, when he slept, roads he had never walked and could now describe turn by turn.

Corvin listened without interrupting, which from Corvin was itself an event. When Tamsin finished, the old man was silent for so long that the fire burned down a whole notch.

"Say me the names," Corvin said quietly. "The ones you say in your sleep."

Tamsin frowned. "I don't know them. They're just sounds."

"Try."

And Tamsin opened his mouth, and what came out was not a sound he had ever made while waking: *"Vel Aldermere i thannen, vel Aldermere i mor."* The syllables moved through his mouth like water finding an old channel, and the moment they left him the lamps in the room guttered—all three of them, in a still room, with the door shut—and Corvin went grey as ash.

"Don't," the old man whispered. "Don't say that name. Not in this house. Not anywhere."

"You know it."

"I know that it's forbidden. I know that men older than me lost their licenses for singing songs in that tongue. I know that the Office of Roads and Records doesn't investigate smugglers and doesn't chase murderers—it chases *that*, whatever that was, and it has chased it for two hundred years." Corvin gripped his cane with both hands. "Boy. Listen to me as if I were your father, because I am the closest thing you have. That man will come back. Men like him always come back, and next time they won't ask politely. You need to be gone before he does."

"Gone where?" Tamsin laughed, and hated how thin it sounded. "Master, I have eleven crowns and a leaking roof. My whole world is four walls and a bad chimney."

"Your whole world," Corvin said, "is roads. It always has been. Go north. Take the smugglers' ways, the ones not on any chart, and don't come back until—" He stopped. Tamsin watched the old man search for an ending to that sentence and fail to find one. "Just go north."

"And leave the guild to burn alone?"

"The guild," Corvin said, with terrible gentleness, "has been burning for thirty years. You are the only ember worth carrying out."

* * *

Tamsin did not go north. Not yet.

He told himself he needed supplies, and that much was true—rope, salt pork, the good boots from the loft. But his feet took him, as the light failed, down toward the river district and the tannery row, to the narrow house above the dyer's shop where a girl with river-dark hair rented a room she paid for in coins of uncertain origin.

Wren opened the door on the second knock, knife already in her hand, and put it away when she saw him. She looked, if anything, worse than he felt—there were shadows under her eyes like bruises, and she glanced past his shoulder at the street twice before letting him in.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"A man came to the guildhall today." He stood dripping in her doorway, rain running off his cloak onto her floorboards, and told her the shape of it: the polite voice, the peddler's tale, the folio he never opened, *all roads are known*. "An Inquisitor named Halvane. He asked about a dead man and a missing map."

Wren had gone very still. "Did you give him the map?"

"No."

"Did you give him *me*?"

"I didn't know about you yet," Tamsin said. "But no. I lied." He heard the strangeness in his own voice and couldn't help it. "I've never lied to a Crown officer in my life. It's the first rule they beat into you at the guild—wayfarers don't lie, because our word is the only thing we carry that can't be stolen. Sixty years of clean hands, and I stood in my own hall and lied through my teeth, and he *knew*, Wren. He knew and he thanked me for it."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—not her street-corner smile, but something quicker and more real, gone as soon as it came.

"Good," she said. "Lying's the first skill of staying alive. Grandmother taught me before she taught me letters." The smile faded. "Halvane. Serle Halvane." She said the name carefully, tasting it, and her hand drifted unconsciously to the hollow of her throat. "My grandmother used to whisper that name to the stove at night. Not to us—to the stove, like it could hear. *If the grey man comes, run before he knocks.* I thought she meant a ghost story."

"She wasn't wrong."

"No." Wren turned away from him, arms wrapped around herself, staring at the rain on the window. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone rough. "She used to sing me a song about a country under the hill. I told you that. What I didn't tell you—what I've been sitting up nights telling myself—is that the song has a verse about a grey man. A man who walks all roads because all roads belong to him. And the song says the only thing he fears is a door he cannot fold." She looked back at him. "Tamsin. The map. Does it still want you?"

"It never stops wanting me. It's quietest when I hold it. Like it's purring."

"Then we leave tonight."

"*We*?" He hadn't meant to say it aloud. The word hung between them, presumptuous and warm.

Wren's chin came up, all sharp edges again, the pickpocket armor sliding home. "You can't read the script. I can't either—but my grandmother could, and she sang pieces of it into me before I could walk, same as she sang everything else. You have roads and a dead man's memories. I have the songs and quick hands and no guildhall for anyone to burn down around me. And neither of us survives a week alone with that man hunting us. So yes. *We.* Unless the last Wayfarer is too proud to travel with a thief."

"I'm not proud," Tamsin said. "That died this morning."

* * *

They went back to the guildhall together through the rain to gather what could be carried, and that was the mistake, or the mercy—history rarely sorts which. Because when they came up the wet lane to the guild's door, the lamps inside were burning, and through the window Tamsin saw Master Corvin seated in his chair by the fire, upright, formal, receiving a visitor.

Brown wool coat. Silver temples. Hands clasped behind his back, studying the map wall.

"He came back," Wren breathed. "First night. He *came back*."

Through the glass, Halvane said something they couldn't hear. Corvin answered, stiff as a fencepost. And then the Inquisitor turned his head—slowly, deliberately, without hurry, the way a man turns when he already knows exactly what he will see—and looked directly out the window at Tamsin standing in the rain.

He smiled. He raised one hand, courteous as a host, and gestured at the door.

*Tamsin felt the fear go through him, clean and total, the way a blade goes through cloth—and then it stopped, somewhere behind his ribs, and hardened into something else. Something with an edge.*

"Stay behind me," he said to Wren, "and let me do the lying."

Then he pushed open the door of the only home he had ever known, and stepped inside to lie to the Crown a second time.
