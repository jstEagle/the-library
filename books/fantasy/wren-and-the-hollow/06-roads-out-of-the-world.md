## 6. Roads Out of the World

### TAMSIN

The road out of Marrowgate was not a road at all.

That was the point. Tamsin had told Wren this twice already—once when they slipped out through the tannery gate before dawn, and once again an hour later when she'd stopped on a hillside of gorse and asked, flatly, where in all the hells he was taking her, because there was no path here, no track, nothing but sheep-cropped grass and the first grey suggestion of the Thornfells to the north.

"There's always a path," Tamsin said. "Most people just can't see it."

"And you can?"

"My guild could." He said *could*, and felt the word land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. "Four of us now. And one leaking roof we sold yesterday."

Wren looked at him sidelong. She had a way of looking at things sideways, as if approaching them head-on might frighten them off. "You sold your own guildhall."

"The Guildmaster sold it. I just watched."

"And then you stole a dead man's map and ran north with a pickpocket you met yesterday."

"I didn't steal it," Tamsin said. "It was given to me. By a dying man who bled silver letters. There's a difference."

"Is there?" She pulled her hood up against the wind coming off the high country. "Because from where I'm standing it looks like theft. The map wanted taking. That man chose you. Same crime, prettier wrapping."

He should have argued. Instead he put his hand in his pocket, where the folded map sat warm as a sleeping bird, and said nothing, and walked on.

The smugglers' road began where the shepherds' path ended—or rather, it began in the space between two paths, the seam nobody mended because nobody saw it. Tamsin found it by feel more than sight: a dip in the turf that drained wrong, a cairn of stones that looked natural until you noticed every stone was the same grey as the mountain behind it, placed by hands that hadn't wanted them found. His master, old Corvin, had taught him to read these seams like a priest reads scripture. *A road is a promise someone made once and hoped would be forgotten,* Corvin used to say. *Our job is remembering.*

They climbed through the morning. Below them Marrowgate shrank to a smudge of smoke and tile, and beyond it the lowlands lay green and innocent under a sky scrubbed clean by wind. Somewhere back there, Inquisitor Serle Halvane was asking polite questions in rooms with good light. Tamsin did not look back. Looking back was how you got caught; his feet knew this better than his head did.

By noon they'd crossed the first ridge and dropped into a hanging valley that appeared on no map Tamsin had ever seen sold in any market town. A stream ran down its middle, loud and white over slate. He stopped there to fill the waterskins, and when he straightened, Wren was watching him with her arms crossed and something almost grudging in her face.

"All right," she said. "I'll say it once and never again. You're useful."

"High praise."

"You have no idea. My grandmother used to say the Wayfarers were the only honest thieves in the world—you steal the roads right out from under the king's nose and give them back to anybody with feet." She took the waterskin from him, drank, wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "She sang about them, sometimes. Along with everything else."

"Everything else being?"

"Aldermere." Wren said the name quietly, the way you'd set down something breakable. The wind seemed to lean closer to listen. "Songs about roads, mostly. Roads and doors and a river with seven names. I thought they were just songs. Then I saw your map, and—" She stopped. Shook her head. "It's the shape. Nobody draws that shape unless they know it."

Tamsin touched the map through the wool of his coat. It had been quiet since Marrowgate, but quiet wasn't the same as still. He'd learned that much in three nights of trying to be rid of it.

"Teach me one," he said.

"One what?"

"Song. An Aldermere song. If I'm going to carry the thing around, I'd rather know what it's singing about."

Wren studied him for a long moment, and he watched her decide—not whether to trust him, exactly, but how much of herself trusting him was going to cost. Then she sat down on a flat stone beside the stream, patted the space next to her with the brisk imperiousness of someone much older than she looked, and said:

"The walking song, then. Everyone learns that one first."

### WREN

Her grandmother's voice lived in her throat like a bird in a chimney, and when Wren opened her mouth to sing it came out older than she was.

*Over stone and under thorn,*
*where the fold is drawn and worn,*
*walk the ones the world forgot—*
*the road remembers, or it's not.*

Tamsin listened the way he seemed to do everything: completely, without any part of himself held back for later. She'd noticed that about him in Marrowgate, in the alley behind the fishmonger's stall with her wrist in his grip and the map between them. Most men, catching a thief, spent the moment deciding what to do with their anger. He'd spent it looking at her face, as if her face were a text in a language he was trying to learn. It had been, frankly, unnerving. It still was.

"That's the chorus," she said. "The verses change depending where you're walking. Grandmother knew eleven. She said there used to be a verse for every mile of the border, hundreds of them, and each one was a direction."

"A song as a map."

"A map as a song. Same thing, maybe, if you did it right." She hugged her knees. The stream chattered on below them, oblivious. "She'd sing it while she sewed, or kneading bread, or whenever her hands were busy and her mind went somewhere else. Sometimes I'd catch her crying after the seventh-name-river verse and she'd say it was onions. We didn't even have onions."

Tamsin laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed, like he wasn't sure he was allowed. "My Guildmaster cries at onions too. Actual onions. He says it's the fumes."

"He sounds terrible."

"He is. He's also the reason I can walk a cliff face blindfolded, so." Tamsin turned the waterskin over in his hands. "Sing it again? The second line. I want to get the shape of it."

So she sang it again, and again, and somewhere in the fourth repetition he joined her, badly, his voice a low rough thing that kept sliding off the melody like a cart wheel finding ruts. She corrected him. He ignored half her corrections and absorbed the other half, and by the time the sun stood over the western ridge they'd sung the walking song six times through and Wren had laughed twice, which was more than she'd laughed since the night the constables burned her grandmother's room for tax arrears and she'd run with a bundle of clothes and nothing else.

*The road remembers, or it's not.* Her grandmother's hands, moving over hers, showing her the stitches. A kitchen that smelled of rosemary and coal smoke. A country Wren had never seen, in a world that insisted it had never existed, held together entirely by an old woman's stubborn throat.

And now here was this earnest, exhausted Wayfarer carrying the actual shape of it in his pocket, humming her grandmother's song wrong, heading north into mountains because a dying stranger had asked him to.

She didn't say any of that. She taught him the second chorus instead, and when his voice cracked on the high note she pretended not to notice, which was, she was beginning to understand, the local currency of kindness among his kind.

### TAMSIN

The map woke on the third night.

They'd made camp in the ruins of a drover's bothy halfway up the Grey Shoulder, walls of dry-stone waist-high, roof long gone, stars for a ceiling. Tamsin took first watch out of habit—he slept badly on the road anyway—and sat with his back against the wall while Wren curled up in her cloak near the dead fire, breathing slow and even.

Around midnight, the map grew warm in his pocket.

Not uncomfortably warm. Warm the way a hand is warm. He drew it out and unfolded it across his knees, and in the starlight the ink was moving.

He'd seen it shift before—in the guildhall, in the small hours, lines creeping like frost across glass. But this was different. This was deliberate. The rivers of Aldermere were flowing, actually flowing, tiny silver currents running along their courses. The mountains rippled. And along the borders of the country—the erased country, the impossible country—a script was surfacing, letter by letter, rising through the paper like drowned things coming up for air.

Tamsin's blood went cold, and then colder, because he knew that script.

He'd watched it crawl out of a dying man's wound on the Grey Stair pass, silver-bright against torn flesh, pressing up through skin as though the man's own body were a page being written on. He'd told himself it was some Inquisitor's mark, some brand. He'd told himself a great many convenient things.

Now he held the map to the starlight and read, as best he could, the characters surfacing along Aldermere's edge. Curved. Interlocking. Each glyph built from smaller glyphs the way a wall is built from stones, and all of it faintly luminous, all of it patient.

The same writing. Stroke for stroke. The script on the stranger's wound and the script on the map were one hand, one language, one purpose—and the map had been carrying it all along, waiting for darkness and solitude to show him what it was made of.

"Tamsin." Wren's voice, sleep-rough, behind him. "You've gone stiff as a corpse. What is it?"

He couldn't answer immediately. His mouth wouldn't arrange the words.

She crawled over, hair full of straw, eyes sharp despite the hour, and followed his pointing finger to the border of the map. He watched her go pale in stages—the recognition arriving, then the understanding, then whatever came after understanding.

"That's what was on him," she whispered. "On the Grey Stair. When you described it. That's the same—"

"Yes."

"What does it say?"

"I don't know. It's not any alphabet I've been taught." He traced one glyph above the paper without touching it. Under his fingertip, the ink stirred, leaning toward his hand like a plant toward sun. He snatched his finger back. "But it knows me, Wren. Whatever this says—it's not addressed to the map. Look."

He held the map up so the starlight fell full upon it, turning slowly. The script brightened as it faced him. Dimmed as it turned away. Brightened. Dimmed. Like a lamp being carried past windows.

"It's reading me," Tamsin said. "Or it's waiting for me to read it."

Wren reached out suddenly and took his hand—not gently; she gripped it like a rope thrown to a drowning woman, hard enough to hurt—and pressed his palm flat against the page.

The script flared.

For one heartbeat every glyph along Aldermere's border blazed silver-white, and Tamsin felt something move through him, a current with a texture to it, cold and quick and familiar as his own pulse, as if a door inside his chest had blown open onto a room he'd never entered but somehow recognized—

Then dark. Ordinary dark. The stars, the ruined walls, Wren's grip slackening around his fingers.

Neither of them spoke for a long time. The stream murmured far below. Somewhere on the slope a fox screamed once and went silent.

"We're not running from the Inquisitors anymore, are we," Wren said at last. It wasn't a question. "We're running toward something. The map's been leading us since Marrowgate. North. Always north."

"Halvane will follow us either way."

"Then let him follow." She said it fiercely, still holding his hand, as if letting go would admit what the touching had cost. "Whatever that writing is, whoever wrote it—they wrote it on a living man and they wrote it on a map and now it's waking up between my fingers, so somebody, somewhere, meant for it to arrive somewhere. And I want to know where before the Crown's dogs do."

Tamsin looked down at their hands, hers brown and scarred from lock-picking, his calloused from rope and rock, both of them filthy, both of them afraid, neither letting go. Two days ago she'd tried to rob him. Tonight she was the only person in the world standing between him and the dark, and he between her and it, and that arithmetic felt older and sturdier than anything the guildhall had ever given him.

"Get some sleep," he said softly. "We cross the Grey Shoulder at first light. There's a road up there the Crown doesn't know exists—I'm going to teach it to you."

"Why?"

"Because roads die when only one person remembers them." He folded the map, and its warmth lingered against his chest like a second heartbeat. "And I don't intend to die on this one."

Above the eastern ridge, hours before dawn, a light was climbing. Not a star. Not the moon.

It moved the way a lantern moves—steadily, deliberately, following the ridgeline north. Following *them*.

Tamsin watched it come, and beside him Wren followed his gaze and went very still, and neither of them sang anything at all.
