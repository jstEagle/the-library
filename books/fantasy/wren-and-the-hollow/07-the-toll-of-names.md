## 7. The Toll of Names

Bellharbour smelled of fish and tar and old rain, and Wren hated it on sight.

Not the village itself—she'd slept in worse places than a harbour town with three working inns and a market square wide enough to lose a pursuer in twice over. It was the way people looked at her. Or rather, the way they looked at Tamsin, and then at her, and then did that small thing with their faces where they decided she was trouble and Tamsin was trouble's keeper.

"You're staring," Tamsin murmured. He had the map rolled inside his coat now, against his ribs, where he'd taken to keeping it since the night it refused to burn. "People stare when you stare."

"I'm assessing exits." She was. The north lane ran between a chandler's and a cooper's yard; there was a gap behind the fish-market stalls wide enough for two people if they turned sideways; the churchyard wall had a stile with a rotten top rail she could vault and he would have to climb like an old man. She'd mapped all of it before Tamsin had finished tying off the mule. Old habits. Marrowgate habits. You learned a town's bones before you learned its name, because its name wouldn't save you but its bones might.

"Assess this," Tamsin said. "The inn has beds. Actual beds. With straw in them."

"Straw. Be still my heart."

"And hot food that isn't hardtack or whatever that grey thing was you ate yesterday."

"That grey thing was pottage."

"It was a crime against barley." He paid the ostler, counted out coins like a man who'd been poor long enough to count twice, and led the way toward the sign of the Salt Bell. And that was the other reason she hated Bellharbour, though she couldn't have said so yet: the name of the inn was written on a board above the door in letters that had been repainted so many times they stood out from the wood like scars, and underneath them someone had carved, generations ago, a line of script.

Silver script.

She stopped dead in the middle of the lane. A man carrying a basket of eels swore at her and went around.

"Wren?"

"Look at the lintel."

Tamsin looked. She watched him see it—the same faint, fine lettering that had been pressed into a dying man's skin on the Grey Stair, the same writing that shifted across the map when Tamsin slept. It wasn't glowing here. It wasn't anything. Just old carving worn nearly smooth, the kind of thing a hundred years of hands had touched without seeing.

"It's everywhere, isn't it," Tamsin said quietly. "It's not just the map. It's in the walls."

*It's in the country,* Wren thought, and then didn't say, because the country didn't exist, and thinking words like *country* about lines of silver in a lintel was how you ended up standing in a market square looking like a girl who'd lost something.

They went inside.

* * *

The Salt Bell's common room was low-beamed and dim, lit by a dozen oil lamps hung on iron rings, and full of the particular evening population of a harbour town: fishermen with salt-cracked hands, a customs officer drinking alone and pretending not to be a customs officer, two old women by the fire who had the settled, immovable quality of furniture that had been there longer than the building.

The innkeeper was a broad, red-faced man named Doss who took their coin without questions, which Wren approved of, and gave them a room at the back with one window onto the yard, which she also approved of. Tamsin ordered stew. Wren ordered the same and then, because she'd been carrying a question since Marrowgate and the question had been growing teeth, leaned her elbows on the table and said:

"How far is it from here to the old border?"

Tamsin's spoon paused. "There isn't one."

"There isn't one *now*. There used to be. Aldermere—" 

The lamps guttered.

All of them. Every lamp in the common room, twelve flames leaning at once as if a door had opened somewhere, though every door and window was shut against the sea wind. The fire dropped to embers and flared again. The whole room went brown and shadowed for the space of a heartbeat, and in that heartbeat nobody spoke, nobody moved, everybody looked at—

At Wren.

She felt the look land on her like a thrown stone. The fishermen had gone still. The customs officer's hand had drifted, casually, terribly, toward the knife on his belt. And the two old women by the fire had turned around in their chairs, slow as tide, and were staring at her with expressions she had no word for except that she'd seen it once before, on the face of a woman whose son's body they'd pulled out of the canal, and the woman hadn't been crying, she'd been somewhere past crying, somewhere where grief went quiet and just *looked* at you.

"What did you say?" asked the older of the two women. Her voice was thin and clear as ice on a puddle.

Wren's mouth was dry. "Nothing. I was asking about roads—"

"You said the name." The woman rose. Up close she was smaller than she'd seemed, bird-boned, with white hair braided in a style Wren had only ever seen in her grandmother's mirror. "You said *Aldermere*, girl. In my mother's day they'd have washed your mouth with soap and salt for less."

"Dydda," the innkeeper said carefully. "They're travelers. They don't know the custom."

"Then they'll learn it." But the old woman—Dydda—hadn't stopped looking at Wren, and what was in her face wasn't anger anymore. It was the other thing. The quiet thing. "Sit down, child. Both of you. Maren," she said to her companion, "fetch the bottle under the bar, the one Doss pretends isn't there."

Maren fetched the bottle. Doss pretended not to notice, which meant pouring the cups himself and setting them down with the tenderness of a priest handling relics.

Dydda sat down across from Wren and folded her hands, and said: "Say it again."

"I—"

"The name. Say it slowly. I want to hear how you say it."

Wren looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked back with the expression he wore when the map had done something impossible overnight, which was to say the expression of a man standing on a road that was moving under him and deciding, stubbornly, to keep walking. He nodded once.

"Aldermere," Wren said.

The lamps dipped. Not all the way out—a shiver, a lean, like wheat under wind—and this time Wren watched it happen and understood it wasn't wind, wasn't draft, wasn't anything a chandler could fix. The fire sighed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked though no one walked on it. And Dydda closed her eyes and breathed out through her nose, long and controlled, the way you breathe when you've practiced not weeping for sixty years.

"There," she said softly. "You say it like it's a place. Like it's *somewhere*." She opened her eyes. "Everyone else says it like a swear. Or a warning. *Don't speak that name, it's bad luck, it calls things.* They say it like a word that bit them once. You say it like a home."

"I never had one," Wren said, before she could stop herself. "A home. My gran used to sing about it. Songs. I thought they were songs."

"What songs?"

Wren hesitated. Singing in public was not something she did; singing was for alone, for rooftops, for the hour before dawn when Marrowgate was too tired to listen. But Dydda was waiting, and the whole room had gone soft-eared around them, fishermen pretending to drink while their heads tilted, and Wren found that her throat had made up its mind before the rest of her had.

She sang the lullaby. Low, barely above the fire's mutter—the one her grandmother had sung every night of Wren's childhood, the one about the little boat and the grey water and the door beneath the hill that opens for those who know the turning of the year. She'd never known what half the words meant. Some of them weren't Common-tongue at all; Gran had said they were *old words*, *our words*, and young Wren had assumed *our words* meant *poor words*, the private dialect of people with nothing.

Halfway through the second verse, Dydda began to shake.

Not dramatically. Her shoulders moved; her breath caught; her knuckles went white around her cup. Maren put a hand on her arm and left it there. And when Wren trailed off, uncertain, Dydda said, in a voice scraped raw:

"*Nen vaela, nen dorath, nen imma-sol.*" The words came out of her whole, unbroken, in a language that made Wren's spine prickle, because it was the language of the silver script, she was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of your own name. "*Sleep, the door keeps, the year turns round.*" She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at herself. "My grandmother sang that. Hers sang it before her. We're harbour folk—there were families here, once, that kept the old ways. Kept more than ways." She looked up, and her wet eyes fixed on Wren with terrible attention. "Where is your grandmother buried, child?"

"She's not. She was burned. Plague year, in Marrowgate, mass pit, no markers." Wren heard her own voice go flat and practical, the pickpocket's voice, the voice that handled things. "Why? What does the song open?"

Dydda laughed—one cracked note, no humour in it. "Opens. Listen to her. Asks the right question like she's haggling over eels." She leaned forward. "Your grandmother's family were keepers. Hollow-keepers. Do you know what I'm saying?"

"No."

"Good," said Dydda, unexpectedly. "Because neither do I, not really—not the whole of it, nobody living knows the whole of it—but I know what my gran told me before she died, and she made me swear to pass it on if ever—" She stopped. Started again. "She made me swear to watch for a song. That exact song. Sung by someone young, someone with the blood, someone asking after roads that aren't on any map. She said if I ever heard it sung right, I was to tell them the rest of the verse."

Wren's heart was doing something complicated against her ribs. "There's another verse?"

"There are four," Dydda said. "You have two. Everyone who carries the blood gets taught two—it's enough to put a child to sleep, and it's all most families remembered anyway. The third verse is the key. The fourth is the lock." She held up a hand before Wren could speak. "I'll teach it to you. On one condition. You tell me why. Why now, after two hundred years, does a girl walk into my common room singing the waking-song with a Wayfarer's coat on her arm and a dead man's business written all over her face?"

Wren glanced at Tamsin. Tamsin, after a moment, reached into his coat and laid the map on the table between the cups.

He didn't unroll it all the way. Just a corner—enough that Dydda and Maren could see the ink shifting, slow as oil on water, roads rearranging themselves with patient intent. The lamps leaned toward it. All of them, together, like flowers toward sun.

Dydda made a sound low in her throat. Maren crossed herself with the wrong hand, the old way, thumb to ring finger, and whispered something in the old tongue that might have been a prayer and might have been a curse and might, Wren thought, have been both.

"Two hundred years," Dydda said at last, very quietly, "and it comes back to Bellharbour first. Of course it does. Harbours remember. Water remembers. That's why they burned the records and drowned the priests and let the towns keep nothing but names—" she gestured at the ceiling, at the town beyond it, at the lintel over the door with its worn silver script— "and even the names they couldn't dig out. You can't rename the sea, girl. You can't rename the shape of a bay. So the Crown left us our geography and took everything else, and every family that knew anything was told to forget it, and some of us forgot, and some of us just got very good at being quiet."

"My gran wasn't quiet," Wren said. "She sang every night."

"Then she was braver than me, and I'm sorry I never met her." Dydda straightened, and something in her posture changed—something formal, something old, the ghost of an office coming up through the bent back like a banner through smoke. "Maren. Bolt the door."

Maren bolted the door. The customs officer, remarkably, found somewhere else to drink.

* * *

Dydda taught her the third verse by candlelight, in the inn's back parlour, with the shutters closed and the map lying on the table between them like a sleeping animal.

It was harder than Wren expected. Not the words—words were easy, words were just shapes your mouth learned—but the melody. The melody of the third verse wasn't a tune, exactly. It was a set of intervals that kept wanting to resolve into the lullaby she knew and refusing to, turning aside at the last note like a path bending around a stone. Twice she got it wrong and the candle flame simply sat there, dumb and ordinary, and Dydda shook her head. The third time she got it right, and the flame stood up tall and burned a colour Wren had no name for—silver-white, cold at its heart—and every shadow in the room leaned away from it.

"Again," Dydda whispered. "Twice more, so it sits in your body and not just your head. A key you carry in your head can be knocked loose. A key in the body stays."

Wren sang it twice more. By the end her eyes were stinging and she didn't fully know why, except that she was learning her grandmother's inheritance from a stranger in a harbour town, and her grandmother had died in a plague pit, and there was no one left alive who could tell Wren whether she'd sung these verses to her on purpose—whether the lullaby had been love, or duty, or both, or whether Gran had spent forty years passing a loaded thing to a child and praying she'd never need it.

"There," Dydda said, when the last note settled. "Third verse. Now hear me on the fourth, and hear me well, because I'll teach it to you last and I may be dead before you need it, and either way you'll wish you'd listened." She gripped Wren's wrist, bird-boned fingers surprisingly strong. "The fourth verse doesn't wake anything. The fourth verse *closes*. Whatever you open with the third, you must be able to seal with the fourth, or you shouldn't open it at all. My gran said the keepers' first law was: *no door without a hand on both sides of the song.* Do you understand me? Promise me."

"I promise," Wren said. And found, to her own surprise, that she meant it—that some promise made in a stranger's parlour by candlelight had settled into her like a keel, steadying something that had been listing her whole life.

Dydda studied her a long moment, then released her wrist and sagged back, suddenly every one of her eighty-odd years. "Blood of the keepers," she murmured, half to herself. "In my common room. Drinking my son's beer." A wet laugh. "Gran always said the song would come home by the sea road. I thought she meant a person coming back. Never occurred to me she meant the song walking on its own legs, wearing somebody new."

"I'm not from Aldermere," Wren said. "I'm from nowhere. Marrowgate gutters and a roof I broke into when I was nine. That's the whole of my country."

Dydda looked at her with an expression of such gentleness that Wren wanted to break something. "Child," she said, "you just sang a dead language's waking-verse and lit a flame that hasn't burned that colour since before my grandmother was born. You can be from nowhere. The song clearly disagrees."

* * *

Later—much later, the candles burned to thumbs, Dydda asleep in her chair and Maren dozing opposite—Wren stood at the parlour window with her forehead nearly touching the glass, looking out at Bellharbour's dark harbour and the black water beyond it, and tried to feel like the same person who'd walked into this town that morning.

She couldn't find her.

The girl who'd walked in had been running from Inquisitors, protecting a map, protecting a Wayfarer boy who was too honest for his own good and whom she had absolutely not been thinking about as anything other than a travelling companion, thank you. That girl had a plan: get the map north, sell it if selling became necessary, survive. Clean. Simple. Pickpocket's logic.

The girl standing at the window now had a verse of a dead song sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal, and a promise on her wrist where an old woman's fingers had been, and the sudden, unbearable knowledge that everything her grandmother had been—every strange word, every odd habit, the way Gran had faced east when she prayed and kept a stone on the sill that she'd never let Wren touch—hadn't been poverty's quirks at all. It had been a country, carried in a single burning woman through forty years of exile, and handed to Wren in lullabies, and Wren had spent her childhood wishing Gran would sing something *normal*.

She'd wanted ordinary songs. She'd been offered keys.

Behind her, floorboards creaked. Tamsin, come down from the room he'd finally claimed, hair sleep-mussed, the map's corner showing at his collar like a second heartbeat. He didn't say anything. He just came and stood beside her at the window, shoulder almost touching hers, and looked out at the black water the way Wayfarers looked at roads.

"My gran was a keeper," Wren said. "Of a door under a hill. And she never told me. She just sang."

"Maybe telling wasn't allowed," Tamsin said. "Maybe singing was the only safe way to carry it."

"Maybe." Wren's breath fogged the glass. Out on the harbour, one of the moored fishing boats rocked against its rope with no wind to move it, and the water around its hull shimmered briefly—just briefly—with light that wasn't moonlight. Silver-white. Cold at the heart. "Or maybe she was waiting for me to ask the right question, and I never did, and now she's a plague pit and the question is asking *me*."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then: "What question?"

And Wren didn't answer, because at that moment every lamp in the Salt Bell—doused hours ago, wicks cold, oil low—flared alight at once, all down the corridor, all through the dark common room, twelve small silver-white flames burning steady as stars.

And on the map, visible even folded, even through cloth, the ink began to move. Northward. Toward the mountains.

Toward Vael.
