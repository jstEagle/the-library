## 8. Halvane's Hounds

They came on the third night out of Bellharbour, and Tamsin knew it before he knew it—the way you know a storm by the ache in an old break, or a change in the wind by the way birds go quiet.

He was on first watch, or what passed for it: sitting against a granite shoulder with the map unrolled across his knees and no fire lit, because fire was a thing other people got to have. Wren slept curled in the lee of the rock with her grandmother's shawl over her face, breathing in the small, stubborn rhythm of someone who had learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing. The map's silver lines had gone still for once, lying quiet under his fingers like a sleeping animal.

Then they weren't still.

The ink crawled. Not shifting to show him new roads this time—contracting, all of it, every river and ridge drawing inward toward a single point on the western edge of the sheet, as if the whole country were flinching from something.

Tamsin looked up.

The valley below was black and ordinary. Mist in the low places. A fox crying somewhere near the tree line, thin and high, and then not crying at all, cut off mid-note in a way that had nothing to do with a fox being answered.

That was the thing about the hounds—he understood this only later, piecing it together from what the script let him feel—but that first night he knew only that the world had stopped making its usual noises. No owl. No rustle. Even the mist seemed to be holding its breath.

"Wren." He said it quietly, then again, sharper. "Wren, up. Now."

She came awake the way she did everything: fast, silent, a knife already in her hand from somewhere inside her sleeve. She didn't ask questions. That was one of the thousand things he'd already stopped taking for granted about her.

"What is it?"

"I don't know. Something's coming up the valley and everything else has left."

She listened. Ten heartbeats. Twenty. Then her face changed in the starlight—not fear exactly, but the particular grimness of someone recognizing a shape from bad stories. "My gran used to say there were men the Inquisition kept," she whispered. "In Marrowgate they called them the quiet kind. You'd hear about a debtor who ran, and then you'd hear nothing, and the nothing would come walking down your street."

"Gran's stories are proving annoyingly accurate lately."

"They usually do. That's what makes them stories." She was already rolling her bedding, cinching knots without looking at her hands. "Which way?"

Tamsin looked down at the map. The silver lines had stopped contracting. Instead, one road—a smuggler's path he knew well, a goat-track along the north rim of the valley called the Shepherd's Cut—had brightened until it seemed to hold its own light, the way iron holds the memory of a forge.

The map was telling him to run. It occurred to him, distantly, that he had no idea whether the map wanted to save him or simply wanted to keep itself out of other hands, and that the two things might look identical from inside.

"North," he said. "Up and over. Move like water."

* * *

They moved like water for six hours.

It was work Tamsin loved even now, even terrified, because it was the work his bones were made of: reading slope and stone, choosing the line that left no line, keeping Wren's lighter steps matched to his so their footfalls landed together and confused the ear. The moon rose and set behind cloud. Twice he heard something far below them in the dark—a sound like footsteps, but wrong, too evenly spaced, as if whatever made them never varied its stride by so much as a stone's width.

Dawn found them on a bald shoulder of mountain above a hanging valley, gray light the color of dishwater, and Tamsin let himself believe, for eleven full seconds, that they'd broken clear.

Then Wren grabbed his arm. "There."

Below them, at the mouth of the valley they'd climbed out of, three figures stood in a row, facing upslope. They had not been climbing. They had simply arrived—as if the mountain had grown tired of carrying them and set them down where they'd decided to end up.

Even at that distance Tamsin could see what was wrong with them. They stood too still. Men who climb all night shift their weight, roll their shoulders, breathe hard. These three stood like posts, and their heads turned in unison, slowly, toward the ridge where Tamsin and Wren crouched.

And on the bare skin of their faces—foreheads, cheekbones, the backs of their shaved skulls—silver script caught the dawn and glinted.

"The same writing," Wren breathed. "Like the dead man. Like—" She stopped, because she'd seen it too: the faint shine on the back of Tamsin's own hand, where the stranger's blood had touched him, where the script had been spreading in slow silver threads ever since.

"Don't," Tamsin said. Not harshly. "Don't say it."

"They're made of the same thing you're catching."

"I said don't."

But she was right, and they both knew it, and knowing it settled into him like cold water into boots. Whatever Halvane had loosed on their trail had been written into existence with the same alphabet that was writing itself into Tamsin's veins. He wasn't running from the script. He was running ahead of it.

The three figures began to walk uphill. Not fast. Not slowing. Just walking, at a pace that would eat the distance between them by midmorning and would not stop for nightfall or blizzard or death, because whatever drove them had never heard of either.

"Can we outrun them?" Wren asked.

"No. Nobody outruns anything on a mountainside; the ground decides the race." His mind was already moving, though, sliding down old grooves worn deep by ten years of apprenticeship—Guildmaster Odo's voice in his head, half exasperated, half fond: *Anyone can walk a road, boy. The trade is in unmaking one.*

"There's a place," he said slowly. "Half a day north. The Scrape—it's a shelf of scree, a mile of loose stone, no soil, nothing to hold a print. Wayfarers used it when the Crown taxed the passes and wanted tolls paid in more than coin. You cross it in single file, stepping only where the stone's already fallen, and by the far side you're a rumor."

"And if they follow us onto it?"

"That's the second part." He met her eyes. "It's also a trap. There's a seam under the shelf—an old slide-path, one good pull on a lever-stone and the whole slope comes down behind you. My grandmaster helped build the trigger. It's in the guild book."

"You brought the guild book?"

"I'm the last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild, Wren. I *am* the guild book."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but the place where one lived. "Then let's go be a rumor."

* * *

They reached the Scrape as the sun cleared the eastern ridgeline and lay flat and white on the stone.

It was worse than he remembered—or rather, it was exactly as bad as he remembered, which after years of fond recollection amounted to the same shock. A mile of shattered grey rock, tilted at the angle of a held-out hand, with nothing on it but stone and more stone, the debris of some ancient collapse that had never finished falling. No trail. No cairn. Only the pattern, which Tamsin carried in his head because Odo had drilled it into him at fifteen with a switch and a stream of curses: *fall-line's your friend, crosswise is death, and never—never—step on the pale ones.* The pale stones sat on frost-heaved ice beneath. Step on a pale one and you'd ride a half-ton of rock down the slope in a long red smear.

"Single file," he said. "My prints. Exactly my prints. And Wren—" he caught her shoulder, "—if I fall, you keep going. Don't think. Keep going."

"If you fall, I'll think about it," she said, which from Wren was practically a sonnet.

He led. For the first third of the mile it was almost meditative—the old rhythm, the choosing of each step, the world narrowed to angle and grain and weight. Behind them, faintly, came the sound he'd been dreading: the hounds reaching the edge of the scree. Three sets of feet beginning to descend. Unhurried. Unvarying. Walking straight down the fall-line as if loose stone were a paved street, each footfall landing with a flat, deliberate knock, like knuckles on a door.

They don't slip. The thought arrived cold and complete. Nothing holds them but what's written on them.

Two-thirds of the way across, Tamsin stepped where he'd meant to step, and the mountain lied to him.

It happens—Odo had warned him of this too, the one lesson delivered gently: *the stone will betray you once a career, lad, and it will pick the worst hour.* A slab the size a coffin shifted under his boot, a pale slab, ice-slicked beneath, and the world tilted. He went down on the scree and felt it open under him, a slow avalanche of his own, sliding him downslope through a shriek of grinding rock—and then his hand hit stone and held, fingers hooked into a crack, his whole body jerking taut like a snared hawk, and the slide went past him on both sides in two grey rivers.

The pain came a heartbeat later, arriving up his left side. Something down there had opened—a gash along his ribs where the rock had taken its toll, deep enough that when he looked down, the front of his shirt was going dark and wet.

"Tamsin!" Wren was above him, flat on her belly, reaching down.

"I'm caught—I'm fine—don't move, the slope's still—"

Behind and below, the knocking footsteps had stopped. All three hounds stood motionless on the scree, heads tilted, listening. Listening to him breathe.

He understood then, with a clarity that felt like the script itself pressing against the inside of his skull: they weren't tracking scent or sight. They were tracking *him*. The silver in his veins rang like a struck bell, and the hounds were ears. Every drop of blood he lost was a shout.

Move. Get off the stone. Get to the lever.

He hauled himself up the crack, hand over hand, teeth locked, Wren's grip finding his wrist at the top and dragging him onto solid shelf. Blood ran warm down his side and soaked cold at his belt. Fifty yards. Forty. The hounds resumed their walk, faster now, the knocking quickening into something like eagerness.

The lever-stone was where the guild book promised: a slab with a weathered chisel-mark, one of nine marks Odo had made him memorize in a single brutal afternoon. Tamsin dropped beside it, spat on his hands, and pulled.

Nothing.

He pulled again, vision swimming, and heard himself make a sound that wasn't words. The seam had silted shut with twenty years of winters. It would take a crowbar and an honest man an hour. It took Tamsin Kavanagh, bleeding, thirty seconds and a fury he didn't know he owned—because Brannic's face had surfaced in his mind, unbidden, the traitor's easy grin, *you'll understand someday why I sold what I sold*, and no. No. He was not going to die on this rock with the guild's last secret buried in silt.

The stone gave with a boom like a felled tree.

The Scrape moved. All of it, the whole hanging mile of it, shrugging its shoulders after two hundred years of waiting, and went down the mountain in a roar that swallowed the sky. Dust boiled up white and total. Tamsin felt Wren's arms around him, both of them flattened against the stable shelf while the world avalanched, and through the roar he thought he heard—one moment, maybe imagined—the knocking footsteps stop forever.

* * *

He woke to pain and the smell of pine smoke, which meant fire, which meant Wren had decided the hounds were dead enough to risk it, which meant either safety or the end of everything.

He was lying under an overhang in a fold of the hills north of the Scrape, evening light slanting gold through the dust haze still hanging over the southern ridges. His shirt was off. His ribs were bound. And Wren was sitting cross-legged beside him with a needle in her fingers and a spool of gut thread and an expression of profound dissatisfaction.

"You're awake. Good. Because I need you to hold still, and unconscious people argue less, but I'd rather you were arguing."

"You stitched me?"

"I stitched you *badly*," she corrected. "I've picked pockets since I was six. Nobody taught me this. There was a woman in Marrowgate who patched sailors, and I watched her twice through a window." She threaded the needle with the fierce concentration of someone defusing a bomb. "So hold still, and don't look at the knotwork, and if you get sick, aim away from the fire."

The stitches hurt. He'd expected that. What he hadn't expected was her hands.

They shook. Not much—she fought it down—but he saw it, saw the tremor run through her fingers every time she pressed the needle in and drew the thread, saw her jaw set against it. Wren, who lifted a purse off a merchant without the man's shadow noticing. Wren, who'd walked a ledge over a gorge two nights ago like a cat on a fence.

"You're frightened," he said.

"Don't be absurd. I'm concentrating."

"Wren."

She tied off a stitch, harder than necessary, and he hissed. "Fine. Yes. I'm frightened." The words came out fast and low, like coins spilled from a torn purse. "I've been frightened since Bellharbour, actually, and before that since the day I recognized the map, and I will be frightened tomorrow. But here's the thing I figured out on that scree slope while you were busy dying heroically at me." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely level. "When that stone went, I didn't think *the map*. I didn't think *Aldermere*. I thought, *if he goes down that slope I don't care about any of it, I'll dig through the whole mountain with my hands.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere distant, the dust was settling over the graves of three written men.

"So hold still," she said, quieter now, bending back to her work, "because I am very bad at this, and you are going to live anyway, and those two facts are not negotiable."

Tamsin lay still. The pain in his side thudded in time with his pulse, and underneath it, deeper, the silver threads in his veins hummed their strange quiet note—fainter than yesterday, he thought. Or maybe just drowned out.

Because here was the truth he couldn't say yet, wouldn't say, holding it behind his teeth like a hot coal: the wound had bled a great deal, and where the blood had soaked the bandage, the script on his skin had spread another inch. Each drop of him lost to the mountain was a drop gained by whatever the stranger had put in him. The cost of running was becoming literal. It was writing itself down his ribs in silver.

He watched Wren's shaking hands work, and thought: *this is what home feels like from the outside. This is what I was stolen from, or saved for. One of the two.*

"Wren," he said. "Look."

She followed his gaze to his own forearm, where beneath the grime and the fresh bandage-tie the silver script had surfaced overnight—new lines now, fine as spider silk, and among them, unmistakably, a shape. Not letters this time.

A road. A specific road, rendered in the map's own hand, drawn on his skin in the same ink that had crawled across parchment: a route leading east and down, beneath the Thornfells, toward a valley marked on no chart he'd ever guided anyone through.

At its end, the script had drawn a small, patient symbol.

A door.

Wren went very still, needle forgotten. "It's not running anymore," she said softly. "Is it. It's done hiding us."

"It's showing us where it wants to be buried," Tamsin said. "Or where it wants to be born. With that family, I've honestly stopped trying to guess."

Outside the overhang, the last light died off the peaks, and far to the south—so faint it might have been wind, but wasn't—something howled that had no throat to howl with.

More of them. Halvane had more of them.

Tamsin closed his eyes and let the map's new road burn itself into his memory, the way the guild had burned a hundred roads into him before it. "Get some sleep," he told Wren. "Tomorrow we stop running *from* something."

He touched the door symbol, feather-light, and felt it hum against his fingertip like a struck string.

"Tomorrow we find out what we've been running *to*."
