## 9. The Cartographer's Grave

*Tamsin*

The map had been pulling them north for three days, and by the third morning Tamsin understood that it was no longer content to be read. It wanted to be *followed*.

He woke at grey dawn with the map already in his hand—he'd gone to sleep with it folded inside his coat, weighted under a stone, and here it was again, warm as a living thing, its silver ink crawling into new shapes while he watched. The roads it drew were not the smugglers' tracks he knew, nor the Crown highways he'd been raised to avoid. They were older. They ran where no road should run: straight up scree slopes, straight through a river without bothering to ford it, straight toward a shoulder of the Thornfells that every map Tamsin had ever memorized marked as blank.

"Blank," Wren said, when he showed her. She crouched over the paper with her elbows on her knees, hair tied back with a strip of red cloth she refused to explain. "That's what my gran called it. The Blank. She said there was a country there once and the King's men scrubbed it off the world like a stain." She glanced up. "I thought it was a story for children. The way you tell children things."

"It's not a story," Tamsin said. His voice came out flat, and he was surprised by how much anger sat underneath it. Two hundred years of guild records, and not one line about a folded country. Either the Wayfarers had never known—and everything he'd built his life on was smaller than he believed—or they had known, and the knowing had been taken from them too.

They climbed all day. The road the map drew did not care about switchbacks; it went up the mountain's face as if the mountain had once agreed to be climbed this way, and Tamsin found his feet following ledges that shouldn't have held a goat, finding handholds exactly where his hands fell. By late afternoon he stopped pretending to lead. He simply walked where the ink told him, and the mountain, impossibly, permitted it.

Wren noticed too. She didn't say anything for a long time—she was getting better at silence, or maybe just better at sharing it with him—but near the top she said quietly, "It's like the country remembers being walked."

And Tamsin, who had spent his whole life believing that roads were made by feet, felt something turn over in him. Maybe some roads were made first, and waited.

The grave was waiting too.

* * *

*Wren*

She smelled it before she saw it: old stone and older earth, and under that, faintly, ink. Not the sharp black of a scribe's pot but something sweeter, silver-sap sweet, the same smell that rose off the map when it shifted in the night.

The entrance was cut into the mountainside behind a fall of rock that someone had arranged to look natural—a wayfarer's trick, or a dead man's last caution. The map pulled Tamsin's hand out flat, palm down, and the stones came apart along a seam no eye could find, sliding aside with a grinding sigh, exhaling two hundred years of held breath.

Wren had grown up in cellars and culverts and the undercrofts of Marrowgate, and she knew dark places the way other children knew their mothers' kitchens. But this dark was different. This dark had been *kept*.

Steps went down. Silver script lined the walls—not carved, she realized, peering close, but written, in a hand that must have worked by feel in the blackness, letters flowing one into another like water finding its level. The same script that pressed through the dead stranger's skin. The same script that now lived, faint and spreading, in the scar on Tamsin's arm.

"Tamsin," she said, and couldn't finish.

"I see it," he said. His voice was strange. Careful. The voice of a man walking on ice over deep water.

The stair ended in a small round chamber, and in the chamber stood a coffin.

Not a coffin, quite. A crypt the size of a coffin, stone-cut, its lid carved with a man's likeness—gaunt, long-fingered, eyes open. Wren had seen tomb effigies in Marrowgate's wealthy quarter, all of them sleeping, hands folded, faces smoothed into pious rest. This man's face had not been smoothed. The carver had given him open eyes and a mouth set hard, and across the lid, in silver:

*I, EDELYN VASQ, ROYAL CARTOGRAPHER OF ALDERMERE, AM BURIED WITH MY WORK BECAUSE MY WORK IS THE ONLY PLACE MY COUNTRY STILL EXISTS.*

Wren's grandmother's voice rose in her memory, singing the lullaby, and her throat closed. She reached out and touched the carved name, and the stone was warm.

"He wasn't buried," she said. "He was *entombed*. Alive. Look—" she pointed at the inner lip of the lid, where shallow grooves scored the stone, dozens of them, hundreds, thin as wire. Scratches. Made from inside.

Tamsin went very still beside her. Then, in a voice stripped down to nothing: "How long would it take to carve that much?"

Neither of them answered. Neither of them could.

The lid weighed as much as a cart, but it moved for them the way the rocks had moved, grinding back along grooves worn smooth—no. Not worn. *Guided.* As if the crypt had been made to be opened by the right people, someday, and had spent two centuries being patient.

Inside, resting on a shelf above where a body had lain—the body itself was gone, or had never been put there, or had been taken; Wren didn't let herself think about which—sat a book.

It was bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth. The map in Tamsin's pocket flared against his ribs; she saw him press a hand to his coat as though something inside had knocked.

"That's it," he said. "That's what it's been leading us to."

* * *

*Tamsin*

The journal opened to a page dated in a calendar Tamsin had never learned, in a hand so fine it looked printed. Edelyn Vasq wrote the way he must have drawn: precisely, tirelessly, each letter placed like a surveyor's mark. And what he wrote dismantled Tamsin's world with the same patience.

*They tell the people it was conquest,* the cartographer had written, in an entry near the middle. *Conquest is a wound the living can understand. What was done to Aldermere cannot be understood, only survived, and so the Crown has chosen to make even the surviving impossible.*

*There was no war. There was a treaty, signed in the night by men who had already sold us. The Inquisition's founders discovered the Fold-work—older than any kingdom, older than their precious Crown—and they did not destroy it. They used it. They took our country, every field and hearth and grave of it, and folded it out of the world like a letter no one wished to answer. Forty thousand souls, folded shut. My king signed the instrument because they held his daughter. I signed nothing. I took my maps and ran, and when they caught me at the Thornfells, they gave me a choice: burn my work and live, or be buried in it.*

*I chose the work. I have been carving these walls for six days. I am not afraid. I am a cartographer. I have made a map of the dark, and it is accurate.*

Tamsin had to stop reading then. He set the book down on his knee and looked at the scratched walls, at the thousands of thin lines, and understood that he was looking at the last map Edelyn Vasq ever drew, and that the cartographer had died with his stylus in his hand, measuring his own ending inch by inch because measurement was the only dignity left to him.

"They didn't conquer it," Wren whispered. She was standing with her hand flat against the wall, over the carved script, as if she could take warmth from it. "They *folded* it. Like laundry. Like it was nothing."

"And the treaty—" Tamsin's mouth was dry. "A treaty means both sides. It means someone on our side of the fold agreed. The Crown didn't just erase Aldermere. It erased the crime of erasing it. That's why there's nothing in the guild records. That's why Halvane wants the map." He looked up at her, and the thing rising in his chest wasn't fear anymore. It was colder and steadier than fear. "The Inquisition doesn't guard a secret, Wren. It guards a *door*. The fold is still there. Still shut. And they've spent two hundred years making sure nobody finds the seam."

The journal's final pages confirmed it. Vasq had written of the Hollow—the door out of the folded country—of keepers sworn to hold it, of a key split into song and blood and stone. He had written, in his last legible entry, a warning that read like a curse passed forward through the dark:

*If you are reading this, then the map has found you, and you are already guilty in the Inquisition's eyes. Know this: they will not call it murder, what they do to you. They will call it cartography. They have been redrawing the world for two hundred years, and they have gotten very good at erasing.*

*Find the Hollow. Open it or close it, but do not let them decide. A country is not a secret. A country is a debt, and debts do not die with the men who bury them.*

The last page held a drawing. Tamsin turned it toward the grey light from the stair and felt the floor tilt under him.

It was the door. Grey stone, tall as two men, carved edge to edge with silver script—and beneath the script, running along the lintel like a signature, a line of notation. Notes. A melody.

Wren made a sound beside him, small and cracked. "That's the lullaby," she said. "That's my gran's lullaby. Note for note."

The map in Tamsin's pocket burned like a coal. When he drew it out, the silver ink had finished whatever it had been writing for three days: the final road lay complete now, running down and down beneath the Thornfells, to a mark shaped exactly like the door in Vasq's drawing. And around the mark, in the cartographer's own hand, added sometime in the last hours of his life, four words:

*THE DOOR REMEMBERS NAMES.*

Outside, far below them in the valley, a sound drifted up the mountainside—thin, wrong, carrying against the wind. Dogs, perhaps. Or something shaped like dogs.

Wren's head came up. "Tell me that's wolves."

Tamsin folded the journal into his coat beside the map, beside the script that was slowly learning his veins, and stood.

"No," he said. "Wolves give up. Whatever that is, it's still coming."
