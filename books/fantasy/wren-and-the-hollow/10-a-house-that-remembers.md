## 10. A House That Remembers

The inn was called the Waystone, which was a lie twice over: it had no waystone in it, and it was not an inn so much as a farmhouse that had grown ambitions. Two floors of grey stone, a slate roof sagging like a tired shoulder, a sign that swung on one chain and creaked a note just sharp enough to set Wren's teeth on edge.

They came to it at dusk, rain chasing them down the last mile of the valley road, and by then Wren would have slept in a ditch with a bear if the bear promised to keep her dry.

"Full up," said the woman behind the bar, before they'd even finished dripping on her floorboards. She was broad and flour-dusted and had the look of someone who'd said *full up* four hundred times this season and meant it every time. "Pilgrims. Some festival at Vael, or they think there's going to be one. You can have the loft over the stable if you don't snore."

"We don't," Tamsin said.

"He does," Wren said.

Tamsin gave her a look that was ninety percent exhaustion and ten percent betrayal, which she decided to treasure.

The loft was hay and dust and one small window that looked out over the back paddock, where a low stone wall ran crooked along a line no fence had any business following—too old for the field, too stubborn to move. Wren noticed it because she'd spent her whole life noticing walls. Walls told you who was afraid of whom, and when.

This wall wasn't afraid of anything. It was just *old*, older than the field, older than the inn, laid by hands that had cared about the fit of each stone the way her grandmother had cared about the fit of each word in a song.

She was still looking at it when she fell asleep.

* * *

The dream began with bread.

Not the idea of bread—the fact of it, warm and specific, rising under a cloth on a windowsill while rain came down outside in long silver threads. Wren stood—or floated, dreams being generous that way—in a kitchen with whitewashed walls and a floor of wide flagstones worn into shallow valleys by generations of feet. Copper pots hung from hooks. A cat slept in a basket by the stove with the absolute conviction of a creature that has never once been wrong about anything.

A woman was singing.

Wren knew the song. That was the first wrong thing, the crack in the dream through which all the other wrong things poured. It was the lullaby—the one her grandmother used to hum with the words half-swallowed, the one Wren had carried across three borders and six years like a coin sewn into her hem. But here it had *all* its words, and they were Aldermere's words, the old rolling syllables, and the woman singing them had flour on her hands and a smudge of it on her cheekbone and eyes the color of strong tea.

She turned toward Wren and smiled as if Wren were expected.

"You're standing in my kitchen," the woman said, not unkindly. "Mind the dough."

"I—" Wren's voice didn't work right in the dream; it came out thin, a child's voice. "This isn't real."

"No," the woman agreed pleasantly, turning back to her work. "It's remembered. There's a difference, and it matters." She punched down the dough with two efficient fists. "You have your grandmother's chin, did you know? The Hollow-keeper chin. Stubborn as a gatepost."

"You know my grandmother?"

"I know *of* her. Everyone knew the Hollow-keepers. They kept the doors we hoped we'd never need." The woman wiped her hands and looked out the window at the rain, and something in her face went quiet and far away. "Marta will be late. She always is, when it rains. She says the roads argue with her."

"Marta?"

"My daughter." The woman said it the way people say the truest things—lightly, like it cost nothing, when actually it was the whole weight of them. "And you'll want to meet the boy, but he's asleep. He sleeps like the dead, that one. I keep telling him the world won't wait for him and he doesn't believe me."

Something cold moved through Wren, slow as water finding a crack.

"The boy," she said.

But the dream was already shifting, the way dreams do—not ending but *turning*, like a page. The kitchen dimmed. Rain on the window became rain on a roof became—

*Boots on stairs.*

That was the second wrong thing, because Wren heard them before she understood them, and her body knew what boots on stairs at night meant even before her mind caught up. Soldiers' boots. Even in a dream, even in a memory that belonged to someone else, her spine recognized the rhythm.

The woman stood very still by the stove. The cat was gone from its basket. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the silence it left behind was worse than the noise.

"They're coming for the valley," the woman said, still calm, still pleasant, and now the pleasantness was the most terrible thing Wren had ever heard. "They'll fold us out, Marta says. Fold us like a letter. And the ones who can't be folded—" She stopped. Started again. Her tea-colored eyes found Wren's. "There's a boy who has to go out. Over the fold. He's only a baby. Someone has to carry him, and someone has to sing him quiet, and someone has to remember, afterward, that he existed. That's the fourth thing, remembering. Everyone forgets the fourth thing."

"Who is he?" Wren asked, and her child-voice cracked on it. "Who's the boy?"

The woman smiled again—that same expected-you smile—and reached out and touched Wren's cheek with a floury hand, and the touch was warm, impossibly warm, warmer than anything in a dream had a right to be.

"You're crying, little keeper," she said gently. "Don't cry. Houses remember. That's what we're for."

* * *

She woke gasping, wet-faced, her hand pressed flat against the hay as if the floor might open under her.

Rain still ticked against the loft roof. The little window showed the paddock wall, black against a sky going the color of ash. Somewhere below, a horse shifted and sighed.

Wren sat up and hugged her knees and let herself shake, quietly, the way you learn to do when you've slept in doorways and doorways don't care about your nightmares. Except this hadn't been a nightmare. That was the awful part. It had been *kind*. Bread rising and a cat asleep and a woman with flour on her cheek—it had been the gentlest thing she'd dreamed in years, and it had gutted her anyway, because none of it was hers and all of it was gone.

*I know the fourth thing,* she thought wildly. *Remembering. I'm the fourth thing. Grandmother, what did you leave me?*

She must have made a sound, because a few minutes later the ladder creaked and Tamsin's head appeared through the hatch, hair full of straw, eyes bleary.

"You were calling out," he said. "In your sleep. Not words. Just—" He gestured vaguely, helplessly. "Sound."

"Go away."

He didn't go away. He came up the ladder instead, moving carefully, the way he did everything, and sat down on the far end of the hay without asking permission, which was somehow exactly right. He didn't ask what she'd dreamed. He just sat there in the dark being solidly, annoyingly present, and after a while the shaking slowed, and after a longer while she heard herself talking anyway, because apparently her mouth had defected.

"There was a woman," Wren said. "Baking bread. She sang the lullaby—all of it, the real words. And soldiers came, and she talked about folding, about a country being folded like a letter, and a baby that had to be carried out over the fold." She wiped her face with her sleeve, furious at her own sleeve. "I woke up crying for people I've never met, Tamsin. What kind of thief cries over strangers?"

"They're not strangers."

"How would you know?"

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had a strange flat carefulness, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust.

"Because I dreamed the same room."

Wren turned to look at him. In the grey light from the window his face was pale and set, and he was staring at the middle distance the way he stared at maps.

"The copper pots on the left wall," he said. "The cat in the basket by the stove. The flagstones worn into dips, there—" he pointed at the loft floor, absurdly, as though the kitchen lay beneath the hay, "—in front of the table, where everyone stands. And a woman singing. I've dreamed it before, Wren. Before the map. Before the Grey Stair. I dreamed it when I was nine years old and living in the guildhall, and I woke up smelling bread, and Master Corvin told me I'd been hungry and dreaming nonsense, and I believed him, because why wouldn't I?" His jaw worked. "I've been dreaming that kitchen my whole life. I just never knew it was a memory."

The cold from the dream came back and settled in Wren's chest and stayed there.

"That woman," she said slowly. "She talked about a boy who had to be carried out. A baby. Over the fold."

"Don't."

"She had flour on her cheek, and she said her daughter was called Marta, and she said the boy slept like the dead and the world wouldn't wait for him—"

"*Don't.*" But there was no force in it. It came out cracked down the middle, and he knew it, and she watched him hear himself say it and hate it.

They sat in the hay with the rain ticking overhead and neither of them said the thing, because saying it would make it a road, and roads, once opened, had to be walked. Wren knew that better than anyone. She'd spent six years refusing to walk toward Aldermere and here she was in a barn loft above a dead woman's kitchen, holding a map-shaped hole in her chest.

Instead she did something she couldn't have explained if asked. She shifted down the slope of hay until her shoulder found his, and stayed there. He went stiff for exactly three heartbeats—she counted, because counting was what she did instead of feeling—and then he eased, and his arm came around her, awkward as a man handling something breakable, and she let her head rest against him and listened to his heartbeat, steady as a waymark.

"This doesn't mean anything," she said.

"Obviously not."

"It's practical. Body heat. Hay is cold."

"Crippingly cold."

"And you snore."

"So you've mentioned."

She felt him smile more than saw it, a small movement against her hair, and she closed her eyes and hated how safe it felt, how much like the kitchen in the dream, warm and lit and doomed. People you loved got folded out of the world. That was the lesson of her entire life. Grandmother, gone. Mother, never known. A country, erased so thoroughly that its name made lamps gutter. And here she was leaning against a boy who dreamed another woman's kitchen, letting herself imagine a future, which was the single stupidest thing a refugee could do.

But she didn't move.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the innkeeper was banking the fire, and the coals settled with a sound like a long exhale, and Wren thought: *houses remember.* The woman had said it like a comfort. Standing in her own ghost-kitchen, held in the arms of a boy who might be the baby from the song, Wren decided to take it as one.

"Tamsin," she murmured, half-asleep already.

"Hm?"

"The wall in the paddock. The old one. It's Aldermere work—I've seen the pattern in grandmother's drawings. This inn sits on a farm. Their farm, maybe. Hers." She felt him go still. "We're sleeping inside the fold-line. Whatever's underneath this place, whatever's left of it—it's close enough to touch."

His arm tightened around her, just slightly.

"Then tomorrow," he said quietly, "we start digging."

Out in the paddock, past the crooked old wall, the rain stopped altogether, and in the sudden silence Wren could have sworn—half-dreaming, wholly unwilling to swear to it—that somewhere beneath the stable floor, faint as breath, someone was humming.
