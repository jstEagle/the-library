## 11. The Traitor's Road

The road to Hollowmere was a road Tamsin had walked eleven times in his life and hated a little more each time.

It wasn't the grade, though the grade was cruel enough—a switchbacking scar cut into the western face of the Thornfells where the mountain shed rock every spring like a dog shedding winter coat. It wasn't the drop, though the drop was four hundred feet of grey air ending in a river that ate everything it caught. It was what waited at the top.

Hollowmere was a mining town that had stopped mining twenty years ago and never quite admitted it. The seams were played out, the company gone, half the houses standing empty with their shutters banging in the wind like a town clearing its throat to say something it never said. What remained were the people too stubborn or too poor to leave, and one of them was Brannic Vael.

Tamsin knew the name the way you know a scar. Brannic had been a Wayfarer once—second chair under Guildmaster Oren, back when the guild still numbered thirty and the guildhall's roof didn't sing in the rain. Tamsin had been nine years old when Brannic left. He remembered the leaving: the man walking out through the courtyard at dawn with his pack on his shoulder and his head down, and old Oren standing on the steps saying nothing, which from Oren had been louder than any curse. Tamsin hadn't understood then. He understood now. There were only two ways to leave the Wayfarers' Guild, and neither of them was walking out the front door with your pack.

Brannic had sold them. Routes, passes, the smugglers' roads the Crown had spent two centuries trying to strangle—the whole living memory of the guild, written down in a fair hand and handed to an Inquisitor's clerk for a purse of silver and a pardon with his name on it. Three wayfarers had died the following winter because the roads they trusted were no longer secret. One of them had been Maren Kavanagh, Tamsin's mother.

He had not thought about that in years. He thought about it now, climbing the switchbacks with Wren a half-step behind him and the map a warm weight against his ribs, because the map wanted Brannic, and Tamsin did not want to want anything the map wanted.

* * *

It had started three nights before, in the shelter of a shepherd's bothy above the Greywater ford, with the rain coming down hard enough to make conversation out of the roof.

Tamsin had woken with the map already unrolled across his chest, its ink moving. He'd learned not to fight it. The ink moved when it had something to say, the way a sleeping man turns toward a window when the light changes. This time the silver lines were drawing themselves westward, up out of the valley they'd meant to follow, over a ridge he'd crossed twice in his life and avoided ever since, and terminating—not at a pass, not at a shrine, not at any of the places the map had led them so far—but at a single small mark shaped like a pick-axe crossed with a walking-staff.

"That's a guild sign," Tamsin said aloud, into the dark, before he'd fully decided to say it.

Wren sat up. She slept badly these days, in short armed intervals, and she came awake the way she did everything—fast, sharp-eyed, one hand already finding her knife. "Whose?"

"Nobody's." He turned the map so the firelight caught it. "That's the mark we put on maps for a retired wayfarer. Someone who knows the roads but doesn't walk them anymore. We leave the sign so if they're needed—" He stopped. His own voice sounded strange to him, reciting the customs of a guild that now fit inside a leaking building with four chairs.

"If they're needed," Wren finished, dry as flint. "And the map thinks this one's needed."

"The map doesn't think."

"No," she agreed. "It wants. Which is worse." She looked at him for a long moment, firelight in her eyes, and then she asked the question he'd been waiting for her to ask since the ink first drew the mark. "Who is it?"

He told her. All of it—the name, the selling, the three dead wayfarers, and last, because it was hardest to say and she would see through him if he buried it, his mother. Wren listened the way she always listened, absolutely still, her chin on her knees, and when he finished she didn't offer comfort, because comfort between the two of them had long ago settled into something quieter and more useful: attention.

"You don't have to go," she said.

"He'll know things," Tamsin said. "About the fold. About how the Crown watches the high country. Halvane bought his knowledge once; he can tell us what the Inquisition paid for." He fed a stick to the fire. "And the map's already decided. I'm just the legs."

"You're more than the legs." She said it flatly, without softness, the way you state a fact of terrain. "But fine. We go see your traitor."

They went.

* * *

Hollowmere announced itself by smell before sight—coal smoke and wet slag and the particular sourness of a town whose well had gone brackish—and then by sound, the endless tick and groan of empty houses settling. They came in on the miners' path at dusk, past the headframe that stood over the closed shaft like a gallows with nothing left to hang, and found the one street lit end to end by exactly two windows.

Brannic lived above the cooperage, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. Tamsin knocked the wayfarer's knock—three, pause, two—and heard, from inside, a silence so complete and so sudden that he knew the man had recognized it. Some knocks you don't hear in twenty years and forget anyway.

The door opened on a chain, six inches of lamplit dark, one eye.

"Guildmaster sent you?" the eye said. And there was something in the voice beneath the wariness that Tamsin took a moment to name and then couldn't stop hearing: hope. Ragged, ashamed, twenty-years-out-of-date hope, the hope of a man who has checked his door chain at every knock for two decades because someday the guild might knock.

"Oren's dead," Tamsin said. "Three years now. I hold the chair."

The chain came off faster than it had gone on.

Brannic Vael was sixty if he was a day, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had carried packs up mountains until the carrying became their skeleton, gone soft in the middle, white-bearded, with hands that shook slightly until he noticed them shaking and made them stop. He looked at Tamsin's face for a long time.

"You're Kavanagh's boy," he said. Not Maren's boy. Kavanagh's. As if he'd chosen the safer of the two names and knew it.

"I'm Maren's son," Tamsin said, and watched the words land like stones dropped down a shaft, counting the seconds for the strike.

Brannic closed his eyes. When he opened them he stepped aside and said, "You'd better come in," and his voice had gone quiet in the particular way of a man who has rehearsed an apology for twenty years and knows the rehearsal will be worthless.

* * *

The room was small and obsessively ordered. Maps hung on every wall—but not guild maps. Old survey charts, county boundaries, a hand-drawn plan of Hollowmere itself with every roof marked occupied or empty. A man who had sold roads had apparently spent the rest of his life mapping things that belonged to no one, that could hurt no one, that no Inquisitor would cross the street to buy. Tamsin looked at those walls and understood something about the shape of a ruined life.

Wren took the wall position by the door without being asked, arms folded, knife where knives go, and Brannic glanced at her and then away with the expression of a man who recognizes a habit when he sees one.

"You travel careful," he said.

"We're traveled after," Tamsin said. "By the Inquisition. By Halvane himself, and by things he makes that used to be men." He laid the map on the table between them, folded, his hand flat on top of it. "I need to know what you sold him. All of it. Every route, every cache, every name."

Brannic looked at the map the way starving men look through windows at bread. Then he looked at Tamsin's hand on it, and whatever he saw in the boy's face made him sit down slowly on his stool, like a man accepting a sentence.

"You know what I did," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I know three wayfarers died on roads you made public. I know one of them taught me to read a ridgeline." Tamsin kept his voice level, and it cost him, and he let Brannic see that it cost him, because pity would have been a lie and rage alone would have ended the conversation. "So before you say anything else, understand that I am not here to forgive you. I'm here because the map brought me, and because you may be the only man alive who knows what the Crown paid for besides the Crown."

For a moment Brannic said nothing at all. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the wind worried the cooperage signs.

Then the old man began to talk, and he talked the way men talk when they have finally been given permission—low, fast, without ornament, twenty years of held breath coming out in order.

He told them about the winter the guild nearly starved, when the caravan trade shifted south and the fees dried up and Oren started selling furniture to buy meal. He told them about the Inquisitor's clerk who came to him not with threats but with sympathy, which was worse, who sat with him over ale for six nights and listened to him complain about the guild's decline until complaining felt like friendship. He told them about the night he copied the routes—that was the part he told slowest, his hands flat on his knees, pressing down as if to keep himself in the chair—the fair hand, the good ink, the sense of order and rightness that came over him as he wrote, as if he weren't betraying the roads but rescuing them, preserving them somewhere they wouldn't die with a dying guild.

"They gave me a pardon with my name spelled right," he said. "Do you know what that's worth to a man who grew up with no surname at all? They wrote *Brannic Vael* on a crown document and sealed it, and I stood there holding it and thought, *now I exist.*" He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "Twenty years to learn what I existed as. A tool. They used my roads to hang three of ours and close the northern passes to anyone but their own couriers, and then they never needed me again, and I came here, and I've been mapping empty towns ever since because it's the only work I can do that can't be sold."

"And the fold," Tamsin said. "Did they pay for knowledge of the fold?"

That changed the old man's face entirely. The shame stayed, but something else came up underneath it—fear, old and deep, the fear of a man who has seen the cellar of a house everyone else admires.

"Why do you think I ran north instead of staying in the capital where they offered me a post?" Brannic said. "Because of what I copied second. There was a second document, sealed, that the clerk didn't know I'd seen—I was in the archive room copying routes and there was a dispatch case on the desk, unlocked, and I am a wayfarer, boy; looking at what isn't yours is the first thing we're trained out of and the last thing we lose." He leaned forward. "It was a survey order. Dated a hundred and ninety years back, but the seal was current—it had been reauthorized, renewed, generation after generation, like a lease. *Survey and maintain the perimeter of the Aldermere Fold. Report all approaches. Let no unsworn party within three leagues of the seam.*" His voice dropped. "There was a list attached. Names of the watchers. It went back two centuries. And the last name on the current page—" he tapped his own chest twice, "—was mine. They'd written me in. Before I ever took their silver. I wasn't hired to betray the guild, Kavanagh's boy. I was recruited. The whole thing—the sympathy, the ale, the pardon—they'd had my name on a list since before I walked out of the guildhall. I just did what the document already said I'd done."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly. Beside the door, Wren had gone utterly still, and Tamsin knew that stillness; it was the stillness of a girl hearing her grandmother's lullaby sung back to her in a stranger's voice.

"They watch the seam," Tamsin said. "The edge of the fold. Where Aldermere was—"

"Where Aldermere *is,*" Brannic said. "Don't make my mistake and use the past tense. That's what the survey order says, in those exact words, in the preamble: *the territory remains.* Not *remained*. Remains. Present tense, two hundred years running." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I've had twenty years to decide what that means and I still can't say it out loud without my skin crawling. But I'll tell you what I've concluded, sitting up here mapping empty houses. Whatever the Crown folded out of the world, they never believed they'd destroyed it. You don't post guards for two centuries around a grave. You post them around a door."

* * *

Later—much later, the lamp burned low, Wren asleep in the corner with her boots off for the first time in six days, which Tamsin chose to read as trust rather than exhaustion—Brannic stood at his wall of harmless maps and asked the question Tamsin had been dreading.

"What's the map want with me?"

Tamsin could have lied. He considered it. But lying to this man, tonight, of all nights, felt like handing a knife handle-first to someone who might yet be family, and family deserved better than management.

"It marked your sign," he said. "Retired-wayfarer sign. The kind we set on maps so a man can be found if he's needed." He hesitated. "It's leading us to the Hollow, Brannic. To the seam itself. And it seems to think we'll need you on the road there."

The old man was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "You know what I am. You know what I cost the guild. Your mother was on one of my roads." He said it plainly, without flinching, and Tamsin realized that Brannic had flinched at it internally every day for twenty years and had finally worn the flinch smooth, the way water wears stone—not gone, just finished.

"I know," Tamsin said.

"So either you're a fool, or you're the kind of desperate that looks like a fool."

"Both, probably." Tamsin looked at his hands. They were his mother's hands; he'd noticed it more since the stranger's memories had begun surfacing, as if grief had sharpened his eyes. "Here's the truth of it. The guild is four people and a leaking roof. I watched the Guildmaster sell the hall out from under us because the world stopped needing the roads. And now the world needs them again—needs them more than it ever has, needs them for the one journey that matters—and there aren't enough of us left to walk it." He looked up. "I swore an oath at fifteen. *No road forgotten, no walker abandoned.* I've been telling myself that oath applies to travelers. Lately I think it applies to wayfarers too. Even the ones who broke everything else."

Brannic's jaw worked. "Even me."

"Especially you. Because you're the only one who knows what the enemy knows." Tamsin paused, and then gave him the harder thing, the thing that wasn't strategy. "And because my mother would have hated leaving anyone behind on a mountain. Even you. *Especially* you, probably. She was like that. It was infuriating."

The old man turned away quickly, and his shoulders shook once, and he pretended it was the lamp he was trimming.

When he turned back his eyes were wet and steady. "I won't ask you to forgive me. I'll ask worse. I'll ask you to use me. Put me between you and whatever Halvane sends, spend me however the road requires, and if I die out there, let it be on a guild road, doing guild work, with the guild's mark on me instead of the Crown's." He drew a breath. "That's all absolution I've got any right to."

Tamsin thought about it honestly, because the man deserved honesty and not comfort. He thought about his mother on a cold road because this man's ink had made her hiding place visible. He thought about the stranger dying on the Grey Stair with silver script pressed through his flesh, spending himself the same way Brannic was asking to be spent. He thought about Wren, who had picked his pocket the day they met and whom he would now trust with his life, and about the fact that every family he'd ever heard of was built out of people who had failed somebody first.

"Take the east wall," he said at last, meaning the watch rotation, meaning the road, meaning everything. "Second watch is mine. We move at first light."

Brannic nodded. Then, as Tamsin rolled the map and turned for his bedroll, the old man spoke again, quieter, in the voice of a wayfarer reporting terrain rather than a traitor asking favors.

"There's one more thing. If the map's taking you to the seam—there's a stretch above the fold-line where the survey posts run thick. Double-watch towers every league, and between them, hounds." He met Tamsin's eyes. "Not dogs, boy. *Hounds.* The remade kind. I saw two of them being walked on chains the day I took their silver, and they don't sleep, and they don't eat, and they don't leave scent, and the script on their necks was the same silver I've seen glowing on that map of yours since you walked in my door."

Outside, far off in the dark below the town, something began to bay—a sound with no animal in it at all, long and patient and wrong, like a bell learning to howl.

Brannic closed his eyes. "They've found us," he said. "We should have moved tonight."
