## 12. What the Stranger Left

The wound woke him before dawn.

Not the pain—pain he knew how to sleep through, had learned it young on the high passes where a man who cried out froze alone. This was different. The gash along his ribs, stitched crooked by Wren's shaking hands two nights past, had begun to *hum*.

Tamsin lay still in the hayloft above the waystation, listening to Brannic's slow breathing below and the wind working at the loose shingles, and pressed two fingers to the bandage. Under the linen, under the ache, something was moving. Not pus. Not heat. A fine, crawling brightness, like frost forming on a window in reverse—as if the cold were inside the glass and trying to get out.

He unwound the bandage by feel.

In the grey pre-dawn light that leaked through the slats, the wound looked wrong. The edges had closed—not healed, exactly, but *sealed*, drawn together with a seam so fine it might have been done by a surgeon's needle. And along the seam, in a script too small for any human hand, ran a line of silver.

The same script that had been pressed into the dead stranger's flesh like watermarking on paper. The same script that crawled across the map whenever Wren wasn't looking, rearranging itself, teaching itself new roads.

"No," Tamsin said aloud, very quietly, because saying things aloud made them either true or absurd, and he needed one of those.

The script did not answer. It simply continued, patient as water finding its level, threading outward from the wound into the skin of his side like ivy claiming a wall.

* * *

He did not wake Wren. He told himself it was mercy—he'd carried enough burdens alone in his life that sharing them felt like a skill gone rusty—but the truth was uglier and simpler: if he said it out loud to her, with her dark eyes sharp and certain, then it would be real, and there would be no road back to the man he had been a month ago. A month ago he had been Tamsin Kavanagh, last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild, and his blood had been only blood, and the worst thing inside him had been doubt about whether the guildhall's roof would survive another winter.

Now there were three of them crammed into a smugglers' waystation that smelled of tallow and old rain, an ex-traitor snoring below, a stolen country folded up in his coat pocket, and silver writing growing through him like frost through a doorframe.

He sat by the loft's small window and watched the light come up over the Thornfells and waited to see what the script wanted.

Around midmorning, it showed him a kitchen.

* * *

It came the way memory comes—not summoned, not asked for, just *arriving*, whole and warm and unbearably specific, as though someone had opened a door in the back of his skull that had always been there, painted over, and now stood open with morning light pouring through.

A kitchen. Low-beamed, blue-washed walls gone pale at the corners where the plaster sweated in winter. A range of black iron with a kettle just off the boil. Bread dough rising under a cloth on the sill, breathing almost visibly in the warmth. There was rosemary in something—oil, maybe, or a bunch hanging upside down from a nail—and beneath it all the smell every house has and no house can be described by, the smell of being lived in by people who love each other badly and well.

And a woman singing.

He couldn't see her face. He could see her hands—flour to the wrists, strong hands, a scar across the left knuckle from a dropped knife or a careless lid—and he could hear her voice, low and unhurried, running through a melody he knew. Knew *intimately*. Knew the way you know your own heartbeat: Wren's lullaby, the Bellharbour song, the key the old woman had wept over. But sung differently, sung the way it must have been sung before it crossed the fold and lost half its notes, with a verse in the middle that Wren's grandmother had never taught her, a verse that turned the melody around on itself like a road doubling back through a valley—

*Tamsin.* The name arrived inside the memory, spoken by the singing voice, soft, absent, the way a mother says a child's name while her mind is on the bread. *Tamsin, love, come away from the door.*

And he was—this was the part that undid him—there was a *body* in the memory, small and low to the ground, hands on cool flagstones, looking out through a gap under the kitchen door at boots passing. Soldiers' boots. Many. The rhythm of them wrong for marching, hurried, scattered, the rhythm of a town coming apart.

The memory folded shut.

Tamsin found himself on his knees on the loft boards, one hand braced against the wall, breath sawing. The silver script along his ribs had spread another finger-width in the time it took to live thirty seconds of someone else's—or someone's own—past.

Below, a floorboard creaked. "Kavanagh?" Brannic's voice, rough with sleep. "You all right up there?"

"Fine," Tamsin said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him; the guild trained your voice last of all, after your feet and your hands, because on a mountain a steady voice is the difference between a party that turns back and a party that walks off a cornice. "Bad dream."

Brannic grunted and let it go. Traitors, Tamsin thought, learn fast which questions not to press.

* * *

Wren came back from the village near noon with bread, a flask of oil, and news shaped like a stone she set down gently between them. "Two of Halvane's riders came through Marrowgate asking after a man with a bound rib and a girl with a southern accent. The ferryman took their coin and lied for us." She shrugged, as though lying ferrymen grew on hedges. "They're casting wider. We've got a day, maybe two."

Then she saw his face, and whatever she read there made her put down the bread.

"What happened?"

He should have told her everything at once. Instead the second memory chose that moment—the moment she knelt close to him, frowning, her braid fraying out of its tie—to arrive.

* * *

This one was cold.

Night, or what passed for night in that country, and a line of people moving through fog along a road that wasn't a road—a track worn through standing grass by feet alone, unmarked on any map because maps were what got people killed. The body in the memory was bigger now, older, legs long enough to hurt with walking. Ahead, the fog glowed faintly silver, the way fog does when there is something behind it brighter than the moon.

The fold.

He understood it without being told, the way you understand a threshold in a dream: here was the edge of Aldermere, and beyond the glow the world went on without it, fields and towns and roads that had never heard the name, and the treaty-ink that held the fold shimmered like heat-haze over a forge.

There were soldiers behind them. There was always the memory of soldiers behind them, receding like a tide. And there was the mother—her hand gripping his now, the scarred knuckle, flour ground permanently into the creases—and she was not singing. She was whispering the lullaby instead, the reversed verse, the turning verse, over and over, faster, like a woman stitching a hem against a deadline.

An old man stood at the fold. He had a keeper's grey robe and a face like weathered wood, and in his arms he held—

He held a bundle.

Small. Blanketed. Making the furious, silent, red-faced effort of an infant who has decided crying is beneath him.

"Tamsin." His mother's voice, right against his ear now, and wet. "Listen to me. Listen. The keepers can take one across. One. The script takes a tithe of everyone who passes grown, and mine's already spent, do you understand me? Do you—" Her breath broke. She knelt. She took his face in both flour-white hands. "You are going to remember the kitchen. You're going to remember the bread and the rosemary and my voice, even when you don't know why, even when they tell you you're from somewhere else. Promise me. Promise me you'll remember."

He promised. Of course he promised. He was six years old, and mothers are the first gods children ever believe in.

She kissed his forehead. Then she took the bundle from the keeper's arms, held it once—just once, cheek to blanket, a breath's worth of holding—and handed it back. And then she took *him* by the shoulders, turned him toward the silver light, and pushed, gently, the way you push a boat off from shore.

"Go," she said. "Walk until the singing stops. Don't look back at the fold. If you look back, you'll see me watching, and I can't bear it, so walk, my heart, walk—"

The memory folded shut.

* * *

"—samin. *Tamsin!*"

Wren had him by the collar, shaking him, and her face was white and furious and frightened, all three at war. "You went somewhere. Your eyes went somewhere. What is happening to you?"

He was crying. He noted this distantly, the way you note weather: rain beginning, expect delays. He hadn't cried since the Guildmaster's funeral eleven years ago, and he hadn't much cried then.

"It's not his memories," he said. His voice cracked in the middle and he hated it. "The stranger's. On the Grey Stair. Everyone assumed—I assumed—the script carried *his* life into me. His mother's kitchen, his crossing, his—"

"Tamsin." Very quietly. Wren had a gift for quiet that could stop a room. "Say the rest."

"He called me Tamsin. In the kitchen. Before I had a name anyone remembers giving me, she—" He stopped. Started again. The words came out flat and strange, like goods listed on a manifest. "There's a verse of your lullaby nobody sings anymore. The turning verse. I know it. I've always known it, I thought I learned it off a carter when I was eight, I *built a false memory around it*, Wren, because the truth didn't fit anywhere—"

"Sing it."

"I can't—"

"*Sing it.*"

So he sang. Badly, brokenly, kneeling in the dirty straw-light of a smugglers' loft with silver script crawling up his ribs, he sang the verse of a country that no longer existed, and Wren's mouth fell slowly open, and halfway through she joined him, her grandmother's notes braiding against his mother's, and the two versions fit together like a lock accepting its key, and the script along his ribs flared silver-bright through his shirt, visible even in daylight, glowing like a seam of ore in a mountainside.

When it faded, neither of them spoke for a long time.

"You're Aldermere," Wren said finally. Not a question. An act of naming, like christening a ship.

"The stranger wasn't carrying his own memories to me." Tamsin heard himself say it as though from a distance. "He was carrying *mine*. He was the keeper's runner, or—he was the man on the other side of the fold, waiting to receive me, twenty-two years too late, and he spent his whole life trying to come back for the child he'd watched cross, and when he finally found me, dying on the Stair, he gave me back—" His breath hitched. "He gave me back myself."

Outside, far off, something began to bay. That sound with no animal in it. Closer than it had been in Marrowgate.

Brannic appeared at the top of the loft ladder, already dressed, already armed, his face grim as a gravestone. "Whatever you two just did," he said, "the hounds heard it. We move now, and we move north. Vael's shrine is four days' hard walking, and the keepers there are the only ones alive who can tell you what that script is doing to your veins—"

"I know what it's doing," Tamsin said. He stood. The script pulsed once beneath his shirt, patient, patient, like a second heartbeat learning the rhythm of the first. "It's finishing the crossing my mother started."

He picked up the map from where it lay, and it opened in his hands without being asked, ink shifting, roads rearranging themselves, drawing him a line north through the Thornfells straight as a struck bell.

Four days. He had spent his whole life not knowing who he was. Now he had four days to find out what that meant—before the hounds, or the script, decided for him.

"Wren," he said. "Teach me the rest of the song. All of it. Every verse your grandmother kept."

Her jaw set. "Why?"

"Because," Tamsin said, "I think my mother wrote me into it. And I think it's the only thing I'll be carrying through that door."
