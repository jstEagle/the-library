## 13. The Hollow-Keepers

The shrine of Vael did not want to be found.

We had been climbing for two days by then, following a road that existed only in silver ink and Tamsin's increasingly strange certainties. He would stop at a wall of scree that looked like every other wall of scree and say, "Through there," and through there it would be—a goat track, a ledge, a stair cut into the mountain so old the weather had tried to eat it and mostly succeeded. He walked it like a man remembering rather than a man reading, and I told myself that was just what Wayfarers did.

It wasn't. I knew it wasn't, because of the way he sometimes touched his own wrist where the script showed through the skin, like a man checking a wound he couldn't see.

"Tell me again why we're trusting a map," I said, mostly to hear my own voice over the wind. The wind on Vael's shoulder came off the icefields and had teeth in it. "A map that has already gotten one man killed and is currently eating another."

Tamsin didn't look back. He'd stopped doing that somewhere on the second day, and I hadn't decided yet whether it meant he trusted me to follow or he'd forgotten I was there. Both possibilities sat badly with me.

"The map isn't eating me," he said.

"You're writing on yourself now. That's not a good sign, Tamsin. In my experience when ink starts moving from paper into people, someone's about to have a very bad week."

That got half a smile out of him, which was all he had to give lately. "Keepers live up here. The map says so. If anyone knows what this script is—what it's doing to me—it'll be them."

"And if there are no keepers? If it's three hundred years of snow and a locked door?"

He considered that honestly, the way he considered everything, like a man weighing grain. "Then we've climbed two days for a view, and you can tell me every way in which I'm a fool."

"I don't need a view for that."

* * *

We found the door at dusk, and it nearly killed us finding it, which I think was the point.

The last stretch of the road went into the mountain itself—a cleft barely wide enough for a body, running with meltwater, pitch dark except for the faint grey light that came from nowhere and everywhere. Tamsin went first because he always went first now, and I hated him a little for it. My grandmother used to say the ones who walk ahead are either protecting you or leading you somewhere you wouldn't go yourself. I hadn't believed her. Grandmothers say things like that because they're old and frightened.

Then the cleft opened without warning into air, and I understood why the door hid itself.

The shrine hung in a bowl of stone high on Vael's face, half ruin and half something older than ruin. A courtyard of black rock, ringed by columns worn to stumps. Beyond them, built into the cliff, a low hall with a roof of overlapping slate shingles gone green as pond water, and windows that glowed—not lamplight, but a slow amber pulse, like the mountain breathing behind its ribs. Snow lay everywhere except in one narrow path, swept clean as a hearthstone, leading to the hall door.

Three figures stood in the doorway.

I had imagined keepers as soldiers, or priests, or maybe both—stern old men with staffs and secrets. What stood there were three small ancient women in robes the colour of undyed wool, leaning on each other and on sticks, squinting down the path at us with the expressions of grandmothers interrupted at supper.

"Well," said the tallest, in a voice like dry leaves. "It took the long way round."

"It always does," said the second.

"He brought a girl," said the third, and her milky eyes found me across forty feet of snow and held me like a hook holds a fish. "Or she brought him. With those two, who could say."

My hand went to the knife at my hip before I could stop it, and the first one laughed—a real laugh, cracked but warm.

"Put that away, child. If we wanted you dead, you'd never have made the cleft. Sit down. You're letting the cold in, and I've been cold for two hundred years already; I refuse to be colder."

* * *

The inside of the hall smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and something else underneath—stone, deep stone, the smell of a place that had been closed for a long time. A fire burned in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. The walls were covered in cloth hangings, and it took me a moment to understand what they were: maps. Dozens of them, hundreds, stitched and painted and embroidered, roads layered over roads going back further than any guild chart I'd ever seen. And along their borders, in thread of tarnished silver, ran the script. The same script that lived under Tamsin's skin. The same script that had been pressed into a dying stranger's flesh on the Grey Stair.

Tamsin saw it too. I watched him go still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Sit," repeated the tall one. She settled onto a bench by the fire with the care of a woman whose bones were older than some countries. "I am Maren. This is Osla and this is Betha. We were Hollow-keepers when there was a Hollow to keep, and we are what remains now, which is to say: custodians of a door no one remembers, for a country no one admits existed." She said it lightly, but her hands, folded in her lap, were white-knuckled. "You carry Aldermere's roads in your blood, boy. Let us look."

He hesitated. I watched him hesitate—the honest man's whole war happening in his face—and then he rolled up his sleeve.

The script had spread since Bellharbour. It used to be only at the wrist, a cuff of silver letters pressed into the skin like a brand. Now it climbed his forearm in fine branching lines, root-systems and river-deltas of it, and where it reached newest, the skin was pink and tender as a healing burn. Osla took his arm in both her small hands and turned it to the firelight, and made a sound low in her throat that I did not like at all.

"This is smuggler's script," she said. "Passage-script. Written on the body instead of paper, because paper burns." She looked up at Tamsin, and her eyes—old, brown, unflinching—were suddenly terrible. "Who carried you?"

"What?"

"Who carried you out?" Her grip tightened. "This script is written around a carrying. It binds a child to the one who bears them across the fold. It doesn't transfer to strangers. It doesn't wake in blood unless the blood—" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it, watched the thought land on her face like weather. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor lamb."

"Tell me," Tamsin said. His voice stayed level. That was the thing about Tamsin; he kept everything level, a flooded cellar holding its doors shut. "Tell me what you're saying."

Maren leaned forward. "Child, how old are you?"

"Twenty-three."

"And where were you born?"

"Hollowmere. On the edge of the Thornfells. My mother was a laundress, my father drove carts, I have a sister in Lowmark—"

"Were," said Betha, from the corner, gently. "Try were."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us the mountain groaned, settling its weight, indifferent.

"Aldermere was folded out of the world two hundred years ago," Maren said, in the tone of a teacher beginning a lesson she has taught herself a thousand times in an empty room. "Not conquered. Not destroyed. Folded—tucked out of the world like a letter into an envelope, by treaty, by treachery, by the founding crime your Crown has spent two centuries burying under roads and taxes and forgetting. But a fold needs edges. And edges need tending. There were those of us who tended them. And there were those who refused to let the country die quietly, who spent those last years doing the only thing left worth doing."

"Getting children out," I said. My voice came out strange.

"Getting children out," Maren agreed. "Past the closing. Through the fold while it still had a seam loose enough to slip a body through. Each child written with passage-script, bound to a bearer, carried into the world beyond and given to families who would raise them knowing nothing." She looked at Tamsin's arm. "Most of the script faded within a generation. It was meant to fade. Yours didn't. Yours waited."

"Waited for what?" I asked, though I already knew. I had known since the Grey Stair. I think he had too. Some knowledge you carry for weeks before you let yourself set it down.

"For the bearer to come back," Betha said.

* * *

They told it in pieces, the way old women tell everything, passing the story between them like a dish too hot for one pair of hands.

There had been eleven children smuggled out in the final days. Eleven, out of a country of thousands. The bearers were sworn couriers of the keepers' order—men and women who volunteered to carry a child through the fold and vanish into the wide world, sworn never to return, because the fold was sealing and the seal would take anything that tried to reopen it. Sworn, and most of them obedient.

One was not.

"He had carried out a babe at the very end," Osla said. "An infant, days old, from a farmstead below the eastern fells. Mother dead in the folding's first convulsions—when a country is tucked out of the world, child, things tear. Father unknown; the mother never said, or the record burned. The courier placed the babe with a carting family in Hollowmere and walked away as sworn. And then he spent twenty-three years walking back."

"Why?" Tamsin asked. His voice had gone thin.

"Because he dreamed of the mother's kitchen every night for twenty years," Betha said simply. "Because he had promised her—she was dying and she gripped his sleeve and asked him to come back for the boy when he was grown, to tell him what he was, to give him the choice no one gave the rest of us. Because a promise made to a dying woman in a folding country is not a promise you set down." She looked at the fire. "And because the fold, once sealed, opens only from one side, for one purpose, once. It would kill him to come back through. He knew that. He came anyway."

"The Grey Stair," I said.

"The Grey Stair." Maren nodded slowly. "He crossed the fold and it began unmaking him even as he walked—hence a wound that bleeds wrong, hence the script surfacing on his skin as his body came apart at the seams. He was looking for a grown man with unfaded passage-script. He found you. And he put into your hands the one thing that could lead you home before his time ran out." Her eyes lifted to Tamsin's face. "His name was Corvan. He was my grandson's son. And he loved you, boy, though he met you for exactly one hour of dying. That's what passage-script is, in the end. It's love, written down in a form that doesn't fade."

Tamsin stood up.

He did it slowly, like a man rising from a sickbed, and walked to the nearest hanging map and put his hand flat against it, and stood there with his back to us. The firelight caught the silver lines climbing his arm. Nobody spoke. Even the wind outside seemed to hold its breath against the shutters.

"My whole life," he said finally, to the map, to the wall, to none of us. "My mother's bread. My father teaching me the cart-tracks. My sister. The guild. Master Aldous taking me in. Every road I ever learned." His hand curled against the cloth. "Every single day of it was built on top of a hole. I wasn't living a life. I was living an exit wound."

"Tamsin." I got up and went to him. I don't know what I meant to do when I got there—I am not a comforting person; my talents run to lifting purses and cutting throats of argument—but my hand ended up between his shoulder blades anyway, flat against his spine, feeling the tremor he was keeping out of his voice. "Listen to me. The life was real. Whatever they tell you, whatever was under it—the bread was real. You ate it. The roads were real. You walked them. They don't get taken away from you because of where you started."

"They weren't mine."

"They're yours now. That's how it works. Nobody's life belongs to them at the start. You earn it forward, not backward."

He turned his head, just enough that I could see his profile, jaw tight, eyes wet and furious. "You don't understand—"

"No," I agreed. "I don't. I've got a grandmother's lullaby and a stolen name and a country I can't visit, and I understand exactly nothing about belonging anywhere. So believe the person who knows least. It's as good a reason as any."

Something broke in his shoulders—not collapse, more the easing of a rope drawn too tight too long. He breathed. Once, twice. Then he turned back to the three old women, and the levelness was back in his voice, but it was a different levelness now, bedrock instead of floodwall.

"The stranger. Corvan. He said, 'Take it to the Hollow.' Take what to the Hollow? Me? The map?"

"Yes," said all three women together, which was somehow worse than any single answer.

Maren rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and beckoned us deeper into the hall, past the maps, to a curtain of grey felt. Behind it a narrow stair spiralled down into the mountain's heart, and from far below came a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it with my ears—a low, vast hush, like the sea heard through a mile of stone.

"The Hollow is real," she said. "It lies beneath us, beneath the Thornfells, at the fold's deepest point. It is the door Aldermere was folded through, and it has waited two hundred years to be either opened or closed forever. And you, Tamsin Kavanagh—last apprentice of the Wayfarers', last child of Aldermere, last living soul marked with the passage-script—are the key to both."

She held the curtain aside.

"There is a price," she said. "Come and see what it is."
