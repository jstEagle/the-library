## 14. The Price of a Door

### Tamsin

The keepers of Vael did not sleep, and so neither did the mountain.

Tamsin lay on a stone pallet in a cell no wider than his shoulders and listened to the shrine breathe — a slow tide of air moving through channels cut into the rock by hands two centuries dead. The sound was almost like snoring. Almost like home. He had not slept in three days, and his body wanted it desperately, but every time he closed his eyes the silver script behind his skin lit up like a road at dusk, and he saw the dead man's mother's kitchen again, and heard an infant cry in the dark of a border crossing that had never existed.

He gave up on sleep near what he guessed was dawn and climbed the stair to the Hall of Names.

The keepers were already there. They were always already there. Three of them remained of what Wren said had once been hundreds: Mother Sable, ancient and dry as a winter reed; a broad-bellied man called Orren who spoke mostly to himself; and a third who had no name anyone used anymore, only a function — *the Listener* — because she had spent ninety years listening to the folded country under the world and could repeat back anything it said.

They sat around a basin of black water carved with the same silver script that lived in Tamsin's veins, and when he entered, all three turned their heads at once, the way birds turn.

"You didn't sleep," Mother Sable said.

"I dreamed enough."

"Then you know why we've called you." She gestured to the basin. "Sit. Both of you."

Wren came down the stair behind him, hair unbound and eyes red-rimmed from her own dreams of other people's houses. She sat close enough that her knee touched his, and did not move away, and Tamsin found he was grateful for that small weight more than he could say.

"The stranger on the Grey Stair," Mother Sable began, "was called Edran Vael. Born in this shrine. He carried Aldermere's last child over the fold when the door was closing, and he spent forty years finding where that child had been put down in the world." She looked at Tamsin without pity, which he appreciated. Pity would have broken him. "You are that child. You know this now. What you do not yet know is what it costs to be the last."

"Tell us," Wren said quietly.

Orren stirred. He had been tracing the script around the basin's rim with one blunt finger, and now he lifted his hand and let the water show them.

It showed them a door.

Not the grey door beneath the Thornfells — or rather, yes, that door, but seen from the inside, standing open, light pouring through it like honey through a crack. And beyond the light, moving slowly, shapes. Fields. Roofs. A church spire. People walking a market street in a town Tamsin recognized because he'd slept in its inn last autumn: Marrowgate, or the place where Marrowgate stood, except there was no Marrowgate, there couldn't be, because the ground beneath it belonged to a country that had been folded out of the world—

"What am I seeing?" he asked, though he feared he knew.

"The price," said the Listener, and her voice was soft as ash. "Open the Hollow, and Aldermere unfolds. Every field returns. Every house. Every grave. And everything built upon the absence—" her hand moved through the vision, through the market street, through the faces "—is pushed aside. Not burned. Not slaughtered. *Unwritten.* The fold does not negotiate. It restores what was and removes what took its place, the way a river takes back its bed after a flood."

"How many?" Wren whispered.

"Two hundred years," Mother Sable said. "Every town on the old borders. Marrowgate. Bellharbour. Half of Thornfell Vale. Perhaps four hundred thousand souls, give or take a generation of births." She folded her hands. "Four hundred thousand people who exist because your country does not."

The silence in the hall was total. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

"And the other choice," Tamsin said. His voice came out flat, the way it did when he read bad weather in a sky.

Orren looked at him for a long moment, then at the basin, then away — and Tamsin understood that the old man could not bear to be the one who said it.

"Let the country rest," the Listener said instead, gently, "and the door must be closed from within. Sealed. Unmade, truly unmade, so that no Inquisitor, no heir, no desperate soul in another century can ever find it again." She paused. "And the seal requires Aldermere blood. The last of it. Standing on the threshold, singing the closing verse, giving up the script in his veins — which is to say, giving up everything the stranger's blood wrote into him. His memories of the true country. His claim to it." Her grey eyes settled on him. "Perhaps simply *him*, child. We do not know. No one has ever done it. The verse was written for a door, not for a boy."

Tamsin looked down at his own forearm, where the silver lines crawled just beneath the skin, faint as veins of ore in stone. Two days ago they had reached his elbow. This morning they touched his shoulder.

"It's spreading," he said. "Even sitting still."

"Yes," said Mother Sable. "Edran's blood finished its work the night you learned the truth. Soon the script will reach your heart. After that, the choice makes itself — the door will open whether you wish it or not, and Aldermere will return, and four hundred thousand people will pay for your hesitation."

"So I have until my heart," Tamsin said, "to decide between a country and myself."

"No," said Wren.

Everyone turned. She was on her feet, fists at her sides, and her voice cracked across the hall like a whip.

"No. That's not the choice. Don't you dare dress it up as arithmetic." She pointed at the basin. "That's what *they* did. That's what the Inquisition did — decided that some people were the price of other people, and folded a whole country shut so no one would have to look at it. And now we sit here and count four hundred thousand strangers against one person I—"

She stopped. The word she had almost said hung in the air, enormous, and everyone heard it, including, visibly, Wren herself.

"One person," she finished, quieter, hoarse. "One person standing right here."

Mother Sable's face did something complicated and old. "Child," she said, "love does not change arithmetic. It only tells you which numbers hurt."

### Wren

Afterward, she found him on the shrine's outer stair, the one carved into the mountain's face, where the wind came up off the valley carrying woodsmoke and thaw and the ordinary smell of a world that might end in a week.

He didn't look at her when she sat beside him. Below them, far below, the morning mist was peeling off the foothills, and somewhere in that mist were roads he knew the way other men knew their mothers' voices.

"You should have let me finish the sentence," she said.

"I heard it."

"That's not the same as hearing it."

His mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the kind he made when the roads were bad and he wanted her to stop being afraid. "No," he admitted. "It isn't."

She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. The wind pulled at her hair. "I've been doing sums since Bellharbour," she said. "Ever since the lamps guttered and the old woman grabbed my hand and wept at the name of a country I'd never seen. I've been adding up what it would cost to get mine back. Four hundred thousand. I can say the number fine. I can even believe it." She turned to look at him, really look, at the sharp tired face of the only honest man she'd ever trusted. "But I can't make the sum come out right, Tam. Because every time I imagine the door opening, I see Aldermere — the harvest fields frozen mid-motion, the bread rising in that kitchen, all those people waiting two hundred years to finish living — and I want it *so badly* I can taste it. And every time I imagine the door closing, I picture you standing in it. Singing yourself shut. And I want to burn the whole mountain down instead."

"Wren—"

"Let me finish." Her voice shook and she hated it and kept going anyway. "My grandmother sang me a lullaby about a country I thought was a story. Everything I have of her is a key to a door, and I never got to hold anything else of hers, and I *want*, Tam. I want it so much it feels like grief. But if the price of getting it is you—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, steadier. "Then the lullaby was wrong. Home isn't a country. I figured that out somewhere on the smugglers' roads, before any keeper told me. Home is a stubborn guildsman who lies badly and stitches maps in his head and lets a thief walk beside him instead of turning her in."

He was looking at her now. The wind moved between them and neither of them moved away from it.

"There has to be a third road," she said. "You're a Wayfarer. Find one."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then: "There isn't always a third road, Wren. Sometimes there's a pass with a cliff on either side and you just choose which wall to fall against." He exhaled. "But I'll tell you what I keep coming back to. The Listener said no one has ever sung the closing verse. No one knows what it costs — maybe nothing. Maybe the script leaves and what remains is still me. Maybe—" He laughed, short and bitter. "Maybe I'm not even the thing making the choice. Maybe I'm just the door wearing a boy."

"Don't," she said fiercely. "Don't you dare talk about yourself like you're a map. You're not ink. You're not Edran's errand. You're Tamsin Kavanagh, last apprentice of the Wayfarers' Guild, and you got lost on purpose once outside Marrowgate just so we'd have an extra night at the inn, and you hum when you stitch wounds, off-key, and you held my hand in the coffin-crypt and pretended it was so I wouldn't trip." Her eyes were burning and she let them burn. "I know exactly who you are. So whatever you decide — whatever you *have* to do — you don't get to decide it alone. That's the toll. That's the price of the door. Me."

Something in his face broke open then, quietly, the way ice breaks on a river in spring — no violence to it, just the long-held thing finally letting go.

"All right," he said.

"All right?"

"All right. Together. Whatever the door asks." He looked back out over the valley, and the wind took his words and nearly lost them. "Because I've been thinking about the fourth option. The one nobody's named yet."

Wren went very still. "Which is?"

"Edran came back for me," Tamsin said. "Forty years searching, and he died on the Grey Stair trying to bring the last Aldermere child home. Why? If the door only needed blood to open, any drop would do. If it only needed blood to close, any drop would do that too. But he crossed the world for *me*. Which means the script chose me for something else. Something none of these keepers remember, because they were children when the fold closed, and the cartographer's journal burned half its pages." He stood, and the morning light caught the silver lines climbing his throat, and for the first time since the Grey Stair he did not look afraid of them. He looked like a man reading a trail sign.

"Wren," he said, "I think the map hasn't shown us its last road yet."

And below them, far below in the valley mist, a horn sounded — long, low, and wrong, a note with no breath behind it, the kind of sound a scentless tireless thing makes when it stops running and starts hunting.

The hounds had found Vael.
