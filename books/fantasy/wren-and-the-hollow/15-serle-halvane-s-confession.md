## 15. Serle Halvane's Confession

The hounds did not sleep, and so Serle Halvane had learned not to either.

He rode at their head through the last miles of the high pass, and they flanked him in perfect silence—nine of them, men once, remade now into something that breathed without wanting anything. Their boots struck the frozen road in unison, a sound like a slow drum. The silver script beneath their collars caught the moonlight and gave it back duller, as if even light came away diminished from touching them.

Halvane had signed the orders that made them. That was the thing no one asked about, because no one knew to ask. They saw an Inquisitor with nine tireless hunters and assumed the Crown's power was vast and clean. It was vast. Clean it had never been.

He thought about the boy on the Grey Stair. Not the stranger—the boy. Tamsin Kavanagh, last apprentice of a guild of four, who had lied to Halvane's face with the wide honest eyes of a man lying for the first time in his life. Halvane had known the lie within three words. He had let it stand, because he had wanted—God help him—to see what the boy would do next. It had been thirty years since anyone had surprised him. The dead man on the Stair had surprised him. And now the boy was running north with a map that could unmake everything Halvane had spent his life holding shut.

"Master," said the foremost hound. Its voice was flat as slate. "The shrine is two days. The pass ahead is clear."

"Clear," Halvane repeated. Nothing was clear. Nothing had been clear since he was nineteen years old and opened a door in the Inquisition archive that had no business being open.

He spurred his horse forward, into the dark, and began—as he did every night when the hounds marched and no one living could hear him—the story he told only to himself.

* * *

He had been nineteen, a clerk's son from the river provinces with a gift for old languages and a scholarship paid for by the Office of Inquiry, and he had believed—truly believed—that the Inquisition was what its seal said it was: the keeper of records, the guardian of lawful memory, the institution that ensured the Crown never forgot what it owed and to whom. His father had wept at his commissioning. *A servant of truth,* the old man had called him, and Serle had carried those three words like coins in his pocket for a decade before they wore through.

The door was in the sub-vault beneath the Chancellery, behind the shelf of treaties nobody read because everyone knew how they ended. It had no lock. That should have been the first warning. Everything worth hiding in the world was hidden behind locks; this was hidden behind boredom.

Inside was a room the size of a chapel, and every wall of it was covered in paper.

Not ledgers. Not decrees. Letters. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, bound in bundles tied with black ribbon, and each bundle labeled in the same careful clerk's hand: *Correspondence, Year of the Folding.* Serle had pulled one bundle down thinking it would be dry diplomatic traffic, the kind of thing that made his colleagues yawn over their wine.

It was a farmer's petition. A woman named Aldous-her-name-had-been, writing to the Crown from a village whose name Serle did not recognize, asking—not begging, asking, with the terrible dignity of the ruined—why her fields had gone grey at the edges overnight, and why her neighbor's well water tasted of iron, and whether the King knew that something was eating her country from its borders inward.

She had signed it. She had written her address in full. She had expected an answer.

Serle had sat down on the cold floor of that archive room and read until the candle burned out, and then he had relit it and read until dawn, and by dawn he understood three things, each worse than the last.

First: Aldermere had not been conquered. There had been no war, no treaty signed across a table between equals, none of the tidy history taught in every schoolroom from the coast to the Thornfells. Aldermere had been *dissolved*. Folded out of the world like a letter creased and creased until it vanished between its own folds—and the folding had been done by the Office of Inquiry itself, working with the first kings of the present dynasty, in the year the dynasty was founded.

Second: the folding had not been ambition. It had been *concealment*. Twenty years earlier, the men who would become the Inquisition's founders—then merely ambitious chancellors and a young king's uncles—had done something in Aldermere. The records were maddeningly oblique about what, exactly. A village emptied. A hostage returned wrong. A ritual performed with borrowed power that had not been theirs to borrow. Whatever it was, it had killed people, and the killing could not be undone, only hidden, and Aldermere's own scholars were close—so close—to proving it. So the chancellors had bought the art of folding from a desperate cartographer's guild at a price in blood, and they had folded the crime away along with the country that held it. Erase the witness, erase the wound. Erase the whole nation, and there was no murder left, only empty grassland and a new border and schoolbooks that said *there has always been a Crown here.*

Third: Serle Halvane, nineteen years old, servant of truth, was standing inside the proof. And the door had no lock because no one in two hundred years had ever been fool enough or curious enough or damned enough to walk through it.

He had reported it. That was the part he could never make peace with, later—the fact that his first instinct, his truest self, had been to climb the stairs and tell the Chancellor-General everything. He had gotten as far as the antechamber. He had been sitting there, the stolen page folded inside his coat like a coal against his heart, when the Chancellor-General's aide had come out, smiled at him, and said: *Master Halvane. You've found the Archive Room, haven't you?*

They had all found it. Every archivist who reached the sub-vault found it. That was the point of leaving the door unlocked. The room was not a secret. The room was a *test*, and it had been running, patient as stone, for two centuries. Those who walked past it served long and rose high. Those who entered it—

There were names, in the personnel rolls, of clerks who had resigned suddenly, taken posts overseas, died of fevers. Serle had looked them up afterward, in the months when he still believed he might be different. Eleven names in two hundred years. He had traced four of them as far as records went. All four trails ended in water, or fire, or a grave with no body in it.

So he had put the page back. Tied the ribbon the way he'd found it. Closed the door behind him and walked up the stairs into the daylight, and accepted, six weeks later, his first posting as an Inquisitor proper.

People imagined that damnation arrived like a thunderclap. It didn't. It arrived like a career.

* * *

Thirty-one years. He counted them the way other men counted sins, which was what they were, though he'd learned to call them service.

He had risen because he was good. That was the joke at the center of his life, the one he told himself on bad nights: he was good at this. He had a talent for finding the crack in a person's story, for knowing which lie mattered and which was only a man trying to keep his dignity. He'd broken smuggling rings and spy cells and one genuinely wicked cult that had deserved him entirely, and each success had bought him more access, more trust, more of the machinery—and eventually the machinery had shown him what it was for.

Because that was the thing the archive room taught and nothing else could: the Inquisition's great work was not finding traitors. Its great work was *guarding the shape of the lie.* Every oath, every interrogation, every file sealed and every scholar quietly ruined—it all bent toward one purpose, older than any living officer, passed down not in documents but in instinct, in the way certain questions made certain doors close. When Serle had finally risen far enough to sit at the inner table, the Chancellor-General had poured him wine and said, without preamble: *You found the room, of course. They always do. Tell me—did you report it?*

*I intended to,* Serle had said.

*And then?*

And then, Serle had said. And the Chancellor-General had nodded as if he'd heard the most natural thing in the world, because he had. He'd stood where Serle stood. His predecessor had stood there too, and hers, all the way back down the line to the men who had done the folding with their own hands and discovered, in the doing, that a crime of that size does not stay buried unless you post guards on the grave forever.

*We are not the Crown's conscience,* the Chancellor-General had said. *We are its bandage. Tear us off and the wound comes with us.*

Serle had drunk the wine. That was his confession, the one he'd never spoken aloud, not to a confessor, not to God, not to the dark: he had drunk the wine, and it had been good wine, and he had stayed.

He told himself the reasons, and the reasons were true, and that made them worse. If the truth came out—if it was shouted in the squares and printed in the pamphlets and sung in the taverns—what then? The present dynasty fell; fine, dynasties fell, the world turned. But the fall would not be clean. Half the realm's land titles ran through grants issued after the Folding, on land that had belonged to someone. Every border, every charter, every noble house's proud lineage dated itself to a founding that had never happened as told. Two hundred years of law rested on the absence of a country, and you could not dig out one rotten stone from under a cathedral and expect the bells to go on ringing. Millions had built their lives on the lie without knowing it. To tell them was not to free them. It was to burn down the only house they had, and hand them ashes, and say *be glad, it's the truth.*

That was what he believed. He had believed it so long it had grown into him like the script grew into the hounds, silver threads through living flesh, and some nights he could not remember whether he guarded the lie because it must be guarded or simply because guarding it was the only self he had left.

But belief was not the same as peace, and peace was not the same as sleep, and Serle Halvane had not truly slept in thirty-one years.

* * *

The stranger had changed everything.

They had brought word of him in autumn—a courier's report, routine, one line among forty: a man found dying on the Grey Stair, unknown, carrying papers. Routine except for what the mountain garrison's physician had written in the margin, in the shaky hand of a man describing something his training had no shelf for: *wound does not bleed as wounds bleed; faint silver markings around the injury, script-like, unfamiliar; subject speaks of a Hollow, and of a child.*

Halvane had read that line eleven times. Then he had burned the report, all copies but his own, and ridden north himself, because he knew the script. Of course he knew it. The hounds wore it. The fold was stitched with it. It was the cartographers' art, the borrowed power, the price paid two centuries ago—and it was walking around in a dying man on a mountain pass, moving toward the one place in the world it should never go.

He had reached Marrowgate too late. The body was gone, the map was gone, and a guild boy with honest eyes had stood in his leaking guildhall and lied to him badly and beautifully, and something in Serle—some starved, buried thing—had wanted the boy to get away.

He had crushed that thing promptly. But he had noticed it. That was the danger of a long life spent alone inside a secret: you started welcoming company, even the company of your own destruction.

Now he camped below the shrine of Vael with nine hounds and two hundred soldiers, and the boy was up there, and the map, and the keepers, and Wren—the girl, he'd read her file, grandmother a Hollow-keeper, bloodline tracked through three generations of quiet surveillance that Halvane had inherited like a debt. They were going to open it. He could feel it the way sailors felt weather. The map had chosen its bearer; the songs were being sung again; Bellharbour's lamps had guttered when a refugee girl spoke the forbidden name. The fold was thinning.

And here was the arithmetic that kept him awake even by his standards. If they opened the Hollow and Aldermere came back—fields rising mid-harvest into towns that had stood there for two hundred years, thousands upon thousands of lives displaced or extinguished by the returning past—then the crime was exposed anyway, worse than exposed, *manifest*, a wound torn open on the face of the world. And if they closed it instead, quietly, the way Halvane had always planned to, then the boy and the girl and the keepers died knowing, and knowing traveled. Dying men talked. Martyrs sang.

Either road ended in the truth getting out. He had known it since Marrowgate. What he had not known—what he was only admitting now, in the dark, in the cold, with the hounds breathing their breathless breath around him—was which ending he wanted.

Because there was a third road. He had seen it in the archive room at nineteen and walked away from it, and he had seen it again in every year since, and he had never once had the courage: confess. Stand in the Chancellery square and open the black-ribboned bundles and read the farmer's letter aloud in his clerk-trained voice, all of it, the whole architecture of the lie, and let the cathedral come down on his own head. The truth would burn the Crown down—but perhaps the Crown deserved burning, and perhaps a thing built on a murdered country was not worth the millions sheltering in it, and perhaps—this was the thought that felt like a blade between ribs—perhaps his thirty-one years of service had not been a bandage at all. Perhaps it had just been a very long way of saying *no* to a question he'd answered *yes* to at nineteen, on the cold floor of the archive, with a candle burning out beside him.

He was fifty years old. He was tired in a way sleep could not touch. And tomorrow he would storm a shrine full of old keepers and a guild boy and a thief-girl, and kill or capture them all, because that was what the machine did, and he was the machine's hand, and a hand does not get to choose.

Unless.

Unless the hand remembered it had once been attached to a boy who wept reading a dead farmer's letter.

"Master." The foremost hound stood at the edge of the firelight, slate-eyed, patient. "Orders for the assault?"

Halvane looked up at the mountain, black against the stars, at the shrine somewhere on its shoulder where the last keepers of a murdered country kept their vigil. Somewhere up there a boy who had never known what he was was learning what Halvane had learned at nineteen, alone, in a locked-and-unlocked room. Learning it faster. Learning it with a map in his blood and a singer at his side and none of the machinery of silence pressing down on him.

*Tell me,* Halvane thought, to a boy he had never met and had already ruined, *what will you do that I didn't?*

"Take the stair at dawn," he said. "Kill no one you don't have to."

The hound tilted its head—a flicker of the old confusion, the ghost of the man it had been. *You don't have to* was not an order it had ever been given.

"And after?" it asked.

Halvane watched the fire settle, and gave the honest answer, the first one he'd spoken aloud in thirty-one years:

"After, I'm going to tell the truth."
