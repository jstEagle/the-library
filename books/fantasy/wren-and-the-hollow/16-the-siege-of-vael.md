## 16. The Siege of Vael

The bells woke them.

Not the shrine's own bells—Vael had none—but the wind through the hollow pillars above the mountain gate, which sang a low warning note when anything crossed the snowline heavier than a goat. Tamsin had heard it twice in his life before tonight. Once when a rockfall came down the eastern face. Once when nothing came at all, and the old keeper Maerlin had stood listening for an hour and then said, quietly, *that one was for you,* and refused to explain.

Tonight the note did not stop. It climbed and climbed until it stopped being a note and became a scream.

Tamsin was on his feet with his boots half-laced before he finished waking. The shrine's undercroft was dark except for the silver script on the map, which glowed faintly in its satchel like a coal refusing to die. Wren sat up across the hearth, hair loose, eyes already clear and sharp the way they always were the instant she opened them—a thief's waking, no softness in it, no wasted seconds.

"Hounds," she said.

"You can't know that."

"I can smell the absence." She pulled her coat on. "No horses. No torch-smoke. Something coming up this mountain that doesn't breathe."

He could hear them now, below. Not footsteps—the hounds made almost no sound, that was the horror of them—but the soldiers behind them were another matter. Halvane had brought men this time, dozens, their boots ringing on the old pilgrim stair, their lanterns swinging up through the pines like a procession of small angry moons.

Maerlin appeared in the doorway, ancient and straight-backed, wrapped in a robe the color of wet slate. Behind her, the other two keepers—Ossen, who never spoke, and little Sister Iven, who was older than Ossen by thirty years and looked younger by forty. Three Hollow-keepers left in the world, and Tamsin watched all three of them arrive at the same conclusion at the same moment, the way people do who have spent lifetimes waiting for one specific catastrophe.

"The door below," Maerlin said to Tamsin. "It must not be reached tonight."

"Then we hold the gate," Brannic said from the shadows near the stairs.

Tamsin turned. The old traitor stood with his axe already in his hand, as if he'd been sleeping with it, which knowing Brannic he probably had been. Snow-melt dripped from the eaves behind him. His face in the firelight was the same flat, unreadable thing it had been since Bellharbour, since he'd knelt in the mud and said *I sold you once; let me buy it back.*

"There are too many," Tamsin said.

"Aye." Brannic tested the edge of the axe with his thumb, unhurried. "There usually are. That's why I brought the good axe."

* * *

They had known, of course. Since Vael itself, since the keepers had spoken the truth into Tamsin's chest and left him staggering, they had known Halvane would come. The Inquisitor had spent his whole life guarding a lie, and now the lie had a name, a face, and a map walking around inside a living man's blood. Of course he would come. Tamsin had simply hoped—stupidly, humanly—for more nights. One more night to sit by the fire while Sister Iven taught Wren the closing verse of the lullaby. One more night to stand at the mountain's edge and look south toward Thornfell and feel, for the first time in his life, that the roads behind him mattered as much as the road ahead.

Instead: bells, and boots, and Brannic rolling barrels of lamp-oil toward the upper gate with the grim efficiency of a man doing arithmetic about his own remaining hours.

"The smugglers' passage," Tamsin said, catching up with him at the gatehouse. "The one behind the cistern. It comes out on the north face, above the icefield. It's narrow, it's unmapped, and half of it is held up by prayer and dead men's timber—"

"That's every road you've ever loved, boy."

"—and it will bring us out where the hounds can't follow. But someone has to make sure they don't reach the cistern before we're through."

Brannic set down the barrel. He looked at Tamsin for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes—not warmth exactly, but the place where warmth had been, the scar-tissue shape of it.

"You're asking me to hold the stair."

"I'm asking you to buy time."

"I held worse stairs for worse reasons." He shouldered the axe. "For gold, once. For fear, another time. This one I'd have held for free." He almost smiled. It was terrible and wonderful, that smile. "Go get your girl and your map and your dead country, Wayfarer. Leave the counting to me."

Tamsin wanted to argue. He catalogued, in the space of a breath, every reason arguing would fail—Brannic's pride, Brannic's guilt, Brannic's twenty extra years and one bad knee—and found that the argument was already lost before it started, and that both of them knew it, and that saying so aloud would only cheapen it.

So instead he did the thing the Guild had taught him, the first lesson, the lesson under all the others: he took Brannic's forearm in the wayfarer's grip, wrist to wrist, and said the words that closed every bargain his order ever struck on the high roads.

"Roads remember."

Brannic's grip tightened until it hurt. "Aye," he said. "And so do I. Go."

* * *

The fighting, when it came, sounded like weather.

Tamsin heard it from below—through stone, through the shrine's deep bones—as they ran. The crack of the oil-barrels going up. The roar of flame finding pine and rope and frozen timber. Shouting, which meant the hounds had reached the men and the men had remembered they were men. Then, cutting through all of it, the sound he would carry for the rest of his life however long that proved to be: Brannic's voice, lifted in a bellow that had no fear in it anywhere, singing—not a song, not a war cry, but the wayfarers' counting-call, the old rhythm used to pace a column over dangerous ground, *one-two-and-over, mind the gap, one-two-and-over*—as though the siege were just another crossing, as though death were a crevasse to be stepped over neatly, on the count.

"He's mad," Wren gasped beside him.

"He's a Wayfarer," Tamsin said. "Or he was. Or—" His throat closed. "He wanted to be again. That counts. That has to count."

They went through the cistern arch together, ducking under the drip-line, into the dark. Sister Iven came with them, moving with shocking speed for her age, her hand light on Tamsin's shoulder like a bird landing. Maerlin stayed above—she would not leave the reliquary, would not leave the records of the fold, and there had been no time to fight her about it. Ossen had vanished upward with a knife and a nod, gone to flank the attackers through passages only keepers knew, and whether that was courage or a death wish Tamsin didn't have room to wonder.

The smugglers' passage swallowed them.

It was everything he'd promised and worse: a crawl of black air behind the cistern wall, timber shoring slick with frost-rot, the ceiling close enough that Wren's breath stirred his collar ahead of him. Tamsin went first with the map-satchel strapped tight to his back and one hand tracing the guild-signs cut into the stone—old ones, older than the shrine, cut by smugglers and priest-smugglers and the kind of men who moved what kings forbade. *Low bridge. Bad footing. Trust the left wall.* A hundred years of dead guides whispering to him in chisel-marks, and for the first time in his life Tamsin Kavanagh understood that he had never walked these roads alone, not once, not ever. Every sign was a hand on his shoulder.

Behind them, muffled by fifty feet of mountain, the battle went on. And went on. Brannic's counting-call rose and fell, rose and fell, and each time it fell Tamsin's heart fell with it, and each time it rose again he hated himself for the hope.

Then the call stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, between one count and the next, the way a snapped rope stops.

Wren's hand found his in the dark and gripped hard enough to grind bone. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing in the world to say that wouldn't be a lie or a wound, and there was a passage to finish.

* * *

The collapse came an hour later, or ten minutes, or a lifetime—it was impossible to measure anything down there except by the burning in Tamsin's shoulders.

They had reached the great fissure, the one place where the smugglers' route crossed a natural fault, a cathedral of broken stone where the passage widened and the ceiling climbed away into darkness overhead. Tamsin was halfway across the scree slope at its heart, reaching back to pull Iven over a slab the size of a coffin, when the mountain flinched.

He felt it first through his boots—a deep concussion, somewhere far above, followed by a second, closer. Halvane's sappers, bringing down the gatehouse, or the cistern, or the whole crown of Vael if that's what it took to seal the shrine like a tomb. Dust breathed out of the fissure walls. Somewhere above, a keening began—the hollow-pillar wind, screaming its warning at last for something that had already arrived.

"Run," Tamsin said. "Don't pick your feet up high, run *light*, let the stones choose—"

The ceiling chose.

It came down in stages, the way mountains do, each fall triggering the next, a slow avalanche of the world's bones. Tamsin shoved Iven forward and she went like a hare, impossibly quick, and he grabbed Wren's sleeve and hauled, and the world behind them became white noise and black air and the taste of old stone ground fine as flour. He ran by guild-craft alone—reading the tremor through his soles, choosing footing a half-second before he needed it, left wall here, low arch there, the signs in the stone flashing past in his mind's eye faster than sight. *Trust the left wall. Mind the gap.* A hundred dead guides shouting at once.

A slab the size of a barn door came off the wall to his right, and Tamsin did the only honest thing available: he threw Wren ahead of him through the narrowing gap and dove after her, and the world clapped shut behind his heels like a book.

Silence. Dust so thick it was a substance, a gray sea they floated in. Somewhere very close, Wren coughed. Somewhat less close, Iven's voice, thin and steady, reciting something in Aldermere's tongue—a psalm, or a lullaby, or perhaps just names, the names of everyone she'd ever kept the door for.

Tamsin lay on his back in the ruin and laughed, a cracked, dust-choked sound that frightened him more than the collapse had.

"What," Wren rasped, "in all the hells—"

"We're alive." He coughed. "I've been running for weeks and I finally remember how."

"Don't." Her hand found his arm in the dark and gripped, and he felt the tremor in her that she would never permit in her voice. "Don't joke yet. Give it a minute. Give it—" Her breath hitched. "He was still singing."

"I know."

"He was still *counting*, Tamsin. Like it was a road. Like we'd all catch him up on the other side of the pass."

Tamsin pulled her in, dust and all, and she let him, and for a while there was only the dark and the dying echo of the mountain settling its weight, and Sister Iven's patient voice naming the dead, of whom there were suddenly so many more.

When they could move, they moved.

* * *

They came out on the north face an hour before dawn, into cold so clean it felt like forgiveness.

Below them the icefield stretched away silver-blue under the last stars, and beyond it, range after range, the empty roof of the world. Tamsin turned once, at the passage mouth, and looked back at Vael.

The shrine burned. Not wholly—the mountain had taken the crown of it, collapsed the gatehouse and half the pilgrim stair, but fire still moved in the wreckage, orange against the snow, and figures moved through the smoke below, small as ants, methodical as ants. Halvane's soldiers, digging. Looking for survivors. Looking for the door.

Maerlin was nowhere in the smoke. Ossen was nowhere. Whether that meant escaped or ended, Tamsin couldn't know, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Three keepers at supper, laughing at Iven's jokes. Now one old woman stumbling down an icefield beside him, and two silences.

"They'll dig for months," Iven said quietly. She had stopped at the passage mouth too, and her ancient face was lit red by the distant fire. "Halvane will tear the mountain apart pebble by pebble before he admits the door isn't where he thinks it is. We bought years, maybe. You bought them." She looked at Tamsin sideways, and her eyes were dry and terribly bright. "Your friend on the stair. Your brother-in-the-order. They'll be songs, someday. Real ones. I've lived long enough to know the difference between songs and stories, and that was a story."

Tamsin couldn't answer. He looked instead at Wren, who stood with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the fire, and whose face wore an expression he'd never seen on her before—not grief exactly, not fury, but the place where the two met and fused into something harder than either. Resolve, maybe. Or inheritance. She had lost a grandmother's country and gained a war, all in one season, and she was standing up straighter under the weight instead of folding.

"The map," she said suddenly.

Tamsin's stomach dropped. He tore open the satchel.

The map was whole. Whole, and *changed*—the silver script that had crawled its slow inches across the parchment for weeks now blazed along every road at once, a web of cold light, and the ink had shifted one final time. The roads converged. All of them, every line, every smuggler's trace and pilgrim path and folded secret, ran together off the southern edge of the parchment toward a mark that hadn't been there at midnight: a small grey circle, and beneath it, in the silver script he now recognized as the stranger's writing and the hounds' writing and the script climbing his own veins—

*Here the map ends. Here the door begins.*

And beneath that, smaller, in a different hand, an older hand, one Tamsin somehow knew he'd seen before—in a kitchen, in a memory that wasn't his and was:

*Come home, little wren. Come home, little wayfarer.*

Wren read it over his shoulder. He felt her go still against his back.

"It knows our names," she whispered.

"No." Tamsin folded the map gently, gently, the way you'd fold a letter from someone dead. He looked south, down the long white miles toward the roots of the Thornfells, where the last road waited, and felt the script in his veins turn toward it like a compass needle, like a homing bird, like a child hearing its mother's step on the stair.

"It's *asking*," he said. "And it knows we're the only ones left to answer."

Below them, the fires of Vael burned down toward morning. Above them, the stars were going out one by one, making room for the day.

They started down the icefield together, three survivors and a map, toward the place where the world's oldest road came to its end—and whatever was waiting at the end of it had already begun, softly, patiently, in a language older than the Crown, to sing.
