## 17. Where the Map Ends

The map stopped being paper three days ago.

I'd been watching it change since Bellharbour—ink deepening, lines lifting faintly off the vellum like veins rising under skin—but it was on the morning we climbed into the high passes that I saw it happen. Tamsin had spread it across a flat stone to take our bearings, and the map simply wasn't flat anymore. The roads stood up from the surface in threads of silver, fine as spider silk, and where the Thornfells should have been there was a gap, a blank place shaped like a held breath, and out of that blankness one road descended, drawn in a hand steadier than any living cartographer's.

"It's showing us down," Tamsin said.

"I can see that."

"Under the mountains, Wren. There's no road under the Thornfells. I would know. It's my guild's whole business to know."

"Maybe it wasn't your guild's business," I said. "Maybe it was Aldermere's."

He didn't answer that. He'd stopped arguing with me about Aldermere somewhere around the cartographer's grave, when we'd read the journal by coffin-light and learned that his whole world was a folded page. But I watched him fold the map with the care you'd give a sleeping thing, and I watched him put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where the silver script had been creeping through his veins for weeks now, and I thought: *it isn't showing him the road. It's showing him home.*

I hated it a little, that thought. I'd carried Aldermere my whole life as a song and a shape—a country with no borders, held together by my grandmother's voice and a handful of words she made me swear to remember. And now the map had chosen him. The script lived in *his* blood. The dead smuggler had died in *his* arms. Even the lullaby, when I sang it, seemed to pass through me and settle in him, like rain finding the lowest ground.

But that was the thing about grief I'd learned young: it doesn't divide fairly. You take what you're given and you walk.

So we walked down.

* * *

The entrance, when we found it, was nothing. That's what unsettled me most. After everything—the hounds, the shrine at Vael, Brannic dying on the stair with his axe planted and his back to us so we could run—after all of it, the door to the end of everything was a crack in a hillside behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, the kind of place a shepherd might shelter a lamb in a storm.

Tamsin found it the way he found everything, with that guild-sense of his, palm flat to the rock, reading stone the way I read pockets. He stood before the frozen fall for a long moment, and then he reached through the ice—bare-handed, no wince, though the cold must have flayed him—and touched something behind it.

"There's a seam," he said. His breath came out in white ghosts. "Cut stone. Dressed and fitted. Wren, this is guildwork. Older than the guild."

"Can you open it?"

"I can open anything with a seam." He smiled, and it was the tired smile, the one that had been getting more common since Vael. "That's the problem. I can open anything. This is the first thing I've been afraid to."

I put my hand on his arm. Under his sleeve, through the wool, I felt it—the faint warmth of the script, like a banked coal. It had been spreading since the hounds wounded him. Some mornings he flexed his fingers like a man remembering how.

"Then don't open it," I said. "Let me."

* * *

Behind the waterfall the passage went down in long switchbacks, stairs cut so true that two hundred years hadn't jostled a single block. No dust. That was the other thing. Every ruin we'd walked since the grave had worn its age like a coat—dust, dripstone, the small soft teeth of time. This stair had none. It was as if the mountain had swallowed the passage only yesterday, or as if time itself had been folded out along with the country and left this road outside its reach.

The silver script was on the walls.

It started sparse—a word here, a phrase there, glimmering when our lantern light crossed it—and thickened as we descended until the stone was covered in it, whole columns of that looping, patient handwriting, the same script that had been pressed into the dead stranger's flesh, the same that now lived under Tamsin's skin. I couldn't read it. Neither could he. But the map could, and the map had stopped needing to be unfolded. It lay against his chest and I could see the glow of it through his shirt, answering the walls, word for word, like two voices singing the same line.

"It's the lullaby," Tamsin said, somewhere around the tenth turning. He'd stopped walking. He was standing with his hand flat against the script, and his face in the lantern light was the face of a man hearing his own name spoken in a language he doesn't know. "Wren. It's your grandmother's song. The whole way down. It's the lullaby, over and over, in different arrangements, like—"

"Like a key cut a thousand times," I said, "because they knew one day someone would come who only remembered one of them."

He looked at me. "How do you know that?"

"Because that's how my grandmother taught it to me. She said: *the song is long, little wren, and you only have the first verse, but a door doesn't need the whole song. It just needs to hear that you know it.*" My throat tightened. "I thought she was talking nonsense. She was always talking nonsense. She sang to the kettle. She thanked the chairs."

"She was a Hollow-keeper," Tamsin said quietly. "The keepers at Vael sang to everything too."

"I know." I started walking again, faster, because if I stopped I was going to cry on a two-hundred-year-old stair and the tears would probably freeze. "I know what she was. I've known since Bellharbour. It doesn't make it hurt less that I learned it from strangers."

He caught up. He didn't touch me. He'd learned that about me somewhere on the road—that grief in me was a bird on a wire, and sudden hands sent it flying. But he matched my pace, and his shoulder stayed near mine, and that was its own kind of holding.

* * *

The stair ended at the door.

I'd imagined it a hundred ways on the way down—a gate of black iron, a wall of thorns, something with teeth. What waited for us at the bottom was worse, because it was beautiful. A door of grey stone, tall as three men, set into the living rock with no frame and no hinge and no handle, carved so that it seemed not to have been built but *grown*, the way a tree grows. And over every inch of it, in silver that moved—actually moved, flowing slow as honey, rearranging itself like water finding its level—ran the script.

Tamsin held up the lantern and the light caught the silver and the silver caught it and gave it back changed, and for a moment the whole door was a sheet of falling stars.

"The melody," I whispered.

"What?"

"The script. Look at the spacing. Look at the—" I stepped closer, my breath fogging the silver, and there it was, plain as print once you saw it: the long phrases and the short, the rests, the repeated figure that my grandmother had hummed at the end of every verse, the one I'd always thought was just her habit, the way she trailed off. It wasn't habit. It was notation. The door was carved with the lullaby. The whole of it, every verse, every arrangement—the thousand cut keys—and somewhere in all that flowing silver was the one verse I carried, the verse my grandmother had pressed into me like a coin into a child's hand at a door she never expected to see me reach.

"It knows we're here," Tamsin said. The script had begun to gather on our side of the door, drawing itself toward us like iron filings to a lodestone, and beneath our feet the stone had begun—faintly, terribly—to hum.

"No," I said. "It knows *I'm* here."

I don't know how I knew. I knew the way you know a room is your room before you've turned on the light. The song in the stone had shifted, had bent itself around the place where I stood, and the humming under my feet had taken on the rhythm of my own heartbeat, which is a thing I will swear to until I die. The Hollow wasn't a door. The Hollow was a listener. It had been listening for two hundred years, down here in the dark, folded up with the country it guarded, and now it had heard the first verse of its own song come walking down the stair in the mouth of a pickpocket from Marrowgate who had never once in her life been wanted anywhere until a dying stranger made her wanted by every Inquisitor in the Crown.

My grandmother had sung me to sleep with a key and never told me it was a key.

I was going to be furious with her forever. I was going to miss her until the mountains wore down.

"Wren." Tamsin's voice came from very far away. "Wren, your hands."

I looked down. My hands were glowing. Not the skin—under it, in the veins of my wrists, the same silver that lived in Tamsin, waking, answering, the script recognizing its own song in my blood the moment I stood close enough to sing. I hadn't been marked by the stranger's wound. I hadn't been chosen by the map. But I was Aldermere's too, in the older way, the way of milk and lullabies and a grandmother's voice in a kitchen that no longer existed, and the door knew it, and the door was *glad*.

That was the worst part. The door was glad. I could feel its gladness like heat from an oven, like a dog pressing its head under your hand after years alone, and I stood in front of it with my shining wrists and I wept, ugly and open, while a country that had been folded out of the world two hundred years ago loved me through a wall of stone.

* * *

Tamsin didn't try to stop me crying. He sat down on the cold stair and waited, the way you wait out weather, and when the worst of it had passed he said, "Do you want me to sing it?"

"No." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "It has to be me. You know it has to be me. The keepers said—the blood at the door, the voice at the door. Your blood opened the road. My grandmother's song opens the door." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Two hundred years of Inquisitors guarding a fold in the world, and the lock was a bedtime song. Halvane is going to be so angry."

"He's going to be here," Tamsin said, and the tiredness in his voice was the whole truth of it. "The hounds found our trail at the river. A day behind, maybe less. Brannic bought us the stair, but he didn't buy us long."

"Then I'd better sing fast."

"Wren." He caught my hand—the glowing one—and held it, and the silver in his veins and the silver in mine touched through the skin and *sang*, a thin bright note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and we both flinched and then, absurdly, both laughed. "Whatever's behind this door," he said, "whatever the keepers said about the price—remember that I'm standing here. Whatever it asks, we decide together. That's the rule. We made it a rule at the grave."

"Rules are for guilds," I said.

"I'm the last of a guild," he said. "I'm allowed."

* * *

I sang the first verse the way my grandmother sang it, low and unhurried, the way you sing to something sleeping. The words were old Aldermere, most of them, words I'd mouthed for years without meaning—*orren vael, orren vael, close your eyes, the road is long*—and the door took them and the silver flowed to meet them, every note I gave it answered by a line of script lighting beneath its carved twin, key finding lock, lock finding key. The humming in the floor deepened. The falling-star shimmer of the silver slowed, and steadied, and began to move in time with me.

And the door began to open.

Not with grinding, not with weight. It opened the way an eye opens, the way a hand opens, the way a fist you've held for two hundred years opens when at last someone says the right word. A seam of darkness appeared down its center, and through the seam came air—real air, moving air, smelling of cut grass and woodsmoke and bread, the smell of a country at harvest, and I heard Tamsin make a sound beside me that I had never heard a man make, a sound like a name he'd been forbidden to say.

The seam widened. Beyond it was not darkness after all but light, gold and green and impossible, the light of a late afternoon that had been waiting two centuries to end, and out of that light came a sound that undid me completely.

Singing.

Far away, faint, many voices—the same lullaby, the whole of it, every verse I'd never learned, sung by throats that had been mid-song when the world was folded, still singing, still keeping the time, a country holding its own note down here under the stone until someone came to sing them the rest.

"They know," I whispered. The silver in my arms was blazing now, and through the widening seam I could see shapes—fields, a road, a farmhouse with smoke rising from its chimney, smoke still rising, always rising. "Tamsin. They know we're here. They've been singing to us the whole way down."

The door stood open a hand's width. Cold mountain air at our backs, warm harvest air at our faces, and between them the threshold where two hundred years met.

And then, from far up the stair, faint but unmistakable, carried down through all that patient stone:

A dog barking.

Not a dog. We both knew it wasn't a dog. The hounds didn't bark like dogs; they didn't make sound so much as *report* it, flat and wrong, and there were many of them, and they had found the waterfall.

Tamsin turned toward the stair, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been doing since Vael—*how long, how many, is there another way*—and I watched the arithmetic come up short. There was no other way. There was only the door, and the door was open a hand's width, and the singing beyond it was growing louder, as if the country on the other side had heard the hounds too, and was calling its children home.

"Wren," Tamsin said, and his hand found mine in the light of the threshold, silver to silver, "I think they're going to make us choose faster than we wanted to."

The door hummed under our joined hands. And in the script that flowed across its face, in letters I still could not read but understood all the same, two names had risen to the top of the flowing silver, bright as new-fallen snow, and they were ours.
