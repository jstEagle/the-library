## 22. A Guild of Our Own

The roof leaked for exactly one more year, and then it didn't.

I stood in the yard on the first morning of spring with a hammer in my hand and shingles under my boots and watched Brannic's nephew—good lad, steady hands, no idea what his uncle had died holding—finish the last course of slate along the eastern ridge. The morning sun came off the wet stone like hammered silver, and for a moment I let myself pretend that was all it was. Silver. Weather. Light on a roof.

But the script was gone from my veins. I knew that now the way you know a tooth has stopped aching—by its absence, by the strange new quiet where the ache used to live. Whatever the stranger had pressed into me on the Grey Stair, whatever Aldermere had written into my blood before it folded itself away, it had poured out through the Hollow with everything else: the harvest fields, the rising bread, the mother's chair, the songs. All of it gone out into the world like weather, like seed.

Except the map. Except the memory. Those we had kept.

"Tamsin." Wren's voice came from the doorway behind me, sharp as ever, softened at the edges by the year the way a river stone is softened—slowly, and only where the water touches most. "There's a woman at the gate says she walked three weeks to get here. Says she heard the roads are being taught again."

"Is she a cartographer?"

"She's a grandmother who knows forty songs her mother never taught her." Wren leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, watching me watch the roof. "Says they're Aldermere songs."

My chest did the thing it still did sometimes—a tightening, a reaching toward something just past the reach of my hand. It didn't hurt anymore. That was the miracle of it. Grief, I had learned, doesn't have to be a wound. It can be a scar, and scars are only the body remembering without bleeding.

"Then she walks in," I said. "Put her in the east room. The one with the good light."

"The one with the good light is the map room."

"I know which room it is. I've been sleeping in it for a year."

Wren grinned—that quick, crooked, unrepentant grin that had gotten her thrown out of better establishments than ours—and went down to open the gate.

* * *

The guildhall had been four members and a leaking roof when I left it. Now it was something I didn't entirely have words for, so I had stolen some. Wren called it a school. The refugees who came through Marrowgate called it a sanctuary. Old Mother Anselm, who had wept in Bellharbour when the lamps guttered at Aldermere's name, called it the remembering house, and that name had stuck fastest, the way true names do.

We took anyone who wanted to learn the roads. Not just the smugglers' ways and the shepherds' cuts through the Thornfells—though we still taught those, because the mountains don't care about treaties and neither should travelers—but everything the year had taught us. Cartography, yes, real cartography, honest ink on honest vellum. But also the singing of roads: how a lullaby can hold the shape of a valley, how a work song remembers a ford that maps forgot, how grief itself will draw you a route home if you listen to it right. Wren ran the singing. She claimed she'd learned it all from her grandmother, and then, one night over cheap wine, admitted that half of it she'd invented and dared anyone to tell the difference. Nobody could. That was the point, she said. Memory isn't a country. It's a craft.

The Crown had not fallen, quite, though it had cracked in places and let some honest light in. Halvane's testimony had done what his swords never could. He lived now in a small house near the capital, an old man writing depositions by candlelight, and once a season he sent us a parcel: documents, testimonies, the Inquisition's own records of what stood where. We copied them into the archive. We never burned anything. That was the whole law of the place, the one rule carved above the hearth in letters Wren had painted herself, silver-grey on grey stone, echoing a script neither of us would ever see again:

**Nothing here gets erased twice.**

* * *

In the afternoons I taught the beginners their first lesson, and it was always the same one, and I always taught it standing at the same window on the second floor, looking north toward the Thornfells.

"A road," I told them, "is a promise someone made and kept until they couldn't. Every track you'll ever walk, somebody cut it, or cleared it, or died keeping it open, or simply walked it so many times that the ground learned their going and remembered. You are not the first. You will not be the last. Your job is to be somewhere in the middle of that line, and to leave it walkable."

A girl in this spring's class—refugee from the southern wars, maybe fourteen, all elbows and suspicion—raised her hand. "What if the road leads somewhere that isn't there anymore?"

I looked at her for a long moment. She stared back, defiant, the way Wren used to stare back, the way I supposed I'd once stared at Guildmaster Corren across this very room.

"Then you carry it," I said. "That's the hardest kind of wayfaring. But the road still exists. It exists in you. And if you're careful, and honest, and stubborn enough—one day you set it down again, in ink, or in song, or in a story told to someone who needs it. A country can be taken off the maps. It can't be taken off the people who remember walking it."

She wrote that down. They all did. And I turned back to the window so none of them would see what my face did when I said it, because even after a year, even with the quiet where the ache used to be, some sentences cost more than others.

* * *

Wren hung the map on the first day of the new term, and I want to get this right, because I've told the story a hundred times since and it deserves its best telling.

It was evening. The students were at supper—the good noise of them, clatter and argument and laughter, the sound a guildhall is supposed to make and had not made in thirty years. Wren had spent the whole winter restoring the map. Not redrawing it; restoring it. Working with the keepers' notes and Halvane's parcels and old Mother Anselm's songs, filling in the roads the fold had eaten, laying Aldermere back onto the parchment stroke by stroke the way you lay a blanket over someone sleeping. Where the silver script had been, she used ink mixed with ash from the hearth—the same hearth, she informed me solemnly, that had cooked every meal we'd eaten since we came home, so the country could smell dinner while it worked.

She hung it by the door. Of course she hung it by the door. Where everyone leaving would pass beneath it, and everyone arriving would see it first, and the light from the entry lamp fell across it just so.

"There," she said, stepping back, wiping her hands on her trousers, ink to the wrists as always. She looked at it the way you look at a fire. "Now it's a door instead of a wound."

The map showed Aldermere whole. Rivers running, roads crossing, the Hollow marked not as an ending but as a threshold, drawn open, with small figures walking through—not fleeing, walking, the way you walk into a market square or a family kitchen. Around the vanished country's borders, where the world had grown over the absence like skin over a scar, Wren had painted the towns that stood there now, and connected them to the old roads with fine dotted lines, and labeled those lines in two hands: the new names in black, the old names in grey underneath, both legible, both true.

Bellharbour, and under it, faintly: *Bellhafan*. Marrowgate, and under it: *Margate-aeld*. The inn that dreamed, and under it the name of the farmhouse that had dreamed first.

People would stand under that map coming and going for years. Children would trace the rivers with their fingers. Some of them would cry, and we would let them, and hand them bread, and teach them the songs. That was the design. That was Wren's whole genius, though she'd deny it: she made grief into furniture. Something you lived with. Something that held your coat by the door.

"It's crooked," I said, because someone had to, and because if I said what I actually felt I'd have had to sit down on the stairs and put my head between my knees like a green apprentice.

"It's perfect."

"It's crooked by half a thumb's width."

"And whose fault is that? You're the one who hung the nail." She tilted her head at me, eyes bright in the lamplight. "Besides. Everything true is a little crooked. Straight lines are what liars use."

I got the ladder. We straightened it together. Her shoulder against mine on the top step, both of us laughing at nothing, at everything, at the sheer improbable fact of being alive and home and unhunted on an ordinary Tuesday evening a year after the end of the world.

* * *

Later—I won't say how much later; a gentleman keeps some things dark—we sat on the roof ridge under the new slates, sharing a bottle of the terrible plum brandy Brannic used to hoard, passing it back and forth in the cold clear dark. Below us the valley lay scattered with lamplight. Somewhere out there, the Thornfells stood black against the stars, and beyond them, under everything, a country slept the sleep of the finished, and did not dream, because it had given its dreams to us.

"Do you ever miss it?" Wren asked. "The script. The... hum. Knowing things in your blood before you knew them in your head."

I considered honestly, which was our house rule about questions like that.

"No," I said. "I miss him. The stranger. I never even learned his name, and I miss him like family." I rolled the bottle between my palms. "He crossed the whole world to come back for a baby he'd carried out thirty years before, and he died on the worst stair in the mountains with the job half done, and he trusted a total stranger to finish it. That's not a haunting. That's a colleague."

"He'd have liked this place," Wren said. "All these people learning the roads."

"He'd have complained about the state of the boot room."

"He'd have been right about the boot room."

We drank. The stars did what stars do, which is burn patiently over everything, the saved and the lost alike.

"Wren." I'd rehearsed this part and immediately abandoned the rehearsal, because rehearsed words go stale in the mouth. "You know you don't have to stay. You said once you're claimed by no nation and you claim yourself, and I'd carve that over the gate if you asked me to, and if you woke up tomorrow and wanted to walk—any road, all of them—I'd pack your bag myself and stand in the yard waving until you were over the hill."

She was quiet for a moment. In the lamplight leaking from the dormer below us I saw her turn the bottle slowly in her hands, the way she did when she was deciding whether to be flippant or true.

"But?" she said.

"But I'm glad you don't want to. And I wanted to say it out loud, at least once, where the roof could hear it, so the building knows." I looked up at the new slates, Brannic's nephew's good work, tight against the sky. "Home was never a country. I spent my whole life thinking it was something you found on a map. It's not. It's the people who walk beside you. That's all it ever was. That's all it ever is."

Wren bumped her shoulder against mine and left it there, warm in the cold.

"Well," she said, when she trusted her voice again, and I pretended not to notice that she hadn't fully, "someone has to keep you from teaching the children that the Grey Stair is safe in April. So I suppose I'll stay."

"For the boot room's sake?"

"For the boot room's sake."

* * *

We came down before midnight, because morning comes early in a school, and because the woman with the forty songs had asked, before bed, whether she might see the map by lamplight. She stood before it a long time in the empty hall, this grandmother who had walked three weeks on old legs to bring us what her mother never taught her. Then she began to sing—softly, so as not to wake the house—and it was a song I knew. Wren's lullaby. The key-song. But this version had verses we'd never heard, a middle passage where the melody turned and climbed like a path switchbacking up a mountain face, and Wren stood beside me in the doorway with her hand pressed flat over her mouth, listening to her own inheritance come home from a direction she'd never thought to look.

When the song ended, the old woman turned to us.

"My grandmother sang that to my mother, and my mother sang it to me, and nobody living knows the words anymore except me," she said. "I'd like to write them down. Before I forget. If you'll teach me how."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked at me. Outside, far off in the Thornfells, wind moved through passes no map had ever named, over stone that remembered footsteps, and came down into the valley smelling of snowmelt and spring.

"Madam," I said, "you've come to the right guild."

And in the morning we began.
