## 1. The Valley That Forgot Itself

There are three smells a tanner comes to know better than his own name: lime, dung, and the particular sweetness of rot that means money. I was elbow-deep in the third when the strangers rode into Hollowmere, and I remember thinking, with the profound stupidity of a man whose hands are full of dead sheep, that their horses were too fine for our road.

That should have been my first warning. Everything in Hollowmere is a little wrong for the world outside—the roads too soft, the light too kind, the prices at market stuck somewhere around forty years ago like a fly in amber. We told ourselves it was good soil and honest living. We were liars with excellent posture.

The riders stopped at the well, which everyone does, because the well is the center of everything here whether we admit it or not. There were three of them. Two wore the gray of King Aldric's hunt—travel-stained, but gray all the same, the color of men who get paid by the confession. The third wore black, and did not dismount.

I kept scraping. A tanner who stops working to stare is a tanner with something to hide, or so I reasoned, though I had nothing to hide except, apparently, an entire valley's worth of it.

"Good water," one of the grays said to no one in particular. "Sweet. Old."

"Old wells hold old things," said the other.

They weren't talking about water. I knew that the way you know a storm is coming—not through evidence, but through some animal ledger your body keeps without consulting you. My hands went on working. My mind went elsewhere: to a cottage on the moor's edge, long cold, where I'd learned everything I knew about herbs and half of what I knew about fear.

Maren. Ten years dead now, or vanished—I never learned which, and she'd have laughed at me for wanting to know. *Curiosity is just greed wearing a scholar's hat,* she used to say. Then she'd steal my bread anyway.

* * *

The trouble with living in a valley that has forgotten itself is that forgetting is a craft, and someone practiced it well. Ask any soul in Hollowmere how long the valley has been at peace, and they'll say "always." Ask them what lies past the northern ridge, and they'll say "more valley," which is impossible, because I have walked that ridge and found the world simply... declined to continue. Not mountains. Not forest. A blankness, like a page torn out of a book and the remaining edges smoothed so you'd hardly notice.

I noticed. But then, Maren raised me from eleven years old, after the fever took my parents, and she raised me strange. She taught me letters, and stars, and which mushrooms lie. She taught me that when the elders say *the ward keeps us safe*, I should ask *safe from what*, and that asking was permitted exactly once per topic before it became rude.

She left me—or her cottage left me—when I fled her service at nineteen, over a quarrel I still can't fully recall, which has always struck me as suspicious. You don't forget why you broke your own heart. Unless someone helps you.

After her, I took up tanning because leather doesn't require belief in anything. It has served me faithfully for fifteen years. It asked only my fingernails and my sense of smell, both long gone.

* * *

The hunters stayed three days. They lodged at the Drowned Lamb, which is named for a sheep, because our founders lacked ambition. On the second evening, the tall one came into my yard while I was stretching a hide, and stood there being polite at me in a way that made my spine itch.

"Fine work," he said. "You're Cyrus, then. Galloway."

Nobody uses my surname. In Hollowmere, surnames are like spare keys—everyone has one, nobody remembers what it opens.

"Everyone's Cyrus if you wait long enough," I said. "What can I do for you?"

"You lived on the moor edge. North cottage." He smiled. He had the smile of a man who has read a file on you and found it entertaining. "You served a wise woman there. Maren. Maren Vael."

The hide slipped from my frame. I caught it mostly out of reflex; my mind had gone white and ringing.

"Vael," I repeated. "That's not a name. That's—" And here is the terrible thing: I opened my mouth to say *that's not a word*, and found I didn't know what I meant. The syllable sat in me like a stone in a shoe. Familiar. Wrong. *Vael.* Where had I heard—

"She was a practitioner," the hunter continued, pleasant as a tax collector. "Unlicensed. The crown has records. The crown has *many* records." He examined a hide rack with theatrical interest. "We understand she died. We understand she left effects. Books, chiefly. We'd very much like to see them."

"I wouldn't know," I said, and it was even true, and it was also the most frightened sentence I've ever spoken, because I *did* know. Or my hands did. Because that morning, before the riders came, I had found a parcel on my doorstep wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a knot I recognized the way you recognize your mother's handwriting.

I hadn't opened it yet. Some part of me had looked at that knot and thought, *not today.*

"Wouldn't know," the hunter echoed. He let the silence stretch until it creaked. "Strange thing, this valley. We've ridden through forty villages since spring. Forty villages full of gossips, drunks, and widows dying to talk. This place—" he tapped the fence post, twice, like knocking on a door—"this place is quiet. Quiet like a held breath. You notice that? Like everyone agreed not to say something, and then forgot what it was, but kept the agreement."

"We're private people," I said.

"Mm." He turned to go, then paused. "Your mistress's records list an apprentice. Unnamed. Taken in young. The entry ends with a single note in her hand." He looked back at me, and his eyes were not pleasant at all. "*He will know where I put the door.*"

He left. I stood in my yard holding a sheepskin and understanding, with total clarity, that my life as a tanner had just ended, and had possibly never really begun.

* * *

I opened the parcel that night with every lamp in the house lit, which is a coward's version of courage but counts for something.

Inside: a book. Bound in leather I recognized because I'd made its cousins—oak-tanned goat, Maren's preference, practical to the point of insult. Her grimoire. Her actual grimoire, the one she'd promised to burn and I'd promised to help her burn, and here it was, smug as anything.

And a letter, in a hand gone shaky near the end:

*Cyrus. If you're reading this, the gray men found the thread, which means I failed, which means you'll want to be angry at me, and you may be, briefly, and then you must work.*

*I bound myself into the valley's ward. Not my power—my memory. Every year of it, cut into pieces and fed to the stones, so the door would stay shut and the valley would stay invisible. It was the only payment the oath would take. I thought I would live to see it unnecessary. I was arrogant. Add it to my list.*

*Here is what we bought our peace with, boy: the valley was not always called Hollowmere. It was the last green shore of a country called Vael, and Vael did not fall. It was stolen—folded out of the world by men who wanted its magic and feared its people, and Aldric's line has been hunting the survivors ever since, generation upon generation, because we are the proof of their theft. The elders know. They have always known. They feed the ward what it asks for, and it asks for travelers, sometimes, and memories, often, and the elders call it sacrifice and sleep soundly.*

*I scattered myself to keep the door locked. Gather me back, and the door opens—and everything I am, everything I saw, comes home to this valley whether it wants it or not.*

*Do not do this alone. Trust the hunter's daughter, if she finds you. She will know why.*

*I'm sorry about the quarrel. You were right. I took the argument out of your head so you could grieve clean. It was cruel and it was kind and I refuse to apologize twice.*

*Burn nothing. Forget nothing. — M.*

I sat there until the candles drowned in their own wax. Outside, the valley lay quiet under a fat, indifferent moon—quiet like a held breath, like a village agreeing not to say something and forgetting what.

*Vael.* I said it aloud, and this time the stone in my shoe turned over, and beneath it I felt the faintest warmth, like a hearth remembered from childhood.

Then I heard footsteps in my yard. Light ones. Deliberate ones. Someone who had counted the distance between my window and the road and chosen her path accordingly.

I put out the candle and picked up the grimoire, and waited to see whether the girl at my door was my death or my errand.
