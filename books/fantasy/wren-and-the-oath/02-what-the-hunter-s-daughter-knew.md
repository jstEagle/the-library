## 2. What the Hunter's Daughter Knew

My father taught me three things before he taught me anything else: how to read a trail, how to hold my tongue, and how to lie with a straight face when the first two failed each other.

He did not teach me what to do when all three turned on me at once, standing in the yard of a tanner in Hollowmere with the moon fat overhead and my father's orders folded up inside my coat like a knife I'd swallowed.

"Name," Cyrus Galloway said through his door.

"You know my name," I said. "You've known it since I was seven and fell off your fence."

A pause. Then the bolt slid back, and there he was—taller than the boy I remembered, broader in the shoulders from the tanning vats, with ink on his fingers that had nothing to do with leather. He looked at me the way a man looks at weather he's been dreading all week and has finally arrived.

"Wren," he said. "Of course it's you. Nobody else in Aldric's service would knock politely before arresting someone."

"I'm not here to arrest you."

"That's a relief. The alternative was going to be so awkward."

I came inside because staying in the yard felt like standing in a doorway between rooms, and because my feet had apparently decided something without consulting me. That was the thing about Hollowmere I'd forgotten in nine years away: the valley made your body honest before your head caught up. The air itself leaned toward truth.

The cottage smelled of candle wax and old paper. There was a grimoire on his table—I knew what a grimoire looked like now; I'd spent six months learning, which is an education no one thanks you for—and a single chair, which he offered me with the grave courtesy of a man who has exactly one chair and knows how ridiculous that is.

"Sit," he said. "Then tell me why the hunter's daughter is in my kitchen at midnight, and why she knocked like a neighbor instead of kicking the door like her father's men do."

"My father doesn't know I'm here."

"Even better. Continue."

* * *

Here is what I told him, more or less in order, less or more honestly:

Three weeks ago, Captain Brannoc of the King's Hunt received a commission sealed with Aldric's own signet. Not the usual kind—the usual kind meant villages, hedge-witches, the sad arithmetic of the purge. This one had a name on it. A place name. *Hollowmere.*

Except Hollowmere wasn't supposed to be anywhere. I'd grown up in the hunt's camps, and I'd heard every story about this valley, and they all agreed on one point: you didn't find Hollowmere. You found a road that bent wrong and a hill that wasn't where you'd left it, and after a while you stopped looking, and you couldn't have said why. The maps had a blank space where the valley should be—not drawn blank, but *unremembered* blank, the kind of gap you only noticed when you held two maps together and saw that the roads ran into nothing on both sides of it.

"And yet," Cyrus said quietly, "here we are. Talking about it."

"Here we are," I said. "Because something cracked. My father wouldn't say what—a ward, a binding, whatever the priests call it. But for the first time in anyone's memory, the road in stayed found. So Aldric sent us. Twelve riders. My father leading." I looked at my hands. "And me ahead of them, because I'm the one who reads trails, and because—" I stopped.

"Because he trusts you," Cyrus said. "Or because he wants you watched closer than he can watch you himself."

"Both," I admitted. "My father is not a subtle man, but he is a thorough one."

"And what are his orders regarding the people of this valley?"

That was the question I'd carried up the whole road from the camp, turning it over like a stone with something under it. "Question everyone. Mark anyone who shows aptitude. Aptitude means—" I made myself finish. "Means they come back to camp with us. Everyone else signs an oath of ignorance and stays. Anyone who resists—"

"Yes. I've read enough history to guess the rest of the sentence."

"It's worse than the histories," I said, because it was, and because lying to him suddenly seemed like the most expensive thing I could do with my mouth.

Cyrus got up and stood at the window for a while, looking out at the dark shapes of his sheds, and I watched his shoulders do something that wasn't quite shaking. When he spoke again his voice was level, which cost him, and I could see it costing him, and somehow that was worse than if it hadn't.

"Maren used to say the valley was a bargain," he said. "She never said with whom, or what the price was. She just said bargains always come due, and the trick was to be holding the right end of the ledger when they did." He turned. "Wren. Why are you telling me this? Your father's men cross the ridge in two days. You're committing treason in a tanner's kitchen, and I haven't even offered you tea."

"The tea offer might have changed my mind," I said, and then stopped joking, because he wasn't asking to be charming. He was asking because he needed to understand me before he trusted me, and he had earned that much. Maybe more.

"When I was seven," I said, "I fell off your fence. You were—what, twelve? Thirteen? And Maren Voss came out and set my arm with a splint and a word I wasn't supposed to hear, and the pain went out of it like water out of a cracked cup. And your mistress looked at me—looked *into* me, the way nobody else ever has—and said, *this one will stand at a door someday and have to choose.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I thought it was a kindness. Old women say things. But I've thought about it every day since the commission came, Cyrus. Every day. Because I know which door she meant, and I know I'm standing in front of it, and I don't know yet which way I'm going to go through."

He crossed the room and put his hand on my shoulder, briefly, the way you steady something you're afraid of breaking.

"Then let me make the choice harder or easier," he said, "whichever you need. Ask me anything."

So I asked him the question I'd been carrying since the ridge, the one my father's priests would have flayed me for asking: *what is this valley?*

And Cyrus Galloway told me about a stolen country.

* * *

He told it badly and beautifully at once, the way people tell things they've only just learned themselves—half from the grimoire open on his table, half from a childhood of half-heard words in a witch's kitchen. A country called Vael, south and east beyond the moors, erased not by conquest but by *subtraction*, cut out of the world's memory so thoroughly that even its own refugees forgot they were refugees. The valley of Hollowmere was what remained: a pocket of the unmade country, kept alive by an oath, hidden behind wards paid for—

He stopped.

"Paid for with what?" I asked.

He looked at the well. Through the window I could see it, the old stone circle at the edge of his yard, older than any house in the village.

"With travelers," he said. His voice had gone very flat, the way voices go when they're holding something heavy very still. "Maren's notes are clear. The ward eats memory—it has to, that's the mechanism, it takes the memory of the valley from anyone who approaches from outside. But a ward that size needs constant feeding. So the elders made an arrangement. Travelers who wander in aren't turned away. They're welcomed. Given supper. Given a bed. And in the morning they wake up having forgotten why they came, and then they forget they forgot, and eventually—" he swallowed— "eventually they forget themselves entirely, and the ward drinks what's left. There are names in these pages, Wren. Peddlers. Pilgrims. A lost child in the year my mother was young. The elders knew. The elders *kept the ledger.*"

I sat with that. Somewhere outside, a nightbird called, ordinary as anything, as though the world hadn't just tilted.

"My father," I said slowly, "was told the valley was full of witches hiding from justice. He was told he was bringing law to lawless people."

"He was told a story that makes him the hero. They always are."

"And the people here—the ones signing oaths of ignorance—they don't know either. They think their peace is innocence."

"They think it's weather," Cyrus said. "Something that just happens. That's the cruelest part of a bargain like this—the victims get to feel virtuous."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my body had gone honest again, and my body wanted to move.

"There's something else," I said. "Something I was supposed to find before the others arrive. My father gave me a list of places to search—Maren Voss's cottage, the mill, the meetinghouse. And the well." I looked at him. "Why does a king's witch-hunter care about a village well, Cyrus?"

He went white in a way I imagine I'll see in dreams for years.

"Because she hid something in it," he said. "Because she told me once—if everything goes wrong, the beginning of me is in the well." He grabbed a lantern and the coil of rope hanging by his door, and his hands were shaking now, no longer bothering to pretend. "Come on. If the Hunt's scouts reach that water before I do—"

We went out into the cold. The well stood black against the starlight, its bucket rope worn smooth by generations of hands that never wondered what else the water might be holding. Cyrus tied off the rope and swung the lantern out over the dark mouth, and the light fell down and down and caught, far below, not stone, not water, but a small glass shape glinting like a trapped star.

And that was when I heard hooves on the north road. Early. Hours early. My father's riders, coming down into the valley with torches lit.

"Cyrus," I whispered. "They're here."
