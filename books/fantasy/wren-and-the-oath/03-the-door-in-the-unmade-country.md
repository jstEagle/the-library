## 3. The Door in the Unmade Country

The moor did not want us crossing it, and it was not shy about saying so.

Three days out from Hollowmere, the ground had turned to a kind of wet sponge that sucked at my boots with the enthusiasm of a creditor, and the wind came sideways with grit in its teeth. Wren walked ahead of me the way she did everything—like the landscape owed her an apology and she'd come to collect. Behind us, somewhere past the second ridge, Captain Brannoc and his hunters were doing their level best to prove that fatherly love survives betrayal, provided you can catch the traitor first.

"She's not stopping," Wren had told me, when we heard the horn on the first night. "My father. He'll follow us to the edge of the world."

"Good thing we're going somewhere with an edge, then."

She hadn't laughed. I'd been trying for three days to make her laugh, and I mention this now only because I would later understand it was the last of my small, stupid ambitions—the ones that fit inside a life.

The grimoire rode against my ribs in its oilcloth, and every hour it grew heavier. Not physically. Physically it weighed what books weigh. But Maren's voice was in it now, threaded through the shards we'd pried loose from well-water and church bell and one deeply undignified episode involving a beehive, and her voice weighed more than any book has a right to.

*The last is in the stones,* she'd written, in that hand like frost forming. *Where the country was unmade. Where I left myself standing.*

I did not want to know what that meant. The moor, obligingly, showed me anyway.

* * *

Wren:

You can tell a lot about a man by how he walks toward the thing he's afraid of. Cyrus walked toward the stone circle the way a man walks to his own execution trying to look like he's late for something else—shoulders up, jaw set, narrating under his breath. He narrates when he's frightened. He probably doesn't know he does it. I've decided not to tell him, because it's the only advantage I have over him anymore, and because if he stopped talking even to himself I think I'd be afraid of what was left.

The stones rose out of the moor fog like teeth. Nine of them, taller than my father, black where the weather should have worn them gray, and arranged in no pattern I could name until I stood at the center and looked up—and then they made a shape. A doorframe. Nine stones making one door, drawn on the sky.

"It's been here the whole time," I said. "Under the fog. All those years."

"That's rather the point." Cyrus set his hand against the nearest stone, and I watched the color leave his knuckles. "It's warm."

The fog moved wrong after that. It didn't drift; it *leaned*, all of it, toward the center of the circle, and where it pooled between the stones I began to see things that weren't reflections and weren't real: rooftops of pale tile, a harbor full of ships with sails like moth wings, a tower that sang—I could hear it, faintly, a note held for years without breaking. A country, showing through the tear in this one.

Vael. The word arrived in my head without anyone putting it there, the way a smell arrives before you find its source.

"Cyrus," I said. "There are people."

He looked. His face did something complicated. "No," he said softly. "Not people. Memories of people. She bound everything she remembered of Vael into these wards—all of it, every face, every street. That's what's kept Hollowmere invisible all these years. My mistress fed a whole country into the lock so a valley could sleep safe." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "And the elders helped her. They chose which travelers. You knew that part already."

I had. I'd carried it since the well, since the shard had shown me the elders' faces, calm as market-day, agreeing that strangers were a small price. I had carried it home and handed it to my father as a reason, and watched him turn it into permission.

"Take out the book," I said. "Before I start thinking about my father again."

* * *

Cyrus:

The final shard was not hidden in the stones. It was the stones—or rather, it was sitting in the middle of them in a chair that had no business existing, because chairs do not grow out of standing circles on moors, and yet there it was, plain oak, worn smooth, exactly the chair Maren had kept by her cottage fire for forty years.

Maren herself was sitting in it.

Not her. Her memory of herself, or her self wearing memory like a shawl—the distinction seemed important and also impossible to care about. She looked up at me with eyes the color of a winter stream and said, "You took your time, Cyrus Galloway."

"You died," I said.

"I got better at being elsewhere." She folded her hands. Behind her, Wren had gone very still, one hand on her knife out of pure habit, watching the ghost of a woman she'd never met judge her son-in-all-but-blood. "The door is yours to open now. You carry the gift; you always did, though you spent it mostly on cheating at cards. But you should hear the price before you pay it, because you won't get a second hearing."

"The shards said the door opens for someone willing to forget what anchors him."

"Mm. My phrasing. Let me be plainer." She rose, and the fog leaned away from her respectfully. "The door remembers Vael. To walk through it, you must become someone the door doesn't recognize—a person with no ties to this side. No valley. No cottage. No debts." Her gaze flicked, briefly, precisely, to Wren. "No names you love. You'd step into Vael whole and new, and everything that ever hurt or held you would stay here, in the wet grass, like a coat you took off."

"And you?" My voice cracked on it. "Do I forget you too?"

Something moved behind her face—the closest a memory can come to smiling. "I'm already forgotten, boy. I spent myself on the lock. What stands here is the receipt." She turned her palm up, and above it hung the binding: a knot of light shaped like a knot of light, if light could be strangled. "One turn opens the door. One blow breaks it. Open it, and you escape clean and empty while Aldric burns a valley that no longer hides. Break it, and everything I am returns to the valley—to the people who forgot what they were, so they could be safe. The wards fall with me. Hollowmere becomes visible. Hunted. Real."

"And Vael?"

"Gone either way. Countries don't come back from being spent. But their memory can go home." She looked at me the way she used to when I brought her the wrong herbs and expected praise anyway. "Choose, Cyrus. Your vanguard is arriving."

I thought she meant some phantom of hers. Then I heard the horns.

* * *

Wren:

They crested the eastern ridge in a line like a wound opening—red cloaks, white sigils, thirty of Aldric's vanguard where I'd hoped for none, and behind them, riding apart from the others the way he always rode apart, my father.

Cyrus stood at the center of the circle with his hand raised, and I saw him look at the door in the fog, and I saw him look at me, and I understood—with a clarity I will keep until I die—that both of those looks were goodbye.

"No," I said, and started toward him.

"Wren—" His voice broke in the middle of my name, which I choose to find encouraging.

"If you walk through that door without me I will hunt you in whatever country erases you. I'm my father's daughter. I'm very good at hunting."

"The door doesn't—"

"I know what it costs. I've known since the well." I reached him and put my hand flat on his chest, over the grimoire, over his hammering heart. "So don't open it. Break it. Whatever Maren was, whatever the elders spent—you said it yourself the night we ran. A peace bought with stolen things isn't peace. It's just theft with good manners."

Behind us the red cloaks were coming down the slope, and my father was ahead of them, and ahead of him, impossibly, the fog was thinning, and through the thinning I could see the tower that sang, still singing, holding its note like a promise nobody had asked it to keep.

Cyrus closed his fist around the binding of light.

"Maren," he said, "you absolute tyrant. Thank you."

He pulled.

* * *

Cyrus:

The knot came apart the way a held breath comes apart. Light went everywhere and nowhere—into the stones, into the fog, into the ground, out across the moor in a silent ring I felt in my teeth. And through me poured everything Maren had been: harbors and songs and a hundred names of rain, all of it streaming home to a valley I could no longer see but suddenly, achingly remembered.

The door in the sky went dark. The singing tower's note faded—not ended, I told myself, *carried*—and the last thing to leave was warmth, like a hand briefly on my shoulder.

Then the wards fell, and Hollowmere lay naked under the morning, and thirty witch-hunters stopped dead on the hillside as every one of them felt the exact moment the hiding ended.

My knees found the wet grass. Wren's hands found my shoulders.

"Well," I managed, "that's the escaping done."

Ahead of us, dismounting, sword drawn, face like a closing door, came Captain Brannoc—and behind him, far to the south, faint as the tower's song but coming closer, the horns of the free cities, answering a message Wren had sent weeks ago with a hope she'd never confessed to me.

They sounded, I thought dimly, almost exactly like the beginning of a war.

Or the end of one.
