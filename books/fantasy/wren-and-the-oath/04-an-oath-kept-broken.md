## 4. An Oath Kept Broken

The door to Vael looked, in the end, like nothing at all.

That was the joke of it. Maren had spent her life — and then her death, which was apparently just a different kind of schedule — building a threshold that could unmake a country from every map in the world, and when I stood before it with the last of her memories burning in my chest, what I saw was a gap between two standing stones. A gap. The kind of gap you'd step over without noticing, if you were hurrying somewhere with an armful of firewood.

"It's smaller than I expected," Wren said beside me.

"Most important things are," I said, and immediately regretted it, because it sounded like something Maren would have said, and saying it made my throat close.

The stone circle sat on the highest moor above Hollowmere, where the heather grew in colors that didn't have names, because names had been part of what was taken. The air here hummed. Not sound, exactly — more like the feeling you get when you've forgotten why you walked into a room, stretched out over a whole landscape, forever.

I could feel all seven shards now. They weren't objects anymore; they'd dissolved into me somewhere on the flight across the moors, Brannoc's hounds baying behind us, Wren's hand in mine, her father's voice cracking across the wind with her name in it like a wound. The shards lived under my sternum now, warm as coals banked for morning. Maren's memories. Her whole self, scattered through the valley like seed grain, waiting thirty years for someone with her gift to come along and gather the harvest.

And the price was written into the door itself, plain as a butcher's bill: *pass through whole, and leave behind whatever anchors you.*

I knew what anchored me. She was standing right there, breathing hard, a cut on her cheekbone from a hawthorn branch, watching me with those gray eyes that had decided my fate twice already — once when she lied to her father about my name, and once when she'd sent the message east to the free cities that was even now dragging three armies toward our valley at a pace no army should manage.

"You're doing the thing," she said. "The face you make when you're about to do something stupid."

"I don't have a face for that."

"You absolutely do. It's the one you're wearing."

Down in the valley, smoke was rising. Not from chimneys. Aldric's vanguard had reached the western farms by dawn, and I could see the witch-hunters' white banners moving up the river road like a slow infection. Behind them, further off, the free cities' columns — Wren's message made flesh, steel and oxblood banners — closing from the east. Hollowmere was about to become the place where two wars met, and its people still couldn't remember why any of it mattered, because we'd traded our memory of Vael for the privilege of being invisible, and invisibility, it turns out, is not the same as safety. It's just a slower way of disappearing.

Maren's will rose up in me then, the final shard speaking with her voice, dry as ever:

*You can go through, boy. Vael is real. It's green, and the wine is terrible, and nobody there has ever heard of King Aldric. You can have all of it. You'll walk through and forget the tannery stink and your mother's lullaby and every face you ever loved, and you'll be free.*

*Or.*

There was always an *or* with her. She'd been a teacher; *or* was how teachers kept you honest.

*Or you can break the binding. Pour everything back. Every memory I hid, every truth the elders buried, every name this valley paid to forget. The wards fall. The valley becomes visible. And everyone in it remembers exactly what they are.*

"And what happens to me?" I asked out loud, because apparently I was the sort of person who argued with dead women's voices on mountaintops.

Wren looked at me sharply. "Cyrus—"

"The wards fall," the memory said, "and the oath breaks, and the gift that carried my memories goes back to the land. You'll be a tanner again, Cyrus Galloway. Just a tanner. In a valley that's about to be very, very interesting."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about choosing: it doesn't feel like anything grand. There's no thunder. No choir. I stood in a gap between two stones on a cold moor with a girl I loved and a dead woman's voice in my ribs, and the choice felt exactly like every other choice I'd ever made — small, and obvious, and terrifying only afterward.

Because I'd already done the arithmetic. I'd done it back in Maren's cottage, the first night, reading her warning by candlelight: *the valley's peace was bought with a stolen country.* Our peace. My quiet years at the tannery, the long safe evenings, the whole drowsy contentment of Hollowmere — all of it purchased with the erasure of a nation, maintained by elders who fed travelers to the ward like feeding a hearth. I had lived comfortably inside a lie so large it had a horizon.

Wren gripped my arm. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking that if I walk through that door, I won't remember you." I said it plainly, because she deserved plain. "And some other man walks out the far side wearing my face, happy, in a green country, and he never once wonders what it cost. I don't like him. I've met him in my head all week and I don't like him."

"Cyrus, you'd be *safe*—"

"So would everyone in Vael," I said. "Safe and gone. And everyone here stays safe and hollow until Aldric finds a bigger knife." I turned back to the gap in the stones. "Maren bound herself into these wards to keep a promise. I'm going to keep the other half of it."

I put both hands against the standing stone, and I broke the binding.

It did not feel like breaking. It felt like opening a window in a house shut for thirty years — the rush of it, the smell of everything kept out finally coming in. Maren's memories poured out of me and into the valley like water finding every low place. I felt them go: the green hills of Vael, the terrible wine, the harbor bells, the names of children born under a sky that hadn't learned to hide. I felt the elders' bargain crack open like an egg. I felt the ward itself sigh — thirty years of held breath released all at once — and the hum in the air stopped, and the heather suddenly had colors with names again, and I was crying without deciding to, and Wren was holding onto me with both hands as if the world might try to take me after all.

Below us, the valley lit up. Not fire — recognition. I can't describe it better than that. From every house in Hollowmere, people came out into their doorways and stood there, remembering. Old Tam the miller dropped to his knees in his yard. The elders came out of their hall looking like men caught mid-theft, which they were, and always had been.

And the wards fell. All of them. Hollowmere stood naked on the map for the first time in a generation, with a usurper's vanguard coming up the west road and three armies of the free cities coming down the east.

* * *

What happened next, Wren claims she planned. I maintain she got lucky, but I've learned not to argue with her about credit.

Aldric's vanguard crested the western ridge at noon and found, instead of a hidden valley full of soft targets, a valley full of people who remembered exactly who they were — and behind them, banners. Oxblood and silver, the free cities' columns arriving precisely as Wren's message had promised, days earlier than Aldric's scouts thought possible, because Wren had sent word the moment she chose me over her father, and armies travel faster when they believe a country is being reborn in front of them.

Brannoc found us on the moor road that evening. He looked older than he had a week ago, and tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. He looked at his daughter a long time. Then he looked at me.

"The hunt's over," he said. "Aldric's vanguard surrendered an hour ago. Trapped between us and the cities' pikes." He said *us* like it cost him something. Maybe it did. "Your mistress's work?"

"Our work," Wren said.

He nodded slowly, and then, in the flattest voice I've ever heard a man use to say something enormous: "Then someone had better teach these people what they're defending. I'm told there's a country that needs remembering."

* * *

We rebuilt through the autumn. That's the short version. The long version involves arguments — so many arguments — about what to call the valley now, whether the old Vaelish names should come back (they should), whether the elders should be tried or merely shamed (both), and whether a tanner with no magic left in him has any business sitting on a council (I lost that one).

Some nights I miss it. The gift, I mean. I'll wake at dawn and reach for Maren's warmth under my sternum and find only my own heartbeat, ordinary as bread. Innocence doesn't grow back. I know things now that I can't unknow, about what comfort costs and who pays it, and some mornings that knowledge sits on my chest heavier than any ward ever did.

But then Wren comes in smelling of woodsmoke and rain, drops a bundle of letters from the coast — ships, actual ships, preparing to sail east toward a country that exists again — and sits down across from me, and steals my bread, and argues with me about the name of the new school.

Home kept. Innocence lost. Her, chosen, every day, over forgetting.

Yesterday a letter came from the harbormaster at Greywater, sealed in oxblood wax. Wren read it first, went very still, and handed it to me without a word.

The first ship out of Vael in thirty years had made landfall.

And aboard it, according to the manifest, traveling under no name at all — a woman, old, sharp-tongued, carrying a grimoire that matched, page for page, the one buried under my floorboards.

Maren had left through the door after all.

She was coming home.
