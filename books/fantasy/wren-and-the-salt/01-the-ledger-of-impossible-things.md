## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The dead, in Orsen Iversen's experience, were terrible gossips.

They meant no harm by it. They had simply been shut up in their bones for so long that when a knife finally touched them — a good knife, a patient hand, a man who listened the way other men listened to rain on a roof — they talked and talked and would not stop. He had carved a spoon once from an old sailor's shinbone and it had hummed sea shanties for three years until he gave it away to a fisherman's widow who found the singing comforting rather than maddening, which told Orsen something about widows he had tried very hard to forget.

This morning's work was a token for Widow Corren, who sat across the counting table with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at her own trial.

"One hour," she said. "That's all. One hour of his voice."

Orsen turned the bone in his fingers. It was a rib, cow-sized, purchased lawful and clean from the knacker's yard down the lane, which was important. Guild salt did its work through anything, but the ledger only accepted vessels with no prior tenant. You could not press a memory into bone that already had opinions.

"Which hour?" he asked, because the form required it, and because the question mattered more than most people understood.

Widow Corren's chin trembled and steadied. She had clearly rehearsed this on the walk over. "The one where he tells me about the geese."

"The geese," Orsen repeated, and began carving.

Here is what the Salt Guild sold, for those who had never needed it: borrowed years, stolen names, memories pressed into salt like flowers pressed between pages. A farmer too old to work his fields could sell twenty summers of them to a merchant who wanted youth back. A debtor could surrender the memory of his mother's face and walk free of his debts, lighter by one grief. And the dead — the dead could be spoken to, briefly, if you had the coin and a vessel and someone like Orsen, whose hands made doors where there had only been walls.

He worked the knife along the rib's curve, thin curls falling like pale snow onto the scarred tabletop. The bone was quiet under the blade. That was normal. The dead didn't speak until the carving was finished, until the vessel held its shape and waited.

"Geese," he said again, gently, as the knife moved. "Tell me about the geese, then. It helps me carve true."

"He kept geese." Widow Corren's voice went somewhere far away. "Forty years. Horrid creatures. They hated everyone but him. He used to say they could smell a bad heart, and that was why they never bit him, and our neighbor Tammas swore it was because Marten fed them brandy-soaked bread when he thought nobody was watching."

Orsen smiled despite himself. "Was anyone watching?"

"Everyone. Always. He wasn't subtle about the brandy."

The knife whispered. Somewhere behind him, in the back rooms of the counting house, the guild's clerks were weighing out this week's inventory: vials of pressed laughter, a jar labeled *SUMMER, 11 YEARS, GOOD CONDITION*, and the salt itself, coarse and gray-white, that came in sealed casks from somewhere north that appeared on no map Orsen had ever been shown. He had stopped wondering about the casks around his third year in the guild. Wondering was a door best left shut in a building full of doors.

The token took shape: a small rounded thing, fist-sized, smooth as a river stone, with a single goose etched into its belly — wings folded, head tucked, sleeping. When it was done he held it flat on his palm and waited.

It woke.

Not loudly. Not like the screaming things, thank every merciful god, the tokens cut from the bones of people who had died badly and remembered it. This one warmed against his skin and produced a sound like a man clearing his throat after a long nap, and then, faint and crackling, like wind through reeds:

*"...the geese know, Nessa. Geese always know. That one there, the white one, she can tell you've been crying..."*

"Oh," said Widow Corren, and put both hands over her mouth.

"There," Orsen said softly. "One hour, measured from the first word. The hour starts when you take it home, not before. Don't waste it listening here."

She took the token the way people took newborns, or live coals. "Will he know it's me?"

"They always do." This was true, mostly. He chose not to mention the exceptions. "And when the hour is done, it's done. No second purchase. The guild doesn't sell the same hour twice, and neither does he." He slid the ledger across the table. "Mark your name, and the price."

The price was steep. Widow Corren looked at it the way she'd already decided to look at it — straight on, without flinching — and signed. Orsen watched her leave through the front room, past the shelves of little jars and boxes, each holding somebody's yesterday, and he felt the familiar low ache that came after every such transaction. Not guilt, exactly. He had made peace with guilt years ago; they were old acquaintances who nodded to each other across taverns. It was something quieter than guilt. The ache of a man who opens doors for a living and knows that some doors should stay shut, and opens them anyway, because the person paying has nowhere else to go.

"You let her pay full rate."

Guildmistress Vess stood in the doorway to the back offices, a ledger of her own tucked under one arm and her silver-streaked hair pinned with the severity of a woman who considered vanity a form of embezzlement. She was sixty if she was a day, sharp-faced, sharp-tongued, and she had run the Greyharbor counting house for longer than Orsen had been alive, which she mentioned often enough that he suspected it was the only part of her own history she still owned outright.

"She's a widow on a fixed income," Orsen said. "I knocked a tenth off."

"I saw. Put it back."

"Vess."

"Put it *back*, Orsen. If we discount grief for one customer we discount it for all of them, and then the whole ledger's worth nothing, and then the guild sends someone from the capital to find out why, and I promise you on my mother's grave—" she paused, "—whoever she was, that you do not want the capital's auditors in this building."

There it was again. Vess spoke about her own past the way other people spoke about weather in far-off countries. *Whoever she was.* He'd asked about it exactly once, young and foolish, and she'd looked at him with an expression he'd never seen before or since — a kind of polite blankness, like a window painted over — and said, *I'm sure it was lovely,* and gone back to her accounts. He hadn't asked twice. Some silences you learned to respect the way sailors respected reefs.

He marked the correction in the ledger. The ink was the usual gray-brown, mixed with a pinch of guild salt, because every entry in the book was binding and the salt saw to it. Lie in the ledger and the lie curdled the page black within a day. In fifteen years Orsen had never once seen a page curdle.

"Anything else come in?" Vess asked, settling across from him with her own book open.

"Tide report from the marsh station. Two casks short again. And the knacker wants to know if we'll be wanting ribs next week or if we've stock enough."

"We're fine on ribs." She signed something without looking. "Oh — and the cask count. Log the shortage as spoilage, same as last time."

Orsen's pen paused above the page. "Third month running we've been two casks short."

"Yes."

"And the tally house upriver says their deliveries are exact."

"Yes."

"So either the salt is walking off by itself—"

"Orsen." Vess set down her pen. Her voice didn't rise; it never rose. It simply acquired weight, the way deep water does. "How long have you worked this house?"

"Fifteen years."

"Fifteen years. And in fifteen years, have I ever once explained where the salt comes from?"

"No."

"And have you ever asked?"

"No."

"Because I told you not to. My first week, my first day. Do you remember what I said?"

"*Never ask where the salt comes from,*" Orsen recited. "*Not because the answer will hurt you. Because the asking is how it finds you.*"

Something crossed Vess's face then, brief as a fish under ice. "Good lad," she said, and it almost sounded warm. "Now log the spoilage."

He logged the spoilage.

The rest of the morning passed in the comfortable rhythm of impossible commerce. A tanner came in to pawn the memory of his brother — *just the arguments,* he specified, *keep the fishing trips* — and Orsen spent forty minutes explaining, for the hundredth time in his career, that memories did not divide neatly along subject lines, that grief arrived bundled, that the guild sold whole hours or nothing. The tanner left angry and came back before noon, sheepish, and sold the lot. Orsen carved him a receipt-token from a pig's jaw and the jaw, being jaw, said nothing, which was a mercy.

A young clerk from the harbor office brought a message sealed in green wax and left without waiting for a reply. Orsen read it twice. *Tide-tables amended for the season. The causeway road east of the marshes will stand exposed at odd hours this month. Advise all carters to consult the new schedule.* Odd, but the marsh roads were odd; everyone knew that. He pinned it to the board and forgot it.

By late afternoon the light through the counting house windows had gone the color of honey and salt, and Orsen was alone with his knives and the day's last task: a commission he'd been putting off. The guild's own order, written in Vess's hand. *One vessel. Bone, large. Unmarked. Purpose unlisted.*

Unlisted purposes were rare and always expensive, and this one bothered him in the way a loose thread bothers a weaver. Every vessel had a purpose; that was how you carved toward it. A voice-token curved inward, cradling. A memory-jar was all smooth containment. But this order said *nothing*, and so his knife had nothing to aim at, and the result — three days of work now — was the strangest thing he had ever made.

He picked it up from the corner of the bench where it had been sitting all week, sulking.

It was beautiful. He could admit that without false modesty. A long oval of dense white bone, polished to a soft luster, carved with no image at all — no goose, no name, no sigil. Just shape. Just silence.

And it was silent. That was the wrongness of it. Bone always murmured. Even fresh-killed stock hummed faintly with the animal's dumb contentment. But this piece, this perfect thing his own hands had made, gave back nothing. He held it to his ear like a seashell and heard only his own blood.

*Purpose unlisted.* He turned it over. *Vessel, unmarked.*

For the first time in fifteen years, Orsen Iversen looked at something he had carved and felt it looking back.

The bell over the front door rang.

He set the silent bone down — carefully, the way you set down a sleeping snake — and called out that the house was closing, but the footsteps that came up the front room were wrong for a customer. Too uneven. Wet-sounding. A smell reached him ahead of the visitor: brine and weed and something underneath it, iron-dark, that raised every hair on his arms.

The stranger appeared in the doorway to the counting room swaying like a man standing on a deck, half-drowned gray, seawater pooling under him on Orsen's clean floor. His eyes were fixed on Orsen with an urgency that burned right through the blue of his lips.

"Bone-carver," the man rasped. "They said — Greyharbor — a carver who *hears*—"

"Sit down before you fall down," Orsen said, already reaching for him, and that was when the stranger grabbed his wrist with a grip far too strong for a dying man and pressed something hard and salt-crusted into his palm.

"Take it north," the stranger whispered. "Before the king forgets there's a north."

And then his eyes rolled back, and his knees went, and the last thing Orsen Iversen heard from him — before the body hit the floor, before Vess came running, before any of it — was not a cry for help, or a prayer, or even a name for help to find.

It was the king's name.

His *true* one.
