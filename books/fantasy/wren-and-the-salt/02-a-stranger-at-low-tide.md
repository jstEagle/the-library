## 2. A Stranger at Low Tide

The tide came in on a Tuesday, which Orsen had always considered its most inconsiderate quality. Tuesdays were ledger days. Ledger days were the only days the counting house was quiet enough to hear what the tokens wanted to say, and he resented anything that interrupted them — clients, weather, fire, and now, apparently, drowning men.

The stranger came through the guild's doorway the way water comes under a door: all at once, and without manners. He hit the floor of the entry hall face-first, which made a sound like a dropped saddle, and lay there steaming.

Orsen set down his knife. He set down the femur he'd been carving — a token for the Widow Caslin, one hour of her husband's voice, commissioned and half-paid — and he looked at the man on the floor with the mild irritation of someone whose afternoon has just developed a complication.

"You're dripping on the accounts," he said.

The man did not apologize. He did, however, move, which put him ahead of several visitors Orsen could name. He rolled onto his back and Orsen got his first proper look at him: youngish, or shaped youngish, the way drowned things were. Salt had gotten into everything — crusted white along his hairline, furred on his lips, packed under his nails like he'd been clawing his way out of the sea itself. His eyes were open and working, and they fixed on Orsen with an urgency so naked it was almost embarrassing, the way grief is embarrassing when you see it on a stranger.

"Carver," the man rasped.

"I'm a bone-carver, yes. If you're here about the posting, we filled it."

The man laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought up a quantity of brine that Orsen decided not to think about too closely. Then the man's hand shot out and caught Orsen's wrist with a grip that had no business belonging to a dying man. It belonged to a live one, a desperate one, the kind of grip you only get from someone who has decided that you, specifically, are going to be the last person they ever talk to, and by god you are going to listen.

"Listen," the man said, which was unfair, because Orsen hadn't said anything.

"I'm listening."

"The map." His other hand went to his chest, to the sodden folds of his coat, and came out with something wrapped in oilskin and stiff with salt — a folded thing, cracked at the creases, white as a fish's belly. He pressed it into Orsen's hands and folded Orsen's fingers over it, one by one, with terrible ceremony, like a priest distributing something holy or a gambler hiding an ace.

"Take it north. The road that isn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. Orsen watched his throat work against the salt crust. "The tower. You'll know it. A carver will know it."

"I don't take commissions without seeing the piece first," Orsen said, because when he was frightened — and he was beginning to suspect, distantly, academically, that he might be frightened — he retreated into shop-talk the way other men retreated into drink.

The stranger smiled. It was the worst thing Orsen had seen all year, that smile: relieved. The man had been carrying whatever this was alone across half a drowned country, and now he'd handed it to a stranger in an apron, and the relief on his face was the relief of a man setting down a load he'd known would break him.

"His name," the stranger whispered.

"Whose name?"

"The king's. His true—" And then the man said it.

Orsen would spend a great deal of time afterward trying to describe what happened in the next three seconds, and he never managed it to his own satisfaction. The name went into the room and the room *leaned*. The lamps guttered toward the stranger like flowers toward sun. Every carved thing on the shelves — three hundred tokens, forty reliquaries, the little ivory wren Orsen kept for no reason he'd admit to — every one of them *shivered*, the way a struck bell shivers after the sound stops. As if they had all, briefly, turned to look.

Then the stranger exhaled, long and slow, like a tide going out, and died on the Salt Guild's entry floor with his hand still resting on Orsen's wrist.

* * *

The dead, in Orsen's experience, did not scream.

They murmured. They gossiped, rather — bones were chatty in aggregate and shy one at a time, and a finished carving held its memories the way a jug holds water: pourable, if you knew where to tap. In eleven years Orsen had heard the dead complain about the cold, correct each other's dates, sing fragments of songs no living person remembered, and once, memorably, demand a better class of wood. He had never, in eleven years, touched remains and heard them *scream*.

He touched the stranger's remains because Guild law required him to confirm death before Vess sent for the watch, and also because his hands did it before his brain finished debating. That was the trouble with hands that talked to bones. They had opinions. They reached.

The scream started in the fingerbones and went up.

It wasn't sound, exactly. It lived behind his eyes, in the meat of his ears, in his back teeth — a shriek with no pitch, a scream of pure refusal, as if the whole skeleton, every jointed inch of it, had been holding one enormous note of *no* since the moment of death and Orsen's touch had finally given it somewhere to put it. He snatched his hand back. The scream cut off. The silence afterward rang worse than the screaming.

"Well," Orsen said, to nobody, in a voice an octave above its usual register.

Vess arrived the way she always did: already there. She had a talent for being present at the exact moment her presence became useful, and Orsen had privately theorized she practiced in a mirror. She stood in the entry hall doorway in her gray guild-wool, hands folded, looking down at the corpse with an expression Orsen could not read, which was unusual. He could usually read Vess the way he read everything — easily, comfortably, like large print.

"Drowned?" she said.

"Salt-drowned. There's a difference." He heard himself say it before he thought better of it. "I've read about it. Lungs full of brine, but not seawater. It's — concentrated. Somehow."

"Hm," said Vess, which was how she filed things she intended never to discuss again. Her eyes moved from the corpse to Orsen's hands, still curled around the oilskin bundle at his side, and Orsen watched her notice, and watched her decide to notice later. "Watch will come at the turn of the hour. Anything he carried is guild property; you know the rule. Inventory it and bring it to my study."

She turned to go, then paused, and looked back, and for one strange moment her face did something complicated and old.

"And Orsen. Whatever he said to you — names travel badly in this house. Let this one lie where he dropped it."

She left. Orsen stood over the dead man, holding a salt-stiffened map he was absolutely not going to surrender, and realized with a small, cold, clarifying shock that he had just decided to disobey her.

* * *

Here was the thing about lying: Orsen had genuinely never done it. Not well, not often, not really. His gift made it impractical. When your bones gossip through your carvings and your carvings gossip through you, you develop an early and lasting respect for the truth's convenience. Besides, Vess had raised him in the trade since he was nine years old and caught carving his dead father's knucklebones into dice, and you didn't lie to someone who taught you your letters and your edge angles both.

So when she asked, an hour later, over the inventory table with the watchmen's lanterns swinging shadows across the wall, Orsen discovered that lying is not a skill but a decision, and that he had already made it, sometime between the scream and now, without consulting himself.

"Map, waterskin, a broken compass, eleven coppers, and a knife," he recited, laying each item out with a craftsman's neatness. "No papers. No seal. Coat's guild-cut but the marks have been picked off."

"The map."

"Chart, more properly. Coastal. Salt-bloody and mostly illegible." He slid it across the table face-down, heart performing a slow, traitorous drumroll, and Vess picked it up and turned it over and unfolded it, and Orsen watched her read it and felt — this was the part that frightened him — *nothing*. No flicker. No guilt-sweat. Just the smooth, warm satisfaction of a joint fitted true.

Because it wasn't the map.

It was a chart. It *was* coastal. He'd spent the intervening hour in his workshop with a blank sheet of vellum, copying — badly, deliberately badly, smudged and mislabeled — the coastline from the real map, which currently lay wrapped in oilskin inside a hollowed-out knucklebone of the dead man's own femur, because even a guilty conscience has a sense of humor. The stranger's bones screamed at every touch except, curiously, that one. That one had gone quiet as a held breath, as if the bone wanted the map carried and was willing to hold still to make it happen.

Bones didn't want things. That was the whole architecture of Orsen's life: bones remembered, bones spoke, but wanting was a living faculty. He turned this over in his mind like a stone with an odd weight to it and could not make it sit right.

Vess set the false chart down. She studied it for a long moment, and then she studied him, and Orsen met her eyes with the calm of a man committing his first crime and finding the paperwork surprisingly easy.

"He came from the north road?" she asked.

"Tide brought him. Ask the tide."

Something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion. Suspicion he could have handled. This was closer to fear, and it was aimed, oddly, past him rather than at him, the way you flinch at something standing just behind someone's shoulder.

"Burn the coat," she said finally. "And Orsen — the body. I'll have the enforcers collect the remains for the rendering ovens tonight. Standard procedure for unclaimed dead."

Standard procedure. Rendering ovens. Bones boiled down to stock for tokens, memories rendered into salt, the guild's great closed loop, the trade that worked and paid and fed them all. Eleven years Orsen had walked past those ovens without asking the question Vess had forbidden on his first day: *where does the salt come from?*

Tonight, for the first time, the question had teeth. Because the dead man's bones had screamed *no*, and because one bone among them had gone quiet and helpful and *wanting*, and because in eleven years of listening to the dead murmur and grumble and sing, Orsen had never once heard any of them beg.

He was going to hear it tonight. He could feel it already, the way you feel weather coming in a scarred joint. Upstairs in his workshop, wrapped in oilskin inside a hollowed femur, a dead man's map lay quiet as a held breath, waiting for him to finish the shift, bank the fires, and climb the stairs.

The dead man had said a name that made the lamps lean. Orsen meant to find out why — and the femur on his workbench, he suspected, was ready to tell him.
