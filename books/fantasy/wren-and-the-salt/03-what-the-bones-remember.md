## 3. What the Bones Remember

The femur was heavier than Orsen expected.

He'd worked bone his whole life — cattle knuckles and whale ribs and the little finger-bones of saints that the guild paid scandalous sums for — but he had never held a man's leg in his hands before, and he was surprised to find it felt less like a tool and more like a question.

He sat at his workbench with the shutters drawn, a single tallow candle burning low, the stranger's femur laid across two folded cloths like something sleeping. Outside, the tide was going out of Saltrow, and the city made its nightly sounds: gulls arguing over fish heads, the guild bell tolling the hour of ledgers, someone three streets away weeping into their wine about money or love, which were often the same debts here.

"Right," Orsen said to the bone. "Let's see what you know."

Carving was listening. That was the first thing Vess had taught him, back when he was eleven and all elbows and ink-stains. *You don't put the memory into the token, boy. It's already there. The dead leave it behind the way a snail leaves slime on a wall. Your job is only to make a door.* He had never entirely liked her phrasing, but he couldn't argue with the results. A widow came in wanting an hour of her husband's voice; Orsen carved her a sparrow from his shinbone, and when she held it to her ear she heard him grumbling about the price of bread, and she laughed, and then she cried, and then she bought a second hour for next month, because grief, like everything else in Saltrow, was best purchased on credit.

So he took up his smallest knife and set the blade against the femur's pale length, and listened.

The first thing he heard was rain.

Not Saltrow rain, which fell sideways and smelled of brine and old rope. This was inland rain, fat and slow, drumming on leaves. A road went north through the sound of it, muddy under boots, and someone was humming — a walking song, tuneless, the kind a man hums when he has been alone long enough that his own voice is company.

Orsen carved a shallow groove along the bone and the rain grew louder. The humming resolved into words:

*North past the standing stones where the road forgets itself. North until the land turns white.*

"The land doesn't turn white northward," Orsen murmured. "It turns to bog."

*Until the land turns white,* the memory insisted, patient as sediment. And then — a turn, a shift, the way dreams slide from one room into another without benefit of doors — and Orsen was looking at a tower.

A tower of white salt.

It rose out of the earth like a tooth out of a jaw, smooth and terrible, catching light that didn't come from any sun Orsen knew. Around its base, fields. Not wheat fields. Fields of salt, white to every horizon, and men moving through them bent double, harvesting, and the way they moved was wrong — too slow, too obedient, like puppets whose strings had been replaced with habit.

And above, at the tower's crown, small with distance, a figure stood wearing gold.

Orsen's knife slipped. He swore, stuck his thumb in his mouth, and the vision collapsed back into candlelight and rain-sound.

"Careful," he told himself around the thumb. "You're not being paid for this."

Except he was, in a way. He was being paid in a dead man's last breath against his ear, in the weight of a salt-stiff map hidden beneath his floorboard, in a name whispered with the stranger's final strength: *the king's true name*, and the word *crown*, and the word — Orsen still wasn't sure he'd heard it right — *hunger*.

* * *

He worked until the candle drowned in its own wax. What he got from the femur came in pieces, the way you get stories from a drunk uncle: fragments, vivid, unmoored from any order that made sense.

*A road north.* Certain.

*A tower of white salt, mined from a grave.* Less certain — the image shivered whenever he reached for it, as if the memory itself were afraid of what it remembered.

*A man in a crown, kneeling in the salt, begging.* This one Orsen got whole, and it was the worst of them, because the man's face kept changing. Old, young, middle-aged, bearded, clean-shaven — the features wouldn't hold, sliding like wet paint — but the voice stayed the same, cracked and dignified and utterly spent, saying over and over:

*Please. Please, I want to go home. Please, let me forget my mother's face, take it, take it, I don't want it anymore —*

"No," Orsen said aloud, to the empty room. "That's not how forgetting works. You don't get to choose what they take."

But of course people did choose, all the time, in Saltrow. That was the trade. They chose badly, desperately, and the guild profited, and Orsen carved the tokens and tried not to think about the arithmetic of it. He had always been good at not thinking. It was practically a craft, and he'd served his apprenticeship in it same as anything else.

The last fragment came near dawn, and it came with Nell.

He knew the stranger's name by now — it had surfaced the second night, attached stubbornly to a memory of a woman laughing at him for losing a boot in a river — Nell Ferris, courier, thirty-one years dead as of four days ago, currently residing in a femur on Orsen's workbench. She was not a ghost exactly. Ghosts haunted places. Nell haunted the bone the way music haunts an instrument: present only when played.

She showed him the capital. Not the palace — the streets below it, ordinary streets, a market, a fountain where children were selling cups of water to each other because even children in the capital understood commerce. And through it all, threaded under everything like a worm in an apple, a feeling: *wrongness*. The sense Orsen sometimes got from certain tokens, certain carvings that came back to the guild unsold because buyers said they made their teeth ache. The feeling of something eating.

"It's in the palace," Nell said, using his own mouth, which was disconcerting. Her voice through him sounded like his voice with all the warmth boiled out. "It's been in the palace for sixty years. Maybe longer. It wears them, carver. One after another. And nobody notices, because who looks at a king and thinks, ah yes, that's not a man, that's a glove?"

"Why tell me?" Orsen asked the candle. "Why me?"

Silence. Then, faintly, from the bone, almost amused: *You lied for me. First lie of your life. I figured you were ready.*

"I am not *ready*—"

Three sharp knocks at his door.

Orsen went very still. Nobody knocked at his door at dawn except Vess, and Vess had a key, and Vess did not knock. These knocks were the knocks of a man whose job description included doors.

"Master Yarrow." A voice like gravel poured slowly. "Guild business. Open, please."

There was a second set of footsteps behind the first. Guild enforcers traveled in pairs, like bookends, like bad news.

Orsen looked down at the femur. At the map's hiding place beneath the loose floorboard by the hearth — too obvious, far too obvious now that he thought about it, the board's edges worn shiny from his nervous fingers these past two nights. At the window, three floors up, over the refuse-strewn alley, doable if one were young, foolish, or motivated.

He was, it turned out, all three.

"One moment," he called, pitching his voice into the bleary register of a man just woken, "I'm — decent-ish."

He swept the femur into his shirt. He yanked up the floorboard. And then he stopped, map in hand, because a thought arrived with the clarity of a struck bell:

They would search everything. His bench. His drawers. His finished tokens, his blanks, the jar of teeth he hadn't gotten around to sorting. Everything carved, everything hearable. But not —

In the corner, half-finished, stood the piece he'd been picking at for a year: a length of whalebone he'd been carving into a walking stick for no customer, no reason, only because his hands wanted work in the evenings. And it was silent. It had always been silent. Every other thing his knives had ever touched spoke to him — hummed, muttered, wept, sang — but this one gave back nothing, a stone-deaf stick, and he'd kept working on it partly out of stubbornness and partly because the silence was restful, like a room with no clock.

He didn't know why it was silent. He suspected, dimly, that someday he would need to know.

For now: he rolled the map tight, slid it into the hollow he'd already begun reaming out of the stick's core — a carver's trick, a place to hide things from wives and tax collectors — and plugged it with a whittled stopper, and ran his thumb along the seam, and heard nothing. Silence, holding a secret. The first object of his life that could keep its mouth shut.

He envied it a little.

The knocking resumed, less polite. Orsen tucked the stick among a forest of umbrellas by the door, opened up, and arranged his face into aggrieved innocence, an expression he had practiced in the mirror and which several acquaintances assured him came naturally.

* * *

The enforcers were named Brakk and Soth, or possibly Soth and Brakk; they were large, gray-uniformed, and identical in the manner of furniture. They turned his rooms inside out with a thoroughness Orsen found almost flattering. Brakk held up the jar of teeth. "What are these?"

"Inventory. Teeth remember names better than any other bone. Very popular with people who've forgotten who owes them what."

Soth sniffed one, grimaced, put it back. Brakk patted down his mattress, checked under the floorboards — Orsen's heart performed a small acrobatic routine — and found nothing but dust and one indignant spider. Neither so much as glanced at the umbrellas. Nobody in Saltrow had ever stolen an umbrella; it was the one honest industry the city had.

"Guildmistress says you were the last to see the drowned man," Brakk said.

"I was the last to see everyone. I keep the shop."

"He give you anything?"

"A cough, mostly. Terrible wet one. You could hear it from the harbor."

Soth looked at him a long moment with eyes like nails. Orsen looked back with the serene blankness of a man who has nothing to hide, which required more effort than he would have believed possible. Lying, he discovered, was exhausting. How did people do this daily? How did the guild run an entire economy on it?

They left at last, taking only the jar of teeth ("evidence," said Brakk, though of what, neither seemed sure), and Orsen bolted the door and leaned against it and breathed.

From inside his shirt, muffled, dry as paper: *"Nice cough."*

"Don't," Orsen said. "Don't talk to me. I'm having a crisis."

*"You're talking to a leg bone in your nightshirt. Crisis achieved."*

He laughed — a short, startled bark of laughter, the first in four days — and then pressed his hand over his mouth as if the enforcers might hear it through the door and come back to confiscate it too.

* * *

Vess summoned him at noon.

Her office sat above the counting house, all dark wood and darker ledger-spines, and she sat behind her desk like a queen behind a wall, small and iron-gray and unreadable. She had sold most of her own past to the guild decades ago — everyone knew this, nobody said it — and whatever remained lived somewhere behind those eyes, doing its work in the dark.

"You lied to Brakk," she said.

"I misdirected Brakk. Lying is different. Lying has intent."

"Orsen." She folded her hands. "Four days ago a man died in our doorway carrying something that has the entire council frightened. Something worth more than this building, than the harbor, possibly than the harbor *towns*. If you have it—"

"I don't."

"—then the wisest thing you can do is bring it to me, and I will make the problem disappear, and you will go back to carving sparrows for widows, and in ten years none of this will ever have happened." She smiled, thin as a coin's edge. "You of all people know how well forgetting sells."

And that was the trap in it, laid bare and gleaming. He could hand it over and be erased — kindly, gently, painlessly, the guild's specialty. He could go back to the warm gray comfort of not thinking.

He thought about the man in the crown, kneeling in the salt, offering up his mother's face like a beggar offering his last coin. *Take it, take it, I don't want it anymore.* About a king worn like a glove, one after another, while the whole country watched and saw only a man.

"No," Orsen said. "I don't have it. And if I did—" His heart hammered. Second lie of his life, arriving already fluent. "If I did, I'd want to know where the salt comes from first. You always told me never to ask. I'm asking."

Something moved behind Vess's eyes — brief, buried, like a fish under ice. For one strange moment she looked almost afraid, or almost hopeful; with her, he suspected, they had become the same expression.

"Get out of my office," she said quietly.

He went. And that night, walking stick in hand and dead woman at his ear, Orsen Yarrow packed a bag, left the ledger unlocked on his desk where anyone might read it, and slipped out into the ebbing dark toward the tidal road — the one that existed only between moonset and dawn, the one that wasn't on any map but his.
