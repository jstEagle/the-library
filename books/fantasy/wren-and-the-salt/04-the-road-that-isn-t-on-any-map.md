## 4. The Road That Isn't on Any Map

Orsen left the city before the bells finished counting four, with a satchel of carving knives, a loaf going stale, and a bone that argued with him.

"You're walking," said the bone. "You're actually walking. I died for this map and you're carrying it in a *bread bag*."

He had made the carved bone himself three winters ago — a small hollow figure of a wading bird, smooth as a river stone. It was the only thing he'd ever whittled that said nothing back. He'd kept it on his shelf as a curiosity, a silence he could hold. Now it held Nell instead: the stranger's echo, folded into the one object Orsen's gift couldn't touch, which apparently meant she could talk all she liked.

"It's not a bread bag," Orsen muttered, hunching his shoulders against the harbor wind. "It's a provision satchel."

"There's a heel of loaf sticking out of it."

"That's the provision."

The tide was out far enough to show the causeway — a long spine of dark stone running west from the mudflats, slick and glistening under a sky the color of dishwater. Most maps didn't have it. The ones that did called it the Drowned Road and advised prayer. It surfaced twice a day, when the sea drew breath, and between those hours it belonged entirely to the water.

Orsen stepped onto it anyway.

"Left foot first," Nell said. "Superstition. My grandmother swore by it."

"I don't have a grandmother anymore. I gave her up when I joined the guild." He meant it as a joke. It came out flat, because everything came out flat lately, ever since a dying man had screamed the king's name into his hands and Vess had looked at him across the counting house with eyes like scales.

"You lied to her," Nell said, quieter now. "The guildmistress. You told her you'd never seen me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Orsen walked six paces before answering. The stones were treacherous, furred with weed, and the wind pushed at him like a creditor. "I don't know," he admitted. "That's the trouble. In thirty-one years I have never once lied to Vess, and then you put a map in my hands and I lied the way other people breathe."

"Maybe you're finally interesting," said Nell.

"Maybe I'm finally damned."

* * *

Nell remembered dying the way you remember a door slamming in another room — you knew it happened, you could even name the sound, but you couldn't make yourself feel it again. What she remembered more was the water. Cold like a hand closing. And then, impossibly, warmth: being poured into something small and smooth, a little bird of bone, and finding she could still think, which struck her as either a miracle or an administrative error.

She had been a courier. That much survived. She'd carried things — letters, ledgers, once a ring worth more than her father's boat — from the capital to the coast and back, riding hard and sleeping rough and liking both. And then someone had learned what she carried last, and the sea had gotten her, or the men who worked for the crown had gotten her and *then* the sea, and the order of events no longer mattered because she was dead either way.

What mattered was that the map was real, and the man carrying it walked like a man reading a ledger with his feet.

"You're doing it wrong," she told him, as the light went long and copper over the flats. "The road. You're hugging the seaward edge."

"The seaward edge is drier."

"The seaward edge is where the water comes back *first*. It cuts the corner off the road near the old beacon. Stay to the landward side or you'll be swimming with your boots on."

"How do you know this road?"

"Because I've ridden it eleven times, mostly at night, mostly with people chasing me." A pause, and when she spoke again there was something brittle underneath the dryness. "I knew it alive. That's mine, do you understand? Whatever else they took, I rode that road myself, with my own knees and my own fear. Don't you dare go telling me it belongs to the bone."

Orsen stopped walking. Gulls wheeled over the mudflats, screaming their small opinions.

"I wasn't going to tell you anything," he said.

"You were thinking it. I can hear you thinking. It sounds like arithmetic."

"That's insulting to arithmetic."

But he moved to the landward side of the road, and after a while, grudgingly, so did the conversation.

* * *

They argued about memory the way married couples argue about money — constantly, seriously, and without any hope of resolution.

"You carve the dead into tokens," Nell said, as dusk came down and Orsen ate his stale loaf in grim, efficient bites. "You press them into salt and sell them by the hour. An hour of a husband's voice. An hour of a mother's lullaby. Who says those hours are yours to sell?"

"The dead say. Mostly." Orsen brushed crumbs from his coat. "Grief is a hunger, and the guild feeds it. I've never pretended it was holy work."

"But whose grief is it? The widow buys her husband's voice — fine. But does the husband get a say? Maybe he spent his whole marriage wishing she'd let him finish a sentence, and now she's bought him by the hour and he can't even die properly without her booking appointments."

Orsen considered this. "You're angrier than most echoes."

"I'm angrier than *any* echoes. I'm the only echo you've got, and I'm furious, so your sample size is ruined." The bone-bird sat in his palm, and though it had no face, Orsen felt it looking at him. "Here's my question, carver. When you pressed me into this thing — did you ask?"

"You were already in it. I didn't press you anywhere. You came."

"Convenient."

"It's true. You're the only voice I've ever heard that I didn't put there myself. Do you know what that's like? Thirty years of hearing the dead through my own hands, and every single one of them arrived through my craft, shaped by my knives, speaking in my grammar. And then you. Uninvited. Uncarved." He turned the little bird over. "You're the first thing I've ever made that isn't mine."

Nell was quiet for a long moment. Out on the flats, the tide had begun its slow turn; far off, the sea stood up like a wall deciding whether to be polite.

"Well," she said at last, less sharp. "That's almost romantic, if you weren't so gloomy about it."

"I'm not gloomy. I'm precise."

"You're a man who talks to bones and calls it precision. Gloomy. Definitely gloomy."

* * *

Night caught them at the old beacon — a stump of masonry rising from the road like a broken tooth, and beyond it, exactly as Nell had promised, the sea had already taken the corner of the causeway, black water sliding greedily over the stones.

"We shelter here till the next low tide," Orsen said. "Then we walk the rest before dawn."

"'We,'" Nell repeated. "Listen to him. Yesterday I was a corpse screaming in his hands. Today I'm a 'we.'"

"You're a we with excellent local knowledge."

"And you're a man fleeing his entire life with a bread bag." But there was no bite in it. "Ask me why I took the job, carver. Ask me what I was carrying besides the map."

Orsen settled against the beacon wall, cold seeping up through his coat, and asked.

"A name," Nell said. "Not the king's true name — that I only learned by accident, at the end, when the man who hired me was past caring who heard. No. I was carrying proof that the crown has been worn before. Four kings, carver. Four. And every one of them went into it whole and came out empty, and the guild knows, and the guild sells the scraps." Her voice thinned. "There's a ledger. There's always a ledger. You keep one, don't you? For the Salt Guild?"

"I keep the ledgers."

"Then somewhere in your neat little columns is the price of a king's childhood. Sold by the pound."

Orsen stared out at the dark water and found, uncomfortably, that he could see it: the entry, the careful figures, the trade recorded in his own handwriting. He had written down impossible things for nine years and never once asked where the salt came from. Vess had told him not to ask. He had told himself that obedience was professionalism.

"I didn't know," he said.

"No," Nell agreed. "But you suspected. Suspecting is how you people sleep."

* * *

He slept badly, and dreamed of the counting house flooded to the rafters, every ledger page dissolving into brine, and Vess standing waist-deep among them saying *you should have asked, you should have asked*, in a voice that was also his mother's, also the widow's, also Nell's.

He woke to gray pre-dawn and the bone-bird humming.

"What are you doing?"

"Waking you. The tide's turning. Also I've been thinking." Nell sounded almost sheepish, which for her was clearly an enormous concession. "About ownership. You said I'm the first thing you ever made that isn't yours. Fine. Then here's my counteroffer, carver. The map shows a road north and a tower of white salt, but maps lie by omission — mine certainly did, or I'd have planned better than to die in a harbor. So: you carry the map, and I carry the road. Eleven crossings of the Drowned Road, the smugglers' paths above Greywater, the ford at Saltmarket that isn't marked because the toll-keepers bribe cartographers. You want to reach the capital before your king forgets his own face? Then we're partners. Not passenger and porter. Partners."

Orsen stood, joints cracking, and looked north along the re-emerging causeway, where the first light was picking out the wet stones all the way to the horizon.

"Partners," he said. "With terms."

"Oh, there are terms. Term one: you stop apologizing to me in your head. It's insufferable. Term two: when this is done — whatever 'done' means for someone in my condition — you carve me something honest. Not a token. Not an hour for sale. Something that's just *mine*."

"And term three?"

"Term three," said Nell, "is that when we reach Saltmarket, you keep your hood up and your hands in your pockets, because I've seen what the guild does in towns like that, and you haven't. Yet."

Orsen picked up his satchel, checked the map inside it — dry, thank the tides — and set out along the road while the sea was still holding its breath.

Behind them, far to the south, back along the dark causeway they'd just abandoned, a lantern flickered. Small. Steady. Moving at the pace of someone who walked these stones often and never hurried.

"Carver," Nell said softly. "Tell me that's a fisherman."

Orsen watched the light come on, patient as bookkeeping.

"It isn't a fisherman," he said.
