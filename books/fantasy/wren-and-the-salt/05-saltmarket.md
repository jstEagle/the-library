## 5. Saltmarket

Saltmarket smelled the way a lie smells when it's been left out in the sun too long — sweet at first, then not.

Orsen had known, in an abstract and comfortable way, that the guild had outposts beyond the capital. Ledgers mentioned them. Columns of figures. *Saltmarket, quarter-day purchases: 412 units.* He had copied those columns for six years with ink-stained fingers and no questions at all, because Vess said questions were like salt water: fine in the sea, ruinous everywhere else.

But 412 units was a number. This was a town.

The guild house stood where the market square narrowed, and unlike every other building in Saltmarket — crooked, salt-scabbed, leaning on its neighbors like tired drunks — it was white. New-cut stone. Clean as a tooth. Above its door hung the guild's sign: three waves beneath a set of scales, carved so finely Orsen could see the individual crests of the waves even from across the square.

He knew that carving. He'd made one just like it. Every apprentice did; it was the first thing they learned, the guild's calling card, and the bone used was always their own — a sliver from the little finger, taken with consent, because everything in the trade was technically consensual if you squinted hard enough in poor light.

*Hood up,* Nell reminded him from inside his coat pocket, her voice coming up through the carved bone she lived in now, thin and dry as wind through reeds. *Hands in pockets. And carver — don't look at the queue.*

There was a queue. It wound out of the guild house door and along the street, and it was quiet, which was the worst part. Markets were supposed to be loud. Fishwives should have been shouting prices, children underfoot, gulls committing petty theft. Instead there was only this slow, shuffling line of people holding numbered tokens, waiting to sell something they couldn't hold in their hands.

Orsen looked anyway. Nell had known he would.

A man near the front held a child on his shoulders — a boy of perhaps seven, swinging his legs, bored in the universal way of bored children. When they reached the door, the boy slid down and went in first. Orsen watched through the open doorway as the child stood on a stool to reach the counter and pushed a token across the wood.

"Name's Pip," the clerk said, in the pleasant, unhurried voice of a man reading a familiar recipe. "Age seven. Selling what, this morning?"

"Me birthday," Pip announced. "All of 'em."

"That's all seven? We can take the lot or you keep the last one for yourself."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a merchant weighing cargo. "Keep the last one," he decided. "Mum says I'll want to remember being small someday." A pause. "Will it hurt?"

"Not a bit," said the clerk warmly. "You'll just wake up tomorrow and be eight years old, and won't that be nice? Growing fast is good luck."

The counter bell rang once, bright as a spoon against a cup. Somewhere in the back of the guild house, something was pressed into salt. The price of seven birthdays, Orsen knew from the ledgers, was enough to feed a family for a winter.

It was also, he understood now, standing very still in the square, the entire reason the family needed feeding. The guild bought cheap and sold dear, and what it sold was people to themselves. Somewhere downstream, a rich woman was buying the warm memory of a birthday cake and seven candles, and Pip would grow up knowing only that he'd been hungry in a way food didn't fix.

*Carver.* Nell's voice had gone strange. *That's how they got me. Did you know that? Not stolen. Sold. My sister was sick, and I sold her my memories of our mother so she'd have something to keep, and then I sold the ones of my sister too, because after the first sale I couldn't remember why I'd wanted them.*

Orsen's throat tightened. "I'm sorry."

*Don't be sorry. Be angry. Anger keeps better than grief — less salt in it.*

Further down the queue, an old woman sat on a crate beside the guild house wall, though she wasn't in line. She simply sat, wrapped in a shawl the color of old kelp, watching the door with the patient attention of someone waiting for a person who had gone inside. A younger man came out eventually, empty-handed, carrying a paper packet — payment — and knelt in front of her.

"Mam," he said gently. "Come home now."

She smiled at him, polite and distant, the smile you give a kind stranger. "That's very good of you, love, but I'm waiting for my son. He'll be along soon. He always comes on market day."

"I'm here, Mam. I'm Tam—"

"You're a sweet boy, but Tam has brown eyes." She patted his hand kindly. "Yours are blue. You wait with me a while, if you like. He wouldn't mind."

The man closed his eyes. Orsen saw him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Orsen himself had been doing since the causeway, the terrible sums the ledger never showed. The woman had sold her memories years ago, whole seasons at a time, to buy bread during the famine winters. Her son had been sold out of her head piece by piece, and what remained was a shape with no face: *a son, brown-eyed, comes on market day.* She was waiting forever for a boy she'd already given away.

The son — Tam — helped her stand and walked her toward a narrow house across the square, moving slowly, matching her shuffle. At his door he settled her into a chair by the window facing the guild house, so she could keep her vigil, and came back out alone. He stood in the middle of the square for a long moment, looking at nothing.

Then he looked at Orsen. Really looked — at the hood, the satchel, the way a man stands when he's carrying something he shouldn't be.

"You're not selling," Tam said, crossing over. His voice was flat, tired, but his eyes were quick and blue and angry. "Sellers don't stand still. Sellers count their steps."

"I'm passing through."

"Nobody passes through Saltmarket. The road bends around us on purpose. Guild pays the toll-keepers to route traffic past the door." He nodded at the white building. "Window shopping. Everyone sees the queue, everyone thinks, *well, they look ordinary, they don't look robbed,* and next bad harvest, they come back." He tilted his head. "Your hands are wrong for a laborer. Ink and knife-work. Clerk? Carver?"

Orsen said nothing, which was itself an answer.

Tam exhaled through his nose. "I worked the Saltmarket counter for nine years. Rang the bell eleven thousand times — I counted, because counting was the only thing they let me keep." He glanced back at the window where his mother sat. "I defected last spring. They let me go, which should tell you how much they fear me. What I know isn't worth killing for." His eyes dropped to the satchel. "What you're carrying might be."

Orsen's hand moved to the bag without permission. *Smooth,* said Nell, dry as dust. *Very subtle. No one will suspect the man clutching the satchel like a lifeboat.*

"The map," Tam said quietly. "Salt-stiffened hide, folded in eighths, tide-marks along one edge. A stranger carried it north along the coast road about a month back. Stopped here. Asked me — me specifically — whether the tower still stood." His jaw worked. "I told him yes, and two days later I heard he drowned off the Teeth. So either the sea took him or something wanted him to drown, and I've been sick about it ever since, because I sent him up that coast."

"He made it further than you heard," Orsen said. "He made it to the guild house in Greyharbor. He died on the doorstep."

Something moved behind Tam's eyes — grief, and under the grief, a cold settling certainty. "Then you have it. The map." He stepped closer, voice dropping. "Listen to me, carver, because I only ever say this once and it cost me everything I have to say it. That map leads to the tower under the palace. And the tower is where they mined the crown. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The crown isn't a jewel. It isn't a symbol. It's a *tool*, cut from the grave of something older than kingdoms, and it does one thing: it eats. Four kings have worn it. Four kings have gone into the palace and come out as smiling strangers wearing their own faces."

"Four?" Orsen felt the word leave him like heat off ice. "There have been — the histories say—"

"The histories are written by whoever remembers longest, and the crown makes sure that's never the king. Aldric is the fourth. Before him was Maerin the Mild, before him Osric Two-Faces — the name's a joke that stopped being funny — and before *him*, the first, whose real name nobody living knows, because the crown ate it first and ate everyone who remembered it second." Tam's voice shook, not with fear but with fury worn smooth by helplessness. "Every king gets lucid sometimes. An hour at dawn, maybe. Long enough to write letters to themselves. The palace burns them, we think. Or worse — files them in the guild archives, because the crown and the guild are the same hunger in different clothes, and don't tell me you haven't suspected."

Orsen thought of Vess. Of her warning: *never ask where the salt comes from.* He thought of the columns he'd copied. *Quarter-day purchases.* He had assumed the salt was harvested from the dead. He had never once asked what fed the dead in the first place, or who tended the graves, or why the guild's white stone always seemed to rise in towns where people were hungry.

"I have to get to the capital," he said.

"Everyone with that map has to get to the capital. That's what the map is *for*." Tam looked at him for a long moment, and then, abruptly, all the anger went out of him and left something younger and more frightened underneath. "There's a cart leaving for the coast road at dawn. Driver owes me. I'll put you on it. In exchange—" he hesitated, hating himself for asking, hating the asking more than anything the guild had ever done to him— "when you get there, if there's any way, any craft-trick, any carver's magic you know, to give a woman back her son's face—"

Orsen thought of the widow in Greyharbor buying one hour of her husband's voice. He thought of the femur he'd worked in secret, and the scream the dead man's bones had made, and the voice now living in his pocket who had been sold out of her own life one memory at a time.

"I can't promise," he said honestly. "But I'll carve it into the ledger of things I mean to try. And carver's promises don't wash out."

Tam studied him, then nodded once, like a man signing a contract he couldn't read but needed badly.

"Dawn, then. The white house with the blue door, by the fish racks." He turned away, then stopped. "Carver. One more thing. The stranger who carried the map — did he say anything? At the end?"

Orsen thought of the doorstep, the salt-stiff hands, the whisper. *The king's true name.* He hadn't told anyone. He hadn't even told Nell the whole of it.

"He said a name," Orsen said carefully. "Not the king's. Someone else's. And then he said, *tell the tower it's overdue.*"

Tam went pale in the flat morning light.

"Overdue," he repeated. "Like a debt."

"Like a debt," Orsen agreed.

Across the square, the counter bell rang again — bright, cheerful, obscene. Another birthday. Another face. Another column in a ledger somewhere, copied out in careful ink by careful hands.

*Carver,* Nell said softly from his pocket, as they walked away from the white building and its patient queue. *Did you feel it? When he said four kings. Did you feel the map move?*

Orsen had. Deep in the satchel, the stiff old hide had shifted against his hip — once, faintly, like a sleeper turning over. Like something that had been waiting a very long time to hear itself spoken of aloud.

"It's excited," he said grimly.

"No," said Nell. "It's *hungry*. And carver — look behind us. Third time today."

Orsen didn't turn. But in the dark glass of a chandler's window he caught the reflection: a figure in traveling grays, keeping pace at the edge of the crowd. Unhurried. Patient as bookkeeping.

And on the figure's collar, catching the light, the tiny glint of a guild sign — three waves beneath a set of scales — carved, Orsen realized with a chill that started in his scarred fingers and climbed all the way to his heart, from a sliver of bone he recognized.

Because he had carved it himself.

* * *

They reached the blue-doored house as the light began to fail, and Orsen knocked, and the door opened, and the woman standing in it wore traveling grays with a guild sign at her collar and a face he had trusted since he was nine years old.

"Hello, Orsen," said Guildmistress Vess. "You've been lying to me. Come in. We should talk before the cart leaves — and before you decide whether that map goes to the tower or to me."

Behind him, at the corner of the street, the gray-cloaked figure finally stopped following.

Because it had never been following at all.
