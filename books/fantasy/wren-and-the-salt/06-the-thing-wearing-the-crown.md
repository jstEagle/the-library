## 6. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The hour began, as it always did, with the taste of copper and the sound of his own breathing.

King Aldric Vane opened his eyes in the dark of his private study, where a single candle burned on the desk before him — lit by no hand he could remember lighting. The candle was the first mercy of the day. Someone, some servant or secretary or ghost of one, had learned that when the king woke lucid, he woke wanting paper.

He was lucid now. He had perhaps an hour. He could feel it draining out of him already, like water through cupped hands, and so he did not waste a moment on despair. Despair took time. He had learned that much, at least, in the years since the crown had settled onto his head at his coronation — warm, he remembered thinking, warm as a living thing, and how he had smiled at that, then. How young he'd been. Twenty-six years old and charmed by a warm crown.

He pulled the paper toward him and wrote, in a hand shakier than his tutors would have wept over:

*To Aldric Vane, from Aldric Vane. If you are reading this, you have woken again. Good. Count your blessings while you can still count.*

He knew the drill. He had written this letter, or ones like it, four hundred and eleven times. He kept the count because the counting was the last thing that was entirely his. The crown could take his mother's lullabies. It could take the smell of rain on the palace gardens where he'd kissed Maren for the first time. But it had never managed to take arithmetic. Perhaps numbers were too dry. Too saltless. Whatever the crown ate, it wanted flavor, and a man's sums had none.

*Item,* he wrote. *You are king. This is still true. Check the seal on your own decrees if you doubt it — it is your face, older than you remember being.*

*Item. Your wife is Queen Maren. She has brown eyes. You wrote that down three hundred times before you understood why: because one morning you will look at her and see a stranger, and this sentence will be the only bridge left across that terrible water. Brown eyes, Aldric. Like river stones in summer. Do not let go of it.*

His hand paused. Somewhere below, faint through the floor, he could hear the council chamber filling — the scrape of chairs, the murmur of men whose names arrived at the edge of his mind and slid off like fish. He had an hour. The crown always gave him an hour. It was, in its way, generous. A farmer leaves the ox a paddock; a jailer leaves the prisoner a window. The crown left him sixty minutes and a candle, and called it kindness.

*Item,* he continued. *The thing on your head is hungry. You know this. Stop pretending it is only a headache.*

That was the truth he had spent nine years refusing, and two years unable to deny. The crown was not an object. He had felt it feed. There was no other word for what happened in the throne room when the petitions came — the way the world went slightly silver at the edges, the way a memory would simply *leave*, plucked clean as a wishbone, and in its place would arrive certainty: the right decree, the perfect alliance, the exact cruelty required, delivered into his mouth like a coin under a pillow. He signed. He always signed. And afterward there would be a hole in him shaped like something he could no longer name, and the crown would sit warm and satisfied, and the courtiers would say, *the king is decisive today.*

Decisive. That was the word they used. He had traded his childhood for decisiveness — the summer house at Ellowmere gone first, then his father's voice, then the name of the dog he'd loved, a big foolish hound whose name sat now in some hollow in his skull like a tooth knocked out. He had traded Maren's face — not all at once, never all at once, the crown was a patient eater — but in installments. Her laugh. Then her hands. Then, on the worst morning of his life, everything except the sentence *brown eyes, like river stones in summer*, which he had carved into himself through sheer repetition until even the crown couldn't dig it out.

She knew. That was the cruelest ledger entry of all. She knew, and she stayed, and every day she looked at him with love aimed at a man who mostly wasn't home, and every day she said, *Good morning, my lord husband,* in a voice that forgave him for not knowing her, and he would smile the smile the crown permitted and die a little more efficiently than the crown could manage on its own.

*Item,* he wrote. *If Maren comes to you during your hour — she sometimes does, she has learned the schedule better than the physicians — do not waste the time on apologies. She does not want apologies. She wants you to tell her about the dog. You don't know the dog's name anymore. Tell her anyway. Say "the dog," and she'll fill in the rest, and she'll laugh, and for ten seconds you will be married to her instead of merely wed.*

* * *

The knock came at eleven minutes past the hour. It always came at eleven minutes past, because Lord Chancellor Hesse had long ago concluded that the king's lucid mornings were best spent on state business, and Hesse was not a man who wasted a resource.

"Your Majesty. The border tariffs."

Aldric set down his pen. Here was the arithmetic of his captivity: fifty minutes remained, and forty-nine of them belonged to Hesse.

"Send them in," Aldric said, and heard his own voice do the thing it did — flatten, deepen, arrange itself into the shape of authority. The crown liked that voice. When he spoke in it, the metal warmed against his scalp like a cat settling in.

Three ministers entered with their documents. Aldric read. That was the horror of it, the part he had tried and failed to explain in four hundred letters: he *understood*. The crown did not make him stupid. It made him efficient. Every clause of the tariff accord was clear to him, every consequence mapped, every advantage weighed — and threaded through the clarity, like wire through wax, ran the crown's own preference, humming. Sign this. This one feeds us. The northern ports. Yes. The memory it wants in exchange is small. What is a harbor master's name? What is one harbor master?

He signed. His hand did not shake. That was somehow worse.

"The delegation from Saltmarket arrives Thursday," Hesse said, gathering papers. "They bring the usual tribute."

"The usual tribute," Aldric repeated. Memories. Crates of them, pressed into white salt, bought from the desperate at scandalous rates — birthdays, first kisses, dead mothers' voices — all of it ground fine and fed up the chain until it reached the crown, which ate like a god and thanked no one. He had approved the trade years ago. He could not remember approving it. He suspected, in his clearer moments, that the approval had been the crown's first great meal off his own conscience.

"Your Majesty approves?"

"I approve." The words came out smooth and correct and alien, and somewhere behind his eyes a door closed on whatever he might have said instead.

When they had gone, he sat very still and took inventory, the way a man pats his pockets after a pickpocket passes. Childhood: mostly gone. Father: a coat without a man in it. Mother: a lullaby with the tune missing. Maren: brown eyes, river stones, summer. Himself — and here he hesitated, pen hovering — himself: a name, a title, a growing conviction that he was mostly a glove, and that the hand inside it was patient, and that one day soon it would flex its fingers and find nothing left of Aldric Vane worth wearing.

There was a boy, he remembered suddenly. A son. They had lost him — fever, small, quick, years ago — and the grief had been enormous, and now the grief was gone too, eaten, and what remained was the knowledge that a grief should be there, a socket without the ache. Sometimes he thought that was the crown's cleverest theft. Not the memories themselves, but the mourning for them. It left him no graves to visit inside his own head.

He looked at the candle. Forty minutes, perhaps less.

* * *

He wrote fast now, the letters coming shorter as the years went on, because there was less of him to write about and more of the crown to write around.

*Aldric. Listen. I have worked out the schedule. The crown sleeps shallowest in the hour after dawn — call it its digestion. If anyone ever comes to you in that hour claiming they can help, believe them. Do not be proud. Pride is a luxury of whole men.*

*I have tried everything else. I have tried fasting (it eats regardless), prayer (the gods of the deep country may hear me, but if so they are slow), and once, in a fit of courage I am not sure I could repeat, I tried to cut it off. You will find the scar above your left ear. The pain taught the crown my fear, and it fed on that for a month. Do not give it fear. Fear is rich food. Give it boredom if you can manage it. Be boring, Aldric. It is your last weapon.*

*One more thing. There is a guild — the Salt Guild — and ledgers, and I begin to suspect the crown's debts are written somewhere in a human hand. Debts can be called in. I was a merchant's son before I was ever a king; I know that much survives. Find the ledger. Find whoever keeps it. Offer anything. Everything. A kingdom is a poor price for a self, and I say that as a man who has paid more.*

The candle guttered. He could feel the hour closing like a hand around a lamp — the familiar softening at the edges of things, the crown stirring against his skull, warm, expectant, waking hungry. In a few minutes he would stand, and walk to the council chamber, and be decisive, and lose something he would not know he'd lost. In a few minutes the letter in front of him would become just another page in just another drawer full of pages written by a madman, unread, unheeded, because he always sealed them and always hid them and never, in four hundred mornings, remembered where.

So this time he did something different.

He took the signet ring from his finger — the seal of Aldric Vane, third of that name, worn thin now from signing away his life one decree at a time — and he pressed it hard into red wax, and beneath the royal arms he scratched, with the penknife he kept for exactly this purpose and always forgot he kept:

*TO THE KEEPER OF THE LEDGER. THE CROWN IS NOT AN OBJECT. IT IS A HUNGER. I AM STILL INSIDE. COME.*

No address. No name beyond the seal. He wrapped the letter in oilcloth, and on an impulse he did not examine — the crown was drowsy, and impulses were safest when it drowsed — he carried it himself down to the tide-gate, where the palace cellars met the sea, and gave it to the one servant he trusted, a gray woman named Brack who had served his father and who looked at him each morning with an expression he had finally, after years, identified as *grief held politely in check.*

"For the post," he said. "Any rider going north."

Brack looked at the letter. Looked at him. Something passed across her face like weather.

"North, Your Majesty?"

"There's a man," Aldric said, and the words came from somewhere deeper than the crown had yet reached, some cellar of him even the hunger hadn't found, "who carves bone. He hears what the dead won't say. Send it to him."

He didn't know how he knew that. He filed the mystery away with the others — the candle, the counting, the stubborn arithmetic — all the small dry stones the crown couldn't swallow.

Then the hour ended, as hours do, and the warmth rose up through the metal, and King Aldric smiled the permitted smile, and turned back toward his throne carrying the day's first loss already folded and waiting inside him like a letter he would never read.

Far to the north, in a city of ledgers and tides, a rider was already saddling.
