## 7. Vess Comes Collecting

She caught them on the fourth day out of Saltmarket, at a place where the road narrowed between two hills like a coin in a purse.

Orsen knew before Tam did. He felt it first in the bone pendant at his throat — the little silent one he'd carved back home, the only thing he'd ever made that said nothing — because it went warm against his skin, the way it always did when someone he knew was near. Then Nell's voice rose up out of the carving in his pocket, thin and urgent as smoke.

*She's here. Orsen. She's — oh, you idiot, she's been behind you since the ford.*

"I know," he said aloud, which was foolish, because there was no one to hear but a dead woman and a defector who already thought him half-mad.

"Know what?" Tam asked, and then the wind turned, and Tam knew too.

She came around the shoulder of the hill alone, which was somehow worse than if she'd come with twenty enforcers. Vess rode the same grey mare she'd ridden for thirty years, in the same oiled cloak, with the same iron-topped walking stick slung across her saddle where another woman would carry a sword. Her hair was white as the guild's finest pressed salt and pinned back with the severity of a closed ledger. She looked, Orsen thought, exactly as she had on the morning he'd arrived at the counting house, a starving boy of eleven with a stolen heel of bread and no name worth writing down.

She had not aged a single day in nineteen years. He had never let himself think about that before. Now, with the map stiff inside his coat and Nell humming warnings against his ribs, he thought about it very hard indeed.

"Guildmistress," he said.

"Carver." She dismounted without hurry. The mare stood as though nailed there. "You've made me ride four days through weather I wouldn't wish on a tax collector. Do you know what that does to my knees?"

"You have knees?"

"I have *everything*, thank you." She set the iron stick down on the road with a click that sounded, to Orsen's ear, exactly like a lock turning. "Tam."

"Guildmistress." Tam had gone the color of old tallow. "I can explain—"

"You sold your guild oath for forty crowns and a mule that limps. There's nothing to explain. It was a bad bargain and you knew it when you shook on it." She said it without heat, the way she read figures aloud. "Stand over there and think about your choices. Not too far. I may want you later."

Tam went. Tam went *very* fast.

And then it was just the two of them, and the wind coming down off the hills carrying the smell of the sea they were both walking away from, and Orsen understood with a strange, floating clarity that this was the moment his whole life had been leaning toward — the way a bone leans toward the knife.

"You lied to me," Vess said.

"Yes."

"In my own counting house. To my face. You told me the stranger died with nothing in his hands, and you put that map inside your shirt while I was standing close enough to count the stitches in your collar, and you lied." She tilted her head. "Nineteen years, Orsen. Nineteen years I've kept you fed and taught and paid, and you never so much as rounded a figure wrong to spare yourself trouble. I used to tell the other carvers you were incapable of dishonesty. That it was a flaw we'd train out of you eventually."

"I'm sorry."

"No, you're not." Something moved across her face — not anger. Anger he could have managed. This was more like weather seen from far away, a storm on a horizon she couldn't quite turn toward. "That's what I came to understand, riding four days through this filth. You're not sorry. And I find I'm not angry. Isn't that interesting?"

"It's not the word I'd pick."

"I didn't ask you to pick a word." She walked past him, slowly, the way she walked the counting-house floor at closing time, checking every drawer. "Here is what I know about myself, carver. Here is the whole of it. My name is Vess. I am Guildmistress of the Salt. I am very good at arithmetic and moderately good at cruelty and excellent at neither loving nor being loved. Everything else—" she tapped her own sternum with two fingers, a small hollow knock, "—is gone. Sold. I signed it away forty years ago, in ink, in my own hand, and the guild keeps the paper, and I keep the wages. That was the bargain. Rise high enough and you won't be burdened by whatever made you want to rise."

Orsen had known this the way he knew the tide tables — as fact, not feeling. Hearing it spoken plainly, out under open sky, was different. It made his chest hurt.

"So you don't remember—"

"Anything. A mother or none. A reason or none. Why I took in a starving boy with thief's hands instead of selling his hours like everyone else would have. Why I taught him the deep trade and not just the shallow one. Why—" and here her voice did something it had never once done in the counting house; it hesitated, "—why, when the enforcers reported that Orsen Iversen had fled south with a dead man's map, I sent them west. Deliberately. Knowing it was wrong. Knowing I was spending guild silver to buy you three days' head start, and having no earthly idea why."

The wind moved the grass. Somewhere behind the hill, Tam's mule complained about something.

"Why did you?" Orsen asked.

"That," said Vess, "is the question I rode four days to ask myself." She reached into her collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small carved thing — bone, yellowed, worn smooth as a river stone. Orsen had seen it ten thousand times and never once looked at it properly. Now he did, and his stomach dropped, because he knew the hand. Everyone in the guild knew that hand. It was the founding hand, the old master-work that hung behind the guildmistress's chair: a tiny figure of a woman, arms folded, chin up, wearing an expression of profound and unimpressed stubbornness.

It was also, unmistakably, a portrait. Younger than the woman in front of him by forty years, rounder of face, softer of jaw. But the chin was the same chin. The stubbornness was the same stubbornness.

"You recognize it," Vess said.

"It's you."

"It's me." She held it up between them, letting the light through its thin places. "The one thing I kept from before. I don't know why. When I try to remember keeping it, there's nothing — no scene, no face, no reason. Only this." She folded her fingers around it. "And for forty years, whenever some clerk suggested selling it, some part of me that isn't memory wanted to put his teeth on the floor. And nineteen years ago, when a boy stood dripping in my doorway holding a stolen loaf and looking at me as if I might feed him or eat him depending on my answer, that same part of me spoke up, and I listened, and I still don't know why."

"Vess—"

"Don't." She raised a hand. "Let me finish, because I've had four days to compose this and I'll be damned if I'll lose my place now. Here is what I believe, carver, and I believe it the way I believe arithmetic — because everything else checks against it. Whatever I sold, I sold it badly. Whatever I was, some piece of her refused the sale and hid inside this bone, and that piece has been running the important parts of my life ever since. It fed you. It taught you. It sent the enforcers west. And now it has brought me here, alone, unarmed, four days from home, to stand in front of the one man in the world I apparently cannot bring myself to destroy." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, dry and bright as salt. "Do you understand? I am betraying myself for you, and I don't even get the pleasure of knowing why."

Orsen found that his eyes were wet. He blinked it away. "Then don't destroy me."

"Oh, I intend better than that." And the warmth went out of her voice all at once, like a shutter closing, and she was the Guildmistress again, entire, and it frightened him how completely. "I intend to offer you a bargain. The guild's best offer. Listen carefully, because I will only say it once, and then I must do whatever the arithmetic demands."

He listened.

"The stranger is dead. The king is dying — has been dying for years, and the guild's ledgers are balanced against that death so deeply that if he recovers, if anyone *unbinds* that crown, half the collateral in our vaults comes due at once. Borrowed years called in. Names returned to owners who no longer want them. Every widow's hour refunded. The guild drowns in its own debts, Orsen. So. Surrender the map. Come home. I will burn the enforcers' report with my own hands. Your rooms, your bench, your wage — all restored. No mark against your name. In five years, if you're patient, the countership. And I will give you—" her hand closed tight around the little carved figure until the knuckles went white, "—whatever this costs me to say. I will give you my protection. Formally. On the ledger. Which is the only forever the guild recognizes."

"And the crown?" His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "The man wearing it? We just leave him to be eaten?"

"We leave everyone to be eaten. That is the trade. That has always been the trade." For one moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — her eyes were not the guild's eyes. They were tired and old and full of a grief with no address. "Boy, I cannot even remember whether I ever had a son. But when I look at you, something in me says *yes, this one, save this one,* and I have learned to trust that thing further than I trust myself. So I am asking you. Not ordering. Asking. Give me the map, and live."

The map was right there against his chest. Four days of road and rain and Nell's voice in the dark — *left at the standing stone, the road only exists between tides, mind the water* — and all he had to do was reach inside his coat and end it.

He thought about the widow from his first chapter of this whole disaster, buying one hour of her husband's voice. He thought about King Aldric folding his losses into himself like unread letters. He thought about an old woman in Saltmarket who no longer knew her son's face, and the clerk who'd written the sale into the ledger with a steady, unashamed hand.

"Vess," he said gently, "you told me once never to ask where the salt comes from."

"I did."

"I'm asking anyway."

Something flickered across her face. Behind the flicker, faint as a lamp through fog, Orsen saw the little carved woman with her folded arms — saw that whatever lived in the bone was looking out through the guildmistress's eyes right now, straining toward him like a dog at the end of a chain.

"Neither do I," Vess whispered. "Neither do I know where it comes from. Forty years, and I never once asked, and I have just now realized — sitting in my chest like a stone — that I *can't*. The question slides off. Someone paid good money to make sure the Guildmistress herself would never wonder where the salt comes from." Her hand trembled, just slightly, around the carving. "What kind of bargain did I sign, Orsen?"

"A bad one," said a new voice.

They both turned. Tam had crept back along the hedgerow, mule-less and miserable, holding his cap like a man in church. "Begging pardon, Guildmistress. But I counted vaults for six years before I ran. And I'll tell you what the salt comes from. It comes from the tower. From under the tower. Same mine as the crown." He swallowed. "Same grave."

The wind dropped. Even the grass seemed to hold still.

Vess looked at Tam for a long moment. Then down at the small carved woman in her fist. Then at Orsen — and the storm finally made landfall in her face, forty years late, and it was not rage and it was not grief but both of them arriving together like relatives who hate each other and share a carriage.

"North," she said at last, swinging back onto the mare in one motion that betrayed none of her claimed bad knees. "The tower is north, then. Well. So am I."

"Guildmistress—" Orsen started.

"Don't mistake me, carver. I have not decided whose side I'm on. I'm not certain I *have* a side; I appear to have a bone." She gathered the reins, and above them the sky was going the color of a ledger page, and far to the north — though none of them could see it yet — a tower of white salt stood drinking the light. "Ride behind me. If you run, I'll assume the worst. If you fight me, I'll win, because I taught you everything you know and at least three things you don't."

She clicked her tongue, and the mare turned north, and Orsen looked at Nell's carving in his palm.

*Well,* said the dead woman dryly. *This should be fun.*
