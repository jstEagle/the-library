## 8. The Tower of White Salt

The palace had a hundred gates, and none of them were the way in.

That was the first thing the map had made plain — or as plain as a thing drawn in salt could be. Orsen had studied it by candlelight for eleven nights on the road, tracing the lines Nell's stiff fingers had pressed into it while she still lived: not the grand approaches, not the river stairs, but a seam beneath the city itself. The palace, it turned out, was built the way a lid is built. Something underneath held it up. Something white.

"The tide gate," Nell said from inside his coat pocket, where the carved bone rode against his heart. Her voice was fainter than it had been at Saltmarket, thinner, like a song played through a wall. "There's an old oyster channel under the east quay. It floods twice a day. You'll have to swim."

"I don't swim."

"You're a bone-carver with a stolen map sneaking into a royal palace to fight a crown that eats kings. Today is full of firsts."

He almost smiled. That was her gift, Nell's — even faded, even stretched to gauze, she could find the funny in the dreadful. He'd stopped asking how she felt days ago. She'd stopped answering honestly long before that.

The channel was exactly where the salt lines said, hidden behind a fishmonger's stall that smelled of low tide and older sins. Orsen paid the fishwife three coppers to look elsewhere, which she took with the professional grace of someone who looked elsewhere for a living. Then he went down into the dark, coat over his arm, boots around his neck, the map wrapped in oilcloth above his head like a lantern he couldn't light.

The water came to his chest. It was cold enough to make his teeth sing.

"Left at the fork," Nell murmured, so quietly he nearly missed it. "Orsen. Left."

"I heard you."

"No, you didn't. You were about to go right because right *feels* correct when you're frightened. Frightened people always think the true path is the one that feels wrong, so they double back into the trap. Go left."

He went left. The channel narrowed, then opened without warning into a cavern, and Orsen stood chest-deep in black water and forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

The tower filled the cavern the way a heart fills a chest. It rose out of the flooded dark — white, seamless, glowing very faintly with its own pale light, as though moonlight had been poured into a mold and set. It wasn't stone. He knew salt when he saw it; he kept ledgers of the stuff, weighed it, sealed it, traded it away in quantities small enough to buy an hour of a dead man's voice. But this was salt the way a cathedral is a house. It climbed and climbed and vanished into darkness overhead, and every inch of its surface was carved — no, not carved. Grown. Into shapes that hurt to look at directly: hands folded over other hands, faces with the eyes worn smooth, mouths open in what might have been song.

"It's beautiful," he whispered.

"It's a grave," said Nell. And there was nothing funny left in her voice at all.

He waded to the tower's base and put his hand against it, because he was Orsen Iversen and he had never once in his life met a surface he didn't need to touch. The salt was warm. That was the worst part. Not cold like the sea that had made it, not cold like the dead. Warm, like something alive, like a brow under fever.

And through his palm — silence.

Not ordinary silence. He knew ordinary silence; most things he made were silent, most of the world was silent, and he had made peace with that. This was different. This was silence with weight behind it, silence like a held breath, like a room where everyone has stopped talking because you walked in. The salt wasn't empty. The salt was *full* — full of the dead, pressed close, packed tight as grain in a sack — and they were all looking at him.

"Orsen." Nell's voice cracked like thin ice. "Get your hand off it."

"Why? What—"

"Because I can feel them pulling." A pause. When she spoke again her voice was smaller, and it frightened him more than any scream would have. "They're so thirsty down here, Orsen. All of them. They've been drinking each other for centuries and it's never enough, and now there's me, fresh, still warm, still *me* — get your hand off the wall."

He snatched his hand back. For a moment the bone against his chest felt lighter. Less. Like a coin with the edge shaved off.

"Nell?"

"Still here." A breath he couldn't see her take. "Still here. But Orsen — whatever we're going to do, we do it fast. The closer I get to that thing, the less of me there is to want anything. And I'd like to keep wanting things. It's the only part I have left."

He dressed in the dark, wrung out what he could, and found the stair the map promised: a seam in the salt, steps worn smooth by feet that had come down here a very long time ago and, judging by the dust, never come back. He climbed toward the palace above, carrying a map, a ghost, and a silence, and tried not to count how much of Nell's silence was new.

* * *

King Aldric had one hour.

He had learned to live inside the hour the way a drowning man learns to live inside a breath. Somewhere in the grey hours before dawn the crown loosened — not kindly, never kindly, but the way a fist loosens when it sleeps — and for sixty minutes Aldric of House Merrin was a man again, standing in a body that fit him badly, in a life that fit him worse.

He wrote letters. That was the ritual. He did not know who received them. He suspected no one. He wrote them anyway, because the writing was the only proof that the hour had happened, that *he* had happened, once a day, every day, for nine years.

*My name is Aldric,* today's letter began, because some mornings he needed to start from the very beginning. *My father was Tomas. My wife was Elenna. She liked to sing off-key on purpose, to make the children laugh, and I have been trying for four days to remember whether we had children, and I believe we did not, and I believe the wanting of them was its own kind of grief, and I am fairly sure that memory is mine and not one the crown left behind like a boot it had finished with.*

His hand shook. The ink blotted. He kept writing.

*I know what it does now. I understand that this is new information and also that I have understood it many times before, and that each time I bury the understanding somewhere it cannot reach, and each time it digs it up and eats it. So today I am doing the thing Vess — no. Not Vess. The stranger. The stranger told me: write it small, write it sideways, hide it in the margins of a letter about grain tariffs, because the crown reads everything I write with love in my own handwriting.*

*It is in the tower. Under us. It grew there. We mined it out of a drowned god's grave and thought ourselves clever, and it has worn four of us, and it will wear a fifth, and somewhere in me is a boy who swore he would never let that happen, and if you are reading this and you are not me — if you are anyone at all —*

Boots in the corridor. The hour was ending early; he could feel the crown turning over in its sleep, tasting the air, tasting the *strangeness*. Someone had come into the deep places. Someone had touched the salt.

Aldric did the only thing left to a king who was mostly absence. He folded the letter, sealed it with wax and the signet he could no longer remember the meaning of, and slid it under the door. Then he sat on the edge of his bed like a man waiting for a coach, folded his hands, and let himself begin to forget.

* * *

They found him by accident, which is to say Orsen found him because he got lost.

The passages above the tide gate were a maze built by three centuries of panic — bolt holes and priest-holes and servants' cuts, none of them marked, all of them breathing cold air down from rooms where people slept. Orsen climbed until climbing stopped making sense, and then a door stood open in front of him that had no business being open, and inside, in a bare room lit by one dying candle, a man sat on the edge of a bed with his hands folded like a patient child.

He wore a nightshirt. His hair was grey at the temples and gone entirely at the crown of his head, where the skin was pale and smooth and bore, faintly, the shape of something that had been wound there so long it had left its ghost behind. His eyes were open. They were terrible eyes, because they were trying so hard.

"You've grown," the man said.

Orsen stopped in the doorway. "I'm sorry — I took a wrong turn, I'll—"

"You've grown so tall." The king — and it was the king, Orsen knew it the way he knew bone, some deep grain of recognition — the king tilted his head, and his face did something heartbreaking: it softened. It softened completely, unguardedly, the way faces only soften for family. "Your mother will be pleased. She worried about you. About your cough. Do you remember your cough? You'd wake the whole wing."

"I—" Orsen's throat closed. Behind his sternum, Nell said nothing, and her saying nothing was louder than screaming. "I don't have a mother anymore," he managed. "I'm sorry. I think you've mistaken me for someone."

"Yes," said the king, agreeably. Sadly. "I do that." And then, with the awful gentleness of the truly lost: "But you have his hands. Have you noticed? You carve. I can always tell. My son used to whittle little boats out of pine knots and float them in the fountain and lie about where they came from." He smiled, and the smile had a crack down the middle of it. "You have a maker's hands. Don't let anyone trade those away. Whatever they offer. Whatever it costs to refuse."

The candle guttered. And Orsen, who had crossed a kingdom on the word of a dead woman and a drawing in salt, who had lied to the only person who loved him and swum through black water toward a god's grave, stood in a doorway holding an oilcloth-wrapped map and could not move, because the enemy at the end of everything was sitting on the edge of a bed in a nightshirt, mistaking him for his dead son, and giving him good advice.

"I came to help you," Orsen said.

"Did you?" The king's eyes drifted toward the window, toward the last of the dark. "That's kind. Everyone who comes to help me ends up helping it instead. But you can try." He looked back, and for one lucid, blazing second — the hour's final coin spent all at once — his gaze sharpened and he was, unmistakably, a king. "The tower. Whatever you mean to do down there — do it tonight. I felt it wake. It knows someone's come."

Then the sharpness drained out of his face like water from a cracked cup, and he smiled politely at Orsen, a stranger, a guest, and asked him, kindly, whether he'd had supper.

Behind Orsen's heart, very small and very far away, Nell began to hum a lullaby neither of them could name — and the salt below the palace hummed back.
