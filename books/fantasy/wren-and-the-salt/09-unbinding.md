## 9. Unbinding

The tower was colder inside than the sea that had drowned it.

Orsen climbed the last stair with Nell's bone pressed to his chest like a second heart, and the silence coming off it had changed. For weeks the carved bone had been his one quiet thing — the only object he'd ever made that said nothing. Now it said something worse than nothing. It said *hurry*, in a voice worn down almost to static, the way a coin wears smooth from too many hands.

"Nell," he whispered, on the landing. "Talk to me."

*Can't,* she said, and even her resentment had gone thin. *It drinks, Orsen. Everything I am is leaking out of me into this place. I can feel myself getting smaller. Like being forgotten while you're still alive.*

"That's not a thing anyone should have to know how it feels."

*No,* she agreed. *But you're about to find out.*

The chamber at the top of the tower was round, and it was full of light that came from nowhere — white light, salt light, the color of a page before anyone has written on it. And there, on a plinth of fused bone and brine, sat the crown.

He had expected something grander. Something wicked-looking. Instead it looked like what it was: a ring of mined salt, cut and polished and set with a god's grave-dust, beautiful the way a trapdoor is beautiful if all you've ever seen are floors.

King Aldric knelt beside it.

That was the wrongness of it — not the crown on the throne but the king on his knees, a big man gone hollow at the edges, his fine clothes hanging like clothes on a stand. He was trying to remember something. Orsen could see the effort of it in his face, the way a man's face works when he's carrying furniture up stairs alone.

"You," Aldric said slowly. "You're my son."

"No, Your Majesty." Orsen kept his voice gentle, the way Vess had taught him to speak to grieving customers. "I'm the one who got your letter."

"My letter." The king's brow furrowed. "Did I write a letter? I write so many letters. I never remember—" His hand went to the crown on the plinth, and stopped, trembling, an inch away. "Don't let me put it back on. Please. Whoever you are. Don't let me."

"I'm going to do better than that," Orsen said. "I'm going to take it off the market entirely."

He set Nell's bone down on the stone between them, and knelt, because this next part was guild business, and guild business was done kneeling. He knew the forms. He had carved them into ledgers for eleven years without once asking where the salt came from.

"Here's the trouble," he said to the crown, conversationally, the way you'd talk across a counter. "You've been eating this man for thirty years, and he's nearly empty. You know it. That's why you've been hungry enough to pull half the dead out of their graves along the north road — I heard them screaming in his femur. A king isn't much of a meal anymore. He's broth after broth after broth."

The light in the room leaned toward him. He felt it listening.

"But I know what you actually want," Orsen went on, "because I keep your books. Not the palace ledgers. *Yours.* Borrowed years, stolen names, memory pressed into salt — every trade runs on the same currency, and it isn't gold. It's *self*. A whole one. Undivided. And a king—" he glanced at Aldric, who was watching him with the dazed attention of a man at someone else's funeral, "—a king sold most of himself off decades ago. Every decree cost him a piece. You've been scraping the bowl."

The light shivered. It wanted him to stop. It also very much wanted him to go on.

"So here's my offer," Orsen said, "in the guild's own language. Debt for debt. Name for name." His mouth had gone dry as the tower itself. "Release this man. All of him. What's left, and the pieces you've been hoarding in your little ring — give them back. In exchange, you get a name that's never been traded, never been spent, never worn by any office or eaten by any hunger. A name that belongs wholly to one person, who is offering it freely."

Freely. That was the word that mattered. Stolen names were gristle; bought names were stringy with regret. But a gift —

The crown's light turned on him like an eye.

*Name,* it said, and the word didn't come through his ears. It came through the bones of his hands, which had heard every dead thing he'd ever touched, and now, apparently, included one living god-shaped hunger. *Name. Yours.*

"Yes," said Orsen Yarrow, bone-carver of the Salt Guild, keeper of impossible ledgers. "My name is—"

"Don't."

Aldric's hand closed on his wrist. The king's grip was shockingly strong, the grip of a drowning man, and his ruined eyes were suddenly, terribly clear.

"Boy. Whatever your name is — don't. I have watched this thing eat my mother's face and my wedding day and the sound of my own laugh, and I am telling you, there is no bargain with it. There is only the first bite."

"There's a difference," Orsen said quietly, "between a meal and a feast. And I brought guests."

He picked up Nell's bone and snapped it in half.

She made a sound he would carry in whatever remained of him forever — relief and fury and freedom all at once, a chord — and then she was everywhere. Thirty years of a smuggler's life poured out of the broken carving and into the salt-light: a road north under three moons, a sister's laughter, a betrayal, a drowning, a dying whisper, a king's true name spoken into a stranger's hands. And she did not come alone. She came the way the tide comes, pulling everything behind her — the dead of the north road, the widows' purchased hours, the children's birthdays, the old woman's son, every memory the guild had ever pressed into salt and every ghost the tower's thirst had dragged thin. They swarmed the crown like gulls on a fish-cart.

And the crown, ravenous, opened wide and drank.

That was its nature. That was the flaw in it, mined from a drowned god's grave along with everything else: it could not refuse. It swallowed Nell's whole unbroken life. It swallowed the widow's hour. It swallowed ten thousand small griefs and joys, and each one went down like a stone into a well already brimming, and the light in the chamber began to gutter and swell and gutter again, overfull, choking.

"Now!" Nell's voice rang off the walls, huge and free and furious. "Orsen! While it's stuffed — *pay it!*"

Orsen spoke his name.

Not shouted. Spoken, the way you settle a debt at a counting house: clearly, once, with witnesses. "My name is Orsen Yarrow. Take it. Take all it carries."

The world took a step sideways.

He felt it begin the way you feel a tooth come loose — not pain, exactly, but a loosening, a parting of things that had grown together. *Orsen* peeled away first, the sound of it, then the shape. Then came the shop on Tanner's Row where he'd apprenticed. Then his mother's kitchen, the smell of it going even as he reached for it — no. No, he'd decided. He'd chosen what to keep. He held on to Nell's name, *Nell*, two syllables, cheap and sturdy as an oarlock. He held on to the weight of a carving knife. The rest he let go in long silver strips: his childhood street, his teacher's face, the taste of the ale he liked, the reason he liked it.

The crown drank him and drank him and began, gloriously, to crack.

Hairline first — a singing in the salt, high and sweet, like ice on a warming pond. Then deeper. The light spilled out through the fissures in colors that had no names yet, and the dead swarmed brighter, feeding it past all bearing, and King Aldric rose off his knees with his own eyes looking out of his own face for the first time in years, and the crown shattered—

—and re-formed—

—and Orsen, half-gone, saw why, and understood the shape of the last problem the way you understand weather: too late to prevent it, just in time to name it.

The crown could break. It simply refused to die while it still owed anything. And it had swallowed a god's portion now, a feast of souls, and debts that size do not vanish. They transfer.

*Holder,* said the broken crown, in the voice of everyone it had ever eaten, *owes. Holder owes. Who holds?*

It hung in the air above the plinth, cracked and blazing, waiting for a hand. Any hand. It would wear whoever reached for it. It would spend whatever was left of them paying down a god's worth of debt, and when they were empty it would look, patiently, for the next.

Aldric stepped back. Wisely. He'd learned that lesson at cost.

And into the ringing silence, unhurried, boots clicking on salt-slick stone, came Guildmistress Vess.

She stood in the shattered doorway with the tidal wind pulling at her grey braid, and she looked older than Orsen had ever seen her — or perhaps he simply finally had the eyes to see it. Behind her, dim in the stairwell, the enforcers waited. In her hand she carried the guild seal, and her face was doing something complicated, the face of a woman reading a ledger line she couldn't account for.

"The map," she said. "The boy. The king. I was sent to collect." Her voice was steady, but her thumb moved over the seal, back and forth, back and forth. "I have been trying to remember, these last hundred miles, why I gave the order. Why I rode through the night. Why I—" She stopped. Her thumb stilled.

"Oh," Vess said softly. "Oh, you clever, stubborn boy. It's *mine*, isn't it. Some piece of me. You hid it somewhere I'd have to come and dig."

Orsen's remaining self had time to be astonished, and then not much else. Because Vess walked forward, past the king, past the floating ruin of the crown, and held out both her hands to catch the falling thing — and as she did, the light of it broke open one last seam, and out of the seam drifted a single small memory, unpressed, unsalted, whole: a young woman with a grey braid, laughing, holding a bone token she had carved herself, saying *this one stays silent. This one's mine.*

Vess caught the crown.

She caught it, and she remembered why she came, and the debt came due all at once — a god's ransom pouring into her, her stolen past flooding home through the cracks, too much, far too much, her eyes going wide and bright as the tower filled with white light and the sound of every freed soul cheering or weeping or both—

And Vess smiled at Orsen, and said, in a voice suddenly thick with forty years she thought she'd lost:

"*Burn it, love. Burn the whole bloody ledger.*"

* * *

Later — how much later, Orsen would never be able to say, because later had gone soft at the edges — the tower was dark, and cold, and empty, and there was a fire.

He sat beside it on the tidal road below, wrapped in the king's own cloak, because Aldric had insisted and kings, it turned out, could be remarkably stubborn when they remembered themselves. The royal ledger of the Salt Guild lay in the flames, page by page by page, and the smoke smelled like rain beginning.

One blank page he kept, folded against his chest.

Beside him, faint as breath on glass, a voice that was mostly gone but not entirely gone said: *You kept my name.*

"I did," Orsen said. "It's a good name. Fits in a small pocket."

*What else fits?*

He considered. His mother's face — gone. The city of his birth — gone, though he supposed he'd recognize it, the way you recognize a stranger's accent. His own name — gone, and the strangest thing was how little it itched. But there was a knife-weight in his right hand that felt like home. There was a king asleep against the rocks nearby, dreaming his own dreams for the first time in thirty years. There was a Guildmistress lying peaceful on the tower floor, smiling, fuller than she had ever been and finished, utterly finished, her long work done.

And there was, Orsen realized, patting his pockets with scarred hands, a truly alarming quantity of himself still unaccounted for.

"Well," he said, and found he was smiling. "There's one way to find out. Someone will have to ask me who I am."

The fire crackled. Down the dark road, small and bright, a lantern came swinging toward them — and a voice, calling out, wanting to know who was alive down there.

"Ah," said the man who had been Orsen Yarrow. "Right on time."
