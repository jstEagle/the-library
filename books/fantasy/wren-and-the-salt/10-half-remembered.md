## 10. Half-Remembered

There is a hole in me shaped like a mother.

I know this because I have a word for her and nothing else. *Mother.* It sits in my mouth like a stone from a river — smooth, familiar, belonging to somewhere I can no longer find. I say it some mornings, testing the weight of it, waiting for the current to come back. It never does.

The healers in the palace called what happened to me *sundering*. A tidy word for an untidy thing. The crown had begun to eat, they said, and when it cracked — when Nell and the drowned dead of that god's grave swarmed it like bees choking a wasp with their own bodies — whatever it had swallowed of me came apart on the way out. Some of me returned. Some did not. The pieces that came back were not always the same pieces that left.

Aldric understood better than any physician could. He met me in the recovery rooms of the palace, a thin man in plain wool who looked like a coat someone else had worn too long, and he held my hands in both of his.

"I know your name," he said. "That's more than I knew last month."

"Orsen," I agreed.

"And mine?"

"Aldric." Then, because I could not stop myself: "And one other. Nell."

His face did something complicated. Grief and gratitude sharing a small room.

"Yes," he said softly. "Keep that one. Whatever else falls through the cracks — keep that one."

I have kept it. It is the one thing I guard the way Vess guarded the guild's ledgers, the way the salt guards its secrets. *Nell.* She rides behind my ribs now instead of inside carved bone. Some nights I swear I hear her humming, tuneless and stubborn, and I hum back until we find each other.

Other things are gone. My mother's face — traded away, spent by a hungry thing in a tower, gone into the dark with no receipt. The smell of my childhood street. Which hand I favored as a boy. Whether I ever learned to whistle. There is a scar on my left forearm I cannot account for, and when I asked Aldric about it he laughed until he cried, which I chose to take as a good sign.

* * *

The first edict of the restored king was read from the palace steps on a gray morning with gulls screaming overhead like creditors at a door.

*The trade in borrowed years, stolen names, and pressed memory is hereby abolished throughout the realm,* the herald read, while below him stood a crowd of people who had sold things they could not remember missing and people who had bought things they could not bear to keep. *All ledgers of impossible commodities shall be surrendered or destroyed. All debts of memory are void.*

Aldric read it himself afterward, his voice rough, diminished but his own. He caught my eye across the square and did not smile. Neither of us smiled. It was not that kind of day. It was the kind of day where you tear something down and stand in the dust of it and hope you were right.

Vess was there. Of course she was.

She had come back to herself in the tower — truly back, the way a house comes back after a fire: standing, but scorched. Remembering why she had protected me had cost her nearly everything the guild had once paid her for her past, because remembering is a debt too, and hers came due all at once. What returned to her was a girl on a dock, a brother sold to the salt barges, and forty years of not knowing why her hands shook whenever she touched my carvings.

"I bought my way up the ladder with myself," she told me later, in the ruins of the counting house, "and then climbed down again to save a boy whose work sounded like home. Do you understand how insulting that is? To spend a whole life and get back one afternoon?"

"I'm sorry," I said, because I was.

"Don't be." She set the guild's great ledger on the table between us — the book of borrowed years, of stolen names, of every hour and face and birthday the Salt Guild had ever pressed into white grains. Her hand rested on it the way you rest a hand on a sleeping animal you may need to put down. "It wants burning. I've wanted to burn it since before I knew why. That should tell you something about wanting."

"It might not come back," I said. "What we burned. The memories in the salt. They might just — end."

"They ended already," Vess said. "Every time we made a sale, they ended. We only pretended the ledger kept them alive." She struck the flint herself.

We burned it page by page, because burning it all at once felt like drowning, and because Vess insisted on reading each entry aloud before consigning it to the flame. A debt of three years, forgiven. A name, returned to the wind. An hour of a husband's voice, released. When we reached the widow's entry — the first token I ever carved for the trade, the one hour of her husband's voice — Vess paused.

"He sang badly," I said, surprising myself. "I remember that much. Off-key, all the time, and she loved it."

"Do you?" Vess asked. "Or do you remember me telling you?"

I didn't know. That was the shape of my life now — a house where I couldn't always tell the furniture from the ghosts. But I found I could live in it.

One page survived. Not by accident. I took it out before the flames, folded it, and put it in my shirt over my heart, next to where Nell lived. Vess watched me do it and raised one eyebrow, which from Vess was a eulogy.

"For what?" she asked.

"So I start empty," I said. "Everything I carve from here goes on a blank page. No debts. Nothing pressed, nothing borrowed, nothing eaten. Just — wood and bone and whatever ordinary truth I can find in them."

Vess snorted. "Ordinary truth. You'll starve."

I didn't starve.

* * *

The shop sits on a low street near the tidal flats, where the road that isn't on any map still appears twice a day if you know when to look. I chose it for that. Some mornings I walk out onto the wet sand at low tide and stand where the road rises, and I think about the stranger who died in our doorway with a map in his hands and a king's true name on his lips. I never learned his name. Nell won't say it either — or can't; the salt drank deep of her before I pulled what remained. But sometimes, walking that road, I feel a presence beside me that is not quite her and not quite him, and we walk together until the water takes the road back.

The shop sells spoons. Buttons. Combs. Little wooden birds that fit in a child's fist. Bone needles and horn buttons and a shelf of walking sticks, none of which say anything at all. I carve them slowly, with my scarred hands, and when I finish a piece I hold it and listen, the old habit, and hear exactly what I want to hear:

Nothing. Silence. Wood being wood, bone being bone. The dead have finally stopped talking through my work, and I will not pretend I don't weep about it some evenings, or that I don't give thanks others.

Nell approves. "Boring," she says, in the voice behind my ribs, with enormous satisfaction. "You've become the most boring man in the realm. I'm so proud I could die. Again."

"You did die again," I point out. "In the tower."

"That was strategic," she says. "It was a tactical haunting. There's a difference."

There is, apparently, a difference. I have stopped arguing.

Aldric visits sometimes, in plain wool, unannounced, because a king who arrives announced brings the crown-shaped hole in the world with him, and neither of us likes the reminder. He has grown into his diminishment the way a tree grows around a fence post. He rules less and listens more. Last month he abolished the last guild charter and spent the afternoon in my shop failing to carve a duck, and it was the happiest either of us had been in years.

"The realm doesn't miss the trade," he said, shaving wood badly. "People miss forgetting, though. You know that. They line up outside my door asking for exemptions. Grief makes beggars of everyone."

"What do you tell them?"

"To grieve." He blew shavings off the duck, which now resembled a potato with ambitions. "It's cheaper than salt, and you keep everything."

Vess runs a school now, teaching children letters and sums and, quietly, how to hold a memory so it can't be taken without a fight. She still doesn't shake my hand. She grips my shoulder instead, hard, once, every time she leaves, like a woman checking a knot she tied herself.

Some days I lose things still. Small ones. The name of a fish. Where I set my flint. Once, terribly, for half a morning, the word *salt*, which sent me into such a panic that Nell talked me down for an hour, reminding me of tides and towers and a god's drowned grave until the word swam home.

"But it comes back," she told me. "That's the trick of it. The crown took things clean away. What's left of you just... misplaces. Misplacing means it's still yours."

I hold onto that. I hold onto *mother*, too, even hollow. I hold onto the scar on my arm and invent stories about it, and Nell invents better ones, and between us we've given that scar six origins, all of them lies, all of them ours.

* * *

On a bright autumn morning, a child comes into the shop.

She is maybe seven, gap-toothed, mud to the knees, holding a coin she has clearly defended against siblings for the privilege. She walks the shelves with the gravity of a magistrate. She considers the combs. She rejects the buttons. She stands before the little wooden birds for a long time, and I begin to suspect I am watching the most serious transaction of my week.

Finally she turns, holds up a spoon — plain birch, sanded smooth, saying absolutely nothing — and points at my hands.

"What happened to them?"

I look down. The scars cross my palms and fingers like tide-lines on sand, maps of a country that no longer exists. The crown's teeth. The tower's collapse. Work I can't entirely recall doing.

"Oh," I say. "Those are from my old trade."

"What was your old trade?"

I open my mouth. And here is the strange thing, the thing I will carry home like a shell from that beach: I don't know what I'm going to say until I say it.

"I used to make things that remembered," I tell her. "Now I make things that don't. These days I figure the world remembers enough on its own. Somebody ought to make things a person can rest in."

She thinks about this with her whole face.

"Did it pay well?" she asks, and I laugh, loud and honest, the kind of laugh that surprises even me, and Nell laughs behind my ribs, and outside the gulls wheel over the flats where a ghost-road waits for the tide to fall.

"It paid everything I had," I say. "This pays better."

She buys the spoon. She holds it up to the light like treasure, and it is one — birch and patience and nothing pressed inside it, no voice, no debt, no hunger. Just a spoon, in the hand of a child, on an ordinary morning.

Behind my ribs, soft as surf: *"Good one, Orsen. Keep going."*

And I do.
