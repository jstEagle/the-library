## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The storms arrived at dusk, as storms in Vell always did, though these came crated rather than howling.

Emrys Zhou stood in the receiving hall of the Guild of Impossible Things with her ledger open on the counting table before her, her pen uncapped, her lamp trimmed to a steady flame. Around her, six longshoremen wrestled barrels off a barge that smelled of tar and far-off rain, and inside each barrel — she could feel it through the staves, the way one feels a struck bell through a door — thunder turned over in its sleep.

"Careful with number four," she said, without looking up from her columns. "It's been agitated since Saltmere. The manifest says it was taken over open water."

One of the longshoremen, a broad man named Doss who had carried impossible cargo for eleven years and had opinions about all of it, shifted his grip on the barrel and gave her a look across the lamplit hall. "They're all agitated. That's what makes them worth the freight."

"They are not all agitated. Number four is *angry*." She made a small notation in the margin of her ledger — *barrel 4: temperament noted; handle accordingly* — because Emrys believed that anything worth knowing was worth writing down, and anything written down could be defended against the day someone claimed otherwise. "If it wakes while you're holding it, you will be the first thing it remembers."

Doss grumbled, but he set the barrel down more gently than the others, and Emrys returned to her work.

This was the whole of her world, most nights: the receiving hall with its high dark rafters, the counting table scarred by a century of clerks before her, the ledger with its ruled lines like a horizon waiting to be crossed. The Guild of Impossible Things occupied three warehouses on the salt end of the Vell waterfront, behind a façade so deliberately dull that customs officers walked past it twice a year without once growing curious. Inside those warehouses, the guild traded in everything fate had mislaid or men had stolen out of the world's own pockets — bottled memories, unmade vows, last words purchased back from the dying, luck by the ounce, grief by the cask. And weather. Weather above all, lately. Bottled thunderstorms were the guild's most profitable line, and its most dangerous, and Emrys kept both facts in the same careful hand.

She had been guild clerk for six years. Before that, she had been nothing anywhere, which was a condition she had worked hard to achieve and worked harder to keep.

* * *

To understand Emrys's precision, you would have to understand what imprecision had cost her.

She had come to Vell nine years ago with nothing but a name she no longer used, a talent she refused to touch, and a storm behind her big enough to have a reputation. They still told the story in the inland villages, though they told it wrong. A storm-caller, they said, who summoned a tempest out of a clear sky and drowned half a harvest and a bridge and two men she'd grown up beside. Exiled for it. Marked for it. Some versions gave her horns. One memorable version, which had reached even the Vell docks, gave her a voice like breaking glass and eyes the color of floodwater.

The truth was smaller and worse. She had never called any storm. She had been standing in her mother's field on an evening so mild that the swallows were still up, feeling — this was the part she could never explain, the part the tribunal hadn't wanted to hear — feeling something *reach* for her. Something vast and patient and pleased with itself, turning its attention toward her across a distance she couldn't name, and the sky had answered it before she could refuse. The storm had come down the valley like a debt collector. It had spoken to her, in the way weather speaks — in pressure and dread and the taste of copper — and she had said, aloud, into the wind, *I didn't ask for you,* and it had rained on her anyway, all night, all ruin, and left her standing in a drowned field with everyone in three parishes certain of her guilt.

She had learned two things from that night. First, that being needed is only another word for being blamed. Second, that if you keep excellent records, no one can ever make you responsible for more than exactly what is written under your name.

So she had built her life the way she built a ledger column: each figure entered, each sum checked, nothing owed that she could not pay, nothing paid that she could not prove. At the guild, where every clerk was some species of exile — smugglers' children, disowned magisters, a man who swore he had once been a footnote — precision was respected more than talent. Corvane himself, the Guildmaster, had looked over her first week's entries and said, "You write like someone who expects to be audited by God." He had meant it as praise. She had taken it as instructions.

And so: competent isolation. A room above the chandlery with one window facing the water. Meals taken standing. No debts but the ones in ink. No friends but the ones who needed nothing from her, which is to say none, which is to say the longshoremen, who liked her in the uncomplicated way sailors like a lighthouse — from a distance, and mostly for what she prevented.

It was a good life. She told herself it was a good life, in the same tone she used for closing out a quarter's accounts, and if the tone wavered sometimes, late at night, when the gulls cried over the harbor like questions nobody would answer — well. That too went unrecorded.

* * *

By the second bell of the night watch, the barrels were stacked and the longshoremen were gone, and Emrys began the audit proper.

Bottled thunderstorms were audited differently than other stock. You did not simply count them. You listened.

She worked down the row with her ear close to each bung, pen ready. Barrel one: a coastal squall, ordinary, honest-tempered, the kind of thing a fishing fleet might buy to break a blockade or a jealous merchant to spoil a rival's wedding. Barrel two: heavier, a proper gale, with a sound like a crowd muttering. Barrel three had gone quiet in a way that made her note *possible hibernation — check weekly.* Barrel four—

Barrel four was awake.

Not raging. Awake, and listening, the way a dog goes still when it hears its name in a stranger's mouth. Emrys stood with her palm flat against the cool staves and felt the pressure inside shift, gather, orient itself toward her hand with a focus that had nothing to do with chance. Storms didn't do that. Storms were appetite and accident. This was attention.

"Well," she said quietly. "What are you?"

Inside the barrel, very distinctly, thunder rolled — once, low, almost conversational.

She wrote: *Item 4 exhibits anomalous awareness. Recommend inspection by Guildmaster before sale.* And beneath it, after a moment, because the pen felt lighter than usual in her hand, she added in smaller script: *It knows I am here.*

Then she capped her ink and moved on, because there were forty-one items on tonight's manifest and a clerk who stopped at the strange things never finished the ordinary ones.

The rest of the shipment was routine. Three crates of bottled memories from a seized estate upriver — wedding days mostly, and one funeral, which was sad but common; people sold their worst days to the guild nearly as often as their best. A jar of unlived intentions. A small, sad parcel of unmade vows, seven of them, each still faintly warm, each tagged with the name of the person who had paid to unsay them. Emrys logged them all with the neutral efficiency that was her craft and, in a way she would not have admitted, her armor. Every item she recorded was an item that could not surprise anyone again.

Near the bottom of the manifest was the entry she had been circling all evening, the way your tongue circles a broken tooth.

*Debt outstanding: one (1) favor, unspecified nature, owed by the estate of Halven Marsh to the bearer of ledger page 112.*

Halven Marsh had been a client of the guild for thirty years. He had bought a bottled spring thirty years ago — the last true spring, the manifest said, from a country that no longer appeared on maps — and had promised in exchange a favor to be named later by the guild. He had died in the autumn. His heirs had sold his estate lock, stock, and library to cover other debts, and the favor had come due, and there was no one left to collect it from.

Favor-debts were the guild's oldest currency and its most troublesome. Most clerks wrote them off with a flourish and a shrug. Debts die with debtors; that was the custom, and custom was the spine of commerce.

Emrys did not write it off. She sat with the lamp burning low and the harbor breathing outside the shuttered window, and she thought about what a debt means. Not the coin of it — the shape of it. A debt is a promise that has outlived its convenience. It insists that the past still counts, that what was given must someday be returned, that nothing simply *ends* because it has become inconvenient. She knew about inconvenient endings. Her whole exile was one.

So instead she drew the line forward, past the estate's dissolution, past the heirs, past death itself, and wrote:

*Debt transferred to estate general. Estate general insolvent. Debt therefore stands against the estate's effects, including all lots herein catalogued. Collection deferred. NOT forgiven. — E.Z.*

It was, strictly speaking, unnecessary. It was also correct. Somewhere in the world there was a value still owed, and she refused to be the clerk who let it vanish because tracking it was hard. She blotted the page, squared the ledger, and felt the small cold satisfaction that was the closest thing to peace her life contained.

A debt can never be collected, and still be kept alive on the books. She understood that kind of arithmetic from the inside. Some nights she suspected she was such an entry herself: carried forward year after year, marked active, marked owing, collected by no one.

* * *

She was blowing out the lamp when she heard the footsteps on the loading dock — quick, light, wrong for the hour.

Tamsin, her apprentice, poked his head around the doorframe. He was sixteen, sharp-eyed, perpetually ink-stained, and possessed of the unshakable belief that the guild's warehouse held doors that opened onto wrong seasons — a rumor Emrys had spent six years telling him was nonsense, mostly because the alternative explanations unsettled her.

"You're here late," he said.

"You're here later." She nodded at the manifest in his hand. "Corvane wants the Marsh lot flagged for priority cataloguing. Says there's a buyer already interested."

"A buyer for what? The memories?"

"The library," Tamsin said. "The whole thing. Books, folios, the lot. He wants it itemized by dawn." He grinned, boyish, oblivious. "You get the old estates, don't you? All that dusty prophecy nonsense nobody else can read."

Something in the phrasing snagged — *prophecy* — but Emrys let it pass. Libraries were libraries. Dead men's books were the safest cargo in the building; they couldn't roll, bite, or wake.

"I'll start at first light," she said.

"Corvane said dawn."

"It's not dawn yet," Emrys said, and blew out the lamp, and in the dark of the receiving hall forty-one barrels of weather sat breathing softly in their rows — and one of them, the fourth, rolled its thunder once more, low and attentive, like something that had finally found the name it had been calling for years, and was content, for now, merely to wait.

* * *

She climbed to her room above the chandlery and did not sleep.

This was not unusual. Sleep and Emrys had negotiated a long armistice: she got the hours between third bell and gray light, provided she surrendered the rest to thinking. Tonight the thinking had a subject, and the subject was a debt that could never be collected, and the way the word *prophecy* had sat in Tamsin's mouth like a coin he didn't know the value of.

She lit her candle, took out her private book — not the guild ledger, but her own, the slim one she kept locked in the drawer with her mother's ring — and wrote, because writing was the only prayer she trusted:

*Tonight's date: [the date]. Audited 41 items. Carried one debt forward that reason says to bury. Felt watched by weather. Am probably tired.*

She hesitated. Then, beneath it, in the smallest hand she owned:

*I have been in Vell nine years today. Nine years since the field. Nobody has needed me for anything except sums. This is what I wanted. Writing it down to see if it looks true on paper.*

It looked true on paper. That was the trouble with paper. It recorded what was, not what should have been, and what was, was this: a woman alone above a chandlery, keeping perfect accounts of other people's impossible lives, having carefully arranged her own so that no one — not the guild, not the harbor, not the sky itself — would ever again have occasion to say her name with need in it.

Below the window, the fourth barrel dreamed its patient, directional dream. Above the window, the stars moved in their ancient indifferent ledgers. And somewhere east, across mountains she had never seen, in a frozen capital she had never thought of, a book a century old lay closed upon a date —

— tonight's date —

— and a name that was hers, waiting, as all good debts wait, for collection.

But that, of course, Emrys could not have known. She only knew that she could not sleep, and that the harbor sounded, faintly, like a storm remembering her, and that tomorrow she would catalogue a dead man's library, and find among the dust and folios one book bound in vellum gone amber with age, and read, in ink a hundred years dried, the exact hour of this sleepless night — followed by her name, her true name, the one she had buried with the drowned field and never spoken since.

She blew out the candle.

Outside, far off over the water, thunder grumbled — though the sky, as any clerk could have attested, was perfectly clear.
