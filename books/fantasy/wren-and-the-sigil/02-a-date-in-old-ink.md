## 2. A Date in Old Ink

The seized estate of Lord Halvern Marrowgate sat above Vell like a drowned man's last breath — a pale, sprawling house on the cliff road, its windows fogged with salt and its gardens gone to seed in ways that suggested neglect rather than poverty. The Guild had taken possession of it eleven days ago, after Marrowgate's creditors proved he'd been paying his debts with vows he didn't own. Emrys had been assigned to the library.

She was always assigned to the libraries. It was not flattery; it was triage. Corvane had learned early that she could look at three hundred uncatalogued volumes and see, not chaos, but an order waiting to be written down, and that she would write it down without stealing so much as a bookmark. *Emrys counts true*, the clerks said, the way sailors said a wind held fair. She had built her whole exile out of that reputation: a woman who could be trusted with other people's treasures precisely because none of them were hers.

So she sat at a trestle table beneath a window the color of old pewter, with the estate's inventory ledger open beside her own working notebook, and she catalogued dead men's books by candlelight because the day outside could not decide whether it was morning or dusk.

The fog had come in off the sea before dawn and simply stayed, pressing its gray face against every window of the house until the light indoors went strange and sourceless — as if the room itself were glowing faintly from within, the way certain bottles in the warehouse glowed when what they held wanted out. Emrys had worked in worse light. She had worked in better. She did not, as a rule, work in light that made her think of the warehouse, but today the comparison arrived anyway and would not leave.

She wrote: *Vol. 214. Sermons of the Tideward Brethren. Water-damaged, spine broken, value negligible.* And then, because precision was her one honest vice, she added: *Ink has run on pages 40 through 60 such that the sermons have become, in effect, prayers for drowning. Note for assessor.*

The quill scratched. Somewhere below her, in the house's gutted ballroom, two junior clerks were arguing over a crate of bottled laughter that had begun, worryingly, to laugh. Somewhere beyond the estate walls, gulls cried over a harbor where ships came and went carrying cargo that no customs officer in Vell would ever be permitted to see. And somewhere across the city, in the Guild's impossible warehouse, doors stood open onto seasons that had no business touching this coast — but those were tomorrow's problems, and tomorrow's problems belonged to tomorrow's ledgers.

Today there were only books.

* * *

Lord Marrowgate had collected the way lonely men collect — indiscriminately, hungrily, and with more money than judgment. His library ran to four thousand volumes, and perhaps sixty of them were worth the shelves they slept on. The rest were vanity printings, pirated genealogies proving his line descended from kings who had never existed, and treatises on subjects he clearly understood nothing about: the migratory habits of stars, the governance of bees, the proper care of griffins in captivity.

Emrys worked through them in strict shelf order, because sentiment had no place in an audit. She handled each book twice — once to assess, once to record — and she wore gloves for anything bound in leather darker than her own ink, because the Guild's insurance against cursed stock was, in its entirety, a pair of gloves and a clerk's caution.

It was on the second shelf of the seventh case, wedged behind a folio of maritime charts and a book of funeral recipes (she recorded it as *Vol. 231. Mourning Cakes and Other Consolations*), that her fingers found a binding that did not feel like paper and board.

It felt like skin that had been very patient.

She drew it out. The volume was small — smaller than her hand was wide — and bound in vellum the color of unbleached linen, soft at the edges from a century of handling by someone who had loved it or feared it, and she knew even before she opened it that those were often the same gesture. There was no title on the cover. There was no title on the spine. There was only, stamped near the bottom edge in silver that had not tarnished — silver never tarnishes when it is holding something shut — a single letter:

**V**

"Codex something," she murmured, and wrote in her notebook, *Vol. 232. Untitled. Vellum. Provenance unknown.* Her pen hesitated over *value*. She wrote *pending assessment* and told herself the hesitation was professional.

Then she opened it.

The first page was not text. It was a list — no, a ledger, and Emrys felt something in her chest tilt toward it the way a compass needle tilts toward north, because she knew a ledger's grammar when she saw one. Dates down the left margin. Entries beside them. But where a guild ledger recorded debts owed and payments made, this book recorded things that had not yet happened, written in a hand that changed across the decades — seven different hands, maybe eight, each scribe inheriting the work from the last like a lamp passed down a dark corridor.

*Third of Thawmonth, 811. The miller's daughter weds the ferryman. Two children. The river takes the second.*

*Harvestmonth, 814. Fire at the Tallow Street chandlery. Cause: a boy, a candle, and a lie about a dog.*

*Deepwinter, 819. The old harbor master dies in his sleep. He dreams of his mother.*

They were small prophecies. Small, domestic, precise — the kind of futures nobody would think to guard, written a century ago by someone who had apparently decided that if fate insisted on existing, it should at least be documented properly. Emrys read three entries and felt her professional soul stir with something dangerously close to admiration. This was accounting. This was accounting raised to its terrible perfection: every entry dated, every entry witnessed by ink, every entry — she checked four of them against the dates in her head — accurate.

The entries marched forward through the decades. Weddings, fires, floods, a hanging, three births, the slow sinking of a seawall, the arrival of a ship that the entry described only as *wrong*. The handwriting aged. The ink changed formula — she could tell, the way she could tell a forged signature from a true one, by the weight of the strokes. And still the book kept its accounts, decade after decade, right up along the years toward—

Toward now.

Emrys set the book down. She took her hands off it deliberately, one at a time, the way one steps back from a ledge. Around her the library waited, sourceless light and dust and the distant sound of bottled laughter, and she made herself breathe the way Corvane had taught all his clerks to breathe when stock behaved strangely: slowly, and while thinking about money.

"Pending assessment," she said aloud. Her voice sounded wrong in the big room. Smaller than usual. "You are Vol. 232. You are pending assessment. Whatever you are, you will be assessed."

The book lay closed and innocent on the table, vellum catching the gray light, looking for all the world like any other seized asset awaiting valuation.

Emrys looked at it for a long moment.

Then she picked up her pen, turned to a fresh page in her working notebook, and began — because if the thing insisted on being read, it would at least be read under controlled conditions — to copy out the last twenty years of its entries verbatim, dating her transcription, noting discrepancies, doing what she had done every working day for six years: making the impossible fit inside a ledger where it could be watched.

She was three entries from the end when she reached tonight's date.

* * *

She knew the date without checking. Every clerk in the Guild of Impossible Things knew the shipping calendar the way priests knew their saints' days, and tonight was a night she had already entered in three separate documents: the audit of the bottled thunderstorms was due, the Marrowgate assessment was due, and her quarterly reconciliation with the Vell customs bribes — officially *harbor maintenance fees* — was due. Tonight was a date she had written with her own hand four times this week.

It was written a fifth time in the Codex Vellum.

Not in the old hands. In fresh ink — black, sharp, recent, as though the entry had been set down that morning, though the paper around it had yellowed a century past. The date sat in the left margin in that same patient ledger-grammar, and beside it, in letters that seemed to press deeper into the page than ink had any right to go, the prophecy waited:

*Tonight the storm-caller comes to the book. She reads herself. The winter in the east grows a tooth. What was summoned wrongly must be answered rightly.*

And beneath it, in a different hand entirely — newer, harder, the strokes cut rather than written, as if the scribe had been carving instead of inking — a name:

*Emrys Zhou.*

Not her guild name. Not the alias she used at the harbor, not the false surname on her residency papers, not any of the careful little fictions she had layered over her life like tarps over cargo. Her true name — the one her mother had spoken over her cradle in a village that no longer appeared on maps, the one she had not heard aloud in nine years, the one she had told no living soul since the trial, since the storm, since the sentence that had taken everything and given her a number in a smuggler's ledger instead.

Her hand was shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way one notices weather through a window — a fact about the world, not yet a fact about oneself.

"That," she said, and stopped. Started again. "That is a forgery."

But even as she said it, the auditor in her was already running the examination she ran on every suspect document that crossed her desk, and the examination kept returning the same verdicts, each one worse than the last. The ink was period-consistent. The paper showed genuine age at the margins of the new entry — which was impossible, unless the entry had been written long ago and somehow remained wet, or unless the book had written it recently and aged it to match, and both of those possibilities were worse than forgery. The hand was not hers, and not any hand she knew, and it had spelled her true name correctly, including the accent mark that even she sometimes forgot, because she had spent nine years teaching herself to forget it.

Forgeries were made to deceive. Forgeries were made for profit. She turned the problem over and over, hunting for the profit, hunting for the buyer, hunting for the angle — because every impossible thing in the Guild's warehouse had a price, and every price had a payer, and every payer had a reason, and if she could just find the reason then this would stop being magic and start being crime, and crime she knew how to survive.

There was no reason. That was the horror of it. The entry stood there in its century-old book with its century-old margins and its fresh-cut name, wanting nothing, priced at nothing, owing nothing — except that it named her, tonight, and called her *storm-caller*, and said *she reads herself*, and Emrys sat in a dead man's library with her heart going like a hammer on a nailed-shut door and understood, with the cold clarity of a balanced column, that she had just read herself.

The prophecy had said she would. She had checked.

She checked again. It still said it. *She reads herself.* The words did not move. They did not need to. They were already true, and they had been true since before she was born, and somewhere in the eastern winter — *the winter in the east grows a tooth* — something had known her name for a hundred years and had simply been waiting for her to catch up to it.

Outside, the fog pressed closer to the windows. Below, the bottled laughter had stopped laughing, which was somehow much worse. And Emrys Zhou, who had built a life on the principle that nothing in this world could reach her if she recorded it fast enough, looked down at the open Codex and saw, with the particular despair of a clerk confronting an unbalanced book, that the next entry had already been written too.

It was dated tomorrow.

* * *

She did the sensible thing. She did the thing Corvane would have praised, the thing the handbook prescribed, the thing that had kept her alive through nine years of exile among people who traded in contraband fate: she closed the book, wrapped it in oilcloth, tied it with archive twine, labeled it in her neatest hand — *Vol. 232. Pending assessment. DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT CLERK OF RECORD PRESENT* — and carried it down through the fogged halls of the Marrowgate estate to report it, immediately, formally, and in writing, to the Guildmaster himself.

Corvane received her in the estate's former study, where he had established temporary headquarters amid crates of inventoried impossibilities. He was a spare man with a merchant's pleasant face and eyes that did arithmetic even when his mouth was being kind, and when Emrys laid the wrapped book on his desk and gave her report — measured, thorough, dated, witnessed by her own signature — she watched something move behind those eyes that she had never seen there before.

She had seen Corvane surprised exactly once in six years, when a shipment of bottled sunrises had arrived pre-opened. This was not surprise.

This was recognition.

"You found it," he said softly, and his hand came down on the oilcloth wrapping the way a man's hand comes down on the shoulder of something he has been hunting — gently, firmly, and with ownership. "After all these years. You found it, and it found you. Of course it did." He looked up at her, and smiled, and the smile was warm and correct and wrong in a way she could not yet name. "Emrys. My dear girl. Do you know how long I have been looking for a clerk clever enough to find this book?"

Somewhere out in the fog, on the salt road from the capital, hooves were striking sparks from frozen ground — but she could not know that yet. She only knew that the study had gone very quiet, and that the Guildmaster's hands had not let go of the book, and that every instinct she had ever trusted was telling her, in the flat clear voice of a final column refusing to balance, that she had just catalogued her own ending.

And that the man she worked for had been expecting it.
