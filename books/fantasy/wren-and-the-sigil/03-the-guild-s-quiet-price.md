## 3. The Guild's Quiet Price

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Emrys finally closed the Codex Vellum.

She had not read further than the line with her name. She had meant to — her hands had actually turned three pages past it before her courage failed, and then she had turned them back again, carefully, the way one returns borrowed things to a house that might notice. The book sat on her desk now beneath a sheet of oilcloth, and even through the cloth she could feel it. Not warmth exactly. Attention. The particular pressure of being looked at by something that had been looking for you for a hundred years.

Outside her window, Vell was doing what Vell always did after midnight: settling into itself like an old man into a chair, joints cracking, lamps going out street by street until only the harbor lanterns remained, burning green over the black water. Somewhere below, the night clerk was logging a delivery of bottled fog. She could hear his pen scratching through the floorboards, a small patient sound, the heartbeat of the only life she had.

*Emrys Zhou, called Wren, junior auditor of the Guild of Impossible Things.*

That was what she was. That was all she had been for nine years, and she had made a religion of it — of the neat columns and the double-checked sums, of the way a ledger never lied to you if you were honest enough with it first. A ledger did not care where you came from. A ledger did not ask why a girl of seventeen had walked out of the ashes of Harrow Fen with no family, no name worth speaking, and a reputation like a wet coat she couldn't take off. It asked only: *what came in, what went out, who owes whom.* And she had answered, faithfully, every night for nearly a decade.

Tonight the ledger owed her an answer instead, and she didn't want to collect it.

She should go to Corvane.

The thought arrived the way most unwelcome thoughts do — dressed as duty. Guild law was plain on the matter: any acquisition of prophecy-grade material was to be reported to the Master within one turning of the glass. She had found the Codex at dusk. Her time was running out, and Corvane would know if she delayed; he always knew. He had a way of appearing in doorways just as you decided not to tell him something, wearing that expression of mild, wounded patience, as though your small deceptions were pebbles in his shoe.

But her hand stayed flat on the oilcloth, and under her palm the book seemed to breathe.

*What if he already knows?*

No. She put the thought down like a hot iron. Corvane had taken her in when every other door in Vell had shown her its back. He had given her a desk, a salary paid in honest coin, a room above the counting-house with a window that faced the sea instead of the gallows-side of town. When the harbor magistrates had come asking about the storm-witch, he had stood in front of them with his ledgers and said, *this girl records weather, gentlemen, she does not make it,* and they had believed him because Corvane was a man people believed. You did not betray a man like that with suspicion. Suspicion was a debt, and debts were sacred.

She told herself this the whole way down the stairs, and it almost held.

* * *

Corvane kept late hours the way other men kept mistresses — discreetly, and with more tenderness than he showed anything else. His study occupied the top floor of the guildhouse, a round room walled entirely in ledgers, shelf upon shelf of them climbing to the ceiling, each spine dated in his narrow hand going back forty years. People assumed the room was a vanity. Emrys knew better. It was a fortress. Every deal, every debt, every secret the guild had ever bought or sold lived in these books, written twice, hidden thrice. Corvane trusted nothing he could not write down, and nothing written down could be taken from him without his knowing which page was missing.

He was at his desk with his sleeves rolled, adding figures by lamplight, and he did not look up when she entered.

"Wren." Not surprised. Not even faintly. "You've ink on your cuff."

She looked down. There was ink on her cuff. Of course there was; there was always ink on her cuff; he knew that too.

"Guildmaster. I need to report an acquisition."

"Mm." He finished his column, drew the line beneath it with the small flourish he permitted himself at the end of completed work, and set down his pen. Then he looked at her, and smiled, and it was his ordinary smile, warm at the corners, cool at the center. "Sit down, child. Would you like tea?"

"I'd like to report—"

"Tea first. Reporting is thirsty work. Ask anyone who does it honestly."

There was no refusing Corvane's tea. There never had been. She sat in the visitor's chair — lower than his, everything in this room slightly lower than his, she realized now, noticing for the first time in nine years — while he poured from the brown pot, two cups, no saucers, the way he did for clerks rather than guests. He handed hers across. Their fingers did not touch. He had never once let their fingers touch, in nine years. She had always read it as respect for her exile, her untouchability. Standing at the edge of some colder country now, she wondered if it had simply been care with merchandise.

"An acquisition," he prompted. "Go on."

"The Pellworth estate library. The seizure. I was cataloguing tonight — third shelf, the water-damaged folios." Her voice came out level. She was proud of how level. "There's a book bound in calfskin, unmarked. It's called the Codex Vellum. It's a century old. And Guildmaster—" here was where she should say *it contains prophecies*, here was where she should stop, because a report needed no more than that — "one of the entries is dated tonight. Tonight's date, written a hundred years ago. And beneath it—" her throat tightened around it, the way the words had tightened around her all evening, like a knot drawing shut — "beneath it is my name. My true name. Written in a hand that died before I was born."

The lamp flame leaned, very slightly, as if the room had inhaled.

Corvane drank his tea. Set it down. Turned his chair — not toward her, toward the wall of ledgers behind him, the years 1189 through 1193, the oldest ones, the ones whose spines had gone soft as bread — and drew out a slim volume she had dusted a thousand times and never once seen him open.

"You understand," he said, conversationally, terribly, "that I have to decide, right now, how much of a fool you think I am. Because the answer will determine a great many things about the rest of your evening."

"I don't—"

"You found it at dusk. It is now past the second bell. Four hours, Wren, give or take the honesty of the night clerk, who tells me you carried a wrapped bundle up to your room at half past seven and have not come down since." He opened the old ledger flat on the desk, smoothing the page with two fingers, an oddly gentle gesture, a man petting a dog. "Four hours is not treachery. Four hours is a person hoping she has misread something. I know that hope intimately. So we will skip the part where you confess, and I will simply show you."

He turned the ledger around and slid it across the desk.

The entry was dated the twenty-third of Frostmonth, eleven ninety-one — twenty-two years ago. Corvane's handwriting, young already precise:

*Acquired, via the Pellworth factor, one (1) codex, calfskin, contents prophetic. Assessed value: incalculable. Disposition: withheld from general inventory. Note to self — the thing wants a reader. Do not be the reader.*

"Do not be the reader," Emrys repeated. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, someone else speaking through her mouth.

"A century-old book that names dates and names," Corvane said, "is not a curiosity. It is a weapon, or a key, or both, and either way it appreciates. I have spent twenty-two years keeping it exactly valuable enough and exactly quiet enough. Do you know what the hardest part of that has been?"

Emrys said nothing.

"Waiting." He laced his fingers together on the desktop. "Prophecy is like wine, or revenge, or certain poisons — worthless until it matures, and then suddenly worth everything, all at once, to exactly one buyer. For twenty-two years I have watched that date approach on my calendar the way a farmer watches a field ripen. And now, tonight—" he spread his hands, and the lamplight caught his rings, four of them, gold and dark iron, "—the harvest."

"You're selling it." Flat. Stupid. She heard herself say it and hated the sound.

"I sold it six years ago, dear. The *delivery* is tonight."

The word *dear* landed like a slap, and she understood dimly that he had used it deliberately, that everything in this room was deliberate, that she had been living inside a machine built of deliberations for nearly a decade and had called it home.

"Six years ago," she managed, "I was fifteen. I was already—"

"You were already here. Yes." No flinch. He met her eyes with the composure of a man reading his own handwriting. "Which is precisely what made the arrangement possible. Listen carefully, because I am only going to explain this once, and then, I'm afraid, events will overtake us both. Six years ago, agents of the Carreth crown approached the guild through intermediaries. They were hunting for something specific. Something the royal house has wanted for a long time — longer, I suspect, than the current king has been alive. They wouldn't say what. They didn't need to. They brought me a copy of a page." He tapped the old ledger. "*Passage to the chosen one*, the contract says. Delivery upon maturation of the specified date, to the specified party, in the specified city. In exchange: the guild receives letters of safe-conduct throughout the northern provinces, tariff immunity at every crown port, and a standing license for trade in materials of class-four impossibility. Do you understand what that license is worth, Wren? It is worth ten guilds. It is worth everything this house has spent a century building, smuggled and bartered and bled for."

"And the price," Emrys said. Each word cost her something and she paid anyway, because she was a ledger-keeper and the sum had to be exact. "The price is me."

"The price is a delivery." His voice hardened, just barely, the merchant offended by sloppy accounting. "Nothing in the contract names you. Nothing in it names a person at all. It specifies a date, a city, and an item to be conveyed. What the item happens to be — a book, a girl, a storm — is a question of interpretation, and interpretation, Wren, has always been the guild's finest product."

"You've known." It wasn't a question anymore; it was arithmetic, and the numbers were obscene. "For six years. You watched me learn the trade. You gave me the Pellworth catalogue yourself — you *assigned* me that library. Three weeks ago. At your own desk, in your own voice, you said, 'Wren, the water-damaged shelves are yours.'"

"Yes."

"You sent me to find it."

"I sent you to *catalogue* it." For the first time something moved behind his face — not guilt; she would have recognized guilt; something more like annoyance, a man interrupted mid-transaction. "Child, sit back down. You're standing like a struck bell and I haven't finished. The crown's agents arrive tomorrow. Or tonight. Depending on the roads and the honesty of ferry captains. They will come here, to this guild, and they will ask for their purchase, and I will give it to them, because a contract is a contract and this guild stands on contracts the way churches stand on foundations. But *how* I give it to them—" and here his voice dropped, went soft, went warm, went sincere, the most dangerous instrument in the room, "—that is still mine to choose. There are ways to deliver a person to a fate and ways to deliver a *book*. There is a version of tomorrow where a calfskin codex crosses my desk, seals are signed, coins change hands, and a junior auditor named Emrys Zhou takes the morning packet south to spend the season auditing the Salt Road warehouses, far from prophecy, far from crowns, far from whatever it is that book thinks it wants with her. That version exists. I can build it. I have spent twenty-two years learning to build versions."

She wanted to believe him. That was the worst of it, the part she would examine later, in the long cold afterward — how badly, how *instantly*, she wanted to believe him. Nine years of loyalty reaching up in her chest like a drowning woman's hand. *He took me in. He lied for me to the magistrates. He is offering, even now, to lie for me again.*

"But," she said. Because there was always a *but* in Corvane's ledgers; she had learned to read his columns, and there was no such thing as a credit without a debit somewhere below it.

"But," he agreed pleasantly, "there is also the version where I refuse the crown its delivery. And in that version, the license dies, the safe-conducts die, the tariff immunity dies, and within a season the Guild of Impossible Things is thirty clerks and one warehouse of bottled weather with nowhere lawful to sell it. And within two seasons it is nothing at all. And every soul under this roof — Tamsin, old Merrick, the night boy with the cough, all of them — is out on the Vell streets with guild marks on their wrists and magistrates who remember our names." He picked up his tea again, unhurried. "So you see my difficulty. One clerk against ninety. One girl's fortune against a house full of fortunes. I don't expect you to forgive the arithmetic, Wren. I only expect you to *do* it. You're the best auditor I ever trained. Check my sums."

She checked his sums. That was the obscenity of it — she actually sat there, in the low chair, with the cooling tea untouched in her hands, and ran his numbers, because her mind did that, her mind had *always* done that, it was the one part of herself exile had never touched. Ninety livelihoods. One girl. A contract signed six years ago by a man who had taught her that debts were sacred, who had then sold the largest debt of all before she even knew she owed it.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, the thing she could not put a number to: the Codex had not said *passage to the chosen one*. It had said her name. It had known her true name, the name from Harrow Fen, the name she had buried with her family — and Corvane, for all his twenty-two years of careful husbandry, had never known that name. Which meant the crown's agents, whoever they were, whatever page they had copied, might know it too. Which meant this was not a sale she could haggle out of. The buyer already knew the goods.

"Who are they?" she asked. "The agents."

"That," said Corvane, "is above your clearance and below my knowledge. They pay in mint coin and they sign with a sigil I've never seen in any heraldry book." He paused, and for one unguarded instant the lamplight showed her something in his face that might, in a better man, have been unease. "A crowned circle. Like a sun wearing a ring of thorns."

The candle between them guttered, though there was no draft.

Somewhere below them, faintly, the guild's great warehouse bell rang — one stroke, the signal reserved for arrivals at the water gate outside of posted hours. Corvane's head lifted. He glanced at the clock, and Emrys watched him do the same arithmetic she had just done, the roads and the ferries and the honesty of captains, and saw the answer come up wrong in his face.

"Early," he murmured.

Then, louder, smooth as poured oil, already rising, already the guildmaster again: "Wren. The morning packet south leaves at dawn. I keep my word, such as it is. Get to your room, pack light, and stay away from the windows until I send for—"

The bell rang again. Twice. Then a third time, rapid, ragged — not the arrival code at all but the old alarm, the one drilled into every clerk and never once used in Emrys's nine years, and beneath it she heard boots on the gravel of the courtyard, many boots, moving in the disciplined rhythm of soldiers rather than the loose scatter of smugglers, and Corvane stopped talking mid-word.

Through the study's high round window, beyond the rooftops of Vell, Emrys saw them coming up the salt road from the north — torchlight, a long double file of it, riding through the freezing drizzle toward the guild gates with the unhurried certainty of people collecting a debt. And at the head of the column, taller than the rest, a rider carrying no torch at all, and above her, where a crest should have been, a standard that caught the harbor light and threw it back wrong:

A crowned circle. Like a sun wearing a ring of thorns.
