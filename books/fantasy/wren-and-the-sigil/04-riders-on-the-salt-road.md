## 4. Riders on the Salt Road

The storm came out of the north two days before the riders did, and Sable Vane counted it an omen, though she had stopped believing in omens somewhere between her oath and her exile.

She rode at the head of six, all of them oathbreakers like herself, all of them wearing the grey cloaks of the Crown's Errantry — a company that existed, officially, for the recovery of lost royal property. That was what Emrys Zhou was now, in the eyes of Carreth: property. A thing with a page number in a book a century old. Sable had read the warrant herself, stamped in wax the color of dried blood, and the phrase *the vessel of the prophecy* had sat in her stomach ever since like a stone swallowed whole.

The Salt Road ran white and flat between dunes crusted with frost, and ahead of them the port city of Vell rose out of the morning haze like something half-drowned — a sprawl of slate roofs and tarred spars, gulls wheeling over it in slow, screaming circles. Somewhere in that tangle of streets was a warehouse that did not exist on any map, belonging to a guild that traded in things no honest market would touch.

"Vane," said Dree, riding at her left. He was the youngest of them and still bothered to shave. "You've been quiet since Carreth."

"I'm always quiet."

"You've been *quieter*."

Sable said nothing. She was watching the road, where the frost had begun to sweat in the strengthening light, and thinking about the last time she'd been sent to collect a person instead of a parcel. The last time had gone differently. The last time she had done exactly what the seal commanded, and it had cost a queen her name and Sable everything else.

She had sworn, on the ice of a frozen courtyard, that she would never again be the hand that simply closed around whatever the crown pointed at.

So here she was, being the hand again. Life had a sense of humor like a headsman's: precise, patient, and never funny twice the same way.

* * *

Vell smelled of brine and lamp oil and, beneath it, faintly wrong — a smell Sable had learned to recognize in her years outside the capital. Impossible things left a residue, the way lightning leaves a taste in the air. The Guild of Impossible Things had been operating out of Vell for longer than anyone could comfortably prove, and the crown had tolerated it for exactly as long as tolerating it was useful. Useful, apparently, meant until the guild's own ledger-keeper turned out to be written into a prophecy.

They found the guildhouse by asking the kind of questions you weren't supposed to ask. A fishwife took Dree's coin and pointed with her chin. A beggar who was almost certainly not a beggar gave them a street. By late afternoon they stood before a door of black oak in a wall of whitewash, set into a narrow lane that smelled of nothing at all, which was itself suspicious — every lane in Vell smelled of something.

Sable knocked.

The door opened on a clerk — a girl of perhaps nineteen, ink to the elbows, freckles like spilled pepper across her nose. Her eyes went from Sable's grey cloak to the five behind her and did something complicated: fear first, then arithmetic, then a decision.

"We're closed," the girl said.

"We're not shopping." Sable held up the warrant. "We're collecting. Your ledger-keeper. Emrys Zhou. By order of the Regency of Carreth."

Something flickered across the girl's face — grief, quickly filed away, the way clerks file things. "She's not here."

"Then we'll wait."

"She's really not—"

"Dree," Sable said quietly, "check the back. Wren—" the girl flinched at the word, and Sable noted it, "—if she comes back tonight, tell her we'll be at the Salt Anchor inn. Tell her there's no need for anyone to be stupid about this. The warrant is for transport, not harm. She'll be treated as a guest of the crown."

*A guest of the crown.* The phrase tasted like the wax on the seal. Sable had used those exact words once before, to another frightened person in another doorway, and both of them had believed it, and one of them had died believing it.

The clerk — Tamsin, someone had called her — looked at Sable for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her jaw tighten.

"And if I don't tell her anything?"

"Then I'll have to come back, and I'd rather not." Sable lowered her voice. "I'm giving you a night because I remember what it is to have one. Don't make me regret the memory."

* * *

They did not wait at the Salt Anchor. Sable doubled the watch on the lane with the black door, and it paid off within the hour: a runner slipped out the back, quick as a rat along a skirting board, heading for the harbor district. Sable followed alone, telling the others to hold the front, because some instinct older than her discipline whispered that whatever happened next should happen with as few grey cloaks in the room as possible.

The runner led her through fishmarkets closing for the evening, past taverns already roaring, down a stair cut into the sea-cliff — and stopped at a door set directly into the cliff face, unremarkable except that the air around it hummed like a struck string. The runner knocked a pattern, the door opened, swallowed him, and shut.

Sable stood in the salt wind and listened to the hum.

Then she drew her sword — not to fight, but because holding it steadied her the way prayer used to — and knocked the same pattern, badly.

The door opened on darkness that was definitely too large for the cliff it lived in.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its warehouse folded up inside a space the size of a chapel, and Sable walked into it knowing, distantly, that she would never fully understand what she was seeing. Aisles stretched away farther than the cliff was deep. Shelves rose into gloom overhead, stacked with jars that glowed softly, crates that ticked, boxes wrapped in chains and labeled in three hands apiece. Lanterns hung from a ceiling that might have been sky; when she looked up too long, she thought she saw clouds moving up there.

And doors. Doors everywhere, standing free of walls, each one framed in different wood, each one leaking weather. Frost crept from under a door of pale birch. Heat shimmered above a slab of ironwood. From a third, green-painted and peeling, came the sound of rain on leaves.

Dree appeared beside her, breathing hard, having ignored his orders as usual. "Saints' teeth," he breathed. "Which way?"

"That way," said a voice, "is midsummer in the Ashvales, so I'd dress lightly."

A man came down the aisle with a lantern held high: broad, silvering, dressed in merchant-black trimmed with silver thread, wearing a smile like a coin — bright, round, and minted for circulation. Guildmaster Corvane. Sable knew the face from intelligence briefings; the crown had files on him thicker than most barons'.

"Guildmaster," Sable said. "We're here for your clerk."

"Yes, yes, the famous page." Corvane's smile didn't waver, but his lantern hand did, slightly. "You'll forgive my confusion. Royal agents usually *buy* passage to the chosen one. Collecting her outright seems — wasteful."

"The arrangement has changed."

"Arrangements do." He sighed, a man burdened by commerce. "Third aisle, past the bottled fog. There's a door of driftwood. She went through it not ten minutes ago. I'd hurry — driftwood doors are temperamental, and she's a clever girl."

"Why help us?" Dree asked.

Corvane looked at him with mild, genuine surprise. "Because the crown pays its debts, sergeant, and I keep accounts on both sides of any ledger. Go on. Time, unlike inventory, cannot be warehoused."

Sable ran.

* * *

The driftwood door opened onto winter.

Not the polite winter of Carreth, with its ordered snowfalls and swept courtyards, but a raw, howling winter — a mountainside under a bruised sky, wind driving ice sideways, pines bent like penitents. Sable stepped through and the cold hit her like a wall swung on hinges. Footprints stitched away down a slope toward a valley floor where, absurdly, a village glowed with lamplight, warm gold against the blue dark.

Emrys Zhou was halfway down the slope, small and dark against the snow, running with the desperate economy of someone who had clearly thought this through and hated every part of it.

Sable went after her.

The mountain fought them both. Snow broke underfoot in crusts, sank them to the knee, filled their boots. The wind screamed in a register that sounded almost like language. Twice Sable lost sight of the fleeing figure and found her again by the dark splash of her coat against the white. The village lights below never seemed to come closer — distance here was negotiable, and the door behind them was already negotiating.

At the lip of a frozen stream, Emrys stumbled, caught herself on a stone, and turned.

For the first time Sable saw her face properly, lit by the strange violet light of the wrong-season sky. Younger than Sable expected. Sharper, too — cheekbones like a keel, eyes the gray of the sea before weather. Not beautiful in any way the court would bother cataloguing. Awake, though. Terribly awake.

"Stop," Sable said, and hated how the word came out — official, breathless, borrowed from the crown's mouth.

"No," said Emrys Zhou, with feeling.

"I'm not going to hurt you. The warrant—"

"I know what the warrant says. I can read. It's rather the point of me." She was backing along the streambank, arms out for balance, voice pitched to carry over the wind. "It says *vessel*. Do you know what happens to vessels? They get *filled*. And then they get emptied, or broken, whichever is more convenient."

"Come back through the door and no one touches you. You'll be housed, fed—"

"A guest of the crown." Something bitter crossed her face. "Your clerk told me. Lovely phrase. What does the crown serve its guests, knight?"

Sable didn't answer, and the silence answered for her, and Emrys Zhou nodded as if a private theory had been confirmed, and turned, and ran — straight out onto the frozen stream.

Sable swore and followed.

The ice held, groaning. They ran down the white ribbon of the streambed, the village lights sliding closer now, real distance reasserting itself, and Sable gained on her by inches, by years of drill and hard roads, until they were nearly abreast and Emrys glanced sideways at her with an expression that wasn't fear at all but furious calculation —

— and then the ice spoke.

Not cracked. Spoke. A long, rising note under their feet, the pitch of a struck bell, and ahead of them the stream's surface bulged, and Sable understood with animal clarity that this water had never agreed to freeze, that this winter was a costume worn by a season that belonged somewhere else entirely, and that none of it — none of the door, the mountain, the ice — was built to bear weight honestly.

The ice let go.

It happened fast: a seam racing ahead of them, slabs tilting, the black water underneath exhaling its stored summer warmth into the air. Emrys went down sliding. Sable dropped flat, got a hand to the edge of a stable pane, felt her boots find the bedrock shelf beneath, and shot out her other arm.

Her fingers closed on Emrys Zhou's wrist.

They hung there a moment — the oathbreaker and the vessel, strung over black rushing water in a winter that had no business existing — and Sable looked down into the clerk's face and saw her hanging there not screaming, not begging, just watching Sable with those awake gray eyes, waiting to learn what kind of person had caught her.

Sable knew the answer. She had known it since the courtyard, since the queen, since the seal and the wax and the stone in her stomach. She was the kind of person the crown sent. She was the hand that closes.

But.

But she remembered a woman with snow in her hair saying, *you always have one moment, Vane, one moment where the oath and the truth part ways — and who you are is what you do in it.* She remembered choosing the oath, and she remembered the funeral, and she remembered that she had promised herself, in the only church she still attended — the cold open one, the one with wind for a choir — that she would never again be faster than her own conscience.

The crown wanted a vessel delivered. The crown had not asked Sable Vane what she wanted. The crown, as far as Sable could tell, had stopped asking anyone anything years ago; it only wore people now, like a glove wears a hand.

She thought of the warrant. She thought of the phrase *guest of the crown*, and how many guests of crowns ended in towers, and how the girl hanging from her grip had been sold to her by her own master with a coin-bright smile and a lantern held high.

One moment, where the oath and the truth part ways.

Sable hauled her up.

For a heartbeat Emrys thought she was caught — Sable watched the resignation settle over her face like frost — and then Sable turned her, shoved her, sent her stumbling up the bank toward the tree line, away from the stream, away from the village lights, away from her.

"Run," Sable said.

Emrys stared at her. Wind ripped her hair across her face. "*What?*"

"You heard me. Up the slope, east, there's a shepherd track — it'll take you off this mountain wherever it actually is. Don't go back through the door; Corvane will sell the door behind you. *Run.*"

"Why?" The word tore out of her, ragged. "You're supposed to— why would you—"

"Because I've been the hand before." Sable's voice came out lower than she intended, scraped raw by the wind. "Once. I did exactly what the seal said, and a woman died of my obedience, and I have spent nine years learning the difference between loyalty and being *used*. I'm not going to deliver a person to a tower because a century-old book says her name prettily."

"But you'll be — they'll—"

"Punish me? They'll have to catch me first, and I've been catching people for a living since before you could write in a ledger." Sable pushed her, gently, firmly, up the bank. "Go. And clerk — Emrys Zhou — whatever they told you the prophecy says, whoever told you it's safe to trust any institution that profits from you — don't. Trust the ones who hesitate. Hesitation is the only honest thing left in either of our professions."

Emrys stood one second longer, snow whirling between them, and something passed across her face that Sable couldn't name and didn't try to — recognition, maybe, of the specific loneliness of being handed mercy by a stranger with no reason to give it.

"Thank you," she said. Then, absurdly, clerk to the bone even while fleeing through a stolen winter: "If you're ever in Vell, ask for the guild. Ask for *me*. We keep accounts. Debts of honor included."

Then she was gone, up the shepherd track, a dark figure swallowed by the driving white, and Sable Vane stood alone on the bank of a river that shouldn't exist and felt lighter than she had in nine years, and colder, and more afraid.

* * *

The walk back to the driftwood door took an hour and cost her a glove to frostbite. When she stepped through into the humming dark of the warehouse, Dree and the others were waiting, and behind them, unhurried, lantern raised, stood Guildmaster Corvane.

"Well?" he said.

"Gone," Sable said. "The ice took the trail. If she lives the night, she's off the mountain."

Corvane studied her for a long, long moment — the ruined glove, the wet sword-sheath, the particular blankness of a face doing accounting — and Sable watched him decide, visibly, coin by coin, what version of events he could afford to believe. Merchants, she thought, believe whatever balances.

"A pity," he said at last. "The crown will want a report."

"It'll get one. Accurate." She met his eyes. "I'm told your guild deals in honest goods these days. Consider this my first entry."

Something moved behind Corvane's smile — there and gone, a fish under ice. "Of course, Dame Vane. Accuracy. Our founding principle." He turned to go, then paused, lantern light carving his face into coins and shadows. "Though you should know — the Codex doesn't lose track of its own. Whatever path she runs, the words will bring her where they want her. Prophecies are patient creditors." His smile widened by a degree. "We deal in impossible things, knight. But fate deals in *due dates*."

He bowed, slightly, and was gone into the aisles, and the lantern light shrank to a spark and died.

Sable stood in the humming dark among the impossible inventory, her frozen hand aching, and told herself the job was done, the report written, the debt discharged.

But that night, at the Salt Anchor, she could not sleep. She lay listening to the harbor bells and watching frost feather the window glass, and in the frost she kept seeing a pattern — lines crossing, converging, all of them running toward the same point on the map of her mind. North. Inland. Toward the frozen capital, where a beloved king smiled at his court with pauses a beat too long.

Toward Carreth.

She got up, dressed, and began packing, telling herself she was going to intercept the vessel before the crown's slower hands could — telling herself several things, in fact, none of which quite fit together, all of which pointed the same direction.

Outside, far to the north, above a city she could not see, the sky had begun, very quietly, to refuse to dawn.
