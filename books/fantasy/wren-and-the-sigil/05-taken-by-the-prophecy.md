## 5. Taken by the Prophecy

The Codex began to move before Emrys understood it was moving.

She had been reading it again — of course she had been reading it again; she had not put it down since the warehouse, had carried it to her narrow room above the counting floor with the guilty tenderness of a woman carrying a lit fuse — and she had been tracing the line of her own name with one fingertip, telling herself that names in old books meant nothing, that ink was not a hook, that she was a clerk and clerks did not believe in prophecy the way sailors did not believe in storms, professionally, while checking the rigging twice.

*Emrys Zhou,* said the ink, in a hand a century dead. *Called on the ninth night of the Drowning Moon.*

And then the ninth night of the Drowning Moon arrived, because it always had.

The words lifted off the page.

Not metaphorically. Not the trick of lamplight or tired eyes. The letters rose from the vellum like midges from standing water at dusk, a fine black swarm that hung in the air between her face and the candle, rearranging themselves, reordering themselves, and Emrys had time for exactly one thought — *this is not how books behave* — before they came for her.

They went into her the way cold water goes into a coat dropped from a dock: all at once, everywhere, without asking. She felt them settle along her spine like a second skeleton made of grammar. Her mouth opened. She did not choose to open it. What came out was not her voice, or rather it was her voice wearing someone else's sentence, a century old, patient as tide:

*The unbinding comes on the ninth night. She will walk out of Vell. She will arrive before morning.*

"No," Emrys said, and it was the truest word she owned. "No, I don't consent to — I am not *inventory*—"

The candle went out. So did every other light in Vell, she would learn later — the harbor lanterns, the guild's counting-room lamps, the lighthouse itself, all snuffed in the same instant, as if the whole port city were a page and something enormous had passed its hand across it.

Then the room left her.

---

It did not carry her so much as fold around her. That was the horror of it, once she could think at all: there was no sensation of travel, no wind, no rushing. There was only the sense of distance being *removed*, the way a clerk strikes through an error. Vell's salt smell was there, and then the smell of pine resin, and then snow — actual snow, the dry mineral cold of high country — and each arrived complete and total, as though the world between them had simply never existed and she had been mistaken ever to think it had.

She was falling. Or walking. Or being *written*. The distinction stopped mattering.

She tried to fight it the way she fought everything: precisely. She fixed her mind on the ledger habits, the small disciplines that had kept her sane through seven years of exile — count what is countable, record what is real. She counted her own heartbeat. Eleven beats in Vell's harbor fog. Twelve beats in pine dark. Thirteen beats in snow. The numbers kept going but the places kept changing under them, and somewhere past twenty she lost the thread and screamed instead, which was also a kind of record-keeping, the oldest kind.

The storm-gift woke in her without her permission, which frightened her worse than the folding did. It rose up her arms like a cat rubbing against its owner, eager, useless — thunder gathering in her knuckles, lightning itching behind her eyes — and when she flung it outward, desperate, at the darkness carrying her, it broke apart like foam on rock. Whatever held her had been built to hold weather too. Had been *built by* weather, maybe. Had been waiting for weather the way a lock waits for its key, and now that she was inside it, her gift was not a weapon but a signature, confirming delivery.

*Received in good order,* said the century-old voice, everywhere, gently. *One storm-caller, condition as described.*

"I'm a *person*," she shouted into the folding dark, and heard how thin it sounded, how much it sounded like a ledger entry arguing back.

---

She landed on stone.

Not fell — landed, upright, both boots striking flagstone in the same instant, the way a book closes flat. Her knees buckled anyway and she caught herself on something iron: a railing, frozen, biting cold enough to burn. She clung to it and vomited bile into the snow at her feet, and when she straightened, wiping her mouth with a shaking hand, she was standing on a terrace overlooking a city she knew only from shipping manifests and the gossip of smugglers who'd done time there.

Carreth. The frozen capital.

It lay below her in a bowl of white, tiered and glittering, a city built upward against a mountainside the way ivy climbs a wall. Even at this hour — and the sky said it was near dawn, though dawn was refusing to commit to anything, just a bruised gray suggestion along the eastern peaks — the city was awake. Lamplighters worked their rounds with the grim economy of people for whom daylight had become theoretical. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys in columns so straight and still they looked drawn rather than risen. And over everything, at the summit of the tiers, a palace of pale stone with towers like held breath.

Snow was falling. Emrys watched it for a long moment before she understood what was wrong with it.

It was falling *up*.

Thin streams of flakes rising off the rooftops, lifting past her terrace, climbing toward a sky that had no clouds to receive them. She stared until her eyes watered. In seven years of keeping accounts of impossible things — bottled memories, unmade vows, a jar of last words that whispered if you held it to your ear — she had never seen winter misbehave like this. Winter was the one honest thing left. Winter kept its books.

"Where am I," she said aloud, stupidly, to the railing, to the rising snow, to the book.

Because the book was here. Of course the book was here. The Codex Vellum lay on the flagstones at her feet, closed, innocent, its leather cover dusted with snow that was not melting on it. She kicked it away from her — actually kicked it, a childish, furious gesture, sending it skidding across the terrace — and it stopped at the edge of the light with a sound like a struck bell, and did not seem damaged, and did not seem chastened either.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them, cataloguing damage the way she'd catalogue any shipment: frostnip on the fingertips, a scrape along one palm where the railing's rust had bitten, nothing broken. Her satchel had come with her — the folding had taken the bag but not, apparently, considered whether she wanted it. Inside: her spare stylus, half a loaf gone stale, three ledgers of guild business she suddenly could not imagine caring about, and the little tin of tea leaves Tamsin had given her two birthdays ago, saying *you hoard everything else, might as well hoard something nice.*

Tamsin. Corvane. The guild. All of it was a thousand miles south, across mountain passes that closed by now, in a city where she had spent seven years building a life whose entire architecture was *not being needed anywhere else.*

She sat down on the frozen steps of the terrace, pulled her knees up, and allowed herself exactly sixty seconds of despair. She timed it. It was a habit from the bad first year in Vell, when the nights got long and the storm-memory got loud: grieve on schedule, then get back to work. Sixty seconds. She used fifty-eight of them shaking, and spent the last two breathing, and stood up.

"All right," she told the palace. "You wanted me here. Let's discuss terms."

The palace did not answer. But the snow kept falling upward, patiently, like a debt climbing interest, and Emrys thought: *someone is paying for this.* Weather did not misbehave for free. Somewhere in this beautiful frozen city, something was spending fuel to keep the season wrong, and whatever it was spending, it had spent some of it on her. On fetching her. A century ago, someone had written tonight's date in ink and waited, and waited, and tonight the invoice had finally come due — and she was the payment.

She was not a person to this thing. She was a *line item.*

The fury that rose in her then was cleaner than the fear, and she welcomed it. Fury had a ledger of its own. Fury could be balanced.

---

She found the city gate by following the smoke, and the guard post by following the argument.

Two soldiers stood at the archway, stamping their feet, watching the upward snow with expressions she recognized from the guild's warehouse floor: the look of working people encountering contraband reality and deciding, for the sake of their wages, not to have noticed. When they saw her — mud-spattered, frost-burned, clearly having arrived from nowhere — they did not reach for weapons. They reached for a document.

"You'll be the guest," said the older one, not unkindly.

"The *guest*."

"Expected." He checked the paper against her face the way she checked manifests against crates. "Ninth night of the Drowning Moon. One arrival, by unusual means." He lowered the paper. "Begging your pardon, miss, but you're the fourth this winter."

The words went through her colder than the snow. "The fourth *what?*"

"Guest of prophecy." He said it the way men say things they've been instructed to say and have privately decided mean nothing. "Palace sends a sled for each of 'em. Warm blankets. Good soup, I hear. Better than we get." He jerked his chin at his companion, who whistled twice, and somewhere in the white streets below a bell answered, and hooves began to climb.

Emrys wrapped her arms around herself and did the arithmetic she could not stop doing. *Fourth.* Four arrivals, this winter alone. A century-old book naming dates and names. A palace prepared to receive them, with sleds and soup, with the smooth practiced hospitality of an institution that has done this many times and expects to do it many more.

The guild took in strays and sold passage to fools. Emrys had known that for years and called it commerce. But this — a kingdom with a standing process for harvesting prophesied strangers — this was something else, something with the scale and patience of the crown itself, and she stood in the falling-up snow and understood with terrible clarity that whatever had folded her across the country had not acted alone. Books did not arrange sleds. Someone in that pale palace had read the same line she had, a hundred years ago or yesterday, and built a receiving dock for it.

The sled arrived. It was black lacquer and silver trim, absurdly luxurious, drawn by two gray horses with frost in their lashes, and driven by a woman in palace colors whose face was pleasant and whose eyes were careful in a way Emrys had learned to read over ledgers full of lies: the eyes of someone who has counted the money and knows it doesn't add up.

"Wren?" the driver asked, using the name from the manifest, the false name, the guild name — and Emrys's blood went cold all over again, because the prophecy had known her *true* name, but whoever wrote this driver's orders had used her alias, which meant the two documents disagreed, which meant somewhere in Carreth there were two different accounts of who she was, and discrepancies were the first place every fraud she'd ever audited came apart.

"That's me," she said, and got in, because the alternative was freezing on a mountainside, and because a clerk knows: follow the paperwork to its source.

The sled moved down through the waking tiers of Carreth. People came out of doorways to watch it pass, and they smiled at her — warm, delighted smiles, faces lifted toward the sled the way faces lift toward a festival. A child ran alongside for a dozen strides, waving. An old man pressed a hand to his heart.

"They think you're a blessing," the driver said, without turning. "Winter's been wrong for three years. Longer. The king promised the prophets would mend it." A pause, the reins creaking. "The last three guests went up to the palace and the winter got worse, and folk loved them all the same. Folk love hoping more than they love being right."

"What happened to them?" Emrys asked. "The last three."

The driver's shoulders did something small and final. "Nobody asks that, miss."

"I keep ledgers," Emrys said. "Asking is my trade."

The driver glanced back then, and for a moment her careful eyes met Emrys's, and something passed between them — not information, the woman was too well-paid for that, but its shape, its absence, the hole in the record where the answer should be. Then she faced forward again, and the horses climbed, and the palace grew.

Up close it was worse than beautiful. The pale stone had veins running through it, hairline threads of silver that caught the bruised dawnlight and seemed almost to pulse, slow as a sleeping heart. Emrys had appraised enough cursed inventory to know material that was *fed*. Every window glowed warm. Every door stood open, as if the building had been holding them open all night, waiting.

On the topmost tower, against the graying sky, something glinted. She squinted. Gold, perhaps. A weathervane.

No. A crown, mounted on a spike of stone like a trophy or a warning, turning slowly in the windless air, and even at this distance Emrys's skin prickled with a recognition she hated — the same pressure she'd felt in the warehouse, the same taste of ozone and old decisions. Whatever wore the king of Carreth, a piece of it lived up there, and it had seen her coming for a hundred years, and it had set out a sled.

The sled halted at the great doors. Servants descended, smiling, murmuring welcome, wrapping a fur around her shoulders with hands that were gentle and impersonal as weather.

"Welcome to Carreth," said the chamberlain, a dry neat man with ink-stained fingers — a fellow accountant, she thought wildly, they've sent an *auditor* to receive me — "the court has awaited you with great joy."

"Has it," Emrys said.

"For nine days short of a century," said the chamberlain, and smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face except the eyes, and over his shoulder, through the open doors, down a hall hung with the portraits of kings, Emrys saw the throne at the far end — and saw, seated upon it in the pre-dawn gloom, a silhouette that was already looking at her.

Had been looking at her, she understood, the whole ride up.

Had probably been looking since Vell.
