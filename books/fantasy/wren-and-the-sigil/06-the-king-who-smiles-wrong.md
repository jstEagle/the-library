## 6. The King Who Smiles Wrong

They dressed her in borrowed blue.

Two handmaidens with careful hands and no names they were willing to share worked the laces of a gown that had clearly been made for someone else — someone taller through the shoulder, softer through the waist — and Emrys stood on the little stool before the tall mirror of the tower room and let them, because there was nothing else to do. The glass had a flaw in one corner that bent her reflection sideways, so that she seemed to be leaning perpetually toward something just out of frame.

*That's honest at least*, she thought. *Everything here is going to pull you off-center.*

"The court receives guests of prophecy at the fourth bell," said the elder handmaiden, in the flat recitation of someone who has been made to say a thing many times without understanding it. "You will be presented. You will not speak unless the King addresses you. If the King addresses you—" a hesitation, so brief Emrys might have imagined it, "—answer simply."

"And if I answer complicatedly?"

Neither woman smiled. The younger one finished the last knot at her spine and stepped back, and both of them looked at Emrys in the mirror with an expression she had spent five years learning to read across a guild ledger: the look of clerks balancing accounts they were not permitted to discuss.

"Guests of prophecy," the elder said again, as if repetition were explanation, "are rarely presented twice."

Then they were gone, and the door locked behind them from the outside with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and Emrys was alone with the borrowed gown and the crooked mirror and the long slide of afternoon light down the tower wall, which she had already measured twice that day and would measure again. Fourth bell. She had perhaps three hours to stand in a room designed to be pleasant enough that no one thought of it as a cell.

She used them the way she used everything: keeping accounts.

The window: barred, but the bars were old and the mortar around them older, and she marked which stone would come out first if it ever came to that. The door: oak, iron-banded, opening inward — which meant whoever came through it would have to turn their back to the room's far corner for half a heartbeat, and she memorized the corner. Her own body: fed, rested, uninjured, and utterly without leverage, because whatever the prophecy wanted of her, it had crossed a kingdom in a single night to collect, and things that want that badly do not generally negotiate.

She wrote none of it down. There was no ink in the room, no paper, no quill; someone had thought of that, and the thinking frightened her more than the lock. In Vell, Corvane kept his ledgers under three seals and a warding-salt circle, and still Emrys had found ways to read what he didn't want read. Whoever ran this palace did not bother hiding anything from her, because they did not consider her someone who could matter. Guests of prophecy were not threats. They were deliveries.

*And yet*, she thought, turning once in the gown, watching the blue swing, *someone paid for this dress. Someone chose the color.* It was almost her shade — the grey-blue of storm-light over water, the color she'd been told her eyes went when the weather in her blood stirred. Almost as if the invitation itself had been written by someone who knew her.

Or by a book that did.

At the fourth bell, the lock cracked again, and Sable was there.

She looked different out of the salt road's mud: same battered armor, but brushed down; same grey cloak, but clean; and beneath it all a court uniform she wore the way some people wear a debt, collar high, every button fastened as if to hold the past against her throat. Her oathbreaker's brand showed at her wrist where the gauntlet didn't meet the vambrace — a black circle, broken open at the bottom, like a cup that had failed to hold something. Emrys had seen brands like it on two other guards since arriving, but Sable's was older, and scarred over, as though someone had tried to cut it out and thought better of it halfway.

"You're to walk behind me," Sable said. "Left side, half a step. You don't speak in the corridors. You don't look anyone in the eye except the King."

"That last rule seems oddly specific."

"It is." Sable's face did the thing it had done on the salt road when she'd let Emrys run and then reported her caught anyway: a small tightening at the mouth, a ledger-entry of a expression, recording a cost. "It is also the only rule I will tell you the reason for, later, if there is a later and we are somewhere with thicker walls. For now, walk, be silent, and if I put my hand on your shoulder, go wherever I push you. Immediately. Without asking."

"Is that an oathbreaker thing or a guild-clerk thing?" Emrys asked. "Because I've given instructions like that myself. Usually right before the shipment catches fire."

Something moved at the corner of Sable's eye — not quite a wince, not quite its opposite. "Both," she said, and opened the door.

* * *

The palace of Carreth was built for warmth the way ships are built for storms: obsessively, defensively, with an air of apology about the whole endeavor. Every corridor was doubled — an inner hall of pale wood and glass, an outer gallery of stone — and between them ran channels where hot water murmured day and night, so that the walls themselves sweated gently in winter. Tapestries of summer hung everywhere. Summer meadows, summer hunts, summer queens with their sleeves rolled in orchard light. Emrys counted eleven of them between the tower and the great doors of the audience hall, and not one tapestry of snow, in the capital of the long winter, as if the century of cold could be embarrassed into leaving by sheer refusal to depict it.

The people, though — the people were warm in earnest.

Courtiers lined the processional route in clusters, and as Sable marched and Emrys followed at her prescribed half-step, the clusters turned, and hushed, and *looked*. Not at Emrys. She might have been furniture, and was content to be. They looked at the doors ahead, at the throne room's threshold, the way crowds look at a stage before the curtain — hunger and tenderness at once, an appetite for the sight of him.

"He's beloved," Emrys murmured, too low for anyone but Sable to hear.

"He's perfect," Sable said, in a voice with nothing in it. "There is a difference, and it is the entire problem, and you will understand it within the minute, and I am sorry for it."

The great doors opened.

The throne room of Carreth had been built to make a person feel small before a king, and it succeeded even now, even with what sat — *stood*, she corrected herself, *he stands*, the correction arrived late and slightly wrong — upon the dais. Banners of white and gold fell from the vaulting. The floor was polished to the color of frozen honey. And the court was arranged in its crescents, hundreds deep, and every face in the first three rows wore the same expression: that soft, upturned, faintly damp-eyed devotion Emrys had previously seen only in chapels, and only among people praying to something they believed was listening.

King Aldous of Carreth came down the steps to meet his guests himself.

That, she learned later, was unheard of. Kings do not descend for clerks. But he descended, unhurried, smiling, and the smile preceded him through the room like weather — she watched it move along the front row of courtiers, watched faces loosen and brighten as it passed, a tide of small involuntary joys — and Emrys understood, in the abstract, generous way one understands a legend, why they loved him. He was handsome in the unthreatening manner of a man who has never needed to prove it. He moved like someone genuinely glad to be exactly where he was. When he stopped before them, the warmth coming off him seemed almost literal, a hearth-glow, and the whole hall leaned toward him the way plants lean at a window.

"Welcome," he said, and his voice was beautiful. Deep, easy, amused at some private comedy that included everyone. "Carreth has waited a long time for you."

And Emrys looked at his eyes and felt the bottom drop out of the morning.

* * *

Here is what the ledgers had taught her, and what no one warns you about numbers: arithmetic does not care how much you wish to be wrong.

His eyes lagged.

Not much. Not enough that anyone in this adoring hall would ever catch it, because no one here was looking for it, because looking for it would be unthinkable. But Emrys had spent five years watching merchants' faces for the half-beat between question and answer where the lie lives, and she knew what she was seeing. When King Aldous turned his head, his head began the turn and his gaze arrived afterward — a fraction, a sliver, the smallest possible delay between the instrument and the player. When he blinked, the blink was a shade too slow on the closing side, like a door easing shut on hinges that had begun, very quietly, to stick.

And his pauses. He spoke to Sable — "Warden Zhou. Your house disgraced itself; you, it seems, did not entirely; interesting" — and the sentence flowed like water, but between each clause there hung a pause, tiny, regular, metronomic, and the pauses were all *the same length*. Human pauses vary. These did not. These were the pauses of a thing counting.

He turned to Emrys then, and the full weight of that lagging gaze settled on her, warm as lamplight, and behind the warmth — far back, at the very bottom of the grey-blue irises, down where most people keep their oldest grief — something *looked back*. Something that was not warm. Something that regarded her with the patient, evaluating attention of a clerk reading a manifest to see whether the shipment matches the invoice.

*Found you*, said the thing in the depths of the king's eyes, or perhaps said nothing at all, and it was Emrys's own skin crawling that put the words there. She couldn't tell anymore. That was the horror of it. The charm was real. The warmth was real. Whatever wore this man was wearing him *well*, had studied him for years, and gave the court a better Aldous than Aldous had probably ever managed alone — kinder, funnier, more present. No wonder they loved him. He was the improved edition.

And underneath the improvement, the original was being drunk.

She saw it plainly then, once she knew to look: the pauses weren't gaps in the king's speech. They were swallows. Each one a fraction of a second where the light in his face dimmed like a lamp drawing oil, where the man behind the smile reached for the next word and had to wait while something else decided whether he could have it. His hands, folded graciously at his waist, were beautiful hands, cared-for hands — and the fingernails had the faintest grey cast, like frost forming on a window from the inside. His hair was thick and dark and not one strand of it had gone silver, and the man was fifty if he was a day, and no dye in the world holds that evenly.

And above his brow — bare-headed, no crown today, of course, crowns are for coronations — sat a mark. She had taken it for a courtly ornament at first, a gilded circlet tattooed into the skin, elegant, antique, a line of script-like figures ringing his forehead like a diadem painted on. Now she saw the truth of it and her stomach turned slowly over. The mark was not painted on. It was *growing out*. The gold of it continued below the skin in fine dark threads, running back along his temples, down behind his ears, following the lines of his veins the way ivy follows a wall — and at the center of his forehead, where the threads converged, the skin was thin and faintly luminous, and it *moved*, very slightly, with each of those metronomic pauses, contracting like a mouth.

The sigil-crown. Not worn. Rooted.

"Guest of prophecy," the king said to her, warmly, warmly, and paused, and drank, and smiled. "You crossed a kingdom in a night. The book must want you very much."

Every instinct she owned told her to say something small and safe — *I'm honored, majesty* — the answer of a woman who had survived five years in a smugglers' exchange by being furniture when furniture was safest. She opened her mouth to give it.

But the lag was still there, hanging in his eyes a half-beat behind his smile, and in that lagging gap, down at the bottom of the grey, the original Aldous — the drowned man — looked out at her. Just for the length of one pause. One swallow. And what was in his face, in that sliver of a moment, was not rage or madness or any grand operatic suffering.

It was the ordinary, exhausted pleading of a man standing at a window, watching helpmates pass on the street below.

"Your majesty," Emrys heard herself say, quietly, in her flattest guild voice, the one she used for men counting coins, "who is speaking?"

The hall did not react, because the question sounded like courtesy. Sable's hand arrived on her shoulder — *go wherever I push you* — but did not push, merely gripped, hard, a warning delivered through the palm.

The king smiled. The smile took longer than it should have to arrive — a full second, maybe two, the longest pause yet, and in it the whole radiant machinery of him seemed to hang, a lamp with the wick turned low, the hall's collective breath held by hundreds of people who did not know they were holding it —

— and then the warmth flooded back, brighter than ever, and he laughed, a rich and genuine sound, and the court laughed with him in relief and delight, and he said:

"What a question," King Aldous said. "I ask myself that every morning."

And the court roared with laughter at the wit of their beloved king, and the sigil at his brow pulsed once, softly, like a throat swallowing, and Emrys laughed too, because not laughing was unthinkable, and her laugh came out in the exact key of all the others, and that — more than the tower, more than the lock, more than the missing ink — was the moment she understood precisely what kind of place had collected her. Not a prison.

A household where everyone had learned, to survive, to laugh in the same key.

* * *

The presentation ended; the court dissolved into wine and murmur; Sable walked her back through the doubled corridors with her hand never quite leaving Emrys's shoulder blade. Only when the tower door had shut behind them — unlocked now, interestingly, from the inside; Sable had a key — did the knight speak, staring at the wall, in the tone of someone reading a confession aloud from a document she'd hoped never to sign.

"The reason for the rules," Sable said. "You asked on the stairs. Here is the reason. Everyone who looks him in the eye too long starts to *love* him. Not admire. Love. Courtiers have ruined themselves for him, thrown away titles, marriages — there is a lady of the bedchamber who tried to walk into the winter garden in her shift because he remarked once that the roses must be lonely." She finally turned. Her face was grey. "You looked at him for four minutes and asked him a question instead. In eight years of presentations, I have never seen anyone ask him a question."

"I balance ledgers," Emrys said. Her voice shook; she let it. "When something is drinking from the till, the number comes up short. You learn to see short."

Sable looked at her for a long moment, and Emrys watched the oathbreaker decide something — file it, weigh it, seal it under wax.

"Fourth bell tomorrow," Sable said, moving to the door. "The King has requested you attend him privately. To discuss the prophecy."

The lock clicked shut behind her.

Emrys stood in the failing light, in the borrowed blue, and looked at the crooked mirror where her reflection leaned forever toward something out of frame — and on the surface of the glass, in the dust no handmaiden had wiped, she saw that someone had scratched a single word, long ago, in letters small enough that only a clerk who audited everything would ever find them:

*count the pauses.*

She counted backward through the presentation from memory, the way she counted everything, and the total came out wrong — one pause too many, near the end, a swallow she could not account for.

And in that extra pause, she realized with a cold sliding feeling, the king had been looking not at her face.

At her hands. As if checking a delivery against an invoice.

As if confirming the shipment matched what had been ordered, a century ago, tonight.
