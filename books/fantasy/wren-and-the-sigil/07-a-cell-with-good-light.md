## 7. A Cell with Good Light

They did not put her in a dungeon.

Emrys had expected a dungeon. She had spent the whole of the collapsing night — that terrible hour of folding country, of Vell's salt-smell giving way to pine and then to nothing at all — composing a reasonable inventory of what awaited her at the end of the prophecy's pull: chains, straw, darkness, the usual architecture of captivity. She had even, in the last moments before the world set her down like a chess piece on a board she'd never agreed to play on, taken a certain grim comfort in the expectation. Dungeons were legible. Dungeons could be survived by a woman who knew how to count.

Instead they gave her a room.

It occupied the top of a tower on the eastern face of the palace of Carreth, and it was, by any honest accounting, beautiful. The walls were paneled in pale birch wood, unvarnished, so that the room smelled faintly of forest. There was a bed with a goose-down mattress, a writing desk with an inkwell of blue glass, a shelf holding eleven books whose spines had been selected, it seemed, by someone who knew exactly what a clerk would want to read when bored past endurance. A fire burned in a hearth small enough to be ornamental and efficient enough to be no such thing. And there were windows — three of them, tall as doors, glazed with glass so clear it was nearly a boast — looking out over the frozen capital and the white plain beyond, where the long winter lay over everything like a hand pressed flat against the throat of the world.

*Guest of prophecy*, the summons had read, when they carried her through corridors hung with tapestries of kings. *The Crown welcomes the storm-caller Emrys Zhou to Carreth, in fulfillment of that which was written.* No one had said *prisoner*. No one had needed to. The door had no lock, but it had a guard, and the guard had a face like a closed ledger, and the tower stairs went down and down and ended in a corridor that ended in more guards, and Emrys had done the arithmetic before they'd finished showing her the water closet: this room was a cell with better lighting, and cells with good light were still cells.

But she was a clerk. And a clerk, given paper, ink, a window, and time, will do what clerks do.

She began, on the first morning, with an audit.

* * *

The palace revealed itself slowly, the way debts do.

From the eastern windows she could see the sweep of the capital below: the frozen river Carreth cutting the city in two, its current locked mid-motion beneath grey ice; the market squares buried under snow that no one seemed to be shoveling, as though the city had agreed collectively to stop pretending spring might come; the temple district, where a hundred chimneys stood cold. The long winter was in its fourth year — she knew this from the books on the shelf, someone had thoughtfully provided histories — and the histories described it the way ledgers describe a slow bleed. Not famine yet. Not collapse. Just less, every year. Fewer ships, fewer children, fewer bells rung for anything.

And above it all the palace itself, and within the palace, at its heart, the throne hall — which she could not see directly but whose position she could fix by the behavior of everything around it. Snow, she noticed on the second day, fell strangely near the palace's center. It drifted inward. She watched for an hour, testing herself against her own eyes, and confirmed it: flakes that should have passed over the roofs bent instead toward the great central spire, gently, persistently, like iron filings finding north.

She wrote it down. *Day 2. Snow converges on the throne hall. Note: does not melt on contact. Accumulates.*

On the third day she counted guards. This was a discipline of hers, born of years auditing the guild's warehouse, where knowing how many hands were on a floor told you what that floor was worth. From the western window she could see the changing of the watch at the inner gate: four reliefs, eight men each, rotations at dawn and dusk and midnight. From the eastern window, the tower stair door opened onto a landing where a single sentry stood, relieved every six hours. She noted their kit. Palace livery, yes, but under the livery — and here was something interesting — every guard wore a thin grey sash at the waist, and every sash bore a small stitched mark she could not resolve at this distance. She drew it anyway, from memory, seventeen attempts, until the shape felt right: a circle interrupted by a vertical line that continued past the circumference, like a keyhole seen from the wrong side.

She wrote: *Mark unknown. Appears on all crown personnel. Appears nowhere in the histories provided.*

On the fifth day, the king rode out to bless the river festival, or what remained of it — a handful of bundled figures on the ice, a canopy, trumpets whose sound reached her tower thinned by distance into something almost tender. Emrys watched him through the glass. She had seen him once before, briefly, at the court presentation she preferred not to think about: King Aldous, beloved Aldous, warm as a hearthfire, moving through his court like sunlight moving through water. The people of Carreth spoke his name the way sailors speak of lighthouses.

Through the glass, at distance, he looked tired. He paused too long between gestures. When he raised his hand to bless the crowd, there was a lag — a half-breath — between the crowd bowing and his apparent awareness of them bowing, as though he were an echo arriving late to its own sound.

And on his head, even outdoors, even in the freezing wind: the sigil-crown. It did not glitter. That was the wrongness of it, she realized, watching. Crowns are meant to catch light. This one *took* it. The winter sun sat on the ice behind her window and the whole white world blazed, and the crown drank every ray that touched it and gave none back, so that it looked less like metal than like a hole shaped like a crown.

She wrote: *Day 5. The king pauses 3 seconds between sentences. The court pretends not to notice. The court is very practiced.*

Then she sat for a while without writing, because the next thing she wanted to write was true, and true things about kings have weight.

*He is being eaten slowly, and everyone who serves him has agreed to call it governance.*

* * *

It was on the ninth day, cleaning — because no one came to clean, another detail she'd noted, another thread of isolation dressed up as honor — that she found the first scratch.

Behind the headboard. She'd moved the bed to chase a draft, wedged it sideways with a grunt and the particular efficiency of a woman who had hauled ledger-chests through a smugglers' warehouse for six years, and when she knelt to check the wall for cracks her fingers found grooves in the birch paneling. Deliberate. Old. The varnish — no, there was no varnish, that was the point of pale birch, you could see everything done to it —

Letters. Cut with something small and hard, maybe a spoon-handle, maybe a bodkin. Shallow, because whoever cut them had been weak. She fetched the lamp and held it low and raking across the wood, the way you hold light to read watermarks, and the words rose out of the paneling like drowned things surfacing.

*I AM THE THIRD,* the scratches said. *THEY TOLD ME I WAS THE FIRST. THEY WILL TELL YOU THAT TOO.*

Emrys did not move for a long time. The lamp oil guttered. Her knees ached against the floor and she let them ache, because the pain was simple and the words were not.

She checked the rest of the wall. Behind the headboard, more, in a different hand — rounder letters, hurried: *the crown calls the storm to find us. the storm is a net.* And lower, half-obscured where later paneling repair had been nailed over: *do not sign anything.*

That night she did not sleep. She took the desk chair apart — the guild taught you to disassemble furniture quietly and quickly; Corvane had called it *inventory management* — and used the chair-leg's foot as a lever, and pried up the boards along the base of the walls one by one, working clockwise from the corner, cataloguing as she went, because if there was one message hidden then there were others and if there were others then someone before her had been very busy with very little time, and busy frightened people hide things in the order that makes sense to them, and order was the one language Emrys Zhou had never failed to read.

Under the third board, wadded into the cavity like a bird's nest of shame: hair. Black going white at the roots, tied with a cord. A keepsake or a warning or just the last thing someone owned.

Under the seventh board: a bone stylus, worn to a nub.

Under the eleventh: nothing, and under the twelfth, scratched inside the cavity where no passing eye would ever fall, in letters so fine they must have taken days:

*There were nine of us. I counted, because counting was all I had left. Nine storm-callers brought to this tower in thirty-one years. I was the ninth. If you are reading this, you are the tenth, and I am sorry, and here is what I learned before they took me down to the hall:*

*They do not lie to us. That is the worst part. Ask and they will tell you true things in gentle voices. I asked why me and they said: because you were written. I asked what was written and they showed me a book. I asked what happens to the ones before and they said: the crown keeps them.*

*The crown keeps them.*

*Nine storms called across thirty-one years, and none of them the storm-caller's fault, though every one of them ruined her name in whatever town she came from. The storm is not weather. The storm is the crown reaching. You were not summoned, cousin. You were harvested.*

*Count your days. Count everything. It is the only part of you they cannot drink.*

Below that, a final line, pressed so deep the wood had split slightly:

*The tenth scratch is yours. Leave it for the eleventh.*

Emrys sat back on her heels in the lamplight, in her beautiful cell with its good light and its eleven well-chosen books, and understood — completely, finally, in the place where understanding stops being information and becomes a fact about your body — what she was.

Not a guest. Not even a prisoner, exactly. Prisoners are kept for punishment or ransom or strategy.

She was inventory.

* * *

She worked through the dark hours with the bone stylus and a candle stub, and by the time the false grey dawn came up over the frozen river she had filled six pages of the paper they'd given her — oh, the irony of it, they'd given her paper, mountains of it, a clerk's ransom — with the true map.

Not the map of the palace. She had that already, assembled from nine days of watching: towers, gates, watch rotations, the strange gravitational pull of the throne hall where the snow leaned inward like grass in wind. That map was useful and she kept it folded inside the false back of the inkwell, because useful maps get confiscated.

The other map was of the tower room itself. Every board. Every cavity. Every scratch, transcribed and dated by handwriting where she could guess the sequence — the third caller's cramped script appearing again near the window, older now, the letters wobbling; the ninth caller's fine hand everywhere, patient, methodical, a bookkeeper to the last, and Emrys had caught herself halfway through transcription thinking *I would have liked her* before remembering the woman was decades dead, drunk by a crown, *kept* —

Kept. She turned the word over all morning. The crown keeps them. What did a crown do with nine storm-callers? Where in this palace of wings and cellars did one keep nine women? She laid the palace map over the question the way she'd lay a ledger over a suspicious account, looking for the column that didn't sum. Cellars: too visible. Deep cells: possible, but the ninth caller had written *they took me down to the hall*, and *the hall* appeared twice more in other hands, always lowercase, always without elaboration, as though the writers assumed any future reader would know which hall. As though it were obvious.

The throne hall, then. Down to the hall. Under it, maybe. Beneath the dais, beneath the king's feet, in the dark under the seat of power: nine storms' worth of harvested callers, keeping the long winter fed.

Or — and this thought arrived around noon, quiet as frost forming — not nine. The ninth had said *nine of us*. But the ninth had also written *you are the tenth*. The ninth had counted only those who came before her. If the crown had gone on calling after the ninth was taken downstairs — if there had been a tenth, an eleventh, callers in years whose names no one in Vell had ever heard because the storms that ruined them happened far away, in towns too small to make history's margins —

Then the number wasn't nine. Then the number was whatever the crown needed it to be, and the tower room's walls were a guestbook written in desperation, and somewhere under the throne hall the accounting ran much longer than anyone dared say aloud.

Emrys picked up the bone stylus. She turned to the birch paneling behind the headboard, beside the third caller's defiance and the ninth's careful testament, and she cut, in neat clerk's letters, shallow and clear:

*I am the tenth. My name is Emrys Zhou, formerly of the Guild of Impossible Things, Vell. I was not summoned. I was delivered. The date in their book is my doing-date, not my doom-date — I intend to make that distinction matter.*

*To the eleventh, if there is one: they will tell you true things in gentle voices. Believe the voices, distrust the truths. Count everything. And look under the twelfth board from the door, northeast corner. I've left you the only thing I own that matters.*

She wrapped the bone stylus in cloth and sealed it into the cavity with wax from the desk candles, and she sat back, and she was surprised to find her hands steady. Fear was still there — fear had moved in permanently, taken a room in her chest, unfurling its flag — but alongside it sat something harder and colder and, she recognized with a start, familiar. It was the feeling of a ledger that finally balanced. Of a column of lies that summed, at last, to a number she could name.

Corvane had sold her passage to these people. The prophecy had dragged her here like cargo. The crown had been casting for her for thirty-one years with nets made of weather. Fine. Let all of it be true. A clerk who knows the real figures is dangerous to everyone who profited from the false ones.

*They can keep me*, she wrote in her private ledger, the one she kept in her head, in no hand anyone could confiscate. *But I keep the accounts.*

A key turned — not her door; the landing door below. Footsteps on the tower stair, unhurried, one set, boots with a slight unevenness to the rhythm, a hitch on the left stride like an old injury badly healed. The sentry changed at fixed hours and walked evenly; this was neither the hour nor the gait.

Emrys slid the true map back behind the false inkwell wall, put the histories on top of her papers, and stood facing the window with her hands clasped behind her, the picture of a guest admiring the view.

The door opened without a knock.

"I'm told," said a voice she had heard once before — in a warehouse corridor full of wrong seasons, attached to a woman who had caught her fleeing and, for reasons Emrys had spent nine days failing to explain, let her go — "that I've been assigned as your keeper."

Sable stood in the doorway in palace grey, the oathbreaker's sash at her waist, her sword peace-knotted, her eyes going past Emrys's shoulder to the tower room — the bed moved slightly out of line, the chair reassembled a half-degree wrong, the baseboards sitting a finger proud of the plaster in twelve places — and Emrys watched the exact moment those eyes understood that this guest had been conducting an audit.

"Congratulations," Emrys said. "You're keeper of the tenth."

Something crossed Sable's face — quick, quickly mastered — that was not surprise.

"The tenth," Sable repeated quietly, closing the door behind her. "So you found the scratches."
