## 8. Sable's Oathbreaker Winter

The tower room they gave Emrys had good light, which was how Sable knew it had been used before.

Palaces did not waste good light on prisoners. Light went to nurseries and chapel windows and the dowager's neglected garden on the south wall. A cell with a broad window facing east, fitted with a cushioned bench and a brazier that was actually fed, meant someone had decided long ago that the person kept here should be comfortable, cooperative, and easy to watch. Someone had made that decision more than once. Sable counted four sets of scratch-marks near the window's base when she took up her first watch — tally marks in old varnish, some of them decades faded — and said nothing about them to anyone.

Her orders were simple. The guest of prophecy was not to be mistreated. She was not to be spoken with at length. Her meals were to be taken from the palace kitchen, tested as always, and her door was to remain locked from the outside between the evening bell and dawn. Sable had been a knight for nineteen years and an oathbreaker for six, and she recognized the shape of these instructions the way a sailor recognizes weather. They were not the orders of people who wanted the prisoner harmed. They were the orders of people who wanted her *ripe*.

So she stood her watches by the door and said little, and Emrys Zhou said less, and for eleven days the two of them conducted their entire acquaintance through the grammar of silence.

It was Sable who broke it first. Later she would try to tell herself it was strategy — that a guard who learns her charge's habits is worth three who learn only her face — but the truth was smaller and more human than that. It was snowing upward outside the tower window, fat flakes rising from the courtyard like sparks off an inverted fire, and Sable could not bear to be alone with the sight of it.

"That's not right," she said, to the window, to no one.

Emrys looked up from the inventory of her own room — she had been cataloguing everything since the day she arrived, the blanket, the bench, the chamber pot, as if the cell were a warehouse shipment and she its clerk. "No," she agreed. "It isn't."

"The rivers froze mid-current last week. Whole barges standing in the ice like toys left out." Sable crossed to the window because standing still had become unbearable. Below, the flakes climbed. "I've wintered in Carreth my whole life. Winter is a beast but it's an honest one. This—" she gestured at the rising snow, at the pale sky beyond it that seemed to have forgotten which direction down was, "—this is something eating."

"You've seen it before?" Emrys asked, and there it was again, that ledger-keeper's precision, every question filed exactly where it belonged. "The eating."

Sable considered lying. It would have been easy. Six years of exile had made lying nearly a native language to her; she'd broken one oath with the truth and spent the years since learning how many smaller falsehoods it took to survive without oaths at all. But the woman in the tower had been dragged across half a country by a book, and had stood in the throne room watching the king smile wrong, and had come back to this room and begun writing an inventory of her captivity as though accuracy itself might save her. Sable had known people who coped that way. She had been married to that coping, once, before the crown ate the marriage too.

"I've seen it once," she said. "Sixteen years ago. The winter the old queen died."

* * *

The bench by the window was wide enough for two if neither of them minded shoulders touching, and after a while they stopped minding. Sable sat because her legs ached from the stairs, and told herself the story was owed — that Emrys had a right to know what manner of thing guarded her, the way a patient has a right to read the label on the medicine.

"My name was Sable Vane," she began. "Before. Knight-Captain of the Queen's Oath, which was a real order then, not just a phrase on a gatehouse. We weren't bodyguards, whatever the songs say now. Our oath was specific. We swore to protect the queen's *self* — her memory, her judgment, her mind — against any power that sought to wear her. It was an old oath. Older than the sigil-crown, I think, though nobody knew that then. There are clauses in it that never made sense until later. Clauses about crowns."

Emrys had gone very still beside her. Clerks, Sable was learning, went still the way other people drew weapons.

"There were nine of us when I swore. I was the youngest, seventeen, so proud I could barely fit through doors." The rising snow caught the last grey light and threw it back pinkish, like embers. "Queen Maren. You'll have heard of her. The Beloved Mother, they called her after she was dead. She was — " Sable stopped. Started again. "She was funny. That's what none of the histories keep. They make her solemn and sainted and she'd have hated it. She hid sugared plums in the pockets of state robes. She learned all our mothers' names. When the winters got bad she'd go out on the ice herself, wrapped in plain wool, and hand out bread until her guards nearly wept."

"And the crown?"

"Came to her gently. That's the part I need you to understand, because everyone thinks the sigil is a thing that seizes. It doesn't seize. It *arrives*. It came as a gift from the Regent's council — a new ceremonial piece for the coronation anniversary, old gold, older than the dynasty, found in the deep vaults and cleaned up. And she wore it for an hour at a feast, and gave it back, and laughed, and said it was heavier than it looked." Sable's hands had closed around each other in her lap without her noticing. "Then it started coming back. On its own, the servants said. Set out on her dressing table when no one had put it there. In her council chamber. Once, floating in her bath, upright, dry."

Emrys made a small sound, quickly suppressed.

"We thought it was a courtier's prank gone strange. Then we thought it was sorcery aimed at her, an attack, and the Oath was built for attacks. Nine knights watching a queen against assassins and poisoners — we were good at that. But you can't stand between a woman and a hat." Sable heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when feeling pressed too hard against the inside of it. "It didn't take her all at once. It took her in *pauses*. First the pauses between her words got longer. Then her eyes started arriving late at things — you'd hand her a letter and she'd look at your chin for a heartbeat before her gaze climbed to your face. Then she began smiling at nothing, warmly, the way you smile at a child. And the pauses filled up. I don't know how else to say it. Something moved into the space where her thinking had been, and it furnished the rooms, and it smiled with her mouth."

"How long?" Emrys whispered.

"Four years. Four years of watching the finest woman I ever knew get edited, sentence by sentence, and no weapon in the armory that could touch it. We tried everything. Cold iron, holy water, burying the crown under consecrated ground — it turned up on her pillow two days later, warm. Serah, my second, cut her own palm open on it swearing it to destruction, and the blood just — sat on the gold. Beaded up. Refused to soak in, like water on wax." Sable unclenched her hands, examined them as if they belonged to someone else. "In the fourth year she had a day of clarity. One afternoon. The sigil loosened — I think it rests, or digests, or whatever feeding things do between meals — and she was entirely herself, and she grabbed my wrist with both hands and said, *Vane. Whatever they tell you afterward, I am in here. Do not let them finish.*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a bell rang the hour, and even the bell sounded muffled, as though the whole city were being slowly packed in wool.

"So I broke my oath," Sable said. "That's the short version. The long version is that I gathered the eight others and we planned to take her out of the palace — not abduct her, gods, we told ourselves it wasn't abduction — carry her north to the mountain shrines where the old oath was written, and find out what clause covered a crown that eats. And the night before we moved, the sigil finished early. Whether it felt us coming or whether it simply grew impatient, I'll never know. By morning the queen who woke was wearing the smile full-time, and she ordered me arrested for treason, and she ordered it in her voice, and she wept while the guards took me, and I have spent six years not knowing whether those tears were hers or its."

"What happened to the other eight?"

"They were given a choice. Recant and re-swear to the crowned king, or follow me into exile. Two recanted. I don't blame them. Hunger makes theologians of us all." Sable shrugged, and the shrug hurt more than the words had. "One died on the road south. Five scattered — I hear from one of them at midwinter, a card, no signature, just a drawing of a wren."

She hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. Emrys turned to look at her then, fully, and Sable saw the recognition land — the flinch of a woman hearing her own name in a stranger's grief.

"A wren," Emrys repeated.

"It's an exile's bird. Small, brown, sings through frost. We used to joke that the order would reform someday and its crest would be a wren, because a wren is the only thing honest enough to survive a Carreth winter." Sable looked away, out the impossible window, where the snow continued to fall toward heaven. "You asked why I'm telling you this. Because when I watched you in the throne room — watching him — I saw a woman counting pauses. You counted his pauses the way I used to count hers. And I thought: either the book sent me a fellow mourner, or it sent me a witness. Either way, someone in this tower ought to know what she's guarding against."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm after that, the two of them, though Sable would have denied it was friendship. Emrys inventoried; Sable watched. But somewhere in the twelfth day the silences changed texture, from the silence of strangers to the silence of people conserving words they trusted each other to understand. Emrys began leaving the window bench clear on Sable's side. Sable began bringing up two cups instead of one on the pretext that the kitchen over-counted.

On the thirteenth night, with the curfew blizzard rattling the shutters hard enough to shake dust from the lintel, Emrys asked the question Sable had been circling for days.

"Why did they choose you? To guard me. An oathbreaker. They must have hundreds of knights who never broke anything."

"They do." Sable turned her cup slowly on her knee. "And every one of them looks at you the way you look at a delivery of goods. I don't. The Regent wants a guard who understands what's happening to the king — who won't panic at the pauses. Who won't start asking dangerous questions, because I already know the answers." She paused. "And perhaps because I'm compromised. A woman with nowhere else to go guards her post carefully. Exile is a leash you hold yourself."

"Do you regret it?" Emrys asked. "Breaking it. Knowing what it cost."

Sable was quiet long enough that the blizzard became the loudest thing in the room, and she let it be, because the answer deserved the room's whole attention.

"No," she said at last. "That's the obscenity of it. I lost everything — name, order, pension, country — and I'd do it again before the words were out of their mouths. Because I saw her, that last clear afternoon, and she asked. When someone in there asks you, Vane, do not let them finish — you don't weigh the cost. You just refuse." She looked at Emrys directly. "Which is why I need to ask you something, clerk, and I need the true entry, not the tidy one."

Emrys set down her cup. Waiting. Filing.

"In Vell — the storm. The one they exiled you for. Everyone in this palace has a version of it; the guild's file says you called a gale that drowned a harbor district, that you were sentenced and fled before sentencing. Corvane's agents sold the Regent's men a story that you're a storm-caller of unusual strength, untrained, dangerous, prophesied." Sable leaned forward. "But I've met storm-callers. They call weather the way I draw a sword — deliberately, or badly, but never by accident. And I watched your face in the throne room when the king described the great storm of his grandfather's reign, and you went white as the snow that falls upward. So tell me true, Emrys Zhou. What really happened the night of your storm? Because I have seen the crown reach for someone across a distance before. I watched it try for four years. And I am beginning to suspect that the storm that ruined your life was never yours at all."

Outside, the blizzard struck the tower a blow like a fist, and in the sudden dark of the guttering candle, Emrys's face held something Sable recognized from a mirror sixteen years deep — the look of a woman about to confess to a crime she was finally, terrifyingly, beginning to doubt she had committed.
