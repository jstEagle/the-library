## 9. What the Storm Remembered

The tower room had good light, which was its cruelest feature.

Emrys had learned to distrust comfort in the Guild's warehouse — a chair that was too soft was usually a trap, a door that opened too easily usually opened somewhere wrong — and so she treated the palace's hospitality with the same professional suspicion. The bed was warm. The window was wide. Someone had left a carafe of water that tasted faintly of honey and something older, something that hummed against her teeth like a struck bell.

They wanted her comfortable. Comfortable guests stayed.

She had been in Carreth six days now, by her own count — scratched into the windowsill with the point of a hairpin she'd smuggled in her braid, because a woman who kept ledgers for impossible things did not trust anyone else's arithmetic. Six days of being called *guest* by servants who would not meet her eyes. Six days of the king smiling at her across banquet tables she wasn't permitted to eat at. Six days of watching the sigil-crown drink him, sip by patient sip, his pauses stretching longer each morning like taffy pulled thin.

And six days of Sable.

The oathbreaker came every evening when the light went amber, carrying two cups of tea she was not authorized to bring, and sat on the far side of the room with her back to the wall and her sword across her knees, and said nothing for a long time. It had taken Emrys four of those evenings to understand that the silence was not hostility. It was courtesy. It was an oathbreaker's way of saying *I will not ask you anything you have not chosen to tell me.*

Tonight, on the sixth evening, with the snow coming down past the window in long silver ropes and the city below gone quiet as held breath, Emrys broke the silence herself.

"I want to tell you about the storm."

Sable did not move. She had a stillness about her that Emrys had come to recognize as trained rather than natural — a knight's stillness, the kind you learned so that horses and kings and frightened people could all trust you not to startle. But her eyes came up from the tea in her hands, grey as the sea off Vell in November, and waited.

"You've read the record," Emrys said. "Everyone has. It followed me here, didn't it? I saw it in the Regent's file when they brought me through the court. *Emrys Zhou, storm-caller, exiled from Vell after the drowning of the Salt Road district, eleven dead.*" She said the number flatly, the way she entered figures in ledgers, because if she let any feeling into it the whole sentence would collapse and take her with it. "Eleven dead. That's what the record says."

"That's what I read," Sable said carefully.

"The record is wrong." Emrys got up and went to the window, because it was easier to say this looking at the snow than looking at another person. The glass was cold under her fingertips. Below, a lamplighter was making his rounds along the frozen canal, his little flame swallowed and reborn, swallowed and reborn. "The record is wrong in the way records are always wrong. It records what happened and calls it what I did."

She heard Sable set down her cup. The small ceramic click of it sounded enormous in the quiet room.

"Tell me," Sable said. "Or don't. Either way I'll be here."

That, Emrys thought, was the most dangerous thing anyone had ever said to her. Not an interrogation dressed as kindness. Not Corvane's warm merchant's voice offering her tea and ruin in the same breath. Just a person, sitting in the amber light, willing to wait.

She told it anyway. She had been carrying it for seven years, and the weight had grown teeth.

* * *

"It was the autumn I turned nineteen," she began. "I'd been in Vell three years by then. I came off a fishing boat out of the southern isles — my mother sold me to the crew when I was eleven because the storms followed me and she couldn't feed a girl who attracted weather. You'll want to write that down as an excuse. Don't. It's just where I started."

She felt Sable shift slightly, wanting to object, and choosing not to. Good. This was going to be hard enough without mercy interrupting it.

"Vell was the first place I understood what I could do instead of just what I couldn't stop doing. A storm-caller who doesn't call storms is just a girl with bad luck. But a storm-caller who can *quiet* them—" She laughed, short and bitter, breath fogging the glass. "There's a market for that. Fishing fleets pay well for calm water. And the Guild of Impossible Things pays best of all, because they deal in bottled weather, and bottled weather needs someone who can talk a storm into a bottle without it tearing the glass. That was my trade. I was good at it. I liked being good at something."

"Corvane found you," Sable said. Not a question.

"Corvane finds everyone eventually. He found me haggling over the price of a squall and offered me a ledger and a room with a lock on the inside, which is more freedom than I'd had since I was eleven." Her reflection watched her from the darkening glass, pale and narrow, a ghost wearing her face. "I trusted him. That's the part I keep having to forgive myself for. Not the storm. The trusting."

She turned away from the window then, because there were parts of this she needed to say facing a human being, even if her hands wouldn't stay still while she said them.

"The night it happened, I hadn't called anything. I want that on the record first, before everything else, because everything else only makes sense if you believe me about that. I was asleep. I'd closed a shipment of monsoon-bottles that afternoon — forty of them, nasty things, all teeth and humidity — and I'd eaten too much and slept like the dead. And I woke up because the *sky* woke up."

Even now, seven years later, saying it aloud put the taste of ozone in her mouth.

"There was no cloud. That's the first impossible thing. There was no cloud and the sky went green-black anyway, the color it gets before hail, and the pressure dropped so fast my ears bled. I ran down into the warehouse because that's what you do — you go to your work, your tools, the place you know. And I stood in the middle of the storm-shelf row with forty bottled tempests screaming in their glass around me, and I reached out with the sense I use to talk to weather, the way you'd reach out a hand in a dark room—"

Her voice failed. She pressed her knuckles to her lips for a moment, breathed, continued.

"And it reached back."

The word she wanted was *hunger*. She made herself use it.

"Not like a storm reaches back. Storms are stupid. Beautiful and stupid, like big dogs. They push, they pull, they don't *want*. This wanted. It came down the channel of my gift like something swimming up a pipe, and it looked at me — I know how that sounds, but it *looked* at me, and what it felt like was being read. Being *appraised*. Like I was goods on a shelf and something was deciding whether I was worth the freight."

Sable had gone very still. Stillness of a different quality now. Emrys recognized it, distantly, the way you recognize a chord — the stillness of a soldier hearing a sound she knows from a war.

"I pushed it off," Emrys said. "I spent everything I had pushing it off, and when it broke contact it — I don't know how to describe this either — it *slammed*. Like a door. All that force it had brought with it had to go somewhere, and I wasn't holding it anymore, so it went into the sky, into the harbor, into the Salt Road district where the poor houses lean against the seawall." Her hands had stopped pretending to be calm; she folded them together hard enough to hurt. "Eleven dead. Forty-one homeless. Half the district underwater before dawn. And I was the only storm-caller in Vell, and the only person alive who knew the storm had *come looking for someone*, and no one — no one — believed that. Why would they? 'I didn't call it, it called me' is what every storm-caller says before the tribunal. It's the oldest lie in the trade."

"So they exiled you for it."

"They exiled me for lying about it, technically. The magistrate was almost kind about it. He said he believed I hadn't meant to do it." Emrys's laugh came out broken. "He thought I was confessing to negligence. He gave me exile instead of the rope because I was young and wept nicely. And I took it, because exile was better than the truth, because the truth was *the storm talked to me and I couldn't prove it*, and a madwoman hangs faster than a murderer."

The room was very quiet. Outside, the strange snow kept falling in its silver ropes, upward-drifting at the edges, wrong, wrong, the winter itself sick now, feeding-time near.

"That's the confession," Emrys finished. "Seven years. Seven years I built my life around the shape of that night. Every rule I live by — count everything, trust nothing, need no one — every one of them is a wall I built around that one hour. Because if it was my fault, then at least the world makes sense. If it was my fault, then I deserved the exile and I earned my loneliness honestly, like wages." She finally looked at Sable straight-on. "Say something. Tell me it was my fault. Everyone does, in the end, even the ones who love me. *Especially* the ones who love me. Tamsin never said it, but she thinks it. I've seen her think it."

Sable rose.

It was not the movement of a guard changing position. It was slow, deliberate, the way a person moves when they are afraid their legs won't hold them, and Emrys watched the color drain out of the oathbreaker's face like tide off a flat.

"When," Sable said. Her voice had gone rough. "What date. What night."

"What?"

"The storm. The night in Vell. What date."

Emrys frowned. Dates were the one language she spoke fluently; she didn't even have to think. "The ninth of Duskfall. Seven years ago this autumn."

Sable sat back down. Slowly. Like an old woman. She stared at her own hands for a long moment, and when she looked up, her eyes were wet, and Emrys felt the floor tilt beneath a conversation she had not been steering.

"I need to tell you something," Sable said, "and I need you to let me finish it, because if you interrupt me I'm going to lose my nerve, and I have lost my nerve once already in my life and it cost me everything."

Emrys nodded. She could not have spoken if she'd wanted to.

"I was the old queen's shield-knight. Before Aldous. Before all of this. Queen Maerin — you'll find her name in the histories with a nice bland line about a long illness, and the histories are lying. She died of the crown. Or rather—" Sable's jaw worked. "The crown drank her husband first, King Edvard, slowly, over twenty years, and when he was mostly gone, it started reaching further. Looking. *Shopping*, the way Corvane shops. And Maerin figured out what it was doing, because she was smarter than all of us and twice as brave, and she started hunting for a way to unbind it. Quietly. Off the books. She found references — old ones, pre-war ones — to a rite, and the rite needed a specific kind of person. A storm-caller. Born of salt water and exile. A person the crown itself would reach for, because the binding recognizes its own counterweight, and it *hungers* for the one thing that can undo it."

The room seemed to lean in. Even the snow outside seemed to fall more slowly.

"She sent agents looking," Sable said. "Not to recruit you. To protect you. She'd worked out that the crown would try to summon such a person long before they were ready to be summoned — that it would cast its net years early, the way a fisherman chums water. She wanted to find the storm-caller first and hide her, ward her, teach her nothing, tell her nothing, just *keep her safe* until—" Sable stopped. Pressed both hands flat against her knees. "Until Maerin could finish decoding the rite. She never finished. The crown noticed what she was doing. It accelerated. It killed her inside a year, and it made it look like grief, and I—" 

Her voice cracked clean through.

"I broke my oath because I tried to tell the court the truth about how she died, and the Regent — the man who *isn't* a man — had me branded and exiled for treason in front of everyone I'd served for fifteen years. That's my story. You know it. Everyone knows the shape of it. But here's the part nobody knows, the part I've kept locked in my chest for twelve years because saying it felt like handing the enemy a knife—"

She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and wrecked all at once.

"On the ninth of Duskfall, twelve years ago — no, wait. No. *Seven* years ago. Seven years ago, on the ninth of Duskfall, I was in Vell."

The world went white and silent around Emrys, the way the air goes silent before lightning.

"I was following a lead," Sable said. "A rumor of a storm-caller in a smugglers' port, a girl who could bottle thunder. Maerin's last living agent. I was supposed to find her, assess whether she was the one, and report back. And the night before I could make contact — the night before, Emrys, I had a name and a street and I was going to knock on a door in the morning — the sky over the harbor turned green-black with no cloud in it, and the pressure dropped hard enough to taste blood, and I stood on the seawall and I *felt* it. Felt what it was doing. It wasn't weather. It was a hand, reaching down the world's arm, feeling along every thread of power in that city for one particular shape. And it found a shape. And the shape fought it. And when it lost the grip—" 

"Forty-one homeless," Emrys whispered. "Eleven dead."

"It was never a storm," Sable said. "It was a summons. The crown's first attempt to call you. Seven years early, across half the width of the map, because it felt you the way a wound feels a thumb — and you *refused it*, Emrys. Do you understand? Untrained, unwarned, alone in the dark, you refused a summons from the sigil-crown of Carreth, and the refusal is why eleven people died, and the refusal is also the single most extraordinary act of strength I have ever heard of, and the crown has been trying to finish the job ever since."

Emrys discovered she was shaking.

Not weeping — she had expected weeping, had braced for it the way you brace for cold water — but shaking, deep, structural, like a building whose foundation has been quietly removed. Because the shape of her whole life had just been picked up and turned over, and the underside, which she had never been allowed to see, was not guilt at all.

Seven years of exile. Seven years of ledgers and locked doors and needing no one. Seven years of a number — *eleven* — carved into the inside of her skull like a debt she'd assumed she owed.

And the debt had never been hers.

"I pushed it off," she said, and her voice sounded like someone else's, small and astonished. "I thought — all these years I thought I'd done it. That my reaching out had drawn it, that I'd—"

"You were asleep," Sable said. "You told me yourself. You were asleep and it came for you, and you survived it, and it cost eleven lives that belong on *its* ledger, not yours. The crown owes Vell a debt of eleven souls and forty-one homes, and it has never paid, and it does not intend to." Something hard came into her voice then, the old knight surfacing through the wrecked woman. "But debts are what I understand, Emrys. Debts are what you understand. And I am telling you, as an oathbreaker who knows precisely what it costs to carry a false debt for years — it is time to enter this one in the right column."

Emrys laughed. It came out wet and cracked and real, the first true laugh she'd given in seven years, ugly and relieved and furious all at once.

"A ledger," she managed. "You're telling me my whole ruined life was an accounting error."

"I'm telling you the books were cooked," Sable said, "by the crown, seven years ago, and again tonight, and every night in between. And I am very, very good at catching cooked books."

Emrys pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Behind them, the storm-memory played differently now — the green-black sky, the reaching hunger, the slam of refusal — and it looked, for the first time, not like her crime but like her *evidence*. The crown had come for her once and failed. Everything since — the Codex, the prophecy, tonight's date written a century ago in old ink, Corvane's soft-voiced betrayal, this gilded cell with its cruel kind light — all of it was simply the second attempt. Slower. More patient. Wearing the face of destiny instead of weather.

They hadn't summoned her with a storm this time.

They'd summoned her with a *story*. With her own name in a book, with the guild that sheltered her sold out from under her, with a prophecy shaped exactly like a key turning in the lock of her loneliness.

And it had worked. She was here.

"Then it knows," she said quietly, lowering her hands. "The crown. It must know what I am — what I did that night. That I can refuse it."

"It's had seven years to study that night too," Sable said grimly. "Which means whatever it wants from you now, it has designed a trap specifically shaped around the way you fight."

The words settled over the room like frost forming.

Because Emrys had already seen the shape of the trap, hadn't she — glimpsed it in the banquet hall, in the king's lagging smile, in the way the Codex had moved her across the map like a piece on a board. It didn't want to break her. Breaking was crude. It wanted her *willing*. It had spent seven years learning that she could not be forced, and so it had arranged a world in which she would choose.

And downstairs, somewhere in this palace of long pauses and borrowed smiles, there was an old queen's mother-in-law — the dowager, half-forgotten, growing flowers out of season in a walled garden — and Sable had mentioned her once, offhand, in the way people mention the one loose thread they haven't dared pull.

"If the crown summoned me," Emrys said slowly, standing, some new heat kindling behind her sternum where the storm used to live, "then someone bound it in the first place. And whoever bound it will know what it wants from me — and what I can take from it."

Sable looked up at her, and for the first time in six evenings, she smiled. It was a tired smile, a soldier's smile, but underneath it was the thing Emrys had been starving for since Vell burned behind her: belief.

"The dowager's garden," Sable said, rising, hand already moving to her sword out of pure habit. "Gate's on the east colonnade. She walks at moonrise."

"Then we shouldn't keep her waiting," said Emrys, and went to fetch her coat, and for the first time since the green-black sky, she walked toward a door instead of away from one.
