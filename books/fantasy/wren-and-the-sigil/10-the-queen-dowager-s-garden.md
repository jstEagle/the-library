## 10. The Queen Dowager's Garden

There were three hundred and eleven steps from the dowager queen's apartments to her garden, and Maren of Carreth counted them every morning, because counting was the only way she had found to know what she still possessed.

She counted other things too. The number of servants who still bowed to her — four, and one of those was deaf and did it out of kindness rather than memory. The number of times her grandson-king had written to her in the past year — none, though his letters used to come monthly, warm and full of small jokes about the chancellery. The number of names she had lost — that one she could not count, which was the whole cruelty of it. You cannot tally a subtraction when you no longer remember what stood on the ledger's left side.

The garden itself was the reason for the walk. It sat in the oldest courtyard of the palace, a square of stone and frost-killed roses that no gardener had tended in twenty years, because Maren had dismissed them all and would permit no one else inside. It looked, to anyone who glanced down from the upper galleries, like neglect. It was not neglect. It was the only place in Carreth where the sigil could not watch her.

She had made certain of that at great cost, in the years when she still had costs to spend.

This morning the frost lay on the rosebushes like a second bark, grey and glassy, and the fountain at the garden's center had frozen mid-pour — a sculpture of water stopped in the act of falling, which Maren found herself staring at longer than usual. Rivers freezing mid-current. She had seen it once before, sixty years ago, in the winter the bargain was struck. The world pausing to listen.

*It is feeding again,* she thought, with the flat certainty of an old wound recognized by weather. *And faster now.*

She was arranging her morning — the pruning shears she no longer used, the basket of dry bread crumbs for birds that no longer came — when the door at the far end of the colonnade opened without a knock, and a guard she did not recognize ushered in a young woman wrapped in a borrowed cloak, escorted by that oathbreaker woman with the ruined hands.

Maren set down the shears.

She knew the girl at once. Not by face — she had never seen her before in her life, and she was honest enough with herself these days to trust that honesty — but by the air around her. Storm-callers carried weather in their skin the way pines carried resin. Sixty years ago there had been others; she remembered the smell of ozone on the wind off the mountains, the taste of lightning on the back of the tongue. This girl smelled of a storm held carefully leashed, and beneath it something rawer — grief, and fury, and the particular exhaustion of someone who has been lied to by everyone she trusted and is only now beginning to count the lies.

"You are the one the Codex named," Maren said. Her voice came out rustier than she liked. She cleared her throat. "Emrys Zhou. Of Vell."

The girl's chin came up. Good. Fear was useless here, and obedience worse. "You know my name."

"I know what was written about you a hundred years before your birth." Maren gestured to the stone bench beside the frozen fountain, the only seat in the garden she ever used. "Sit, if you wish. Or stand, if sitting feels like surrender. I have done both, over the years, and I no longer recommend either."

The oathbreaker — Sable; Maren remembered the name from the girl's trial, one more piece of paper that crossed her desk in the years when papers still meant something to her — hesitated at the colonnade's edge. "Your Majesty, I'm assigned to—"

"To guard her. I know. Guard her from out here, Captain Sable. What is said in this garden stays in it, or I will make certain it does, and neither of us will enjoy how." Maren let a little of the old steel into her voice, the voice that had commanded a kingdom through plague and famine and one very bad harvest festival. It still worked, mostly. Mostly was enough.

Sable withdrew to the colonnade with the stiff grace of a soldier given an order she distrusted. The girl sat — chose the bench, then, interesting — and pulled the cloak tighter against a cold that seemed, lately, to be coming from everywhere at once.

"You wanted to see me," Emrys said. "The chamberlain told me I didn't have a choice."

"The chamberlain tells everyone they don't have a choice. It saves him the trouble of persuasion." Maren lowered herself onto the bench, joints announcing their objections as always. Up close, the girl's weariness had a specific shape. Maren had seen it before, in mirrors, in other faces, in the eyes of every person the crown had ever decided to use. "I asked for you because you are going to die, probably within the month, and I find I am no longer willing to let people die in this palace without being told why."

A pause. Snow ticked softly off the frozen fountain.

"That's blunt," the girl said.

"I paid for bluntness with everything I owned. I intend to get my money's worth." Maren folded her hands in her lap, where they did not shake, because she had trained them not to, because shaking hands forget things faster. "Before I tell you what I know, tell me what you know. All of it. Leave nothing out because you think an old woman will be shocked. I was crowned before your grandmother was born, child. Shock died long before my memory started following it."

So the girl talked. Maren listened the way she had learned to listen in her widowhood — completely, cataloguing, letting silence do half the work. Emrys Zhou laid it out in a clerk's cadence, precise and self-deprecating by turns: the guild in Vell, the ledgers, the impossible warehouse with its doors onto wrong seasons. The Codex found in a dead man's library, tonight's date written in century-old ink, her own true name beneath it. The Guildmaster who had known for years. The ride across the country in a single night, delivered like a parcel. The king — and here the girl's voice changed, went quieter and more frightened — the king smiling wrong, pauses lengthening, eyes arriving late to his own face. The tower cell with its scratched warnings. The storm that ruined her life, and the oathbreaker's terrible arithmetic: the crown had called that storm, hunting for her, years early, and missed.

"And now I'm here," Emrys finished, "and I don't know if I'm the unbinding or the sacrifice, and everyone keeps saying both like they're different things, and I—" She stopped. Started again, angrier. "I keep ledgers. I know when numbers don't balance. Nothing here balances."

"No," Maren agreed. "It never has."

She looked out at her dead garden, at the frost on the graves of roses she had planted with a husband whose face she could no longer entirely summon — she remembered that he laughed from the belly, that he smelled of cedar and horse, that he loved her; the specifics were gone, paid out year by year like a pension to something hungry — and she began, at last, at the end of her hoarded courage, to tell the truth.

* * *

"Sixty-two years ago," Maren said, "Carreth was losing a war."

"Not badly. That was the horror of it. Badly we could have survived — badly means surrender, terms, treaties, the ordinary humiliations that kingdoms endure the way families endure funerals. No. We were winning and losing simultaneously, which is the war that eats countries. Every victory cost a city. Every retreat cost a bloodline. The eastern lords had broken away, the mountain clans were selling passage to our enemies, and the man I loved was dying of a wound that festered in a season when we had no medicines left and no roads to fetch any." She heard her own voice go distant and did not fight it. Some stories are better told from far away. "Aldous's grandfather. The first Aldous. You should understand that the kings of this line are all named Aldous, partly for tradition and partly because the sigil prefers continuity. Names are anchors. It likes its anchors familiar."

The girl had gone very still. "The sigil."

"The crown. Yes. Though it was not a crown when we found it. It was a *sigil* — a mark, a design, pressed into gold the size of a coin. We bought it from a man who claimed to have taken it from a temple that no map confirmed existed. He sold it to us as a relic of victory." Maren smiled, and felt the smile fail on her face, and let it. "We were desperate, so we believed him. Desperation is the finest currency in the world, you know. Everything accepts it, and nothing gives change."

"What did it do?"

"It ended the war in nine days." Maren let that sit. Nine days. She remembered each one with a clarity that had outlasted her wedding day, outlasted her son's first steps, outlasted nearly everything — because those nine days were priced into her forever, the principal payment on sixty years of interest. "My husband wore it pressed against his brow, as its instructions demanded, and his fever broke, and his generals woke knowing the enemy's positions as though they had dreamed them, and the eastern lords' envoys arrived at our gates offering terms we had begged for a month earlier. Nine days, and the war was over, and I knelt by my husband's bed and wept with gratitude while something ancient settled into his mind like a tenant into a house whose owner had gone out and forgotten to lock the door.

"We thought it was a blessing. That is the sentence I most need you to hear, Emrys Zhou, because I think you have spent your exile believing that only fools and villains are deceived. We were neither. We were tired, and grieving, and offered exactly what we needed, and we took it, and the taking was the sin entire."

Emrys's hands had curled in her lap. "What did it want?"

"At first, almost nothing. A little warmth. A little attention. My husband spoke of it as a companion — a voice at the edge of hearing, patient, courteous, *grateful*, he said, grateful to be worn. It advised him. Its advice was good, which is the trap of all such bargains; poison works best in wine. Within two years he was consulting it on everything. Within five, I noticed he no longer dreamed. Within seven, he paused before answering questions — just a moment, just a breath, as though listening to a second opinion — and the pauses grew." Maren's throat tightened around the old grief, familiar as a stair she climbed nightly. "He died at forty-one, still king, still beloved, still smiling. And at the funeral, when they lifted the circlet from his brow, the sigil did not lift with it. It had grown. It had rooted. And it moved — to our son, who was twelve."

The sound Emrys made was small and involuntary. Maren nodded. Yes. Exactly that sound, she remembered making it herself, once, alone, into a pillow, where no one could count it against her.

"A crown cannot simply seize a child," she continued. "Not openly. So it arranged matters. There was a regency, a crisis, a war-scare — I confess I no longer recall which crises were real and which were staged, my memory of those years was among the first payments collected — and by my son's nineteenth birthday the sigil rested on his brow in its new form, worked into a proper crown, and no one alive could say precisely when the coin had become the coronet. It grows, you see. It adapts. Each king it wears, it wears more completely, and each wearing takes longer to establish and goes deeper once established. My husband fought it for fifteen years without knowing its name. My son surrendered to it in ten, knowing exactly what it was, because I told him — God forgive me, I told him everything when he turned eighteen, and he chose to accept it anyway, because by then the alternative was civil war, and he was kind, and kind men will pay anything to spare others pain.

"And Aldous — your present Aldous — never got the choice at all. It passed to him at his coronation, grown so practiced at the transfer that he smiled through the whole ceremony, radiant, perfect, while it poured into him like water into sand. The court wept at his grace. I sat in the front row and watched my grandson's eyes arrive late to his own face, and I applauded, because what else does a dowager do?"

The fountain ticked. Somewhere beyond the colonnade, boots — Sable, pacing, faithful and furious at being kept outside a conversation she clearly sensed mattered.

"You said it can't watch you here," Emrys said. Her voice had roughened. "How?"

"Because I bound this courtyard against it, at a price I will not describe, using a craft I no longer fully possess, taught to me by a woman whose name I have already paid away." Maren met the girl's eyes steadily. "You asked how an old woman in a forgotten wing knows anything. This is how. For thirty years after my husband's death I studied what I had helped unleash. I read the temple records the seller hadn't known he'd stolen. I interviewed — discreetly, oh, so discreetly — every hedge-witch and exiled scholar the palace would permit near its kitchens. And I learned three things that you must carry out of this garden even if you carry nothing else.

"First: the sigil is not evil. Do not mistake me — I have no love for it, and I will dance on its grave. But it is older than our moral categories. It is a *binding*, made to hold something else, and crowns are merely the handle it has grown so that hands can pick it up. Whatever it truly is, whatever purpose it was bent to before some temple forged it into gold — it has been *worn* so long that wearing may be all it remembers how to do. You cannot reason with a flood. But you can learn its course.

"Second: it feeds. Not on blood — blood is crude, and it has never needed to be crude. It feeds on sovereignty. On the *selfhood* of kings. Each Aldous it consumes gives it more reach, more patience, more subtlety. Your king is the fourth it has worn to maturity, and it is drinking him faster than it drank the last, and when it finishes him it will be strong enough, for the first time, to reach beyond the royal line. To touch the country directly. Dreams, weather, oaths — the deep infrastructure of a nation. That is what this winter is. It is not a season anymore, child. It is an appetite.

"Third — and here is where your book and my research finally agree — it can be unbound. Not destroyed; I do not believe it can be destroyed, any more than you can destroy north. Unbound. Returned to what it was before kings. And the rite requires a storm-caller, because storms are the one force in the world that belongs to no sovereign — weather answers no throne, swears no oath, kneels to nothing. Lightning is the only power in Carreth that the sigil has never tasted." Maren reached across the bench and, with the deliberation of a woman spending her last coins, took the girl's cold hand. "Which is why it hunted you. Which is why it called your storm. It has known for a century what would end it, and it has been trying to kill you since before you were born, and failing, and each failure has made it more careful — and more afraid. Fear makes it reckless. Remember that. Reckless things make mistakes, and mistakes are doors."

Emrys was crying. She did it silently, the way clerks cry, without disturbing the ledger of her posture, and Maren did not comment, because some mercies are silences.

"The prophecy says I'm the unbinding," the girl said at last, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily. "Everyone keeps telling me I'm also the sacrifice. Both. Like they're the same word."

"They are not the same word." Maren released her hand and sat back, and felt, strangely, lighter — lighter than she had in decades, as though truth were ballast she could finally begin to jettison. "They are two readings of the same line, and the difference between them is choice, which is the one thing prophecies cannot write and tyrants cannot tax. I have read the Codex, or what fragments of it reached me before the crown locked the rest away. It offers a storm-caller as *price*. It does not specify the price of what. The crown reads it as: a storm-caller consumed completes the binding's century, and the sigil ascends. I read it as: a storm-caller who *gives freely* what the crown would steal can tear the thing loose — because the sigil's whole architecture is theft, and theft has no defense against a gift. You cannot rob a person of what they have already handed over."

"But giving it means—"

"Losing it. Yes. Whatever your storm is to you — and I suspect it is everything; it was certainly everything to the last caller who stood in this garden, forty years ago, whose warning scratches you found in that tower — giving it up means becoming, permanently, an ordinary woman." Maren smiled, and this time the smile held. "I have been an ordinary woman for thirty years, Emrys Zhou. It is the freest I have ever been."

The girl stared at her. "You gave it memories. You said — payments. What did it take from you? How much is gone?"

"All the parts of me it feared." Maren said it plainly, because plainness was the last luxury of the old. "My knowledge of the binding rites. My fluency in the old tongues. The names of everyone who helped me. My husband's face." She gestured at the frozen fountain, the frost-barked roses, the courtyard where no sigil-crowned servant could follow. "This garden is what I bought with what was left. A room it cannot enter. And now — perhaps — a listener. Which is worth more than I expected it to be."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The snow ticked. The frozen water hung above the basin, mid-fall, mid-century, waiting.

Then Emrys leaned forward, and her voice dropped, and Maren saw the clerk surface through the grief — the auditor, the woman who balanced books — and ask the question Maren had been dreading and honoring her for asking:

"You said it feeds on sovereignty. That when it finishes Aldous, it reaches beyond the throne. Then answer me this, Your Majesty, because I've been keeping accounts since I walked into this palace and every column comes up wrong." Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "*Who is running the country right now?* The king smiles and waves and signs what's put in front of him. Someone issues the decrees. Someone commands the Regent's guard. Someone licensed a smugglers' guild to supply the crown. If the sigil is busy drinking a king, who holds the cup?"

Maren closed her eyes.

Sixty years of careful forgetting, of paying away the dangerous knowledge coin by coin, and yet some things remained because they were not knowledge but dread, and dread, she had learned, was the one possession the sigil never collected. It liked her afraid. Fear kept her docile. Fear kept her in her garden.

"There is a man," she said slowly, "who came to court thirty years ago, the year Aldous was crowned. No history anyone could find. No family. Charming, indispensable, brilliant with money and favors and fear. He was Chancellor within five years and Regent within ten, and every decree that has issued from this palace in my grandson's reign bears his seal, and I have watched him across state dinners for three decades and I have never — not once — seen him eat."

She opened her eyes and found the girl already pale.

"His name is Corvane," Maren said. "Guildmaster Corvane, of Vell. And if he sold you to this palace, child, then you were never delivered to the prophecy at all. You were delivered to *him* — and I have spent thirty years wondering what a man who never eats could possibly want with the only storm in the world that could unmake his master."

Beyond the colonnade, Sable's pacing had stopped.

And down at the far gate of the old courtyard, where no one had entered uninvited in twenty years, snow swirling behind him like a debt coming due, stood a slender figure in a merchant's grey, smiling, and bowing, and holding a ledger under one arm as though it were a gift.
