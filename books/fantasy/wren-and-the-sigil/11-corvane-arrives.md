## 11. Corvane Arrives

The road to Carreth had been salted with miracles, and Corvane hated every one of them.

He sat in the guild's traveling coach — black lacquer, silver fittings, wheels that whispered rather than rattled because he paid a wheelwright in Vell to keep a minor silence-charm on the axles — and watched the frozen country slide past the window like a page turning. The rivers were locked mid-current, which was wrong; he had crossed this route eleven times in his career, and the water had always at least pretended to move. Snow fell upward in slow spirals when the wind gusted, rising off the drifts and climbing back into a sky the color of old pewter, as though the winter were trying to take itself back.

Corvane did not care for weather that behaved as though it had opinions. Weather was inventory. It could be bottled, priced, and sold, and anything that refused to be either bottle or price was simply bad stock.

"Master," said Quill, from the bench opposite.

Quill was his senior factor, a narrow woman with ink-stained cuffs and a face like a folded receipt. She had been with him nineteen years and had never once asked him a question he wanted answered.

"The manifest," she said, and handed it over.

He read it without hurry. He was a man who did everything without hurry; hurry was for clerks and corpses. Nine crates of bottled thunderstorms, provenance Vell, quality mixed. Two jars of unmade vows — wedding oaths, mostly, purchased from people who regretted their marriages more than they feared the consequences. One cask of secondhand courage, going flat. And, sealed in lead and packed in wool at the bottom of the third crate, the thing that made the journey worth its misery: a letter of introduction bearing the Regent's private seal, naming Guildmaster Corvane of Vell *licensed supplier of impossible things to the Crown of Carreth*.

Licensed. He turned the word over the way a jeweler turns a stone. For thirty years the guild had survived by being unlicensed, unlisted, unwritten — smugglers of fate, dealers in the contraband of destiny, invisible to every crown that might want to tax them or hang them. And now here was a seal making him official. Making the whole rotten, beautiful enterprise official.

It should have felt like triumph. It felt instead like a bill arriving early.

"Your assessment?" Quill asked.

"That the capital is dying," Corvane said, "and someone in it has finally decided to pay retail."

* * *

Carreth announced itself an hour before they reached it: first the walls, blue-white with frost, then the spires, then the great slow breath of woodsmoke hanging over the city like a held sigh. The streets inside the gate were full and quiet at once — crowded, and yet hushed, the way a room goes quiet when the sick are sleeping. People moved through the snow with their heads down and their hands tucked into their sleeves, and everywhere, on every door and shutter and lamppost, Corvane saw the sigil.

It was stamped into the ice on the fountains. It hung as an iron sign above the chandler's shop. Children had scratched it into a snowbank with sticks, badly, and an adult had come along after and corrected the lines, precisely, reverently. A crown drawn as a single unbroken line, a loop that fed back into itself, no beginning and no end. Corvane had seen it before, of course — every dealer in his trade had seen it, the way every sailor has seen the horizon — but he had never seen it worn so openly, repeated so hungrily, across an entire city's skin.

"They love him," Quill observed, watching a woman press her bare palm to the sigil-marked door of the granary before entering. "The king."

"They love what he lets them feel," Corvane said. "Which is not the same thing, and is worth considerably more per ounce."

The palace received them the way a well-run bank receives a depositor: warmly, and with an inventory already taken. Guards in white-and-silver escorted the coach through courts where the snow lay undisturbed except by one set of footprints — a single line of prints crossing the vast white yard in a perfectly straight diagonal, as if someone had walked from one corner to another refusing all sensible paths. Corvane noted it, filed it, forgot it, in the way of a man whose mind is a warehouse with excellent shelving.

They were brought to a receiving room that smelled of cedar and cold iron. Fires burned in both hearths, and the room was warm, and the warmth had the specific texture of warmth that is being paid for.

The Regent received him alone.

He had heard descriptions, naturally. Lord Regent Maren Vael, keeper of the king's declining health, administrator of the realm, a man of precise manners and imprecise mercy. What the descriptions had failed to convey was the stillness. Vael stood by the window with his hands clasped behind his back, and he did not shift his weight, did not turn his head, did not blink at any rhythm Corvane could catch, and after a while Corvane understood that the man was breathing the way furniture breathes — technically, out of habit, without conviction.

"Guildmaster." Vael's voice was pleasant. Pleasant the way a well-made lock is pleasant. "You came quickly."

"You paid for speed," Corvane said. "I am not in the business of disappointing paying customers."

"No," Vael agreed. "You are in the business of supplying them." He turned at last, and his eyes went to the crates being carried in behind Corvane, and there was something in his face — hunger, but housebroken, trained to sit and wait — that told Corvane more about the state of Carreth than the frozen rivers ever could. "My lord the king has need of... comforts. Small ones. The winter has been long."

"The winter," Corvane said carefully, "has been behaving strangely."

"The winter," said the Regent, "is doing exactly what it is meant to do." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a glove fits a hand — completely, and yet you could see it was a covering. "You have brought my shipment. You will also bring me something else. I am told you have a clerk in Vell who reads old books."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the corridor, a door closed with the softness of a secret.

"Emrys Zhou," Corvane said.

"You know your inventory well."

"I know my people well," Corvane corrected. "Inventory is what I sell. People are what I keep." It was a good line. He had used it before, in negotiations, and it had the double virtue of sounding loyal and meaning nothing.

Vael studied him for a long moment. Then he walked — glided, almost; there was something unsatisfying about the mechanics of his walking, like watching a puppet operated by someone very patient — to a writing desk, and drew out a paper, and laid it before Corvane.

It was a contract. Corvane read contracts the way other men read scripture: completely, and looking for the parts everyone else skips. This one granted the Guild of Impossible Things exclusive license to supply the Crown of Carreth. In perpetuity. Immunity from seizure, from taxation, from prosecution under every statute Corvane had spent three decades learning to dodge. In exchange: quarterly shipments, priority fulfillment of royal requisitions, and — clause nine, written small, the way important things are always written small —

"— the delivery," Corvane read aloud, "of the individual Emrys Zhou, storm-caller, into Crown custody, condition unharmed, disposition compliant."

"She is already in Crown custody," Corvane said. "I'm told she arrived without anyone's help. Rather dramatically, I understand. The book did the carrying."

"And now she sits in a tower room eating the palace's food and hating everyone in it, including herself." Vael's tone did not change. "Compliance is a matter of persuasion, Guildmaster. The Crown believes you are persuasive. The Crown believes she trusts you."

*She does*, Corvane thought, and the thought landed in him like a coin dropped down a dry well — he heard it fall a long way before it stopped. That was the trouble with taking in strays. They developed attachments. Attachments were liabilities. He had known that for thirty years and had let a half-starved girl with a dead storm in her chest shelve his ledgers anyway, because she was precise, because she was honest in a profession built on lies and therefore useful precisely for knowing where the lies were kept, because —

Because he had liked hearing her say *good evening, Master Corvane* at the end of the day. There. That was the truth of it, and truths were stock too, and he knew what this one cost.

"She trusts the guild," Corvane said aloud. "Not me. There's a difference, and it's worth money to whoever understands it."

"Then teach me the difference," said the Regent, "and name your price for the lesson."

* * *

They gave him quarters in the guest wing, which Corvane recognized immediately as a cage with better upholstery than most cages. The windows were real glass and looked out on the frozen gardens; the locks were on the outside. He unpacked anyway, methodically, hanging his coat and setting his writing case on the desk, because a merchant's first act in any new territory is to establish that he intends to stay until the terms improve.

Quill appeared in the doorway with the evening's intelligence, delivered in her flat factor's voice.

"The tower prisoner takes her supper alone. Her guard is a knight named Sable — oathbreaker, exiled, reinstated under the Regent's authority six months ago. The two of them talk. At length. Through the door, on the stairs, in the garden when the weather permits, which lately it doesn't."

"A knight and a clerk." Corvane turned his quill in his fingers. "That's either nothing or everything. Which?"

"Unknown. But the dowager queen visited the prisoner yesterday. Unannounced. The guards were ordered not to record the conversation."

Corvane stopped turning the quill.

Old Queen Isolde. Half-forgotten, half-mad, or performing madness so long the difference had eroded — he'd sold her sleeping draughts once, years ago, through an intermediary, and remembered thinking her eyes were the only fully awake thing in the entire palace. If the dowager was visiting his clerk, then the game in Carreth had more players than the Regent's contract admitted, and players the contract didn't mention were players who hadn't signed anything.

And that, Corvane thought, was where the leverage lived. Not in the Regent's paper. In everyone the Regent couldn't make sign.

"Anything else?"

"One thing." Quill hesitated, which she did perhaps twice a decade. "The clerks in Vell. Tamsin has opened the strongroom twice since we left. Both times she logged it as routine audit. Both times the audit entries were in your handwriting."

Corvane considered this. Forgery was a hanging offense in the guild — not for moral reasons, but because the ledger was the only god the guild worshipped, and falsifying it was blasphemy. Tamsin was forging his hand to hide whatever she was really doing in the strongroom.

He found, to his mild surprise, that he was not angry. He was interested. A clerk who could forge a master's hand was either a traitor or a successor, and the two categories overlapped more often than merchants admitted.

"Leave it," he said. "Note it in my private book. Date, time, and the observation that loyalty is a commodity whose price rises sharply during wars."

* * *

He requested his meeting with the prisoner for the following morning, and the Regent granted it with a graciousness that Corvane distrusted on principle. They walked him up the tower stair himself — Vael, gliding, and two guards who breathed normally enough — and at the landing the Regent paused with his hand on the iron door.

"She will ask you to take her side," Vael said. "That is what she will actually want, beneath whatever she says. Everyone wants someone on their side; it is the last luxury people surrender." His eyes, in the gray light from the arrow-slit, had a depth to them that seemed incorrect for their size, like windows into a much larger room. "Consider whether selling her that luxury is not kinder than selling her freedom. Freedom, in Carreth, this winter, is merely a slower shape of death."

"I don't sell kindness," Corvane said. "No margin in it."

"No," Vael agreed, opening the door. "But you've been buying it for years. I can tell. It shows in the face."

The door shut behind Corvane before he could decide whether he'd been insulted.

* * *

The tower room was bright — that was his first impression, and an absurd one, in a city where the sun had become a rumor. Someone had arranged the cell to catch what light existed: the bed placed under the window, the desk angled to it, even the water pitcher set where the morning would strike it and fill the room with a coin of reflected brightness. Good light, in a prison. Either the jailers were sentimental or the prisoner had rearranged her own captivity, and Corvane knew which possibility his money sat on.

She stood when he entered. Not the way courtiers stand — no performance in it, no bow folded into the spine. She just stood, the way people stand when a debt has entered the room, and looked at him across twelve feet of frozen flagstone, and waited.

"Emrys." He spread his hands, an old gesture, warm as a hearth he kept swept for exactly such occasions. "You look terrible. The food here is criminal; I'll send down to my own stores."

"You sold passage to me," she said.

No *good morning*. No *how was the road*. He had prepared three openings, each calibrated — sympathy first, then grievance, then offer — and she had skipped all of them and gone directly to the ledger's darkest column.

"For eight years," she said, when he didn't answer fast enough. "Royal agents asking after a storm-caller born under a burning sky. Passage to the chosen one. You took their deposits, didn't you. You kept entries."

"Of course I kept entries," Corvane said. "I keep entries for everything. That's what makes me trustworthy — everyone knows my books can't lie, even when I do." He crossed to the window, giving her his profile rather than his face; some conversations went better when the other party wasn't watched too closely. "Yes, Emrys. I sold them hope of you. Deposits against a delivery that I never intended to complete, because delivering you was worth less than keeping you. Do the arithmetic. Eight years of retainers from agents who believed I might someday produce you. You have earned this guild more sitting in Vell writing in ledgers than you could have earned us in a lifetime of being heroic."

"So I was income."

"You were *an asset*," he said, "which is the highest compliment I pay. Assets are protected. Assets are maintained. Do you imagine the storm that broke your life would ever have found you in Vell if I hadn't buried your name in six false identities? I bought you twenty quiet years. The invoice has only now come due, and I confess the collector outranks me."

"The crown."

"The crown," Corvane agreed, "or whatever wears it." And there — he saw it land, saw her eyes flick toward the window, toward the palace's central tower where the beloved king's banner hung rigid in the frozen air. She had seen it. Of course she had seen it; she'd been living beside it for days, and Emrys Zhou had spent eight years cataloguing impossible things, which meant she was the single best-trained observer of wrongness in this entire kingdom.

"Sit down, child," he said, gentler now, because the moment had ripened. "I came a thousand miles through a winter that has forgotten which direction snow falls. Hear my offer before you decide I'm your enemy. I promise you'll still believe it afterward — but you'll believe it *warmly*."

She didn't sit. She never did what was comfortable. That was the flaw in her, and the value, and he had never resolved which outweighed which.

"The Regent offers me a license," Corvane said. "Exclusive supply to the Crown. Immunity. Legitimacy — the guild stepping out of the shadows after thirty years, into the open, untouchable, *chartered*. All I must do is deliver you: compliant, cooperative, willing. And here is what I offer you in return." He drew the folded paper from his coat — his own draft, written last night by candlelight, every clause weighed — and laid it on the desk between them. "Your freedom. Real freedom. I have purchased passage on a ship leaving Carreth within the week; the captain owes me and cannot afford not to. South, past the ice, to anywhere you like. New name, new papers, new ledger — a blank one, Emrys. No guild debts, no prophecy, no crown, no past. You walk out of this tower a free woman and no one in this kingdom ever writes your name again."

Silence. The light moved a fraction on the wall.

"And the price," she said quietly, "is that I stand before the court and confirm the prophecy. That I legitimize the trade. That I tell the world the Codex is true and the crown's purchase of chosen ones is holy commerce, and that I am its willing merchandise." Her voice didn't shake. It got *flatter*, which he recognized, with a merchant's eye for quality, as the more expensive material. "So that the next one after me ships smoothly. So the guild's new charter opens markets."

"The next one after you," Corvane said, "will exist whether or not you bless the trade. Prophecies are like storms, child — they arrive regardless. The only question ever on the table is who profits from the shipping." He stepped closer, and let his voice drop to the register he used for closing. "I am offering you the only exit that exists. Take it, and live. Refuse, and the Regent finds other persuasions — he has deep cells, and patience, and you've seen what his patience does to winters. I cannot protect you from him. I can only sell him you gently instead of roughly, and split the difference in your favor. That is rescue, Emrys. Dressed plainly, priced fairly. It's the best thing on the market."

She looked at the paper on the desk for a long time. He watched her read it — she read the way she did everything, completely, hunting the clauses others skip — and he saw her find clause four, the one where she confirmed the Codex before the assembled court, and he saw her understand exactly what he was asking, and what it would buy, and whom it would cost.

When she raised her head, her eyes were bright and terribly clear.

"You wrote in your first letter to me," she said, "when I was sixteen and freezing and alone in your warehouse — you wrote, *the ledger remembers what the world forgives.* Do you remember?"

"I remember everything I write. It's how I stay rich."

"Then you'll remember this too." She picked up his contract — his careful, fair-priced rescue — and tore it in half, and tore it again, and set the pieces on the desk with the neatness of a clerk filing documents, which was somehow worse than throwing them. "File that," she said, "under *declined*."

Corvane looked at the torn pieces for a moment. Then he laughed — genuinely, briefly, because there was real quality in the gesture and he was a connoisseur — and straightened his coat.

"There it is," he said. "Honesty. The most expensive thing in my catalogue, and you give it away for free." He moved to the door, and paused with his hand on the iron, and gave her the truth as a parting gift, because truths were stock, and this one cost him nothing now and would cost her everything soon. "You think you've declined my offer, child. But the Regent doesn't need your signature — he needs your *compliance*, and there are more ways to write a person compliant than by contract. Ask yourself, tonight, why a book of prophecies carried you a thousand miles in a single night and then set you down in a cell with such very good light. Books that fetch people, Emrys, are books that intend to use them. And ask yourself what Sable will do when the crown offers her the same arithmetic I offered you — her freedom for your cooperation. Everyone sells eventually. The only variable is the price."

The guard opened the door. Cold air and torchlight spilled in from the stair.

"Oh — and one more entry for your ledger," he added, almost as an afterthought, and it was not an afterthought at all. "The ship I mentioned. It leaves within the week. But seats on it are limited, and I have already sold one — and I'll give you a hint as to who bought it. She signs her letters with a broken oath."

He went down the stairs without hurrying, because hurry was for clerks and corpses, and behind him he left the tower room very quiet, and the torn paper lying on the desk in four neat pieces, and a question settling over the cell like the winter itself:

*What would Sable sell?*

Down in the frozen gardens, unseen beneath the upward-falling snow, the Regent's footprints crossed the white yard in a single perfect line — from the deep cells' door, straight to the tower, refusing all sensible paths.
