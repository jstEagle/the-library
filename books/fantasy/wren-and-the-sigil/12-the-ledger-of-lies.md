## 12. The Ledger of Lies

The guest wing of the Winter Palace smelled of beeswax and old ambition.

Emrys had learned its rhythms in four days of pretending to be exactly what everyone believed her to be: a clerk on holiday from a provincial counting-house, elevated by prophecy, flattered by proximity to power. She rose when the kitchen bells rang, took tea in the solarium where the frost ferns grew up the inside of the glass like a garden that had given up on spring, and smiled at the right intervals when courtiers drifted past her chair and murmured about the storm-caller, the chosen one, the girl the Codex named.

She was very good at pretending. It was, she had come to understand, the one skill the Guild of Impossible Things had actually taught her — not the bottling of memories or the brokering of unmade vows, but the quieter craft of standing in a room full of liars and keeping your own face smooth as an audited page.

And Corvane had lied to her. That was the wound she kept turning over, the way her tongue returned to a cracked tooth. Not *the crown lies*, not *prophecy lies* — she had made her peace with those, or thought she had. But Corvane. Corvane who had taken her in off the salt road with nothing but a burned reputation and a talent for numbers. Corvane who had said, *you're safe here, Wren, you're mine to protect,* in the same tone he used for everything else, which should have warned her, because every word out of Guildmaster Corvane was inventory. Everything was stock. Including, apparently, her.

*Passage to the chosen one.* He had sold royal agents passage to her the way he sold bottled thunderstorms: by weight, by rarity, with a markup.

So on the fifth night, while the palace settled into its creaking sleep and the wind off the icefields worried the shutters like a creditor at a door, Emrys stopped pretending and went looking for his books.

Corvane kept rooms in the east range, near the Regent's offices — of course he did, the honored supplier, the licensed merchant of impossible things, welcomed into the machinery of the crown with all the warmth of a man coming home to a second shop. The guards knew him. The guards did not know her, not well enough, but they knew she was *expected*, and expectation, Emrys had found, opened more doors than keys did.

She went instead through the servants' stair behind the tapestry of the First Crossing, a route she had mapped from her tower window weeks ago with a clerk's patience, noting which corridors filled with lamplight at which hours and which stayed dark. The stair smelled of tallow and boiled cabbage. Her slippers — she had traded her boots for court shoes and regretted it with every step — whispered on stone worn hollow by generations of people carrying things they weren't supposed to be seen carrying.

Corvane's outer door was locked. His inner door was not, because Corvane trusted locks the way he trusted people: as a formality, useful mainly for establishing blame afterward. What he actually relied upon was the conviction that no one would dare.

Emrys dared. Daring was easier than she'd expected. It turned out that betrayal burned clean, like good lamp-oil, and left very little smoke.

His study was exactly what she'd known it would be and it still hurt to see it: the ledgers racked by year, spines stamped in guild green; the brass scales for weighing memory-vials; the little tin of lemon drops he kept for apprentices, because sugar made arithmetic kinder. A dozen small kindnesses she had mistaken for a whole person. She stood in the doorway a moment and let herself feel it — really feel it, the full weight of the man she had thought he was — because grief deferred was grief doubled, and she had work tonight that required both hands.

Then she lit no lamp, worked by the blue spill of moonlight off the snowfield outside, and began to audit.

The public ledger sat where it always sat, top shelf, fattest spine: *Guild of Impossible Things, Vell House, Year of the Long Winter.* She read it first out of habit and discipline. Shipments of bottled weather to the crown's household guard. Three crates of unmade wedding vows sold to a duchess who wished to be a widow without the mess. Memory-tinctures, courage-decantations, a vial labeled *one hour of being loved, vintage unspecified* — sold, she noted with a cold twist in her stomach, to the King's Own chamberlain, at a price that suggested the buyer had not been permitted to negotiate.

All of it ugly. None of it new. The guild had always traded in contraband fate; she had balanced those books with steady hands for six years, telling herself the ledger only recorded, never caused.

But there were pages missing. She saw it the way she saw everything wrong with an account: the foliation skipped. Page forty-one ran to page fifty-two, and the binding thread had been cut and restitched, and whoever had done it was better at stitching than Corvane was, which meant he'd paid someone, which meant the missing pages mattered.

Emrys knelt. She pulled each ledger from the rack and weighed it in her hands, and the ninth one was wrong — too heavy for its thickness, the way a book gets when something else has been sewn inside it, beneath the endpapers, where cowards hide things.

She slit the endpaper with her thumbnail. Inside, sewn flat against the board, wrapped in oilcloth against damp: a slim book, unmarked, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling. No title. No guild stamp. Just a number burned into the cover's corner, small as a brand on livestock: **I**.

She unwrapped it on Corvane's own desk, using his own letter knife, and told herself the shaking in her hands was the cold.

It was a ledger. Of course it was a ledger. He was Corvane; even his sins were double-entry.

But it was not a ledger of goods.

It was a ledger of people.

*Subject One,* the first entry began, in Corvane's handwriting but younger, less certain, the loops still carrying a student's hesitation. *Female, nineteen, hedge-witch lineage, minor weather-sense. Acquired: village of Harrowmere, debt settlement. Delivered to Crown agent (name withheld per contract) at the Salt Gate, winter of the false thaw. Consideration received: exclusive license, provincial memory trade, ten years.*

Below it, in the same hand, added later in different ink, the way a merchant closes an account:

*Outcome: failed. Deceased. See codicil.*

Emrys turned the page.

*Subject Two. Male, twenty-three, self-described storm-caller, credentials unverifiable. Acquired: debtor's auction, Vell. Delivered [date]. Consideration: reduction of tariff penalties.*

*Outcome: failed. Deceased.*

Page after page. Subject Three, a girl of sixteen bought from a family that had been paid in grain. Subject Four, a weather-priest who had *volunteered*, the entry noted, in the tone of a man recording a technicality. Subject Seven, twins, entered together in a single line, delivered together, closed together: *outcome: failed. Deceased. Deceased.*

Twelve entries. Twelve names — some real, some purchased, some so obviously invented by frightened people trying to seem valuable that Emrys's chest ached reading them. Twelve considerations received, itemized with a bookkeeper's loving precision: licenses, waivers, tariffs forgiven, a monopoly here, a blind eye there. And twelve outcomes, each ending the same way, each written in ink that never wavered, never smudged, never once betrayed the smallest tremor of a man who understood what he was writing down.

*Failed. Deceased.*

They hadn't been failures. They had been children and frauds and desperate priests and one pair of twins who had probably never called so much as a gust of wind in their lives, and the crown had taken them anyway, fed them to whatever hunger wore the king's smile, and when they died — and they all died — the guild had invoiced for the trouble and gone looking for the next.

Emrys set the ledger down very carefully, because if she did not handle it carefully she was going to put her fist through the desk, and noise would end this, and this could not end yet. There was a thirteenth entry. Of course there was. She had known since the first page; she had simply made herself turn twelve times first, the penance of an auditor, reading every name aloud in her head so that none of them would be able to say, afterward, that no one had counted them.

*Subject Thirteen. Female, twenty-four, storm-caller, verified. Emrys Zhou. Exiled, Vell Province, charge: unauthorized storm (see attached deposition). Acquired:* —

and here the ink changed. Here the handwriting slowed. Here, for the first time in twelve years of entries, Corvane had crossed something out.

*Acquired: ~~debt~~ ~~rescue~~ taken in.*

He had tried, then. Some part of him, some fossil conscience buried under all that green binding thread, had started to write what it truly was — *taken in* — and had not been able to leave the words standing. She traced the crossed-out line with one finger, feeling the dried ridge of the ink, the ghost of a hesitation he must have believed no one would ever audit.

*Consideration pending. Outcome: pending.*

Pending. As though she were a shipment still on the water. As though her whole life since the salt road — the warm corner of the warehouse, the brass lamp Tamsin had polished for her, the way Corvane had said *you're mine to protect* — were merely the packaging a commodity arrived in.

She had thought the worst thing in the world was the prophecy, the words that had reached across a century and picked her up and carried her here like a piece on a board. She had lain awake in the tower hating the Codex for stealing her agency, hating the sigil-crown for drinking a king, hating the storm for ruining her life.

None of them had ever pretended to love her.

That was the difference, and it went through her now like a splinter working toward the heart. The prophecy was honest in its cruelty; it wanted her dead and said so in old ink. The crown was honest in its hunger; it drank and did not send thank-you notes. But Corvane had built her a life. He had handed her the ledgers himself, taught her the codes, praised her neatness, defended her to the senior clerks — *best auditor I've had since my sister* — and all the while there had been a thirteenth line waiting in a hidden book with her name on it and the word *pending* where her future should have been.

She had spent six years balancing his books. She had never once suspected he kept a second set, because she had made the oldest mistake in accounting: she had confused the integrity of the numbers with the integrity of the man.

Her rage, when it finally came, did not arrive hot. She had expected heat — expected to sweep the vials off the shelves, to scream, to do something the storm-girl of Vell would have done. Instead it came cold and level and terribly clear, like the air above the icefields, like the first breath of a morning that will freeze the river solid. It organized itself. It itemized. Somewhere in the back of her mind, the clerk she had trained herself to be opened a fresh page and began, methodically, to take notes.

Because that was what he had left her. That was the only inheritance worth taking: the craft. You did not burn the fraudulent ledger. You copied it. You witnessed it. You entered it into evidence and you made the man read his own handwriting aloud in front of everyone whose children he had sold.

Emrys took out the little pencil stub she carried everywhere — a clerk's superstition, a talisman against a world that kept rewriting itself — and a fold of paper, and she began to transcribe. Names. Dates. Considerations. Outcomes. She wrote small and exact, in the hand Tamsin had once admired across a warehouse table and asked her to teach. When she came to the twins she wrote both their names separately, even though the ledger had filed them on one line, because they had been two people, and the least the record owed them was two lines.

Outside, the wind rose. Snow ticked against the dark glass. In the throne room at the heart of the palace, she knew, the king was sleeping his long smiling sleep while something ancient drank him by the sip, and somewhere below, the Codex lay open to a date that was coming for her. All of it — the crown, the prophecy, the century-long feeding — had needed supply. Had needed merchants. Had needed a respectable guild in a port city with a good reputation and a master who could look a grieving village in the eye over a signed bill of sale.

The guild had not been caught in the prophecy's machinery. The guild had *built* it. Delivery by delivery, invoice by invoice, the Guild of Impossible Things had spent twelve years making the impossible possible, one child at a time, and called the profit margin survival.

Her loyalty did not die loudly. It died the way accounts die: line by line, until the balance came to zero and there was nothing left to carry forward. She felt it go — the warm corner, the polished lamp, the belonging she had guarded more carefully than her own gift — and she let it go, and what remained in the place where it had been was not emptiness but space. Room to stand. Room to decide.

She finished the transcription. She rewrapped the true ledger in its oilcloth, resewed the endpaper with thread pulled from the hem of her slipper, and replaced every book on the rack exactly as she had found it, spine-true, because if Corvane was going to fall, he would fall to an account he could not dispute, not to a ransacked room he could explain away.

Then, because she was still, despite everything, a guildswoman to her bones, she dipped his pen and wrote, on a scrap of his own paper, in his own style, a single line, and weighted it under his brass scales where he would find it at morning:

*Audited. Balance due.*

She was three steps from the servants' door when she heard voices in the corridor beyond — low, unhurried, familiar. Corvane's voice, and with it another, dry as old parchment, that she had last heard offering her freedom in exchange for her signature.

The Regent.

They stopped just outside the study door, and Emrys pressed herself into the shadow of the tapestry-lined alcove, transcription folded tight against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and listened.

"—subject thirteen is proving resistant," Corvane was saying, and there was no apology in it, no shame, only the mild irritation of a factor reporting a difficult cargo. "But resistance is negotiable. Everything is negotiable. Give me until the solstice."

A pause. Then the Regent's voice, and though it was a man's voice, something underneath it spoke too, many-throated, patient as winter: *"We have waited a century for her. We can wait until your solstice, merchant. But know this — the book grows impatient. And the book remembers every promise ever made to it."*

The footsteps moved away down the corridor. Emrys stood in the dark with her back against the cold stone, and understood, with the terrible clarity of a final column summed, that the solstice was not a deadline the crown had set.

It was one Corvane had offered.

And she had five nights to make sure that when it came, the only ledger that mattered was hers.
