## 13. First Frost of the Long Winter

Sable had served the crown of Carreth through eleven winters, and she had learned to read them the way sailors read water. There was the honest cold of early frost, which squeaked underfoot and made the breath a small white animal that followed you. There was the river-cold, when the Serle slowed and thickened and the ice-boats came out, their runners singing. And there was the deep cold of midwinter, which did not feel like anything at all — a cold so total it passed beyond sensation, the way grief does after enough years.

The cold that came on the ninth day of her guarding Emrys Zhou was none of these.

It began as a wrongness in the light. Sable stood her morning watch at the tower door — she had insisted on taking it herself, over the objections of the palace guard who still called her oathbreaker behind their hands — and noticed that the sun, well clear of the rooftops, cast shadows that leaned the wrong way. Not long shadows. Shadows that pointed *toward* the sun, thin and dark as compass needles, all across the frozen courtyard, every sundial's gnomon dragging its shade against the light.

She had seen the sigil-crown work its quiet thefts for two years now: the king's pauses lengthening, the warmth going out of his hands while his smile stayed warm, the courtiers learning not to notice. But this was different. This was not a crown feeding politely at a king's edge.

This was hunger with the lid off.

* * *

By noon the snow was falling upward.

Sable saw it from the tower window where Emrys stood beside her, both of them wrapped close to the glass. The flakes rose off the courtyards in slow spirals, unhurried, dignified almost, climbing past the eaves and the banners and up into a sky the color of old pewter. Where they went above the cloud line, no one could say. They simply left, the way heat leaves a dying body, and the ground beneath grew bare in patches that should have been knee-deep in drift.

"That's not weather," Emrys said. Her voice was flat, clerk-flat, the voice Sable had learned meant she was frightened enough to reach for numbers. "Snow is water. Water falls. Whatever is pulling it—" She stopped. Recounted something in her head. Started again. "The sigil isn't just drinking the king anymore. It's drawing on everything. The whole winter. It's treating the season itself as a ledger."

"Can it do that?"

"I don't know." Emrys turned from the window, and in the grey light her face looked older than her years, ledger-ruled and tired. "I don't know what it can't do. That's rather the problem."

Below them, in the courtyard, a guard dropped his pike and stumbled back from the armory steps, where a drift of snow was rising off the stones like smoke returning to a fire. He crossed himself with the old sign, the one the dowager's priests used, thumb to heart and heart to brow. Sable watched him do it and thought: they can feel it too. The ones who never think about the crown at all, the ones who only know Aldous as the good king, the smiling king — even they could feel the world tilting wrong around them.

That was the thing about Carreth, Sable thought. The city had been living inside the sigil's bargain for a hundred years without knowing it. Every mild harvest, every plague that burned out early, every war that ended in an improbable peace — paid for, all of it, paid for out of a purse no one counted. And now the purse was emptying faster than it filled, and the difference was coming due in snow and rivers and light.

* * *

In the afternoon, the Serle froze mid-current.

A fisherman brought word of it up from the docks, shouting it before he even reached the gatehouse, and by the time Sable reached the river wall half the city had come out to look. The Serle ran brown and heavy under the bridges, swollen with mountain melt, and then — between one bridge-stone and the next — it stopped. Not slowed. Stopped. A standing wave hung in the air over the middle of the channel, curled and trembling, holding the shape of its own motion like a held breath. Ice bloomed backward along the wave from a single point, feathering outward, until the whole impossible crest was a sculpture of frosted glass suspended over running water that had forgotten how to run.

The crowd made a sound Sable had never heard a crowd make. Not a gasp. Something lower, collective, involuntary — the sound of a hundred people understanding at once that the rules under their feet were negotiable.

"The current will start again," said a voice beside her, and it took her a moment to recognize Regent Vael, the crown's chief servant, wrapped in grey fur, his face as smooth and patient as ever. He spoke to the crowd without raising his voice, and somehow they all heard him. "His Majesty has the matter in hand. Go home. Light your fires. Trust your king."

And they did. That was the horror of it, Sable thought, watching the people turn away obediently, murmuring reassurances to each other. *Trust your king.* They would go home and light fires and tell their children the river had paused for the feast-day, some old story dredged up and repurposed, because the alternative — that the beloved king was a hollow being eaten from the inside by the thing on his head — was unthinkable, and human beings will think anything rather than the unthinkable.

Vael's gaze slid to her. For a moment the smoothness cracked, or seemed to, and Sable saw something looking out of the Regent's face that was very old and very tired and did not blink often enough. Then he inclined his head, courteous as a knife laid flat, and moved on down the wall.

She stayed until dark, watching the frozen wave. Underneath it, she could see, the water still moved — a faint brown churn below the ice, like a heartbeat trapped in amber. The river wasn't dead. It was *held*. Everything the sigil touched was held: the king held in his warmth, the winter held in its falling, the century held in its turning. Held, and drunk from, slowly, forever.

Unless someone let it go.

She didn't know yet how that thought arrived, or what it cost. She only knew that standing before the frozen river, she felt for the first time not fear but arithmetic — the cold, clear arithmetic of a woman counting what remained.

* * *

The tower room was cold that night despite the brazier, and Emrys had covered the floor with paper.

Sable climbed the stairs past midnight and found her kneeling amid dozens of sheets, copying passages from memory — the Codex's unbinding verses, or fragments of them; she'd been permitted three readings before the crown's scholars locked the book away, and she was spending them like a miser's last coins, writing everything twice, checking herself against herself. Her fingers were blue at the tips. She didn't seem to notice.

"You should sleep," Sable said.

"So should you." Emrys didn't look up. "One of us should be awake anyway. Might as well be both."

Sable lowered herself onto the window bench, armor creaking, sword across her knees because old habits are older than oaths. Outside, the upward snow had stopped, but the silence it left was worse — a held-breath silence, the whole city listening without knowing why.

"It froze the river today," Sable said. "Mid-current. Standing wave."

Emrys's pen stopped. "I know. I felt it." She flexed her hand, the one that sometimes ached, she'd admitted once, in weather that hadn't happened yet. "Storm-callers feel pressure changes. What I felt today wasn't pressure. It was more like—" She searched. "Like a page being torn out. Somewhere south. Something ended today that hasn't finished ending yet."

"The sigil is accelerating."

"It's accelerating." Emrys set down the pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she spoke again her voice had lost its clerk-flatness entirely. "Do you understand what it means? The Codex gives the crown a hundred-year term. One hundred winters, each one fed, and at the end of the term the binding completes and the sigil doesn't need a host anymore — it *is* the kingdom. Every mind in it, folded into that smile. We have—" she counted days on her fingers with terrible precision, "—we are inside the last month, Sable. Maybe the last week. And it's speeding up because it's nearly full."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, there came a sound like a bell struck underwater. Neither of them moved. In the streets, dogs began to howl, all of them at once, all of them in the same direction — toward the palace.

"What happens if we fail?" Sable asked. It was not a question she wanted answered. She asked it anyway, because someone had to hold the ledger open and look at the final column.

Emrys was quiet long enough that the brazier popped twice.

"Then in a hundred years," she said finally, "no one remembers there was ever a choice. Children are born already smiling. Grief gets filed away as inefficiency. Everyone is warm and everyone is loved and nobody is *anyone*, and the snow falls upward forever, and it looks like peace." She looked at Sable then, and her eyes were dry and enormous. "I keep books for a living. I've seen what it looks like when a debt comes due that no one agreed to pay. This is that. The whole world, Sable. The interest alone—"

"Stop." Sable heard her own voice come out rougher than intended. "You're describing the end of everything as a ledger entry."

"How else would you have me describe it?"

"Honestly." Sable stood, crossed the paper-strewn floor, and knelt so they were level, so Emrys couldn't retreat into her columns. "Honestly. Are you afraid?"

A long pause. The dogs howled and fell silent.

"Yes," Emrys said. Just that. No precision, no hedging. And Sable found, strangely, that the fear in the room became bearable the moment it was named — became something two people could stand inside together instead of something each carried alone.

"Good," Sable said. "Then we're not fools. Fools die calm. We'll be afraid and we'll work. Show me the verses again. All of them. If I'm going to stand between you and that crown, I want to know exactly what you're walking into."

* * *

They worked until the sky began — wrongly, hours early — to lighten.

But it was not dawn. Sable knew dawn; she had kept more watches than she could count against it. This light came from the north, low and sourceless, a bruised gold bleeding up over the horizon like something rising rather than setting. The snow on the windowsill began to glow with it. Frost crept across the glass in patterns too regular to be natural — spirals, concentric rings, a geometry that repeated and repeated, and at the center of every ring, tiny and unmistakable, the same shape: the sigil's mark, drawn by no hand, blooming in ice on every window in Carreth at once.

Emrys stood at the glass with her palm flat against the frost, and the frost melted around her fingers in a perfect circle, and outside the light strengthened, and somewhere below them the bell-that-was-not-a-bell sounded again, closer.

"It knows we're reading," Emrys whispered. "It's always known. It's been writing us, Sable — the Codex, the summons, the storm that ruined me. We're not finding our way to the last page." Her breath fogged and cleared, fogged and cleared. "We're walking down a corridor it built a hundred years ago. And it's lighting the lamps ahead of us because it wants us to keep walking."

Sable put her hand on her sword and found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid anymore. The existential dread had passed through her sometime in the night and left behind something harder: the particular clarity of a woman who has already lost her name, her order, her queen, and her honor, and discovered that underneath all of it there was still a self that chose. Whatever corridor this was, whatever waited at the lit end of it, she would walk it as herself.

"Then we'll walk it," she said, "and we'll read faster than it writes. Come away from the window. Tell me the price again — the exact words. If the unbinding demands what you think it demands, I want to hear you say it aloud, in full, before that light finishes rising."

Emrys turned from the frost-ringed glass. Behind her, in the north, the false dawn brightened another degree, and on every sill in the frozen capital, the ice drew the sigil's spiral tighter.

"The exact words," Emrys said, and reached for the pages.
