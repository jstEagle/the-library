## 14. The Unbinding Rite, Half-Read

The Codex chamber smelled of cold iron and old candle smoke, and it did not want to be read.

Emrys had learned this over three days of trying. The book resisted the way a horse resists a strange rider — not with violence but with small refusals, pages that turned damp under her fingers, ink that swam when she looked at it directly, a binding that seemed to tighten whenever she found a passage worth copying. It was not a book so much as an animal that had been made to wear the shape of one, and it knew she was hunting something in its hide.

So she stopped hunting. She sat instead at the reading desk by the window — her one concession, the cell's good light — and treated the Codex like any other ledger. She did not read it. She *audited* it.

Ledgers, after all, were just books that told the truth about what someone wanted hidden.

She began on the fourth morning, or what she guessed was morning; the winter had stopped keeping honest hours since the frost began falling upward in the courtyards, and the sun had become a rumor the sky repeated out of habit. She opened the Codex to no particular page and simply recorded what was there: date of inscription, hand of the scribe, marginalia, watermarks, the wear-pattern of the vellum where thumbs had rested. Inventory, not interpretation. A clerk's patience applied to a prophet's madness.

By the second hour she noticed the pattern in the wear.

Certain pages had been handled far more than others — and not recently. The softening of the corners, the ghost-grease of old fingers, belonged to hands a century dead. Someone had read this book obsessively once, long ago, and then never touched it again. She mapped the worn openings against the text and found they clustered around eleven folios near the book's end, past all the fulfilled prophecies, past the summonings written in triumphant red, into a section where the handwriting changed.

The later entries were not prophecy at all. They were *notes*. Corrections. A reader arguing with the book in a cramped, furious script that grew shakier down the years.

*It lies about the cost,* the old hand had written, pressed so hard the nib had torn the page. *It always lies about the cost.*

Emrys copied that line twice before she realized her own hand was shaking.

* * *

The unbinding, when she finally dug it out, was buried like a coin in bad bread.

The Codex gave it to her in fragments, scattered across three gatherings of leaves as though the book hoped she would lose interest between them. One fragment described the sigil-crown in terms that made her skin crawl — not as an object but as a *debt*, a thing owed to something older than the war it had ended, feeding century by century on the interest. Another fragment listed what the crown could not abide: true names spoken without title, oaths broken freely rather than for gain, and — here the ink darkened, almost bruised — *the storm that answers to no crown*.

And then, on a leaf so worn it felt like cloth, the rite itself.

She read it four times. She wrote it out once in her own neat clerk's hand, because writing things down was how she made them real, and because if the words were going to be terrible she wanted them at least to be legible.

*The sigil is bound by consent,* the Codex said, in language older than the capital, older than Vell, older perhaps than the idea of kings. *What was given freely cannot be taken by force. But what was given freely may be unmade freely — by one who stands outside every bargain the crown has struck. Not royal. Not bound. Not bought.*

*A storm-caller who owes the crown nothing, who wears no sigil, who holds no oath — such a one may lay hands upon the crown and call the storm out of it. The storm is the key. The storm is the only key. And the door does not open unless the key stays in the lock.*

Emrys set down her pen.

She read it again. Then again, slower, the way she read contracts for Corvane, hunting the clause tucked into the shadow of the generous-sounding sentence. There was always a clause. In her experience there was *only* clauses, and everything else was decoration.

There. Near the end, in a subordinate phrase so quiet it might have been an afterthought:

*...and the storm, being called out, does not return to the caller. The gift passes through and is spent. What remains afterward is what the caller was before the storm chose her — which is to say, nothing that the sky remembers.*

Nothing that the sky remembers.

She sat very still in the good light and let the words finish arriving.

To tear the sigil from Aldous's head, she would have to pour her storm-gift into the rite — all of it. Not spend it the way a caller spends power, in bolts and gusts and borrowed thunder that replenished with the next weather. Spent the way a purse is spent. Emptied. The gift itself, the thing that had lived under her skin since she was nine years old and the first thunderhead had leaned down over the harbor at Vell and spoken to her like a grandmother, gone. Permanently. Irrevocably. *Which is to say, nothing that the sky remembers.*

She thought: *I can do that.*

She thought it quickly, the way you grab a hot pan handle before your sense catches up, and then she stood and walked to the window and looked out at the frozen city and admitted to herself that she was lying.

Because here was the truth she had never said aloud, not even in the tower room at night with Sable's breathing steady across the chamber, not even to herself in the privacy of her own ledgers: the storm was the only part of her that had ever been worth anything.

Everything else had been taken away. Her city, her name, her place in the world — confiscated along with her reputation the night the storm came. The Guild had taken her in, yes, but as a tool; Corvane kept her the way he kept his rarest inventory, valuable precisely because she was irreplaceable. Even Sable — and Emrys's chest did something complicated at the thought of Sable — even Sable had first come to her as a jailer with kind eyes. Every bond she had was built on top of the storm. Strip the gift away and what remained? A woman who was good with numbers. Exile. Nobody.

*You'd be free of it,* whispered a voice that sounded suspiciously like Corvane, smooth as oiled leather. *Free of the curse. Free of being hunted. An ordinary life. Isn't that what you've wanted since the night they put you on the ship?*

And underneath it, quieter, another voice — hers, the real one, the one that audited everything including herself: *You are confusing the price with the purchase. Read the clause again.*

She went back to the desk. She read the clause again.

*What remains afterward is what the caller was before the storm chose her.*

Before the storm chose her. She had been eight years old, then. A dock-keeper's daughter with salt-tangled hair who could multiply faster than the harbormaster's abacus and who talked to gulls because gulls listened better than people. That girl had existed. That girl had been — she searched for the word and found it reluctantly — *enough*. Not remarkable. Enough.

But that girl had also been loved, and loved people back, and look what loving had gotten anyone. Look what it got the old queen, whose oathbreaker still paced the corridor below with a sword she no longer believed in. Look what it got King Aldous, beloved by an entire nation, smiling his wrong smile while something drank him down to the rind.

Emrys pressed her palms flat against the Codex's worn leaf, feeling the ghost-grease of the century-dead reader, the one who had scrawled *it always lies about the cost*.

"Who were you?" she asked the empty chamber. "Did you find someone to pay it?"

The book, naturally, said nothing. Books only answered clerks, and only in arithmetic.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the day, because grief and fear were both, in her experience, best processed as labor.

The rite required more than the storm. It required the crown's *consent* — not the king's, the sigil's — extracted at the moment of tearing, which meant the unbinding could not be done by ambush or force. The sigil had to *want* to let go, or be made to believe letting go served it. That was a problem for a different day, and possibly a different person; Emrys filed it under *unsolvable* and moved on.

It required witnesses. Three, the text said, standing outside the bargain: one who had broken faith and regretted nothing (*Sable*, she wrote, and then crossed it out, and then wrote it again), one who had given the crown more than it ever repaid (the dowager queen, most likely, though the old woman's connection to the original binding made Emrys uneasy — *outside the bargain* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence), and one who had been consumed and yet endured. She did not know who that third witness was. She suspected, with a cold settling in her stomach, that she would find out.

And it required the storm-caller to speak the rite aloud, in full, *knowing the price* — the Codex was emphatic on this point, repeating it three times in three formulations, as though previous readers had tried to weasel around it. Ignorance voided the rite. Hesitation voided the rite. A heart secretly hoping to keep the gift voided the rite. She would have to stand before the crown wanting, with her whole self, to give up the only thing that had ever been hers.

"That's not a rite," she said to the empty room. "That's an audit of the soul."

Her pen hovered over the ledger. At last she wrote, in the tidy column where she tallied costs against gains, the way she tallied everything:

*Gain: one king, restored. One kingdom, thawed. One prophecy, closed.*

*Cost: everything I am.*

She looked at the entry for a long time. Then, because she was who she was, she corrected it. Crossed out *everything I am* and wrote beneath, smaller: *everything the sky made me. The rest is uncounted.*

She wasn't sure she believed it. But it balanced better.

* * *

The light was failing — or thickening; the winter's hours had become unreliable as a drunkard's promises — when the knock came. Two knocks, spaced evenly, unhurried. Sable.

The knight entered the way she always did, as though the room might contain an ambush and she intended to be disappointed if it didn't. Frost had gotten into her hair at the temples, silvering her further, and she carried two cups of something steaming that the kitchens insisted was tea and Emrys suspected was hot water with ambitions.

"You've been at it since before the bell," Sable said, setting the cups down. "I could see the candle from the courtyard. The whole tower lit up like a signal fire." A pause, and then, in the tone of a woman asking a question she already dreaded the answer to: "Did you find it?"

Emrys considered lying. It lasted about a second and a half — she had never been any good at it, which was the whole reason exile suited her, and besides, lying to Sable had begun to feel like writing false figures in a ledger: possible, but it corrupted everything downstream.

"I found it."

Sable went very still. Knights, Emrys had noticed, did stillness the way other people did shouting.

"And?"

"And it works." Emrys turned the ledger toward her, the rite copied out in her careful hand, the costs tallied beside it. "It will take the crown off him. It should take the crown off him. The mechanics are sound — I've checked the logic three times, and the old annotations confirm it. Whoever wrote those notes in the margins tried it, or tried to. They understood it completely."

"But," Sable said.

Emrys wrapped her hands around the cup. The heat bit pleasantly at her cold fingers. "But the storm doesn't survive it. The rite spends the gift. All of it. Permanently." She heard her own voice go flat and clerical, retreating into inventory, and made herself stop. Made herself say it plainly, the way she'd say it to Tamsin, the way she wished someone had said it to her years ago. "If I do this, I'll never call weather again. Never feel a thunderhead coming. The thing they exiled me for — the thing that ruined my life and the only thing that's ever made my life mine — gone. I'd be ordinary, Sable. Ordinary forever."

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, the palace groaned under the weight of the unnatural frost, ice expanding in stone that had stood for centuries and was now, like everything else in Carreth, being slowly squeezed.

Sable picked up the ledger. She read the rite with the same unhurried attention she gave everything — sentries, wounds, weather, Emrys — and when she reached the clause about the storm she read it twice, and Emrys watched her jaw tighten, watched the exact moment the meaning landed, and braced for pity.

It didn't come. What came instead was worse, or better: anger.

"They knew," Sable said quietly. "The ones who wrote this. The ones who summoned the others before you. They knew the price and they summoned anyway, and they never told the callers, did they? The girls in the cells downstairs. The scratches on the walls." She set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you want to throw. "They were going to hand you to this rite like a coin, and never mention it cost the whole purse."

"It's in the text."

"It's in a dead language in a sealed room in a tower, behind a Regent who decides who reads what." Sable's eyes came up, grey and fierce. "Emrys. You don't have to decide tonight."

"The winter doesn't care what I decide tonight. The snow is falling *upward*, Sable. The river froze mid-current. Whatever the crown is doing, it's accelerating, and the Codex says—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The Codex says the century completes soon. When it completes, the feeding stops being gradual. I don't think there's a king left inside Aldous after that. I don't think there's a city either."

"So you'll do it." Not a question. Sable said it the way she reported terrain: factually, grimly, already planning the route across it.

"I don't know what I'll do." Emrys laughed, and it came out cracked. "That's the hell of it. I've read the rite forty times. I've costed it out. On paper it balances — one man's soul against my gift, and the man is *drowning*, Sable, he surfaced long enough to beg me, I heard him. Any decent auditor signs off on that trade. One life fully lived against one gift half-buried in exile anyway." She stared into the cup. "But I can't make my hand write *yes*. Because every time I imagine it — the moment after, the silence in my head where the storm used to be — I don't recognize the woman standing there. And I'm afraid that's not cowardice. I'm afraid it's accuracy."

For a long moment Sable said nothing. Then she did something Emrys did not expect: she pulled a chair around to Emrys's side of the desk and sat, close, shoulder nearly touching shoulder, and looked at the ledger between them like a shared map.

"When I broke my oath," Sable said, "everyone assumed it was because I was weak, or bribed, or in love — well. Two of those were true. But the real reason was that I ran the numbers. Same as you. The oath said protect the crown. The queen *was* the crown, and the crown was eating her, and I realized the oath had a flaw in its wording that nobody had ever audited." Her mouth curved, dry and humorless. "I've trusted arithmetic ever since. It's the only authority that never asked me to lie."

"So you're telling me to trust the math."

"I'm telling you that whatever you choose, choose it the way you do everything else — honestly, in full, with the true figure written down. Not the figure fear gives you and not the figure heroism gives you. The real one." She tapped the page, beside Emrys's crossed-out line and the correction beneath it. "And for what it's worth — the woman who wrote *the rest is uncounted*? I'd know her anywhere. Storm or no storm. I knew her in a cell with a locked door and no gift at all."

Emrys looked at her, and the loneliness that had been her native country for seven years shifted its borders, just slightly, just enough to admit one more settlement.

"The third witness," Emrys said, because if she didn't put words between them she was going to do something reckless with her face. "Someone who was consumed and endured. I've been through every name in the Codex. Every king. They're all still *in* the crown — Aldous said so, the ones before him, screaming politely. None of them endured. None of them got out."

"Then it's not a king."

"No," Emrys agreed. "It's not a king." She pulled the Codex toward her again, and as her fingers settled on the worn leaf, the book did something it had never done before.

It turned its own page.

The vellum moved under her hand like the flank of a waking animal, and fresh ink rose to the surface of the new leaf as she watched — not the old brown script of the prophets, not the furious cramped hand of the annotator, but black, wet, gleaming letters forming themselves letter by letter, in a hand she knew intimately, because it was her own.

The Codex had begun writing tomorrow. And the first line of tomorrow, inscribed in Emrys Zhou's own handwriting, read:

*She tells Sable everything, and Sable does not forgive her.*
