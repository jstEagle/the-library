## 15. A Night of Borrowed Honesty

The blizzard came down on Carreth like a verdict.

Emrys heard it before she saw it — a sound through the tower stone like ten thousand fists knocking politely at a door that would never open. By the time she reached the arrow slit in her cell wall, the city had vanished. The harbor, the frozen river, the spires of the palace chapel where she'd watched the king smile his wrong smile three days ago: all of it erased behind a curtain of white so thick it seemed less like weather than like erasure. The world was being unwritten, and the snow was simply what remained after.

*Curfew*, said the placard someone had nailed to her door an hour earlier, in the palace's infuriatingly courteous hand. *By order of the Regent. All guests will remain within their chambers until the storm abates.*

*Guests.* She was a guest now. Not a prisoner, not a sacrifice, not the woman whose name was written in a century-old book of prophecies in ink that still hadn't dried from its latest revision. A guest. The word sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

She had spent the day trying not to think about the line the Codex had written in her own handwriting. *She tells Sable everything, and Sable does not forgive her.* She had told herself she would not obey it. That whatever the book wanted, she could refuse. That she was not a piece to be moved, even by a prophecy, even by her own future hand.

Then came the knock.

Not the polite fist of the blizzard against the walls — a real knock, knuckles on wood, three beats and a pause and two more. Sable's knock. Emrys knew it already, after only days: the rhythm of a soldier who had learned to announce herself without sounding urgent, because urgency drew guards, and guards asked questions Sable no longer had patience to answer.

The door opened before Emrys spoke. Sable slipped inside with the snow still on her shoulders — actual snow, unmelted, which should have been impossible in a room kept warm by pipes the palace claimed ran from deep furnaces. It sat in her grey hair like a crown of small cold stars.

"The stairwell's sealed," Sable said. "Storm took the east face of the tower. We're shut in till morning."

"We?"

Sable set her sword against the wall — deliberately, Emrys noticed, within Emrys's reach rather than her own, which was either trust or a very elegant trap. "The Regent ordered all posts maintained during curfew. I informed him I would maintain my post." She nodded toward the room. "This one."

"You're supposed to guard me."

"I am guarding you." Sable began unbuckling her gauntlets, finger by finger, as if peeling off armor were a kind of undressing. "From everyone else. Including myself, possibly. Including the book downstairs, if it comes to it."

Emrys laughed — a short, startled sound she didn't recognize as her own. "The book doesn't climb stairs."

"It wrote you climbing them," Sable said quietly. "Didn't it? You told me. Your own handwriting. Tomorrow, it says, you tell me everything." Her eyes came up, pale and level. "And I don't forgive you."

The wind filled the silence between them. Somewhere below, a shutter tore loose and went spinning into the white dark.

"So," Sable said. "I thought we might get ahead of it."

* * *

They built a fire in the hearth from furniture Sable broke apart with the efficient violence of someone who had once been paid for exactly this. A chair leg, a table slat, the frame of a mirror Emrys was glad to see go. The flames caught blue-green at first — palace wood, treated with something alchemical — then settled into honest orange.

Emrys wrapped herself in the blanket from the bed and sat on the floor across from Sable, close enough to feel the heat, far enough to pretend she'd chosen the distance.

"Everything," Sable said. "That's what the book says you'll tell me. So start anywhere. Start nowhere. But don't perform it. I've watched courtiers confess for forty years, and every confession I ever heard was a performance with the truth sewn in somewhere, like a coin in a pudding."

"You were at court forty years?"

"I was at court since I was nine years old." Sable fed another slat to the fire. "My father sold my oath along with his land. Queen Maren's household guard, third rank. I held doors for her for eleven years before she ever learned my name."

Emrys had heard fragments — the trial, the exile, the broken oath — but fragments only, and Sable had guarded those fragments jealously. Now she watched the knight sit in firelight in a borrowed room in a stolen tower, and understood that the blizzard had done what neither of them could: it had removed every exit.

"What happened," Emrys said. Not a question. An opening.

Sable was quiet long enough that the wind seemed to answer for her. Then:

"I loved her," Sable said.

The words landed in the room like something dropped from a height.

"Queen Maren. Aldous's mother. And before you ask — yes. Yes, it was that. Not devotion, not service dressed up in finer words. I loved her the way the tide loves the shore, which is to say stupidly, constantly, and with total disregard for what the shore wants." She turned her hands toward the fire. "She was thirty-four when I figured it out. I was twenty-two. I never told her. I want that entered into the record, clerk — I never told her, and I never touched her, and I never once let it change how I stood my post. I just carried it. Like everyone carries something."

"And the oath."

"The oath came later." Sable's voice changed — flattened, the way voices do when they approach a wound carefully, testing the ground. "You've seen him. Aldous. You've seen the pauses. The way his smile arrives half a second after the reason for smiling. You've seen what the crown does when it drinks."

"Yes."

"Maren saw it first. Before anyone. Before the dowager, before the priests, before the Regent — who knew, by the way, who has always known. She saw it because she'd been married to the thing's hunger for fifteen years and she knew her husband the way you know handwriting. One morning at breakfast he paused mid-sentence — just a pause, a heartbeat, a breath — and when he came back he was still smiling, but it was the crown's smile arriving through his face, and Maren dropped her cup." Sable stared into the fire. "She came to me that night. Not as queen to guard. As one drowning person to another. She said: *Sable, my husband is being eaten, and if I say so out loud they'll call me mad, and if they call me mad they'll put the regency in the Regent's hands, and the Regent is the crown's hands.*"

"What did she want?"

"She wanted proof. Something that couldn't be dismissed. So we did a foolish thing — the kind of thing lovers do, though we weren't lovers, though I'd have burned the world for the chance to be. We went into the crown chamber. There's a room under the throne hall where the sigil is kept when it isn't worn — or was, then, in the intervals between kings. Maren wanted to see it unworn. To know what it looked like when it wasn't wearing someone she loved."

The fire popped. Emrys found she had stopped breathing and made herself resume.

"And I broke my oath to get us there. That's the whole of it. I forged a rotation order, I lied to four men whose funerals I later attended, I walked my queen past her own guards in the dead of night, and we stood in front of the sigil while it slept. And it wasn't asleep." Sable's jaw worked. "It woke up. Just enough. It showed her something — I don't know what, she never said, and I never asked — and she came out of that chamber white as the snow outside your window and said one sentence to me on the stairs: *It's been wearing us longer than it's been wearing them.*"

"What does that mean?"

"I've had twenty years to wonder." Sable shook her head slowly. "I think it means the bargain goes deeper than crowns. I think it means Carreth itself — the winter, the throne, the whole arrangement — is the sigil's body, and the kings are just its favorite mouth. But that's a knight's guess, not a scholar's. What I know is this: two weeks later Maren tried to warn the council. Two hours after that she was declared unfit. Three days after that she was dead — fever, they said, sudden fever, though I'd held her hand the night before and she'd been cool as riverstone and sharp as a needle. And when I stood up in the mourning court and said the word *poison*, when I said the word *crown*—" she spread her empty hands, "—that was the oathbreaking. Not the forgery. Not the lies. The truth. They exiled me for the truth, Emrys. They let me keep my life because I'd been useful for thirty years, and they let me come back last year because the Regent decided a known traitor guarding the prophecy's chosen sacrifice made a tidy story. Everyone believes a jailer who hates her prisoner."

"Do you hate me?" Emrys asked, before she could stop herself.

Sable looked at her across the fire for a long moment, and something moved in her face that was too complicated to be called an expression.

"No," she said. "That's the problem. I was supposed to. I've been trying to since Vell, since the salt road, since the moment I caught you running and had you by the wrist and let go instead. I keep waiting for it to arrive, the hate, the way duty used to arrive — clean and on schedule — and it doesn't come. Instead I find myself breaking curfew orders to sit in your firelight." She almost smiled. Almost. "So there. Everything. Your turn. And clerk — the book says you tell everything. Don't let it have this. Make it yours."

* * *

Emrys looked into the fire and felt the truth rising in her like floodwater.

She had rehearsed versions of this confession for seven years — in the ledgers' margins, in the dark of her narrow room above the guild warehouse, in the space between one entry and the next where her life had quietly accumulated. But every rehearsal had an audience of strangers. This audience was worse.

"My gift came late," she said. "Storm-calling usually shows at six, seven. Children make rain over the garden and their mothers cry with pride. Mine came at nineteen, the week before my trial, and I'd hidden it before that because in Vell, storm-callers are cargo. The guild ships them. Did you know that? Bottled weather needs a caller to decant it, and Corvane pays callers a tenth of what he charges, and calls it generosity." She pulled the blanket tighter. "So I hid it. Precision was safer. Numbers don't summon anything. That's why I love ledgers — a ledger can't lie back at you. Every column balances or it doesn't. No weather in it at all."

"And the storm."

"And the storm." Her throat closed. She waited it out. "There was a ship. The *Kindly Fortune*. Out of Vell harbor, bound south with a cargo of bottled spring — actual bottled spring, whole seasons in glass, the guild's most profitable line. And the man who owned the manifest was a smuggler named Oskar Vane, and Oskar Vane had cheated Corvane on a shipment of unmade vows, and Corvane decided to make an example." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the ledger voice, because the alternative was screaming. "He gave the decanting job to me. Nineteen years old, first solo assignment, told it was routine. Told the bottles held fog. Forty bottles of fog, he said, vent them over open water."

"But they weren't fog."

"They were storms. Four decades of harvested storms — hurricanes, squalls, the kind of weather that gets *named*. And when I cracked the seals over the harbor road, they didn't disperse. Storms remember being free, Sable. You pour them out and they don't thank you, they *take*. They took me. They took my gift and rode it and I was nineteen years old and lying on my back on the harbor road with lightning coming out of my hands like I was the bottle now, like I was the thing being poured." The fire swam in her vision. "Eleven people died. The harbor quarter burned. And when the magistrates asked me whose cargo it was, I said Corvane's name, and Corvane produced a manifest with my signature on it — my actual signature, purchased from a forger who worked for him, dated three days before — and the court believed paper over a girl, because paper always wins, that's the first thing they teach you about ledgers."

Sable said nothing. She had gone very still in the way soldiers go still, Emrys realized — not absence of motion but presence of attention.

"They exiled me for a storm I never called," Emrys said. "And here is the part I've never said aloud, to anyone, not even to myself in the dark. Ready? Here's the part the Codex knows and I didn't." She made herself look up. "Corvane didn't choose me at random. He told me so, the night before the riders came. He's known about the Codex for years. He's known the prophecy names a storm-caller born in Vell, exiled for a storm she didn't call, summoned north in her twenty-sixth year. He read that page a decade ago, and he went looking for a girl who fit, and he found one, and he *built the rest*. The shipment. The false manifest. The trial. My entire ruin was a costume fitted to a prophecy, so that when the date came, the book would find me ready-made." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I wasn't cursed by fate, Sable. I was *stocked*. Like inventory."

The wind screamed against the tower. Neither of them moved.

"That night," Emrys whispered. "The night of the storm. When the lightning was coming out of me and the harbor was burning and I was begging it to stop — for one moment, one moment, it stopped. It gathered itself up over the water and it *looked* at me. I've spent seven years telling myself I imagined it. But it looked at me the way you look at something you've been sent to fetch. And it left. It went north. Over the sea, into the dark, straight north." She hugged her knees. "I thought I was remembering wrong. But the crown sent that storm, didn't it. Years early. Hunting for me. And it found me, and it marked me, and it went home to wait for the date."

Sable crossed the space between them in one motion.

It wasn't graceful. She was too old for grace and too tired for ceremony, and she simply folded down onto the cold stones beside Emrys and put one arm around her shoulders, awkward as a drawbridge, heavy and certain. Emrys stiffened — seven years of flinching had built themselves into her bones — and then, slowly, like ice deciding to become water, she leaned.

"The book says I won't forgive you," Sable said into her hair.

"The book says a lot of things."

"I know. I've read it." A pause. Wind. Fire. "Here's what I actually think, and you can enter it in whatever ledger you like. I think the book is right about everything except the part it wants most. I think it wrote *she tells everything and Sable does not forgive her* because the sigil needs you alone. Because a woman who is forgiven has someone standing beside her at the unbinding, and the crown cannot afford witnesses. The prophecy isn't a prediction, Emrys. I finally understand that. It's an *instruction manual*, and every line of it is written by the thing it describes." Her arm tightened. "So no. I'm not going to forgive you tonight, because there's nothing to forgive. You were nineteen. You were lied to. You were inventory. And tomorrow, when the sun claws back up over that ridiculous frozen city, you and I are going to walk down into that library and we're going to find the page where it says you die alone, and we are going to break it like a chair leg."

Emrys laughed — wet, wrecked, real. "Break a prophecy."

"I broke an oath for less."

They stayed like that as the fire burned low, and the blizzard knocked and knocked and was refused, and at some point Emrys slept — truly slept, dreamless, for the first time since Vell — with her head on the shoulder of an oathbreaker who was staying by no law but her own.

* * *

Sable woke to a sound that did not belong to the storm.

A bell. Single, silver, patient, ringing somewhere far beneath the tower — not the alarm bells of the watch, not the chapel's bronze. This was older. She had heard it exactly twice in her life, both times in the reign of Maren's husband, both times followed by days the court later pretended had not happened.

The crown chamber bell. Ringing on its own.

Beside her, Emrys stirred but did not wake, her face in sleep stripped of all its careful precision, young and unarmored. Sable eased out from under her, took up her sword, and went to the arrow slit.

The blizzard had stopped. Completely. The sky over Carreth hung cloudless and black and full of furious stars, and the snow lay waist-deep and untouched in the streets below — and in the silence, every window of the palace's lower floors was lighting, one by one, gold and steady, as if the building itself were opening its eyes.

And down the frozen length of the processional avenue, moving slow and unhurried through the drifts with no escort and no lanterns, came a single figure in a nightrobe, bareheaded, barefoot in the snow.

King Aldous. Walking toward the tower where the prophecy slept.

Walking, Sable realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, like a man who had just woken up — and who remembered everything.
