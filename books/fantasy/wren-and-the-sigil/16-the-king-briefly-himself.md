## 16. The King, Briefly Himself

The drowning man does not know he is drowning.

That is the first mercy of it — the only mercy. King Aldous Carreth had learned this the way one learns anything inside the sigil: slowly, in fragments, like reading a letter through frosted glass. For long stretches there was nothing wrong at all. He woke in his own bed, in his own body, and loved his kingdom with a warmth so complete it felt less like feeling than like weather. He smiled at ambassadors. He wept at the right moments during the Festival of First Snow. He signed what was set before him, and his hand never trembled, because the crown would not permit trembling; trembling was inefficiency.

Then, sometimes — perhaps once a season, perhaps once a year, he could not keep time in there — a crack opened.

Tonight the crack came at the ninth bell, while he sat alone over the maps of the northern grain routes, and it opened the way ice opens on a spring river: all at once, with a sound only he could hear, a groaning deep beneath the surface, and then light everywhere.

He came up gasping into his own skull as though into air after years underwater.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh, no. Oh, she is still here.*

He knew, in that first raw minute, three things with terrible clarity. He knew how long it had been — not by counting but by looking at his own hands, which had aged twenty years since the last time he had truly inhabited them. He knew that the warmth he felt toward everything, everyone, was not his warmth but the sigil's, poured through him like dye through water until the cloth forgot its original color. And he knew that somewhere in this palace, behind a door he had signed an order to lock without ever really reading what he signed, there was a girl from Vell who smelled faintly of rain and ink, and whose name the crown had begun whispering to itself in the dark hours like a prayer said backward.

Emrys Zhou.

He did not know how he knew her name. That frightened him most of all. The crown kept its ledgers too, and it had written her into them.

Aldous stood. His knees protested — twenty years of a stranger walking in his legs had left their stiffness — and he gripped the edge of the map table and laughed, once, a small broken sound that no king should make alone at night. The laugh was his. The first thing in decades that was entirely his.

"How long," he said aloud, to the empty chamber, to himself. "How long do I have."

He did not know. He had learned not to waste these hours on inventory or grief. There would be time for grief later, if there was a later; grief was a luxury the crown permitted him only in retrospect, when it surfaced him briefly just to remind him what drowning felt like. No. When the crack opened, Aldous had one purpose, refined across every previous surfacing into something hard and singular as a keel:

Get to her. Warn her. Beg her.

And if she refused — and she might, she should — tell her everything anyway, so that whatever she chose, she chose it knowing.

* * *

The corridors of Carreth Palace at night were lit by frost-lamps that burned without flame, a gift of the sigil's early centuries, and they cast light the color of old teeth. Aldous moved through them quickly, wrapped in a plain cloak against the cold that lived in these halls now the way mortar lives in walls. Two guards at the west stair saluted him with the easy warmth of men who loved their king, and he returned their salute and hated himself for the love in their faces, because he knew now that some fraction of it was not theirs either. The crown leaked. Like a lamp with a cracked casing, it leaked its terrible counterfeit warmth into everyone who lingered near its wearer, and a kingdom that loved its king this uniformly, this painlessly, was a kingdom already halfway swallowed.

*Not tonight,* he told the guards silently. *Tonight you are loving a man who is robbing you. Forgive me. I am trying to stop.*

The tower where they kept her was called the Guesthouse of the Prophecy, a piece of court nomenclature so obscene that Aldous wanted to spit at its plaque. He climbed. His lungs burned. At the third landing he passed the alcove where, two winters ago, he had — the other one, the thing wearing him — had stood for six hours in perfect stillness, listening to snow fall upward, and had found it beautiful.

He reached her door. It was locked, of course, but the key hung on his own belt, because every lock in the palace answered to the crown, and the crown did not consider the girl a prisoner. Prisoners were people. She was an appointment.

Aldous knocked.

"Who's there?" Her voice was wary and awake. Of course she was awake. They never let summoned ones sleep properly; sleep made the prophecy restless, the Regent said, though Aldous suspected the truth was crueller — sleep made them dream, and dreaming made them remember they had wills.

"Aldous," he said. "King Aldous. Open to me, mistress Zhou, or don't, but listen either way." He pressed his forehead briefly against the cold iron. "I have perhaps an hour. Less, if I'm unlucky. Please."

Silence, long enough that he began composing the speech he would deliver through the door. Then the lock turned — she must have taken the key from a guard, or picked it, or persuaded it, and he found he didn't care which — and the door swung open on lamplight and a young woman standing with her back against the far wall, a heavy brass inkstand held in both hands like a weapon.

She looked at him the way people look at beautiful weather they have learned not to trust.

"You're supposed to be charming," she said. "You're very good at it. Everyone says so."

"I know," Aldous said. "It isn't mine. May I come in? I'd rather not spend my hour being looked at through a doorway like a painting of myself."

She didn't lower the inkstand, but she stepped aside, and he took it for the grace it was.

* * *

Her room was spare but she had colonized it — he noticed this first, and it steadied him more than anything else could have. Ledgers stacked in careful columns. A quill mended with waxed thread. A window seat worn pale where someone sat for hours watching the frozen city, and beside it, on the sill, small scratches in the stone: tallies, and letters, and words he could not read from where he stood but recognized the style of instantly, because he had seen them before, in other rooms, under other layers of paint.

The storm-callers before her had scratched their warnings into their cells too.

"You've been reading the walls," he said.

"I've been *adding* to them." Her chin lifted. "Someone has to keep honest accounts in this place."

Something in his chest cracked further, painfully, wonderfully. He sat down heavily in the room's single chair — uninvited, because his hour was bleeding out and courtesy was a currency the crown had counterfeited beyond use — and he folded his hands, and he looked at her, and he began.

"I am going to tell you what it is like in there," he said, "and you are going to believe me, because you are a clerk and clerks believe testimony that is precise. Am I right?"

Her eyes narrowed. But she nodded once.

"It is warm," Aldous said. "That is the horror of it, so understand it first. Inside the sigil it is warm, and bright, and full. There is no loneliness there at all. Do you know why?" He watched her face, saw her begin, unwillingly, to follow him. "Because there is no *room*. A hundred years of kings, Emrys Zhou. Nine of us, wearing it, and every one of us still in there. We are packed together like singers in a choir stall that keeps getting smaller. I can feel Harrow the Third's shoulder against mine. I can feel old Maravel humming — she hummed when she was alive, some northern lullaby, and she hums it still, forever, because the sigil does not let a thing end, it only lets a thing *continue*." His voice was steady. He was proud of that. He had rehearsed steadiness across many surfacings. "And we scream, of course. All of us. But the sigil is courteous. It was made to be courteous — you'll have heard the stories, it ended a war, it *saved* us — and so it teaches us to scream politely. Quietly. In harmony. A century of kings screaming in four-part harmony, and outside, the whole kingdom hears music."

The inkstand lowered, slowly, to her lap.

"The warmth," she said. Her voice had gone thin. "When you smile at people. When the court loves you."

"Borrowed," Aldous said. "Poured through me. The sigil wants to be loved — I think it was made by people who were afraid of being hated, and it has spent a hundred years making sure no one in Carreth ever hates their king, and it has never once asked whether anyone loves the *man*." He laughed again, that broken sound. "I cannot even hate it cleanly, do you understand? Part of the love in this room right now — yours, if any, your poor reluctant pity — some of it may be leakage. I cannot audit my own heart anymore. I was an auditor once, in my way. Kings audit kingdoms. Now I can't find the ledger line where I end."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in the flat practical tone of a woman doing arithmetic she despised: "Why are you telling me this? What do you want from me?"

And there it was. The center of the hour. Aldous leaned forward, and let the last pretense of majesty fall away from him like a cloak off a shoulder, and spoke as one drowning man to another.

"I want you to unbind me," he said. "Tear it off. End it. I know — I know what it costs, or I guess at it, the crown hides the rite but it dreams loudly — I know it takes something from the one who does it. Perhaps everything. Perhaps your storm, if the rumors of what you are hold true. And so I want you to refuse, if refusing is right. I release you from refusing badly. Do you see? I am not here to beg you onto an altar. I am here because for twenty years I have been a puppet who occasionally wakes up inside his own strings, and the worst of it — the very worst, worse than the screaming, worse than Maravel's humming — is that no one has ever *told* me anything. Everyone decides around me. My regent. My council. This prophecy. Even my rescue, if it comes, will be decided for me." His hands were shaking now; he let them. "So I am deciding one thing myself, tonight, while I am me. I am telling you the truth, and asking, and meaning the asking, and meaning also that you may say no. That is mine. No one gave me that. I took it."

The wind moved against the tower glass. Somewhere below, the frozen city lay silent under a moon like a coin too long in a miser's fist.

Emrys Zhou looked at him for a long time. Then she set the inkstand down entirely, and pulled her ledger across the table toward herself, and dipped her pen, and said, in the voice of a woman opening a fresh page:

"Start from the beginning. The binding. Names, dates, terms. If I'm going to weigh whether to save you, Your Majesty, I'm going to do it properly — in ink, with citations."

Aldous felt tears arrive and did not forbid them. "You magnificent creature," he said. "Yes. From the beginning."

* * *

He talked. She wrote. It was the first conversation he had had in twenty years in which he was not performing, and it lasted — he counted the bells — fifty-one minutes.

In minute forty, he told her about the interior again, because she asked, because she was thorough: the geometry of the sigil's inside, which was not space at all but *agreement*, nine kings pressed into consensus like flowers pressed into glass, each one's screams harmonized into the crown's great patient hum. He told her that the oldest of them, a king named Vessant whom the histories remembered kindly, had stopped screaming centuries ago and now only whispered, and that his whisper sounded almost contented, and that this was the fate Aldous feared above death: not to be destroyed but to be *converted*, worn smooth, made into another polite voice in the choir.

"They'll offer you things," he said. "At the end. It always offers. It offered Harrow his dead son back, in a dream, for eleven years, every night, until Harrow walked to the throne room himself and knelt and asked for the crown to be tightened." He saw her flinch. "I tell you so that when it offers, you will recognize the voice. It will sound like mercy. It is inventory management."

In minute fifty-one, the crack began to close.

He felt it the way a man feels dawn withdraw — not suddenly, but as a dimming at the edges, a softening of his own outlines. The warmth came first, seeping back in at the collar like meltwater, gentle, insidious, kind. His shoulders loosened without his permission. His breathing slowed into the sigil's preferred rhythm, deep and serene.

"No," he said. He gripped the table. "No, not yet, listen — Emrys —"

She was on her feet. "What do I do?"

"Nothing. Nothing can—" He fought for the words while they were still his. "The Regent. Trust nothing from the Regent. The Codex lies by omission, it was built to— my daughter's portrait, in the east gallery, behind it there is—" What was behind it? The memory was already going soft at the edges, dissolving like sugar in warm water. He clawed at it. "Behind it there is something I hid while I was still mostly myself. I don't — I can't remember what — find it, please, find it, it matters, I hid it *on purpose*—"

"Your Majesty—"

"Aldous," he said. "My name. Say it while I can still hear it said by someone who means it."

"Aldous." She said it firmly, like a word being entered into evidence.

And then the warmth closed over his head like the surface of dark water, and King Aldous Carreth smiled — warmly, perfectly, terribly — and straightened his cloak, and thanked the lady of the prophecy for the pleasant conversation, and wished her restful sleep, and walked out of the room with a gait like music.

Deep underneath, in the warm bright crowded nowhere, a man screamed politely, and kept the tally of his remaining self, and waited for the next crack in the ice.

And in the tower room, Emrys Zhou stood a long moment with her pen uncapped, looking at the fifty-one minutes of testimony drying on the page — and then at the door he had gone through, and beyond it, down the dark stair, toward the east gallery and a portrait with something hidden behind it.

She blew out her lamp, took up the ledger, and went to find out what a half-drowned king had thought worth hiding from his own crown.
