## 17. Corvane's Counterfeit Codex

The forgery took nine days, and Corvane resented every one of them.

Not for the work itself — he had built a guild on the patient manufacture of impossible things, and ink was the least of his materials — but for the waiting. Waiting was a clerk's vice. Waiting was what happened to men who had lost control of their own ledgers, and Guildmaster Corvane of the Guild of Impossible Things did not consider himself such a man. Not yet. The column in his books that mattered most — *Emrys Zhou, asset, unpriced* — had been drifting out of his hands since the night the Codex Vellum pulled her north like a fish on a line, and Corvane had learned long ago that an unpriced asset was either a fortune or a funeral, and the difference usually came down to who wrote the entry first.

So he wrote first.

He worked by lamplight in the cabin of a river barge crawling up the frozen Thessaly toward Carreth, because a man in his position could not be seen doing this work in the capital, and a barge had the additional virtue of being easy to burn. On the table before him lay two books. One was genuine: a century old, calfskin gone soft as candle wax, its pages the color of weak tea. He had bought it honestly, or as honestly as he bought anything, from a defrocked archivist in Vell who wept while counting the coin and thanked him afterward, which Corvane had always found touching about people selling things they loved.

The other book was his.

He had commissioned it eighteen months ago, before he ever knew he would need it — that was the difference between merchants and masters; the masters ordered rope before the flood — from a bindery in the Free Ports whose proprietor believed she was making a prop for a traveling theater company. She had aged the leather with smoke and tannin, cracked the spine with a vise, seeded the endpapers with mold spores and then killed them again with heat, until the book smelled convincingly of a century's neglect. It had cost him more than the barge. He had not blinked.

The pages were the hard part.

Corvane dipped his pen and held it over the page without writing, the way he did when the numbers were difficult. He was not a calligrapher. He was better than a calligrapher, which was the problem — real scribes slanted, hesitated, blotted. Perfection read as forgery to anyone trained, and the royal archivists were trained, and more importantly the crown's Regent was *not* a man at all but a thing wearing a man's posture, and no one alive knew what a thing like that noticed.

So he made himself worse. He practiced the old scriptorium hand for a week on sacrificial quires — the letterforms of the reign of Alderic II, the particular way a certain school of monks crossed their thorns and doubled their serifs, the brown-black iron-gall ink that bloomed faintly through the page from the reverse side. He wrote passages of the true prophecy copied from memory and checked them against his notes, and where his memory failed him he invented confidently, because confidence was the whole trade. A forged prophecy did not need to match the original. It needed only to match what the reader already feared or hoped, and then arrive one day too late to be questioned.

He wrote:

*In the last winter of the hundred-year crown, two shall stand before the throne: the storm-born, who carries the rite in her blood, and the oath-broken, who carries the door in her hands.*

He paused there, letting the lamplight gutters fill the silence, and permitted himself the small pleasure of admiring his own sentence. It had everything. Symmetry. Two names where the court would look for two names. And the word *oath-broken*, which was doing more work than any honest word should do, because Sable Vane was famous in precisely the wrong way — the knight who broke her oath publicly, spectacularly, in front of the old queen and half the palace guard, and had been exiled for it, and had never once explained why. A kingdom loves a mystery it can punish. Every soul in Carreth who heard the phrase *the oath-broken* would think of her within a breath, and think, immediately after, *of course*.

That was the secret of prophecy, Corvane thought, dipping his pen again. Nobody wanted to know the future. They wanted permission for what they already believed. He had sold bottled memories to grieving widows and unmade vows to runaway brides for twenty years, and every transaction ran on the same current: give a person a story where their worst impulse is destiny, and they will pay anything, and thank you, and sleep well.

He continued:

*And the rite demands a choosing: one life freely given to unmake the crown, and the crown will name its price. But hear the mercy written under the words: the storm-born may walk away, and the door may close upon another. The book does not choose. Only the reader chooses. Blessed is she who reads wisely.*

There it was. The trap, laid out in a dead monk's careful hand, dressed as mercy.

Because that was the genius of it — if he dared apply the word genius to his own work, and privately, alone, at midnight, he did — the forgery did not order Emrys to sacrifice Sable. Orders could be refused. What it offered was an *out*. Read between the lines, dear storm-caller: the prophecy says the choice is yours, and the book blesses the wise reader, and isn't wisdom simply survival with better grammar? He had watched Emrys for two years across the ledger desk. He knew her precision, her economy, her terror of waste — of wasted effort, wasted risk, wasted self. She kept accounts because accounts were how she kept the world at arm's length, and a woman who keeps accounts can always be reached through the arithmetic. One friend against herself. One debt discharged against another incurred. Any honest bookkeeper would see the sense of it.

Any honest bookkeeper, Corvane corrected himself, dipping the pen. That was the flaw in his beautiful machine. Emrys Zhou was many things — exiled, precise, lonely in a way she'd mistaken for independence — but dishonest was not among them, and he had built his entire operation on the assumption that she was exactly what she appeared. He had spent years cultivating her trust precisely because she trusted so rarely. Now he needed that same quality to bend. Steel that has never bent, he reminded himself, snapping clean instead. He had seen it happen to better metal than this.

It didn't have to bend all the way. It only had to hesitate. One hour of hesitation at the right moment, one afternoon where she sat with the false page and said nothing to Sable — that was all the crown required, and all *he* required, because his arrangement with the Regent was specific and the Regent's patience was not.

The terms of that arrangement sat in his breast pocket, and Corvane touched the pocket once, briefly, the way a gambler touches a losing card he refuses to fold.

*Passage to the chosen one.* He had been selling it for three years. Royal agents came down the salt road in plain cloaks with heavy purses, asking after the guild that traded in fate, and Corvane had shown them the Codex Vellum — the real one, behind glass, reverent as a relic — and let them read tonight's date in century-old ink, and named a figure, and watched them pay. Twelve times now. Eleven of those chosen ones had died in Carreth, quietly, their cells cleaned, their names struck from the rolls, and Corvane had entered each payment in the guild's books under *consultation fees, archival*, and slept without difficulty. He told himself the standard things. He told himself they would have found her anyway; he merely set the toll. He told himself the crown would have taken the whole guild otherwise, and everyone in it. He told himself that Emrys, unlike the others, was necessary — the storm-born, the actual unbinding — and therefore his sale of her was different in kind, though he was too good an accountant to believe that and too old a liar to care.

What he had not anticipated — what still sat in his chest like a swallowed stone whenever he thought of it, and he thought of it more often than he liked — was that the girl was genuinely the one. The prophecy had moved her across a country in a single night. He had seen the scorch-mark of her arrival on the palace flagstones. Somewhere beneath his ledgers, in a place he kept locked even from himself, something that might have been guilt had begun keeping its own books.

This forgery would settle those accounts. If Emrys chose Sable, the rite would fail — he was nearly sure the rite would fail; the crown's own scholars believed a coerced choice poisoned the unbinding — and the sigil would complete its century hungry and unsatisfied, and Corvane's position as licensed supplier to the throne would be secure forever, or at least as forever as anything was. If Emrys refused and died trying to save her knight, the rite might succeed and the crown fall, but Corvane would already have burned his barges and his bridges and his books, and a man who could vanish could survive any dawn. Either way he lived. That was the whole design. That had always been the whole design. He was a merchant, not a monster; monsters picked sides.

He finished the page near dawn, somewhere past the ice-locked mouth of the Thessaly, and blew the ink dry, and read it through four times, and hated the fifth reading, because on the fifth reading he saw what the crown's scholars would see and what Emrys might see and what *he* saw, all at once: a dead language promising that betrayal was blessed.

"Mercy," he said aloud to the empty cabin, testing the word. It sounded like what it was.

He inserted the leaf into the binding with a bone folder and a thin blade, splitting the gutter along a seam he'd weakened months ago, and pressed it flat, and closed the book, and the counterfeit became simply a book — old, soft, smelling of smoke and mildew and a century of other people's certainty.

Then Corvane did the thing he was actually best at, the thing no forger and no smuggler and no king could do: he waited for the market to come to him.

* * *

Carreth received him the way it received everything now — silently, under snow that fell in slow spirals that sometimes, at the edges of vision, reversed themselves and rose. Corvane had watched a snowflake climb back into the clouds outside the palace gates and decided not to think about it, which was becoming his principal method of surviving this city.

The Regent met him in the Map Room, a long gallery where every territory the crown had ever claimed hung painted on vellum, and where the maps had lately begun to change — coastlines creeping, rivers fattening with ice, borders of countries that had never existed bleeding in at the margins like water stains. The Regent stood before the largest map, the one of Carreth itself, and did not turn around.

"You are late," the Regent said.

"The river froze mid-current." Corvane kept his voice level. "Your winter is ambitious."

"It is *hungry*." The Regent turned. His face was handsome in the way furniture is handsome, carved and symmetrical and warm-toned, and his eyes had the fixed shine of varnish. Corvane had negotiated with dukes and pirates and one dragon that wore a bishop's ring, and none of them had frightened him the way this did: not menace, but absence. Talking to the Regent was talking to a room where a man used to be. "The century closes at solstice. The crown requires its rite. You promised the storm-born would act."

"I promised she'd *arrive*. She arrived." Corvane set the forged Codex on the map table, between Carreth and the sea, and rested his hand on it. "Arrival and action are different commodities. I sell passage, my lord. Persuasion is above my station."

"Nothing is above your station. Your station is whatever I say it is." The Regent drifted closer — drifted, there was no other word; his feet moved but his body seemed to travel separately, like a reflection sliding along water. He looked down at the book. "You brought me a forgery."

"I brought you a *tool*." Corvane opened it to the new leaf and stepped back, giving it room, the way you gave a snare room. "The girl won't take the bait if it smells of the crown. It smells of her own book — the same hand, the same age, the same prophecy, continuing. Read it."

The Regent read. The varnish eyes moved. When he looked up, he smiled, and the smile arrived a half-second late, like thunder after lightning, and Corvane understood with perfect clarity that he was standing in a room with something that was currently practicing being pleased.

"The oath-breaker," the Regent said. "The one who guards her."

"The one she trusts. Which makes her the lever, and levers are cheaper than crowbars." Corvane tapped the page. "Plant it where the girl will find it. Her tower room, her cell, wherever you keep her — no. Better. The Codex chamber. Let her discover it herself, beside the real book, comparing hands. Nothing convinces like evidence she thinks she stole."

"And if she refuses? If she warns the knight instead?"

"Then she dies loyal and the rite fails, and we are all ruined together, and I will at least die having balanced my books." Corvane smiled his merchant's smile, the one that had closed a thousand deals. Inside, the stone in his chest shifted its weight. "But she won't refuse. I've read her ledger for two years, my lord. She is a woman who believes everything can be survived if you account for it correctly. I am offering her an accounting."

The Regent closed the book with both hands, gently, the way a man touches a sleeping child, and something in the gentleness was worse than violence. "Solstice," he said. "Dawn. Or the crown completes itself, and the century ends full — and everything it has eaten stays eaten. Including," the varnish eyes settled on Corvane with terrible patience, "your little guild. Including your clerks. Including the girl. The crown remembers everyone it swallows, Guildmaster. Forever. Would you like that? To be remembered forever?"

"No one would," Corvane said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

"Then see to your tool."

* * *

He planted it himself. Of course he planted it himself — he trusted no one else's hands near the work, and besides, he needed to see the chamber.

Two of the Regent's guards walked him down through the palace at dusk, past windows where snow fell upward in slow silver threads, down stairs worn hollow by centuries of royal feet, to a round chamber in the oldest wing that the guides' histories called the Archive of Outcomes. Shelves curved with the walls. Candles burned without flickering, which Corvane noted and elected not to think about. And in the center, on a lectern of black oak, under glass that lifted at a touch, lay the Codex Vellum.

The real one. He knew it instantly, the way a father knows his child's cry in a crowd of crying children — though *father* was a poor word for what he felt, standing before the book he had sold eleven lives against. The true pages were open to the passage naming the storm-born, and the old ink had changed since he'd last seen it. New lines had surfaced in the century-old script, darkening even as he watched, the letters rising through the page like drowned bodies coming up through water:

*She stands at the choosing. The door opens both ways. What she carries, she may pour out or keep. What she loves, she may spend or save.*

The book was negotiating. Corvane felt the hair rise on his arms. In thirty years of impossible trade he had never seen a text argue with its own readers, and here it was, revising itself in real time, offering terms. He thought suddenly, absurdly, of Tamsin — of his clerks, his family of strays and runaways he'd collected the way misers collect coins — and of what they would say if they could see him now, standing in a dead king's library, helping a crown forge a lie about a girl who had once stayed late without being asked to reorganize his shipping manifests because she couldn't bear untidy columns.

He put the thought away where he put all such thoughts, in the drawer that was getting very full, and did his work.

The swap took ninety seconds. Glass up, true Codex shifted six inches left on the lectern, false Codex open to the new leaf and laid beside it, angled just slightly, invitingly, the way a dealer angles merchandise. Then glass down, guards summoned, corridor, stairs, done. As he climbed back toward the lit floors he passed a window and saw, far below in the frozen court, two small figures walking the wall-walk together: a tall woman in a disgraced knight's coat, and beside her a slighter figure whose hair caught the last grey light like wire. They were talking. The knight laughed at something — Corvane had never once heard Sable Vane laugh and was surprised by the sound of it, even through glass — and the girl did not laugh, but she looked up at the knight with an expression Corvane recognized from his own ledgers, from the entries he never wrote down: the expression of someone who has decided, against all sound accounting, to trust.

He stood at the window longer than he intended.

*One hour of hesitation,* he told the glass, the figures, himself. *That's all the design needs. Not treachery. Just silence, one evening, one unread warning, one choice not to share what she knows. She'll tell herself she's protecting Sable from the knowledge. They always do. Mercy, she'll call it. Everyone calls it mercy.*

The two figures turned at the wall's end and walked back the way they came, closer to the window now, and for a moment Emrys Zhou's face passed below him, upturned and unaware, and Corvane looked down at the face of the woman he had sold, and raised his hand — halfway, meaninglessly, a gesture aimed at no one — before catching himself and lowering it, and turning from the window, and going up to supper, where he ate well and tasted nothing.

* * *

Three days later, the report came up from the archive guards: the girl had found the false codex. She had sat with it for four hours. She had compared hands — the guards reported ink stains on her fingers, the particular hunched posture of a clerk checking a forgery — and she had copied the false passage out in her own neat hand, and taken the copy with her, and said nothing to anyone.

Said nothing to anyone.

Corvane read the report twice, alone in his rented rooms above the chandlery, with the wind worrying the shutters and the upward snow ticking softly against the glass like fingernails. This was the moment he had engineered. This was victory, by every measure he had ever kept: the bait taken, the silence achieved, the lever set. Four hours over the page and then quiet — quiet toward the knight, quiet toward the dowager, quiet toward the lucid king. She was carrying it alone, exactly as designed, exactly as every mark he'd ever fleeced had carried their worst secret alone, hoarding it, turning it over, telling herself that knowing was not yet choosing.

And yet.

He had been a merchant of human weakness too long not to recognize the flaw in his own instrument, and it was this: she hadn't burned the copy. She hadn't torn the leaf out. She had *transcribed* it — carefully, completely, in a clerk's faithful hand — and a woman planning to betray a friend did not make a fair copy of the betrayal. She made a case file. She was checking his work. Somewhere in the tower rooms, in the hours when the candles burned without flickering, Emrys Zhou was auditing him, and Corvane sat very still in the dark and realized that he could not tell — could not *know*, with the certainty his whole life was built on — whether she had swallowed the lie or was merely digesting it slowly, looking for the bones.

The stone in his chest had grown heavier all winter. Now it moved.

He rose, lit a lamp despite the expense, and pulled out the one ledger he had never shown anyone — not the guild's books, not the crown's copies, but his own private volume, bound in black, wherein he recorded the only currency he truly believed in. Debts. Real ones. He turned past the royal agents' payments, past the eleven names of the dead chosen ones that he had finally, some weeks ago, in a fit he did not examine, entered in full — names, dates, causes of death where he knew them, which was most of them — and found the newest page, and uncapped his pen, and wrote:

*Emrys Zhou. Owed: one country, one storm, one life. Paid: nothing.*

His pen hovered. Below it, after a long moment, he added a second line, smaller, the handwriting less steady than any forgery he had ever made:

*Sable Vane. Owed: nothing. About to be charged with everything.*

He capped the ink and closed the book and sat in the lamplight listening to the wrongness of the snow, and told himself the design was sound, and the design was sound, and the design was sound — and then, far off across the frozen city, from the direction of the palace, he heard bells begin to ring out of order: not the hour, not the watch, but a pattern he had never heard, urgent and broken, and a moment later the chandlery boy came hammering up his stairs with the news that the oath-breaker knight had been arrested in the great hall before the whole court — seized by the Regent's guard, disarmed, dragged down to the deep cells, accused of conspiring with the storm-born against the crown.

Corvane stood motionless in the middle of the floor.

He had not ordered this. It was not in the design. The lever was supposed to stay free until solstice, until the choosing — and now the crown had taken it, clumsily, ahead of schedule, unless — unless this too had been written somewhere, in some page he had not read, in some ledger older than his own. Unless the book was moving pieces he hadn't priced.

He grabbed his coat. He was three steps from the door when he stopped, because he understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading his own final column, exactly where he had to go next — not to the Regent, not to his handlers, but down through the frozen city to the tower rooms, to stand in front of Emrys Zhou and answer, if she asked, one question he had spent his whole life avoiding:

*Whose hand wrote the forgery?*

He opened the door onto the dark, and the snow was falling upward, and he went out into it.
