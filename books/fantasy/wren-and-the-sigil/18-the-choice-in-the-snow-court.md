## 18. The Choice in the Snow Court

The herald's voice carried through the Snow Court like a knife drawn slowly from a sheath: *"By command of His Majesty, the storm-caller Emrys of Vell will present herself before the throne and speak the prophecy's truth."*

Emrys stood at the edge of the great hall, her breath fogging in the frozen air that even the palace's hundred hearths could not fully warm. The court had gathered — three hundred nobles in furs dyed the colors of a summer none of them remembered, clustered beneath windows glazed with frost-ferns. Above them all sat King Aldous on the Winter Throne, smiling his perfect smile, and beside the dais, robed in guild-grey with his ledger chains glinting at his throat, stood Corvane.

*Master*, she thought, and hated how the word still ached.

She had spent the night with both Codices open before her — the true one smuggled from the archive in a bread basket, the forged one delivered to her cell with Corvane's compliments and a note in his tidy clerk's hand: *Choose carefully, little wren. One of these makes you free; the other makes you dead. I have always found the arithmetic simple.*

He was right about one thing. It *was* arithmetic. She had spent eleven years as the Guild of Impossible Things' finest auditor, and she knew a falsified ledger when she held one. She knew the weight of honest ink versus the sheen of ink laid over a lie like gilt over rot.

The forged Codex named Sable.

Page forty-one, in script so close to the century-old hand that only someone who had catalogued four thousand volumes of seized estates could see the tell: the forger's pen had hesitated at the descender of every *g*, lifting a hair too soon, the way a modern nib does when it fears its own weight. And beneath Sable's name, in letters the color of dried blood, the forged prophecy promised what Corvane knew she wanted most: *and the storm-caller shall go free, her exile dissolved, her gift restored.*

All Emrys had to do was say nothing. Let the court read the false book. Let Sable be taken to the unbinding stone in her place. Walk out of Carreth whole.

Corvane caught her eye across the court and inclined his head, almost tenderly. *I taught you everything*, the gesture said. *Including this.*

"Approach," said the Regent, and his voice was the sound of a door opening onto an empty room.

---

Her boots left prints in the frost that coated the marble — small, precise prints, the prints of a woman who had spent her life making records no one could dispute. Three hundred pairs of eyes followed her. Somewhere among them, she knew, Tamsin was watching with her heart in her throat. Somewhere behind the guards' ranks, Sable stood under arrest, wrists bound in oath-iron, watching Emrys walk toward the throne with the forged Codex cradled in her arms like something precious.

That was the performance Corvane had arranged. That was the scene he had written. The chosen one, presenting the prophecy that would save the realm — and naming her sacrifice.

Emrys reached the foot of the dais and knelt, because the choreography demanded it, and because kneeling put her hands flat against the frozen floor, and cold ground had always helped her think.

"Rise," said Aldous.

And then — for half a heartbeat — the king's voice cracked, and underneath the crown's honeyed warmth came something raw and drowning: *"Don't. Please don't read it. It lies. They all lie—"* before the sigil closed over him again like water closing over a stone, and the king smiled on.

But Emrys had heard. A century of kings screaming politely, Aldous had told her in his lucid hour, and now one of them had screamed for *her*.

She rose. She opened the forged Codex to page forty-one.

"My lords and ladies of the court," she said, and her voice did not shake, because she had rehearsed this in the dark for six hours and shaken enough for a lifetime already. "Before you stands a book of prophecy. I am told it names the sacrifice required to end the long winter. I am told it is genuine."

She let the silence stretch. Courtiers leaned forward. The Regent's hollow face tilted by a degree, attentive as a weathervane.

"I am the Guild of Impossible Things' senior auditor," Emrys continued. "Eleven years. Four thousand volumes catalogued. Every ledger in our keeping has passed through my hands, and I can tell you the age of ink by its shadow and the honesty of a hand by its hesitations." She lifted the book high, turning it so the light caught page forty-one. "This book was written last month."

The court erupted — a rustle like wind through dead wheat. On the dais, the Regent raised one finger, and the noise died as if smothered.

"You lie," the Regent said, without inflection. "The Guildmaster attests to its authenticity."

"The Guildmaster attests to whatever sells." Emrys turned, deliberately slow, to face Corvane across thirty feet of frozen marble. "Don't you, Master?"

Corvane's expression never moved. He had the stillness of a man who had gambled everything on a single throw and was content to watch the dice fall. "My child," he said gently, "you are frightened and far from home. No one will think less of you—"

"Page forty-one," Emrys said over him. "Look at the letter *g*. In the true Codex Vellum, written a century ago, every descender lands full-weight — you can see where the pen rested between strokes, because the scribe wrote by candlelight and was not hurried. In your forgery, the pen lifts early. Every time. Because whoever wrote it used a modern Vellish nib and was *very* hurried indeed." She flipped pages, letting the court see. "Furthermore, the binding thread is guild-issue flax, dyed grey, from a lot we purchased eighteen months ago. I signed the requisition myself. Entry 4,417, second ledger, autumn inventory."

A noblewoman near the front made a small sound, somewhere between horror and delight. Courts loved a scandal; they only feared being on the wrong side of one.

"And the prophecy trade itself?" Emrys pressed on, her voice climbing now, burning off the last of her caution like fog before a gale. "Shall I tell you what else the Guild of Impossible Things sells? Bottled thunderstorms. Unmade vows. Second chances pried out of dying men's chests. And for the last nine years — passage to the chosen one. Royal agents paid the guild gold to know where prophecy's next victim would surface, and the guild took the coin, and I—" her breath caught, and she forced it steady, "—and *I* was the cargo."

She heard it land. Three hundred people, hearing for the first time that their beloved king's salvation had been shopped around like winter wool.

"The true Codex exists," she said. "It names me. It has named me since before I was born, and the crown has known, and the guild has known, and both of them have been selling tickets to my death. There is no version of this prophecy in which anyone else dies in my place. There is only the version where you let them convince you otherwise, because it was comfortable, because it was *for sale*."

"Enough." The Regent descended one step. Frost bloomed outward from his boot heel in a perfect circle. "Guards. Take the book. Take her tongue if she continues."

"No," said a new voice.

Tamsin stepped out of the crowd of clerks along the eastern wall — small, freckled Tamsin, who had once spilled ink on Emrys's first ledger and cried about it for a week. She held a ledger above her head like a lantern. "Entry by entry, my lords! Nine years of royal payments to the guild, itemized! Passage to the chosen one, sold four times — *four times* — and each buyer's agent recorded, and each 'chosen one' delivered!" Her voice broke and rebuilt itself. "I kept the duplicates. We all did. We're done lying for him."

Along the wall, a dozen guild clerks raised their ledgers in turn, grey-flax-bound books held up like torches, and Emrys felt something crack open in her chest that she would not be able to close again.

Corvane looked at his clerks. All of them. His family, his machine, his beautiful honest-dishonest enterprise — turned inside out on the frozen floor of the Snow Court. And then he looked at Emrys, and for the first time in eleven years, she saw him calculate and find nothing to calculate with.

"You were mine," he said quietly.

"I was never anyone's," Emrys answered. "That was rather the point of me."

He smiled then — thin, professional, terrible. "Ledgers balance, little wren. Always. Sooner or later, every debt comes due, and you've just written yourself into arrears with the only buyer in Carreth who pays in anything but coin." His eyes flicked upward, briefly, to the crown on the king's brow. "Winter is long. See that you stay warm."

Then he drew a folded paper from his sleeve, set it on the dais rail before the Regent, bowed with impeccable courtesy, and walked out of the Snow Court through the parting crowd, unhurried, unhindered, his ledger chains singing softly at his throat — and every instinct Emrys possessed told her that whatever he had just handed the Regent would cost more lives than any storm.

"Seize the Guildmaster," the Regent said — but lazily, an afterthought, and two guards who went after Corvane returned alone, reporting the man had vanished between the doors, as impossible things are wont to do.

The Regent turned back to Emrys. For a moment, beneath the hollow calm, she saw it plainly: the thing wearing him looking out, assessing, recalculating a century of plans around one stubborn auditor with a loud voice.

"You have made an interesting choice," the Regent said.

"It wasn't a choice." Emrys held up the forged Codex and tore page forty-one from its spine, then forty-two, forty-three, letting the false prophecy flutter down over the frosted marble like dirty snow. "Truth doesn't choose. It just *is*. Everything else is accounting."

From her place among the guards, wrists still bound, Sable was looking at her — not with gratitude, Emrys realized, but with something closer to grief. As if Emrys had just done something magnificent and unforgivable, and Sable knew better than anyone alive what the crown did to people who stood in its light.

The Regent gestured, and the guards moved.

Not toward Sable.

Toward Emrys.

"Since you love truth so well," the Regent said, and the frost spread from his feet across the marble in reaching fingers, "the crown will give you a room with very good light, and time enough to reconsider which book matters. Take her to the Codex chamber."

As they took her arms, Emrys looked back once — at Tamsin holding the ledger high, at Sable straining against her bonds with her broken-oath fury rising off her like heat, at Aldous on the throne, whose lips, behind the smile, were shaping words no sound could carry:

*Run, wren.*

But the Codex chamber doors were already opening, and the true book, she knew, was waiting inside — and tonight, the pages had begun to write her next days before she lived them.

* * *

They gave her a chair, a lamp, and the Codex Vellum chained to a reading stand, and locked the door behind her with a sound like a tomb exhaling.

Emrys sat for a long time without touching it. Her hands were still trembling from the court — not fear, she discovered, but the strange aftershock of having finally said everything aloud. Eleven years of careful entries, of debts recorded and never collected, of being needed by no one — burned in an afternoon, and what remained felt scoured and strangely light.

*I have no bridge home now.* The thought arrived without grief, which frightened her more than grief would have. Vell was gone. The guild was gone — or remade, or at war with itself, depending on what Tamsin's ragged band of clerks could build from the ashes Corvane had made of them. She had traded belonging for truth on the frozen floor of a court that would forget her name within the month.

Except that it wouldn't. Not the crown's part of the court. The sigil had watched her denounce its forgery with those lagging, drinking eyes, and it had not punished her — it had *accommodated* her, moved her to a better room, given her the very book she'd demanded. Which meant the crown believed the true Codex served it. Which meant the true Codex, read correctly, ended the way the crown wanted.

Emrys pulled the lamp close and opened the book to the date that had started everything.

Tonight's date. Her name. And below it, in the century-old ink she trusted more than she trusted any living person:

*The unbinding requires a willing hand. The storm-caller must come to the stone of her own unforced will, or the rite fails, and the crown completes its century.*

She read it three times. Then she understood why she was here, in comfort, with good light.

They couldn't force her. The prophecy itself forbade it. Every dungeon, every threat, every torture applied to her body would only fail the rite — so instead they would apply it to everything *around* her. To Sable in her cell. To Tamsin and her clerks. To a king begging silently behind his smile. The crown didn't need to break her will. It only needed to make the world break it for her, one hostage at a time, until walking to the stone willingly became the only mercy left to choose.

"That's the trap," she whispered to the empty room. "You've built a trap where the door out is also the door through."

The Codex lay open under the lamp, patient as winter.

And as Emrys watched, the ink on the next page began to move — slowly, like frost growing across glass — writing tomorrow.
