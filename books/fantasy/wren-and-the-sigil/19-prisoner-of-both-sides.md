## 19. Prisoner of Both Sides

They came for Sable at the hour when the palace lamps were trimmed low and the corridors belonged to the wind.

Emrys heard it first as a change in the sound of the guards' boots — a rhythm she had learned to read through stone the way sailors read weather through a hull. Measured steps all day, unhurried, bored. These were different. Quick. Purposeful. Six sets of them, moving not toward her door but past it, down the stair, along the gallery toward Sable's quarters at the end of the west wing.

She was out of bed and at the window before her mind had finished deciding to be afraid.

Snow came down in the courtyard below, but wrong — drifting sideways, then upward, catching in the torchlight like ash from a fire that burned beneath the world. The winter had been misbehaving for three days now. Rivers frozen mid-current, their fish suspended in the ice like flies in amber. Snow that fell upward over the cathedral square. The court called it a marvel. The servants crossed themselves when they thought no one was watching. Emrys knew what it was: appetite. Something vast and patient had stopped being patient.

The boots reached Sable's door. There was a pause she could feel even two floors up — a held breath in the architecture — and then voices, too low to parse, and the scrape of iron.

"No," Emrys said to the empty room, uselessly, the way people do.

* * *

They did not take her to see him. That was the first cruelty, and it was an efficient one, because it required nothing but absence. She asked the guard who brought her breakfast — a young man with chapped hands who had never once met her eyes — and he looked at the floor and said, "I'm not told, lady," which meant *I am told not to say*, which meant *yes, something has happened*, which meant everything.

By midday she had assembled the truth from fragments, because assembling truth from fragments was the one craft they could not confiscate. A chambermaid mentioned that the oathbreaker had been taken to the deep cells. A page, running messages on the gallery stairs, let slip the word *Regent*. And when Emrys pressed her palm flat against the wall of her tower room and listened with the part of her that had once heard storms coming across open water, she felt — faintly, horribly — the pulse of the sigil-crown somewhere above and east of her, beating faster than it had any day before.

It was frightened. Or excited. With the crown, she suspected, the two had long ago become indistinguishable.

And she understood, sitting on the edge of her narrow bed with her hands still cold from the wall, what the arrest actually was. Not punishment. *Leverage.* The crown had spent a century learning how to make kings compliant; it knew precisely how much pressure a person could bear before they agreed to anything. It had taken the one person Emrys — 

The sentence refused to finish itself. She made herself finish it. *The one person I would trade myself for.*

That was the trap's whole geometry, laid out plain as a ledger entry. Confess willingly. Agree to the rite as the crown wished it performed — or whatever version the Regent intended, and Emrys no longer trusted that any version served Aldous — and Sable walked free. Refuse, and Sable rotted in the deep cells until the winter finished eating the city, guards and prisoners alike.

She had faced this arithmetic before, in Corvane's office, in the shape of a forged Codex naming Sable as the sacrifice. She had chosen truth then, loudly, publicly, and burned every bridge home to do it. She had assumed — naively, she saw now — that choosing truth once would be enough. But the crown was not a man to be shamed before a court. The crown was patient as ice and it simply moved the board.

"Fine," she said aloud, to the walls, to the book downstairs, to whoever was listening. "You have my attention."

* * *

They came for her at dusk.

Not soldiers this time. The Regent himself sent a chamberlain — a soft-spoken woman with ink-stained cuffs and the careful blankness of someone who had learned that opinions were dangerous currency — to inform Emrys that, in light of recent events, her accommodations had been reassessed.

"You'll be more comfortable," the chamberlain said, "in the Codex chamber."

"I don't want to be comfortable," Emrys said. "I want to know where Ser Sable is."

"The deep cells are not comfortable either, lady." The chamberlain's eyes flicked up, held hers for half a heartbeat, dropped again. In that half-heartbeat Emrys read something she would turn over for hours afterward: not malice. *Pity.* Perhaps even apology. "The King's household acts for the King's good. You will be treated gently. Please bring your things."

"My things," Emrys repeated. "You mean the Codex."

"The chamber is prepared for study," the chamberlain said, which was its own kind of confession.

Two guards escorted her down through the palace's older bones — past the modern galleries with their gilded mirrors, into corridors where the stone went dark and the windows narrowed to arrow-slits and the air smelled of candle tallow and cold iron. Emrys counted turns and doors out of habit, mapping as she went, though she suspected the map was a courtesy the crown permitted because it changed nothing. Where was there to run? Outside, the snow fell upward. The roads were rivers of standing ice. And somewhere below her feet, in a cell without light, Sable —

She made herself stop. Grief spent in advance was grief wasted; she'd learned that in Vell, tallying debts that could never be collected. Save it for when it could buy something.

The Codex chamber occupied the top of the old keep, a round room beneath a vaulted ceiling painted with constellations so old the stars no longer matched the sky. Bookcases lined the curved walls. A great table stood at the center, and upon it, chained at four corners by clasps of dull silver, lay the Codex Vellum.

It looked smaller than she remembered. Books often did, once you'd seen what they could do.

"Supper at eight bells," said the chamberlain from the doorway. "Anything you require will be brought." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Within reason."

Then the door closed, and Emrys heard the lock, and then — a longer, deeper sound, like a bar of wood the thickness of her arm sliding home — and then silence.

She stood a long while in the middle of the round room, turning slowly, taking inventory the way she took inventory of everything: shelves, ladder, brazier, water jug, privy behind a screen, one window too narrow for a cat. And the book.

"Well," she said to the Codex Vellum. "Here we are again."

The book did not answer. Of course it didn't. Books only answered when they wanted something.

She sat down at the table, as far from the chained thing as the room allowed, and pulled her knees up, and allowed herself — briefly, in the privacy of her own skull — to be afraid. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, stood, and went to work. Fear was fuel; Corvane had taught her that much that was true. A clerk afraid of nothing was a clerk who missed things.

* * *

The first page turned on its own at midnight.

Emrys had been asleep in the chair, head on her folded arms, when the sound woke her — a dry whisper, like a finger drawn across linen. She came up sharp in the dark. The brazier had burned to embers. Moonlight fell through the arrow-slit in a single thin blade, and it fell, she realized with a crawling scalp, directly across the open Codex.

She had left it closed. She was certain she had left it closed.

She approached the way she'd approach a sleeping animal. The book lay open not to the prophecy of her summoning, nor to the unbinding rite she had half-deciphered weeks ago, but to a fresh page — fresh ink, the letters still carrying a faint wet gleam in the moonlight.

Her own handwriting looked back at her.

Not her handwriting, she corrected herself. Her handwriting was precise, small, slanted forward, the script of a girl trained by ledgers. This was close. This was *almost* close. As if someone had studied her hand for years and practiced it in secret, getting everything right except the small human errors — the way her *e*'s went crooked when she wrote fast, the blot where her pen always hesitated at the start of a new paragraph.

The page read:

*Nineteenth night. She wakes in the chair. She reads what is written and does not believe it. By morning she believes it.*

Emrys sat down slowly, never taking her eyes off the page. "Clever," she whispered. "But you've made a mistake. That already happened. I'm reading it now."

As if it had been waiting for exactly that objection, the ink shifted.

Below the first lines, new letters surfaced — rising through the vellum like drowned things coming up through dark water:

*Twentieth day. The Regent comes at the third bell. He offers the exchange: the oathbreaker for the willing sacrifice. She refuses.*

"She does," Emrys said. Her voice sounded strange in the round room. Smaller than usual. "Watch her refuse."

*Twenty-first day. The oathbreaker is moved to the upper cells. She is given bread and water and a window. The Regent is pleased. The Regent is always pleased when the pieces move as written.*

Something cold slid down Emrys's spine and pooled at the base of it. Not because of the threat — she had already priced in threats — but because of the *tone*. This was not prophecy. Prophecy was thunder, riddle, the grand grammar of fate. This was a *log*. A ledger. Dispassionate, dated, itemized. The crown wasn't predicting her future.

The crown was *keeping books on her*.

And there was only one person alive whose accounting style the crown could possibly have absorbed, whose neat, merciless, itemized hand the Codex might have studied for years in secret.

"Corvane," she breathed. "You sold them your ledgers too."

Of course he had. Of course. Somewhere in a decade of licensed trade, the Guildmaster of Impossible Things had sold the crown sample pages, teaching methods, the whole clerical discipline of the guild — because the crown paid in privileges and Corvane had never met a price he wouldn't take. And the sigil, hungry for order, for the tidy grammar of debt and payment, had learned to think in ledger-lines. Had learned to write her days the way she wrote everyone else's: dated, itemized, *collectible*.

She turned the page with a fingertip, hating herself for doing it.

Blank. Waiting.

"No," she told it. "You don't get tomorrow. Tomorrow you get nothing from me."

But even as she said it, she was thinking — the clerk in her unable to stop, the flaw and gift of her whole life — *if it writes true, then what it writes about Sable is also true, and if it writes Sable moved to the upper cells, then Sable is alive, Sable is alive tonight, and knowing that is worth more than pride—*

The ink began to rise.

She watched it come, letter by letter, surfacing through the vellum like bones through snow, and she did not touch the book, and she did not look away.

*Twenty-second day. The oathbreaker refuses the Regent's questions. The Regent grows tired of the oathbreaker.*

And beneath that line, after a pause long enough for Emrys to stop breathing:

*Unless she comes to the deep cells before the twenty-third bell.*

* * *

She did not sleep. She tried the door (locked), the window (arrow-slit, thirty feet of sheer stone to a courtyard of upward-falling snow), the shelves (dusty histories, dead languages, nothing). She tried, out of desperation more than hope, calling the storm — reached for that place inside her where the weather lived, the pressure and the promise of it — and found it there, coiled and sullen as ever, but thin. Thinner than it should have been. As if something had been drinking from her for weeks, slowly, the way the crown drank from kings.

At some point near dawn she found herself back at the table, staring at the page with the conditional clause. *Unless she comes.* The book was bargaining now. Openly. It had dropped the pretense of neutral record-keeping and revealed itself as what it had always been: a creditor. Every line it wrote was an invoice. Every date, a due date.

"You know what the funny thing is," she said to it, hoarse, gray-lit, mad with sleeplessness and grief. "I've read ten thousand ledgers. I know every trick a ledger can play. Round the numbers so the error hides in the remainder. Date the entry late so the debt looks younger. Bury the real terms under the polite ones." She leaned close, close enough to see the grain of the vellum, the faint shimmer of the ink not yet fully dried. "So here's what I notice about you. You write what happens *unless*. You never write what happens *if*. Because if I come to the deep cells, you don't know what I'll do. If I tear you apart page by page, you don't know. Your whole book is full of futures, and not one of them survives contact with someone who's actually free to choose."

The Codex Vellum lay silent in the gray morning light.

But as she straightened up, wincing at the stiffness in her neck, the ink stirred one final time. Just one line. Small. Almost gentle.

*She will come. They always come. That is why we choose the ones who love.*

Emrys read it twice.

Then, very calmly, she picked up the brass water jug from the sideboard, carried it to the table, and poured it slowly over the open page — watching the ink bloom and blur, watching the conditional clause dissolve into gray wash, watching a hundred years of certainty run off the edge of the vellum and pool, useless, on the wood.

The book would rewrite it. She knew that. The book would have it back by noon, pristine, smug.

But her hands were steady now, and her mind was cold and clear and working, and somewhere beneath her feet, in a cell without light, Sable was refusing to give her name to a hollow man — which meant Sable was buying time with pain, which meant the least Emrys owed her was to spend that time well.

"All right," she said softly, wringing out her sleeve, looking around the round room — at the shelves, the ladder, the arrow-slit, the locked door, the whole elegant cage. "Let's audit."

Because here was what the crown had forgotten, in its century of kings and its borrowed grammar of debt: it had put her in a room full of books. And Emrys Zhou had been reading other people's secrets since she was nine years old, and there was not a lock, a cipher, or a lie in this palace that could survive her with time, lamplight, and spite.

Somewhere in these shelves was the history of the binding. Somewhere was the name of the first king. Somewhere — she could feel it, the way she'd once felt storms over water — was the flaw in the sigil's accounting, the remainder where the error hid.

Eight bells came and went. Supper arrived and went untouched.

At the third bell of the following night, exactly as a blurred and rewritten page had promised, footsteps climbed the tower stair — slow, heavy, unhurried, and wrong, wrong, wrong — and a voice she had last heard dripping courtesy across a throne room spoke pleasantly through the locked door.

"Lady Zhou. The Regent wonders if you have had time to reconsider."

Emrys closed her hand around the one loose page she had cut from the Codex with a sharpened spoon-haft and hidden in her bodice — the page bearing the unbinding rite, the half-read words, the terrible price — and smiled at the door with all the warmth of a blade.

"Come in," she said, "and find out."
