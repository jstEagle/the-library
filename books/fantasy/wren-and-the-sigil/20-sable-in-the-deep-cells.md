## 20. Sable in the Deep Cells

The deep cells of Carreth were older than the palace above them.

Sable had known that, once, in the way every knight of the realm knew it — a fact recited in training yards, a story told to frighten squires. The founders had cut their dungeons into the bones of the hill before the first wall went up, and the walls had been built around the cells rather than over them, the way a body grows around an old wound. But knowing a thing from a training yard and knowing it from inside were different countries, and Sable had crossed into the second one four days ago.

She measured time by the lamp outside her door. It burned on a schedule she could not see but could hear — a faint mechanical ticking somewhere below the floor, some ancient counterweight system that raised fresh oil to the corridor lamps at fixed intervals. Six ticks between refills. She had counted eleven hundred of them, give or take, which meant four days, give or take, which meant the solstice was very close now.

They had taken her boots. That had been the first cruelty, and in its way the most honest one, because it told her exactly what kind of interrogation this would be. Not the quick violence of soldiers who wanted information fast. The patient subtraction of comfort. Cold floor. Thin blanket. Food that was food only by technical definition — barley porridge, twice a day, never warm enough to steam. They wanted her slow. They wanted her grateful for whatever they offered next.

She sat against the back wall with her knees drawn up and worked through the exercises her swordmaster had taught her forty years ago, when she'd been a girl with scraped knuckles and a name she hadn't disgraced yet. Breathe from the floor of yourself. Count what can be counted. Own the territory behind your eyes, even if they own everything else.

The lock turned.

She had learned the sound of it in four days — the particular double click of the deep-cell locks, heavier than the ones above, iron into iron. Her head came up before she had decided to lift it.

The Regent entered the way he always did, without ceremony, carrying his own chair. That detail still unsettled her more than any of his words. A man of his station should have had servants bear the chair, should have swept in with torchbearers and clerks. Instead he came alone down the long stairs each time, chair balanced under one arm as easily as a market woman carries a basket, and set it down precisely in the center of the cell, facing her, and sat, and folded his hands, and waited for her to speak first.

He never spoke first. It was the oldest trick in the book and it worked anyway, because silence is a debt and prisoners are the ones who pay it.

"You've brought the lamp closer tonight," Sable said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. Four days of disuse will do that. "I'm flattered."

The Regent's face did not change. It never changed, not really — it *moved*, the way a mask moves when the man beneath it shifts his weight, but the expression itself stayed fixed in a gentle, reasonable middle distance, the face of a man listening patiently to a petition he has already granted. His skin was the color of old candle wax, smooth in the wrong way, without pores or weather. His eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they reflected light oddly, like the surface of a frozen pond.

"I have been reviewing your file," he said. His voice was soft and dry, like pages turning. "It is a thick file, Dame Sable. Thirty years of service. Commendations at Redford Ford. The Vey Medal. You carried the old queen's standard at her funeral, did you know that? There is a drawing of it in your file. An artist was commissioned."

"Your archive work is impeccable," Sable said. "Shall I applaud?"

"The queen you served." He tilted his head slightly, considering her. "You loved her."

It was not a question, and Sable did not answer it, and the silence stretched out between them, and this time she let him hold the debt. She watched him carry it. He was good at carrying things — she'd give him that. Most men who came to break her would have filled the quiet with threats by now. The Regent simply sat in it, hands folded, waxen face serene, as though silence were a room he had furnished himself and felt comfortable in.

At last he said, "You broke your oath to protect her. That is the official record — your own confession, given freely at your trial. You struck your commanding officer. You revealed the location of the queen's private chapel to foreign agents. You were exiled from the order and stripped of rank." He paused. "But confessions are documents, Dame Sable, and documents are written by the frightened. I am curious about the unwritten version."

"Why?" It came out of her before she could weigh it — genuine curiosity, sharp as a splinter. "What does my treason matter to you? You have a kingdom. You have a king who smiles wrong and a court that claps for him. You have a prophecy and a prisoner upstairs and a solstice coming. What possible use is the unwritten version of a dead woman's disgrace?"

The Regent looked at her for a long moment. And then — this was the thing Sable would turn over in her mind for days afterward, in the dark, counting lamp-ticks — he smiled. Not the fixed courtly smile. Something smaller, wearier, rising from underneath the mask like a fish rising under ice, visible for a moment and gone.

"Use," he said softly. "Yes. Everything must have use. That is the doctrine of the crown, isn't it. The sigil teaches it well." He unfolded his hands and placed them flat on his knees. "Very well. I will be plain with you, since plainness seems to be the coin you trust. We do not require your confession, Dame Sable. We require your testimony. The prophecy names the storm-caller as the unbinding. The unbinding requires consent — a willing sacrifice, freely given, witnessed and recorded. The Codex is quite specific. Willingness cannot be compelled. It can only be *testified*."

Ice moved through Sable's chest, slow and total, colder than the cell had managed in four days.

"You want me to swear," she said, "that Emrys agreed to die."

"We want you to swear that she came here willingly. That she spoke of the sacrifice without fear. That she accepted her part in the design." The Regent's voice remained gentle, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining arithmetic to a slow student. "A small thing. A sentence or two, sworn before a clerk. In exchange — warmth, food, your boots, and in time, perhaps, your rank restored. A disgraced knight made whole again. The realm is forgiving, Dame Sable, when it is given a reason to be."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you remain here," the Regent said, "in the cold, until the cold finishes its work. And the testimony is taken from someone less scrupulous. There is never a shortage of witnesses willing to remember what we need remembered. Your refusal changes nothing except the temperature of your last weeks." He rose, unhurried, and took up his chair. At the door he stopped, half-turned, and added — almost kindly, and that was the worst of it —

"For what it is worth, I believe you would have died for her too. The old queen. It is written all through your file, in ink and in what the ink leaves out. Loyalty is a beautiful thing, Dame Sable. It is why we ask so little of yours. One sentence. Think on it."

The lock closed behind him. The lamp ticks resumed.

* * *

She thought on it.

That was the humiliation of it — she couldn't stop thinking on it. Not the offer itself; the offer was filth and she knew filth when it was handed to her across a cell. But the shape of it. *Willingness cannot be compelled. It can only be testified.*

Because here was the truth Sable had been carrying since the tower room, since the night of borrowed honesty when Emrys had finally said aloud what she feared most — *that she was nothing without the gift* — and Sable had answered with her own truth, the old queen's mouth going cold under hers, the oath breaking in her chest like a bone:

Here was the truth: Emrys *would* say yes. Eventually. If the winter kept eating the city, if the snow fell upward and the rivers froze mid-current, if the king surfaced one more hour and begged with a century of screaming kings behind his eyes — Emrys would stand on that dais and give herself to the rite and call it choice, and she would mean it, and that was exactly what the prophecy wanted. The Codex didn't need to compel her. It only needed the world to press hard enough that surrender looked like agency.

And the Regent knew it. The whole apparatus knew it. Her testimony wasn't needed to make Emrys willing. It was needed to make the willingness *legible* — to write the sacrifice into the record as consent, clean and documented, a signature in a ledger. The guild had taught Emrys that everything was accounting. Apparently the crown had learned the same lesson.

*They don't want to break me,* Sable thought, lying on the cold stone with the thin blanket pulled to her chin, staring at the ceiling where the mortar had crumbled into a map of nowhere. *They want to file me.*

So she held the line the only way she could. When they brought the morning porridge, she ate it — refusing food was a vanity, a way of performing strength while actually handing them your weakness faster; she'd seen proud men starve themselves into pliancy in three days flat. When they brought the clerk on the second evening, a nervous young man with ink-stained fingers and a document already drafted, she listened to the whole preamble with her hands folded, and then said, "Recite it to me again, slowly, and I'll tell you which word is false." The clerk faltered. She said, "It's the word 'willing.' Bring me a document that tells the truth and I'll sign anything on it." The clerk fled. She heard him stumble on the stairs.

On the third night they changed tactics, and the new tactic had a name, and the name was Master Quillon.

He was a small neat man with kind eyes and soft-soled shoes, and he did not bring instruments. He brought conversation. He asked after her knees — he'd read in the file about Redford Ford, the freezing river, the wound that had ended her riding career years before her disgrace ended everything else. He sympathized about the cold; cold found old wounds, didn't it, that was well known. He mentioned, in passing, that Dame Sable's family seat in the Marchlands still stood, that a cousin held it now, that cousins wrote letters. He wondered aloud whether she had anyone at all who would grieve her properly if the winter took her here in the dark.

Sable let him talk. She catalogued him the way she'd catalogue an opponent in a melee: reach, rhythm, favored openings. Quillon favored the opening of pity. Every third question curved toward loneliness. *No one is coming for you, Dame Sable. No one knows where you are. The girl upstairs cannot come even if she wished — she is a guest of prophecy, closely attended. You are entirely alone down here.*

"No," Sable said, on the fourth repetition of the theme. "Not alone. I have the lamp ticks. Eleven hundred a day. I have the crack in the ceiling shaped like the coast near Vell. I have thirty years of remembering a woman whose mouth went cold under mine, and none of those things can be confiscated." She leaned forward. "You keep telling me no one is coming. You keep saying it the way a man keeps checking a locked door. Which makes me think you're not certain the door is locked."

Quillon's kind eyes flickered — just once, just briefly — toward the far wall of the cell. Toward the place where, if the old builders' stories were true, there had once been a secondary passage, sealed these two hundred years, bricked and forgotten.

Then he smiled his kind smile and said, "Delirium sets in quickly in the cold," and gathered his papers, and left.

Sable lay awake a long time after that, watching the crack in the ceiling, turning the flicker over in her mind like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. Delirium. Perhaps. Four days of cold and barley could make a woman read meaning into a bureaucrat's eye movement. But she had spent thirty years reading men's bodies in rooms where reading them wrong meant death, and bodies did not lie the way faces did, and Quillon's body had flinched toward that wall like a dog flinches at a sound it hopes its master didn't hear.

Something was behind the wall. Or something might be.

Or — and this possibility she handled carefully, the way you handle a blade you've been told is dull — the Regent wanted her to hope. Wanted her to wait for rescue instead of spending her strength on escape. Hope as a chain, softer than iron and longer.

She filed both possibilities in the territory behind her eyes and went back to counting.

* * *

On the fifth night, the Regent came again, and this time he did not bring the chair.

He stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind him, and for the first time since she'd met him, he seemed — diminished. Not weak. *Thinner.* As though the mask fit less well than it had five nights ago, as though the man-shaped thing wearing the Regent had been stretched over too many duties and was beginning, at the seams, to show what was underneath. The solstice was close. Sable could feel it in her ruined knee, in the way the cold pressed harder against the stones, in the strange pressure in her ears like the whole hill was holding its breath.

"You have not slept," he observed.

"I've slept fine."

"That is a lie, and we both know it, and it does not matter." He stepped inside but did not approach. His colorless eyes moved over her the way an appraiser's eyes move over goods. "I am going to tell you something true, Dame Sable, because true things are the only currency I have left to spend, and I find I wish to spend it on you. Is that not strange? I have worn this office for sixty years. I have forgotten how to want things. And yet I find I want you to understand what is coming, so that you do not waste your loyalty on a misunderstanding."

"Then speak plainly," Sable said, "for once."

"The girl believes she can tear the crown away and survive the tearing," the Regent said. "The Codex permits her to believe it. Belief is the mechanism. Willingness requires hope; hope requires error." He folded his hands. "There is a version of the coming night in which she stands on the dais, and tears free the sigil, and lives — diminished, powerless, ordinary. I have seen that version. I saw it a hundred years ago, when I stood on that dais myself, young, and terrified, and *willing* — oh, so willing, Dame Sable, willing as a bride. And the crown took my storm-gift and my name and my face and my sixty years, and what remains wears this office and carries chairs and forgets how to want." His voice did not waver. It simply went quiet, the way a fire goes quiet when it runs out of air. "I am the proof that the merciful version of the prophecy exists. I am also the proof of what mercy costs. Whatever she chooses — whatever you testify or refuse to testify — she will join me. That is the whole of the arithmetic. There is no ledger entry where everyone walks away."

Sable got to her feet. Her legs shook — four days of cold and hunger, and she let them shake, because hiding it cost more than showing it. She crossed to within arm's reach of the Regent, close enough to see, in the lamplight from the corridor, the almost-invisible seam of scarring around his hairline, circling his skull like a coronet.

"Sixty years ago," she said quietly, "did anyone stand at the door for you? Did anyone refuse to give the testimony? Did anyone choose you?"

The mask did not move. But behind the colorless eyes, something looked out — something very old, and very tired, and drowning politely, the way she imagined the hundred kings screamed politely inside the crown — and for one heartbeat the deep cell was silent enough that Sable could hear the hill itself breathing.

"No one came," the Regent said.

"Then she'll be the first," Sable said. "And I'll be standing at the door."

He regarded her for a long moment. Then he inclined his head — a small, precise bow, almost respectful, the bow of one soldier acknowledging another across an impossible line — and withdrew, and the lock closed, and the lamp ticks resumed their counting.

Sable stood in the dark with her heart slamming and her breath smoking, and understood that she had just spent her last coin. They would not offer again. What came after refusal was not negotiation; it was procedure. By tomorrow, perhaps sooner, they would stop needing her testimony at all, because the solstice would arrive and the prophecy would arrive with it and the machinery would run on rails, and her remaining use — her final entry in their ledger — would be as a hostage. Leverage against the girl upstairs. A reason for Emrys to walk willingly to the dais with her heart already surrendered.

*Let them,* Sable thought, pressing her forehead against the cold stone. *Let them write me down as leverage. Let them write me down as nothing. I have been a disgrace and an exile and a liar by omission for thirty years, and the only clean page I ever wrote was the one where I broke my oath for love, and I will break whatever else they hand me the same way.*

She slid down the wall to the floor and began, again, to count the lamp ticks.

Four hundred and twelve ticks into the night — she was nearly sure of the number, though the cold made the numbers slippery — she heard a sound.

Not the lock. Not the stairs. Not Quillon's soft shoes or the Regent's measured tread or the oil-cart's rattle in the corridor above.

A sound like a door opening where no door had ever been. A breath of air that smelled impossibly of green things — of spring, of wet earth, of a season that had no business existing within a hundred miles of Carreth in the long winter — drifting thin and sweet through the seams of the far wall. Through the wall Quillon's eyes had flickered toward.

Sable came off the floor with her heart in her throat and her hand curled around the memory of a sword hilt that wasn't there, and stared at the crumbling mortar, and listened to the impossible smell of growing things seeping through two hundred years of brick.

Someone was coming through the seasons themselves.

Someone was coming for her.
