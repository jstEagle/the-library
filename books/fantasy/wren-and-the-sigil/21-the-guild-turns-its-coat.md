## 21. The Guild Turns Its Coat

Tamsin Ferrow had learned bookkeeping from a woman who believed that numbers were a form of prayer. *Every line honest, every entry balanced,* Emrys Zhou used to say, tapping the ledger with one ink-stained finger, *because the moment you lie to the page, you'll lie to yourself, and then you're no use to anyone.* Tamsin had been sixteen and furious about it, and she had copied out columns of figures until her wrist ached and the lesson sank in past all her resistance, the way water sinks into drought-cracked ground.

She was twenty-three now, and she had been lying to the page for eleven days.

It was not even difficult. That was the worst of it. Corvane's true ledgers were kept in a warded case behind his office, but his *false* ledgers — the ones he showed the clerks, the ones he intended to survive any audit — were kept in the common counting room, and Tamsin had been adjusting them by candlelight each night with a steadiness that would have made her old teacher proud and sick at once. A shipment of bottled grief rerouted through Carreth: she wrote it as spoiled stock. Three payments from a royal agent for "passage services": she wrote them as tariff fees. And beneath it all, in her own private book, bound in grey calfskin and hidden inside a hollowed-out copy of *A Treatise on Riverine Customs* that no one had ever wanted to read, she recorded what was actually happening.

What was actually happening was this: the Guild of Impossible Things had become a shop that sold people.

* * *

The counting room smelled of tallow and salt, as it always had. Through its high windows Tamsin could see the harbor of Vell, grey under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and beyond it the sea road where ships came in carrying crates that whispered. She had loved this room once. She had loved the whole warehouse — the impossible doors, the shelves of bottled weather, the smell of ozone and old paper, the sense of being part of something that mattered precisely because it should not have existed.

That love had died quietly, over several years, the way most things die — not with a scream but with a series of small acceptances. The first time she'd seen a crate marked with a name instead of a cargo description, she'd asked Corvane about it and been told it was a "legacy contract." The second time, she hadn't asked. The third time, she'd signed the manifest herself.

But the night Emrys had been taken — the night the royal riders came up the salt road with their black banners and their warrant smelling of prophecy — something in Tamsin had cracked along an old fault line, and everything since had been sliding.

"You're doing it again."

Brannick's voice came from the doorway. He was the guild's oldest clerk, sixty if he was a day, with hands like knotted rope and a face that suggested he had personally witnessed every bad decision of the last half-century. He shut the door behind him and stood with his back against it, which in Brannick's language meant *this conversation does not end until I am satisfied.*

"Doing what?" Tamsin said.

"Balancing." He crossed the room and dropped into the chair across from her, the way he'd done ten thousand times when she was an apprentice and he was checking her work. "Your columns balance. They've balanced every night for eleven days. Perfectly." He said the word like an accusation. "Emrys Zhou's columns never balanced perfectly. She left little errors on purpose — a copper here, a day there — so she could tell if anyone else had been in the books. Said perfection was a signature too."

Tamsin looked down at her neat figures. Her throat was tight. "I didn't know that."

"No," Brannick said. "You didn't. Because she got exiled before she finished teaching you, and because Corvane made sure none of us talked about her after." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped low, rough as gravel underfoot. "Which is why we need to talk about what you've been writing in the river book, girl, because I'm old and observant and I've watched you hide it three times this week."

The candle between them guttered. Outside, a gull cried once and gave up, as if even the birds had decided winter wasn't worth the effort.

"The river book," Tamsin said carefully, "is a treatise on—"

"Tamsin." Brannick said her name the way Emrys used to — flat, patient, immovable. "I helped carry the Codex Vellum into the archive six years ago. I saw Corvane's face when he realized what it was. I watched him sell passage rights to royal agents while telling us the estate seizure was routine. And I watched him hand that woman to the riders like a bolt of cloth he'd finally found a buyer for." His knotted hands folded on the desk between them. "So either you're falsifying the master's records out of spite, which I don't believe, or you're keeping a true account of his crimes, which I do. And if it's the second thing—" he turned his hands palm-up, an old merchant's gesture, *show me your goods* — "then I'd take it kindly if you let an old man read."

* * *

They did not decide anything that night. That was the thing about conspiracies, Tamsin discovered: they didn't begin with oaths or midnight meetings. They began with two people admitting they'd noticed the same wrongness, and then a third, and then a fourth, until one evening you looked around the counting room and realized that everyone who touched the ledgers was already guilty of the same quiet crime — honesty — and that the only person in the building still deceived was the man whose name was on the door.

There were nine of them in the end. Nine clerks out of forty-one. Tamsin had expected more, and grieved the number, and then understood it. Fear was not cowardice; fear was arithmetic. Most of the clerks had families, debts, histories that Corvane held in his files like knives. Nine was not a betrayal of the rest. Nine was what courage looked like when it had to be paid for in secret.

They met in the archive stacks, among the seized libraries, where the dust was thick enough to muffle sound and the wards were old and half-asleep. Brannick brought the first evidence: a shipping manifest from four years back, a crate bound for Carreth, contents listed as "one (1) vessel, prophetic, certified compatible." Not a storm. Not a memory. A *person*. Tamsin had read the name on the manifest three times before her eyes would accept it — a girl called Merrin Vale, nineteen years old, a hedge-seer from the fen country, sold to the crown for the price of a warehouse lease.

"She went before Emrys," said Odile, the youngest of the nine, who kept the intake registers. "There were others too. I've started pulling them. Every few years, someone 'certified compatible,' someone 'passaged.' Corvane calls it fulfillment contracts." Her voice cracked on the word *fulfillment*, and she pressed her lips together until it steadied. "He's been feeding them chosen ones like grain into a mill."

"To what end?" asked Hesk, who handled the coin. "The crown pays well, but—"

"Not the crown," Tamsin said. She had spent two nights with the false ledgers and one terrible hour extrapolating backward, and she knew the shape of it now, the way you know the shape of a thing swimming toward you in dark water. "The sigil. Or whatever wears the sigil. Corvane isn't selling to men. Men pay in coin and want returns. This buyer pays in *protection* — licenses, exemptions, the right to operate impossible doors across five kingdoms without interference. It wants prophecies fulfilled. It wants its chosen ones delivered." She closed the grey calfskin book. "And it has just received the only one that ever mattered."

The silence in the archive was total. Dust drifted down through the lamplight like slow snow.

"So we get her back," Odile said. Simply. As if it were a delivery route.

"It's not that simple," Hesk began—

"It is exactly that simple," Brannick said. "We are the Guild of Impossible Things. Fetching a woman out of a frozen capital guarded by a god-crown is the single most possible thing this organization has ever attempted."

* * *

The problem, of course, was keys. The warehouse's impossible doors answered to three sets: Corvane's master ring, the wardens' working rings, and — buried in a drawer in the old registry, wrapped in oilcloth and forgotten for eleven years — the apprentice set. Tamsin had taken hers the night of the arrest, before the shock had even faded, on some instinct older than thought. An iron key stamped with a wren. Emrys had worn it on a cord around her neck for the whole of Tamsin's apprenticeship; when she'd handed it over on Tamsin's certification day, she'd said, *Doors remember who opened them first. Be careful which ones you open.*

Tamsin had carried the wren key for seven years without using it once. Now she sat in the registry at midnight with it laid on the table before her, and beside it the real ledger — Corvane's true ledger, which Brannick had lifted from the warded case using a forgery of the master's own seal that Hesk had spent four nights perfecting — and she understood that there was no version of tomorrow in which she was still the person she had been yesterday.

The real ledger was worse than she'd imagined. She had thought she would find accounts, figures, transactions. What she found was a *history*. Corvane wrote to himself the way other men prayed. Entry after entry, year after year: the Codex acquired; the Codex deciphered; the first contact with the Regent's agents; the pricing structure for miracles. And threaded through it all, in a hand that grew steadily more satisfied, the same refrain:

*The book wants the storm-daughter. All others are rehearsal.*

Rehearsal. Tamsin read the word and thought of Merrin Vale, nineteen, certified compatible, passaged north and never entered in any return column. Rehearsal. She thought of the hedge-witch from the Salt Road years, the dreamer from Coriander Street, the names she was still finding, each one a person reduced to a trial run for the real performance. She thought of Emrys at eighteen, teaching her to keep columns honest, tapping the page with an ink-stained finger, saying *the moment you lie to the page* —

Tamsin pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the burning stopped.

Then she did what Emrys would have done. She opened the grey calfskin book and began to copy, line for line, date for date, sum for sum, a true and complete account of the Guild of Impossible Things' traffic in human beings. She worked until the candle drowned in its own wax, and when the last figure was set down she blew out the light and sat in the dark and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, the full weight of what her family had done.

The minute ended. She struck a new match.

* * *

"They'll hang us," Hesk said, two nights later, in the archive. "If it fails. If we fail, he'll have us all in the deep cells before the tide turns, and the crown will call us traitors to the prophecy, and—" He stopped himself, breathed. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying I want it said aloud, once, so we all know what we're buying."

"We know," Brannick said.

"I know," Tamsin said. "I've known since the night they took her. Here's what I keep coming back to." She laid the forged seal on the table between the lamps, next to the wren key. "Corvane built this guild on one rule: nothing leaves the books. Every debt gets collected, every entry gets settled. He taught us that balances matter more than mercy." She looked around the circle of faces — old Brannick, frightened Odile, skeptical Hesk, the five others who had come tonight despite everything. "Fine. Then let's collect. There is a debt on these books eleven years old. A woman taken off the salt road by men our master licensed. We are the guild. We settle our accounts."

Odile laughed — a wet, cracked sound, but a laugh. Hesk shook his head slowly and then, deliberately, placed his own key ring on the table. One by one, the others followed, until the center of the table glittered with iron like a handful of fallen stars.

* * *

Getting the ledger and the keys to Carreth was the kind of problem Tamsin would once have brought to Emrys, and the absence of her was a physical ache, a missing tooth the tongue keeps finding. But the guild's own magic provided the answer, the way it always did for those who knew how to ask. The warehouse doors opened onto wrong seasons, wrong places, wrong distances — and one of them, Door Nineteen in the long cellar, opened onto a wine merchant's storeroom in the lower city of Carreth, a route Corvane had established years ago for smuggling contraband fate to the capital's fashionable despairers.

"He'll have changed the wards," Brannick warned.

"Then we knock politely," Tamsin said, "and if knocking fails, we go through Door Twenty-Two, which opens onto a snowdrift outside the palace walls, and we walk in like honest smugglers."

They chose Door Nineteen. The wards had indeed been changed — Corvane was many things but never careless — but wards were written by clerks, and clerks left signatures, and Hesk read the new binding like a letter from an old rival. It took him a night and cost him a nosebleed, but at dawn the cellar door swung open onto cold air and the smell of wine and stone, and the capital of Carreth lay on the other side, white-roofed under a sky that had not properly brightened in weeks.

They went through in twos, disguised as the wine merchants they appeared to be. Tamsin went last, carrying the ledger against her chest inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, bound with cord, heavier than any object she had ever carried. In the storeroom above, a Carrethian factor accepted their fictitious shipment with the bored courtesy of his profession, and asked no questions, because the guild had greased that particular hinge for a decade.

From the storeroom, the palace was visible across the frozen canal — a black mass of towers crouched under the unnatural winter, its windows lit with a light that did not flicker. Somewhere in that mass, in a tower room with good light, Emrys Zhou was a prisoner of a prophecy. Somewhere below her, in cells Tamsin refused to imagine, an oathbreaker named Sable was answering questions. Somewhere in between, a king was being drunk like wine.

Tamsin pulled her coat tighter and stepped out into the falling snow.

* * *

Finding a way to deliver a forbidden ledger to a prisoner of the crown took six days, three bribes, one forged work order, and the accidental friendship of a laundress named Petra who had strong opinions about the tower guard rotation and weak scruples about accepting good Vellish brandy. On the seventh day, Tamsin stood in the servants' stair of the west tower with a basket of "mending" — linens, in truth, but with the grey calfskin book sewn into the lining of the largest pillowcase — and waited for the guard to make his round, and discovered that her hands were shaking.

Not from fear, she realized. From the strangeness of it. Eleven years ago she had been an apprentice standing in a counting room in Vell, resenting a teacher's obsession with honest columns. Now she was a smuggler in a frozen palace, risking her neck to carry those same honest columns to the woman who had taught her, because it had turned out that the obsession was the whole point — that honesty was not bookkeeping but a kind of spine, and that a guild without it was just a cage with better shelves.

The guard's boots receded. The door at the top of the stair, the one Petra had described, was unlocked for the laundry deliveries, and beyond it the corridor ran pale and quiet to the tower room where the prophecy kept its guest.

Tamsin climbed.

When she knocked — three short, two long, the old guild signal for *private inventory, admit the counter* — there was a long silence, and then footsteps, quick and wary, and the door opened a hand's width, and Emrys Zhou looked out at her.

Eleven days of imprisonment had carved her thinner. There was frost in the room behind her despite the fire — frost climbing the inside of the window glass, ferns of ice growing where no breath should reach — and her eyes had the look of someone who had been reading things that read her back. For a heartbeat she simply stared, and Tamsin watched recognition arrive like dawn crossing a field: confusion, disbelief, and then something that broke open in her face, sudden and unguarded, before she mastered it.

"Tamsin." Her voice was rough with disuse. "You can't be— how did you—"

"You left your columns unbalanced," Tamsin said. Her voice nearly failed her, so she made it crisp, ledger-voice, apprentice-voice, and pressed the bundle into Emrys's arms. "Someone had to come correct the entry." She swallowed hard. "It's the real ledger. Everything — the Codex, the sales, the ones they sent before you. Merrin Vale. There were others. Corvane knew for years. He sold you, Emrys. He's been selling chosen ones to that crown since before I could write a proper invoice." She fumbled at her collar and drew out the wren key on its cord, holding it out. "And the warehouse keys. The clerks sent them. Nine of us. Brannick, Odile, Hesk — the ones who stayed true to the actual guild, not to him."

Emrys looked down at the ledger in her arms as though it might burn her. When she raised her eyes again they were wet and furious and more alive than Tamsin had seen them since the night of the arrest.

"The guild," Emrys said. "You mean the guild *sent* you. Corvane's guild."

"The guild that matters," Tamsin said. "The one you built before he poisoned it. Emrys — Wren — we read the entries. We know what he did. And we voted." She managed, barely, a smile. "The Guild of Impossible Things has turned its coat. Consider this our first honest transaction: one ledger, delivered. One debt, acknowledged." She stepped closer, lowered her voice to almost nothing. "One clerk, asking her teacher how to settle the rest."

Emrys closed her arms around the book, and around the key, and for a moment she bowed her head over them like a woman receiving communion — or a sentence. Frost bloomed and faded on the window behind her, breathing with her.

"Tell the others," she said at last, quietly, "that the account stands. Tell them I intend to collect." She looked up, and there was storm-light in her face, banked but burning. "And Tamsin — find Sable Oathbreaker. They've taken her to the deep cells. Whatever Corvane has promised this crown, whatever game he thinks he's playing — she is the reason I'm going to finish this. Find her. Bring her word that she hasn't been forgotten down there in the dark."

"That I can do," Tamsin said. "Petra knows the stairs."

Down in the city, the bells began to mark the hour — and stopped halfway, all across the capital, every bell in Carreth choking silent at once, as though the winter itself had put a hand over their mouths. Tamsin felt the silence press against the windows, and saw Emrys lift her head toward it, listening, her eyes gone wide and dark.

"It's starting," Emrys whispered. "The longest night. There's less time than we thought."
