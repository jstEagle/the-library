## 22. Through the Impossible Doors

The warehouse keys were heavier than Emrys remembered.

Tamsin pressed them into her palm in the dark of the coal cellar beneath the Guild of Impossible Things, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The keys were old brass, seven of them on a ring worn smooth by a century of clerks' hands, and they were warm — which was wrong, because the cellar was cold enough to frost breath, and nothing in Vell stayed warm without a reason.

"They'll know the moment we use them," Tamsin whispered. Her voice had changed since Emrys had last heard it — harder at the edges, like a ledger line ruled twice to make certain. "The doors don't open quietly. They never have."

"Then we'd best not waste time being quiet about anything else." Emrys closed her fingers around the ring. It bit cold against the keys' strange warmth. "Show me where you've hidden them."

* * *

They came up through the cellar stairs into the warehouse proper, and Emrys's heart broke a little at what she found.

She had worked in this building for six years. She knew its rhythms the way she knew her own handwriting: the creak of the third floorboard before the manifest desk, the way the lamplight pooled amber over the bottled-memory crates, the smell of cedar and ozone that clung to everything the guild touched. She had catalogued ten thousand impossible things here and told herself, every night as she climbed to her narrow room, that this was enough. That a life of careful entries and unclaimed debts was a life.

Now the warehouse stood stripped. The great shelves were bare or half-toppled, crates pried open and emptied, straw scattered across the flagstones like the leavings of some panicked migration. Corvane had gutted his own exchange when the exposure began — sold off, smuggled out, burned what wouldn't move. What remained was the shell of the only home Emrys had known since her exile, and it looked exactly like what it was: a place someone had decided to abandon.

"It's not all gone," Tamsin said, watching her face. "We hid the dangerous stock before he could reach it. The storms. The unmade vows. The doors themselves — he never understood the doors, not really. He thought they were storage." She smiled, thin and fierce. "He thought everything was storage."

"The doors are the guild," Emrys said slowly. The truth of it settled into her as she said it, obvious and enormous. "That's the whole secret, isn't it? Not the inventory. Anyone can bottle a memory with the right glass and the right grief. But the doors—" She looked down the long central aisle, toward the far wall where seven iron-banded doors stood in an uneven row, each one painted a different season's color, faded now, dust-sheeted like furniture in a dead relative's house. "The doors were always how he moved things no customs house could ever find."

"And tonight," Tamsin said, "they're how we move you."

* * *

Emrys had passed those doors a thousand times. She had dusted their frames, checked their hinges against rust, entered them in the master catalogue under a single dismissive line: *Seven (7) doors, nonfunctional, provenance unknown.* She had believed it, because believing it was easier. A clerk who started wondering about the doors would start wondering about everything, and wonder was a debt the guild did not carry.

But she had read Corvane's true ledger. She had seen the column headed *DELIVERIES — CARRETH* and understood that twelve chosen ones before her had traveled from this room to the frozen capital without ever crossing the four hundred miles between. The doors opened onto wrong seasons. And wrong seasons, apparently, could be made to open onto Carreth.

"How?" she asked.

Tamsin pulled the dust sheet from the first door — green paint, spring-green once, now the color of old pond water — and produced a battered notebook from inside her coat. "Marta figured it out. Before they took her." Marta had been the senior clerk, the one who'd taught Tamsin double-entry for impossible goods; the one whose name now appeared in the crown's arrest rolls. "The doors don't go to places. They go to *weathers*. You open one in deep winter and step through into somewhere having a wet April. The trick is that weather doesn't stay put — it drifts. And every weather in the world touches every other weather eventually, if you follow it far enough through." She flipped pages. "There's a door that opens into a thaw. Thaws travel north this time of year. Follow the thaw far enough, fast enough, and it ends where the thaw is fighting the Long Winter. Which is—"

"Carreth," Emrys finished. "The palace sits on the oldest cold in the kingdom."

"The Regent's undercroft," Tamsin said. "Where Sable is."

The name landed in Emrys's chest like a dropped coin. Three days since Sable had been taken. Three days since Emrys had been locked in the Codex chamber while the book wrote her future in ink that dried before her eyes, until Tamsin's smuggled note had come up through the coal chute with the real ledger folded inside it and two words at the bottom: *say when.*

Emrys had written *now* and pushed it back down the chute, and had spent every hour since imagining what the Regent's people might be doing to an oathbreaker who refused to name her.

"Say the cost," she said. "There's always a cost. I keep the ledgers. I know."

Tamsin's jaw tightened. "Time moves differently between seasons. An hour on this side can be a day on that side, or a minute. And the doors want payment to hold shape — they feed on attention. Someone has to keep looking at the door while it's open, really looking, remembering what season it's supposed to be, or it forgets itself and becomes just a wall." She met Emrys's eyes. "I'm staying on this side. Me and three of the others, taking shifts. You go through alone."

"I've been alone in every scene of my life," Emrys said. "At least this time I chose it."

* * *

The spring door opened onto rain.

Not the gray sideways misery of a Vell winter — real rain, warm and fat, falling through gold late light onto a hillside that should not have existed behind an iron door in a stripped warehouse. Emrys stood on the threshold with the key ring biting her palm and felt the wet air roll over her like a benediction, and for one absurd moment she wanted to laugh. Six years she had catalogued wonders and never once stepped through a door. Now she understood why Corvane had kept them locked and dismissed: a thing this beautiful made everything else seem possible, and possibility was terrible for business.

"Door holds for six hours on this side," Tamsin called over the rain. "After that it closes whether you're through or not. Don't be through-adjacent."

Emrys turned back. Tamsin stood in the doorway's frame with rain running off her chin, seventeen years old and older than any clerk the guild had ever employed, holding a lantern against the gold light like a small defiance.

"Tamsin." Emrys's voice caught. She steadied it the way she steadied a shaking hand over an entry — deliberately, precisely. "When I'm gone, burn my old ledgers. All of them. Every debt I recorded for him. Let the guild start clean or not start at all."

"Already done," Tamsin said. "Page by page. We used them to light the stove." A grin, quick and crooked, the first Emrys had seen from her in days. "Go get her back, Wren."

The nickname undid her more than any farewell could have. She stepped through.

* * *

The thaw was a country.

That was the only way Emrys could describe it afterward, though afterward she suspected there would be no describing any of it. The rain-hillside gave way within an hour's walking to a river valley in full meltwater spate, brown and roaring, willows budding along the banks — and then the valley narrowed and the spring began to *move*, visibly move, a front of warmth rolling northward across the land like a slow tide, snow retreating before it in ragged lines, ice groaning off the rivers in sheets. She walked inside the season as though inside a corridor, and the corridor had walls: to her left, eternal April; to her right, a hard white January that watched her pass with the patience of something very old.

The doors had not been exaggerating about time. Her body told her she had walked an hour; her shadow told her a day; her hunger told her three. She ate the bread Tamsin had packed and could not say whether it was breakfast or supper, and stopped trying to know.

It should have been terrifying. It was, in its way. But underneath the terror ran something else, a current she recognized slowly and then all at once, and it was *anger* — clean and rising, the anger of a woman who has kept other people's books for years and finally opened her own.

Corvane had walked these corridors. He had walked them a dozen times, leading frightened girls and boys through the wrong seasons toward a frozen capital, selling each of them to the crown as *the chosen one*, and he had charged the royal treasury by the head. He had probably admired the scenery. He had certainly calculated the mileage.

"You used this," she said aloud to the melting hills, to no one, to him across the miles. "You used all of this, and you never once saw it."

The thaw rolled north around her, indifferent and magnificent, and carried her answer for her.

* * *

The cold came at her like a wall she had known was coming but never truly believed in.

One step, and the air went from soft to knife-edged; the next, and the meltwater stream beside her froze mid-cascade, a sculpture of itself, hanging in the air like a held breath. The Long Winter did not begin gradually. It simply *was*, ahead of her, the way night is beyond a lit doorway — and Emrys stood at the border between April and forever and understood, with a clerk's clarity, exactly what she was looking at.

This was the edge of the sigil's hunger. Everything south of this line still remembered how to be a year. North of it, the season had been eaten down to the bone. The snow here didn't fall so much as hang, reluctant, and the light was the flat pearl-gray of a sky that had forgotten the sun was an option. Somewhere in that direction, in a palace carved from the oldest stone in Carreth, a man who loved his kingdom was being drunk drop by drop by a crown, and a woman who had broken one oath to tell the truth was sitting in a cell paying interest on it.

Emrys pulled her coat tight, took out the second key — the blue-painted door's twin, which Tamsin had told her to look for "when the thaw runs out" — and walked into the winter.

* * *

She nearly missed the door.

That was the worst of it, afterward — not the cold, not the walking, but the finding. The winter side of the corridor had no landmarks. Snow covered snow. She circled the same frozen waterfall twice, three times, her feet going numb inside her boots, before she understood that the doors didn't hide in the landscape; they hid in the *calendar*. She had to stop looking for a shape and start looking for a date — a place where the winter disagreed with itself, where the snow fell a half-day out of true.

She found it at the base of a cliff: a seam in the air where the wind blew from the wrong quarter, carrying a smell that did not belong — stone dust, lamp oil, and underneath it, faintly, the particular chill of a cellar that had been cold for longer than any winter.

The blue door stood there when she looked at it properly, half-sunk in a snowdrift, its paint the exact blue of the hour before dawn. The key fit. Of course it fit. It had been cut for her before she was born, probably; the prophecy had excellent penmanship and terrible manners.

The lock turned. Cold cellar air breathed out against her face.

And Emrys Zhou, exiled storm-caller, guild clerk, latest in a line of sacrifices, stepped out of the wrong season into the undercroft of the royal palace of Carreth, and shut the door behind her on the sound of distant April rain.

* * *

The undercroft was older than the palace above it.

She saw that immediately, with the part of her mind that had spent six years dating documents by their paper. The vaulted ceilings were rough-hewn, pre-imperial, the kind of stone raised by hands that had built for defense against things rather than people. Lamp brackets followed the arches at intervals, most of them dark, but a few burned with a steady yellow light that made the shadows between them absolute. Doors lined the walls — cell doors, iron-banded, numbered not in any royal cipher but in tallies, hundreds of tallies scratched into the wood by fingers that had run out of better things to count.

Emrys's stomach turned over. She had seen the previous storm-callers' cells from the inside — the warnings scratched into tower-room walls. These were the cells below. The ones that didn't have good light.

She moved down the corridor, listening. Above her, muffled by feet of stone, the palace went about its endless curfew evening: boots on marble, the far clang of a gate, voices calling numbers to other voices. Down here there was only the lamps' small hiss and, beneath it, something rhythmic and faint that she couldn't place until she stood still and let it reach her — a sound like breathing, very slow, coming from everywhere and nowhere, as if the whole undercroft were a chest rising and falling around her.

The sigil's feeding peaked at solstice. Two nights away. This was the sound of a hundred-year hunger drawing breath.

"Sable," she whispered, just to hear a human word in the dark. Then, louder, risking it: "*Sable.*"

Silence. Then — from three cells down, behind iron — a scrape. A pause. And a voice, hoarse as a winter road but unmistakably alive, unmistakably hers:

"If this is the Regent testing a new interrogation, you've wasted your money on the acoustics."

Emrys crossed the distance in six strides and had her hands on the cell door before she could think, fumbling the key ring, laughing and furious and half-blind with relief. "If this is the Regent," she managed, "then he's learned to do a very bad impression of a clerk."

A beat of silence. Then the voice again, closer now, right against the door, and Emrys could hear the smile in it even through the hoarseness, even through everything: "*Wren?*"

"Hold on. Hold on, I've got the keys, I've got—" The lock was old and the key fought her, and her numb fingers slipped twice, and then the mechanism surrendered with a clank that echoed down the whole corridor like a shot, and the door swung open.

Sable stood in the lamplight looking like a woman who had been disassembled and reassembled by someone in a hurry — split lip, wrist bruised dark where shackles had sat, one eye still swollen from whatever the Regent's people called questions. But she was upright. She was standing on her own feet. And when she saw Emrys, she did not reach for her, and did not step back; she just looked, long and careful, the way she looked at everything worth trusting, and said:

"You came through the doors."

"The thaw corridor. Tamsin's idea. Marta's notes." Emrys heard herself babbling and let it happen. "Six hours on the far side, I have maybe four left before it closes, we need to move now, and also you look terrible."

"You look," Sable said, "like a ledger that learned to walk." Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, and her good eye was bright. "Three days I've been telling them nothing. Three days of the Regent's polite voice. And you just—" She shook her head slowly. "You walked through April to get here."

"Somebody had to return a stolen knight," Emrys said. "I filed the paperwork myself."

For one moment — one — Sable laughed, low and ruined and real, and the sound filled the ancient corridor and pushed back against all that slow, patient breathing in the stones.

Then the lamps guttered, all together, down the whole length of the undercroft, and the breathing in the walls stopped.

In the silence that followed, from the far end of the corridor, past the ranks of tally-scratched doors, came footsteps. Unhurried. Dry as pages turning.

Sable's hand found Emrys's arm in the dark. "That's not a guard," she breathed. "Guards have weight. That's—" She swallowed. "That's the thing the guards clear the halls for. Wren. That's the Regent."

Emrys reached back and touched the blue door at her spine, feeling the last warmth of April bleeding through the iron bands, four hours of thaw at her back and a hollow man coming down the corridor — and understood that the guild's magic had gotten her in.

Getting out would take everything else she had left.
