## 23. The Regent Unmasked

The warehouse doors opened onto cold that had a smell to it — stone and old water and something sweeter beneath, like flowers left too long in a sealed room. Emrys stepped through a doorway that had shown her, from the Vell side, a shelf of pickled mermaid eggs, and found herself standing in a corridor of black granite lit by lamps that burned without flickering, as if even fire down here were afraid to move.

Tamsin came through behind her, hugging the real ledger to her chest like a child. "This is wrong," she whispered. "The keys should have opened onto the wine cellar. The undercroft. Not — wherever this is."

"This is where the wine cellar goes," Emrys said. "When the crown wants it to."

She had mapped this palace for weeks from a tower room with good light. She had memorized stair counts and corridor turns, the rhythm of guard rotations, the way sound carried in the frozen halls. None of that map applied here. The deep cells did not obey architecture; they obeyed appetite. Corridors curved when they wanted someone lost. Doors appeared where guilt required them. And at their center, she was certain, waited the thing that had interrogated Sable — the Regent, the crown's chief servant, the voice that had ordered her friend held and questioned while the true Codex sat locked in Emrys's own chamber, writing her days before she lived them.

They had come for Sable. That was all. Get her out, get out themselves, let the clerks' small rebellion stay small.

Emrys had learned, over a winter of prophecies and ledgers, that *all* was never all anything turned out to be.

* * *

The first cell door stood open.

That was the wrongness she noticed before anything else. Every other door they'd passed had been iron-banded oak, locked, warded with sigils she could feel pressing against her skin like a change in air pressure. This one hung ajar on silent hinges, and beyond it lay not a cell but a room furnished like a study — if a study could be carved entirely from ice that wasn't cold. Bookshelves of frozen glass. A desk of blue-white stone. A chair facing away from them, toward the far wall, where frost had grown into patterns that Emrys recognized with a lurch: they were ledgers. Columns of numbers, written in frost, floor to ceiling.

"You brought the book," said the Regent's voice from the chair. It was a pleasant voice. That was the horror of it. She had imagined, hearing it secondhand through Sable's bruised account, something ruined and grinding. Instead it was warm, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because everyone around him had always hurried instead. "I did ask you to bring it. You've been very rude about my requests, Mistress Zhou."

"Your requests," Tamsin breathed, "were delivered through bars."

"I find bars simplify conversation." The chair turned.

Emrys had seen the Regent once, across the throne room, during her presentation to court — a tall figure in grey who stood one step behind and to the left of King Aldous's shoulder, precisely where a shadow would stand if shadows could be promoted. She remembered thinking he looked like a man wearing his own face rather well.

Now, in the lampless light of the ice study, she saw what she had refused to see then.

He didn't blink.

Not the way people don't blink when they're trying to unsettle you. He simply *didn't*, and when she watched closely — clerk's habit, watch the entries, watch what doesn't balance — she saw that his eyes moved as a unit, both together, always together, sliding like beads on a wire rather than looking. And his skin. In the throne room's gold light it had seemed merely pale. Here it was translucent at the edges, at the ears, at the hollows of the throat, thin as paper over candlelight, and beneath it there was nothing moving. No pulse at the neck. No breath lifting the chest. He sat the way furniture sits.

"You're dead," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was the only inheritance her storm had ever given her that she liked.

"I am *maintained*," the Regent said, with the mild correction of a man adjusting an entry. "There is a difference. Dead is what happens when maintenance stops." He folded his hands — and she watched the fingers bend, all ten at once, exactly together, like the legs of a spider curling — and regarded her with those wire-sliding eyes. "You are the unbinding. I have been waiting for you for ninety-one years."

* * *

"Ninety-one," Emrys repeated. She made herself walk further into the room. Fear told her to stand near the door, near Tamsin, near retreat; the clerk in her knew that you never audited a ledger from the doorway. You went to the source documents. "The Codex is a century old. If you've been waiting since its writing—"

"The Codex was not written a hundred years ago." He gestured at the frost-ledgers on the wall. "It was written *ninety-one* years ago, by me, or through me, or with my hand — the distinction stopped mattering around year three. The crown required a document. Documents require hands. I had hands, then. Better ones than these." He lifted his right hand and rotated it slowly at the wrist, a full circle, the way no living wrist turned. "The prophecy of the unbinding is not a warning, Mistress Zhou. It is a work order. I wrote it myself so that I would know what was coming. So that *it* would know. The crown likes to know things in advance. It finds surprise undignified."

Tamsin had backed until her shoulders met the doorframe. "Then everything — the summonings, the storms, the chosen ones—"

"Were procurement." He said it without pride and without shame, which was worse than either. "A binding requires a counterweight. To tear the sigil free costs a storm-caller's gift entire — you've read that much, I assume, in your stolen half-read rite. But the crown is efficient. It does not wait for storms to occur. It *orders* them. Each caller summoned, each gift located and tested, each failure recorded—" he touched one of the frost-columns, and numbers shimmered under his fingertip, rows upon rows of names, and Emrys understood with a sickness rising in her throat that she was looking at Corvane's true ledger's original, the master copy, the account from which all the guild's copies had been transcribed — "here. Twelve callers before you. Eleven failed. One almost succeeded, in my grandmother's reign, and we had to burn a valley to stop her. The crown apologized to no one. Crowns do not apologize. They are worn."

"By you," Emrys said. "First."

The stillness that followed was total. Even the lamps' unflickering seemed to hold its breath.

"Yes," the Regent said at last, and for one moment — one — the pleasant warmth dropped out of his voice like a coin through rotted cloth, and what remained underneath was so tired, so bottomlessly, anciently tired, that Emrys felt her revulsion stagger. "By me. First. Would you like to hear how?"

* * *

He rose from the chair, and the movement was wrong in a new way: not fluid, not jerky, but *sequential*, as if some inner clockwork decided on standing and then executed it joint by joint, ankle, knee, hip, spine, each segment reporting completion before the next began.

"There was a war," he said. "Before your guild, before your port city had its third pier, before the name Carreth meant anything but a river crossing. A war between two kingdoms over a border neither could find on the ground anymore, fought so long that both royal lines had forgotten what they were fighting for and continued fighting because stopping required admitting the cost. I was king of the northern line. Aldous's ancestor in every sense except blood — blood ran out of us long ago; the crown prefers continuity to family." He walked — sequentially — to the wall of frost-ledgers. "In the last year of the war, my queen died of fever in a besieged keep, and I was offered a solution. An artifact. Recovered from a drowned temple by men who asked no questions, sold by a merchant whose guild, I am given to understand, still exists in degraded form." His eyes slid to Tamsin. "Corvane's predecessors. Trade is the most immortal thing humans ever built."

"A sigil-crown," Emrys said quietly.

"A rescue," he said. "That is the word I used. That is the word I want you to write down, somewhere, in whatever ledger you keep of this — it was a *rescue*. My kingdom was losing. My son was nine years old. I put it on my head to end a war, and it ended the war in an afternoon, and every man on both sides who saw it kneel forgot why they had ever stood up, and I remember — I remember *everything* — the exact weight of it settling, like a hand learning the shape of my skull. Like a key entering a lock that had been cut for it." He turned. The lamplightless light caught his translucent cheek and showed, faintly, far down beneath the skin, a glimmer that might have been a face, or the memory of one. "And then it began to wear me back."

"How?" Tamsin whispered.

"Slowly. Politely. It is nothing like the stories — it does not seize you, it does not roar. It *suggests*. First it takes your sleep, because decisions are easier when you're tired enough to stop checking them. Then it takes your doubts, one per week, neatly, the way a good clerk clears an inbox. Then your griefs — oh, it loved taking my griefs; it fed on them, that's the mechanism, sorrow is its food and love is the soil sorrow grows in, so it keeps you loving everything around you while it drinks everything you mourn. By the fifth year I could not remember my wife's face and I wept about that beautifully, publicly, and the weeping felt wonderful, and I understood even then, underneath, in the last cellar of myself, that the feeling was not mine. The crown lent me feelings the way a lender lends coin. At interest."

"And your body?" Emrys asked. Her voice was doing the thing it did in front of terrible ledger entries — flat, precise, holding. "The — the maintenance."

"It needed a servant," he said simply. "A century is long. Kings age. Kings die. But a king who dies cannot be worn, and the crown had grown — accustomed — to being worn. So it kept me. The part of me that walks and speaks and signs decrees. It scraped out everything else and used the shell. I have signed the death warrants of eleven storm-callers with these hands." He raised them, palms up, an usher's gesture, a steward presenting a hall. "I felt nothing. That is the mercy of it, and the obscenity. Do you understand now, Mistress Zhou? You came here hating the villain of the piece, and I will not insult you by claiming innocence — the warrants are mine, the war I ended was worth ending, the bargain was mine to make. But the villain you came hating has been dead for ninety-one years. What wears his shape is the crown's *clerk*. Its bookkeeper. Its Regent in the precise sense: the one who rules while the true authority is absent. Only the true authority is never absent. It is three feet above my head at all times, and it is hungry, and tonight — solstice night, the century's closing — it finishes feeding."

* * *

"Then help us," Emrys said.

The words were out before strategy could catch them. Tamsin made a small alarmed sound. The Regent's bead-eyes fixed on her — both together, sliding — and something happened in his face that she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe: a movement like weather behind glass, like a man drowning inside a portrait of himself.

"Help you," he repeated.

"Tear it off. Now, tonight, before the hour. You know its weight better than anyone. You know how it sits, where it grips. You wrote the rite—"

"I wrote the rite," he agreed, "because the crown required the possibility of the rite to exist. It writes its own escapes, Mistress Zhou. It has never once been afraid of the door it leaves unlocked, because it chooses who learns the lock. A storm-caller gives up the gift entire. Do you know what it offers in exchange? It offered me the same bargain, at the start. Keep the crown a century — feed it faithfully, deliver it the callers, maintain the peace it purchases — and at the century's turn, be released. Whole. Alive. *Forgiven.*" His sequential laugh was the worst sound yet, a series of separate exhalations with nothing joining them. "I believed it for thirty years. Then I read my own frost one morning and found I had written, in columns, the names of children in villages where the harvest had failed, ranked by how much grief their mothers could yield. It was not going to release me. There is no release. There is only the next century, and the next king, and the next shell to keep the accounts. It has already measured Aldous's son. Did you know there is a son? Eight years old. Sweet boy. Draws horses."

Emrys's hands had gone cold in a way unrelated to the ice room. "So you won't help."

"I did not say that." He crossed to the desk — sequentially, terribly — and pressed one finger to the frosted glass of its surface. Lines bloomed under the touch: a floor plan, drawn in frost, of the throne room and the approaches to it. Guard positions. The moments when the sigil's attention pooled deepest in Aldous and shallowest everywhere else. "I will not help *you*. I am past helping; helping implies a future in which I am a party. But I have kept this palace's accounts for ninety-one years, and a clerk's revenge is the only kind left to me, and it is this:" the finger tapped the plan, twice, "I can show you the errors. Every system has errors. Even hunger gets careless at the feast. When the sun fails to rise tonight and the crown begins its final feeding, there will be four minutes — four, perhaps five — when it draws inward, all its attention into the wearing, and the Regent-shell stands empty. Unwatched. Un-*worn*." He looked up at her, and in the translucency of his face she saw it again, the glimmer, closer now, a face behind frosted glass, mouthing something without sound. "In those minutes, someone must hold the shell's attention. Someone must stand in front of the empty thing and talk to it, keep it occupied, keep it *looking* at them, because if it remembers itself for even one moment it will remember what it is guarding."

"And whoever stands there," Emrys said slowly, "is standing between the crown and its own body. If it notices—"

"If it notices, it puts me back on like a coat," the Regent said, "and the person in front of me discovers what ninety-one years of maintained strength is for." He straightened. Somewhere far above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, a bell began to toll — not the evening bell, too early, too urgent, the bell that meant the curfew blizzard had closed the city and the long night's preparations had begun. "The dowager will tell you the rest. She remembers more of the binding than I do; the crown never bothered to drink her memories — it thought her finished. Arrogance is the one flaw it inherited from me." He returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands, becoming again the pleasant, terrible fixture she had first seen across a throne room. "Go. Take the east stair; the guards are mine, and they will see a grey cloak and forget it. And Mistress Zhou—" 

She paused at the door, Tamsin trembling beside her, the frost-plan's image burning in her memory.

"When you tear it off him," the Regent said, and the tiredness was back in his voice, the bottomless cellar-tiredness, "when you give your storm to the rite and pull a century off a living man's head — look at what comes out. All of them. They will only have a moment. Someone should look." His bead-eyes slid, together, to the wall of frost and the twelve names and the thirteenth, freshly written, glowing faintly white: **EMRYS ZHOU**. "No one looked at me. Ninety-one years, and no one ever once looked."

* * *

They climbed the east stair in silence, Tamsin clutching the ledger, Emrys climbing with the plan laid out in her mind like an entry awaiting posting. Four minutes. An empty shell that must be kept looking. A rite that cost everything she was.

At the first landing, Tamsin grabbed her sleeve. "Emrys. The cells — Sable's cell was on the west side. We passed it. We *passed it*, we were right there—"

Emrys stopped. Closed her eyes. Saw the Regent's courteous horror, the frost-written thirteenth name, the four minutes.

"We go back for her," she said. "But not through him — if he sees us take her, the four minutes die on the spot. There's another way. The dowager's garden connects to the deep cells through the old chapel; she told me once, when she thought I wasn't listening, that she'd had a door put in so she could visit prisoners the crown wanted forgotten." She was already turning up the stair, faster. "Sable's been in those cells two days answering questions none of us would survive answering. And whatever the Regent is — however much of him is pity — he is also the thing that ordered it."

Above them, muffled and slow, the bell tolled on, counting the hours down toward a dawn that was not coming — and somewhere below, in an ice study full of frost-ledgers, a dead king's clerk sat alone with his accounts, waiting to be looked at.
