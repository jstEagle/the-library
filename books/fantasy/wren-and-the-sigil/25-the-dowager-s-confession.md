## 25. The Dowager's Confession

The dowager queen had not been asked to remember anything in thirty years, and she had grown skilled at it — at the art of letting things go. It was, she sometimes thought, the only skill the crown had ever taught her that she had learned willingly.

Her rooms were on the garden floor of the western wing, past a door the household staff had long ago stopped dusting around. In summer she kept the windows open and let the roses do as they pleased; in winter, which was now most of every year, she sat beneath the glass of the orangery with a shawl and a cup of tea gone lukewarm, watching snow bury the graves of things she had planted and could no longer name.

She had forgotten the names of some of the roses. That was the truth of it, the small domestic horror of her life: there were white ones and red ones and one stubborn yellow climber against the south wall whose name had been her husband's joke, and the joke had gone first, then the name, then the year he'd planted it. She could still feel the shape of his laughter when she looked at it, like a footprint in snow that would melt by morning if she didn't keep looking.

The girl came to her garden on the ninth day of the deep cold, escorted by the oathbreaker woman, who stood at the gate with the particular stiffness of someone guarding a prisoner and hating the arithmetic of it.

"You wanted to see me," the girl said. Emrys. The storm-caller. The name had come to the dowager in fragments over the past week, carried by servants' gossip and the strange pressure in the air that meant the sigil-crown was hungry again. She had sent for her because there were so few people left to whom she could send anything, and because guilt, after enough decades, becomes a kind of appetite.

"I did." The dowager gestured to the bench beside her, iron and stone, furred with frost. "Sit. The tea is terrible. Drink it anyway."

Emrys sat. She was younger than the dowager had expected, and more tired-looking, with the watchful stillness of a person who has learned to be furniture in other people's houses. Her hands were ink-stained. A clerk's hands. The dowager had once had hands like that herself, before she had been a queen, back when she was only a hedge-witch's daughter from the river country who knew how to bind things — small things, mostly. Fevers. Floodwater. Grief, sometimes, though grief always worked itself loose eventually, the way roots crack stone.

"You're the one who bound it," Emrys said. Not a question.

"Direct." The old woman smiled without much humor. "Yes. I'm the one who bound it. Sit closer. What I have to say doesn't travel well across cold air, and I've spent thirty years making sure it travels nowhere at all."

* * *

She began, as she had begun the story a hundred times in her own head, with the winter her husband died.

"Aldous's grandfather was named Osric," she said. "You'll find him in the histories — *Osric the Peacemaker*, they call him, which is a joke so cruel I've never had the heart to laugh at it. He ended the War of the Two Rivers. Eleven years of it. Do you know what ended an eleven-year war? Not armies. Not treaties. A bargain. And bargains, girl, are just debts wearing their good clothes."

Emrys said nothing. She had the look of someone who dealt in ledgers, who understood that the best thing you could give a confession was silence and a straight line down the page.

"He was dying," the dowager went on. "Not quickly. Slowly, the way a fire dies when the wood is wet — coughing, thinning, going gray at the edges while the whole country watched and pretended not to. And the war wasn't finished. There were two provinces still holding out, two rivers' worth of grudges, and everyone knew that the moment Osric closed his eyes for good, the whole map would catch fire again. His peace lived inside his heartbeat. That's what they told me. That's what I believed.

"And I was young, and I was clever, and I loved him the way you love something when you're young — completely, stupidly, with no accounting whatsoever." Her hands tightened on the teacup. "I had been raised on bindings. My mother bound fevers. My grandmother bound a flood once, whole, into a jar, and traded it to a drought country for a decade of grain. Binding was our trade. You take a thing that cannot be stopped and you put it somewhere it can be *kept*, and you pay the price, and you count yourself lucky the price was yours to pay.

"So I went looking for something big enough to hold a dying king's life. And I found it. Or it found me. In those days the difference never seemed important."

She looked out at the garden. Snow was falling on the yellow rose, softening its thorns.

"It called itself a sigil. A crown, eventually, because crowns are how kingdoms wear their magic — but at the root of it, it was a sigil, a mark, a written thing. Older than any kingdom. It had been buried under the Two Rivers since before the war, and the war, I understand now, may have been it dreaming. It offered me exactly what I wanted. Osric lives. The peace holds. The succession is secure. All of it, guaranteed, for as long as the sigil is worn.

"And I asked the question every fool asks. *What does it cost?*"

"And?" Emrys said quietly.

"It told me the truth. That's the worst part. It has never once lied to me. Lies are for people who need them; the sigil has never needed anything from anyone. It said: *I must be worn. Wearing me costs memory. Each year the wearer will lose a portion of himself, and I will keep it, and keep him, and keep everything.*" The dowager's voice went flat and careful, the voice of a woman reciting terms she had signed in blood sixty years ago. "One year of memory per year of wearing. That was the price. I thought — I actually thought — *that's cheap*. One year of memories, for a life? For a kingdom? My mother bound floods for less profit than that."

"What did you not understand?"

"That the price compounds," the old woman said. "That memory is not a coin you spend and forget about. Memory is *load-bearing*. Take away enough of them and the whole structure of a man starts to sag. Take them year after year, and you don't get a man missing pieces. You get a man-shaped room with the sigil living in it, keeping the walls warm, smiling at visitors, remembering to be beloved." She set down the cup. Her hand was steady now; steadiness was another thing she had practiced. "Osric wore it eleven years and died anyway, because the sigil keeps a body the way a museum keeps a king — perfectly, and forever, and dead. But by then it had learned the taste of the royal line. It passed to his son. Then to my grandson's father. Then to Aldous.

"And every king who wore it, I watched. I told myself each time: *this one will be different, this one is strong, this one has more self to spare.* And every king smiled a little wider and paused a little longer between his words, until the pauses became hours, and the hours became years, and the man behind the smile was a tenant who no longer paid rent."

* * *

Emrys had gone very pale, or perhaps the orangery light simply made everyone look that way. "The kings," she said. "Aldous described — he said they were still *in* there. All of them. Screaming politely."

"They are." The dowager did not flinch from it; she had flinched from it for forty years and it had never once helped. "I have spoken with Osric twice. Through the crown, in the gaps, when it grows careless or generous. He remembered me, the second time. He remembered the garden. He said—" and here, for the first time, her voice cracked along an old fault line, "*he said he was sorry the roses got away from me.* As if that were the tragedy. As if the tragedy weren't that I did this to him. That I took the finest man of his age and turned him into a well-kept house for a parasite, because I could not bear the arithmetic of losing him."

She laughed then, briefly, horribly. "Do you know what the sigil takes from *me*, girl? I never wore it. I was never entitled to the honor. But I made the bargain, and bargains bind both parties, and so the sigil collects its interest from me in the only coin I have. Memories. Not all at once. A trickle. It takes the ones I love most first — it says this is efficiency, that strong memories carry more value — and it leaves me the dull ones. I can tell you the exact rainfall of every spring for sixty years. I cannot tell you the sound of my husband's voice. I have his letters. I read them the way you'd read a stranger's."

Emrys's hands had curled in her lap, ink-stained fingers pressing hard against knuckles. "And you let it continue. Sixty years. Four kings."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The dowager looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the snow thickened, erasing the paths between the graves of roses.

"Because unbinding it kills the wearer," she said. "That's in the terms too. I read them afterward, when it was too late to matter — I went back to my mother's books, I read every clause three times. The sigil is woven through whoever wears it like frost through a windowpane. Tear it free, and the pane comes with it. So every year I had a choice: confess, and kill my husband, or my son, or my grandson — or stay silent, and let it feed. And every year I chose the wearing. I told myself it was mercy. I told myself a king alive and hollowed was better than a king dead and whole, better than a kingdom at war. And then, somewhere around the fourth decade, I stopped telling myself anything at all, because telling requires remembering why, and it had eaten most of my whys."

She turned to face Emrys fully. Up close the girl looked even younger, and there was something in her face the dowager recognized with a lurch: the expression of someone doing arithmetic they already dreaded the answer to.

"The Codex," Emrys said. "The prophecy book. It names an unbinding. It names—"

"Me," the dowager said. "Yes. I know what it names. I helped write it."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow load on a roof.

"After Aldous's father — after I watched what it did to him, after I finally understood that the thing would simply pass down the line forever, king to king, century to century, feeding — I went looking for a way out that didn't end in regicide. And binding was my trade. So I bound a prophecy. I took the unbinding rite — the true one, the terrible one, the one that demands a storm-caller surrender the storm itself as payment — and I wrote it where it couldn't be lost or burned or bought. Into a codex. With a date. And a name-shape, a description of the soul required, because I couldn't know who it would be. I only knew what it would cost them." Her eyes were dry and very bright. "I built a trap, girl. I baited it with hope. And then I hid in a garden for thirty years and let it wait for someone like you to walk into it."

* * *

"You didn't warn anyone," Emrys said. Her voice was low and even, the voice of a clerk reading back figures. "All these years. You watched kings get eaten. You watched it send storms hunting — it came for *me* when I was nineteen, it drowned my village trying to summon me early, and you sat in your garden and said nothing."

"Yes."

"Do you understand what it cost? Do you—" Emrys stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice shook, barely. "People died. My mother's house is under the sea. And you were *here*. You knew the mechanism. You could have—"

"Could have done what?" For the first time the old woman's composure broke entirely, and what was underneath was not grandeur but something small and scoured. "Tell me what I could have done. Speak, and the Regent hears — you've met the Regent, haven't you, the hollow gentleman with the kind voice? He was the *first chamberlain*, girl, the first man the sigil ever emptied, before it learned to prefer kings. Confess publicly, and be silenced within the day. Flee, and leave it fed and fat and patient. Every road out of this garden ran through a grave, and I have walked them all in my head ten thousand times, and they all end the same way: with the crown still shining, and me still sitting here, forgetting."

"Then you should have died trying."

"Yes," the dowager said simply. "I should have. That is the truest thing anyone has said to me in thirty years, and I include everything the sigil has taken from me in the count. I should have died trying, and instead I grew roses and forgot their names." She reached into the folds of her shawl and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, worn soft at the corners. "Which is why I sent for you. Not for absolution. I'm owed none, and I'd know if I'd earned any, because I'd remember earning it."

She unwrapped it. Inside lay a key — black iron, older than the palace, with a sigil worked into the bow in a script that hurt to look at directly — and a folded page in faded brown ink, dense with binding-marks.

"My mother's books survive," the dowager said. "Sealed in the crypt beneath the chapel of the Two Rivers, under wards of my own making, wards the sigil has spent sixty years gnawing at and never cracked. This key opens them. And this page—" she pressed it into Emrys's ink-stained hands, closing the girl's fingers over it with her own papery ones, "—is the original term-sheet. Every clause. Including the three the Codex omits, including the price of the price, including the one thing the sigil fears, because I wrote its fear into the bargain myself, in the last hour, when I still had the courage I have since mislaid."

Emrys stared down at the page. "Why give this to me? If unbinding kills the wearer — if it kills Aldous—"

"It kills the wearer who wears it *willingly*," the dowager said. "Read the third clause. Read what happens when the crown is torn free by force, in storm, on the longest night, by a hand that owes it nothing." She smiled, and there was, buried under sixty years of ash, a spark of the hedge-witch's daughter who had once bound a flood into a jar. "I designed the trap to catch it, child. I only lacked the strength to spring it. That failure is mine to carry into whatever comes after memory. But the design—" she nodded at the page in Emrys's hands "—the design was always sound."

Somewhere beyond the glass, bells began to ring — wrong bells, arrhythmic, the palace's alarm, and the light through the orangery dimmed as though a hand had passed over the sun. The frost on the windows bloomed suddenly inward, spreading in patterns like script, like writing, like a ledger being opened.

The dowager looked at the darkening glass and felt, for the first time in decades, something other than guilt.

"It knows," she said quietly. "It's read your name in my face. Go, girl. Run. And when you stand on the dais with your hand in the storm — say my husband's name. Osric. Say it so he remembers it was *his*."
