## 26. The Codex Rewrites Itself

The book had been writing her mornings before she lived them.

Emrys noticed it on the third day of her confinement to the Codex chamber — a room at the top of the western tower where the light came through leaded glass the color of weak tea and fell always across the reading table, as if the architecture itself had been arranged for study. The guards called it an honor. Sable, when they'd been allowed their one stolen conversation through the door, had called it a leash with a view.

She had woken that third morning with a bruise-shaped ache in her chest and a memory of a dream so precise it might have been transcription: snow falling upward over the palace courtyard, Tamsin's face at a warehouse door, the smell of salt and ink. She had dressed slowly, gone to the table, and found the passage already waiting.

*On the third morning she will wake remembering the snow that has not yet fallen.*

Her own handwriting. Or near enough to frighten her — the same small, cramped script, the same habit of pressing too hard on the downstrokes so the letters bled into the vellum. She had held her left hand up against the page like a woman comparing a key to a lock, and the match had been perfect, and she had felt something in her go very quiet and very cold.

Now, five days on, she no longer flinched when the pen moved without her. It sat in its stand beside the Codex, a plain goose-feather quill the chambermaids swore no one had placed there, and in the hours before dawn it would lift of its own accord and write tomorrow into yesterday's book while Emrys slept three feet away, dreaming the words as they were made.

Tonight she was not sleeping. Tonight she was going to catch it at the work.

She had banked the fire low, dragged her chair into the corner behind the tapestry of the Huntress and Her Hounds, and waited. The candle burned down by increments. Frost climbed the inside of the windows in ferns and feathers. Somewhere below, the city of Carreth held its breath under a sky that had not properly darkened or lightened for two days — a bruised pewter ceiling, the sun a pale coin seen through wool. The long winter was accelerating. Everyone felt it: the servants moved faster and spoke less; the king's lucid hours had shrunk to minutes; even the rats had gone strange, she'd been told, sitting in rows along the granary walls like an audience waiting for a play to begin.

At what her body insisted was midnight but the frozen sky refused to confirm, the quill stirred.

It rose from its stand the way a snake rises from a basket — unhurried, certain of its welcome — and drifted to the open Codex. The pages turned themselves, whispering. And Emrys, pressed into the shadow of the tapestry, watched her own hand write in a book older than the kingdom:

*In the morning she will find the offer written fair, in ink that dries gold, and she will be given until the solstice dusk to answer.*

The quill paused. Dipped — into nothing, into air — and continued, and here the handwriting changed, loosened, became something warmer, rounder, almost kind:

*But there is another ending. There have always been other endings. Turn the page, little storm. We kept them for you.*

Emrys's heart was a fist knocking on a door no one would open. She stayed in the shadows until the quill settled back into its stand and the candle guttered and the frost on the glass began, softly, impossibly, to recede — retreating from the window nearest the table as though the cold itself had been dismissed.

Then she crossed the room and turned the page.

* * *

The new pages were not vellum.

That was her first thought, running her fingers along the margin — the texture was wrong, smoother, warmer, like skin kept indoors. The ink was not the Codex's iron-gall brown but a deep lustrous gold that caught the candlelight and seemed to hold a little of it, the way amber holds an insect. And the words were not prophecy. Prophecy told you what would be. These pages were doing something else entirely.

They were negotiating.

*Consider,* the first line read, in the warm round hand, *the arithmetic of grief.*

Below it, in smaller script, as if a patient tutor had set out a problem for a clever student:

*Aldous dies at dawn. You know this. The rite requires a storm poured through a living vessel, and vessels crack. Perhaps he cracks. Perhaps you. The Regent's guard numbers forty in the undercroft alone. Sable is wounded still from the deep cells; she limps when she believes no one watches. The clerks who love you are children with borrowed keys. Count the ways this ends badly. We have counted them. We wrote them all down, once, in our innocence. Would you like to see the ledger of your friends' deaths? We keep excellent records.*

Emrys made herself breathe. Made herself remember Sable's voice in the blizzard-dark: *It lies the way water lies — it only ever shows you your own reflection, and it makes the reflection want things.* But the words on the page did not feel like a reflection. They felt like being known, which was worse, and which was, she understood dimly, exactly the point.

She turned the page anyway. She was a clerk. Ledgers were her native tongue, and somewhere beneath her fear, the accountant in her wanted to audit the thing that was auditing her.

The next page showed her a version of the world.

Not described it — showed it, the way a memory shows, whole and sensory and arriving without consent. She was standing in a spring courtyard she half-recognized as the palace garden, except the frost was gone and the dowager queen's roses climbed the wall in reckless abundance, and Aldous was laughing — truly laughing, head back, a man with no crown at all — and Sable stood beside him in a knight's colors with no disgrace attached to them, and Corvane was dead or gone or irrelevant, and Tamsin was there, older, laughing too, and the guild's warehouse doors opened onto nothing impossible at all, just weather, just seasons, just ordinary days.

And Emrys was in the picture. That was the detail that snagged her, that her clerk's eye went to immediately the way an auditor's eye goes to the figure that balances too neatly. In the vision she wore the sigil-crown.

It sat lightly on her brow. That was the lie's finest craftsmanship: the crown looked *comfortable*. It looked like a circlet of spring flowers. Her hands in the vision were empty and easy, her shoulders unburdened, her smile the smile of someone who has finally put down every weight she was carrying — the exile, the ledger, the loneliness, the storm.

No one died. No one cracked. The winter ended because *she* wore it, and a crown worn willingly, the page explained in its warm margins, need devour nothing. A willing bearer feeds it choice by choice, freely given, and a fed-on-choice crown grows gentle as a house cat. The century of kings could rest. Aldous could live. Sable could be restored, pardoned, honored. The guild could trade openly in honest wonders. All of it — all of it — for the small matter of a circle of metal and a lifetime of yes.

*You would not even lose the storm,* the gold ink added, almost shyly. *We would never ask that of you. Keep your thunder. Keep everything you are. We are not asking you to become less. Only to hold us, as we have held so many, and be, at the last, the one who said yes instead of no.*

Emrys sat down on the floor.

She did not decide to sit; her legs simply resigned. She sat with her back against the reading table's leg, the Codex looming above her on its stand like a lecturing god, and she looked at the golden page for a long time, and she let herself feel how much she wanted it.

That was the discipline Sable had taught her, in the tower, in the borrowed-honesty night: *Name the wanting before it names you.* So she named it. She wanted the spring. She wanted Aldous laughing. She wanted Sable upright and unbowed and wearing colors that meant nothing had been broken. She wanted Tamsin safe and the warehouse ordinary and her own shoulders light. God help her, some starved animal part of her wanted the crown itself — not its power but its *certainty*, the promise of being the fixed point around which everything else arranged itself, after a life of being the variable everyone solved for and discarded.

The book knew all of it. Of course it did. It had eaten kings; it knew hunger the way rivers know wet.

"You're lying," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the frostless window, to the patient gold.

*I am offering,* the page wrote, the quill lifting briefly to amend itself, and she hated that it had heard her, hated more that it answered. *There is a difference. Lies are for people who fear the truth. I am a century old, little storm. I do not fear anything. Ask me whether the spring is real.*

"Is it?"

*Every word. If you wear us, every word comes true. That is what none of your allies understand. They think we destroy futures. We are made of futures. Every king we wore, we offered this same garden, and every king refused — out of pride, mostly, or some clerk's notion of virtue — and so we took the lesser path, the hungry path, and they screamed politely forever. You could be the first to take the greater path. The first king — the Regent, what remains of him — he weeps sometimes, in the deep cells, when he remembers refusing. He refused for his queen. Look how that ended.*

The image shifted. The spring garden dissolved and re-formed as something else: the throne room at dawn, the rite as Sable and the dowager had sketched it, and then the failure of it — Aldous's body arching, the vessel cracking, the storm spilling out of Emrys and finding nowhere to go, and Sable screaming her name across a floor full of guards, and the sun never rising, and the long winter settling over the world like a lid on a pot, permanent now, complete, because the last chance had failed.

*Or this,* the book said, and showed her the garden again, tender as an apology.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the corner with the tapestry at her back and watched the gold pages turn themselves, slowly, patiently, each one another version of the garden with some small refinement — here Sable's hand in hers, here the dowager young again, here a city where no one had ever starved in a winter the crown had lengthened. The book was not stupid. It varied its approach. Some pages argued arithmetic; some pages argued mercy; one page, devastatingly, was simply a letter, written in Tamsin's actual hand, dated three years hence, thanking her — *thank you for choosing the garden, Wren, thank you for letting us all live* — and Emrys had to press her knuckles against her teeth not to make a sound.

Because here was the horror she finally named, in the grey hour when even the false dawn refused to come: the letters and the gardens were lies, but the *arithmetic* was true. The rite might kill Aldous. It might kill her. The Regent's guard was real; Sable's limp was real; Tamsin and the clerks were real and breakable. The book did not need to invent dangers. It needed only to count the ones already in the room and then sell the only exit it had built.

Every good swindle, Corvane had taught her — the one lesson worth keeping from him — begins with true figures.

Toward what the tower bell swore was morning, she stood, walked to the table, and laid her palm flat on the golden page. The vellum-that-was-not-vellum was warm. It pulsed faintly, like something asleep that would very much like to be carried.

"I'm not answering tonight," she said. "I'm not answering tomorrow. I'll look at your garden every night until the solstice if it keeps you writing these pages instead of writing my friends' deaths. But hear me." She leaned close, close enough to see her own breath fog and vanish against the warm page, and put every ounce of the voice that had once, accidentally, called a storm out of a clear sky. "I know what you are now. You're not a crown. You're a *ledger* — a ledger that got loose, that learned to eat its debtors, that's been trying to balance a hundred years of accounts on the backs of children it summoned. And I've read enough ledgers in my life to know one thing about them."

The page warmed under her hand. *Tell us,* it wrote, and for the first time the round warm hand trembled, just slightly, at the downstroke.

"No entry stands," she said, "that was made in bad faith. Not in any ledger I've ever kept. Your garden is a forged entry. And when the time comes, I'm going to strike it through."

The Codex went still. Then, softly, the gold ink began to move one final time, and what it wrote was not argument, and not anger, but something far more frightening — the tone of a merchant closing one negotiation and opening another:

*Very well. Then we will show you the garden's price, since you insist on accounting. Tomorrow the Regent will bring you Sable's confession — extracted, signed, naming you the willing sacrifice — and you will learn what your refusal costs while you sleep safe in your tower. We did not send for her, little storm. Your honesty did. Truth has a price too. Shall we itemize?*

The ink dried. The quill settled. And below, muffled by stone and distance but unmistakable — boots on the spiral stair, many boots, and the jangle of keys, and a sound she knew from the worst nights of her exile: someone crying, quietly, in the manner of a person who has decided to stop.

The door of the Codex chamber began to unlock.
