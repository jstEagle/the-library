## 27. Corvane's Last Ledger Entry

Tamsin had counted the warehouse's doors a hundred times in her three years as a guild clerk, and she had never once gotten the same number twice.

Tonight she made it forty-one, which was wrong — the true count was thirty-nine on solstice weeks, when the winter door grew shy and hid behind the wine cellar — but she wrote *41* in the margin of her inventory book anyway, because Corvane had taught her that an honest ledger recorded what was seen, not what was wished for. It was the last lesson of his she intended to keep.

The Guild of Impossible Things smelled, at midnight, of cold iron and old paper and the faint green sweetness of bottled springtimes. Tamsin moved down the central aisle with a lantern held low, past the shelves of unmade vows (their glass jars humming faintly, like struck tuning forks that couldn't decide to stop), past the caged regrets that rustled when she passed, past the long rack of borrowed courage that the clerks were forbidden to touch without a second signature. Everything in its place. Everything accounted for. That had been the whole point of the guild once, before she'd understood what the accounting was *for* — before she'd found Emrys's name written in a column marked *disbursable assets*, in Corvane's small, patient hand.

She had been Emrys's apprentice for two years. She had learned ledgers from the woman the prophecy had come to collect, and now Emrys was a thousand miles north in a frozen palace, writing her own days before she lived them, and Tamsin was here, in Vell, keeping the books of the man who had sold her teacher like a shipment of thunderstorms.

The lantern guttered. Not from any draft.

Tamsin stopped. She knew every way the warehouse breathed — the sea-door sighing with the tide, the attic vents exhaling summer when the season-shelves were overstocked. This was none of them. The flame bent *toward* the far end of the aisle, toward the counting room, as if pulled by something hungry.

Then she heard the sound she would remember for the rest of her life: the voice of the Codex Vellum, or rather, the sound of a man reading aloud from it in a counting room at midnight, alone, his tone the careful, unhurried tone of a merchant appraising goods.

Corvane's voice.

* * *

She should have gone for the others. She knew that later, and forgave herself for not doing it, because at the time there was no *others* — the night clerks had scattered after the exposure, half of them to their beds and half of them, like Tamsin, to the warehouse, unwilling to let the place out of their sight while its master's ruin was still fresh enough to rot. Merrick was somewhere above, checking the vow-jars. Osla was asleep in the loft with a crossbow across her knees, because Osla slept the way soldiers slept. And Tamsin was here because someone had to be, and she had decided, in the six days since the forged Codex had been dragged into the light of a frozen court, that the someone was going to be her.

So instead of running for help, she did what Emrys had trained her to do. She set down the lantern where its light wouldn't reach around corners, took off her boots so the floorboards wouldn't speak for her, and went toward the counting room in her stockinged feet, one silent step at a time.

The counting room door stood open a hand's width. Through the gap: candlelight, and Corvane at his desk, and on the desk — laid flat, weighted at both ends with brass ingots — the Codex Vellum.

It had come back to them. That was the thing nobody in the guild could explain. After Emrys had stood up in the snow court of Carreth and called the forgery a forgery and named her own guild's trade in chosen ones before the assembled crown, the real Codex had simply — left. One morning it was chained in its case; by evening the chain hung empty, and a week later a fishing boat out of Vell reported a book floating on a calm sea two hundred leagues north, pages open, turning themselves, and then somehow it was back on its shelf as though it had never gone anywhere, drier than sin, waiting.

Books didn't do that. But then, Tamsin thought grimly, watching through the gap, neither did masters who sold their own people. The world had stopped being reasonable some time ago, and Corvane had helped it along.

He was not reading the unbinding rite. He was *copying* it — no. She narrowed her eyes. He wasn't copying it either. He was transcribing it onto a single sheet of good vellum in his smallest hand, the hand he used for contracts, the hand that never smudged, and beside the sheet lay a second document already sealed with black wax, and Tamsin's stomach turned over slowly, the way a boat turns when the wave comes from a direction you weren't watching.

Because she recognized the seal. Everyone in the trade recognized it: a crown within a circle, pressed in wax mixed with ash. The sigil's mark. The mark that had come north on every royal purchase order for eleven years, buying bottled memories and unmade vows and, once, horribly, a jar of *someone's last kind day* — Corvane had sold it without asking whose, and Emrys had argued with him about it for a week and lost.

"You're awake, Tamsin." He didn't look up. His pen didn't pause. Of course he knew; he always knew; that had been the whole terror of him. "Come in. You'll want to see this. It's the largest entry I'll ever make."

"I saw enough entries," Tamsin said, and pushed the door wide, because hiding in a doorway was for people who still believed the conversation could be survived by stealth. "I audited them."

"Ah." Now he looked up, and she hated how unchanged his face was. Six days of ruin — his buyers gone, his name a curse in three capitals, his life's architecture collapsed around him — and Corvane sat at his desk like a lighthouse keeper in fair weather. Grey at the temples, precise at the cuffs, eyes the color of ledger ink. "Yes. Your audit. Very thorough. You found the column I meant you to find."

The sentence landed in her chest like a dropped weight. "What?"

"You think I kept the true ledger in the third-floor strongroom by accident?" He returned to his transcription, dipping the pen, drawing a line of letters so fine they might have been stitched. "Emrys found it because I let the lock be picked. A master who hides nothing is never betrayed, Tamsin. A master who hides everything is never trusted. I needed the clerks to find it *eventually*. Loyalty that survives a betrayal is worth ten loyalties that never get tested."

"That's not—" Her voice cracked and she steadied it. "You fed twelve people to that crown. Twelve. I read the names. There was a girl from Sennet who could call rain, and a ferryman, and—"

"And every one of them dead of a crown that eats what summons it, yes, I know, I did the arithmetic years ago." Something moved behind his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Do you imagine I enjoyed it? I am a merchant, not a monster. I priced each loss honestly. Grief is deductible too, if you keep clean books."

"You're selling them the unbinding." She said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. The sheet of vellum. The sigil-sealed contract. "You're giving the crown the one thing that can destroy it, and they're paying you—"

"They are paying me the only coin that matters anymore." For the first time, Corvane set down his pen. "Not gold. Gold is paper with delusions. They're paying me in *exclusion*. My name, struck from the prophecy's interest. Mine and mine alone. The sigil has need of Emrys Zhou alive until the solstice, and it has no further use for anyone who might complicate that transaction. I have arranged to be uncomplicated. It is, if I may say so, the finest negotiation of my career: I sell my enemy the knife, and the price is that the knife forgets my throat."

"You're trading Emrys's life for your own safety."

"I'm trading *nothing*. Do you understand that? Nothing. She was promised to that crown a century before either of us was born — it's written in ink older than the guild, older than the kingdom. I cannot save her. No one can save her. All I can do is choose whether I die in the same tide." He said it with terrible gentleness, the gentleness he used on new clerks explaining why their first mistake hadn't ruined them, and Tamsin realized with a cold, clarifying shock that he believed it. Every word. That was the horror of Corvane: not cruelty, but accounting. Somewhere in the ledgers of himself he had entered twelve lives under *costs of operation* and balanced them against the guild's survival and signed the page without trembling.

And Emrys had worked for him for four years. Had trusted him with her exile, her name, her nights.

"The storm," Tamsin said suddenly. "The one she was exiled for. The crown sent it hunting her — Sable figured it out, it's in the letters. You *knew*. When she came to you, soaked and ruined and half-mad with guilt, you knew what had really happened, and you hired her anyway, and you kept her close, because a summoned sacrifice is safest kept where you can watch the asset." Her hands had closed into fists without her permission. "You didn't take her in. You took *delivery*."

For a moment — just a moment — the lighthouse-keeper composure flickered, and underneath it Tamsin saw something old and tired and almost human look out at her, like a man glimpsed through a window at dusk.

"She was the best clerk I ever had," Corvane said quietly. "Better than me, if it comes to that. She counted things truly." He straightened. "Which is more than can be said for you at present, apprentice. You're blocking my doorway, and the tide of your argument is going out. Step aside."

"No."

"Tamsin."

"*No.*" Her voice came out bigger than she'd expected, big enough to reach down the aisles, and somewhere overhead a floorboard answered — Merrick, awake, listening. Good. Let the whole warehouse hear. "The guild isn't yours anymore. You made sure of that yourself, when you taught us all to audit. Well. We audited *you*. And the clerks voted last night, in the loft, with Osla holding the tally-book, and I'm here to enter the result in your own ledger, Master Corvane, since you always said the record should be made by the person who witnessed the event." She stepped forward and laid her inventory book on the desk, open to the page she'd ruled that morning with Emrys's own neatness. "Item one: the master is removed. Item two: the guild rechartered under honest trade. Item three—" she looked at the transcription drying under his pen, at the rite that could free a king and kill her teacher, "—that page comes with us."

Corvane rose. He was not a large man, but he filled rooms the way debt fills a household — quietly, completely — and for three years that presence had been the fixed point of Tamsin's world. She watched it bear down on her now and found, to her own surprise, that her feet did not move.

"You would be wise," he said, "to consider what happens to clerks who seize a warehouse full of impossible things. Doors to wrong seasons. Jars of things that bite. You've barely learned which shelves not to dust."

"I know exactly which shelves not to dust," said Tamsin. "You wrote the list. Chapter four of the *Clerk's Companion*. In your handwriting. Which we also know how to read."

Above them, deliberately, Merrick dropped something heavy. Then something else. Then Osla's voice came down through the ceiling like the verdict of a very patient god: *"Master Corvane. The stairs are occupied. The windows are watched. And the sea door was bricked over Tuesday, which you'd know if you'd done your own inspections these past six days instead of sulking over contracts."*

Something in Corvane went out then. Not surrender — Tamsin doubted he had ever surrendered to anything in his life — but the particular collapse of a man whose accounts have finally refused to balance, however he carries the numbers.

"It won't matter," he said. He sounded almost sorry for her. "You'll take the rite, and you'll send it north, and it will change nothing. Prophecies don't fail, child. They only wait. The crown will have its storm-caller whether I sell it the knife or you hide it under your bed. I have merely chosen to be paid for the inevitable. When you're older, and the losses start arriving on your own doorstep, you'll understand the mercy of it."

"I don't want to understand it," Tamsin said. "That's the difference between us. I'd rather lose."

* * *

They took the transcription, and the sealed contract, and the strongroom keys, and — at Osla's insistence, over Tamsin's objection that it felt like theft — Corvane's personal signet ring, because half the seals in three kingdoms still answered to it and they could not afford to leave that much power in a locked drawer of a man they were putting out on the street.

They did not put him out violently. That was the strange part, the part Tamsin turned over in her mind for hours afterward while she copied the unbinding rite in triplicate by candlelight, preparing it for the road north. Corvane gathered his coat, and his hat, and one small satchel — only one, a man who had owned a warehouse of wonders leaving with a single bag, and Tamsin understood even as she watched him pack it that the bag held no goods at all, only papers, because Corvane had never valued anything except records — and he paused at the warehouse's front door, under the old painted sign, and looked back at the clerks standing among the shelves of impossible things with lanterns raised like a jury.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the fourth shelf in the east aisle, behind the jar of unlived summers. There's a ledger there I never showed anyone. Green binding. Read the last entry before you judge me entirely."

"We already judged you entirely," said Osla.

"Yes," Corvane agreed mildly. "But read it anyway. A good accountant shows his work." And he tipped his hat — actually tipped it, the old-fashioned gesture he'd used at every closing of accounts for twenty years — and walked out into the snow of Vell, and the city swallowed him, and the door shut behind him with the soft, final click of a book closing.

They found the green ledger within the hour. Tamsin read it alone, by her own candle, because some entries belong to only one pair of eyes.

Most of it was what she expected: decades of trades, prices, the slow sediment of a merchant's conscience calcifying page by page. But the last entry was recent, dated the night the exposure broke, and it was not a trade at all. It was written in the smallest hand he owned, the contract hand, and it said:

*Final entry. Sold the unbinding rite to the sigil's servants for my exclusion from the prophecy. Note for whoever audits this: the rite I sold them is genuine in every particular except one — I have reversed the order of the final two clauses. Performed as written, it will not unbind the crown. It will complete it. I am told the sigil cannot tell truth from well-kept falsehood; it has spent a century believing itself a bargain instead of a hunger, so it will pay, and it will believe, and it will be wrong.*

*I have spent my life teaching clerks that the ledger must be honest even when the trader is not. Consider this my work shown. I could not give them the girl's life back — the tide was always coming for her, and I am not a brave man, whatever my ledgers say. But I could make certain that when the moment came, the knife sold to the crown would be false, and the true rite would be wherever her people chose to keep it.*

*Tamsin reads everything. She will find the reversal. Tell her: the error is deliberate, and it is the only honest entry I ever made twice.*

*Balance: owing. To whom it may concern — forgive the arithmetic. C.*

Tamsin sat for a long time with the candle burning down and her breath fogging in the cold of the east aisle, and then she did the thing Emrys would have done, the thing she'd been trained for: she checked.

She fetched the transcription they'd seized from the counting room and laid it beside the Codex Vellum itself, and read the final clauses forward and backward, three times, lips moving. The order of the last two lines in Corvane's copy was inverted. The copy in his own hand, the one bound for the sigil, was a lie dressed as truth — and the lie was aimed, with a dead merchant's precision, at the heart of the crown.

He had cheated the devil, in the oldest language there was: bookkeeping.

Tamsin laughed once, a short, wet sound with nothing funny in it, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and reached for fresh paper. There was no time to feel anything properly. The solstice was four days away, the roads north were freezing, and somewhere in a tower in Carreth, Emrys was living days that a book had already written for her.

Tamsin ruled a fresh page with Emrys's neatness — her own neatness now, she supposed; things passed down were things owned — and began the letter that would travel faster than any honest courier could carry it:

*To Emrys Zhou, and to Sable, and to all who stand with her at the unbinding —*

*Beware the rite you are offered. There are two copies in the world. One is true.*

*We are sending you the true one.*

*— T., Clerk of the Guild of Possible Things, first entry of the new charter*

She signed it, sealed it in wax mixed with ash from the burned forged Codex — let the sigil taste *that* — and carried it herself down to the harbor, where the fastest smuggler in Vell owed the guild a debt that even a revolution could not erase, and where the ice was already forming at the edges of the water, thin and white and spreading, as if the long winter had leaned in to read the address.
