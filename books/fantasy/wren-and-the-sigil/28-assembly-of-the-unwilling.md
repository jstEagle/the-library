## 28. Assembly of the Unwilling

The dowager's garden had died a century ago and refused to admit it.

Emrys stood among the frozen roses — black stems sheathed in ice, blooms that had never been allowed to finish falling — and watched the light come up gray through the glass roof of the old orangery. Somewhere above the frost-furred panes, the sky was doing what it had done for six days now: hesitating. The sun rose late and set early, as if it too were being fed upon, as if the long winter were drinking even the daylight down in slow, patient swallows.

She had chosen this room for its dead things. Everything here had been killed by the sigil's hunger and yet still stood upright, still held its shape, still cast a shadow on the marble floor. It seemed an honest place to ask people to die.

The table at the center of the orangery had been the dowager's writing desk once, wide enough for ledgers, and Emrys had spent the night covering it with exactly that. Ledgers. Her own, kept these years in Vell in a hand so precise her master used to say she wrote like someone settling accounts with God. And beside them, weighted open with a candlestick, the Codex Vellum, whose pages had stopped pretending to be innocent.

She read the entries one last time while the others gathered. Not because she needed to. Because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

*The unbinding requires four consents freely given,* the Codex said, in ink that had appeared overnight without anyone dipping a pen. *A storm-caller who surrenders the storm. A keeper who opens what was closed. A blood of the binding who releases the debt. A crown that consents to be emptied.*

Four consents. Four people in this room who might have to die giving them.

* * *

Sable arrived first, because Sable always arrived first; Emrys suspected she slept standing in doorways like a soldier's horse. She came in from the cold with frost in the short gray of her hair and her broken sword — reforged now, badly, by a palace smith who hadn't asked questions — slung across her back where an oathbreaker's blade traditionally went unworn. She wore it anyway. That was rather the point of Sable.

"The corridors are clear," she reported, in the flat tone she used for reports, and then ruined it entirely by adding, "You haven't slept."

"I've slept since the solstice began."

"Lying down?"

"Leaned against a wall."

"That counts as furniture." Sable pulled out a chair — the only chair, dragged from some servant's corridor — and did not sit in it. She set it behind Emrys instead, an argument conducted entirely in carpentry, and took up her post by the door.

Emrys sat. It was easier than losing.

Tamsin came next, and with Tamsin came the sound of the guild arriving: eleven clerks filing into the dead garden in their travel-stained coats, carrying satchels of ink and chalk and the particular silence of people who had betrayed their master and meant to do it thoroughly. They arranged themselves along the walls the way they'd once arranged themselves around Corvane's counting room, and when Emrys looked up at them, Wren — little Wren, who used to misfile shipments of bottled thunderstorms out of pure mischief — gave her a nod so solemn it nearly undid her.

They had smuggled her the true ledger. They had opened the impossible doors. Eleven people who owed her nothing but an apprenticeship, and had chosen her over the only family most of them had ever known.

"You're all aware," Emrys said quietly, "that I can't pay you."

"We're guild clerks," Tamsin said. "We knew the position was unpaid when we accepted it." A ripple of something almost like laughter moved through them, quickly smothered. Grief-humor. The guild had always run on it.

Then the doors opened again, twice.

The dowager queen came first, moving slowly, leaning on the arm of no one, which was itself a kind of defiance. Maren the Unremembered, the servants called her now — though not to her face, and rarely above a whisper. She had given sixty years of memories to the sigil's bargain, and what remained of her mind was a country with most of its map burned off. But she walked into the room where her husband's ghost had been caged for a century with her chin up, and when her eyes found the Codex on the table, they did not flinch.

Behind her came the king.

Aldous walked unaided these days, which was new, and terrible, and wonderful. The sigil had been loosening its grip since Emrys first touched the crown in the snow court — a hand prying fingers open finger by finger — and what surfaced between the gripings was a man. Thin. Gray-faced. With pauses in his speech like gaps in a bridge, places where the thing beneath him had eaten through. But his eyes, when he looked at you, were *his*, and everyone in Carreth remembered what that used to mean.

He bowed to the assembled company. To clerks and oathbreakers and a half-erased old woman, the King of Carreth bowed, and waited until every one of them had risen before he straightened.

"Thank you for coming," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "All of you. What I'm about to show you — what I'm about to ask —"

"Don't apologize," Aldous said gently. "I've had a hundred years of apologizing done on my behalf. It's a wretched custom. Get to the ledger."

So she did.

* * *

She laid it out the way she'd been taught to lay out any account: plainly, completely, hiding nothing in footnotes.

"The sigil-crown has ruled this kingdom for ninety-nine years and three hundred and fifty-eight days," she said. "It completes its century at dawn tomorrow — or it would, if tomorrow had a dawn. The Codex is explicit. If the feeding finishes, the binding becomes permanent. Every future king of Carreth will be born already worn. There will be no more Aldouses surfacing. No more moments of lucidity. Just the crown, wearing bodies until the sun forgets how to rise here at all."

She turned the Codex around so they could see the page. The ink had rewritten itself again in the night; it did that now, offering versions, testing them, the way Corvane used to test a buyer with a false price to see how much they truly wanted. This version was the honest one, or as close to honest as prophecy ever got.

"The unbinding requires four consents. Freely given — that word matters, the book repeats it nine times, and I've checked all nine contexts. No coercion, no sacrifice taken unwillingly, or the rite fails and the sigil knows everything we intend. Which brings me to the four."

She touched the first entry.

"A storm-caller who surrenders the storm. That's me. I call the storm tonight — the storm I was exiled for, deliberately, at full strength, through every hall of this palace — and I pour my gift into the rite as the crown tears free. I won't lie to you about what the book says happens to the gift afterward. It doesn't say I survive it. It doesn't say I don't. It says only that the storm goes *into* the unbinding, and what goes into a thing does not come back out."

Sable made a sound, very small, like a door closing somewhere far away. Emrys didn't look at her. If she looked at her she would stop being able to do this.

"The second consent is a keeper who opens what was closed. The sigil was bound inside a warded vessel — the dowager can tell you what it was — and the vessel must be reopened from within the rite, by the one who holds the key of the original binding." She looked at Maren. "That's you. Your memories are the lock. Opening it means letting the rest go — whatever's left. The book says the keeper must consent to *emptiness*, and it uses that word lovingly, which tells me everything about how old this book is and nothing good about what it means."

The dowager queen smiled. It was a terrible smile and a brave one, the smile of a woman who had been paying installments on a bargain for sixty years and had finally seen the final bill.

"My girl," she said, "I have been emptying myself into that thing since before your parents met. You're asking me to finish the job. I have wanted to finish the job for sixty years." Her hands, knotted like the frozen roses, folded on the table. "Read on. It gets worse, or I'm a queen."

"It gets worse," Emrys agreed. "Third consent. Blood of the binding who releases the debt. The original bargain was sealed in royal blood — the dowager's husband's blood, willingly spilled to save his life. To release it, a descendant of that line must stand within the circle and consent to let the protection die. The protection *is* him, in a sense. It's the reason he's alive at all after a century of being eaten." She made herself meet the king's eyes. "If the sigil tears free and the debt isn't released, it takes the whole line with it. Every drop of royal blood in Carreth, everywhere, all at once. The book is very clear. So either you stand in that circle knowing the tearing may take you —"

"— or everyone I am related to dies of me," Aldous finished. He said it without drama, the way a man reads his own obituary in a shipping notice. "Yes. I worked that out around the fourth time the book tried to bribe you with prettier versions. It always leaves that clause intact. Even its lies keep that clause." He looked down at his own hands, pale and steady on the marble. "Do you know what's in the crown? Truly in it? I've told you — kings. Nine of us. The first one screams the loudest because he's been there longest. The others scream politely. We scream politely. A century of learning manners inside our own executioner." He raised his head. "Clerk Zhou. Emrys. Whatever you call yourself when no one is grading your penmanship. I have wanted to die human since the day my father put that thing on my head and told me it was duty. You are not asking me to risk my life. You are offering to return it to me at last, briefly, at the end, where I can spend it on something that matters. Consent the third is given. Write it down. You're the ledger-keeper. Make it official. I want it *official*."

Emrys wrote it. Her hand shook on the king's name, and she let it shake, because a record should be honest even when the clerk wasn't steady.

"One more," she said. "The worst one."

"No," Sable said, from the doorway.

"Sable —"

"No." She crossed the room in five strides, and there she was at the table's edge, frost melting off her shoulders onto the marble, her eyes the color of a winter sea. "Whatever the fourth line is, whoever it names — no. Find another way. You're clever. You rewrote a forgery in front of an entire court. Rewrite this."

"The fourth consent is a shield who stands at the door," Emrys said, because lying to Sable had become impossible somewhere in the last week and she'd stopped fighting it. "When the rite begins, the sigil will know. It will send everything it has left — the Regent, what remains of him, and every guard the crown still wears. Someone has to hold the throne room door so the rite can finish. The book says the shield's consent is to *stand*, and to keep standing, past the point where standing is survivable." Her voice cracked on the last words and she hated it and let it happen. "It doesn't name you, Sable. The book doesn't name anyone anymore — that's the one mercy in it. Anyone could stand there. But it has to be someone who won't break, and it has to be someone whose consent is real, and I am asking you — I am *begging* you — to refuse. Let one of the clerks —"

"The clerks can hold a door," Tamsin said immediately, and eleven voices said some version of the same.

"They can't," Sable said simply. "No insult meant. I've fought the Regent. He was a man once and now he's a hinge the crown swings on, and holding a door against him is not a matter of courage. It's a matter of twenty years of knowing exactly how a knight breaks another knight. I'm the only person in this room with that knowledge." She turned back to Emrys, and her voice dropped into something private, something with the whole frozen world excluded from it. "You told me once you were afraid you were nothing without your gift. I've had twenty years to learn what I am without mine. I'm this. I'm the person who stands in doors. Let me be it one more time, for something worth standing for — not for an oath. Oaths are what other people write on you. This one I'd be writing myself."

"And if you die?" Emrys asked, and heard her own voice go small and young and exiled, the voice of a girl in a storm she never called.

"Then you tear the crown off a king and give a country back its morning," Sable said. "And you keep the ledgers of it. Every entry. Even the hard ones." A pause, and then, softer, the truth under all of it: "*Especially* mine."

* * *

The dowager spoke next. She did it standing, both hands on the table, and her voice carried the strange doubled weight of a woman speaking partly from memory and partly from whatever is left when memory burns away.

"I bound the sigil," she said. "Let the record show it, since we're keeping records. I bound it to save my husband's life, and I would bind it again, because the war it ended was killing ten thousand children a season, and I chose, and choosing is what queens are for. But I chose without reading the whole contract, and the thing fed on him for thirty years while I forgot, year by year, why I had ever thought it was a rescue." She looked around the room, at each face in turn. "I release the debt. I open the vessel. I consent to emptiness — gladly, because emptiness, at last, will be *mine*, chosen and finished, instead of a tax collected forever." Her eyes glistened. "And I ask one payment. When it's done, when the sun comes up — someone tell me what I did. All of it. Even the shameful parts. I'd rather have my sins returned to me than live another clean and hollow day."

Tamsin stepped forward before Emrys could speak. "The guild will remember you," she said. "Properly, this time. Written down, witnessed, kept. That's the only magic we've ever actually had, madam — the keeping. We'll keep you."

Something crossed the old queen's face that might have been the first new memory she'd gained in sixty years.

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued; Emrys had planned two hours for it and it ran to four, and it was the best four hours of her life, though she didn't know that yet.

The clerks objected to the route — the storm would shatter the orangery glass, so the non-combatants had to be moved to the undercroft, and Tamsin drew the warehouse-door sequence in chalk on the marble until the dowager's dead garden sprouted a second, invisible geography of wrong seasons. Sable drilled the guards' likely approach with a saltcellar for the Regent and a bread roll for the vanguard, and corrected herself twice, grimly, when the bread roll did something no living guard could do. Aldous insisted on walking his own route to the dais, alone, practicing the steps his body had forgotten how to take without the crown's borrowed warmth, stumbling, rising, stumbling again, refusing help with a courtesy that broke everyone's heart and mended it in the same motion.

And through all of it, Emrys kept the ledger. Open on the table, in plain view, because that had become the whole point of her.

*Consent the first: Emrys Zhou, storm-caller. Given freely.*
*Consent the second: Maren, dowager queen, blood of Vell. Given freely.*
*Consent the third: Aldous, ninth king of the sigil. Given freely.*
*Consent the fourth:*

Her pen hovered.

Around her, the room had gone quiet. The clerks stood in their ragged line. The dowager sat with her empty hands folded. The king leaned against the wall, breathing hard from practice, smiling his newly-human smile. And Sable stood where she had stood all night, at the door, guarding it out of habit, waiting to sign away her survival with the same steadiness she brought to everything.

Emrys dipped the pen. Wrote the name. Blotted it. Read it aloud, so the room — so the *book* — could hear it given freely:

"Sable of Carreth, oathbroken, oath remade. Shield at the door. Given freely."

The Codex's pages stirred, though there was no wind. Beneath the four entries, in fresh ink, a single line surfaced, and Emrys felt it land in her chest like a hook finding flesh:

*All four consents given. The rite is possible. The rite begins at moonset.*

She looked up. Through the frosted glass roof, through the dead roses and the dying year, the moon was already sliding down toward the rooftops of Carreth — and below it, at the far end of the garden, at the edge of her hearing, a door she had locked and chalked and triple-sealed swung softly open on its own.

From the dark beyond it came the sound of footsteps. Measured. Patient. Wrong.

The Regent had found the garden first.
