## 29. The Longest Night Begins

Emrys knew the sun was not going to rise because she had checked the Codex at midnight, and the Codex had told her so in her own handwriting.

That was the worst of it. Not the darkness pressing against the frost-furred windows of the tower room — she had lived through dark winters before, in Vell, where the fog came off the harbor thick as wool and swallowed the lamps by four o'clock. No, the worst was turning the page and finding *The sun will not rise* written in a hand that trembled exactly the way hers trembled when she recorded debts she knew could never be collected.

The book had stopped writing ahead of her days sometime around the solstice's approach. Now it wrote with her, or she wrote with it; she had honestly lost track of which. She dipped her pen, and the ink moved on its own, and somewhere below them all, under the palace, in the deep cells where Sable was still recovering from the Regent's interrogation, a woman with a broken oath was sharpening a sword that had no business being sharp again.

*One hour past midnight,* Emrys wrote. *The sigil feeds.*

She set the pen down and went to the window.

Carreth lay beneath her like a city holding its breath. The snow had stopped falling upward an hour ago — that had been the first sign, Tamsin said, when the clerks' smuggled messages reached her through the impossible doors: *the snow's gone strange, Wren, it's climbing back into the sky like it's ashamed.* Then the river Vane had frozen mid-current, white water caught in blue glass, boats locked in place with their sails full of nothing. Then the bells of the Dawn Chapel, which rang at sunrise without human hands, had rung at midnight instead — twelve cracked notes that everyone in the city pretended not to hear.

And now: silence. The particular silence of a world waiting to see if morning would come.

Emrys pressed her palm flat against the windowpane. The cold bit through her skin and found the place where her storm-gift lived, low behind her ribs, a coiled grey thing she had spent ten years trying to starve and three months learning to love. It stirred against her hand like a cat recognizing footsteps.

"Not yet," she whispered to it. "Not until I say."

It settled. Obedient, for now. The rite demanded everything — she had read the unbinding eleven times, had copied it out in her own fair clerk's hand, had checked the arithmetic of sacrifice the way she checked ledgers, column by column, hoping to find an error. There was no error. A storm-caller gives up the storm. All of it. The sigil tears free or the caller dies empty; either way, the gift is spent down to the last thunderhead.

She had not told anyone about the second clause. The one the Codex kept trying to soften, offering her versions — she could almost hear the book purring — versions where she kept a little, just a little, just one small storm saved in a bottle for a rainy day—

"Liar," Emrys told the Codex.

From its shelf, the old book rustled its pages like a man clearing his throat.

Footsteps on the stair. She knew the tread — uneven on the left, from a knee the Regent's interrogators had not been gentle with. The door opened without a knock, because Sable had stopped knocking three weeks ago, and Emrys had decided that this was one of the things she loved about her.

"You're not sleeping," Sable said.

"You're limping worse than yesterday."

"The knee and I have an arrangement." Sable crossed the room and stood beside her at the window, close enough that their shoulders touched. Below, Carreth glittered — every window lit, every candle burned. Nobody was sleeping tonight. A whole capital city keeping vigil against the dark, and most of them didn't even know what they were waiting up for. "Tamsin says the doors are ready. All seven. She's mapped them twice."

"Tamsin maps everything twice. She learned it from me."

"She learned stubbornness from you too. Gods help the guild." Sable's breath fogged the glass. In the reflection, Emrys watched her face — the scar through the eyebrow, the jaw set the way it always set before a fight she expected to lose. "Aldous is asking for you."

Emrys turned. "Now? At this hour?"

"He surfaced an hour ago and hasn't sunk again. The dowager thinks—" Sable paused, and Emrys saw her choose honesty over comfort, the way she'd been choosing it more and more, like a muscle growing stronger with use. "The dowager thinks the crown is letting him stay above water deliberately. Letting him see the last night. Letting him feel it end. Cruelty as craftsmanship."

"Or mercy," Emrys said quietly. "Maybe it wants him awake so he can watch himself be finished."

"That's not mercy."

"No," Emrys agreed. "But it's the kind of thing a century-old hunger would tell itself."

They stood together in the cold window-light, two exiles watching a city they had no country's right to care about, and Emrys thought: *this is what belonging looks like. Not a guild seal. Not a pardon. Just someone standing next to you at a window at the end of the world, complaining about your sleep.*

"Come on," Sable said. "Before he goes under. Before the crown takes him back."

* * *

Sable counted doors on the way down. It was a habit from her knighthood — count the exits, count the blades, count the men — except now she counted seasons. The corridor outside Emrys's tower opened onto autumn, gold leaves drifting through torchlight that belonged to some other castle entirely. The servants' stair breathed summer heat and the smell of cut grass. The great hall's side door exhaled spring, wet earth and blossom, and the guards stationed there — the loyal ones, the ones Aldous had surfaced long enough to dismiss from duty — stood with their visors up, letting the impossible air wash over them like a blessing.

Seven doors between the tower and the throne room. Seven wrong seasons standing open in a palace buried in ice. Tamsin and the clerks had worked three nights without sleep to hold them, chalk circles and ledger-lines and bottled moments pried from the guild's oldest stock, and Sable had watched a sixteen-year-old apprentice argue with a forty-year warehouse door like a carpenter arguing with a warped board.

The guild's magic, turned inside out. Corvane had built his empire on doors that opened onto anywhere; his own clerks were using them to smuggle hope into a dying court. Sable wished she could hate the man cleanly. But she had seen him in the courtyard yesterday, escorted in chains of his own accounting — every entry in his true ledger read aloud by Tamsin in a voice that never shook — and what she'd felt wasn't hatred. It was the pity you feel watching a man drown in coins.

"Aldous first," Emrys said beside her. "Then the dowager. Then the assembly."

"'Assembly of the unwilling,'" Sable quoted. "You have a talent for naming things."

"I keep ledgers. Naming is half the work." Emrys glanced sideways at her. "You could still leave, you know. The doors go everywhere. Vell is four turns away. You could be on salt water by dawn."

"I could," Sable said.

They walked on through the smell of summer grass, and Sable did not explain, because there was nothing left to explain. She had spent years believing her exile was a debt she owed — that she had broken her oath to the old queen and therefore owed the world a lifetime of penance, walking borders, guarding prisoners, doing penance's grim arithmetic. But penance was just another kind of obedience, and obedience had been the crown's favorite food for a hundred years.

Staying was different. Staying was hers.

The throne room doors stood open, flanked by two clerks with chalk-dusted fingers. Beyond them, the hall blazed with every candle the palace owned, and on the dais, alone except for the dowager sitting quietly at the foot of the steps, King Aldous sat upright on his throne with his hands flat on his knees like a boy reciting lessons.

He looked *terrible*. And he looked, for the first time in the month Sable had known him, entirely present.

"Wren," he said, and his voice was rough, unused, a well gone dry. "Oathbreaker. Come up here where I can see you properly. The light's poor at this distance and I don't trust my eyes anymore."

They climbed the steps. Up close, Sable could see what the crown was doing to him — the silver threads of the sigil-crown sunk into his temples like roots into soil, the faint shimmer along his jaw where his skin wasn't quite sure it wanted to remain skin. But his eyes were his own. Brown, tired, furious.

"How long?" he asked.

"Since you surfaced?" Emrys said gently.

"Since I have left. Since I am myself. How long do I have before it pulls me under for good?"

Emrys looked at Sable. Sable looked at the floor. Neither of them lied to kings who asked honest questions anymore; it had become something like policy between them.

"Dawn," Emrys said. "If we succeed, you wake tomorrow as a man. If we fail—"

"If you fail, there is no tomorrow for anyone, so the question resolves itself." He smiled, and it was a real smile, crooked and human, and Sable felt her chest ache with how much it cost him. "Good. I've hated not knowing. Do you know what it's like, being a guest in your own skull? There are others in there. Kings. Nine of them that I've counted, though I think there are more, deeper down. We sit in the dark together and we scream politely, because screaming impolitely only feeds it." His hands tightened on his knees. "I want you to understand what you're buying with whatever this costs you. Not my gratitude. Gratitude is cheap. I'm telling you: there are nine dead kings in this crown who have waited a century for someone to open the door. Whatever you tear loose tomorrow night — tonight — let them out. Promise me."

"We promise," Emrys said, before Sable could.

"And the price." Aldous leaned forward, and the sigil flickered, and for a moment his face went smooth and pleasant and *wrong*, and then he wrestled himself back, visibly, like a swimmer fighting current. "The girl knows the price. Does the knight?"

"I know enough," Sable said.

"You know she means to give up her storm." The king's voice dropped. "Do you know what that makes her, afterward? I was ordinary once. Before the crown. Ordinary is not nothing, but it is a grief, and grief needs tending, and I will not have her tended by someone who came out of duty and stays out of guilt. So: knight. Are you staying because of the oath you broke, or despite it?"

The question landed in Sable like a blade going home into its sheath.

"Despite it," she said. "There's no oath left. I burned it. What's left is mine."

Aldous studied her for a long moment, king's eyes in a drowning man's face, and then he nodded once, satisfied, and sat back.

"Then we're ready," he said. "Gods forgive us all the arrogance of that word. Go. Gather your unwilling assembly. And Wren—" his voice caught, "—when it speaks to you tonight, and it will speak, it will offer you everything you ever lost — remember that I offered you nothing. Remember which of us respected you enough to tell the truth about the cost."

* * *

The dowager queen's garden existed in a pocket of the palace where the winter had never quite managed to take hold — an old binding of the old queen's, Sable suspected, though the dowager claimed it was merely good masonry and better luck. Roses gone woody with age still clung to trellises. Frost ferned across the glass roof but did not enter. In the middle of the longest night in a century, it was the only green thing left in Carreth.

The assembly gathered among the dead roses: Emrys with the Codex strapped to her back like a weapon; Tamsin and six clerks smelling of chalk and candle smoke; the dowager queen, small and silver and steady; and Sable, standing at the garden door with her reforged oath humming in her blood — not an oath to any crown now, but the private one, the one she'd written herself: *hold the door.*

"Read it aloud," the dowager said. "All of it. Every clause. If we consent in ignorance, our consent is worthless, and worthless consent is exactly what the sigil has fed on for a hundred years. Make us all honest, child, or send us to bed."

So Emrys read it. The unbinding entire, in the Codex's oldest voice and her own newest: the storm-caller who surrenders the storm; the kings released whether they wish it or not; the risk that the sigil, torn, might take the caller's life as change; the risk that the city's winter, suddenly unfed, might collapse inward like a breath held too long finally released — avalanche, flood, a thaw too fast for frozen ground. Each clause laid out like a line item. Each cost entered in its proper column.

Silence followed, the good kind, the kind where people are actually deciding.

"I consented to this war fifty years ago," the dowager said at last, "when I bound the sigil tighter rather than tearing it out, because I was a coward and my husband was dying and I told myself a rescue could not be a villain." Her voice did not waver. "I consent to the unbinding. And I consent to remembering, afterward, exactly what I did. That is my price — let me keep the guilt. It's the last honest thing I own."

"Consented," said Tamsin, and the clerks after her, one by one, six voices, then seven, and the youngest — a boy called Pell who still had ink on his chin — said it loudest.

Sable spoke last. "You all know mine. Door's mine to hold. I consent." She looked at Emrys. "Ask me anyway."

"Why?" Emrys asked, and Sable understood the question was not *why do you fight*, but *why does your consent matter when you've already given everything*.

"Because you asked the king to be honest about the cost. I want to hear you ask me."

Emrys held her gaze across the dead roses. "Sable of the broken oath. The cost of the door is possibly your life. Consent?"

"Consent," Sable said, "freely, and would again."

* * *

Outside, the sky began to do something no sky should do.

Emrys felt it first — the storm-gift rising off her ribs like a dog scenting smoke — and then Tamsin gasped, and then they were all running for the glass roof, and beyond it, above the frozen spires of Carreth, the stars were going out.

Not covered by cloud. Going out. One by one, in order, like candles pinched by a careful hand, from the east toward the zenith, a tide of perfect black rolling across heaven where blackness ought already to be. Where the stars vanished, the dark was not empty. It was *full*. It had texture. It had appetite.

"It's eating the night itself," the dowager whispered. "Making room."

"Room for what?" Pell asked, young enough to need the answer.

"For a century," Emrys said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm, clerk-calm, the voice she used for totals. "The bargain ends at dawn. A hundred years of feeding, complete. After that, the crown doesn't need the kingdom alive. Only worn."

The last stars died. Somewhere across the city, the Dawn Chapel bell rang a single note — cracked, hopeless, hours early — and did not ring again.

In the absolute dark, Emrys felt the Codex warm against her spine, and heard — not with her ears, but in the place where the storm lived — the sigil begin, very softly, to speak.

*"Wren,"* it said, in a voice made of nine kings and a hundred winters. *"Let us talk about what you're owed."*

She gripped Sable's hand once, hard, and let go.

"Took the words right out of my mouth," Emrys said, and stepped toward the longest night.
