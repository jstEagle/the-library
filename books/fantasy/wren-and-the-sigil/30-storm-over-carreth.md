## 30. Storm Over Carreth

The sky over Carreth had been dying all day, and by the hour before dawn it had finished.

Emrys stood at the top of the Codex tower with her palms pressed flat against a window that had frosted from the inside out, and watched the sun fail. It was not an eclipse. She had seen eclipses — they were tidy things, orderly, the moon crossing the sun like a clerk's seal on a page. This was different. The light simply thinned, the way ink thins when a forger stretches it too far, until the horizon was a bruise-colored smear and the stars came out at what should have been morning, burning hard and cold and wrong above a city that had stopped believing in warmth.

Below her, Carreth lay silent under snow that had fallen upward an hour ago and now hung in the air, suspended, ten thousand flakes waiting without instruction. The river was a white scar through the middle of the city, frozen mid-current, its last wave preserved like something caught mid-sentence.

*The unbinding must happen before dawn,* the Codex had said, in ink that was still wet. *Or the crown completes its century.*

Dawn was not coming.

"Then we make our own weather," Emrys said to the glass, and her breath fogged it, and behind her in the dark of the tower room, Tamsin looked up from the ledger she was copying and went very still.

"You're certain," Tamsin said. Not a question, really. A last door left open, in case Emrys wanted to walk through it instead.

Emrys turned. The tower room was lit by three candles and one impossible thing: the guild's warehouse key, which Tamsin had brought up from the undercroft, hanging on a nail and glowing faintly the color of a door opening somewhere else. The Codex lay open on the table between them, its pages turning themselves now, slowly, as if breathing. On the page it had stopped at, the old prophecy had rewritten itself one final time, and the words were simple enough to hurt:

*The storm returns of its own will or is called by its own name.*

Her own name. Her own storm. The one that had taken the harbor town of Saltmere apart eleven years ago — the masts, the seawall, the fishing fleet, forty-one lives, none of which she had asked the sea for. She had sworn then, kneeling in the wreckage with salt in her mouth and someone's child's shoe in her hand, that she would never call anything again. That the gift was a wound dressed up as a talent. That silence was the only honest use for her.

She had built her whole exile on that vow. Ledgers didn't call storms. Ledgers sat in warm rooms and made columns balance, and if you were precise enough, careful enough, small enough, nothing would ever again come out of you that could kill.

"Tamsin," she said, "if I do this wrong, I don't just lose the rite. I lose everything I am. The gift goes into the sigil and it doesn't come back."

"I know." Tamsin closed her copybook. She was nineteen, and she had been Emrys's apprentice since she was twelve, back when Emrys still believed the guild was a family and Corvane was a father of sorts. She had smuggled the true ledger past the Regent's spies with it sewn into her coat lining. Her hands, Emrys noticed, were not shaking. "I did the arithmetic. Twice. Like you taught me."

"And?"

"And there isn't another column." Tamsin's voice cracked on the last word and she pushed through it. "There's just this one. So stop checking my work and go be a storm."

Emrys laughed — a short, broken sound, surprised out of her — and crossed the room and took the girl's face in both hands and kissed her forehead, the way she had done on Tamsin's first day, when the child had been so frightened of the bottled memories that she'd refused to shelve them until Emrys stood beside her the whole afternoon.

"If I'm not back by whatever passes for dawn," Emrys said, "finish the ledger. All of it. Every entry. Even mine."

"Especially yours," Tamsin said. "You always told me the record matters more than the person keeping it."

"The record matters because of the person keeping it," Emrys corrected. "That was the other lesson. You never listened."

"I listened fine. You just changed your mind about it halfway through your life." Tamsin stepped back, wiped her eyes with her sleeve, businesslike again. "Go."

* * *

The dowager queen was waiting at the base of the tower, wrapped in a fur so old its dye had faded to the gray of her hair, leaning on a cane carved with the sigil's original binding-marks. Beside her, moving stiffly, blinking in the starlight like a man remembering how eyes worked, walked King Aldous.

He looked smaller than he had at court. Without the crown — they had gotten him out of the throne room during the first chaos of the night, Sable holding the doors while the clerks dragged the king bodily through a warehouse door that opened onto spring — his head seemed oddly bare, a man-shaped absence. But his eyes were his own. They had been his own for six hours now, and every hour he spent lucid was a gift the sigil could not take back, and he knew it, and he was spending them recklessly, the way poor men spend found money.

"You're really going to call it," Aldous said. His voice was rough. A century of kings had been screaming politely inside his skull for three years, and even freed, his throat hadn't recovered. "The storm."

"I'm going to call the storm," Emrys said. "Not *it*. Mine. There's a difference. It has to believe I mean it."

"And do you?" the dowager asked quietly. "Mean it?"

Emrys looked at her. Maren, they called her, when anyone remembered to; she had bound the sigil to the royal line sixty years ago to save a dying husband, and had paid for it since in memories, feeding it piece after piece of her own past until she could no longer recall her wedding day or her son's laugh but could recite the entire binding-rite backward. Guilt had kept her alive when the sigil would have preferred her dead. Guilt was a stubborn currency.

"No," Emrys said honestly. "I don't mean it the way it wants me to. But I mean *this*: I want it back. Every terrible inch of it. And wanting is enough to call with, if you're angry enough." She pulled her gloves off finger by finger. Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. "It ruined my life. It's owed the chance to save one."

Aldous reached out and gripped her shoulder with a king's old instinct and a drowning man's strength. "When it's out of me," he said, "whatever's left — I'll account for it. Every year. Every hunger. I swear it on what's left of my name."

"Don't swear," Emrys said. "Oaths are how this whole mess started. Just live. That'll be accounting enough."

She walked out onto the tower's open balcony.

* * *

The cold hit her like a wall she had to climb. Below, the palace courtyards were filling — not with armies, but with what the alliance had managed to gather: two dozen guild clerks in mismatched coats, armed with warehouse keys and inventory hooks; the dowager's handful of remaining household guards, loyal to a woman history had mislaid; and at the great hall's doors, alone, upright, armor scorched from the last fight, Sable.

Sable looked up. Of course she looked up. She had a knight's habit of knowing where the people she loved were standing, and Emrys felt that attention land on her like sunlight, thin and freezing and absolutely real. Neither of them had said the word yet. They had traded everything around it instead — the old queen, the storm, the fear of being nothing — and the word sat between them now like a coin neither dared spend, though both had already agreed on its value.

Sable raised one fist to her breastplate. The oathbreaker's gesture, reforged: not *I swear*, but *I choose*. Then she turned back to the doors, sword drawn, and became a wall.

*Right,* Emrys thought. *No more delays. The book is watching. Make your entry.*

She spread her arms. She opened the place inside herself she had kept shut for eleven years — the locked room, the sealed ledger, the vault where she had buried the storm along with the girl who'd accidentally been it — and she broke her own seal.

Nothing happened.

For three heartbeats, four, five, the night stayed dead and the air stayed empty and Emrys stood on a frozen balcony with her arms out like a fool, calling into a silence that did not care. Panic rose in her, the old panic, the Saltmere panic — *it won't come, it's gone, you killed it, you deserve to have lost it* —

And then she stopped calling politely.

"**I know you're there.**"

She didn't say it aloud. She said it the way storms are said, down in the pressure of the blood, in the language she had spoken before she could walk, the one her mother had begged her to hide and her exile had forced her to bury.

"**Eleven years. You've been circling me for eleven years — I've felt you over every ocean I've sailed, every winter I've wintered. You think I didn't know? You're mine. You were born out of me on the worst night of my life, and you've been homesick ever since, and so have I, and I am DONE being ashamed of the only wild thing I ever made.**"

The suspended snow trembled.

"**Come home.**"

The sky answered.

It did not build gradually. There was no gathering wind, no thickening cloud. One moment the stars were fixed and indifferent; the next, the whole dome of the night tore open like wet paper, and the storm poured through the tear as if it had been standing on the other side for a decade with its hand raised, waiting to be called on. Clouds boiled out of clear black sky. Pressure dropped so fast that windows shattered outward across three districts, sucked toward her, and the temperature — impossibly, wonderfully — *rose*, five degrees, ten, the first thaw Carreth had felt in a hundred days, because the storm carried its own climate with it, salt and rain-warmth and the smell of a harbor town it had destroyed and perhaps regretted.

And it *sang*. Low, at the edge of hearing, a note Emrys felt in her sternum and her teeth and the roots of her hair — a note she recognized. It was the sound the sea had made the night of Saltmere, right before. Her storm had kept the sound. Had kept everything. Had waited, all these years, to be understood rather than condemned.

"Oh," Emrys whispered, tears freezing on her cheeks. "Oh, you *poor thing.* Come here."

Lightning struck the tower. It did not hurt. It picked her up the way a parent picks up a child who has run too far ahead, and the world dissolved into wind.

* * *

Riding a storm through a palace is not like flying. It is like being the argument a hurricane makes with architecture.

Emrys came down through the roof of the west wing in a column of rain that fell *upward* as it passed her, because the storm bent its rules around her the way a horse bends its gait around a rider it trusts. Thunder went ahead of her, announcing her down corridors that had not heard weather in a century — the sigil had kept the palace's skies scrubbed and dead, a taxidermied heaven — and the sound of real thunder in those halls woke something in the stone itself. Frost retreated from the walls in scorching lines. Torches that had burned blue and joyless flared gold. In the servants' passages, she saw faces appear at doorways — cooks, laundresses, a chamberlain hiding with a candle — staring up at the impossible spectacle of a woman wrapped in lightning walking on wind through the royal residence, and she heard someone whisper, in awe and terror, "*the storm-caller*, the prophecy's true—" and she had no time to correct the theology.

The Regent's creatures came for her in the long gallery.

They had been men once — the sigil's chief servant hollowed them the way he himself had been hollowed, the first king the crown ever wore, animated by borrowed will. Now they moved wrong, joints bending at angles grief had never taught the human body, and they poured out of side doors in silence, blades drawn, and the storm met them like a dog meeting wolves at its master's heels.

Wind took the first three off their feet and pinned them to the gallery wall with a gentleness that was somehow worse than violence. Rain flooded the floorboards. Lightning wrote jagged signatures across the ceiling. Emrys did not kill them — she could not make herself, not tonight, not when she knew now what the Regent was and what waited inside the hollowed ones, screaming politely — but she buried them, ankle-deep, knee-deep, in a sudden growth of hail that hardened like iron, and left them shouting muffled curses behind her.

"*Faster,*" she gasped, and the storm, reading her intent straight out of her bloodstream, obeyed.

They went through the great hall together, rider and weather, a single creature with two intentions.

The throne room doors were closed, and against them, bleeding from a cut above her eye, sword nicked to the quick, stood Sable — holding alone, as she had promised to hold, against the Regent and the last eight of the crown's guards. The Regent himself stood back from the fight, hands folded, watching with the patient expression of a man who has been dead for a very long time and finds everything mildly interesting. Behind him, on the dais, the sigil-crown hovered above the empty throne, spinning slowly, drinking the failing light of the longest night, growing brighter as dawn failed to come.

Sable saw Emrys and something in her face let go of its last reserve — relief, ferocious and undisguised.

"Wren!" she shouted, using the name the clerks had given her, the small honest name, over the thunder. "The dais! I can't—"

"I know!" Emrys hurled the storm forward like a thrown cloak. It went over Sable's head — curving, deliberately, a hand shielding a candleflame — and crashed into the guards and the Regent in a wave of pressure that drove them back across the marble, skidding, the Regent's folded hands finally, *finally* unfolding in something that might have been alarm.

Emrys landed on the dais steps in a ring of lightning, facing the crown.

Up close, it was worse than any description. It was beautiful the way a debt trap is beautiful — filigree of a metal that did not exist in nature, set with stones that were not stones but condensed moments, a century of bargains crystallized. Inside its spinning, she could see faces. Kings. Dozens of them, layered like drowned things under ice, mouths open, eyes aware. The oldest face, deepest down, wore the same patient expression as the Regent, because it *was* the Regent, before he'd been poured out into a body and left standing.

The crown stopped spinning.

It looked at her.

And the storm around Emrys — her storm, her wild rescued child — suddenly *hesitated*, buffeted by a will older than hers, and Emrys felt the temperature of the moment change, felt the sigil begin, in a voice made of every king it had ever worn speaking in perfect unison, to speak—

"—Emrys Zhou," said a century of dead kings, warmly, reasonably, in the tone of an offer one would be foolish to refuse. "At last. We have been keeping your seat warm—"

Behind her, Sable slammed the throne room doors and dropped the bar, buying seconds with blood. Ahead of her, the crown opened its bargain like a merchant opening a ledger.

Emrys stepped forward anyway, rain streaming off her, her storm roaring around her shoulders like a mantle she had finally, after eleven years, earned the right to wear.

"Let's talk terms," she said. "But understand one thing first. I've read your handwriting. And I don't sign what I haven't audited."

The crown's light flickered — with anger, or with laughter. On the longest night of a hundred years, with dawn refusing to come and her friend bleeding at the door and her whole stolen self gathered in her fists, Emrys of Vell, exiled storm-caller, guild clerk, Wren, walked up the last steps to meet the price of everything.
