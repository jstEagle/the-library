## 31. The Crown's Bargain

The throne room of Carreth had been built to make a person feel small, and it was very good at its job.

Emrys crossed the snow court with the Codex clutched against her ribs, its pages fluttering though there was no wind inside — there was never wind inside anymore, only the sigil's slow breathing made of cold. Ice sheeted the windows so thickly that daylight came through green and drowned, like looking up from underwater at a sun that had given up trying. The long winter's longest night, and the palace had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Behind her, somewhere back through the frozen halls, Tamsin's footsteps were still echoing — then not echoing, swallowed by doors and distance and the storm Emrys had ridden in through. Ahead, on the dais beneath the canopy of white bear-fur and older darkness, King Aldous sat upon the Ironwinter Throne.

He was not alone.

She saw it as she came closer, and her heart did the terrible thing hearts do when horror arrives wearing beauty: it stilled, admiring. The air above and around the king shimmered with figures — faint as breath on glass, layered like pages held to light. Kings. A dozen kings, perhaps more, standing in the same place at slightly different angles, wearing crowns from different centuries, their faces blurred where the sigil had worn them smooth. They stood in ranks that overlapped, translucent hands resting on translucent hilts, and every one of them was smiling.

Aldous was not smiling. Aldous, in the brief lucidity he had begged from her two nights past, had wept and said *tear it off me, please, whatever it costs, tear it off* — and now he sat rigid beneath the crown of black metal and silver script, his eyes rolling like a man listening to something dreadful said very politely.

"Emrys Zhou," said a voice.

It came from all of them at once. From the dead kings and the living one, from the ice on the windows, from the Codex warm against her ribs — warm, always warm now, like a living thing pressed to her side. The voice was a chord: many throats, one intention. It was gentle. That was the worst of it. It sounded like an accountant reading favorable terms aloud.

"You have come far," the sigil said. "Through wrong seasons. Through our Regent's poor hospitality. Through a storm you were told you could not call." A pause, delicately measured. "We watched you do it. It was magnificent."

Emrys stopped at the base of the dais steps. Her boots had left wet prints on the marble, and the prints were already frosting over, reaching toward her heels like slow fingers. She made herself breathe the way she did over a difficult ledger entry: in through the nose, out slow, numbers first, feelings later.

"I've read your terms," she said. "In a hundred-year-old book. They're not favorable."

"The book is honest," the kings said together, and some of them — she heard it — some of them grieved as they said it. "That has always been our cruelty and our courtesy. We never lie, Emrys Zhou. We only wait for people to lie to themselves."

She climbed the steps. One. Two. The cold coming off the throne was not weather-cold; it was the cold of an empty room in an empty house, the cold of things that had stopped being used. Three. Four. Aldous's eyes found hers and for one flickering instant they were his own — terrified, human, *hurry* — and then the shine slid over them again like water closing over a stone.

Five steps. She stood level with the dead.

Up close, the kings resolved into individuals. The nearest wore a beard braided in the old Salt-King fashion, Vell style, three centuries out of date. Behind him a young king no older than twenty, freckles ghosting through his translucence. A queen among them, tall and stern, her crown a circlet of iron thorns. Their smiles were identical and their eyes were not; the eyes were each in their own particular hell, and the smiles were not theirs at all. They were the sigil's smile, worn by a century of heads.

"We know what you have lost," the chord said. "May we show you what we can return?"

* * *

The throne room went away.

Not gradually — it simply stopped being insisted upon, the way a ledger line stops mattering once it's reconciled. She stood instead on the salt road outside Vell, in autumn, in the amber light of a late afternoon she remembered with her whole body. Gulls hung motionless over the harbor roofs. Somewhere downslope, someone was frying fish, and the smell arrived on the wind exactly as it had arrived eleven years ago, oil and salt and home.

Her exile. The day of it. She turned in a slow circle and there was the town gate, and there was the crowd, and there — her breath caught — there was *she*: a girl of seventeen, thin and wild-haired, walking out of Vell with one bag and no farewell, while the townspeople watched from their doorways with that particular mixture of pity and relief that exiles are carried out under.

"This is memory," Emrys said hoarsely. "You're showing me memory."

"We are showing you possibility." The kings stood behind her now, all of them, ranked on the salt road in the amber light, casting no shadows. "The storm you were exiled for was ours. You know this now. Called by us, hunting you across a country, arriving too early and too hungry. The town blamed you. Your own mother signed the warrant." A pause, soft as a page turning. "One word from us, entered into the record of the world, and that day unwrites itself. The storm never comes. You are never needed anywhere but home."

And because she was made of ledgers, Emrys looked closely. The illusion — no, the *offer* — was flawless. The gulls. The fish smoke. The crooked shutter on the cooper's house that her father had been meaning to fix the summer before he died. Even the light on the water was right, that heavy gold that only happened in Vell in October, and only for a week.

"You can't unwrite anything," she said. "You rewrite. I've seen your revisions. Chapter nineteen of your book wrote my next four days before I lived them, and got three of them wrong, because the world refuses to hold still for even you."

"Close, then," the kings conceded, and the concession was more frightening than a boast would have been. "Close enough. Is closeness worth nothing? We are prepared to offer much, and we begin honestly: here is what you want most."

The salt road dissolved and re-formed as the guild warehouse in Vell, the great impossible interior with its thousand aisles and its doors onto wrong seasons. And the warehouse was *full*. Clerks at every desk, lamps lit, ledgers open, the smell of ink and tallow and the sea coming under the doors. Tamsin was there, laughing at something, ink on her fingers — Tamsin, who had chosen her. Corvane's chair at the head table stood empty, and everyone seemed lighter for it.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," the kings said, "remade and restored, with you at its head. Master Emrys. Every clerk who ever walked out returns. Every debt owed you is paid. The trade continues — bottled memories, unmade vows, the contraband of fate — but honest now, because you would make it honest, because we have watched you for eleven years and we know what you are. You would run it better than it has ever been run."

"I don't want to run it," Emrys said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated that, hated being *moved*, hated that the sight of Tamsin laughing had done what the Regent's torturers could not do to her composure.

"No," the sigil agreed gently. "You want to belong to it. That is different, and rarer, and harder to buy. Fortunately we can afford hard things."

* * *

The third vision was the cruelest, and she knew it would be, and she braced anyway, and it did no good at all.

Storm.

Not the gray murder that had ruined her — this was the storm of her childhood, the one she had loved before she learned to fear her own hands. Lightning walked the horizon of an inland sea like a god taking a stroll. Thunder rolled not as threat but as conversation, and she could feel it in her teeth, in her sternum, in the old places behind her eyes where the gift had lived — and *oh*, it was there again, she could feel it, the pressure and the promise, the thing that had been scraped out of her leaving a hollow that ached worse than any wound she'd taken in the palace. For one shining moment she was seventeen and whole and the sky answered her when she called.

"Yours," said the kings, all around her, within the storm, *of* the storm. "Kept. Not spent on us, not poured into a rite, not traded for a stranger's soul. You have read the unbinding. You know what it asks. The storm for the crown — your sky for his sanity. We merely point out the arithmetic. One aging king, already half-consumed, against everything you are. Let the winter complete itself. Let the century close. Sign, and keep the sky."

The Codex against her ribs grew hot. She drew it out with numb hands and it opened itself across her palms, pages riffling to a fresh leaf, and on the leaf, in ink that gleamed like the sigil's script, words were writing themselves in a fair, patient hand:

*A covenant between the Sigil-Crown of Carreth and Emrys Zhou, called Wren, called Storm-Caller, called Chosen.*

Below it, a single line awaited her signature, and below that, itemized in the tidy format she used herself every working day, the terms:

*Item: the exile of Emrys Zhou, undone and expunged.*
*Item: the Guild of Impossible Things, restored, chartered, and hers.*
*Item: the storm-gift, retained entire, forever.*
*Item: the price — one signature. Nothing more. The winter completes. The king sleeps. No one is harmed who was not already being spent.*

"No one is harmed," Emrys repeated. "Except him."

"Aldous of Carreth," the kings said, and now she heard it clearly — the grief underneath, real grief, a hundred captives mourning in unison, *we were kings and we know what we ask* — "has been dying for nine years. We have worn him as a coat is worn. His lucid hours grow fewer. He begged you himself to tear us free; he begs you now, silently, behind our smile. But hear us, Emrys Zhou, and mark that we do not lie: if you refuse, he dies anyway. If you accept, he dies anyway. Only the timing differs, and the manner, and whether your hands are empty or full when it happens."

"That's not true," she whispered.

"It is entirely true." And it was; that was the trap's perfection; she checked the logic three times and could find no flaw. Aldous was beyond saving regardless. The unbinding would cost her everything. The offer cost her nothing but a signature and a stranger already lost.

A pen appeared. Of course it did. It appeared in the air before her, floating, offered hilt-first by the ghost of the young freckled king, whose eyes over the smile were screaming.

Emrys took the pen.

She held it the way she held all pens, and the habit of a decade asserted itself, the clerk's discipline stronger than fear or longing: *read the whole document before signing.* Her eyes went down the itemized terms again, and then, slowly, deliberately, to the fine print at the bottom — the line she herself would have written, the line Corvane always buried, the line every honest ledger keeps and every dishonest one omits.

*All other accounts closed. All other futures foreclosed. The book of prophecy, fulfilled, ends.*

Ends. Not blanks itself and goes quiet. *Ends.*

"There it is," she said softly.

"You see it," the kings said, without shame. "Good. We respect a careful reader."

"If I sign, the Codex closes. No more unwritten pages. No more future that isn't already accounted for. Everyone lives inside a finished book forever — a book *you* finished." She lifted her gaze from the page to the ranked dead. "You're not offering me my life back. You're offering me a beautifully bound cage, with my name embossed on the cover, and I almost didn't look at the colophon."

The storm-vision shuddered. The salt road, the warehouse, the lightning — all of it flickered like a lamp in draft, and for the first time something moved through the chord of kings' voices that was not gentleness, not grief, not patience.

Something like hunger.

"Eleven years," the sigil said, and the warmth drained from it syllable by syllable, "we have reached for you. A storm, when you were green. A book, when you were lonely. A guildmaster's greed, a knight's hesitation, a cell with good light, a forged prophecy, a friend in chains. Eleven years, and every net we wove, you cut — or someone cut for you. Do you understand what you are, Emrys Zhou? You are the one account we have never balanced. Sign the ledger. Close us. Let it be *done*."

"No," Emrys said.

The word was small and it was enormous, and she felt it land in the throne room the way a single correct number lands in a column of lies.

She snapped the floating pen in half. Ghost-light spilled from the break like yolk, and the visions died all at once — salt road, storm, warehouse, gone — and she stood again on the dais of the Ironwinter Throne in the drowning green dark, with the Codex slamming itself shut against her chest as if offended, and the century of kings arrayed before her, their smiles fixed, their eyes —

Their eyes were not screaming anymore. In the youngest one, the freckled boy-king, she would have sworn she saw something else. Something like hope, and something like apology, and something like a warning thrown down a stairwell ahead of running feet:

*It will not accept refusal quietly.*

"Then we spend the rest," the sigil said, and Aldous's body rose from the throne without Aldous's consent, and the black crown blazed, and every torch in the throne room guttered toward the walls as if the whole palace had inhaled. "The Regent. The guards. The winter entire. You will sign in the end, little storm. Everything signs, in the end. Everything balances."

"Not me," Emrys said, and reached for the crown.

And behind her, from far down the length of the throne room, from the great double doors she had come through, came the ring of steel on stone — one voice, one blade, one person planting herself between Emrys and everything the palace could send up its frozen stairs.

She knew the stance before she turned. She knew the ragged edge of that broken oath reforged into something sharper than any oath had ever been.

"Sable—" Emrys started.

"Hold the line, Wren," Sable said, drawing her sword the rest of the way out with a sound like the winter cracking, as the doors boomed and the Regent's footsteps began to descend. "I've got the door. You've got the king. *Go.*"
