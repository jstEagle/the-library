## 32. Sable's Stand at the Door

The doors of the throne room were older than the dynasty that had commissioned them — black oak from forests that no longer existed, banded with iron gone blue with age, carved with the faces of kings who had all, Sable now knew, been screaming behind their smiles for a hundred years. She had walked past them a thousand times in her service to the crown. She had never once thought to look at them properly.

She looked at them now, because she was about to die in front of them.

Behind her, through the gap she had left open at her shoulder, she could hear Emrys climbing the dais steps, her boots ringing on the frozen marble, and the sound of Aldous — poor, hollowed Aldous — being helped upright by the dowager's small hands. And beyond all of it, filling the throne room like water fills a hull, the sigil-crown's hunger: a pressure in the ears, a taste of old copper, a cold that did not come from the weather.

In front of her, down the length of the Long Gallery, came the Regent.

He walked the way he always walked, unhurried, his boots making no sound at all on the ice-slicked floor, which was wrong in a way Sable had never permitted herself to name until this winter. A man of flesh made sound. This one glided the way a shadow glides along a wall, and his face — the first king's face, worn thin as vellum over the thing beneath — was arranged into its usual expression of mild, paternal regret.

"Lady Sable." His voice carried without effort, gentle as snowfall. "You are wounded, and tired, and confused. Stand aside. No one needs to know you hesitated."

"I'm not confused," Sable said. "That's the first thing anyone has gotten right about me in eleven years."

She rolled her shoulder and felt the wound there pull and weep — a guard's spear in the corridor above, during Emrys's escape from the Codex chamber, when Tamsin's clerks had come through an impossible door carrying keys and torches and a ledger full of dead girls' names. The blood had soaked through her coat and frozen stiff against her side. It was, she thought, a fair price so far. It would not be the last installment.

The guards came up the gallery behind the Regent in two files, twelve of them, pikes leveled, faces hidden behind visors fogged with their own breath. Sable knew some of them. Hargen, who had a daughter learning the harp. The twins from the river garrison, whose names she'd never learned but whose faces she'd shared mess tables with for three years of border duty. They were not evil men. They were frightened men serving a thing that wore their king, and they believed — because the Regent told them so, gently, reasonably — that they were putting down a rebellion and saving the realm.

She had been that kind of soldier once. She had broken her oath precisely so she would never be that kind again.

"Emrys," she said, without turning around. "Don't listen to any of this. Whatever you hear behind you — steel, screams, my voice saying things I don't say now — keep reading. Keep your hands on the rite."

"You'll die out here," Emrys said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "Sable—"

"That's the plan." Sable drew her sword. "Plans are allowed to be simple. Yours is complicated enough for both of us."

* * *

The Regent stopped ten paces away, exactly where a courteous man would stop to converse, and folded his hands.

"Consider the arithmetic," he said. "Twelve against one. You are bleeding. The door behind you closes only from our side — you saw to that yourself, clever girl — which means you cannot retreat. The unbinding requires time you will not give it. In every version of the next quarter hour except the ones the book has already discarded, you die in this gallery. Why choose the losing shape of the story?"

"Because I've read the book too," Sable said. "And here is what it never understood, in a century of writing itself: it can only record what people do when they're trying to survive. It has no pages for what they do when they've already decided how they want to end." She set her feet. "I broke my oath once because keeping it meant lying. I am about to keep it, or something like it, and this time the words are mine. So let me reforge it properly, in front of witnesses."

She raised the flat of her blade, and though there were only enemies before her, she spoke to the whole room — to the guards, to Aldous shivering on the dais, to the dowager with her ruined memory and her iron spine, to Emrys, who she hoped was not listening, and who she knew was listening anyway.

"I swore to protect the crown of Carreth from all harm," she said. "I unsay that oath. The crown is the harm." Her voice rang off the black doors. "I swore to defend the throne against all enemies. I remake that vow tonight, on my own authority, with no god and no guild and no book to underwrite it: while I stand, nothing crosses this door that means Wren harm. Not kings. Not crowns. Not arithmetic." She lowered the point toward the Regent. "There. Eleven years late, but sworn. Now it's just fighting."

For a moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — the Regent's mild face did something it had perhaps not done in a very long time. It hesitated. Something moved behind the eyes like a man remembering a room he'd lived in as a child, and Sable felt, absurdly, a spike of pity so sharp it almost undid her stance. He had been the first. He had put the crown on his own head believing it a rescue, and it had eaten him down to this: a servant shaped like a king, sent to fetch sacrifices for the thing that had unmade him.

"I am sorry," the Regent said, and meant it, and that was the most horrible thing in the whole frozen palace.

"Guards," he said. "Take the door."

* * *

They came on in the disciplined line of Carreth's household troops, which was to say well, which was to say it nearly killed her at once.

Sable had fought duels and skirmishes and one disastrous ambush in the mountain passes, but she had never held a doorway alone, and she learned in the first exchange that a door changes everything about a fight. A door is narrow. A door cannot be flanked. Twelve men in a corridor become two men in a doorway, and two men in a doorway can be fought by one woman who has nothing left to lose except the minutes she is buying.

So she bought minutes.

She gave ground grudgingly and took it back instantly, working the width of the threshold like a scythe works a field's edge. Pike points splintered against the doorframe; she shortened her grip inside their reach and let their own numbers jam them. A spearhead opened her forearm and she turned the pain into a riposte that took Hargen's pike out of his hands rather than his life, because she could afford mercy in twos and threes but not in twelves. The twins came together, the way twins do, and she caught both their blades on her crossguard and headbutted the nearer one through his visor and felt her own vision go white at the edges.

Blood on the ice. Hers, mostly. That was fine. Blood tells a story too, and hers said: still here.

"Push," the Regent said quietly, from his place far back in the gallery, where the light did not seem to touch him quite correctly. "She tires. Push."

They pushed, and Sable discovered the difference between tiring and ending. Her legs shook. Her sword arm had gone distant, like an arm reported in letters from another country. But behind her she could hear Emrys's voice rising into the cadence of the rite — old Vellish, the accent terrible, the conviction absolute — and underneath it the sigil's pressure changed, the way a storm changes when the barometer falls. The crown was afraid.

A crowned thing, afraid of a clerk with a book. Sable laughed, and the laugh cost her a cut along the jaw, and she paid it gladly.

"You should hear yourselves," she rasped at the guards, spitting red onto the marble. "Twelve of the finest soldiers in the north, holding a hallway for a hat."

"Hold," the Regent said, and this time the word came out of him in the crown's own voice, layered, a chorus wearing one mouth, and the guards faltered — even now, even armored in duty, some animal part of them recognized that the thing commanding them was not their regent. In that faltering half-second Sable killed no one and disabled three: a knee, a wrist, an ankle swept so the big man went down like felled timber.

Then the Regent came forward himself.

* * *

She had wondered, in the long dark weeks of this winter, whether the Regent could fight. She had her answer immediately: he fought the way water fights, without effort, without haste, without anything so human as anger. His sword appeared in his hand from nowhere and arrived where her guard was weakest before her eye could report the movement.

She blocked the first stroke and her arm went numb to the shoulder. She blocked the second and heard bone creak. The third he did not throw; he simply stepped inside her reach, took her sword wrist, and regarded her with those patient, borrowed eyes.

"The queen," he said. "The old queen. You loved her."

It was not a question, and it was not the crown speaking anymore. It was the man beneath — the drowned king, surfacing for one gasp, as Aldous surfaced in his lucid hours. His grip loosened by a degree.

"She was my granddaughter's granddaughter," he whispered. "Or her cousin. The line blurred. I used to count them. I used to—" His face convulsed. "Girl, run. While I hold him. I can hold him for moments only, I have practice at nothing else—"

And Sable, bleeding in five places, holding a door alone against a hollow king and the remnant of the royal guard, did the last brave thing available to her, which was to refuse.

"No," she said. "You hold him. I'll hold the door. We'll each do the thing we're best at." She twisted free of his loosened grip, regained her stance, and set her point at his heart with hands that would barely close. "And when this is over — whatever is left of you after — someone is going to write your true account in a proper ledger. First victim, first entry. Wren keeps honest books now. You'll get your page."

The Regent's face broke — grief and hope and a century of polite screaming all at once — and then the crown clamped down over him like ice closing over a hole in a frozen lake, and the patience returned, and he raised his sword again.

"I am sorry," the thing said, in the crown's voice this time, with no man left inside the apology.

"Everyone keeps saying that tonight," Sable said. "Come and mean it."

* * *

The second phase of the fight lasted, by her later reckoning, four minutes, and she remembered almost none of it in sequence — only flashes, the way storms are remembered afterward. The Regent's blade opening her coat from collar to hem and the cold rushing in like it had been waiting. Hargen back on his feet and refusing, deliberately missing, shouting at the others that this was madness, that this was the Lady Sable, that the king himself had begged the storm-witch to unbind him — and the Regent striking Hargen down with a gesture, casually, the way a man brushes ash from his sleeve, and something in Sable going very quiet and very clear.

Because that was the moment she understood she had already won, whatever happened to her body in the next minute. Hargen falling had converted the fight. Two more guards threw down their pikes outright. The twins stood fast but their points wavered, and the wavering of their points was worth more than her sword arm, which was finished, and her left leg, which had stopped answering messages.

The crown needed obedience. Obedience was dying in this gallery faster than blood could leave her veins.

"Fall back," the Regent ordered, and only three obeyed, and he turned on them with the chorus-voice and the last two guards dropped their weapons and knelt, not to him, but to the fallen Hargen.

Which left the Regent alone, facing a wrecked woman in a doorway, with the unbinding rising behind her like weather.

"You cannot stop it now," the Regent said. There was something new in his voice. She thought, distantly, it might have been relief. "But I can slow it. The book says the sacrifice must witness. If I kill you here, in her sight, the rite may break on grief—"

"Sable!" Emrys's voice, torn raw from the dais. "SABLE!"

"Keep reading!" Sable screamed back, and did not look, because looking was a luxury of people with spare seconds, and she had none. "I said I'd stand! I'm standing!"

The Regent came at her one final time, and Sable Zhou, oathbreaker, reforged, met him with everything left in a body that had been spending itself all night like a reckless clerk emptying an account. She parried once. Twice. On the third stroke she did not parry at all. She stepped inside the arc — the oldest cheat in swordplay, the one every master forbids because it trades flesh for position — and let his blade take her across the ribs rather than through the neck, and in the same motion drove her sword up under the Regent's guard and into his chest.

He looked down at it with something like curiosity.

"It doesn't kill me," he said. "Nothing kills me. Only the crown's death frees—"

"I know," Sable said, through her teeth, through the blood rising in her throat. "I'm not killing you. I'm pinning you to the door."

She put her weight on the hilt and her back against the black oak, and the hollow king hung there, transfixed, immobile, his borrowed strength unable to act against the one thing the crown truly feared: being witnessed, being held, being *accounted for*. His mouth worked. Behind the crown's patience, faintly, the man surfaced one more time.

"Thank you," said the first king of Carreth, in the voice of a boy, and closed his eyes, and went still.

* * *

Sable slid down the door and sat in the spreading red on the frozen marble, and listened.

She listened to the rite crest behind her — Emrys's voice cracking and rising, the dowager chanting the counter-words, Aldous weeping openly on the dais like a man coming up from underwater. She listened to the sigil begin, at last, to scream — not politely anymore, not through a king's smiling mouth, but raw, in a hundred voices at once, a century of stolen monarchs crying out together as the thing that ate them began to die.

She listened to the guards kneeling in the snow around Hargen's body, and the twins quietly taking off their helmets.

And she listened, hardest of all, to the sound of the great doors trembling at her back as something vast poured out of a dying crown and went looking for the dawn.

"Wren," she murmured, because names mattered at the end of oaths, and hers had just been rewritten. "Hurry. I don't want to miss the sun."

Through the high windows of the Long Gallery, far off beyond the frozen city, the sky cracked — a thin seam of gold splitting the endless night from edge to edge — and the whole palace shuddered as the unbinding reached its height, and the doors at Sable's back flew open as if thrown by the hand of the storm itself.
