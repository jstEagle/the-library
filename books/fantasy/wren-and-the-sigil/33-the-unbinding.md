## 33. The Unbinding

The throne room of Carreth had been built to make a person feel small, and Emrys had spent thirty-one years learning never to feel small anywhere. She stood on the dais steps now with her boots planted on frost-slick marble, and she let the architecture do its worst. Let the pillars rise like the throats of giants. Let the vaulted dark swallow every candle she could not see by. She was small, and it did not matter, because what she carried was not.

The storm came in ahead of her.

She felt it the way she had once felt weather in another life — as pressure behind the sternum, a taste of iron and rain at the back of the throat. But this time she had called it *on purpose*, and that changed everything. The storm that had ruined Vell's harbor district nine years ago had been an accident of grief; this one was a decision. It broke over the palace like a wave breaking over a wrecked ship, and through the high windows Emrys watched lightning walk the frozen gardens, and she felt no guilt at all. Only terror. Only readiness.

The Codex lay open on a reading stand someone — Tamsin, probably, in one of her brisk, impossible deliveries — had dragged up onto the dais. The pages turned themselves, restless, the ink crawling like insects disturbed under a stone. Emrys did not look at it. She looked at the king.

Aldous knelt at the center of the dais, exactly where the rite required, and for the first time since she'd been dragged into his court as a "guest of prophecy," he looked like a man and not a portrait of one. The sigil-crown sat on his brow — a thing of black metal worked into shapes that hurt to follow, loops that suggested letters in no alphabet, set with a stone that was not a stone but a depth, a hole wearing the costume of a gem. It pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. Like something that had been feeding for a hundred years and saw no reason to hurry now.

"Emrys." His voice came out cracked, human, small. He had perhaps an hour of himself left; he'd already spent twenty minutes of it telling her about the screaming. "You should know — when it offers you the bargain, and it will —"

"It already did," she said. "Twice. Once through the Codex, once through Corvane's mouth before Tamsin put him in a warehouse full of doors."

"Then you know what it sounds like when it lies." He smiled, and the smile was terrible because it was ordinary, a tired man's smile, a father's smile. "It sounds like everything you ever wanted, itemized."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Emrys said. "I know what a bill looks like."

Behind the great doors at the far end of the hall, something struck steel — a boom that rolled up through the floor and into her knees. Sable. Sable was out there with a reforged oath and a borrowed sword, holding the door against the Regent and every guard the crown still owned, and each boom meant she was still standing, and each boom also meant she was paying for standing in a currency Emrys could not help her count.

*Minutes,* Sable had said, shoving Emrys toward the dais with both hands flat between her shoulder blades, as if Emrys were a door that needed opening. *I'll buy you minutes. Don't you dare spend them being brave at me instead of at the crown.*

Emrys climbed the last step.

The Codex rustled open to the page it had been trying to show her for weeks — the unbinding, written in an ink that shifted color depending on whether you were reading it with hope or with dread. She had read it forty times. She could recite it. *The sigil is torn free only by a storm-caller who pours the storm into the tearing, who gives the gift entire, holding none of it back, for the sigil will keep whatever is withheld and grow fat on it.* Holding nothing back. That was the whole of it. That was the price with its skin off.

Nine years ago she would have hesitated. Nine years ago her gift had been the only part of herself she'd trusted.

She laid her hands on either side of Aldous's head, just above his ears, fingertips brushing the cold rim of the sigil, and the cold went up her arms like water going up a straw.

"Tell me something true," Aldous whispered. His eyes were lagging again, drifting half a heartbeat behind the world. "While I'm still here to hear it."

"The storm wasn't mine," Emrys said. "I never called it. And I've stopped needing anyone to believe me, which took longer than it should have." She bent close, so the words landed somewhere the crown could not drink them out of the air. "And your brother's memory-bottles are in the guild warehouse, third aisle, blue wax, all of them accounted for. I kept the ledger myself."

He made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and then his eyes rolled white and the sigil *woke.*

* * *

It happened all at once, the way storms happen — a pressure, a hush, and then everything everywhere.

The crown fought.

Emrys had imagined resistance as weight, as a door stuck on swollen hinges. It was not that. It was a voice — a chorus, actually, layered voices speaking in the cadence of kings, each one trained from birth to sound reasonable while ordering men to die — and it spoke directly into the bones of her hands.

*Storm-caller. Wren of Vell. Exile. You have been robbed your whole life — your name, your city, your sky. We offer restoration. One signature. One small agreement, and the storm stays yours forever, and Carreth loves you, and Vell builds you a statue—*

"I don't want a statue," she said through her teeth. "I want a desk."

The chorus faltered — genuinely faltered, the way Corvane used to when she found an error in his books — because it had offered thrones and pardons and undying gifts to every chosen one before her, and not one of them had answered with *a desk.* Good, she thought, viciously. Let it be confused. Confusion buys seconds, and Sable is bleeding for my seconds.

She began to pull.

The sigil came off Aldous's brow the way a leech comes off skin — reluctantly, clinging, and worse than the cling was the *giving*, because the crown had drunk a century of kings and it tasted of all of them. It poured itself into her awareness as she wrenched it free: coronation feasts and border wars, a queen's funeral where it had wept through the king's eyes to salt the crowd's devotion, the old dowager young and desperate at a sickbed making the original bargain with shaking hands. A hundred years of being loved for someone else's warmth. It showed her all of it, hoping she'd flinch.

She flinched. She did not stop.

Aldous screamed — not the crown's scream but his own, raw and human, the sound of a man surfacing from deep water — and the crown came loose by a finger's width, and the hall *noticed.* Every torch guttered. Frost bloomed up the pillars in ferns. Somewhere beyond the doors the fighting stopped for one held breath, and then started again, harder, closer.

*Emrys.* Now it spoke with only one voice, and she knew it: the Regent's voice, the hollow king, the first victim. *I was a rescue once. I was needed. Everything I did, I did because they asked—*

"You're a debt," Emrys said. "And I am calling in accounts tonight."

She gave it the storm.

That was the rite's true demand — not the tearing, which any strong arm might manage, but the *pouring*. The gift entire. She opened whatever door it was inside her that had let thunder in since childhood, the door she'd nailed shut in exile, the door she'd broken open over the palace roof an hour ago — and instead of letting the storm out through her hands into the sky, she let it go *into the crown*, all of it, deliberately, completely, holding back nothing.

Holding back nothing was the hard part. Holding back nothing was the whole price.

Because the gift didn't come alone. It came with the girl who'd first heard thunder answer her, age six, laughing in her mother's garden. It came with the harbor storm, the drowned crane-keeper whose name Emrys had carried nine years like a stone in her shoe. It came with every night she'd stood on a cliff in Vell and felt the clouds lean down to listen to her, the only conversations that had never lied. It came with the identity of *storm-caller*, which exile had taught her to hate and the prophecy had taught her to need, and which she now understood had been hers all along in a way no book could inscribe or revoke.

She poured it all, and the pouring was agony — not sharp agony but subtractive, an agony of things going missing. Warmth left her hands. Then the sure knowledge of which way weather would turn. Then the hum under her skin that she'd stopped hearing years ago only because she'd never stopped hearing it. She felt the shape of her own gift leave her the way you feel a tooth leave the gum: relief and ruin in the same instant, a socket suddenly, a gap where certainty used to live.

*Hold something back,* the crown begged, and it sounded, finally, like what it was — a starving thing. *Keep one cloud. One drizzle. Take the bargain, take anything—*

"No," said Emrys Zhou, clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things, and she emptied herself out like a ledger being squared at year's end, line by line, balance demanded and given, until the storm was entirely in the sigil and there was nothing of it left in her at all.

The crown burst.

Not dramatically — that was the strange part. All that feeding, a century of kings, a stolen storm, and it ended like ice ending: a crack, a fog of black vapor, a scatter of metal fragments across the dais that rang and rang and then, impossibly, made no sound at all, as though the room had briefly forgotten acoustics. The stone that was not a stone collapsed inward and was gone, taking the hole with it. Cold rushed outward through the hall like a tide reversing.

Emrys fell to her knees beside the king.

For a moment she felt nothing at all — not triumph, not grief, just the enormous ringing emptiness of a scale after the weight is removed, a body suddenly lighter than it knew how to be. Her hands wouldn't close. The air tasted like nothing, and the nothing was wrong; the air had always tasted of *something*, of coming rain, of receding rain, of the million small moods of sky she had read without effort her whole life. Now it was just air. Weather was happening outside the window — the blizzard was dying, she could see it settling, snow falling down for the first time in days, falling *correctly* — and it meant nothing to her. It was a stranger's business now.

*Oh,* she thought, with a distant, clerical clarity. *So this is what everyone else has always felt. This is what it's like to be ordinary.*

Then Aldous drew a breath under her hands — a whole breath, deep and unassisted, the breath of a man whose head belonged only to himself — and opened his eyes, and they were his own, lagging not at all, wet with tears that were his tears, and Emrys thought: *worth it,* and discovered the word was true even arriving in a heart that felt scraped hollow.

"Is it—" His voice broke. He reached up and touched his bare brow, his hair, the simple mortal skull beneath, and began to shake. "It's quiet. It's *quiet.* Nineteen years, it has never once been quiet—"

Somewhere below and behind them the great doors gave way — a splintering roar — and Emrys twisted around on her knees to see, expecting the Regent, expecting the end of the minutes Sable had bought.

What came through the wreckage of the doors instead was light.

* * *

It rose out of the shattered crown fragments scattered across the dais — out of the black vapor still unraveling above them — and it was not one light but many, a procession, figures made of pale luminescence climbing out of the dying sigil the way smoke climbs out of a snuffed candle. Kings. A dozen kings, maybe more, each one distinct: a broad-shouldered man in armor three centuries out of fashion, a thin boy with a scholar's ink-stained ghost-hands, a woman — two women — crowned in styles the history books had argued about. The kings the sigil had worn. The kings it had kept.

They had been screaming politely inside that metal for a hundred years. Emrys had promised herself she would remember that.

They passed her without seeing her, or seeing her in the way you see weather — she was simply part of the landscape of their leaving. The first of them, the hollow one, the shape that had walked Carreth's deep cells as the Regent, paused at the edge of the dais and looked down at his own translucent hands with an expression she recognized from ledgers: the look of a debt discovered to be finally paid. He inclined his head to her — a king's bow, shallow but real — and then the procession moved on, streaming across the hall, up through the ruined doors, out into the settling snow, going wherever a century of stolen men go when the thing that kept them lets go.

Aldous got to his feet slowly, one hand braced on the throne he would never sit in the same way again, and watched them pass. His face was doing several things at once. Grief was one of them.

"That tall one," he said hoarsely, as a gaunt figure drifted by. "My grandfather. He taught me to fish, before—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Before it took him. He's not screaming anymore."

"No," Emrys said. Her voice sounded strange to her — thinner, unaccompanied, no thunder underneath it. Just a woman's voice in a cold room.

Through the broken doors, far off across the snow court, someone was shouting her name. Two someones. Tamsin's bright carrying yell, and underneath it a lower voice, ragged, alive, refusing to stop — Sable, coming for her at a dead run over the ice, and Emrys realized she was smiling, and realized too that she could not feel her hands, and that the cold in the room was winning, and that the enormous subtraction she had just performed had left a bill of its own, coming due in her body, right now.

The last thing she saw clearly was the sun.

It clawed up over the horizon of the frozen capital — the first dawn in four days, weak and red and grudging, catching the edges of the departing ghosts and gilding them — and then the marble came up very fast, and the long winter's last night ended somewhere above her without her watching it fall.
