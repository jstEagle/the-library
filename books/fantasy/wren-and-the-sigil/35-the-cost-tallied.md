## 35. The Cost, Tallied

Emrys woke to the sound of her own breathing and nothing else.

No wind. That was the first wrongness — the absence she felt before any other. For eleven years the storm had lived somewhere beneath her sternum, a low pressure system of the soul, always audible if she listened: the distant roll of it, the static hush of rain that had never fallen anywhere but inside her. She had hated it in Vell. She had blamed it for everything — the exile, the drowned fields, the look on her father's face when the Guild's clerks came with their ledgers and their careful, pitying voices.

She had not known what silence would cost until she woke inside it.

Her body reported its grievances one by one, like clerks filing past a desk. Her hands were the worst. They had been inside the sigil-light when it tore free of Aldous's head; they had held the rite open while a century of screaming kings poured through her fingers. Now they were wrapped in linen stiff with frost-melt, heavy as stones at the ends of arms that felt borrowed. Her feet she could not feel at all. Her chest ached in a way that was not muscular, not bruising, but structural — as though something had been pried out of her and the space left behind had not yet learned how to be empty rather than full.

She opened her eyes.

Dawn light came through a narrow window — real dawn, honest dawn, the sun having clawed its way above Carreth's frozen skyline sometime in the hour after the crown crumbled, or so Sable would tell her later. The room was small and warm and smelled of woodsmoke and melted snow drying on wool. Someone had built a fire. Someone had stacked her wet clothes by the hearth and draped a blanket over her that was not hers, army-grey, smelling faintly of leather and iron and, underneath all of it, of Sable herself.

The tower infirmary, then. Or what remained of it. The palace above them was still half-ruined from the storm she'd ridden through its halls — doors blown off hinges, windows starred with cracks, corridors scoured down to bare stone where the gale had passed through looking for something to unmake. But this room had been tended. This room had been kept.

Emrys lay still and did the thing she did best, the only thing left to her now, perhaps: she took inventory.

*Storm: gone.* Not sleeping. Not wounded and waiting to heal. Gone the way a river is gone once you dam it, the way a voice is gone once the throat is cut. When she reached inward — carefully, the way one reaches toward a burn — there was no answering pressure, no gathering dark on the horizon of herself. There was just Emrys Zhou, thirty-one years old, clerk of the Guild of Impossible Things, with a talent for numbers and a memory for names and nothing else that anyone had ever wanted.

*Hands: ruined, possibly temporarily.* The healer — Tamsin had fetched a healer from the lower city at knifepoint, or so rumor claimed — had said the frostbite was the storm's own doing, the last cold of it leaving her body the way heat leaves a dying coal. Her fingers might recover. They might not. A ledger-keeper could live without perfect hands. A storm-caller could not.

*Aldous: alive.* That was the entry she turned over longest, because it was the one the whole night had been for. Alive, mortal, diminished — the physicians said the sigil had taken most of a century of his strength along with itself, and he would age now like any man, quickly, humanly. But alive, and *his*, and weeping freely in front of his entire court when he woke on the dais with an ordinary head and no one screaming politely inside him.

She closed her eyes again and let the silence have her.

It should have felt like victory. In the Codex chamber, with the book rewriting itself around her, offering versions where everyone lived if only she would wear the crown instead — in that moment she had understood the price and paid it gladly, or told herself she had. The storm was the thing they exiled her for. The storm was the reason her name meant ruin in two provinces. Trading it back should have been easy arithmetic.

But she had not understood, until she woke inside the silence, that she had never once in her life been *only* Emrys. The storm had been her curse and her exile and her proof, and beneath all of that it had simply been hers — the one part of her that was vast, that was weather, that was more than a girl with good penmanship. She had poured it into the rite like water into sand, and now there was no horizon inside her at all. Just walls. Just a small room, a fire, a blanket that smelled of someone who stayed.

Someone who stayed. Her breath caught on that thought, and she turned her head on the pillow.

Sable was asleep in a chair beside the bed, upright, armored from the waist down and not from the waist up, her sword across her knees and her chin dropped to her chest. She had the particular posture of someone who had not meant to sleep — someone who had sat down to keep watch and lost the argument with her own exhaustion hours ago. There was a cut healing along her jaw from the Regent's guards, and another on her forearm, and her right hand was bandaged where she had caught a blade bare-handed at the throne room door because there had been no time to draw. Emrys knew all of this secondhand. She had been on the dais, pouring herself out, when Sable made her stand; she had heard the door hold, and heard what holding cost.

*Sable Ondris, oathbreaker,* Emrys thought, watching her sleep. *With no oath left to break.*

Because that was the truth under all of it, the entry she couldn't bring herself to write: Sable's oath had died with the old queen, years ago, and everything since — the guarding, the standing at doors, the bleeding in frozen corridors — had been done on nothing but choice. And now the prophecy was dead too, the Codex blank, the whole architecture of fate that had dragged them both across a country dismantled page by page. There was no duty left in the world that required Sable Ondris to sit in this chair.

She stayed anyway. Emrys held onto that fact the way she'd once held onto the ledger-bar of the guild's counting desk, both hands, white-knuckled, in storms.

She must have moved, or made some sound, because Sable stirred — the soldier's waking, instant and total, hand finding sword-hilt before eyes finding light. Then her gaze landed on Emrys and every soldierly thing fell off her face at once.

"You're awake." It came out rough, half-swallowed. "You've been — three days. Three days and a night."

"Have I?" Emrys's voice was gravel and rust. "I only meant to rest my eyes."

Something cracked in Sable's expression — not quite a laugh, not quite the other thing. She leaned forward, elbows on knees, studying Emrys's face with the thoroughness she gave to terrain she intended to defend. "How much do you remember?"

"All of it." Which was true and insufficient, and they both knew it. "The rite worked. Aldous is—"

"He's asking everyone to call him Aldous now. Not 'your Majesty.' He says king is a job title and he's resigning." Sable's mouth twitched. "The regency council is furious. He doesn't care."

"And the sigil?"

"Dust. I brought you a piece." Sable reached into her breastplate and produced something small, wrapped in cloth — a fragment of black metal no bigger than a thumbnail, dull now, ordinary-looking, the way a snake's shed skin looks like nothing at all. "Couldn't leave it lying about. Old habits."

Emrys looked at it and felt — nothing. No pull, no dread, no whisper of the hundred kings. Just metal. She started to laugh and it hurt her ribs and turned into coughing, and Sable was up off the chair with a cup of snowmelt at her lips before she'd finished, steadying her shoulder with a hand that was gentle the way siege engines are gentle when they choose to be.

"Easy," Sable said. "You're held together with thread and stubbornness. The healer's orders."

"The healer," Emrys managed, "has never met me."

"No," Sable agreed, settling back into her chair, closer now, close enough that their knees almost touched. "But I have. Stubbornness is your primary material. Thread is a formality."

Emrys drank the water. It was the best thing she had ever tasted, which told her how thirsty she was. When she lowered the cup, Sable was watching her hands — the bandaged ones — with an expression carefully stripped of pity, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"They'll mend," Sable said. "Mostly."

"Mostly," Emrys repeated. She flexed her fingers inside the linen. They obeyed, sluggishly, like servants woken too early. "And the rest of it? You may as well ask. Everyone else has been tiptoeing around it for three days, and I'd rather hear it from you."

Sable didn't pretend to misunderstand. She was quiet for a long moment, choosing words the way she chose footing on ice.

"The storm's gone. Tamsin asked the healer, and the healer asked the dowager, and the dowager said — in her way, all sideways and sorrowful — that a gift poured out entirely doesn't refill. Sometimes. Rarely. Maybe." She met Emrys's eyes. "You may never call weather again. You may wake up one morning ten years from now and feel rain coming and know it's yours. No one can promise either."

Emrys nodded slowly. Inside her, where the storm had lived, the silence answered nothing.

"I spent eleven years wishing it gone," she said. "I used to lie awake in the warehouse loft in Vell and imagine handing it over to someone — anyone — and walking away clean. Unremarkable. A woman nobody crossed the street to avoid." Her laugh was thin. "Be careful what you put in a ledger, Sable. The world reads over your shoulder."

"Do you regret it?"

The question was direct, and Sable's questions always were; it was one of the first things Emrys had loved about her, back when love was a word she refused. She considered the answer honestly, turning it over like a coin of uncertain mint.

"No," she said at last, and found it was true. "Aldous is alive. A hundred kings are free. The winter's breaking — you can feel it, even in here, the air's changed." She looked down at her ruined hands. "I regret that it was mine. I don't regret that it's spent. Those are different entries."

Sable absorbed that. Then, quietly: "And the rest? The part you haven't said?"

Emrys looked at her. Of course she saw it. She had always seen it, from the first night on the salt road when she'd caught Emrys fleeing through the impossible warehouse and — against every order, against her own training — let her go.

"That I'm only a clerk now," Emrys said. "That everything I was, besides the penmanship, is gone. That when the guild rechartered yesterday and Tamsin read the new charter aloud — *trading only in things that can be honestly owned* — I stood there thinking I wasn't sure I qualified anymore. That I'm not certain what's honestly mine." Her voice frayed on the last of it. "There. Tallied."

Sable was silent. Then she stood, and Emrys braced for comfort — the awkward, well-meant kind, the kind that lands like snowfall on a roof already strained — but Sable did not embrace her. Instead she drew the chair around to the far side of the bed, sat, and laid her sword across her knees facing Emrys, the old watch-posture, the one she must have held a thousand nights outside a thousand doors.

"Eleven years ago," Sable said, "I broke the only sacred vow of my life. I told the truth about the queen's death instead of protecting the lie, and they struck my name from the rolls, and I walked out of Carreth with nothing but a sword and the knowledge that I would never belong anywhere again." She turned her bandaged hand over, examining it. "For nine years I told myself the oath still bound me. Guarding things. Standing at doors. Because an oathbreaker with no oath is just a woman with weapons, and I couldn't bear to be only that."

"Sable—"

"Let me finish. I'm bad at this and I won't get better with interruptions." A ghost of her dry humor, there and gone. "Then the crown sent me to collect a clerk, and she ran, and I caught her, and I let her go — and I told myself that was duty too. That the prophecy needed her free. That the old queen would have wanted it." She shook her head slowly. "It was a lie I told myself for a month. Maybe longer. The truth is I let you go because you looked at me in that warehouse like I was a person and not a function, and I would have burned the salt road down for another hour of that feeling."

Emrys's throat tightened.

"So here is my tally," Sable said. "No oath. No order. The prophecy's dead, the crown's dust, the regency has no claim on me and the guild never did. Every structure that ever told me to stand next to you has collapsed." She looked up, and her grey eyes were steady and terrified in equal measure, the way they had been at the throne room door. "And I am telling you that I will be here tomorrow. And the day after. In this chair or whatever chair, in whatever city, doing whatever's needed — because I *choose* it. Not because anything binds me. Because nothing does, and I stay anyway. That's the whole ledger. That's everything I have."

The fire popped. Down in the city, faint through the cracked window, a bell rang the hour — an ordinary bell, an ordinary hour, the first in a century.

Emrys found she could not speak for a moment. When she could, her voice came out small and clear.

"Then I have an amendment to file," she said. "Under *things that can be honestly owned*." She extended one bandaged hand across the blanket, clumsy, imperfect, hers. "I'm keeping you. Entered as of tonight. No conditions, no collection date. The debt runs forever, in both directions."

Sable took the hand — carefully, mindful of the frostbite, mindful of everything — and did not let go.

* * *

Sable woke before dawn three days later, as she always did, in the narrow bed the infirmary had given her one floor below Emrys's room — though she had spent every night since in the chair upstairs, and they both knew the bed was ceremonial.

She dressed in the dark by touch. Her armor went on piece by piece, each buckle a small familiar argument, and she noted as she always did the places the Regent's guards had marked it — the dent over her left ribs, the gouge across the pauldron where a blade had skidded. She would have the armorer beat them out eventually. Or she wouldn't. There was no company inspecting her kit anymore. There was no company at all. The realization arrived each morning with the same strange taste, like drinking snowmelt after a lifetime of wine: *free*. Ungarrisoned. A woman with weapons, and worse — a woman with a choice, renewed daily, with nothing to hide behind.

She climbed the stairs.

Emrys was asleep sitting up, which she had taken to doing — propped against pillows with a ledger across her knees, refusing to surrender the habit of work even to convalescence. This ledger was new: plain covers, unmarked, purchased from a stationer in the lower city with money Sable had lent her because the guild's treasury was frozen pending the recharter. On the open page, in handwriting still uneven from damaged fingers, Sable could read the beginnings of an inventory.

*Recovered from the sigil's collapse:* one kingdom, winter-bound ninety-nine years, thawing. One king, restored, abdicating. One hundred kings, freed — destination unknown, presumed at peace. One dowager queen, confessing at her own pace, owed a debt no ledger can price. One apprentice, promoted beyond all sense, running a rechartered guild on honesty and nerve. One codex, blank, stored under seal—

Sable read the last line twice.

*One oathbreaker, self-assigned, terms indefinite. Value: not for resale.*

Emrys slept on, chin dipped, breath slow, the morning light beginning to find the frost-ferns on the window and turn them briefly to gold. Sable stood in the doorway a long moment, unwilling to disturb the scene and unable to leave it.

Then Emrys's eyes opened — she had always woken like a clerk catching an error, instantly, mid-thought — and found her.

"You're reading my ledger," Emrys said, voice sleep-roughened.

"I'm auditing it." Sable came into the room, sat on the edge of the bed. "I have concerns about the valuation on line six."

"Oh?"

"It's too low." She said it plainly, the way she said everything that mattered. "An oathbreaker with no oath, self-assigned — the market rate for that is nothing. Less than nothing. Most buyers would charge you storage fees."

Emrys studied her, and Sable watched the grief move through her face — the storm-shaped absence, still raw, still bleeding at the edges — and watched her set it aside, deliberately, the way she filed a completed account. Not denial. Order.

"Noted," Emrys said. "I'll revise the entry." She picked up the pen, and her damaged fingers shook slightly, and she wrote anyway, slow and deliberate, while Sable sat close enough that their shoulders touched. When she finished she turned the ledger so Sable could see.

Beneath the old line, in fresh ink: *Amended. Value: incalculable. Holder: Emrys Zhou, in perpetuity. Witnessed.*

"Witnessed by whom?" Sable asked.

Emrys looked at her, and for the first time since the dais, since the tearing-free, since the long silence where the storm had been — she smiled. Small and crooked and entirely unremarkable, the smile of a woman with good penmanship and nothing else that anyone had ever wanted, except perhaps one thing, honestly owned.

"By you," Emrys said. "You're the only auditor I trust."

Below them, in the waking city, the bells began — and among them, clear and unexpected, a new voice: the great bell of the palace, silent for a century, ringing out the news that runners were already carrying through the melting streets. Sable caught only fragments through the window, shouted up from the courtyard by a breathless palace page: word from the regency council, urgent, addressed to *the lady Emrys Zhou, keeper of accounts* — and to the knight Ondris, by name, by *both* their names, though no official record had ever linked them.

Emrys and Sable looked at each other over the open ledger.

"Well," Sable said, reaching for her sword. "Someone's been reading our entries."

The page's voice rose again, excited, half-lost in the bells — and this time Emrys caught the words clearly enough to go still, pen frozen mid-air above a page that had, moments ago, held only their two names:

They were requesting an audit. Of the royal treasury. Because three nights ago, on the dawn the sigil died — every locked strongbox in Carreth had opened itself, and inside each one, beside the crown's gold, someone had left a single pressed flower.

Snowdrops. Out of season. Impossible.

Blooming in every box, as if spring itself had signed its name.
