## 36. A King Made Human

The first thing Aldous learned about being human again was that his hands shook.

Not the fine tremor of a man grown old — he was not yet fifty, though the crown had aged him in other currencies — but the coarse, honest shaking of a body remembering its own weight. He sat on the edge of his bed in the royal apartments and watched his fingers move against each other like strangers greeting after a long separation. They were thinner than he remembered. There was ink on the left one, a stain he did not recall earning, and when he pressed his thumbnail against it the sensation arrived a half-second late, as if traveling a great distance to reach him.

A century of kings had lived inside that crown. He had been the last of them, or nearly — the sigil had been prying him open for eleven years, drinking him by slow spoonfuls, and what remained when Emrys tore it free was perhaps two-thirds of a man. He knew this the way an amputee knows the missing limb: not by seeing it but by reaching for things that weren't there anymore. Certain memories had gone soft at the edges. His coronation day. The name of his first tutor. The smell of his mother's hair. He could feel the shapes of these things, the way one feels a word on the tip of the tongue, but the substance of them had been poured out through the circlet's cold gold and spent feeding something older than the kingdom itself.

And still — still — he was more himself this morning than he had been in a decade.

That was the terrible arithmetic of it. The court would look at him now and see a diminished king: paler, slower, prone to standing too long before windows, startled by loud noises. But Aldous knew the truth, which was that the man they had loved for eleven years had never been there at all. The warmth they remembered, the perfect patience, the unerring grace — those had been the sigil's craftsmanship, a puppet's polish applied to a face going hollow underneath. What sat on the edge of the bed now, hands trembling, was the actual Aldous: flawed, forgetful, occasionally petty, afraid of the dark in a way he had never admitted to anyone.

He was, in other words, finally qualified to rule.

The thought made him laugh — a dry, cracked sound, the laugh of a man whose humor had rusted from disuse — and the laughing turned briefly into weeping, and he let it, because no one was watching, and because a king who could not weep alone was only furniture with a pulse.

* * *

They came to him at midmorning, when the light through the eastern windows had gone from white to gold. Sable first, because she had appointed herself his guard in the days since the unbinding, standing at his door with her broken oath reforged into something quieter and stranger — not duty anymore, she'd said, just choice, wearing duty's old coat because nothing else fit. Then the dowager queen, moving slowly, leaning on a cane she had once used only for ceremony, her memory-gnawed mind holding its shape by visible effort. Then Tamsin and three of the guild clerks, who stood awkwardly among the throne room's grandeur like cats carried somewhere they hadn't consented to go.

Emrys came last.

She looked worse than any of them. The storm-gift had gone out of her in the tearing, and what remained was a small, gray-faced woman wrapped in borrowed furs, moving carefully, as though the floor might be thinner than it appeared. She had slept — they told him — for nearly two days, waking to eat and sleep again, and even now she seemed to be listening to some interior weather, checking whether anything still moved behind her ribs.

Aldous rose to meet her. His knees objected. He let them.

"Your Majesty," Emrys began, and he stopped her with a raised hand.

"No." His voice came out rougher than intended. He tried again. "No titles. Not today. Today I need to say some things, and I can't say them to a king, because the king you're addressing is mostly dead. I need to say them to whoever's left."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, in the snow court, the city was beginning to move again — the thaw had come with the sun like a tide coming in, water running loud in every gutter of Carreth, and the sound of it reached them even here, four floors up, a whole kingdom exhaling.

"I remember pieces," Aldous said. "Of the last eleven years. That's the cruelest part — I wasn't asleep. I was *there*, most of the time, watching him wear me. Watching my hands sign things. Watching me smile at my councilors while something behind my eyes counted their worth like a merchant counting coin. Do you know what it's like to applaud your own theft?"

He saw Emrys flinch slightly, and understood — she knew. She had watched the pauses lengthen, the eyes lag. She had seen it before anyone.

"The sigil kept us," he went on. "All of us. Every king it wore, folded down small and stored in the dark, still aware. I met them, near the end. In there. A hundred years of men screaming politely at each other in a space the size of a prayer. The first of them — the Regent, whatever he is now, whatever's left of him — he told me the trick of surviving it was to stop hoping. Hope, he said, was how it fed. So I stopped. For years I lay inside my own skull and felt nothing, and called it peace."

He turned toward the window. Below, the frozen river was breaking up; great plates of ice ground against each other with a sound like distant applause.

"Then a woman walked into my throne room with winter in her fists," he said, "and tore the lid off the world."

* * *

The council convened that afternoon, and Aldous did the thing no living monarch of Carreth had ever done voluntarily.

He stood before them — the full court, packed shoulder to shoulder into the hall where he had smiled his manufactured smiles for a decade — and he told them the truth. All of it. The sigil, the feeding, the dozen chosen ones the guild had sold to the crown like lambs to a ledger. Corvane's name, spoken plainly. The forged Codex. The Regent, and what the Regent had been. He spoke for over an hour, and his voice held, mostly, and where it broke he simply paused until it returned, because a man who had spent eleven years unable to control his own mouth was not going to apologize for a pause.

"You loved a king who did not exist," he finished. "I am sorry for it. Not because the love was wasted — your love was real, and it deserved a real object — but because I took it under false pretenses. Or rather, something wore my face and took it, and I lacked the courage or the means to stop it. Either way, the debt is mine to carry."

Silence. Then an old duchess in the front row — Aldous could not recall her name, and the gap in his memory stung like salt — began, quietly, to cry. And then others. Not the performative weeping of courts, which Aldous had heard all his life and distrusted instinctively, but the raw, embarrassed crying of people discovering that their grief had been legitimate all along, that the thing they had mourned without knowing it — the slow vanishing of their king — had actually happened.

"Here is what I propose," Aldous said, when the hall had steadied. "I abdicate."

The word landed like a dropped chandelier.

"Not tomorrow. Not in disgrace. I will serve as caretaker until a regency can be properly formed — the dowager queen has agreed to help select it, and I trust her judgment more than my own, which is precisely the point. But I will not wear a crown again. Not any crown. Because I have learned what crowns are for in this kingdom, and it is not the wearer."

A young lord near the back — brave, or foolish — called out: "Sire, the people love you! You freed yourself! You're the king who survived—"

"I am the king who was *eaten*," Aldous said, "and spat out. And if I stay, within five years you will have rebuilt the warm, perfect, impossible king you remember, and you will begin measuring the shivering man in front of you against him, and finding him wanting, and looking about for something — some sigil, some prophecy, some convenient miracle — to close the gap. That is how it started last time. Not with ambition. With disappointment." He spread his trembling hands. "I cannot be what you loved. I never was. Let me be what I am instead, and let Carreth find a ruler who doesn't require a monster's tailoring."

It took three hours to talk them into it. It took the dowager rising from her chair, cane in hand, to say in her thin, iron voice: "I bound that thing to save a dying man I loved, and it cost a hundred years of grief. Do not ask this one to sit on that chair out of sentiment. Sentiment is how the last century began." After that, the hall yielded.

* * *

He found Emrys that evening in the library annex, where the real Codex Vellum lay under glass now, guarded around the clock, its pages still slowly emptying themselves like a wound closing. She was sitting beside it with a lamp and a blank notebook, writing — he could see from the doorway that she was writing fast, the way people write when they are afraid the words will leave if they slow down.

"Recording?" he asked.

"Everything." She didn't look up. "While I still have it straight. The pardon came today — did they tell you? For the storm I never called. I signed it." Her pen paused. "Then I wrote four pages describing what actually happened, and filed those too, in the same archive, directly beneath it. Let some clerk in fifty years find both and wonder."

Aldous laughed — really laughed this time — and lowered himself into the chair across from her. Up close he could see how much the gift's loss had cost her: a certain stillness was gone, a coiled readiness, the sense he'd had at their first meeting of a woman with weather folded inside her coat. What remained was smaller and, he thought, more durable. A thing that had survived its own burning.

"I have a request," he said. "An imposition, rather. Feel free to refuse. Everyone in this palace refuses me things lately; it's wonderfully educational."

Emrys set down her pen.

"When I abdicate — formally, next week — there will be histories written. Official ones. The court chroniclers are already circling like gulls. And they will write it beautifully and they will write it wrong. They will make me noble. They will make the sigil a metaphor. They will round off the edges until the story fits on a monument." He leaned forward. "You keep ledgers. Real ones. I've seen your work — the guild's accounts, the true ledger Tamsin smuggled out, the record you filed under my pardon. You write what happened, not what flatters. Numbers don't flatter. Dates don't flatter."

"I'm not a historian," Emrys said. "I'm a clerk."

"Yes," Aldous said. "Thank the stars. Historians tell you what a reign meant. Clerks tell you what it cost. I want the second thing. I want a true ledger of the reign of Aldous of Carreth — every payment, every debt, every entry the monuments will leave out. The twelve dead chosen ones, with their names spelled correctly. What the dowager paid, and keeps paying. What you paid. What Sable—" he caught himself, and saw Emrys's mouth twitch, "—what certain former knights paid, in ways they may prefer to itemize privately. I want it kept honestly, in a hand that doesn't tremble toward mercy or malice. And I want it archived where no king can burn it."

Emrys was silent for a long moment. Outside, the meltwater ran loud and constant, the sound of a country unfreezing.

"You understand," she said finally, "that a true ledger of your reign includes entries about you. The things you signed while he wore you. The taxes. The executions. You'll read your own name on lines you didn't choose, and some you half-chose, and you won't get to look away."

"I know."

"And it includes entries about the guild. My guild. Which I intend to rebuild differently, but which was, for years, exactly what the ledger will say it was. If I keep your book, I keep my own indictment in the same binding."

"I know," Aldous said again, softly. "That's rather the point. A ledger that spares me will spare everyone, and then it's worthless. Humility isn't thinking little of yourself, Miss Zhou. I've learned that much, being emptied. Humility is agreeing to be recorded accurately." He smiled, and it was a strange, unpracticed expression, a muscle relearning its work. "Call it my restoration. A king made human should leave behind a human document. Not a legend. A *record*."

Emrys looked at him for a while — and he had the odd sensation of being audited, kindly, thoroughly, by someone deciding whether he was solvent.

"All right," she said. "One condition. I keep it in Vell. At the new guild house, once we charter it. Not in any royal archive. If the regency wants to consult it, they send a clerk, and the clerk reads it under supervision, and copies nothing without a countersignature. The truth about kings shouldn't live in palaces. Palaces edit."

"Agreed," Aldous said instantly. "Vell. Under guild seal." He extended his hand across the table, and she took it, and his grip was weak and hers was careful, and between them they shook on the strangest commission either had ever accepted: a deposed king paying a powerless storm-caller, in advance, in honesty, to keep the books of his own diminishment.

"One more thing," Emrys said, as he rose to go. "The line in the Codex. The last one, before the pages went blank. Have you read it?"

"'A long spring,'" Aldous quoted. "Naming no one. Yes. I've read it forty times." He glanced toward the glass case, where the great book lay open to its final page, white as fresh snow. "Do you believe it?"

"I believe it's the only prophecy I've ever trusted," Emrys said. "Because it doesn't tell anyone what to do. It just says the winter ends. The rest is ours."

Aldous walked back through the corridors of his shrinking kingdom, past guards who saluted him with something new in their faces — not reverence, quite; recognition, maybe — and stood a long time at his window watching the ice go out of the river, plate by grinding plate, toward the sea.

Tomorrow he would draft the abdication. Tomorrow the regency question, the succession, the hard arithmetic of a wounded country. Tomorrow the world would resume demanding things of him, and he would give what a mortal man could give and no more.

But tonight there was only the sound of water running everywhere, in every gutter and eave of Carreth, and somewhere south, in a port city called Vell, clerks were already sweeping out a warehouse full of impossible doors — and Aldous found, to his surprise, that he envied them. Not their freedom. Their *ledger*. The clean page at the start of a new book, waiting for the first honest entry.

He dipped a pen, and on the first line of his final decree, in a hand that shook and did not care, he began to write.
