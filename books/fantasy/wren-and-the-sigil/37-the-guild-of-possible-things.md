## 37. The Guild of Possible Things

Tamsin had swept the great floor of the Vell warehouse three times before she admitted she was not sweeping away dust. She was sweeping away the shape of Corvane.

It was in everything, if you looked for it. In the ledgers still stacked on his desk with their elegant double columns — *acquired* on the left, *expended* on the right, as though a dozen dead storm-callers were line items that had merely failed to appreciate. In the brass fittings of the impossible doors, polished by decades of clerks who had never once been told what waited behind them. In the way the morning light came through the high salt-glazed windows and lay across the floor in long pale panels, exactly where a master would stand to address his people.

She swept it anyway. The broom whispered. The gulls argued over the harbor beyond the walls. And when the third pass was done, Tamsin leaned the broom against the counting-desk, sat down in the master's chair — it was only wood; it could not bite her — and pulled the true ledger toward her across the scarred planks.

The true ledger. Emrys Zhou's find, smuggled out of Corvane's private strongroom in a crate marked *dried herring*, because even now Tamsin took a small fierce pleasure in that detail. She opened it to the last written page and did not flinch from it, though she had made herself read every entry twice. Twelve names. Twelve dates of acquisition, each one a night like the one Emrys had been taken. Twelve dates of expenditure, each one a death in Carreth, recorded in the same tidy hand, with the same little marginal note Corvane used for inventory losses: *unavoidable.*

Tamsin had been his apprentice for six years. She had loved him the way an orphan loves the roof over her head — gratefully, uncritically, without ever asking what held it up.

She dipped her pen and drew a single rule beneath the final entry, the way clerks do at the close of a book, and wrote above the fresh page in letters she tried very hard to keep steady:

**Guild of Possible Things. Charter Day.**

Then she sat back and looked at the words and thought: *that's either the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever done,* and then, because she was a clerk to the bone, she corrected herself: *no. It's both. Write them both down.*

* * *

They came through the impossible doors all afternoon.

That was the thing about the warehouse no one outside the guild had ever understood — that the doors were not passages so much as promises, and promises, unlike prophecies, could be kept by ordinary people. Tamsin stood at the head of the long room with the keys Emrys had sent back hanging heavy in her apron pocket, and she watched them arrive: the junior clerks who had walked out rather than stay under Corvane's shadow, the two porters who had carried Emrys's crate of bottled thunderstorms and never been paid properly, old Mistress Quill from the records annex who remembered the guild from before Corvane and could recite its older charter from memory, which turned out to be three sentences long and mostly about honesty.

And through the door that opened onto a hillside in late spring — the door they'd used to bring Emrys and Sable home from Carreth, walking through a season that had no business touching this coast — came Emrys herself, with Sable a half-step behind her the way she always was now, not guarding, exactly. Accompanying. There was a difference, and Tamsin had learned to see it, and it made something loosen in her chest every time.

"You've changed the sign," Emrys said, stopping just inside.

Tamsin had. The old board above the counting-floor had read, in Corvane's flourished hand, **GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS — ALL THINGS CONSIDERED — NOTHING REFUSED**. Beneath it, nailed up crooked because Tamsin was a clerk and not a carpenter, hung a fresh plank: **GUILD OF POSSIBLE THINGS.**

"It's not finished," Tamsin said. "I couldn't reach high enough to make it level."

"Leave it crooked." Emrys touched the edge of the plank the way other people touched relics. "Crooked things are harder to lie about."

Sable said nothing, but she had the look she got in council chambers — the assessing sweep of a woman who had guarded a palace and now found herself cataloguing sightlines in a smugglers' warehouse out of pure habit. She noted the windows, the doors, the corners where a person might wait. Then she caught Tamsin watching her do it, and something almost like embarrassment crossed her face, and Tamsin decided on the spot that she liked the oathbreaker enormously.

"The others are coming," Tamsin said. "All of them who'll come. Which isn't everyone."

"No," Emrys agreed quietly. "It wouldn't be."

Some of the guild had scattered when Corvane fell — fled to rival exchanges in Solenne and Greyharbor, or simply vanished into the coastal towns where men who dealt in fate went to become unremarkable. Two had sent letters that Tamsin kept folded in the strongroom, not for bitterness but for the record: *You have traded a working enterprise for a conscience, girl. See how far conscience pays the wharf fees.* She had answered neither. The true ledger answered for her.

But more had come than she had dared hope. By the time the light through the high windows went amber, there were perhaps forty souls gathered on the counting-floor among the crates of legitimate salvage — actual cargo now, hemp and salt and honest Vell tin — and Mistress Quill had set out the ancient guild seal, a stamp older than Corvane, older than the war the dowager queen had ended with her terrible bargain, stamped with a wren.

"A wren," Emrys said when she saw it. Her voice did something complicated.

"The first masters chose it," Quill said. "Small bird. Loud voice. Builds its nest anywhere and defends it against anything ten times its size." The old woman's eyes crinkled. "We seem to have gone looking for you rather early, Miss Zhou. Or you for us."

*Wren.* The name the clerks had called her in her first year, before anyone knew what slept in her blood — before the storm, before the exile, before any of it. Tamsin saw Emrys hold very still for the length of one breath, the way she did when a feeling was too large to move through quickly. Then Emrys nodded, once, and Tamsin understood that some gifts are given back and forth for years before either party notices the exchange.

"Right," Tamsin said, and climbed onto the counting-desk because the room was tall and her voice was not. "Charter business."

* * *

She had drafted the new charter eleven times. She had burned four drafts in the counting-house stove out of sheer terror and saved seven, and the one she read aloud that evening was the seventh, and it was short, because she had learned from Quill that short charters are hard to corrupt.

*"The Guild of Possible Things trades only in goods that can be honestly owned. No memories that are not freely sold by their rememberer. No vows unmade save by the consent of both vow-takers. No futures bought, sold, foretold, or delivered. No person acquired, expended, or entered in any ledger as anything other than a person."*

She paused there, because the next part was hers alone to say, and her throat had gone tight.

*"No entry in any book of this guild shall be false. Where we cannot speak truth, we shall keep silence. Where silence would serve a lie, we shall speak anyway, whatever it costs."*

Silence in the great room. Forty faces. Somewhere a door creaked on its hinges — one of the impossible ones, breathing in its sleep.

"And enforcement?" asked a voice from the back — Master Helder, formerly Corvane's man for acquisitions, who had come with his hat literally in his hands, which Tamsin suspected was calculated and respected anyway. "Words are cheap. What binds us?"

Tamsin had expected the question. She lifted the true ledger.

"This does. Every transaction entered here, open to any member to read, any hour, no exceptions, not for me, not for a master, not for a king. And this—" she patted her apron pocket, where she now carried, always, a slim duplicate book. "Every page copied within a day. If the guild ever burns, the record walks out the door in someone's coat. That's the whole law. We keep honest books, and we make sure the books survive us." She looked around the room. "Corvane's crime wasn't dealing in impossible things. Plenty of folk want what can't be had honestly; that's trade. His crime was that he lied in ink. He made murder balance. So we don't get magic laws, or oaths, or seals that glow. We get arithmetic that can't be argued with."

Helder laughed — a surprised, rusty sound — and put his hat back on, which Tamsin chose to read as a signature.

They signed through the evening. Not a grand ceremony; Quill had wanted candles and the seal pressed in red wax, but Tamsin ruled that signatures go in ink, in the book, dated, and that was that. One by one the clerks and porters and runners of the old guild came to the desk where Emrys sat — because of course Tamsin had put her there, keeper of the book, the position she'd held in exile, except now it meant what it said — and wrote their names beneath the charter. Some wrote large. Some wrote small. The porter Brannoc, who could not write, pressed an inked thumb beside a line Tamsin ruled for him, and Emrys entered him in the margin as *Brannoc, porter, honest weight and willing back,* and he went away looking ten years younger.

Through all of it, Sable stood by the wall near the door that opened onto the spring hillside, arms folded, saying little, watching everyone the way she watched everything — and when the last signature was dry she came forward, and the room went quiet, because even those who didn't know her face knew her reputation, the Oathbreaker of Carreth, the knight who told the truth and lost everything for it.

"I have nothing to sign," Sable said. "My name's worth less than Brannoc's thumbprint, in most company."

"It's worth exactly what it's always been worth here," Tamsin said. "Read the charter. We deal in honest value."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes — the flicker of a woman deciding whether to deflect or to let a kindness land. Tamsin had watched her make that calculation a hundred times since Carreth. Tonight she let it land.

"I'm not joining your guild," Sable said. "I don't trade. I've never been good at owning things; I broke my last ownership on purpose." She glanced sideways at Emrys, and the whole warehouse saw her do it, and Emrys pretended very hard to be straightening the inkwell. "But I'll stand at your doors. Unpaid, unsworn, whenever I'm in Vell. Consider it—" she searched, and found, "—a habit I intend to keep."

"That," said Quill, "is the most honest offer this room has heard in thirty years. Enter it."

So Tamsin entered it. *Sable of Carreth: stands watch at the guild's doors, by choice, no wage, no term.* She underlined *by choice* twice, and did not explain why, and saw that Emrys understood perfectly.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the members had gone home to their lodgings and the lamps had been trimmed to embers — Tamsin found Emrys still at the desk, the charter page before her, not writing. Just sitting with her hand flat on the paper, the way you touch a thing to confirm it is real.

"You should sleep," Tamsin said. "You've been awake since the solstice, near enough."

"So have you."

"Yes, but I'm young and bitter. It fuels me."

Emrys laughed — really laughed, short and startled, the first Tamsin had heard from her since before the Codex. The sound seemed to surprise Emrys too. She rubbed her face with both hands.

"Tamsin. Why did you do it? Come to Carreth. Turn the clerks. Risk the warehouse, the guild, everything — for me. You barely knew me anymore. I'd been a name on a letter for three years."

Tamsin considered lying, briefly, professionally, and rejected it as bad policy in a house built against lies.

"Because I found the entries," she said. "After the Regent fell, when we seized the strongroom — I found your apprenticeship file. Your intake assessment. Corvane's own hand." She pulled the folder from the drawer and laid it down, because Emrys deserved to see it even if it cut. "*Subject shows exceptional precision, low social need, suitable isolation profile. Ideal candidate: no attachments to complicate retrieval.*" She watched Emrys read it. Watched her go still in that particular way. "He picked you because he thought nobody loved you. And I sat there reading it and realized the joke of it — that the loneliest woman in the guild had a whole warehouse full of people who'd noticed. Who liked you. Who were frightened of him and not of loving you. He did his accounting wrong. He left out the largest asset on the books." She shrugged, suddenly embarrassed. "So when the chance came to correct the entry, I took it. It's what clerks do."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "He wrote *low social need.*"

"He was wrong about storms, too," Tamsin said gently. "Wronger than about anything else."

Emrys looked down at her own hands — the hands that had torn a crown off a king and poured a lifetime of storm into the rite, and come away from it empty. Tamsin knew what she was feeling; everyone in the guild had learned to recognize it, the reflexive flex of fingers reaching for weather that no longer answered. Emrys had not called so much as a gust in weeks. The healers said the gift might return. The healers said it might not. Emrys herself had stopped asking after the second week, which worried Tamsin more than grief would have.

"I spent six years being useful precisely because I needed no one," Emrys said at last. "Precision instead of people. And then the prophecy took even that — made me useful *against* my will, moved me like a piece on its board. And now—" she spread her empty hands. "Now I can't call rain. I can't audit fate. I can't do the one impossible thing I was ever able to do. So tell me honestly, Tamsin, since we've chartered ourselves a house of honesty—" her mouth curved, wry and fragile, "—what use is the Guild of Possible Things for someone who can't do anything possible *or* impossible anymore? I can copy pages. That's nearly all I am."

Tamsin had rehearsed nothing for this moment, which was how she knew her answer was true.

"Master," she said.

Emrys blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. The charter needs a Keeper of the Book — someone who reads every entry and refuses the dishonest ones, someone whose word on the ledger is final. Quill's too old for it and says so. Helder wants it too much, which is exactly why he can't have it. And there's exactly one person alive who has already refused a king, a prophecy, and a crown in the same month because the entries were false." She tapped the charter page, right beneath the last rule. "The seat's empty. It's yours. It was always going to be yours; I just wrote the rest of the charter around the hole where you'd fit."

"Tamsin—"

"You'd be terrible at it," Tamsin pressed on, merciless, because she could see Emrys building walls out of unworthiness and intended to demolish each one. "You'd refuse half our trade in the first year and make enemies of every fence from here to Solenne. You'd correct people's spelling in the margins. You'd never sleep. But you'd be honest, and you'd be ours, and you'd be *here* — in a building full of people who stayed, who chose you back, who are going to call you Wren regardless of what the pardon courts decide to call you." She slid the pen across the desk. "There's a line under the charter for the Keeper's mark. It's blank. It's been blank for two days, and it's driving me mad."

Emrys looked at the blank line for a long time. Outside, the tide turned in the harbor with a long soft sigh, and somewhere upstairs a junior clerk snored through the floorboards, and one of the impossible doors breathed in its frame like a sleeping animal — the ordinary music of a building becoming a home.

"My gift may never come back," Emrys said. It was not a refusal. Tamsin recognized a last condition when she heard one.

"Then you'll be a master clerk with a famous temper and a crooked sign," Tamsin said. "The guild has survived worse. The guild has survived *Corvane.*"

And Emrys Zhou — exiled, summoned, sacrificed, unbound, powerless, and, as of tonight, impossibly, ordinarily *wanted* — picked up the pen. She wrote her name in the careful, exact hand Tamsin had admired since apprenticeship, and beneath it, unprompted, in smaller letters:

*Keeper of the Book. Called Wren.*

"There," Tamsin said, and had to pretend the ink was blurring for a mundane reason. "Entered. Dated. Witnessed."

"*Witnessed*," Emrys agreed, and blew on the page to dry it, and the small wind that stirred the lamp-flame was only her breath, only ever going to be her breath — and she smiled at it anyway, at the plainness of it, like a woman greeting an old friend who has come back poor.

* * *

They were still at the desk — Tamsin ruling the closing line of Charter Day while Emrys annotated the margins with corrections she absolutely should not have been making to her own appointment — when the knock came at the warehouse's front door. Not the impossible doors. The plain human one, the one that faced the salt road and the world.

Three knocks. Measured. Official.

Sable appeared from the shadows of the doorway she'd claimed, hand already finding the knife at her belt out of a habit older than her oathbreaking, and she crossed to the peephole, and Tamsin saw her whole body change.

"What is it?" Tamsin whispered.

"Riders," Sable said slowly. "Four of them. Regency colors." A pause. "They're carrying a document case. The sealed kind." Another pause, longer, and when she turned from the peephole her face held something Tamsin had never seen on it — not fear, exactly. Wariness worn smooth by repetition, like a stone the same wave had hit for years. "Wren. They're carrying the case they carry when the regency sends pardons. And they're carrying the other kind of case too."

"The other kind?"

"The kind they brought for you," Sable said, "the night the prophecy sent them to Vell the first time."

Outside, the gulls had gone silent, and the knock came again — patient, formal, waiting to be entered in somebody's book.
