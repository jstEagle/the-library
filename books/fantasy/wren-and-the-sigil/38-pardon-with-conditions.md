## 38. Pardon, With Conditions

The pardon arrived on a Tuesday, in a box.

Not a scroll case, not an envelope sealed with the regency's interim arms — a box, wooden, iron-cornered, and heavy enough that Tamsin grunted setting it on the counter of the guild's new front office. The Guild of Possible Things had been open for eleven days. In that time they had sold exactly four things: a repaired promise (the kind people make to themselves and break by spring), one honestly labeled memory of a first snowfall, a jar of ordinary honey, and a map to a house that did not yet exist but would within the year, purchased by a young couple who wanted to walk toward something instead of away from it.

Emrys was cataloguing the honey when the box came.

"It's from Carreth," Tamsin said, turning it over. "Regency seal." She looked up with the particular wariness she had learned at Corvane's elbow and unlearned, mostly, since. "Should I be excited or afraid?"

"With the regency," Emrys said, "both. Usually in that order."

The box held straw, and in the straw, a scroll of white vellum — real vellum, calfskin, expensive as a horse — and beneath it, wrapped separately, a second document in a plainer hand, and beneath *that*, a small tin of wax tablets and a note in a clerkly script so tidy it could only belong to someone being paid by the letter:

*The Regency Council of Carreth presents its compliments to the Guild of Possible Things, and to the storm-caller Emrys Zhou particularly. Enclosed: instrument of full pardon, countersigned. Also enclosed: our copyist's transcription of the testimony you gave before the Council in the winter, which we return to you as is proper, records belonging to their author. The Council notes that your account contains matters inconvenient to certain surviving reputations, and has elected, by a vote of six to two, to enter it into the official record regardless. The two dissenting votes are recorded by name, because we are trying, these days, to write things down.*

Emrys read the note twice. Then she read it aloud for Tamsin's benefit, and Tamsin laughed the short, sharp laugh of someone who had spent years watching Corvane destroy inconvenient documents and could not quite believe a government might do the reverse.

"They entered it anyway," Tamsin said. "Six to two."

"Democracy is very strange," Emrys said. "I see why kings avoided it."

She carried the scroll to the window, where the light off Vell harbor came through clean this time of year — the long spring the Codex had promised in its final line, though she was not yet ready to trust even weather to mean what it said — and broke the regency seal.

The pardon was written in the old formal style, all of its clauses stacked like masonry. *Whereas it has been established to the satisfaction of this Council that the tempest of the seventeenth of Thawmonth, nine years past, which caused the sinking of three vessels in the harbor of Vell and the death of the fisherman Dannel Ostra and his two sons, was not called, summoned, invited, or otherwise raised by the hand of Emrys Zhou; and whereas said Zhou was at the said time a minor of sixteen years and possessed of no training in the calling of storms; and whereas the exile pronounced against her was procured in part through testimony later shown to have been purchased by agents of the crown's then-Regent, acting in service of the sigil known as the Crown of Carreth; now therefore be it known that the sentence of exile is voided, her name restored to the rolls of Vell, and all lawful rights of person and property returned to her entire—*

There were conditions. Of course there were conditions. There were always conditions; Emrys had spent nine years keeping ledgers, and she knew that anything offered without conditions was either a gift or a trap, and governments dealt exclusively in traps dressed as gifts.

*Provided always,* the scroll continued, *that the said Zhou present herself at the harbor of Vell within one season of this date and stand witness, formally, to the amended finding; and provided further that she surrender no account of the events in Carreth save such as she chooses freely to give, the Council having determined that compelling testimony from those who gave it once under duress is a habit the kingdom means to break; and provided finally that she forgive the Council nothing that does not deserve forgiveness, this clause being inserted at the insistence of Councillor Vess, who wished it noted that a pardon is not the same thing as an apology, and that the kingdom owes her the latter besides.*

Emrys stood at the window for a long moment.

Nine years. Nine years of salt wind and borrowed bunks, of keeping other people's impossible accounts because no one would let her keep her own name clean, of flinching every time the sky went green over water. She had rehearsed this moment the way you rehearse a conversation with the dead — knowing it would never come, rehearsing anyway, because the rehearsal was the only version of justice available. And now here it was, in calfskin and six-to-two votes, with a clause demanding she be apologized to, drafted by a councillor she'd met exactly once, across a table, while frost climbed the windows of a palace where a king had been screaming politely inside his own skull.

"You're crying on the honey inventory," Tamsin said gently.

"I'm annotating it," Emrys said, and her voice cracked on the joke, and Tamsin came around the counter and put her arms around her, and for a while neither of them kept any records at all.

* * *

They went down to the harbor on a Thursday, because Emrys insisted on a weekday — Sundays drew crowds, and crowds made spectacles, and she had been a spectacle once already in her life, a sixteen-year-old girl standing on the customs quay while men who had never seen her call so much as a gust pointed at her and said *storm-witch* like a diagnosis. She would take her restoration in daylight, among working people, without ceremony.

Sable came too. She wore no colors — her oathbreaker's grey had been formally struck from the record along with everything else, but grey had become hers by now, worn into her like a riverbed into stone — and she walked half a step behind Emrys the way she still did in unfamiliar places, some old knight's habit neither of them had managed to break. At the edge of the quay she stopped.

"This is yours," Sable said. "I'll wait."

"It's ours," Emrys said. "You were there for most of the worst parts."

"I was there for the middle," Sable corrected. "The worst part happened before I ever met you. That's why I'm waiting." And when Emrys opened her mouth: "Wren. Let me do this one small thing correctly. I've been doing things incorrectly for twenty years. Let me stand at the edge of something and simply *not interfere*."

So Emrys walked the last forty yards alone.

The harbormaster of Vell was a broad woman named Ilsette who had held the post for six years and whose own predecessor had been one of the men who signed Emrys's original expulsion. Ilsette had the amended finding spread on a trestle table on the quay itself, in the open air, where anyone could read it — the regency's reforms had reached even here, apparently, or perhaps Vell had simply always been a town that liked its business done in public, salt-stained and visible. A modest crowd had gathered anyway. Fishermen, mostly. Dockhands. An old woman Emrys did not know until she was close enough to see the faded mourning band, black gone grey with years, sewn into a coat sleeve.

Dannel Ostra's widow.

Emrys stopped walking.

She had prepared words for the harbormaster. She had prepared words for the council clerk. She had prepared, in the sleepless small hours of the past week, words for almost everyone. She had not prepared words for the widow of the man her storm — *not* her storm, never her storm, and still, in the ledger of grief that no pardon could amend, *her* storm, because it had come out of a sky that answered to her blood — had drowned.

The widow looked at her. Her face was weathered into something past anger; anger was a luxury for the recently bereaved, and she had had nine years to spend hers.

"They say it wasn't you," the woman said. Not unkindly. Not kindly, either. Honestly, which was more than either.

"It wasn't me," Emrys said. "But it came from a sky that knows my name. I won't insult you by pretending that doesn't matter."

Something shifted in the woman's face — not forgiveness, nothing so complete, but the recognition of one honest creature meeting another. "My husband used to say the sea keeps honest books," she said. "Everything in, everything out. No page left blank where a debt should be." She glanced at the trestle table, at the amended finding with its careful clauses. "Sign your book, girl. Then go live the life they took. That's the only payment he'd want."

Emrys signed.

Her hand shook on the first letter and steadied after, the way hands do when they've done ten thousand signatures and know the work is in the steadying, not the shaking. *Emrys Zhou,* in her clerk's hand, precise as typeset. Beneath it, where the form asked for her mark, she wrote: *Called no storm. Answered to one. Both facts recorded.*

Ilsette read that, and raised an eyebrow. "Most people just sign."

"Most people weren't a footnote in someone else's ledger for nine years," Emrys said. "If my name goes back in the book, it goes back in whole."

* * *

That evening, in the guild office above the counting room, with rain starting against the windows — ordinary rain, west-to-east, no green in the clouds, she checked twice out of nine years' habit and probably would check forever — Emrys sat down to file the last document of the affair.

The regency's clerk had returned her testimony to her, as promised. It lay on the desk in its plain wrapper, forty pages of the account she had given under the winter lights of Carreth: the Codex, the summonings, the twelve dead chosen ones in Corvane's hidden ledger, the Regent's hollow face, the century of kings inside the crown. The Council had entered it into the record. Six to two. Written down.

And still it was not enough.

She had realized this somewhere over the mountains on the ride home, watching Sable sleep upright against her saddle in a way that spoke of years of sleeping wherever stopping happened, and the realization had settled in her like a splinter: the official record now contained what happened. But records contain facts; they do not contain *truths*. Facts were what. Truths were why. And there were truths in her that had never made it onto any page — small ones, human ones, the kind that never survive the trip into official language.

So she pulled a fresh folio toward her, dipped her pen, and began the document she had been composing in her head since the thaw: *A True and Personal Account of the Winter of the Sigil, Being the Things the Record Does Not Hold.* Filed, as all her filings were, in the guild's great cabinet under her own name — the first document she had ever filed there about herself.

*Item,* she wrote. *I was lonely for nine years and called it precision. Precision is a virtue. Mine was also a wall. I built my life so that no one would need me, because the last time I had been needed, the needing had drowned a man and his sons. This was cowardice wearing competence's clothes, and I commend to whoever reads this the observation that exile does not end when the sentence ends. It ends when you stop building walls. For me that was the night Sable Vey held a door against the Regent's guard and I understood she was not doing it from duty, because her duty had been struck from the record years before. She was doing it because she chose to. Choice is heavier than duty. It is also the only weight worth carrying.*

*Item. Corvane did not begin as a villain. He began as a man who found a way to feed people with impossible things and could not stop scaling the operation. Every horror he committed was, in his own ledger, a line item justified by the ones it funded. I catalogued his accounts for six years and never saw the rot because I was reading the columns and not the premise. Audit your premises. Columns will lie to you if the premise is rotten, and they will lie beautifully, in perfect sums.*

*Item. King Aldous, in the hour he was himself, asked me not to save him but to remember him — to write down that he had fought, every day, however briefly, from whatever small room of himself the crown had left him. I am writing it down. He fought. He is fighting still, in the quieter way of a man learning to rule without borrowed warmth, and some mornings I watch him fumble a ceremony he once performed flawlessly while something else wore him, and I think that fumbling is the bravest thing I have ever seen a king do.*

*Item. The dowager queen paid for her husband's life in memories, one per season, for thirty years, and remembers none of the reasons and all of the guilt, which is the cruelest arithmetic I have ever audited. If the kingdom builds her a statue, build it facing away from the palace. She never wanted to look at it again.*

*Item. I loved the storm. Before it ruined me, before it was taken from me in payment for tearing the sigil free, I loved it — the green light on the water, the pressure change like the world inhaling, the sense that the sky knew me and I it. I do not know if it will come back. Some mornings I wake and reach for it the way you reach for a limb that isn't there anymore. I record here, for whoever needs it: the loss does not shrink. You grow around it. Those are different things, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.*

The pen slowed. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the sill, and footsteps sounded on the stairs — Sable, home from the docks where she'd been consulting, absurdly, on harbor defenses, because a reformed oathbreaker with twenty years of soldiering turned out to be exactly whom port cities wanted advice from. Emrys heard her pause outside the door, heard her decide not to interrupt, heard the soft sounds of her settling in the next room to mend a cloak by lamplight.

*Last item,* Emrys wrote. *I believed for nine years that I was nothing without the gift. I was wrong, and I know I was wrong because I have spent the last season being nothing-without-the-gift in the fullest sense, and I have never in my life been less nothing. I have an apprentice who chose me back when choosing me cost something. I have an oathbreaker with no oath left who stays anyway. I have a guild that trades only in honest things, a name on the rolls of Vell, and a signature drying on a page that says the storm was never mine to answer for.*

*I keep ledgers. It is the work I was made for. And the first law of the ledger is that the record must be true, or everything built on it falls.*

*This record is now true.*

She signed it, sanded it, and filed it in the cabinet — third drawer, under Z, in the space that had waited empty for nine years.

When she straightened, Sable was leaning in the doorway, needle and thread forgotten in her hand, watching her with the expression Emrys had privately begun to think of as the *unarmored face* — rare, unguarded, given without ceremony.

"What did you file?" Sable asked.

"The truth," Emrys said. "All of it. Even the embarrassing items."

"Especially the embarrassing items?"

"*Particularly* the embarrassing items. A record without them is propaganda." She closed the drawer. "Besides, the Council got the facts. The guild gets the truth. That's the whole difference between a government and a family."

Sable crossed the room and looked down at the cabinet, at the neat brass label she herself had engraved last week — *POSSIBLE THINGS, HONESTLY OWNED* — and then at Emrys, and something in her face suggested a question she had been carrying since the thaw and had not yet found the right hour for. Emrys saw it forming. Saw her decide, for once, not to wait for the right hour but simply to take the next one.

"Wren," Sable said. "Now that the record's straight—" and stopped, because from the shelf by the window, where it had sat untouched and silent since the day they brought it home from Carreth, the Codex Vellum opened itself.

No wind. No hand. Just the sound of a cover lifting, and pages turning — slowly, deliberately, the way a sleeper turns toward morning light — and when Emrys crossed the room with her heart hammering against nine years of hard-learned caution, she looked down at the ancient vellum and saw ink fading, line by line, prophecy by prophecy, a century of foretold winters dissolving like salt in warm water.

Except one line. One fresh line, dark and new, in handwriting that was almost — *almost* — her own:

*Spring comes to Vell. No one is written in it. Come and see.*
