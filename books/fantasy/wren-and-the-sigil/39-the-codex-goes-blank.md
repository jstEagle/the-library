## 39. The Codex Goes Blank

The book began to die on a Tuesday.

Emrys noticed it the way she noticed everything in her work — not with drama but with discrepancy. She had carried the Codex Vellum from Carreth to Vell wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs for the whole of the journey south, half afraid the crown's remnants would want it back and half afraid it would want something from *her*, and all through the thawing roads she had felt its weight like a sleeping animal: heavy, warm, faintly resentful of being moved. Now it lay open on the long table in the guild's new reading room — the room that had once been Corvane's counting hall, before Tamsin had the ledgers burned and the floors scoured and the great iron door rehung on hinges that opened honestly — and the page she had read a hundred times was empty.

Not blank the way paper is blank before ink. Blank the way a page is blank after.

The words about tonight's date — about her name, written a century before her birth — were gone. The vellum sat smooth and pale as fresh snow, holding nothing, asking nothing. She touched it with one finger and felt only paper. No pull. No hum beneath the skin of it. For the first time since the night it had seized her and dragged her across a country in a single collapsing night, the book did not know who she was.

"Tamsin," she said, and her voice came out strange, thin as thread.

Her apprentice appeared in the doorway with a crate of bottled memories balanced on one hip — the honest stock now, each bottle labeled with its true provenance and its true price, because the Guild of Possible Things did not deal in anything a person could not truthfully own. Tamsin was twenty now by the calendar though she had been forty since she was fifteen, the way guild clerks went old early, and when she saw Emrys's face she set the crate down without being asked.

"It started three days ago," Emrys said. "I should have marked it in the ledger."

"Marked what?"

"The pages." Emrys turned the leaves, and there it was, the thing she had been refusing to see: whole quires gone pale, prophecy after prophecy dissolved back into animal skin. The storm that ruined her — the account of it, the *justification* of it, the century-old sentence that had made her ruin necessary — gone. The summons. The unbinding rite. The list of names, hers among them, that had been the book's whole terrible reason for existing. "It's eating itself. Or un-writing. I don't have the vocabulary for it and I hate that."

Tamsin leaned over the table, careful not to touch. In the lamplight her face was young again for a moment, awed and a little frightened. "Is it dying?"

Emrys thought about the kings. She had seen them pour out of the sigil-crown at dawn on the longest night — brief ghosts in ermine, a century of stolen men saying *thank you* in voices worn polite by screaming — and she had watched the last of them go the way smoke goes when a fire is truly out. A thing that had been fed for a hundred years, ending. It had looked less like death than like permission.

"I think," she said slowly, "it's finishing."

* * *

They catalogued it, because that was what they knew how to do. Emrys fetched the ledger — the true ledger now, the one with her handwriting in every column, no second set of books, no shadow accounts, because Tamsin had made her swear on the recharter that the guild would never keep two truths again — and they worked through the Codex page by page while the afternoon light went amber and then gray across the reading room floor.

Some pages held. The histories did — the dry records of the sigil's binding, the dowager queen's bargain, the war it had ended and the king it had begun to eat. Those stayed black on the vellum like scars stay in skin. But everything that pointed forward, everything prophetic, everything that had claimed the future as its territory, was dissolving in whatever order the book chose. By dusk, the pages concerning Sable's arrest had gone. By full dark, the forged prophecy — Corvane's counterfeit, the one that had named Sable as the sacrifice — had vanished so completely that Emrys had to check her own testimony transcripts to prove it had ever been written at all.

"It's not just going blank," Tamsin said. They had ordered supper up and not eaten it; the bread sat forgotten, going stale in its cloth. "Look at the order. It's erasing itself backward. Last things first."

Emrys turned back to the beginning and understood, and the understanding sat in her chest like cold water. "No. Not last things first. *Nearest* things first." She smoothed the final legible page flat. "It's unwinding from the present moment outward. Everything it wrote that has already come true — that's staying. Everything it predicted that hasn't happened yet—" she gestured at the growing whiteness, the quires gone pale as birchwood, "—is being returned."

"Returned where?"

"To whoever owns the future." Emrys heard her own voice go quiet. "Which, as of the longest night, is nobody."

They sat with that a while. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, in the warehouse that still smelled faintly of salt and impossible weather, the night clerks were checking seals on crates of unmade vows, and their footsteps came up through the floorboards soft as rain.

"You know what this means," Tamsin said at last.

"That we can't sell it."

"That we can't sell it," Tamsin agreed, "and that you're free of it." She said it plainly, the way she said most things, and Emrys found she could not answer, because underneath the relief there was something that took her a long moment to name, and when she named it she was ashamed of it: grief. A century-old book had hunted her across a country, used her body as a piece, marked her for slaughter in ink older than her grandmother — and some small traitorous part of her mourned it anyway, because it had also been the only thing in the world that had ever known she was coming. The storm had called her. The crown had wanted her. The book had *written* her. And now all of those hungers were going out one by one, and what remained was a woman with good handwriting and no gift, in a city that had exiled her, waiting to find out whether anyone wanted her without a prophecy's help.

She wrote in the ledger: *Codex Vellum — self-emptying. Provenance: fulfilled. Value:* and left the column blank, because she did not yet know how to price a book that had stopped lying.

* * *

Sable arrived on Thursday, riding hard through the last of the slush, with a letter from the regency council sealed in green wax and mud to the knee.

The palace had changed hands quietly, the way important things do. Aldous had abdicated within the week — Emrys had stood in the throne room and watched him set the empty circlet down on the cushion where the sigil-crown had lived for a hundred years, and he had done it with both hands, deliberately, like a man setting down a load he intended never to pick up again — and the regency that governed now was a committee of tired, decent people who kept sending letters north because the woman who had saved their king refused titles and would only accept correspondence. Sable had stayed south anyway. She said the capital made her itch. Emrys suspected the truth was simpler and closer, and she did not press, because pressing was what oaths did and Sable had sworn off oaths.

Sable read Emrys's report on the Codex standing up, coat still dripping, and frowned at the pale pages the way she frowned at broken gates.

"So it's finished with you."

"So it's finished with everyone." Emrys turned the leaves again. More white every day; she could almost watch it happen if she stared. "There are maybe thirty pages left with any writing on them. All history. Nothing forward."

"And that doesn't frighten you?"

Emrys considered the question honestly, which was a discipline she had learned late and paid for dearly. "It should. A year ago a blank future would have terrified me — mine especially. I built my whole life around not having one. Ledgers don't need futures. Columns balance or they don't." She laid her palm flat on the empty vellum. Cool. Ordinary. Dead as any book. "Now it just feels like a room after the furniture's been sold. Empty. But *ours* to fill."

Sable set the letter down. "What does the regency want?"

"For me to bring the Codex to Carreth. For study." Emrys smiled without much humor. "For safekeeping, they say. Which means they've already got scholars arguing over who gets to guard it, which means someone's already imagining what a prophecy book might be worth to the next ambitious fool. A blank book, Sable. They're fighting over a blank book, because it used to be dangerous and they can't tell the difference between danger and power."

"What will you do?"

"I'm going to finish watching it die first. Then I'll decide." She looked up. "Stay for this? I'd rather not be alone when it goes."

Sable unbuckled her sword belt and hung it on the peg by the door — an act so domestic, so unremarkable, that it caught in Emrys's chest like a fishhook. There had been a time when Sable's hands went to that belt the way other people's went to prayer. Now she hung it up like a hat.

"I'll stay," Sable said. "I wasn't planning to ride back tonight anyway."

That was a lie and they both knew it; she had planned exactly that, and had un-planned it somewhere between the letter and the door. Emrys let the lie stand. Some lies were just shyness wearing armor.

* * *

It finished on Sunday morning, in lamplight, with snow falling straight and gentle past the reading-room windows — ordinary snow, downward snow, the kind of snow that obeyed.

Emrys had slept badly and come down early, and Sable had come down earlier, and they had found the book together the way you find a stopped clock: suddenly, and with the odd silence of it filling the room.

Every page was white.

Emrys turned the leaves one by one, slowly, and Sable stood beside her with her hand resting light between Emrys's shoulder blades, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the turning of pages didn't say better. The histories had gone too, in the end — even the binding of the sigil, even the dowager's bargain, even the record of the war it ended. The book had kept only what was true and owed to no one, and apparently even that had been a loan.

The last leaf turned. White on both sides.

And then, because the world was not entirely merciful and not entirely cruel but mostly precise, Emrys saw the line.

One line. On the final page, low near the gutter, in the same old brown ink that had written her name a century before her birth — except that this line named no one. This line promised nothing to anyone in particular. It read:

*A long spring follows, and after it, years no one has written.*

Nothing else. No date. No name. No condition, no sacrifice, no *chosen*. Just the spring, and the openness after it, wide as a road with no destination inscribed.

Emrys read it aloud, twice, because the first time her voice shook. And then she did the clerk's thing, the reflex of a lifetime: she reached for her pen.

"What are you doing?" Sable asked.

"Recording it. Verbatim. If this is the last sentence the book ever speaks, it goes in the ledger exactly as written." She copied it out in her neat hand — *A long spring follows, and after it, years no one has written* — and dated it, and signed it, and then sat looking at the two versions side by side: the dead prophecy and the living record, the same words in different inks, one that had demanded everything of her and one that asked only to be remembered.

"You realize," Sable said, "that's the first line in that book that was ever kind."

"It's the first line that was ever *honest*." Emrys closed the cover. The Codex Vellum weighed almost nothing now, she realized — lighter than a ledger, lighter than a hymnal, just a book, just bound skins and thread. "It spent a hundred years telling people the future was already decided. Its last words are an apology."

"Books can't apologize."

"This one just did." She stood, and her knees cracked, and outside the snow went on falling in its law-abiding way. "Help me carry it to the vault. Not the forbidden shelf. The archive. Shelf nine, with the tax records and the shipping manifests — somewhere it can spend the rest of forever being exactly as interesting as an ordinary book, which is to say, not at all."

* * *

They shelved it together, and Tamsin witnessed the entry, and the three of them stood a moment in the archive's lamplit quiet among the honest boxes of the guild's remade trade — bottled thunderstorms sold at fair price to farmers who needed rain, unmade vows returned by post to the people who'd spoken them, memory-vintages labeled vintage by vintage with nothing hidden in the columns — and Emrys felt something settle in her that had been unsettled since she was seventeen years old and the sky broke over her head.

The prophecy was dead. Not defeated — dead. Fulfilled the way a debt is fulfilled: fully, finally, with receipt given. Whatever power had reached out of the past to write her life had released its grip, and the pages of tomorrow stood open and unwritten, and no one — no book, no crown, no guildmaster, no god — held the pen.

"Years no one has written," Tamsin murmured, testing the phrase.

"Someone will write them," Emrys said. "Just no one who knows how they end."

She locked the archive door behind them, and the lock sounded small and clean, like a period at the end of a very long sentence.

* * *

But that evening, sorting the post that came up from the harbor — bills, manifests, one crate-seal request, the ordinary grain of an honest guild's day — Emrys found a letter with no seal and no signature, addressed in a hand she did not know, and inside, a single sheet bearing a single question:

*If the future belongs to no one, wren-of-the-storm — who taught the spring to wait?*

She read it three times. She checked the ledger for debts outstanding, the manifest for anomalies, the window for watchers on the wet street. Nothing. Only the lamplight, and the smell of the sea coming in off the harbor, and somewhere below, the night clerks calling the hour in voices too casual to hide that they were happy.

Emrys folded the letter once, precisely, and slid it under the blotter — filed, not answered — and told herself it was nothing. A philosopher. A child. A joke.

But her hand lingered on the blotter a moment longer than a clerk's should, and upstairs, in the dark archive, shelf nine held its blank book close, like a secret not quite finished being kept.
