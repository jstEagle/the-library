## 40. Wren and the Sigil

Spring came to Vell the way honest things always did — slowly, without ceremony, and only after you had stopped watching for it.

Emrys noticed it first in the ledger ink. For a whole winter the guild's ink had frozen in its wells by mid-morning, and she had kept the books with a candle stub balanced beside her elbow, warming the pot between entries like a nursemaid. Now, on an ordinary Tuesday in the fourth month, she dipped her pen and found the ink flowing smooth and black as ever, no skin of ice on the surface, no need for fire at all. She sat very still for a moment, listening. Through the shutters of the counting room she could hear gulls arguing over the harbor, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the sound of meltwater running in the gutters — that bright, insistent chatter that meant the city had decided, at last, to live again.

"Wren!" Tamsin's voice from the stairwell, breathless in the way she got when she had news or trouble and hadn't yet decided which. "Wren, the *Kestrel's* come in, and half her manifest is lies."

"Half?" Emrys called back, already reaching for the shipping file. "That's better than last week. The *Merrow* was all lies."

She heard Tamsin laughing on her way down and felt the old, familiar settling in her chest — not the heavy settling of resignation, which had been her constant companion through the exile years, but something lighter. Something like a key turning in a lock she'd forgotten she owned.

The Guild of Possible Things kept offices above the salt warehouse now, in rooms that smelled of paper and tar and, since Tamsin had taken over the kitchen, aggressively good coffee. Below, the great doors stood open to the spring air for the first time in months, and clerks moved through them carrying crates that held exactly what their manifests claimed: salted herring, Vell blue wool, bolts of Carreth linen still smelling faintly of the northern pines. Honest cargo. Boring cargo. Cargo that would never once, in all its long life, be mistaken for a miracle.

It was, Emrys thought, dipping her pen again, the most radical thing they traded.

* * *

Sable arrived at midday, as she always did, though she had no duty requiring her here and never had. She came up the outside stairs two at a time — a habit from soldiering she'd never shed, even in peacetime, even in a city where the most dangerous thing on any given street corner was a fishmonger's cart — and pushed open the counting room door with her shoulder because her hands were full.

"Bread," she announced. "Actual bread, with actual wheat in it. The baker swears this year's crop. I bought four loaves out of sheer disbelief."

"You bought four loaves of bread as a scientific experiment."

"I bought four loaves because last winter we ate sawdust extender and I have *feelings* about wheat." She set them down on Emrys's desk, directly on top of the harbor manifest, which was very much like Sable, and then leaned against the windowsill in the patch of sun that fell there, and looked around the room with the particular satisfaction of a woman taking inventory of a life she had chosen.

Emrys watched her over the top of the ledger and felt the familiar warmth climb her throat. It still caught her off guard, after everything — how easy it was, how unremarkable. A year ago she had torn a god-crown off a king's head while a century of dead monarchs screamed politely inside it, and the price had been everything she was. And now the great drama of her morning was whether the *Kestrel's* captain had honestly declared his cargo of dye-stones, and the great love of her life ate bread in her doorway and argued about wool prices with Tamsin, and nobody anywhere needed her to be anything other than what she was.

"Page forty-one," Sable said, nodding at the ledger. "You've smudged it."

"I have not—"

"You have. There's a thumbprint right through the *Kestrel*'s tonnage figure. Very unprofessional. In my day, a clerk who smudged a royal ledger would be flogged."

"In your day, a knight who broke an oath was exiled into a blizzard, so perhaps your day wasn't the golden age of personnel management you remember."

Sable grinned — that crooked grin that had taken Emrys three weeks to learn to read and a lifetime, she suspected, to stop being undone by — and crossed the room. She pulled the stool close to Emrys's desk, the one that had been hers since midwinter, the one whose legs she had repaired herself with a length of rope and an unreasonable amount of pride, and sat down with the ledger pulled toward her.

"All right," she said. "Teach me."

They had been doing this since February. After the abdication, when Aldous — plain Aldous now, a tall tired man learning to cook his own meals and finding it harder than ruling — had asked Emrys to keep the true ledger of his reign, she had discovered something unexpected: that she liked teaching. That there was a pleasure in watching someone else's pen learn the shapes of her columns, in hearing someone else ask *why* a debt was entered twice, that reminded her of nothing painful at all. Sable had asked to learn, initially, she said, because a former oathbreaker needed honest work and knew nothing honest except swordwork. But Emrys had noticed she kept coming back past the point where any reasonable person would have learned enough, and she had begun to suspect that the ledgers were not really the lesson.

"Start with the *Kestrel*," Emrys said. "Tell me what's wrong with her manifest."

Sable bent over the page, tongue between her teeth. Sunlight moved across her shoulders. Outside, a clerk laughed at something, and the gull noise swelled and faded, and somewhere below Tamsin was shouting cheerfully at a stevedore about crate weights.

"Cargo declared as dye-stone," Sable read slowly. "Forty crates. But the tonnage is wrong — look, the ship's displacement says she's carrying heavier than stone. And the port fees are paid on the lighter weight." She looked up, pleased with herself. "So either she's smuggling something dense under the dye-stone, or..."

"Or?"

"Or the captain's just bad at arithmetic."

"He's bad at arithmetic *and* smuggling. Metal ingots under the dye-stone. He's been doing it since autumn; we let him run until we could prove the pattern, because Tamsin wants our enforcement to be about evidence, not suspicion." Emrys turned the page. "Now. How would you record it?"

Sable took the pen. Her handwriting had improved from the murderous scrawl of winter to something merely aggressive, and she wrote out the entry line by line while Emrys watched, correcting nothing, because today there was nothing to correct. When she finished she sat back and blew a strand of grey hair out of her eyes — more grey every month, since Carreth, since the deep cells, since the night she had held a throne room door alone against the Regent's guard with a reforged oath and a broken arm — and looked at what she'd written with the fierce satisfaction of a woman who had once guarded a queen and now guarded a column of figures, and found the second guarding no less honorable.

"There," she said. "True and entered."

"True and entered," Emrys agreed softly.

That was the motto now. Tamsin had painted it over the warehouse doors in letters visible from the harbor: *TRUE AND ENTERED.* Half of Vell thought it was a joke about bookkeeping. The other half had started bringing their disputes to the guild to be settled, because a place that promised true records was rarer than a place that promised miracles, and worth more.

* * *

In the afternoon they went down to the floor together, because the *Kestrel*'s captain had arrived to argue, and Tamsin had sent up word that he was arguing loudly.

He was a big man, Captain Ossler, weathered to the color of his own deck, and he filled the warehouse's front room the way a storm fills a valley. "I don't know what you think you've found," he was saying as Emrys and Sable came down the stairs, "but my manifest is clean as a christened hull—"

"Your displacement is off by eleven tons," Tamsin said. She was standing behind the receiving table with the real ledger open before her, nineteen years old and utterly unmoved, arms crossed, one boot up on a crate. Around her stood six clerks — the ones who had stayed, who had smuggled Emrys the keys through the impossible doors, who had chosen her back when choosing her had cost something. "Eleven tons of something denser than dye-stone. We weighed your crates at the dock, Captain. Would you like to see the weights?"

Ossler's eyes flicked to Emrys — and here it was, the thing she had braced for a year ago and still felt sometimes, the small electric shock of recognition. Word had traveled, of course. It traveled everywhere eventually, mutating as it went. Some versions said the exiled storm-caller had died tearing the crown free. Some said she'd become the new queen. One particularly persistent version, which Tamsin refused on principle to correct because it was good for business, said she could still call thunder if she truly needed to.

She couldn't. She had checked, in the early months, quietly, standing alone on the breakwater in weather that should have answered her. The sky had remained the sky: indifferent, beautiful, entirely closed. Whatever she had poured into the unbinding was gone, and the healers in Carreth said such gifts did not grow back, and she had made her peace with it the way one makes peace with an amputation — badly at first, in the cold dark of the tower room, and then, gradually, in the strangest way, gratefully. Because the storm had never loved her. It had only used her, the way the prophecy had, the way Corvane had, the way every power in her life had reached for her and taken. What remained, after, was just Emrys Zhou: a woman who was good at sums and bad at lying, who had a desk by a window and a lover who brought her bread and an apprentice who ran the best-run guild on the coast.

Just that. Only that. Everything.

"Captain Ossler," she said now, and watched him straighten like a man called before a magistrate. "Here's what will happen. You'll pay the duties on the true weight, plus a penalty Tamsin will set, which will be fair and which you will find annoying. Your ingots will be released back to you once the accounts are settled, because we're not customs and confiscation isn't our business. And your real manifest will be entered in the guild ledger, truthfully, which means next season every port from here to Carreth can see exactly what kind of trader you are." She laid her hand flat on the open book. "Or you can argue, and I'll enter the argument too, word for word. We have room."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, meltwater chattered in the gutters.

"...Penalty," Ossler said at last, with the air of a man stepping off a ledge and finding the drop short. "Fair, she says. Tamsin, your mistress has a cruel streak."

"She's not my mistress," Tamsin said, "she's my partner, and yes, she does. Sign here."

And that was that. That was the entire battle. No storms, no prophecies, no kings screaming politely inside their crowns. Just a man, a lie, a number, and the slow patient machinery of people who had decided that the world could be made slightly more honest one shipment at a time. Emrys signed where Tamsin indicated, and Ossler signed beneath her, grumbling, and the clerks carried his crates to the weighing floor, and the great ordinary day rolled on like a cart with a squeaky wheel toward evening.

* * *

Evening found them on the breakwater, as it often did, sharing the last of loaf number three. The harbor lay gold and pink in the low light, fishing boats coming home with the day's catch, and beyond the mole the open sea ran out to a horizon scrubbed clean by wind. Gulls hung motionless in the updraft off the wall. The air smelled of salt and thawed earth and, faintly, of bread.

"Do you ever miss it?" Sable asked. She was sitting close, shoulder to shoulder, her arm warm along Emrys's back. "The storm."

Emrys considered the question properly, because Sable deserved proper answers and had earned them across a year of asking hard things gently. Out on the water, a boat's sail caught the last light and flared orange.

"When it first left," she said, "I missed it like a limb. There were mornings I'd wake reaching for it — that feeling, you know, of pressure building behind my ribs, the sky leaning down to listen. I'd stand on the breakwater at dawn and wait to feel anything, and feel nothing, and go home hollow." She tore a piece of bread, handed half to Sable without looking. "But here's the thing I understand now, that I didn't then. The storm never gave me anything. It only lent. Every flash of it, every hour of power — it was all borrowed, and the interest was my whole life. My name, my city, my chance at being ordinary. The crown did the same thing, did you know? That's why the kings stayed in it. Not trapped, exactly. *Invested.* They'd given so much of themselves that leaving felt like dying." She looked at Sable. "You didn't stay invested. That's the whole difference between you and the Regent, in the end. Same oath, same master. You were willing to lose everything you'd put in."

"The Regent," Sable said, "was a hollow man wearing the first king's face. Let's not flatter me too extravagantly."

"I'm not flattering you. I'm entering it in the ledger." Emrys bumped her shoulder. "It goes in the true account. Chapter of Sable. Line one: *willing to lose everything she'd put in.*"

"And what does your chapter say?"

Emrys looked out at the water. A year ago — almost exactly, if you counted by the solstice — she had stood in a frozen court and burned every bridge home, denouncing the forged Codex and her own guild's trade in the same breath, with no idea what would be left afterward. What had been left was this: a rechartered guild trading in possible things; an apprentice who had grown into a master; a king learning humility in a small house with a garden; a pardon filed with conditions and a counter-account filed beside it, so that the record of that terrible winter would show both the official mercy and the messy truth; a prophecy book gone blank except for one promise of a long spring; and a woman beside her who had stayed with no oath left to justify staying.

"My chapter says I'm a clerk," she said. "Good at sums. Bad at lying. Exiled for a storm she never called, pardoned for it anyway, and glad — do you want to know the strange thing? Glad the pardon came late. If they'd pardoned me at twenty, I'd have spent my whole life trying to deserve the gift. Instead I spent ten years learning to live without needing anyone's permission, and when the storm finally demanded its interest—" she spread her hands, "—I'd already learned how to say no to powers that wanted me to sign."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then: "Wren."

"What?"

"That's what I came up the stairs to tell you this morning, before the bread distracted me. Tamsin's ordered new signet stamps for the clerks. All the old names are going on them — the ones who stayed. She showed me the proofs." Sable turned, and in the last of the light her crooked grin went soft at one corner. "Yours says *Wren*. Not Emrys Zhou, not the storm-caller, not the Unbinder or whatever they're calling you in the ballads this month. Wren. She said—" and here Sable pitched her voice into a fair imitation of Tamsin's brisk certainty, "*a wren is a small brown bird that sings louder than anything its size, and nobody writes prophecies about wrens, which is exactly the point.*"

Emrys laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, loud enough to scatter the gulls off the breakwater wall. Small brown bird. Louder than its size. No prophecies. She turned the idea over in her chest like a coin of pure gold and found no falseness in it anywhere.

"Well," she said, when she could speak. "Then it's official. Enter it."

* * *

They walked home along the quay as the lamps were lit, one by one down the waterfront, and the first stars came out over Vell, and the tide breathed in and out against the pilings with the patience of something that intended to go on forever. At the guild doors, Tamsin was locking up, keys singing on their ring, and she waved them through without breaking her count. Inside, the warehouse smelled of honest cargo and lamp oil. On the wall above the great doors, freshly repainted for spring, the letters caught the lamplight:

*TRUE AND ENTERED.*

Emrys paused beneath them, hand in Sable's, and did the thing she did every night now, the small private ritual no one had taught her and no prophecy had foretold. She took out the little pocket ledger she carried — the truest book she kept, the one with no columns for debts or cargo — and made the day's entry in her head, where it could never be seized or sold or forged:

*One ordinary day. Bread with wheat in it. A smuggler corrected. A name returned. Home.*

Tomorrow there would be another manifest with lies in it, and another entry to make true, and another morning of ink flowing smooth instead of frozen, and a hundred small ordinary battles worth fighting because no one had written them in any book a century ago. Tomorrow Sable would sit on her rope-mended stool and learn the trade columns, and get one wrong and argue about it, and stay anyway — staying, always, by choice rather than oath, which was the only kind of staying Emrys had ever trusted.

Tonight, though, tonight was complete. The lamps burned steady. The sea kept time. Somewhere overhead, small and brown and utterly unprophesied, a wren sang into the dark — louder than anything its size — and the last page turned, and the long spring went on.
