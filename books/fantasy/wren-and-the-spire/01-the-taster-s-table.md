## 1. The Taster's Table

There is an art to dying gracefully in front of the court.

I don't mean that the way it sounds. I mean that when a man has swallowed poison professionally for twenty years — or what might be poison, or might be nothing worse than bad fish and the cook's wounded pride — he learns to make even his survival look like ceremony. You lift the spoon as though it were a question you've been waiting all evening to answer. You hold the flavor on your tongue like a compliment you're considering. And then, if your throat doesn't close and your vision doesn't swim, you bow your head slightly and say, in the flat voice of a man reporting weather: "The pheasant is safe, Your Radiance."

Tonight the pheasant was safe. Tonight everything was safe. That was the problem with Tuesdays at the palace of Vessarin: nothing ever happened, and I had built my entire reputation on being ready for the alternative.

"Vasquez." Lord Chamberlain Aldous Vey didn't look up from his ledger. He never looked up from his ledger; I sometimes suspected the ledger contained a portrait of someone he preferred to my face. "You hesitated."

"I savor," I said.

"You hesitate, then savor. There's a difference, and the difference is four seconds during which the King could be dead."

"And yet His Radiance lives, and the pheasant was excellent, and the chef will sleep well knowing his saffron wasn't insulted by assassins. Shall I note anything else in the record, my lord? The vintage? The mood of the candles?"

Aldous finally looked up. He had the particular grayness of a man who had been important for so long that importance had eaten everything else about him, the way damp eats the inside of a wall. "You are paid to taste, Vasquez. Not to editorialize."

I am paid, I thought, to be the most expendable man in a room full of the most indispensable ones, which means I am in fact the only person here whose judgment anyone trusts. But aloud I said only, "Of course, my lord," because twenty years of careful living teaches you which truths are load-bearing and which are merely decorative.

Here is what the court of Vessarin knew about Lior Vasquez, royal food-taster: that he was punctual, sober, unremarkable, and had survived eleven years in the post without so much as a stomach complaint, which some found eerie and most found reassuring. Here is what they did not know: that the reason I had never once sickened was that I had spent those same years teaching my body, drop by patient drop, to welcome things that should have killed it. A taster who can be poisoned is a taster who dies. A taster who cannot is a taster who lies very convincingly with a straight face and steady pulse, and there were nights — more of them every year, since the Edict — when lying was the only honest work left in this city.

Because that's the other thing. The other life. The one that begins when the palace lamps gutter down and I trade a servant's corridor for a drainpipe and a drainpipe for the crooked mouth of Tanner's Row, where the night market opens its stalls under awnings the watchmen pretend not to see, because half the watchmen buy from them too.

* * *

The law against magic in Vessarin is called the Clean Hearth Act, and like most laws named after comfortable domestic images, it exists to burn something down. Passed nine years ago by the usurper's first council, it declares that charms, wards, blessings, hexes, memory-works, and "all traffick in unreal things" are crimes against the crown. The penalty for selling is the block. The penalty for buying is a fine, because the council understood its customers. You do not fine the hand that feeds you.

I tell myself I sell charms for money, and some nights that's even true. But money is a thin broth of a reason, and underneath it sits something older and less flattering: I sell charms because I remember — vaguely, the way you remember a room from childhood — what it felt like when magic was simply part of the city, like rain or bread or gossip. Before the Spire fell silent. Before the border wards came down and the fae realms went dark behind their curtain. Before the stars over Vessarin began, one by one, to go out.

Ah — yes. The stars. I'll come to those. Everyone comes to those eventually now. But that night, the night this story properly begins, the sky above Tanner's Row was merely the ordinary bruised black of a capital city that has forgotten how to look up, and I had a widow to see.

Maren Ostrey lived above her late husband's chandlery, in rooms that smelled of tallow and grief. She was waiting for me at the window exactly as arranged, and she let me in with hands that shook.

"You said no promises," she whispered, before I'd even got both feet through the sill. "You said whatever it does, it isn't a promise."

"I did. It isn't." I set my satchel on her table and began unwrapping the cloth inside it, layer by layer, the way you peel back bandages from something precious. Underneath lay a small disc of gray-green stone, no bigger than a coin, carved with a spiral that seemed — if you looked too long — to be turning. A memory-charm. One of six I had. Possibly the last six in the city; the inquisitors had been thorough, and thoroughness, unlike courage, is cheap.

"It holds a memory," I said. "One you give it. Yours, or someone else's, if you have a token that carries theirs. While you wear it, the memory plays against the inside of your mind like light through a window. Not real. Never real again. But near it."

Her husband had died in the ward-collapse riots three winters past, though officially he had died of a fall. Officially, everything in this city dies of falls. She had a lock of his hair wrapped in wax thread, and she held it now the way people hold things they cannot afford to set down.

"How much?"

"For you? Nothing." I said it quickly, before pride could get between us. Maren had once slipped me a heel of bread through the kitchen gate of the chandlery when I was fourteen and newly orphaned and pretending not to be starving, and she did not remember doing it, and I have never told her. This is the arithmetic of kindness: it compounds silently, across decades, until one night a stranger climbs through your window and refuses your coin.

"That's not how markets work," she said suspiciously. Widow though she was, she had been a merchant's wife.

"No," I agreed. "This isn't a market. It's a debt. Twenty years old. Take the charm, Maren."

She took it. She pressed the lock of hair into the spiral's center and it sank in like a stone into still water, and her breath went out of her in a sound I have heard perhaps forty times in my career and have never once gotten used to — the sound of a person meeting a ghost that loves them.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, he's *laughing*. Why is he laughing?"

"Because you kept his shop swept," I said. "They notice things like that, the remembered dead. They notice what you carried on without them."

She cried. I stood in the corner of her candle-smelling room and watched her cry, and felt the peculiar double loneliness of my profession: present for the most intimate moments of strangers' lives, belonging to none of them. A taster at the table of everyone's grief, permitted to swallow but never to sit.

"You should stop," Maren said later, at the door, suddenly fierce, holding my sleeve. "The inquisitors hanged a charm-seller in Coppersmith Square last month. They made his family watch. You should stop, Lior Vasquez."

She knew my name. That startled me more than the warning did.

"Everyone knows the taster's name," she said, reading my face. "You think nobody sees you. Everybody sees you. That's the trouble with being invisible — you forget you cast a shadow."

I went home the long way, along the canal, because the long way let me think, and thinking is the one luxury my two lives never otherwise allow.

Everybody sees you. Absurd. My entire art was unseeing — the court saw a function, the market saw a masked broker with a soft voice and fair prices, and no one, anywhere, had ever once looked at the actual man, because I had spent twenty years sanding him down to nothing. Essential and invisible, like a keystone: everything rests on it and no one admires it.

Except that lately the sanding hadn't been sitting right. Lately, when I swallowed for the court, I caught myself listening — really listening — to the food, as though the flavors were trying to say something. Lately the poisons I tested on myself in the small hours (a taster must keep his immunity sharp, or so I told my conscience) had stopped tasting like enemies and started tasting like messages. Bitter letters. Sweet warnings. Last week a trace of wolfsbane in the King's wine had hummed against my tongue in a frequency I had no name for, and I had declared the wine safe and drunk a full glass alone in my rooms just to feel the humming climb into my teeth.

And last night — last night, walking home from the market — I had looked up, the way no one in Vessarin looks up anymore, and counted the stars.

There had been fewer. I was nearly sure of it. Three gone from the Crown constellation since midsummer, and the oldest stargazers in the night market had started leaving offerings on their windowsills, little dishes of honey and salt, the way you feed something that is leaving you.

The city said the stars were dying of natural causes. The priests said it was punishment. The usurper's criers said it was nothing at all, please go about your business, report all traffick in unreal things to your nearest inquisitor.

But I had spent twenty years training my body to know the difference between poison and medicine, between harm and hard cure, and when I looked up at those missing stars, my blood — my careful, poisoned, patient blood — sang a single low note of recognition.

Something was coming. Something was calling. And whatever it was, it was calling to the exact shape of the hollow I had spent two decades digging in myself.

I reached my door. The lamp inside was lit, which was wrong — I always left it dark; a taster keeps no habits a knife can find — and for one absurd heartbeat I thought of Maren's warning, of Coppersmith Square, of shadows.

Then I opened the door, and the smell hit me first: smoke, and under the smoke something green and ancient, like a forest remembering itself. And through my window, over the rooftops toward the palace district, the sky had begun to flicker — not the stars going out this time, but all of them at once, guttering like a hall of candles in a draft.

Somewhere below, in the night market I had just left, a woman screamed.

I did what any sensible man would do, the kind of man I had spent twenty years pretending to be.

I dropped my satchel, and I ran toward the fire.
