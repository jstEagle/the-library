## 2. Night Without Stars

I want it on the record that I was not doing anything wrong when the sky went out.

Well. Not anything *particularly* wrong. I was standing in the shadow of the dyer's awning in Copperlane Market with my hand halfway into a merchant's coin-purse — a purse so fat it was practically begging for redistribution — when the stars above the Spire began to go out.

Not dim. Not clouded over. *Out.* One by one, like candles being pinched by a very large, very patient hand. The whole market did that thing crowds do when something impossible happens: they went quiet all at once, a thousand people inhaling in unison, and then everyone started talking at once, which is what crowds do instead of screaming.

"Clouds," said a woman near me, firmly, to no one.

"There aren't any clouds," said her companion.

"There are now."

I looked up. The sky was clean as a scrubbed pot and blacker than the inside of my own skull, and where the stars had been there was nothing — not darkness exactly, but an *absence*, the kind of nothing that has teeth. And at the center of it, above the palace hill, the Spire stood against that nothing like a broken finger pointing at a wound.

Then the wards went.

You've heard the stories since, everyone has, but you didn't hear them first from me, so let me tell you what it actually felt like, standing under them when they fell. It sounded like bells. Every ward-bell in the city — the little silver ones hung over doorways to keep out ill luck, the great bronze ones in the temple towers, the ones sewn into the hems of old women's cloaks because my grandmother swore by them — every single bell rang once, a single cracked note, and then went silent forever. And underneath the ringing, or maybe behind it, I heard something else. Something vast shifting its weight, like a dog turning over in its sleep, except the dog was the border between our world and the other one, and it had just stopped guarding the door.

The purse I'd been lifting dropped back onto the merchant's belt untouched. That's how you know it was serious. I have stolen through plague quarantines, through two royal funerals, through the Great Flood of the Tannery Quarter, and I have never once voluntarily given up money.

Then the market caught fire.

* * *

Here's the thing about panic: it's faster than fire and dumber than both of us put together. Nobody set the market alight, near as I could ever work out afterward. A lamp got knocked over, maybe, or a cookfire shoved aside in the crush — some small ordinary disaster that would have been stamped out in a heartbeat on any other night. But tonight a thousand people were trying to run in eleven directions through a space built for three, and the flames found the silk merchants' stalls and went up like a celebration.

I did what I always do in a crisis, which is get small and get gone. There's a drain-grate behind the spice-seller's row that drops into the undercity, old smugglers' tunnels from before the covenant made smuggling between realms a matter of state rather than trade. I'd had that grate memorized since I was nine years old. It is, if I'm honest, the closest thing I have to a home address.

I was three steps from it when I saw him.

He was face-down beside a collapsed fruit stall, half-buried under an awning that had come down burning, and even from ten feet away I could smell what was wrong with him. Not blood-smell, though there was that too. Something else — bitter, green, like crushed stems, like the air after lightning. His hands were pressed flat against the cobbles and his lips were gray, and he was breathing in these thin whistling pulls like a man drinking through a straw someone was stepping on.

Now, in fairness to me, my first instinct was the correct professional one: keep walking. Dead men can't report you to the watch, but dying men can grab your ankle, and either way he was not my problem. The city was ending. Everyone in it was on their own. That's not cruelty; that's arithmetic.

But here's what happened instead. A beam from the fruit stall's frame — burning, groaning, the whole works — chose that exact moment to give way, and it came down toward him, and my body moved before my brain finished voting. I crossed those ten feet, hooked him under the arms, and dragged, and we both went down behind the overturned stall as the beam hit the cobbles where his head had been and burst apart in a spray of embers.

"Why," I said out loud, to myself, to him, to whatever god had just made me stupid, "did I do that."

His eyes opened. Gray eyes, unfocused, swimming. He looked at me the way drowning men look at driftwood — with gratitude and resentment in equal measure, because driftwood is salvation but it's also work.

"The stars," he rasped. His voice was hoarse, like he'd been swallowing something all night. "Did they—"

"They're gone. All of them. Congratulations, you're caught up." I got a shoulder under him. He weighed more than he looked, the way all half-dead men do, as though dying adds ballast. "Can you walk?"

"I don't—" He coughed, and what came up was dark, and it steamed wrong in the cold air. "Market. People. Help them."

"People are helping themselves. It's the one civic duty everybody shows up for." I hauled him upright and he swayed and I took most of his weight, which I want noted was deeply unfair, because he was a grown man and I was, then as now, generously described as slight. "Who are you? You're dressed like a servant. Palace livery."

"Lior," he said. Just that. Then, with a strange, stubborn dignity: "I'm nobody."

"Nobody, you're about to be dead, so move your nobody self."

* * *

The grate fought me — rust and panic and a man's whole weight hanging off my shoulder — but I've opened harder locks with less motivation. We went down into the dark together, him half-collapsing down each rung while I braced him from below, and the last thing I saw of the surface world was the market burning against that starless black, orange light climbing a sky with no ceiling anymore. Somewhere above, the watch bells were ringing alarm, and somewhere beyond them, faint as a memory, I swear I heard music. Fiddle-music, sweet and strange, coming from outside the walls. From past the borders.

From the direction of the fae lands, where the wards used to be.

I pulled the grate shut over us and sat down hard on wet stone and tried to breathe.

The undercity is quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Old tunnels, old bones, the drip of water that's been dripping since before my grandmother's grandmother. Lior had slid down against the wall and lay with his head tipped back, throat working, and in the dark his skin had a faint sheen to it, like oil on water, like something was *coming out* of him.

"You're poisoned," I said.

"I'm a taster." He said it like that explained everything, and I suppose it did. "Royal food-taster. Twenty years. What hit the market tonight wasn't smoke." His laugh came out cracked. "It was the wards breaking. Ward-dust. Everyone who breathed it—"

"—is dying," I finished. The tunnels suddenly seemed very full of echoes. Half the city had been in that market. "And you're not."

"Not quickly." He turned his head and looked at me properly for the first time, and there was something in his face that unsettled me more than the burning sky had — recognition, almost. Like he was seeing something in me I hadn't shown him yet. Because I hadn't. Shown anyone. Ever. "You pulled me out. Why? You don't know me."

"I misjudged the distance to my escape hole," I said. "It won't happen again."

He smiled. It was a terrible smile, gray-lipped and shaking, and it was also, annoyingly, warm. "Liar."

"Thief," I corrected. "Get your insults accurate if you're going to die in my house." I dug in my coat — past the lockpicks, past the two purses I'd lifted earlier that felt suddenly like artifacts from a lost civilization — and my fingers found it. The shard. On its cord around my neck, where it had lived for six years, through three fences who refused to touch it and two buyers who died before the sale and one long stretch where I'd convinced myself it was just a pretty rock.

It was hot. First time ever. Warm as a held bird, humming against my sternum like it had just remembered it had a job to do.

Lior's eyes went straight to my fist. To the light leaking between my knuckles, faint and blue-white, the color the missing stars should have been.

"Oh," he said softly. And then, in the tone of a man reading his own death warrant and finding it interesting rather than frightening: "*Oh.* You're carrying a piece of the Spire."

I should have denied it. Any sensible thief would have denied it, run for it, buried him in this tunnel and been in Vellamare by dawn with a new name. Instead I stood there in the dark holding a glowing stone over a dying stranger, and heard myself say:

"If I show you this, you'd better know what to do with it. Because I stole it six years ago and every bad thing in my life since has been downhill, and tonight the sky ate itself, and I am starting to think those facts are related."

He pushed himself up the wall, slow as syrup, until he was sitting mostly upright. The poison-sheen on his skin pulsed once. He held out his hand.

"Wren," I said, before I could stop myself. Giving a name to a mark, to a dying man, to anyone — it's the first rule you break and the last one you learn to stop breaking.

"Wren," he repeated, like he was tasting it, and of course he was; tasting was the whole of him. "Show me."

I lifted the cord over my head. The shard swung between us, blazing gently, and the moment it touched his palm, the light flared — and he didn't flinch, didn't burn, didn't scream, though the heat of it was singing the hairs on my wrist at a foot's distance. He just closed his fingers around it and breathed out, long and steady, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there.

"Well," said Lior, the royal food-taster, the nobody, the man I'd saved for reasons I still couldn't account for, as the shard's light filled the tunnel and somewhere far above us the burning city went on screaming. "I think you'd better tell me exactly where you stole this from. And Wren — I think we have until morning before the inquisitors start knocking on doors. Yours will be first."

That's how the worst night of my life ended: in a smuggler's tunnel, holding a piece of a broken god, with a poisoned stranger smiling at me like I was the answer to a question I'd never asked.

I've had worse mornings since. But only barely.
