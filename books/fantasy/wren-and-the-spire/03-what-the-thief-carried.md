## 3. What the Thief Carried

I woke to the smell of wet stone and someone watching me.

The second part I knew before opening my eyes. Twenty years in a palace teaches you the weight of a gaze—the bored flick of a chamberlain, the hungry assessment of a rival, the flat professional attention of a man deciding whether you're worth poisoning. This was none of those. This was the look of someone taking inventory.

I opened my eyes. The ceiling above me was vaulted brick, older than the palace, weeping a slow damp that had gone green at the seams. Somewhere nearby, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had been dripping since before either of us was born. Wren sat on an upturned crate three feet away, knees drawn up, chewing on something that might once have been bread, and staring at me like I was a locked strongbox she hadn't yet picked.

"You snore," she said.

"I've been told I'm dying," I said. My voice came out as gravel. "It seemed rude to do it quietly."

"You're not dying." She tossed me the rest of the bread. It was stale enough to qualify as architecture, but my stomach seized it with embarrassing gratitude. "You were dying. Yesterday. In the market. There's a difference, and the difference is that I carried you down two ladders and through a flooded cistern while the sky ate itself."

I remembered pieces. The stars going out—not clouded, not veiled, *gone*, as if someone had reached up and pinched them from the fabric of things—and then the wards along the city gates flaring white and shattering like sugar-glass, and then the fire. Gods, the fire. The night market had burned with a color I'd never seen flame make, a violet-edged gold that climbed without heat until it did, all at once, everywhere.

"You dragged me into a cistern," I said.

"You fell into a cistern. I mostly aimed you."

"And why would a stranger aim a half-dead man into a flooded cistern during a riot?"

She shrugged, which cost her nothing and told me everything: she didn't know either. "You looked like someone worth not letting burn. Don't make it strange."

"Too late," I said. "I'm making it extremely strange."

* * *

We spent the day learning our prison. It wasn't one, exactly—it was an undercity, the kind of place every old capital grows beneath itself like scar tissue, a warren of forgotten cellars and culverts where the smugglers' lamps still hung from hooks rusted soft. Wren knew it the way I knew the palace kitchens: by heart, by habit, by the particular loneliness of people who live in spaces others have abandoned.

She talked. That surprised me. Thieves, in my experience, hoard words like coin, but Wren spent hers freely—small sharp observations about which culvert flooded in spring, which lamp-oil was cut with tallow, which of the smugglers who'd worked these tunnels ten years ago had been hanged and which had been promoted. She never said anything about herself. I noted that. I noted it the way I note everything: quietly, completely, and for later use.

Twice, boots passed overhead—many boots, in formation, the measured tread of soldiers rather than the scatter of panic. Once there came a sound that made even Wren go still: a horn, deep and wrong, blown from the direction of the palace. Not a call to arms. A call to *surrender*, pitched so that everyone in the city understood it was addressed to them personally.

"Inquisitors," Wren said, when it faded. "Corvane's lot. They'll be going door to door by tomorrow."

"What are they looking for?"

And there it was again—that inventory stare. Weighing me. Deciding.

"You really want to know?" she said. "Because knowing is the kind of thing that gets a man hanged twice."

"I've been swallowing poison professionally for twenty years," I said. "My relationship with consequences is already quite intimate."

Something moved behind her eyes—not trust, nothing so generous, but perhaps the recognition of a fellow professional. Then she dug under her shirt, worked loose a cord at her neck, and held out her fist.

"Swear you won't touch it."

"I swear nothing until I know what it is."

"Then just look."

She opened her hand.

I want to describe what I saw honestly, because what I saw first was almost nothing: a shard of stone the size of a knucklebone, gray-white, unremarkable, the kind of thing a mason sweeps off a cutting floor. My eyes, trained on palaces and their poisons, found it dull.

But my body did not find it dull. My body reacted the way it reacts when a dish is dosed—a cold brightening at the back of the throat, a prickling along the gums, the fine hairs of my forearms lifting in a wave. Every taster learns to read his own flesh like a ledger. And my flesh, looking at that little gray stone, read *power*, and read *old*, and read—this was the part that made my hands shake—*familiar*. As familiar as the taste of the palace well-water I'd been quietly immunizing myself against for two decades.

"Where did you get this?"

"Six years ago. The Spire." Her voice changed—flattened, guarded. "Before they walled the ruins up. Everyone knows thieves got into the old Spire grounds; half the relics on the black market wear its dust. Most of it's junk. This—" she closed her fist around the shard, slow, almost reluctant, "—this sang."

"Sang."

"Hummed. Whatever. When I took it, it hummed against my palm like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. And every year since, on the longest night, it does it again. Just once. Just enough to remind me I stole something that steals back." She scowled at her own hand as if it had betrayed her. "I've tried selling it four times. Four buyers. Every one of them died within a season—fevers, falls, one fellow eaten by his own dogs, don't ask. The stone comes home to me every time. I stopped trying after the third buyer. The fourth was greed."

I stared at her. Pieces were moving in my head, sliding together with the horrible smoothness of a mechanism finally oiled.

"The ward-poisons," I said.

"What?"

"When the covenant wards stood—when the border between here and the fae realms was whole—the palace maintained them. Quietly. Tasters weren't only tasting food, though no one outside the kitchens ever knew that. We tasted the ward-tinctures too. Trace preparations drawn from Spire-stone residue, dosed into the household water over years, so the royal line's blood would carry the covenant's signature. Protection against fae debt, against glamour, against the small hungry magics that slip across borders." I heard my own voice go distant, clinical, the voice I used in reports. "The program ended twenty years ago when the old king died. But I'd already been drinking the stuff for years by then. Daily. In increasing doses."

Wren's frown had gone very still. "So?"

"So my body has spent twenty years learning to hold what that stone is made of." I looked at my own hands—ordinary hands, careful hands, hands built to be unnoticed. "When you showed it to me, I didn't just see it. I *read* it. The way I'd read a poisoned dish. Because it isn't poison to me. It's the same lesson I've been studying my whole life, in a language I already speak."

"That's a lot of words to say you're weird about rocks."

"Wren." I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was the only mercy available. "That shard is a fragment of the Spire itself. The anchor-stone. The thing the inquisitors are tearing the city apart to find. You haven't stolen a relic. You've been carrying the keel of the world in your shirt for six years."

For a long moment she said nothing. The drip in the corner kept its time. Above us, faintly, more boots.

Then she laughed—one short, hard syllable with no joy in it, a laugh like a slammed door. "Of course. Of *course*. Six years of dead buyers and nightmares and a rock that won't let me go, and the answer is that I should have given it to the soup-man." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Why you? Why does it want the *soup-man*?"

"It doesn't want me," I said slowly. But even as I said it, doubt crept in, because the shard had hummed when she opened her fist, hadn't it? Faintly. And my teeth had ached in answer, a low harmonic, like a tuning fork finding its twin. "Or—it may. I don't know. What I know is this: anyone else who touches it burns. Buyers dead within a season. But I could hold it. I could read it. Maybe more than read."

"You could *die*, is what you mean."

"I could die doing anything. That's the trade I made years ago. Every meal I swallow is a wager that today isn't the day the dose is doubled. At least this death would be for something."

"Don't." The word cracked out of her harder than she meant it—I saw her flinch at her own voice. "Don't do that noble-resignation thing. I've seen it. Nobles do it right before they hand their problems to someone poorer."

"I'm not noble. I'm a taster."

"You're a man who drank poison for twenty years so other people wouldn't have to." She was on her feet now, pacing the length of the cellar, restless as water. "That's not invisibility, whatever you tell yourself. That's the opposite."

I didn't answer. I couldn't, quite. For twenty years I had built myself into a man the world slid past—unremarkable face, unremarkable name, essential and unseen, the perfect vessel. I had thought it cowardice, or craft, or both. I had never once thought it might be *preparation*. That some patient hand—fate, or the covenant, or merely the arithmetic of a paranoid old king—had been seasoning me for this the whole time.

It should have felt like comfort. It felt like being read for the first time in my life, and finding annotations in the margins written by someone else.

* * *

Near dusk—or what we guessed was dusk; the undercity keeps its own hours—Wren built a tiny fire of smugglers' coal, smokeless and mean, and we shared a can of barley broth she produced from somewhere I decided not to ask about. The shard lay on the brick between us, wrapped in her scarf. Even wrapped, I could feel it. Like sitting near a lit window in winter.

"They'll find us," she said, eventually. "Not tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. But Corvane's inquisitors have ways—hounds that smell magic, mirrors that show truths, all the ugly toys the usurper's been stockpiling. And when they find us, they'll want this." She nudged the bundle with one toe. "Question is what we do before then."

"*We*," I said.

Her jaw tightened. "Don't make it strange."

"Too late," I said again, and this time, briefly, she smiled—an actual smile, quick and crooked and immediately regretted, but real.

Then she went serious in a way that made the fire seem smaller. "There's talk in the tunnels. Smuggler-talk, so take it with salt. Says the wards didn't just fall—they were *pulled*. From inside. Says the usurper's men have been hunting Spire-fragments for a year, buying up every relic that sings. Says there are others. Other pieces. Maybe—" she hesitated, hating the word before she said it, "—other bearers. People the stones stick to, the way it stuck to me."

"Other people carrying the world's keel in their shirts," I said. "Comforting company, at least."

"If the fragments can be brought together—" She shook her head. "That's priest-talk. Old stories. The covenant broke when the Spire broke, and everyone knows broken things stay broken."

"Everyone also knew the stars were permanent," I said, "and last night I watched them get pinched out like candle-flames."

She glared at me. Then, slowly, she unwound the scarf, and the shard lay bare in the coal-light, gray and humble and humming its unfinished note.

"Touch it, then," she said. "If you're so sure. Show me what your twenty years bought."

I reached out. My hand was steady—taster's discipline, the one inheritance of my trade I'd always valued—and my fingers closed around the stone.

It was warm. It was warm the way a living thing is warm, and it *knew* me—the hum climbing through my bones, my old immunized blood answering it note for note, and for one vertiginous instant I felt the shape of something vast: a lattice, a covenant, a web of starlight and law with a hole torn in its center. And at the edges of that vastness, faint as lights across a dark harbor—

Other shards. Other bearers. Far away, scattered, singing.

I pulled my hand back. My heart was slamming. Wren was staring at me, and in the coal-light her expression was stripped of everything clever: pure, naked hope, the most dangerous thing a thief can carry.

"Well?" she whispered.

"There are others," I said. "I felt them. And I think—" I swallowed, tasting the old ward-bitterness that lived forever now at the root of my tongue, twenty years of quiet preparation dissolving into purpose. "I think the road east is about to get very crowded. Because if the inquisitors are hunting bearers door to door in this city, then somewhere beyond the walls, the free cities are about to learn exactly what's coming for them."

Above us, muffled by stone and earth, the horns began again.
