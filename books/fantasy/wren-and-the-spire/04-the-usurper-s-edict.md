## 4. The Usurper's Edict

*Corvane*

I have hanged men for less than a missing shard, and I would hang them again, gladly, if hanging produced results. It does not. Hanging produces silence, and silence is not the same as obedience—though in my experience the two are often mistaken for one another, usually by men who end up on the scaffold themselves.

The edict took me an hour to draft and three days to make honest. Martial law, curfew at dusk, gates sealed, every door subject to inspection. And above it all, rendered by the best limner money could buy from a woman who had never once looked at her own portrait: the thief. Dark hair cropped short, quick eyes, a mouth that suggested laughter at inappropriate moments. *WANTED ALIVE*, the placard said, because dead things cannot be interrogated and I had questions that required answers measured in years, not minutes.

My clerks pasted her face onto every gate in the capital before dawn. By midmorning the rain had begun peeling the corners, so that by afternoon she seemed to be lifting away from the walls of her own accord, escaping even in effigy. I found this irritating in a way I could not justify to my subordinates, so I did not try.

"You believe she still has it," said Under-Inquisitor Hask, who is competent in the way a blunt knife is competent—it cuts, but you must put your whole weight behind it.

"I know she does. The wards fell the night the stars went out. Something anchored them, and something unanchored them, and both somethings are stone." I touched the cold iron of my signet ring, which does not warm no matter how long I wear it. "A girl does not survive what happened in that market district by accident. Someone pulled her through it, or she pulled herself, or—" I stopped. There was no need to say the rest aloud. The Spire did not choose randomly. It had chosen me once, and look how well that ended.

"Shall we sweep the undercity?"

"The undercity is a thousand throats and every one of them lies. No. We let hunger work. We seal the gates, close the markets, and wait until turning her in becomes worth more than sheltering her. Sentiment has a price, Hask. Everything does. That is the first law, and unlike most laws, it has never once been repealed."

Hask bowed and went to make my cruelty orderly. I stood at the window a while longer, watching the placards flutter, watching the city learn to be afraid in a new direction.

Somewhere below, a woman was running with a piece of heaven in her pocket.

I intended to take it back. Not for the usurper—for myself. But the distinction, I find, matters less each year.

* * *

*Lior*

There is a particular skill to reading a room while appearing to read nothing at all, and twenty years at the taster's table taught me all its variations. So when I saw the crowd gathered at the Tanner's Gate, quiet in the way crowds are only quiet when they're frightened, I adjusted my course without adjusting my face.

Wanted placards. A dozen of them, dripping in the drizzle. And on every one—

"Well," said Wren beside me, in the flat tone of someone meeting their own likeness for the first time. "They flattered me. Look at that jaw. I've never had a jaw like that."

"WANTED ALIVE," I read. "Congratulations. Dead is cheaper."

"That's the part that worries me." She pulled her hood lower, and I noted—professionally, clinically, with the part of my mind that catalogued poisons and their antidotes—that her hand was shaking. "Alive means questions. Alive means Corvane."

"Inquisitor-General Corvane," I said, and something in my chest went cold and small. "He swears the dishes at the usurper's table personally. He trusts no one else's palate." I had seen him twice from across a banquet hall: a grey man in a grey coat with eyes like ledger entries. Men like that do not post notices out of hope. They post notices out of arithmetic.

We moved. You learn to move in a city wearing your face on its walls—you become nobody, shoulders down, gait shortened, a man with somewhere tedious to be. Wren learned faster than I'd expected. Thieves, I suppose, already know how to be invisible; I'd merely spent two decades being essential instead, which is invisibility with better meals.

The market district was closed. The fish stalls shuttered. Even the memory-sellers had gone dark, their little booths of outlawed comfort standing empty like birdcages after the cat's been through. My own stock—three charms sewn into my boot heel, a fourth in my hatband—felt suddenly less like livelihood and more like evidence.

"We can't stay in the capital," Wren said, low, as we cut through a tannery yard reeking enough to make soldiers wrinkle their noses and pass. "Every gate has my face. Every road out has a checkpoint. And the shard—" she pressed her ribs where, beneath the shirt, a fragment of fallen star rode against her skin like a second heartbeat, humming faintly whenever I came near. "It's getting louder, Lior. Whatever's happening to the wards, it knows we're here."

"Then we leave tonight," I said, "through the river gate."

"The river gate is triple-manned."

"Yes. Which is why we won't be going through it. We'll be going through Maren Ostreicher's washhouse."

She stared at me. "Who?"

"A widow," I said, "who once bought a charm from me to forget a man who died badly. It didn't work. She came back and asked for her coin back, and when I told her charms don't refund, she asked instead what else I sold." I permitted myself a thin smile. "Kindness, Wren, is the one currency that compounds."

* * *

*Corvane*

The free cities' council convened at noon by proxy-mirror, seven faces floating in silvered glass, seven voices arguing about surrender as though surrender were a policy rather than a symptom.

Marrowgate spoke first, naturally. Marrowgate always speaks first; pride travels fast along trade roads. "Inquisitor-General," said their speaker, a merchant-noble named Casselin with the well-fed certainty of a man who has never been hungry near a soldier, "we petition the crown to reconsider the edict. Sealing the borders strangles commerce. Whatever this thief carries—or doesn't carry—is surely not worth the ruin of legitimate trade."

"Legitimate trade," I repeated, tasting the word the way I taste everything, looking for the poison beneath. "Master Casselin. Three nights ago the stars went out. Your caravans crossed a border with no ward on it and found, on the far side, meadows where the grass grows toward the sun and sheep that sing back when you name them. Is that the commerce you wish to protect? Or shall we discuss what else walks through an open door?"

Silence, of the expensive kind. Good.

"The edict stands," I continued. "The gates stand. The reward stands. And I will remind the council that the usurper's patience is a finite resource currently being replenished by armies he believes—falsely, thanks to certain arrangements I have made—to march under restored starlight. When he learns otherwise, he will want someone to blame. I would prefer he blame a thief rather than a council. But blame is flexible."

One of the seven mirrors—the fifth, Greyharbor, old Vetch with her ink-stained fingers—did not argue at all. She simply watched me with an expression I recognized, because I wear it sometimes when I think no one can see: the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't like.

Vetch knew something. Vetch had perhaps guessed what I was, or what I had refused to be, twenty-five years ago at the foot of the Spire. I made a note. Notes, in my experience, keep longer than mercy.

After the mirrors dimmed, Hask reported: no sightings at the east checkpoints. The undercity sweeps had turned up nothing but smugglers, who were hanged anyway on general principle—one must keep the tools sharp.

"She'll run for Marrowgate," Hask said. "It's the nearest free city. Everyone runs to Marrowgate."

"Everyone runs to Marrowgate," I agreed, "which is why I have already written to Marrowgate. Pride makes people predictable, Hask. Send riders. If the council won't deliver the bearer, I will collect her myself—and Master Casselin will discover that hospitality has a price too."

Everything does. I am very tired of the first law. I intend, someday, to break it.

* * *

*Lior*

Maren Ostreicher's washhouse sat hard against the river gate's outer wall, which is precisely why she'd bought it—laundry steam, inquisitors assume, is just laundry steam. She met us at the back door before I'd finished knocking twice, which meant she'd been watching the alley since dusk.

"You look terrible," she told me. It was, I reflected, the closest thing to affection anyone had offered me in years.

"You look prosperous."

"Grief agrees with some women." She stepped aside to let us in, and her eyes flicked to Wren—quick, assessing, the gaze of a woman who reads faces the way I read dishes. Then down to the placard corner Wren had torn off a wall, crumpled in her pocket, because apparently my companion collected souvenirs of her own manhunt. Maren's jaw tightened. "So it's true. The stars, the wards. And you've brought the reason into my washhouse."

"Maren—"

"Don't 'Maren' me, taster. You sold me a charm to forget a dead man and it didn't work, and when I raged at you, you didn't take offense and you didn't take more coin. You told me forgetting was a door that locks from the inside, and someday I'd want to walk back through it, and you were right." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "Do you know what that's worth, in a city where everyone's afraid? Being right kindly. Once. To a stranger."

Wren shifted beside me. I felt her looking at me differently—not with suspicion, exactly, but with recalculation. People do that, discovering I have a history. Most assume the history is sordid. Sordid is easier to dismiss than decent.

"The gate," I said gently. "Maren. The laundry carts go out at dawn?"

"They go out at dawn. And there's a new checkpoint—two soldiers checking every basket, every bundle, faces against a list." She was already moving as she talked, pulling sheets, sorting whites, building the ordinary architecture of an extraordinary morning. "But soldiers check what soldiers expect. They expect contraband in baskets. They do not expect a thief folded into a delivery of the governor's personal linens, because no thief alive would volunteer to smell like another week of starch." She glanced at Wren, and something almost like humor moved through the grief in her face. "No offense intended, girl."

"None taken," said Wren faintly. "Some taken. Mostly none."

"And you?" Maren asked me, hands never stopping. "You're known at the palace. Your face isn't on any wall yet, but Corvane keeps lists of everyone who ever touched the trade. I've heard the name Vasquez whispered in queues."

I had wondered when she'd ask. I had prepared, as I prepare everything, a careful answer. What came out instead was the truth, unpracticed: "I'm going with her."

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Steam rose between us.

"Twenty years I wondered what kind of man sells forgetting," she said finally. "Now I know. The kind who's been buying it for himself the whole time." She thrust a bundle of linens into my arms—my disguise, my cover, my shroud of clean starch. "Dawn. Don't be late, and don't be grateful. Gratitude's just debt wearing its good coat, and I'm owed nothing."

* * *

*Wren*

Dawn came up grey and starless, the way dawn had come up for six days now, and I lay folded in a laundry cart between the governor's bedsheets and tried not to breathe like a fugitive.

Lior walked beside the cart. Of course he did—he couldn't fit in it, and he wouldn't let me ride alone. Through a gap in the linen I watched him at the checkpoint, offering the soldiers his papers with the bored competence of a man delivering linens, and watched one soldier hold those papers a beat too long, and watched Lior say something—quiet, dry, unremarkable—and watched the soldier laugh and wave us through.

Later, on the river road with Marrowgate's smoke on the horizon, I asked him what he'd said.

"He asked if the governor had changed his laundress. I told him governors change everything eventually. It's practically philosophy. Soldiers don't arrest philosophy; it reminds them of school."

I laughed. It surprised me, coming out—I hadn't laughed since the night the sky went dark. And then the shard against my ribs hummed, soft and rising, and Lior went still beside me, and we both turned to look.

Ahead on the roadside stood a weathered stone, older than the road, older probably than the city behind us—and around it, the air shimmered like heat-haze in cold morning light.

"Lior," I said. "Tell me that's laundry steam."

"It is not," he said, "laundry steam."
