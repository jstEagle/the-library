## 5. Road of Hollow Bells

The first thing you learn on the road out of a dying city is that grief has a smell. Smoke, mostly. Wet ash and scorched grain and the sweet-rot of things that burned too fast for anyone to bury properly. The second thing you learn is that grief travels slower than fear, so you'll be three villages gone before it catches up to you, usually at night, usually when you've finally stopped walking.

I was two days ahead of my own grief when Lior asked me why I'd saved him.

"Not complaining," he said, hands raised like I was a magistrate about to fine him. He had a way of holding his hands up that suggested he'd done it often enough to have opinions on technique. "Just cataloguing. A thief who risks her neck for a stranger is either a saint or a bad businesswoman, and you've already told me saints don't exist in your district."

"Your district," I corrected. "You're the palace one. I just work near it." We were walking the old pilgrim road toward Marrowgate, the free city everyone said would still be free once the panic finished eating everything else, though nobody saying it sounded like they believed it. "And I didn't save you because I'm nice. You were between me and the cellar door."

"You dragged me by both arms."

"I was being thorough."

He laughed — a small, surprised sound, like he'd forgotten he owned it — and then coughed, because smoke does that to you even days after the fire. I kept walking. Behind us, the road unwound through country that used to have wards on it. You could tell where they'd been, now that they weren't. The hedges grew wrong. The streams ran a half-beat late, like music with the drummer missing. And in the fields, here and there, stood stones that nobody remembered putting up.

That's what happens when the border dies. It doesn't leave. It *settles*.

* * *

Hollow Bell was the third village we came to, named for its church bell, which had been cracked since before anyone living was born and which rang anyway, sometimes, when the wind came down off the moor. Except the wind hadn't come down off the moor in two days, and when we walked into the square at dusk, the bell was ringing.

Not ringing, exactly. Tolling. Slow and deliberate, the way bells do for funerals, except the rope was hanging slack and no one stood in the tower. The villagers stood in their doorways instead, every single one of them, faces turned toward the bell like sunflowers toward something considerably worse than the sun.

"They're not seeing us," Lior murmured.

"They're seeing something."

An old woman sat on the church steps, and she was the only one whose eyes moved. They found mine across the square, and they were wet, and they were afraid, but underneath the fear was something worse: recognition.

"It's been sixty years," she called out, voice cracking like her bell. "We paid in sixty-one. My grandmother carried the coin herself. Tell them it's paid. Tell them the debt is *paid*."

"What debt?" I asked. "Who are you talking to?"

She looked at me as if I'd asked who the sky belonged to. "To them, girl. To the other side. The wards held them back and the wards made promises, and the promises were ours to keep, and now the wards are gone and the promises are coming due." Her hands shook around a small cloth bag. "Every family in this village owes something. Grain. Years. Names. Mine owed silver, weighed against a child's first cry. We paid it in sixty-one. Tell them it's *paid*."

Lior touched my elbow, very lightly. In the taster's trade, I'd learn later, that touch meant: *don't eat anything, don't drink anything, don't agree to anything.* "The shard," he whispered.

It took me a moment to understand what he meant, because I'd gotten good at not thinking about it. But there it was, against my sternum where it always sat, wrapped in three layers of rag under my shirt: the stone I'd lifted from a dead man's strongbox six years ago because it was pretty and warm and heavy enough to be worth something. The shard of Spire-stone that had gotten us chased out of our own city. And it was humming. Not sound, exactly. More like the feeling of a struck tuning fork pressed to your breastbone, a note that lived in the bones and refused to die away.

The old woman's eyes went to my chest. Hers went wide.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, you're one of *theirs*."

"I'm nobody's," I said, and turned to go.

The bell tolled once more, right behind me, close enough that I felt it in my teeth, and every door in the village closed at the same instant like a held breath let out.

* * *

We camped that night in a shepherd's hut on the high ground above Hollow Bell, because neither of us wanted to sleep in a village where debts came due, and because Lior argued — reasonably, infuriatingly — that if something was going to collect, better it find us awake and running than asleep and not.

"You could have told me it did that," I said. We'd built a small fire, small being relative; Lior had insisted on building it inside a ring of ash he drew himself, muttering something about thresholds. "The humming. Back in the undercity. When you figured out what I was carrying."

"I told you what I knew when I knew it."

"You *tasted* it, Lior. You put a piece of the Spire's anchor-stone on your tongue like it was a suspect consommé. That's not telling me what you know. That's showing off."

His mouth twitched. In the firelight he looked less like a corpse the river had returned and more like an actual person, which I resented slightly, because it was harder to keep someone at arm's length when they had a face. "Twenty years of swallowing poison in small doses," he said. "Arsenic in the sauces, nightshade in the wines, ward-residue in everything that came out of the old covenant vaults — because the usurper's kitchens never threw anything away, they just served it and waited to see who died. My body learned to take things in and not break. Including—" he nodded at my chest, at the rag-wrapped weight of it, "—that. When you handed me the shard in the undercity and I didn't burn, I thought maybe my whole miserable career had been training for something. That's a dangerous thought, Wren. I needed a day or two before I trusted it."

"And now?"

"Now I trust it the way I trust a dish that smells perfect and hasn't killed the cat." He fed a stick to the fire. "Cautiously. With a backup plan."

I almost smiled. Almost. Then the hum in my chest changed key, and the temperature dropped like a door had opened somewhere onto winter, and standing at the edge of our little ring of ash was someone who had not been standing there before.

Tall. Too tall, in the way of things that are tall because height means nothing to them. It wore a coat the color of frost on a window, and its face was beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, and it looked at me the way the old woman had — recognition, but colder, older, patient as interest accruing on an old loan.

"Bearer," it said.

"No," I said.

It tilted its head. Somewhere below us, in the village, the cracked bell began to toll again, slow and delighted.

"The stone acknowledges you," the herald said. Its voice was like water finding its level. "The stone remembers the covenant, and the covenant names its bearers. You carry a fragment of the anchor. Therefore you are named."

"I *stole* it," I said, and my voice came out uglier than I intended, all the fear in it stripped bare. "Six years ago. From a dead man's box. I didn't ask for it, I didn't choose it, I took it because it was worth food for a month, and I have been *hunted* for it, and people have *burned* for it, and if your stone wants to name somebody, it can name literally anybody else—"

"Wren." Lior's hand found my shoulder. Steady. Warm. Annoyingly calm. "Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe."

"Then yell. Yelling's fine. I yell at the soup." He stepped forward, putting himself — the fool, the absolute palace-bred fool — between me and the fae herald. "She carries it, yes. What else?"

The herald considered him with the mild contempt of something immortal regarding something brief. "The bearer gathers," it said. "Two more fragments sleep in Marrowgate, in hands that do not yet know what they hold. The road will test her. The Spire will test her worse. When the fragments gather at the root, the covenant will make its request." Its gaze slid past Lior's shoulder, back to me, and there — beneath all that beautiful cold — was something that might, in a kinder face, have been pity. "It always makes a request. That is what covenants are."

"Requests can be refused," Lior said.

The herald smiled. I will be hearing that smile in my sleep for the rest of my life, whatever length that turns out to be. "So the last bearers believed. Ask the stones how that went." And it stepped backward into the dark without moving its legs, and the frost-colored coat folded shut around it like a book closing, and it was gone, and the bell stopped mid-toll as if someone had gripped the clapper.

The silence afterward was enormous.

"Well," I said, when I could. "That went well."

Lior sat back down heavily. For a long moment he stared into the fire, and I watched him do the thing I was learning he did when the world got too large: shrink it down to a problem he could taste. "Marrowgate," he said finally. "Two more fragments. Two more bearers who don't know what they are."

"We're not going."

"We're going."

"*We* are not going. *I* am not going. I'm a thief, Lior. Thieves run. It's the whole job description. Some enormous dead king's rock picked me out of a crowd and now I'm supposed to walk toward the people hunting it, and gather its friends, and answer its *requests*? No. I find a fence in the morning, I sell this cursed thing to some collector with more gold than sense, and I disappear so thoroughly that even the stars won't remember where I went." I heard my own voice shake and hated it. "That's the plan. That's always been the plan."

He didn't argue. That was the worst part. He just reached over, slowly, and laid his hand flat on the ground between us, palm up. Open. An offer, not a demand. Twenty years of tasting other people's food so they wouldn't die, and his instinct, faced with a terrified stranger yelling at the dark, was to simply be there, ungloved, unhurried, taking the risk that she might bite.

"I watched the market burn," he said quietly. "I watched people I'd tasted for — people whose children I'd kept alive without them ever knowing my name — stand in the smoke and have nowhere to go. And the reason they had nowhere to go is that everyone who could have helped decided it wasn't their problem. I decided it wasn't my problem, for twenty years. One dish at a time. Very carefully. Very invisibly." His fingers curled slightly, not closing, just resting. "I'm done being invisible, Wren. If you run, I understand. Truly. But I'm walking to Marrowgate tomorrow."

The fire popped. Below us, Hollow Bell slept its uneasy debt-ridden sleep, and the shard against my chest hummed its stubborn note, warm as a stolen thing, heavy as a promise.

I looked at his open hand for a long time.

Then, because I am a thief and a bad businesswoman and apparently, now, a bearer — whatever that costs, whatever it takes — I put my hand in his, and said, "Fine. But if another one of those coat-things shows up, you're standing in front."

"That," said Lior Vasquez, royal food-taster, charm-peddler, dead man walking, "is the most sensible thing anyone has offered me all week."

We broke camp before dawn. And far to the north, past the hollow-belled hills, the road ran straight toward Marrowgate — and toward whatever the covenant would ask of us next.
