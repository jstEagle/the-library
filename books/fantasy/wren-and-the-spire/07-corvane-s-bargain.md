## 7. Corvane's Bargain

I have been called many things in the last three years—Inquisitor-General, the Usurper's Hand, the Man Who Put Out the Stars—and I will tell you plainly that only the first of these is on my letters of appointment. The second was a gift from frightened men who needed a monster to blame for their own cowardice. The third is a slander.

The stars went out on their own. I merely declined to die bringing them back.

But I get ahead of myself. Let me begin where all honest confessions begin: with a table, and a man sitting at it, waiting for me to lie to him.

* * *

The prisoner they brought me in the autumn of my thirtieth year was a scholar of the old covenant, one of those gray-robed archivists who spent their lives cataloguing the Spire's geometry as though understanding a thing were the same as loving it. He had been caught teaching forbidden resonance theory to a merchant's daughter, which was, under the Edict of Quiet, a hanging offense. The usurper—who was not yet the usurper then, merely the Lord Protector with a very good tailor—wanted the girl found and the archive burned. I wanted something else. I wanted to know why a man would hang for arithmetic.

So I sat across from him with a decanter of wine between us—my own habit, even then, to make every interrogation feel like a dinner party—and I asked him what the covenant actually demanded.

He laughed. That's the detail no ballad ever gets right. They paint the old bearers as saints, luminous with purpose, and here was this broken-nosed little man laughing at me through split lips.

"It demands everything you are," he said, "and it asks nicely."

"Explain that to a practical man."

"The Spire isn't a machine, Inquisitor. It's a promise. It was made when both worlds were young and neither trusted the other—a stone set at the border, and four bearers to hold its corners, the way four hands hold a blanket over a shared bed. And because a promise held by force is no promise at all, it takes payment. Memory. The bearer gives up the years that matter most—the self they built, the life they'd cling to—and the Spire holds those memories in trust so that neither world can weaponize them." He shrugged his ruined shoulders. "You don't lose your soul. You lose your *story*. And the covenant keeps it safe until someone else is willing to pay for theirs."

"And if no one pays?"

His laughter died. What replaced it was worse than fear. It was pity.

"Then the border frays," he said. "Slowly, at first. Stars gutter. Wards thin. Old debts surface in places that forgot they owed them. And men like your Lord Protector find that the darkness is very useful for holding power, and decide that perhaps the dark is simply how things should be."

They hanged him anyway. Orders were orders, and I have never been a man who mistakes curiosity for mercy. But I did not sleep that night, and the next morning I did something I had never done before: I opened the sealed archives and read the names of the last four bearers.

Three were dead or scattered. The fourth name stopped my pen mid-signature.

It was mine.

* * *

You should understand—I had been chosen before I understood there was a choosing. A bearer is marked young; the Spire's resonance settles into certain bloodlines like damp into a wall, and the archivists kept their lists the way farmers keep seed stock. My family had served the covenant for six generations without once being called. I was the seventh. They had intended to tell me on my twentieth birthday.

My twentieth birthday came and went in silence, because by then the Lord Protector's men had already burned the archive, and the archivist who knew my name was three weeks from a rope.

So I lived twenty years as an unblessed thing—a man carrying a covenant inside him the way one carries a swallowed stone, unaware, unburdened, *unpaid*. When the stars finally failed—when the last ward snapped like a harp string and the night came down black as spilled ink—I felt it happen. I want to be honest about that, since honesty costs me nothing now. I felt the Spire call my name. Not in words. In *hunger*. It reached for the memory I would have paid, and found nothing offered, and it hurt, the way an empty socket hurts, and I lay on the floor of my quarters and understood that I could end the pain in one of two ways.

I could give it my story.

Or I could take its power instead.

* * *

Do not judge me too quickly. You were not lying in the dark that night. You did not feel what I felt: the whole weight of the covenant pressing on a single human chest, asking a man to erase himself—to hand over every year that had made him *him*, his mother's kitchen, his first commission, the face of everyone he had ever loved—on the bare promise that the stone would keep it safe. Keep it safe! The wards had just shattered. The archivists were dead. The Spire itself had fallen. And it wanted me to trust it with the only thing I truly owned?

The old bearers called it sacrifice. I called it what it was: surrender dressed in liturgy.

So I refused.

And having refused, I discovered what refusal unlocks. The Spire does not punish a bearer who declines—that would be force, and force voids the covenant. But the power it would have channeled through willing hands has to go *somewhere*, and a man standing at the root of a falling tower can catch the debris. I took the fragment of anchor-stone the archivists had pried loose for study. I learned to draw on it—not to hold the border open, but to close it behind me, to seal myself off from the covenant's hunger entirely. I built false starlight above the Lord Protector's camps, small cold lamps hung in heaven's absence, and the soldiers marched beneath them and believed the sky still loved us.

That is the truth of the darkness, since you'll hear a dozen versions: the stars did not abandon us. One man declined to bleed for them, and they went out like candles in a draft, and I have spent eleven years making sure the world blames anything but me.

Is that villainy? Perhaps. But consider the alternative I was offered. Four bearers, each surrendering their dearest years, forever, generation upon generation, to prop up a bargain struck before either realm could count the cost. Consider that the covenant has been failing for centuries precisely because fewer and fewer people were willing to pay. Consider that the free cities sat in comfort while the border frayed, and mourned loudly only when the bill came due.

I chose safety. Someone had to. The righteous were busy dying beautifully.

* * *

Which brings me to Lior Vasquez.

I had his file within two days of the market fire. Royal food-taster, nineteen years' service, spotless record, poison-immunity certified annually—which should have been impossible for any natural constitution and told me at once what he was: a man whose body had spent two decades quietly metabolizing ward-residue, a living sieve for exactly the resonance the Spire shed as it died. The taster's guild trains its people in small doses. The covenant, apparently, had trained him in large ones without his knowledge or consent. There is an irony there I intend to enjoy fully before the end.

And the thief. Wren. Seventeen, perhaps eighteen, quick as spilled mercury, with a stolen shard of anchor-stone sewn somewhere in her ragged clothing and no idea—none whatsoever—what she carried. She thought she'd lifted a pretty rock. She has since been informed otherwise by events.

Here is what troubles me about them, and I record it here because this journal is the only place I am truthful: they should not work. A taster and a thief. A man whose whole art is swallowing harm and a girl whose whole art is taking what isn't given. Between them they constitute a complete refutation of my philosophy. I built my life on the principle that safety is purchased, hoarded, defended. And these two keep giving things away—the taster his anonymity, the thief her loot, her comfort, her excellent instincts for self-preservation—and the more they give, the stronger they become. It is the covenant's arithmetic. It is also, I confess at three in the morning over cold tea, beginning to look like the better ledger.

Which is why I must close that account soon.

* * *

I received word at dusk: they'd surfaced at Marrowgate, the free city most likely to shelter them, traveling under the protection of a widow named Sefa Ilvane whose memory-charm debt the taster had quietly forgiven years ago. Small kindnesses. Repaid at scale, as I keep telling the council, though they mistake my meaning for threat rather than instruction.

I sent no soldiers. Soldiers frighten a man like Vasquez into heroism, and heroism is contagious. Instead I wrote a letter.

I write it now in fair copy, and I will deliver it myself, because some bargains require a face. The terms are generous, and generosity from me is more frightening than any army:

*Master Vasquez—you are a professional man, and I address you as one. The woman you travel with carries stolen property of the crown. Return it, and the Edict of Quiet is suspended in your case entire. Your charms, your dealings, the widow and the others—amnesty, sealed and witnessed. You may return to your post. The palace kitchens remember you fondly; I have checked. You will taste for a king again. No one need ever know what you almost became.*

*Consider what you are being asked to trade away. A girl you have known for weeks. A stone you cannot keep. Against this: your life, your craft, your name—all the careful invisible years you built, returned to you intact.*

*I am the only man alive who knows what the covenant costs, because I am the only man alive who refused to pay it. Ask yourself why I am still here, whole and hale, while every bearer who said yes is ash or exile. Ask yourself which of us chose wisely.*

*Come alone. Come soon. The army marches at moonrise whether or not we conclude our business, and I would prefer we conclude it.*

There. Read fairly, that is a kindness. I am offering him everything he spent twenty years building back, gift-wrapped, in exchange for one rock and one guttersnipe. Any rational man signs.

And yet.

I met him once, you know—years ago, at a palace function, before either of us mattered to the other. He tasted a suspect dish of quail that had already sickened two courtiers, and he swallowed it without blinking, thanked the chef for the seasoning, and walked out past me into the corridor. I remember thinking: *there is a man who has decided exactly what he will endure, and will endure precisely that, no more, no less.* I admired it. I built my whole doctrine on it.

What I did not understand then is that a man like that doesn't endure harm because he is a coward. He endures it because he has measured the cost of everything else and found it higher.

He will come to Marrowgate's gate. He will read my letter. And he will look at the girl beside him—at the thief who pulled him out of a burning market for no reason she could name—and he will do the arithmetic I dread.

Because here is the flaw in my beautiful offer, the crack running through my false starlight: I am offering Lior Vasquez his old life back.

And I am no longer certain he wants it.

Outside, the camp bells are ringing assembly. The army marches at moonrise, under my cold and faithful lamps, toward a city that thinks itself safe behind walls and borrowed courage. Let them think it. I have taken cities before.

But tonight, for the first time in eleven years, my hand is not steady on the pen, and I find myself wondering—quietly, professionally, the way one wonders about a poison one cannot yet identify—

what the taster will serve *me*.
