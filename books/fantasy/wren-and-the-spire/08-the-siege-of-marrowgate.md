## 8. The Siege of Marrowgate

I want it on the record that I said we should leave. I said it at dawn, standing on Marrowgate's eastern wall with a cup of terrible tea in my hands and a horizon full of torchlight where there should have been nothing but fog.

"That's not an army," I said. "That's an argument for leaving."

Lior sipped his own tea—of course he drank his tea while the end of the world assembled itself across the marshes; the man had swallowed poison for twenty years, what was a bad vintage to him—and said, "The gates will hold."

"The gates are wood."

"Old wood."

"They're going to burn old wood," I said, "with us behind it."

He didn't argue. That was the worst part. Lior never argued when he'd already decided something, which meant that somewhere between the tea and the torchlight he had already decided something, and I was too busy being right about the army to notice.

The child—Pell, eight years old, dreamer of impossible geometry, owner of exactly one coat with one sleeve longer than the other—came up onto the wall because Pell came everywhere, like a burr with opinions. She looked out at the fires and said, "They've got false stars on their banners."

"I know," I said.

"No." Pell pointed with the solemnity only small children and scholars can manage. "Real false ones. Corvane made them. They're wrong inside. Like a song played backwards."

I looked. And she was right—the banners didn't reflect the light, they *made* it, a cold little glow stitched into cloth, the same color as the night the stars went out. The usurper's whole march was lit by stolen starlight, and every step of it said: *we took the sky once, and we can take it again.*

Marrowgate's bells started ringing then. Not the alarm bells. The hollow ones—the ones the free cities rang when the wards were alive, back when ringing them meant *the border holds, you may sleep.* The wards were ash now, but the bell-ringers rang anyway, out of habit or defiance or sheer bloody-mindedness, and I remember thinking: hollow bells for a hollow wall, and here we all stand anyway.

* * *

By noon the army had stopped pretending to be polite.

Corvane's herald rode up under a white flag and read the terms from a scroll so long two soldiers had to hold it open, which felt like theater, because everything Corvane did felt like theater. The gist was simple: surrender the bearers, surrender the shard, and the city would be spared. Refuse, and the usurper's engines would teach Marrowgate what the word "spared" used to mean.

Master Ondrelle, our disgraced scholar, stood on the wall listening with his arms crossed and his face doing something complicated. He'd spent thirty years being laughed out of universities for believing the covenant was real. Now it was real, and it wanted his memories, and the price of belief had come due all at once.

"Well," he said to me, after the herald rode off. "I always assumed I'd die obscure."

"You're not dying," I told him. "We're leaving tonight. The river gate, Lior found a way through the—"

"Wren." He smiled at me, and it was the first genuinely unembarrassed smile I'd ever seen on him. "I spent my whole life arguing with people who wouldn't listen. Let me do one useful thing with it."

I should have argued harder. I want that written down too.

* * *

The first assault came at dusk, and it wasn't men—it was debt.

That's what the fae remnants called themselves now that the wards had fallen and their old bargains had rotted loose: debts. Things owed across the border for centuries, finally collected. Corvane had bound a dozen of them with his false starlight, chained them into shapes like siege-towers with too many joints, and sent them walking at the walls. Arrows went into them and came out the other side confused. The city's few hedge-mages threw everything they had, and the debts absorbed magic the way sand absorbs rain.

It was Lior who worked out the counter, because of course it was. Poison-trained, ward-steeped, a man whose blood had been quietly marinating in covenant-residue for twenty years—he walked the wall touching the hedge-mages' charms, tasting each one like a suspect dish, and telling them which to throw away.

"This one's dead," he'd say, flicking a talisman aside. "This one's lying. This one—" he held up a scrap of embroidered linen, sniffed it, grimaced, "—this one is honest, give it to the archers on the north tower."

And it worked. Gods help me, it worked. For three days we held, and I stopped saying we should leave, and I let myself believe in old wood and stubborn people.

That's how it gets you. Hope is just the trap setting its spring.

* * *

On the fourth night they took Pell.

I'll tell it plainly, because dressing it up would be a lie, and I've spent my whole life around lies—I know good tailoring when I see it, and this deserves none.

The river gate. Lior's way through the marshes. It was supposed to be our escape route, and instead someone sold it—one of the councilmen who'd voted to surrender, I learned later, though learning it later meant I couldn't put my knife in him at the time, which is probably why I'm still alive to write this. Inquisitors came through at low tide, quiet as drowned things, and they went straight for the child's sleeping loft in the scholar's tower.

Pell woke, because Pell always dreamed true, and she screamed the Spire's geometry at them—a shape that shouldn't fit in a human throat—and it bought her ninety seconds. Ninety seconds in which Ondrelle came down the stairs in his nightshirt with no weapon but a candlestick and thirty years of being ignored.

Ninety seconds in which Lior and I ran from the wall, through streets full of smoke, too late, always too late.

When we reached the tower, Ondrelle was holding the doorway. Just holding it. Standing in it like a doorstop of flesh and fury, arms spread, bleeding from places I didn't want to count, and three inquisitors couldn't move him because he'd wrapped himself in every ward-charm the city's mages would give him, and his body was burning through them like a lamp burning oil.

"You're early," he said to us, conversationally, blood on his teeth. "The lecture doesn't start for—"

Then the light went out of him all at once, the way a snuffed candle goes out, and he folded, and the inquisitors stepped over him with Pell screaming over an armored shoulder, and I did the thing I'm best at in this world.

I ran.

Not away. I want that understood too, even if nobody's left to read it. I ran *at* them, into the dark of the marsh road, following the false starlight on their banners like following a wound through a crowd, and I had my knives and I had my anger and I had four years of stealing things from people who deserved it, and none of it mattered, because they were twelve and I was one, and by the time I lost them in the fen they were gone, and Pell was gone, and Ondrelle was cooling on his own doorstep.

I sat in the mud afterward. Just sat. The hollow bells were still ringing over the city, and I hated them, and I hated the shard humming against my ribs like a smug little heart, and most of all I hated the taster, because when I finally dragged myself back inside the walls, soaked and empty-handed, he was waiting at the river gate with a lantern and a face that had already finished grieving and moved on to deciding.

"Don't," I said.

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"You're going to say we trade. You're going to say you go to Corvane in exchange for the child, because your blood is worth more to him than mine, because the Spire needs a taster more than it needs a thief, because—" My voice cracked, which I despise. "Because you've been making yourself essential and invisible your entire life, and invisible men think nobody will miss them."

Lior set the lantern down. Slowly. The way he did everything, the careful hands, the man who never rushed a swallow.

"They'll kill her," he said. "Or worse—they'll use her dreams to find the Spire's heart without any of us, and then Corvane wins everything. But if I offer myself, he has to take the bargain. A bearer who comes willingly is worth ten captured ones. He told me so himself, in his pretty office, with his pretty amnesty." He almost smiled. "He thinks I'm weak enough to be flattered."

"And what happens when you're standing in his citadel and he changes the terms?"

"Then I improvise." He picked the lantern back up. "I'm very good with strange ingredients."

I grabbed his wrist. His pulse was steady under my fingers, infuriatingly steady, the pulse of a man who had tasted death nightly for twenty years and found it bland.

"If you do this," I said, "I'm coming after you. Not sending. Not negotiating. Coming. Into whatever black pit they keep under that citadel, I am coming, and I am bringing the shard, and I am bringing every ugly trick I know, and you are going to stay alive out of pure embarrassment at what I'll do to the people who failed to stop me."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear, not gratitude. Recognition, maybe. Like a man hearing a bell ring true for the first time in years.

"Wren," he said softly. "You don't owe me anything. I pulled a charm off you once and you fed me soup. That's not—"

"It's not about owing, you absolute *idiot*."

And there it was, out in the open, sitting between us in the lantern light, bigger than either of us knew what to do with. Four weeks of shared bread and shared watches and his hand steadying mine over the shard, hers steadying his over the nightmares—all of it suddenly wearing its real name, and the name was the thing I'd been too much of a coward to say since the night I dragged him out of a burning market because my hands refused to do otherwise.

"I love you," I said. "Stubborn, self-erasing, poison-drinking fool. I love you, and I am *terrible* at losing things, ask anyone I've ever stolen from. So you will walk into that citadel, and you will make Corvane feel clever, and you will survive, because if you don't I will spend the rest of my life telling everyone what a coward you were, and I will make it *convincing*, because I'm a thief, lying is my native language."

Lior looked at me for a long moment. Then he did the most Lior thing imaginable: he leaned forward and pressed his forehead to mine, brief and warm, a taster's kiss—checking, always checking, whether the thing before him was safe to swallow.

"Stay outside the walls until the exchange is done," he murmured. "Promise me that much."

I promised him that much.

I am not proud of the promise. But I kept it for almost six hours, which, given who I am, is practically sainthood.

* * *

They met us—or met him—at the broken bridge over the fen at midmorning, Corvane's honor guard in gray and silver, false stars sewn at their throats. Pell was there, small and silent between two soldiers, and when she saw Lior walking out alone with his hands open and empty, she began to cry without making any sound, which is somehow worse than screaming, and which I will hear for the rest of my life.

The exchange itself took less time than buying bread. Words were spoken. Chains were traded for chains. Lior paused once, at the far side of the bridge, and looked back—not at me, I realized later, but past me, toward the Spire-road, toward the place where the covenant slept under stone and asked its terrible polite question.

Then they took him, and the fen closed over the sound of the column, and I stood on the near bank with a humming stone against my ribs and a promise dissolving in my mouth.

Six hours. I gave it six hours, pacing the mudflat like a caged animal, doing sums, hating every sum.

Then I took out the shard, and it was warm—warmer than it had ever been, pulsing like something with a heartbeat, and when I held it up to the gray daylight I could swear the light bent toward the citadel on the far ridge, the usurper's black tower built over the Spire's buried root, and the shard hummed one clear note, and I heard it plain as speech:

*Bearers gather.*

Well then.

I wiped my face, checked my knives, and turned toward the dark.

"Hold on, taster," I told the horizon. "Your thief is coming."
