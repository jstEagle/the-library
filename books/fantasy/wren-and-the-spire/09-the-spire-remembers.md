## 9. The Spire Remembers

They keep me in a room with no windows, which I suppose is a courtesy. If they gave me a view, I might see how far up the citadel goes, and how far down, and understand exactly what it is I'm lying on top of.

The bed is comfortable. The food is excellent. Both facts are meant to unsettle me more than irons would, and they nearly work. There is something deeply wrong about being poisoned gently.

I count the days by the meals. Four so far. The bread is real bread, not the garrison stuff — someone in this fortress of the usurper's has a baker who cares, and I resent how much that comforts me. Twenty years of tasting have taught me that you can learn everything about a household from its kitchen. This one is afraid. The salt is locked away. Someone up the chain of command thinks there are spies in the walls, and honestly, they're not wrong.

* * *

Corvane comes on the fifth day, and he brings tea.

I mean that literally. He carries the tray himself, sets it between us on the little table bolted to the floor — everything in this room is bolted down, including, I begin to suspect, me — and pours two cups with the unhurried grace of a man who has never once been interrupted.

"You'll want to watch the third sip," he says. "That's when it takes."

I look at him. He looks back, mild as a librarian.

"You're telling me which cup," I say.

"I'm telling you that I know what you are, Master Vasquez. A man who spent two decades teaching his body to disagree with poison isn't going to be troubled by anything I could brew." He nudges one cup toward me. "So we skip the theater. Drink it or don't. It's good tea either way. Darjeeling-adjacent. There's a merchant in the lower city who still imports it, God knows how."

I drink it. All of it, watching him the whole time, because if he's going to be honest with me I intend to be honest right back: my body shrugs off his serum the way an old door shrugs off weather. I feel it arrive, a warm loosening behind the eyes, and then feel my blood go to work on it like a housekeeper sweeping up after a party. By the fourth sip my tongue is steady and my secrets are where I left them.

"Nothing," Corvane says, and there's something almost wistful in it. "Not even a blush. Do you know how rare that is? I once dosed a woman for six days straight. She confessed to a murder committed before she was born. They always confess eventually. The body wants to agree."

"The body," I say, "can be trained out of bad habits. Mine had a very strict tutor."

"Twenty years of the taster's table." He turns his cup slowly on its saucer. "Every dish, every vintage, a small gamble. You built yourself into a lock that nothing opens. That's discipline, Vasquez. That's devotion." His eyes come up. "It's also a waste."

Here it comes. I'd wondered when it would.

"You were offered amnesty," I say.

"You've heard."

"Walls talk. Yours especially — they're terrified of you."

Something flickers across his face at that, quickly smoothed. He leans forward. "Then let me say it plainly, man to man, over decent tea. Give me the shard's location. Tell me who else carries. Walk out of here tomorrow with your name cleared, your old rooms at the palace waiting, your pension tripled. The usurper forgets easily; that's the great luxury of being him. And you—" he spreads his hands, "—you get to stop being essential and invisible. Isn't that what you wanted? All those years? To matter?"

And here's the thing I wasn't prepared for: part of me does want it. Not the pension. The stopping. My bones ache with twenty years of swallowed dangers, and there's a small voice in me that sounds suspiciously like reason saying, *you've done enough, you've been enough, take the tea and the pardon and the quiet.*

I think about Wren's face when she told me about the shard — the defiance of her chin, the fear underneath it like water under ice. I think about a widow in a night market whose tears I dried for the price of a memory she couldn't afford to keep whole.

"No," I say.

Corvane sighs, genuinely regretful, which is somehow worse than anger. "I was afraid you'd say that. Well. We have time." He rises, collects the tray, pauses at the door. "The Spire asks everyone, you know. In the end. I said no. Look what I built instead." He gestures vaguely at the walls, the fortress, the false starlight burning cold above us all. "Look what saying no gets you."

He leaves, and I sit alone with the bolted table, and I realize he's just handed me the most dangerous thing he owns: the truth about himself, freely given.

* * *

They move me on the eighth day.

Two guards, faces I've catalogued — the one with the chapped knuckles favors his left knee; the other hums when he thinks no one listens, badly, some lullaby — walk me down through the citadel's floors, each one colder than the last, until the stone stops being quarried and starts being *grown*. That's the only word for it. Veins of pale light run through the rock like frost through a windowpane, and the air tastes the way a struck bell smells.

We descend past sealed doors marked with sigils older than the kingdom. We descend past a landing where a corridor breathes — actually breathes, slow and tidal. And then the guards stop, unlock a final gate, and put me in a cell whose far wall is not a wall at all but a membrane of light, thin as paper, and beyond it: the root of the Spire, vast and slow-burning, a heart of stone the size of a cathedral, beating.

I press my hand to the barrier. Warmth. And beneath the warmth, faint as a voice through several closed doors:

*asking.*

I pull back. The guards exchange a glance — they've seen prisoners do this before, presumably, though none of them lasted long enough to make a habit of it — and leave me alone in the dark with a god's heartbeat.

I sleep badly, which is to say I dream vividly, which is to say the Spire talks.

Not in words. In tastes. That's how it reaches me, of course it is; it found the one sense I never armored. I taste my mother's kitchen, gone these thirty years. I taste the first poison I ever survived, green and bright as spring. I taste Wren's terrible cooking from our week on the road, burnt onions and stubborn hope. And threaded under all of it, patient as tide: *the covenant does not take. The covenant asks.*

I wake with salt on my face and understand, finally, what Corvane refused to hear.

The stars didn't fall because the Spire was broken. They fell because the Spire was *starved* — because a bearer said no, and the covenant, which cannot compel, dimmed and waited, and waited, and waited. Every trial, every demand for memory, every price the bearers feared — it was never a toll. It was a question. *Will you give? Will you trust?* And Corvane answered with a locked door and a stolen flame, and the whole sky went dark because one man mistook surrender for loss.

Memory isn't taken in this place. It's *offered*. And what is offered freely, the Spire keeps safe forever — woven into the light, held, honored. What Corvane hoarded, he lost. What Lior Vasquez gives away—

I sit with that a long while. My twenty years of careful anonymity. The invisible man's entire fortune: every secret, every mask, every small cunning survival. It would be the mortar, the scholars said. It would be enough.

And the strange thing, the thing I keep turning over like a coin: I'm not afraid anymore. Grieving, yes. But grief with a floor under it.

Because I finally understand what I've been doing all along. Twenty years of swallowing poisons so others wouldn't have to. Twenty years of making myself essential and invisible. I have been rehearsing this sacrifice my whole life without knowing its name. The Spire isn't asking me to become something new.

It's asking me to finish.

* * *

On the eleventh night, the humming guard doesn't come to my cell.

Instead there is a scrape in the ventilation shaft — a sound I have learned means Wren, because she moves through enclosed spaces the way water finds cracks — and then a panel swings open and a small furious face appears in it, framed by a child's smaller, grimmer one.

"Took you long enough," I whisper.

"You try navigating this place," Wren hisses. "Everything's grown, Lior. Doors grow shut. Pipa mapped half of it by *dreaming*, which I'd like noted is not a navigation method I endorse." The child — Pipa, the dreamer, the geometry-sleeper — squeezes past her and studies me with eyes too old for the rest of her.

"You've been down to the heart," Pipa says. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And it asked you."

"Yes."

Pipa nods, satisfied, as if I've passed a test whose existence she never doubted. "Good. Then you know what to do. We're leaving the way dreams drain downhill. It's disgusting. Don't ask."

Wren catches my wrist through the gap, her grip fierce. Her eyes search mine, and whatever she finds there makes her jaw tighten. "You're going to do something stupid," she says.

"I'm going to do something *chosen*," I say. "There's a difference. It's the whole point."

"That's exactly what someone about to do something stupid says."

"Wren." I cover her hand with mine. Below us, faint through miles of living stone, the Spire beats. Above us, through the ceiling, the false stars burn their borrowed cold. Somewhere between the two, a man named Corvane is awake tonight, I'd wager, staring at a ceiling of his own, listening to the question he refused twenty years ago and pretending he can't hear it. "When the stars come back — if they come back — find me. Whatever I've become. Find me, and tell me who I was."

She stares at me. Then, quietly, in the voice she uses for things she means: "Every morning. Whether you ask or not."

"That's the deal."

"It's not a deal, it's a hostage situation, and I'm the hostage-taker." She pulls a hairpin from her braid, works it into the cell lock with obscene speed, and the door swings open onto a corridor of pale veins and breathing stone.

Behind us, deep below, the heart of the world beats faster — or perhaps that's only my own pulse, finally, gladly, answering the question it has spent a lifetime preparing to answer.

Above, far above, I feel it: the false starlight flicker.

Corvane knows we're moving.

Let him.
