## 10. Stars, Reforged

**Lior**

There is a particular stillness that comes over a man in the last hour before he does something irreversible. I know this because I have felt it twice: once before swallowing a plate of suspected nightshade-braised quail for a paranoid king, and once now, standing at the root of the Spire with my hand on a shard of broken heaven.

The second time is worse. The quail, at least, only had to stay down.

The Spire's heart was not what I expected. I had imagined something grand — a cathedral of stone and starlight, choirs of the old covenant singing in the walls. Instead it was small. A chamber no bigger than the palace kitchens where I'd spent twenty years tasting other people's fears, its floor worn smooth by feet long dead, its ceiling open to a shaft of darkness where stars should have been and were not. The covenant did not live in grandeur. It lived in maintenance. In showing up. In the unglamorous daily work of two worlds agreeing to share a border.

I recognized it immediately. It was a kitchen. It was a taster's table. It was every job done well and never thanked.

"You're stalling," Wren said beside me. She had her arms crossed and her chin up, which was her way of not crying. I had learned to read her the way I read dishes — by the smallest signs. The set of her jaw said *if you die doing something noble I will dig you up and kill you myself.*

"I'm composing my last words," I said. "They should be memorable."

"They should be *short*."

Marrow stood nearby, the disgraced scholar, holding the child Sef's hand. Sef was humming — she hummed constantly now, the Spire's geometry leaking through her dreams into her waking hours — and the hum resonated in the shard against my chest until my teeth ached with it. Four bearers. A scholar who had failed his examinations rather than falsify them. A child who dreamed in angles. A thief who had stolen a piece of God's masonry because it was shiny and then, when the world ended, refused to give it back.

And me. A poison-eater. A man built out of careful nothing, whose entire self was a series of precautions taken so thoroughly they had become a soul.

"The covenant asks," Marrow said quietly. "It doesn't take. That's what Corvane never understood."

"Corvane," I said, "is currently climbing down our escape route with an army at his back, so perhaps we could discuss his misunderstandings later."

Because he was. Through the shaft above us came torchlight, and boots on stairs, and the particular disciplined silence of soldiers who had been promised a miracle. Corvane had held me in his citadel for eleven days and learned nothing, because twenty years of swallowing small poisons teaches a body to shrug off small truths as well. He had called me a locked door. He had been almost admiring about it.

Now the door was going to open itself. From the inside.

"Lior." Wren caught my sleeve. Her voice dropped out of its bravado into something rawer. "You don't remember things the way other people do. You've told me. Your whole life is — it's habits. Routines. What if the Spire takes the routines? What if there's nothing left?"

"Then you'll tell me," I said. "You've been telling me who I am since the market burned. You're very good at it. Insufferably good."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Boots on the stairs. Closer. And beneath them, faintly, the sound I had been dreading and waiting for all at once: the hum of the false stars, Corvane's stolen light, straining against a covenant it was never meant to carry.

I took the shard from around my neck. It was warm. It had always been warm, from the first moment my immune, poisoned, carefully-ruined body had closed around it without burning, and I understood finally that this was what my invisibility had been *for*. Not punishment. Preparation. Twenty years of making himself essential and invisible, and the whole time the world had been sharpening me into a key.

The covenant asked. I answered.

"I, Lior Vasquez," I said, and my voice sounded strange in the little room, too loud and too steady, "taster, charm-seller, coward by profession and practice, offer what I am. All of it. The twenty years. The names I didn't use. The faces I practiced in mirrors. Take the mortar. Build with it."

The shard flared white.

* * *

**Wren**

He went up like a struck match, and I don't mean he died, I mean he *lit* — light pouring out of him the way heat pours off a forge, and inside the light I could see them, gods help me, I could see the memories themselves. Little scenes burning off him like sparks off a grindstone. Him as a boy learning to hold his breath. Him memorizing poison antidotes by candlelight. Him in the night market pressing a memory-charm into a widow's palm and saying *don't thank me, just go*.

Him pulling away from me in the undercity, the first night, saying *you shouldn't have saved me.*

All of it going. All of it feeding the dark above us, filling the empty shaft like water filling a dry well.

I screamed something. I don't know what. It doesn't matter, because at the same moment Corvane came through the door with six soldiers and a face like a man arriving late to his own funeral.

He looked at the light. He looked at Lior, blazing, giving himself away piece by piece, and I watched something move behind the Inquisitor-General's eyes that I will be thinking about for the rest of my life. Recognition. Longing. He had stood where Lior stood, forty years ago. The covenant had asked him the same question.

And he had said no. He had reached instead for the power without the price, torn a false star out of the Spire's wreckage, and spent four decades propping up a usurper's throne with borrowed light — and I saw, in that one naked instant, that it had never stopped hurting. That every morning of his safe, powerful, memory-intact life had cost him more than Lior's sacrifice was costing now.

"No," Corvane said. To us. To himself. To the light. "It isn't *worth* it. Nothing is worth—"

He lunged for the shard.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about covenants: they are agreements, and agreements have terms, and Corvane had spent forty years in breach. When his hand closed on the Spire-stone while Lior's offering was still burning through it, the covenant did not strike him down. It simply *collected*. Forty years of withheld payment, called due all at once.

He came apart like smoke in wind. No scream. Just a look of terrible, final understanding, and then the man who had chosen safety over everything was unmade by the safety itself, and the false stars overhead guttered out one by one like candles in a draft.

The soldiers ran. I don't blame them. I wanted to run too.

Instead I grabbed Lior as he collapsed, because the light was dying down and what was left underneath was just a man — thinner, somehow, as if remembering had been weight and he'd put it all down — blinking up at me with eyes that had never seen me before in his life.

"Hello," he said politely. "Have we met?"

* * *

**Lior**

I woke on a pallet in a room that smelled of bread and lamp oil, and a thin woman with sharp eyes was sitting beside me eating an apple, watching me the way one watches a pot that might boil over.

"You're Wren," I said. She had told me. She had apparently been telling me for three days.

"And you're Lior Vasquez," she said. "Royal food-taster. Retired. Charm-seller, retired. Coward, retired. Currently: stubborn invalid."

"It sounds like a lot of retiring."

"You earned all of it." She tossed me the apple core, which seemed an odd gift until I realized she was testing whether I'd catch things without thinking. I did. Some habits, apparently, live deeper than memory. "Your hands still know things. Marrow says the body keeps what the mind gives up. He's very smug about it."

"Marrow," I repeated. The name meant nothing. The apple core in my hand meant nothing. And yet when she said *Wren* something in my chest turned over like a key in a lock that had lost its door.

"How much is gone?" I asked.

"All of it. The twenty years. You're twenty-two years old in your own head, standing in a life you don't recognize, married—" she coughed "—*known* to a woman you can't remember, in a city that outlawed everything you ever did." She grinned, and it didn't reach her eyes. "Lucky for you, I keep excellent records."

She did, too. Every morning since, she has sat across from me at breakfast and told me who I was. The taster's table. The night market. The widow with the memory-charm. The way I used to stand so still at palace dinners that guests mistook me for furniture. She tells it like a story, with flourishes, and I suspect some of the flourishes are hers, and I have decided not to mind. A man with no past cannot object to a little embroidery.

* * *

**Wren**

The stall opens at dawn, because he insists, because some part of him still believes in being useful before the city wakes. He sells charms now — legal ones, mostly, though I've noticed he keeps a drawer in the back that he thinks I don't know about, and I've decided the law can catch up to the world whenever it finishes rebuilding itself.

Sef comes by most mornings. She's stopped humming, mostly; the Spire sleeps easy now, its covenant reforged on a taster's twenty quiet years, and the child dreams ordinary dreams. She brings him honey cakes and calls him Uncle Mortar, which he pretends to hate. Marrow is rewriting the histories, and has promised — cross his heart, spit-shake sworn — that Lior Vasquez gets a full chapter. Lior says he'll believe it when he reads it, having no memory of whether Marrow keeps promises, which is the sort of joke that makes Marrow laugh too hard.

The stars come out every night now. Real ones. On clear evenings we climb to the roof and he looks up at them for a long time, and I know what he's thinking, because he tells me: *I gave twenty years for those, and I can't remember a single one of them, and somehow I'm not sorry.* 

This morning he was arranging his charms when he paused over a small stone in the corner of the tray — a dull grey thing I stole off a dead man's altar years ago, worth nothing now that the real Spire burns bright again.

"This one," he said. "Tell me about this one."

So I sat down on my crate, the way I do every morning, and I began: "Well. There was once a food-taster who made himself invisible..."

And he listened, the way he always listens, like a man being handed back his own life one piece at a time — and when I got to the part about the burning market, and the stranger I dragged out of the smoke for no reason I could name, he smiled at me across the stall like a man meeting someone for the first time and knowing, absolutely, that he already loved her.

"Go on," he said. "What happened next?"

That's the hook of it, see. Nobody knows what happens next. We're writing it fresh, both of us, every morning — and tomorrow, when the sun comes up over a city with stars over it and magic in it and room in it for a thief and a taster, I'll start the story again from the beginning.

He always asks what happens next.

I always get to tell him.
