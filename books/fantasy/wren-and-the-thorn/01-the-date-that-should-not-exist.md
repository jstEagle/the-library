## 1. The Date That Should Not Exist

The restricted archive smelled the way all old paper smells, which is to say like a library that had died and been politely embalmed: dust and vellum glue and the faint vinegar tang of decaying leather. Oscar Halloran had spent four years in that smell, and she had come, by now, to find it comforting in the way one finds a creaky floorboard comforting—because it meant the house was still standing.

She sat at the long cataloguing table beneath the one window that admitted light, if you could call it light. Dunlow's sun came through the valley haze thin and gray, like tea brewed three times over. Outside, the mill-wheels of the temple granary turned their slow circles, and somewhere below her father was hauling sacks of barley up stairs that were designed, she suspected, specifically to break men who owed money.

"Catalogue number," she said aloud, because talking to herself was the only conversation available before noon, "four thousand two hundred and six. *On the Proper Tithing of Unlicensed Herbs.* Author unknown, presumably because anyone proud of this would be dead by now."

She wrote the entry in her small, precise hand. Four years of debt-labor had made her hand precise the way a rope makes a tree precise. She could write a full ledger page without lifting her wrist. She could date a document in her sleep. She had once, actually, dated a document in her sleep, and Brother Aldous had framed it as a warning to others rather than praising it, which told Oscar everything she needed to know about temple management.

The morning's crate was the worst kind: uncatalogued overflow from the deep stacks, books that had been shelved by some previous archivist with more enthusiasm than method. The deep stacks went down three levels into the hillside beneath the temple, and nobody went down there voluntarily except debtors, which is to say people whose choices had already been made for them.

Oscar pulled the next volume from the crate and stopped.

It was bound in black leather gone green at the edges, and it was heavier than its size suggested—the way a stone is heavier than its size suggests, or a secret. No title on the spine. No temple seal. Just a small stamped device on the cover that she didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a thorned line, like a rose stem drawn by someone who hated roses.

"Well," she said. "You're not supposed to be here."

Books that weren't supposed to be there were, technically, her favorite kind. She checked over her shoulder out of pure habit—Brother Aldous was at breakfast; the Inquisitors' wing kept different hours and colder company—and opened it.

The first pages were ordinary enough. A record of seasons. Rainfall tallies in a hand so old the ink had gone brown. Then, gradually, the entries changed.

They became predictions.

*The frost will come late this year, and the eastern orchard will suffer for it.* Dated ninety-eight years past. Below it, in different ink, someone had written *true* with a checkmark, the way a schoolmaster marks sums.

Oscar turned the pages faster.

*The granary fire of the third month will begin in the north loft.* Checkmark. *True.*

*A child born under the dark moon in the weaver's quarter will drown at seven unless watched near water.* Beside this one, no checkmark. Instead: *prevented.* And underneath, smaller, almost shy: *the watching worked.*

Oscar sat back. The gray light through the window seemed suddenly very far away.

Prophecy was not, strictly speaking, forbidden in Dunlow. It was simply pointless, which in the temple's accounting amounted to the same thing. There was no magic in the valley—that was the first thing every child learned, along with the names of the Founders and how to scrub a floor. Magic belonged to the world outside the borders, where noble houses glowed like lanterns and common folk paid tithes of blood and breath just to be permitted near it. Here, inside the null, nothing sparked, nothing whispered, nothing dreamed true. The valley was quiet the way a locked room is quiet. The temple taught that this was mercy. Protection. The Founders had built Dunlow's borders after the old war, and whatever lay outside them stayed outside.

So a book of working prophecy, sitting in the deep stacks of a valley where prophecy could not work, was either a very elaborate joke or a very serious mistake.

Or—and her fingers slowed on the parchment—or it was proof that the quiet wasn't total. That something got through.

"You are," she informed the book, "an archival problem."

The book did not answer, but she had the distinct sensation of being listened to.

She catalogued it properly—number 4213, untitled, provenance unknown—and then, because she was a debtor and not a saint, she read it. She read through the midday meal she forgot to eat. She read while the window-light crawled across the table and turned gold and died. The entries marched forward through the decades, predictions and confirmations, the anonymous hand growing surer, more compressed, until the prophecies stopped being about weather and fires and children and began being about the valley itself.

*The border will hold longer than the builders intended,* said an entry dated sixty years past. *Longer than they wanted. That was the flaw in their design. A cage strong enough keeps even the jailers honest, whether they like it or not.*

Below it: *true,* and beside *true*, in newer ink: *and inconvenient.*

And then the entries grew sparse. Years apart. Decades apart. The final blank leaves approached, and Oscar felt something tighten in her chest, the way you feel it when a story knows where it's going and you don't yet know whether you're allowed to follow.

The last entry occupied the final leaf. It was written in the same sure hand as all the others, but larger, deliberate, each letter placed like a stone in a wall:

*The book will sleep a hundred years, and the one who wakes it will wake with it. On this day—*

And there was the date.

Oscar read the date twice. Three times. She took off her spectacles, cleaned them on her sleeve, put them back on, and read it again, because scribes trust instruments before they trust their own eyes.

It was tomorrow's date. Not approximately. Not a coincidence of numerals that might be explained away by calendar reforms or a copyist's error. Tomorrow. The day, the month, the year, rendered in the temple's own notation, the notation she herself had used that very morning on four thousand two hundred and six herbs requiring proper tithing.

And beneath the date, in letters that seemed, absurdly, to have been pressed harder than the rest:

*her name is OSCAR HALLORAN. Daughter of Tammas Halloran of Debtors' Row. Scribe of the fourth bench. She reads this in the year of her twenty-sixth winter, alone, at the long table, and the Thorn wakes.*

Oscar Halloran set down the book very carefully, the way one sets down something sleeping.

Then she stood up too fast, knocked over her ink pot, watched the black spread across her finished catalogue page like a slow thought, and said, to the empty archive, with tremendous dignity:

"No."

The archive declined to negotiate. The gray light lay flat on the table. Somewhere below, the mill wheels turned. Her heart was making a sound like someone running upstairs in heavy boots.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing her mind kept circling like a moth at glass. The handwriting was old. She knew old writing the way a farmer knows weather; four years among the deep stacks had given her an eye for it. The iron-gall ink had burned brown into the parchment. The leaf itself had aged, softened, grown fragile at the margins in a way that took decades, not days. Whoever wrote those words had written them a century ago. A hundred years before tomorrow. A hundred years before a debtor's daughter named Oscar Halloran sat down at the long table with a crate of misfiled overflow and a skipped lunch.

Which meant—

Her thoughts refused to finish the sentence. They kept approaching it and veering off, like horses shying at a bridge.

She looked at her own hands, resting flat on the table to stop their trembling. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained, nib-calloused, the right middle finger permanently dented by a pen. Scribe's hands. Hands that had never done anything more magical than produce a particularly convincing capital F.

*The Thorn wakes.*

She didn't know what a Thorn was. She suspected, with the specific dread of a person who has catalogued enough apocalyptic literature, that she was about to find out.

Footsteps in the corridor. She snapped the book shut and shoved it under a stack of tithing ledgers with the guilty speed of a child hiding sweets, and Brother Aldous appeared in the doorway, round and mild and smelling of porridge, exactly as harmless as he always was and somehow, today, exactly as terrifying.

"You've missed the meal," he said.

"I'm behind on the crate," Oscar said. Her voice came out level. She was almost impressed by it.

Aldous squinted at the spilled ink, the disordered table, her face. He was not a clever man, but he had spent forty years around debtors, and debtors developed tells. "You look pale, girl."

"It's the dust," Oscar said. "Deep stacks."

"Mm." He gathered the fallen catalogue page, frowned at the blot, and left it. "Finish the crate by week's end. And Oscar—" he paused at the door, not turning, "—some books in that crate are misfiled because they were filed wrong. Others are misfiled because someone wanted them forgotten. Try to tell the difference before it matters."

He shuffled out. Oscar stared at the doorway for a long moment.

"That," she said finally, "was not helpful."

* * *

She should have returned the book to the crate. She knew that. Every rule she'd internalized in four years of debt-labor said: log it, shelf it, report anomalies, keep your head down, serve your term, go home. The valley survived on heads kept down. It was practically the local industry.

Instead, as the last light failed, she wrapped the book in her spare shirt, tucked it under her arm, and carried it back to her cell in the scribes' dormitory like a woman smuggling a bomb made of paper. Which, she reflected, was possibly exactly what she was doing.

She lit her candle, barred her door, and read the final page again by flame, because daylight had made it seem deniable and candlelight, she discovered, did not.

*She reads this alone, at the long table, and the Thorn wakes.*

Alone. Yes. At the long table. Yes. Twenty-sixth winter—her birthday was in three weeks, but the winter itself was hers, undeniably hers, and a prophet writing a century ago would hardly split hairs.

Every detail correct. Every single one. Not the shape of a life, not the broad strokes a cold reader might guess—a scribe, a woman, a valley—but specifics. Her father's name. His row. Her bench. The long table. The crate of misfiled books that some prior archivist had buried precisely so that they would resurface exactly when—

When.

Oscar blew out the candle and sat in the dark, and the wonder she had carried all day like a coal in her chest finally curdled into what it had been threatening to become since noon.

Dread.

Because a prophecy that comes true is a marvel. But a prophecy engineered to come true—hidden, misfiled, timed, addressed to *her by name*—that was not marvel. That was mechanism. Someone had wanted this book found, tomorrow, by her, and had arranged a century of storage to accomplish it, and nobody arranged a century of anything for a kindness.

*The Thorn wakes.* Wakes where? In whom? What woke?

Her forearm itched. She scratched it absently, by candlelight she'd already blown out, and paused.

The itch was in a specific place. Inner left forearm, below the elbow. A fine, warm prickling, like a limb waking from sleep, like pins and needles arranging themselves into—

Into letters?

She held her arm to the dead candle as though light could be coaxed backwards out of it. Of course there was nothing. Of course. She was tired, she had skipped a meal, she had spent the day reading a book that should not exist, and tired scribes imagined things; there was an entire drawer in the infirmary devoted to pamphlets about it.

But the prickling persisted, patient as ink drying. And beneath it, faint as a voice heard through a wall, she felt—not heard, *felt*—something turn over in its sleep.

In the dormitory above her, a floorboard creaked. Down in the valley, the mill wheels turned their patient circles. And in the deep stacks beneath the temple, in a crate of misfiled overflow, the space where a black book had lain for a hundred years waited, empty now, its purpose discharged, its message delivered.

Oscar Halloran drew her blanket around her shoulders, looked at the darkness where her forearm was quietly itching, and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives only at the worst possible moments, that tomorrow had already started early.

Whatever the Thorn was, it had woken. And it was writing to her.
