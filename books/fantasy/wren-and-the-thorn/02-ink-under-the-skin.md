## 2. Ink Under the Skin

Oscar Halloran had three rules for working in the restricted archive, and she repeated them to herself every evening as she lit her lamp: copy nothing without permission, tell no one what you find, and never—*never*—let Sister Edweth catch you smiling at a book.

She had already broken the third rule tonight. The book on her desk had made her smile twice.

It was an ugly thing, all things considered—a ledger-bound volume with a cracked spine and covers of boiled leather gone green at the edges, smelling faintly of tallow and old rain. The catalog called it *Miscellany, Prophetic, Unverified (Dunlow Collection)*, which was archivist-speak for *nobody important wrote this and nobody important ever read it.* It had sat in the third cabinet from the window for as long as anyone could remember, wedged between a treatise on drainage rights and a hymnal with half its pages eaten by mice.

Oscar had pulled it out only because the drainage treatise was leaning against it wrong, and she had a professional hatred of leaning books. A scribe's spine should be straight; so should a shelf's.

The first smile had come when she saw the handwriting. Whoever had compiled the miscellany had written like they were being paid by the flourish—great looping capitals, marginal doodles of thorned vines climbing up the page edges like ivy on a trellis. It was the handwriting of someone who loved writing, and Oscar, professionally speaking, was helpless before that kind of enthusiasm.

The second smile had come at the final entry. And it had died there, quickly, completely, the way a candle dies when someone opens a door.

*On the eve of the fourteenth of Harvestmonth,* the entry read, in that same flourishing hand, *the reader will come. She will be a scribe, and her name will be Oscar Halloran. She will have copied this page against every rule she keeps, because she is careful and curious, and those two virtues make a key between them.*

*When she reads these words, the thorn wakes.*

Below that, the ink changed. The flourishes stopped. The last line was written small and cramped, pressed hard into the page as if the writer's hand had been shaking:

*I am sorry. I could not carry it further.*

The date above it was one hundred years old. The date it described was tomorrow.

Oscar sat very still for a long time. Somewhere above her, the temple bell counted out the hour of Compline, and she counted along without meaning to, the way you count your own heartbeat. Fourteen of Harvestmonth. Tomorrow. Her name. Her actual name, in a century-old book, spelled correctly, which was more than most living people managed. Halloran got mangled into Hollerin, Hallorin, and once, memorably, on a tax writ, Hairyman.

She told herself it was a trick. A joke left by some bored archivist decades ago, waiting for a gullible junior scribe. She told herself that the ink wasn't quite dry-looking, that the paper wasn't quite fresh-feeling under her fingertips, and that she was a woman of letters who did not believe in books that wrote back.

Then she looked down at her hands—her good, careful, copying hands—and noticed that her fingers were trembling too hard to hold a pen, and decided that if she was going to be foolish, she would at least be thorough about it.

She broke rule one.

She copied the page. All of it. Every flourish, every marginal vine, even the cramped shaking apology, because some part of her mind—the part that had spent ten years transcribing tithe records and burial rolls—insisted that if something was worth doing, it was worth doing accurately. She worked fast, heart hammering, glancing at the door after every few lines. The lamp guttered once and she nearly put her elbow through the inkwell.

When she finished, she sat back and looked at what she'd done. Two pages of her own neat hand, a perfect twin of a dead person's prophecy, folded small and tucked into the lining of her sleeve where she kept her spare nibs.

"That's not evidence," she whispered to the empty archive. "That's just... transcription practice. Very strange transcription practice."

The archive did not answer, but the silence felt different than it had an hour ago. Thicker. Attentive.

She returned the miscellany to the third cabinet, adjusted the drainage treatise until it leaned properly, and locked the cabinet with the little brass key that lived on her belt. Then she stood in the middle of the room, lamp raised, and looked at the shelves the way you look at a friend whose face has suddenly gone unfamiliar.

A hundred years ago, somebody had written her name in a book and apologized to her. Somebody had known she would sit in this chair, under this lamp, breaking rule one.

"Good night," she said politely to the shelves, because her father had raised her to be courteous even to rooms, and she blew out the lamp.

* * *

Sleep did not come easily, which was unsurprising. What came instead, somewhere past midnight, was worse than insomnia.

It started as warmth. Oscar dreamed of the temple kitchens in winter, of holding her hands over the great stove while Sister Brenna scolded her for stealing warmth that belonged to the bread. But in the dream the warmth moved, gathered, concentrated itself into her left forearm, just below the crook of her elbow—and it stopped being comfortable.

She woke with a gasp in her narrow cell behind the scriptorium, moonlight striping the floor through the shutter slats. Her arm was burning.

Not hurting, exactly. Burning the way a page burns when you hold it too close to a flame—slow, deliberate, as though the fire were reading it aloud.

She fumbled for her flint, lit the stub of candle she wasn't technically allowed to keep, and pushed back her sleeve.

The words were there.

All of them. *On the eve of the fourteenth of Harvestmonth, the reader will come.* Written in ink-dark lines across her skin, following the curve of her forearm, the flourishes intact, the little vines curling around her wrist as if looking for something to climb. Even the cramped, shaking apology was there, small and hard-pressed just below the inside of her elbow, where the skin was thinnest.

*I am sorry. I could not carry it further.*

"No," Oscar said. She said it several times, in several tones of voice, ranging from reasonable to pleading. She scrubbed at it with the corner of her blanket. She wet her thumb and rubbed until her skin went pink and raw. The words did not smudge. They did not fade. They looked, if anything, slightly better rested than she felt, as though they had slept well in whatever place words go before they decide to live on your body.

She sat on the edge of her cot and held her arm in the candlelight and did the arithmetic no one wants to do. She had copied the page at half past ten. It was now past midnight. The transfer had taken less than two hours, which meant it had happened while she slept, which meant—

Which meant it hadn't needed her awake. Or her permission. Or anything at all except her having read it in the first place.

"The thorn wakes," she said quietly, testing the words in her mouth. They tasted like iron filings.

Her arm gave a single, distinct pulse of heat. Not pain, precisely. More like a correction. Like a tutor tapping a ruler on a desk when a student mispronounces something.

"Oh, don't be dramatic," she told her arm. "I'm allowed to say the words. I *wrote* the words. Twice now, apparently, counting my own skin."

The warmth settled back to a low simmer, sulky, and Oscar lay down again with her sleeve carefully pinned above the mark and stared at the ceiling until the birds started, thinking about her father, and debts, and the fact that she had an eight o'clock shift in the tithe office and no idea how she was going to hold a pen without her whole world spilling out of her sleeve.

* * *

There was exactly one good thing about the morning, and it was that the temple issued long-sleeved work robes regardless of season. Dunlow Valley did not believe in showing skin; the Inquisitors said bare arms invited vanity, and vanity invited the old sins, and the old sins invited magic, which had not existed within the valley borders in a hundred years and was therefore either very shy or very dead. Oscar had always found the theology circular, but she had never been so grateful for it.

The mark burned at breakfast.

She hadn't even said anything untrue. Sister Pemmy asked if she'd slept well, and Oscar opened her mouth to deliver the standard *well enough*, and the words died in her throat as her forearm flared hot enough that she dropped her spoon into her porridge.

"Well enough," she managed, through her teeth.

The burn eased. Not vanished—she could still feel it, a banked coal under the linen—but quieted.

Sister Pemmy peered at her. "You look peaked, child. Coming down with something?"

"I slept badly," Oscar said. True. No burn.

"You're pale."

"I'm always pale. I work in an archive." Also true. The coal stayed banked.

"Hmm." Pemmy squinted a moment longer, then returned to her lecture on the moral failings of the mill foremen, and Oscar exhaled slowly and understood, with the particular clarity that comes after a bad night, exactly what she was carrying.

She tested it once more, privately, on the walk to the tithe office. *I have never broken a rule in the archive,* she thought, deliberately, experimentally.

Her arm blazed like a struck match.

"*Fine,*" she hissed at her sleeve, hopping on one foot where she'd stumbled. "Message received. We're honest now. Wonderful. I've become a truth-telling instrument overnight, which is going to make my job extremely difficult, since half of record-keeping is polite fiction."

The coal warmed reproachfully. She got the impression—not a voice, nothing so convenient—that the mark did not consider polite fiction to be the same category of sin as lying to a sister's face, and that it graded on a curve. Small mercies.

By midday she had mapped its temperament the way she'd once mapped the archive cabinets: lies to people burned worst. Lies to herself smoldered. Omissions merely prickled, like a splinter reminding you it existed. And when she caught herself, in a quiet moment between ledgers, thinking *none of this is real, I dreamed it, I'll wake up and my arm will be clean*—the burn had been the gentlest of all, almost sorrowful, the way you'd touch a child's shoulder before telling them something they didn't want to hear.

It wasn't punishing her. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that frightened her more than pain would have. It was *correcting* her. Patiently. Endlessly. The way a scribe corrects a copyist's error—not in anger, but because the text matters more than the hand that holds the pen.

She wondered, folding her sleeves down tight and pressing her palms flat against cool ledger paper, whether the last bearer had also spent their first day negotiating terms with their own skin.

*I am sorry,* the cramped line said, whenever she rolled her wrist. *I could not carry it further.*

"You could have included an instruction manual," she muttered.

The vine around her wrist seemed, very faintly, to tighten. Almost like a squeeze.

* * *

Inquisitor Vane did not hurry. Hurrying, he had found over thirty years of service, communicated anxiety, and anxiety communicated guilt, and guilt was a scent that lesser men followed with hounds while he preferred simply to stand downwind of it.

He stood downwind of it now, in the cloister outside the archive stair, watching the morning shift of scribes file past with their heads bent and their sleeves buttoned. He watched them the way he watched everything: without appearing to.

The seal on the restricted cabinet had been broken.

He had found it at dawn, during his customary walk—an old habit, checking locks that other people believed were checked. The brass lock itself was undamaged. The key was accounted for, hanging on young Halloran's belt as it always did, and Vane noted the way she carried her left arm today, tucked slightly close to her ribs, like a wing held in. Scribes carried their arms however they liked; he had catalogued a thousand such habits. But this one was new, and new habits were how stories began.

He consulted his memory, which was the finest instrument he owned and the only one he maintained personally. The Halloran girl: twenty-four years of age, indentured against her father's debts, eleven years in temple service, no reprimands, no absences, no vices beyond an occasional smuggled apple eaten on the archive steps, which he had observed and forgiven because everyone required one small sin to stay sane. Careful. Curious. Those were the words her instructors used. He had read them in her file and thought, at the time, *a useful combination, or a dangerous one.*

The miscellany. His jaw tightened, minutely, a motion visible to no one. He had requested the Dunlow Collection be transferred to the Motherhouse in Corvane City twice—five years ago and again last spring. Both requests had been denied by the Prior on grounds of administrative burden, which was the church's way of saying *nobody wants to carry heavy boxes.* He had considered taking the matter further and had not, and now a hundred years of patient waiting might have come apart in a single night because a box was too heavy.

He did not yet know that it *had*. He knew only that a seal was broken, a girl was hiding her arm, and the fourteenth of Harvestmonth had arrived at last, punctual as a debt.

Vane straightened his cuffs. Around him the cloister bells rang noon, bright and indifferent, and the scribes hurried to their meal, and among them went a young woman with a secret written on her skin in a dead stranger's hand, walking unknowingly toward an afternoon in which she would lie to him directly and learn what her inheritance cost.

He let her go. For now. There was no pleasure in it—he had never understood colleagues who spoke of pleasure—but there was purpose, and purpose had carried him this far, through the hollowing years, toward the thing he had been promised and the thing he feared in equal measure.

Tomorrow was coming. He intended to be standing downwind of it.
