## 3. Debtors' Row

The mill bell rang the hour of Terce while Oscar was still crossing the yard, and the sound went through her like a hand on the back of her neck—*you're late, you're late, you're late*—even though she wasn't. The bell always did that. Twenty years in Dunlow and she still flinched at it like a girl caught stealing plums.

She was not late. She had, in fact, left the archive early for the first time in four years, which was its own small sin. Scribes were not meant to leave their posts before Sext, but Scribe-First Aldous had looked up from his ledgers as she passed, taken in her face—she could only imagine what he'd seen there—and said, with the weary kindness of a man who had buried two wives and most of his own curiosity, "Go on, then. The dust will keep."

The dust would keep. It was the other thing that wouldn't. She had rolled and unrolled the copied page so many times on the walk over that the paper had gone soft as cloth, and now it sat folded against her ribs, inside her sash, warm from her skin. *Tomorrow's date,* she kept thinking. *Her name.* A hundred years old and warm from her skin, as though she were the one who had written it.

The temple mills stood at the valley's eastern edge where the River Liss came down off the hills fat and gray with snowmelt. You heard them before you saw them: a groaning, rhythmic thunder, the great wheels turning, turning, turning grain into flour and men into shadows of themselves. Debtors worked the day shift. Criminals worked nights. Her father had done both, once, in the worst years, and had come out of it with a tremor in his hands that never fully left.

Oscar showed her token to the gate-warden, a broad woman named Hesta who smelled permanently of rye and had known Oscar since she was small enough to ride her father's shoulders through harvest markets.

"Visiting hour," Hesta said, stamping the token without looking up. "Half an hour. And mind you don't carry anything in to him. Last month somebody's cousin brought in a knife wrapped in bread, if you can believe the stupidity."

"I believe the stupidity," Oscar said. "I work in an archive."

Hesta snorted. "That you do. How's your father's back?"

"Bad on wet days."

"And today?"

Oscar glanced at the sky, low and bruised and promising rain by evening. "Bad."

"They're all bad," Hesta said, waving her through. "Go on. He'll be in the second yard. He's on sorting today—his hands couldn't manage the sacks this week, so they gave him the light work." A pause, and then, quieter: "He asked after you Tuesday. Wanted to know if you were eating."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you were too thin to be eating anything worth the name, and proud about it besides."

"Traitor," Oscar said, meaning it fondly, and went in.

* * *

The second yard was where the mill house kept its gentler labor: sorting grain, picking stones from the hopper feed, mending the endless rot of the sackcloth. Long trestle tables under an open awning, rows of debtors bent over their trays like penitents. The air was thick with chaff and the sweet-dusty smell of barley, and everywhere the sound—hands moving through grain, the sift and whisper of it, hundreds of hands, thousands of hours, all of it counting down debts measured in quarters and pecks.

Her father sat at the third table from the door.

Oscar stopped, as she always stopped, just inside the shade of the awning, and let herself look at him for a moment before he noticed her. This was a habit she'd never examined closely and didn't intend to start. There was something in those first unguarded seconds—the way he hunched over his tray, the careful economy of his trembling hands, the way his lips moved faintly as he counted—that hurt in a place she had no better word for than *home*, and she needed to look at it straight before she put on her smiling face. It felt dishonest otherwise. Like reading only the favorable passages of a ledger.

He was thinner. He was always thinner; the debt took its tithe of him in flesh as surely as in years. His hair had gone almost white at fifty-two, fine and soft as milkweed, and there was chaff in it today, a little golden fleck behind his ear that he couldn't see and wouldn't know about until some kind soul told him, and the thought of that small unnoticed thing undid her more than the hollowness of his cheeks ever could.

"Dad," she said.

His head came up. And there it was—the transformation, every single time, no matter how tired he was, no matter how bad the back or the hands. His whole face lit. Not gradually, not politely, but all at once, like a lamp catching, and for one blazing moment Aldous Halloran looked like the man who had carried his daughter through harvest markets and taught her letters by tracing them in spilled flour on the kitchen table because ink cost money and flour was free.

"There she is," he said. "There's my scholar."

He started to rise, and she pressed him back down by the shoulders and pulled up an empty stool beside him, ignoring the glance from the yard overseer—visitors sat where visitors were put, but rules bent for scribes, another small advantage of her position that tasted worse every year.

"How are your hands?" she asked, taking them before he could hide them. They lay in hers, big and dry and trembling very slightly, like water deciding which way to run. "Honestly."

"Honestly?" He smiled. "They're hands. They hold things. Some days they even hold on."

"Dad."

"Some days," he admitted, "they don't. But the overseer's moved me to sorting, which is mostly patience work, and I've got more patience than grip these days, so we suit each other fine." He turned his hands over in hers, examining them as though they belonged to someone else, some other man whose story he'd been told secondhand. "How's the archive? Still drowning in dust?"

"Still drowning." Her voice came out steady. That surprised her. "Aldous has me cataloguing the restricted stacks now."

"Restricted!" His eyebrows went up, and for a moment he was young again, or she was seeing the ghost of him young—the Aldous Halloran who'd once, according to family legend, talked his way into a baron's wedding feast and eaten enough to be sick for two days. "Is it true what they say? That there are books down there chained to the shelves?"

"Chained to the shelves, yes. Whether they deserve it is another question. Half of them are tax records."

"Tax records chained up like heretics." He laughed, and then coughed, the deep racking cough the mill dust gave everyone eventually, and waved away her concern before she could voice it. "Barley. Goes down wrong. So—restricted stacks. My daughter, keeper of secrets." He said it lightly, proudly, and Oscar felt the folded page against her ribs like a coal.

"Yes," she said. "Keeper of secrets."

* * *

They talked about nothing for a while, gratefully. He told her about a rat in the dormitory that the men had named Chancellor because it was fat and stole from everyone impartially. She told him about Aldous's feud with the temple carpenter over a shelf that sloped—"he swears it's the wood settling; I swear it's spite"—and her father laughed his real laugh, the one that crinkled his eyes half shut, and for a few minutes they might have been anyone, any daughter and father on any visiting day, and the page could have been any piece of paper, and tomorrow could have been any date.

Then a bell changed somewhere in the mill house—a shift bell, distant—and a ripple went through the tables as men stretched and resettled, and her father winced putting weight on his back, and the ordinary world tilted back into focus, and Oscar heard herself say:

"Dad. If I could get us out of here. If I could clear the debt. Would you leave Dunlow?"

He blinked at her. "Leave? What do you mean, leave?"

"The valley. If we could go. Somewhere else. Somewhere—"

"Oscar." Gently. The way he'd said her name when she was six and had broken the good jug and tried, with a child's flawless logic, to hide the largest shard under the rug. "What's put that in your head?"

Nothing. Everything. *The thorn wakes.* A stranger's handwriting a century old, bearing her name like a brand.

"Nothing's put it in my head," she said, and the lie sat easily in her mouth, which frightened her more than the mark had—except that it didn't sit easily, it sat strangely, heavily, and she thought again of the burning on her arm and wondered, with a cold drop through her middle, whether some part of her body knew things her mouth didn't. "I just think sometimes. About elsewhere. Everyone does."

"Everyone thinks about elsewhere," her father agreed. "That's what elsewhere is for." He picked up a handful of grain from his tray and let it run slowly through his fingers, watching it fall, and his face did something complicated. "You know how we got here."

"You've told me." Many times. The same telling, worn smooth as a river stone. The bad harvests. The loans. The loan upon the loan. The letter from the temple bursar offering terms, and his own signature at the bottom of it, and his wife's grave already dug by then, and a seven-year-old daughter who needed feeding.

"I signed us into this valley," he said. "Nobody marched us in. I read every line of that contract—I was proud of myself for reading every line—and I still signed it, because the lines all said the same thing in different clothes: *you owe, you owe, you owe.*" The grain ran out of his fingers. "And I'll tell you what nobody warned me. Debt doesn't just take what you have. It takes what you might have had. Every year I've been in these yards is a year I didn't spend teaching you the trade properly, or fixing the roof, or—any of it. It doesn't come back, that time. It isn't owed to anybody. It's just gone." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were clear and sad and absolutely sane. "So no, love. If someone handed me a bag of silver tonight, I'd pay the debt and stay right here. Because leaving means admitting those years bought nothing, and I can't afford to think that. Neither can you."

"Dad—"

"Besides." He nudged her, the old familiar nudge, elbow to ribs, and his voice warmed deliberately, climbing back up into the light. "Who'd keep the Chancellor fed if I left? Who'd tell Hesta her rye bread's going up a copper? We're not prisoners here, Oscar. We're furniture. Comfortable furniture. And comfortable furniture doesn't run off to elsewhere."

It was a joke. It was meant as a joke. She laughed, because he needed her to laugh, and the laugh came out sounding real, which was the strangest part—as if her body had decided to protect him from her face and had simply overruled her.

And underneath the laugh, quiet as a second heartbeat, the thought she could not stop thinking: *the contract says you owe, you owe, you owe. And last night a book said your name.*

* * *

The visiting bell rang with ten minutes left, and they spent them the way they always did: her father giving her instructions she didn't need. Eat more. Take the shawl. Don't let Aldous work you past dark. Write to Cousin Ilse in Millbrook about the winter boots—

"Ilse died three years ago, Dad."

"Then write to her son, and ask him about the boots anyway, he owes me for the fence." A pause. "Doesn't he?"

"He does," Oscar said. "He owes you for the fence."

"Good man. Terrible with money. Reminds me of someone." He gathered his tray together, the sorted grain neat as everything else he touched—her father, who could not make his hands obey but made every pile perfect—and stood, and this time she didn't stop him, because the standing-up was his dignity and she'd learned not to steal it.

At the edge of the awning he paused, half turned, backlit by the gray daylight, chaff still in his hair.

"You'd tell me," he said. "If something was wrong. Wouldn't you? You've got your mother's face when you're carrying something. Whole courtyard can see it."

The folded page burned against her ribs.

"I'm fine, Dad," said Oscar Halloran, liar.

He looked at her a moment longer. Whatever he saw—or chose not to see—he nodded slowly, and touched two fingers to his brow in the mock-salute he'd used since she was small, and shuffled off toward the tables, and the overseer called the resumption of labor, and the great wheels outside groaned on and on, grinding the hours down to flour.

* * *

She was nearly at the gate when Hesta fell into step beside her, ostensibly to return her token, actually, judging by her lowered voice, to talk.

"Fellow came asking after you," Hesta said. "This morning. Before dawn, if you please. Temple man."

"Aldous sent someone?"

"No. Inquisitor's office." Hesta handed back the token, and her broad, floury face was carefully blank in the way of people who lived near power and had learned what it cost to notice things. "Polite sort. Very polite. Asked when you usually visited, whether your father's debt was near settled, whether you kept regular hours at the archive." She shrugged elaborately. "I told him visitors' privacy is temple policy. He thanked me for reminding him of policy. Then he asked again."

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. "Did he leave a name?"

"Vane," Hesta said. "Inquisitor Vane. Said he'd likely call on you at the archive himself, save you the walk." She patted Oscar's shoulder with a hand like a bread paddle. "Probably nothing. Men from the city poke about, it's what they're for. Go home, eat something, stop looking like you've seen a ghost."

*Probably nothing,* Oscar thought, walking fast through the yard, past the grinding wheels, past the debtors bent like penitents, her father's voice still in her ears saying *you'd tell me, wouldn't you.*

But she had seen the book. And the book had seen her.

And tomorrow—*tomorrow*, said a century-old hand in ink the color of dried blood—*the thorn wakes.*
