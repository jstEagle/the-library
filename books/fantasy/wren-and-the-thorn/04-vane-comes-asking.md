## 4. Vane Comes Asking

Oscar had rehearsed the lie so many times in her head that by morning it felt like scripture.

*I was in the archive until the last bell. I catalogued the Corvane tithe ledgers and the Psalter of Ashes. I touched nothing else. I saw nothing else.*

The trouble with lying, she discovered somewhere around her third bowl of porridge, was that her body had apparently never been consulted on the plan. Her hands shook. Her spoon clinked against the bowl like a tiny alarm bell. And beneath her sleeve, the ink that should not exist lay quiet as a held breath—waiting, she suspected, for exactly this kind of occasion to make its opinions known.

"You're going to spill that," said Brother Fenwick, sliding onto the bench across from her. He was seventy, mostly deaf, and possessed the unerring instinct of the very old for finding the one person at breakfast who wished to be alone. "You've got the look of a woman about to spill something."

"I'm fine."

"Mm." He buttered his bread with the concentration of a man defusing a bomb. "You know, when I was your age, I once dropped an entire censer down the well during the Feast of Lights. Terrible business. Took three days to fish it out. Do you know what I learned?"

"That God disapproves of incense near water?"

"That a guilty face is worth ten confessions." He pointed his knife at her, cheerfully. "Whatever you did, child, do it better next time or confess faster. The middle road is where all the trouble lives."

He shuffled off before she could decide whether to be offended or grateful, which was, Oscar reflected, probably exactly what he intended.

* * *

Inquisitor Vane arrived at the ninth bell, and he arrived the way frost arrives: no drama, no announcement, just a slow silvering of everything he touched.

Oscar watched him come up the temple steps from the scriptorium window. He wore grey—not temple black, not Inquisitorial white, but a grey so precise it seemed chosen specifically to make other colors feel vulgar about themselves. He was younger than she'd expected. That was the first wrong thing. Inquisitors were supposed to be ancient and crooked, all knuckle and scowl, like the woodcuts in the punishment pamphlets. This man could have been thirty. He had kind eyes, which was the second wrong thing, because everyone knew the dangerous ones were always given kind eyes the way baited hooks were given worms.

"He's early," whispered Sister Odile beside her, who catalogued relics and gossip in equal measure and considered both sacred duties. "Inquisitors are never early. Early means eager. Eager means someone upstairs is frightened."

"Of what?"

Odile gave her a look of profound pity. "Child, if we knew what they were frightened of, we'd be the ones doing the frightening."

They assembled the archive staff in the reading room—the six scribes who worked the restricted collections, plus the under-librarian, plus Oscar, who stood at the back and tried to become furniture. Vane did not sit behind the librarian's desk, which would have been the normal thing. Instead he sat *on* the desk, perched on its edge like a guest who has decided the host's home is now also his, and smiled at them all with those kind eyes.

"Thank you for coming," he said, though none of them had been asked. "I'll be brief, because I know you have work, and work in Dunlow is holy. That's rather the point of Dunlow, isn't it?"

No one answered. He hadn't wanted anyone to.

"The temple has received," he continued, "a report of irregularity in the restricted archive. A seal disturbed. Nothing dramatic—a wax impression pressed slightly out of true, the way wax does when a door is opened twice." He let that sit. "Now, in most temples, this would be a matter for the under-librarian and a new candle. But Dunlow holds a particular collection, and particular collections attract particular attention. So I've come to ask some very simple questions, and I'd like you to answer them simply. Who among you entered the restricted stacks in the past week?"

Six hands went up, including, after a heartbeat of paralysis, Oscar's. Lying by omission, she told herself. Omission wasn't lying. Omission was practically a virtue; the whole valley ran on it.

"And who handled anything from the prophecy folios?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind with weight. Sister Odile's eyes went wide and round as communion wafers. The under-librarian, a damp man named Pell, made a small sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"No one?" said Vane pleasantly. "How interesting. Because the folio shelf shows disturbance too. Dust doesn't lie, friends. It's the only honest witness in any archive—I'm sure you all know that better than I." He hopped down off the desk and began to walk the row of them slowly, hands clasped behind his back, boots making no sound at all on the stone, which was the third wrong thing and by far the worst. "Dust remembers being moved. Wax remembers being broken. Ink remembers—" he paused, and something flickered across the kindness of his face, something older and much less kind, "—ink remembers everything. That's its whole nature. So perhaps the question isn't who read the prophecies. Perhaps the question is: who did the prophecies read?"

He stopped in front of Pell first. Asked him about his mother's health, his rheumatism, whether the temple fed him adequately. Pell, flustered by the courtesy, confessed within four minutes to having taken a blank quire of paper home for his son's letters—an offense so minor that Vane thanked him warmly for his honesty and forgave it on the spot, and Pell sat down looking as though he'd been flayed and healed in the same motion. It was, Oscar realized with a cold slide in her stomach, a demonstration. *This is what confession looks like. This is how easy it is. This is how gentle I am when you give me what I want.*

Then Odile. Then old Brother Anselm, who cried, then laughed, then cried again, and was forgiven for nothing in particular.

And then Vane came to stand in front of Oscar, and the world narrowed to the width of his shadow.

"Scribe Halloran," he said. "Oscar Halloran. You catalogue the restricted collection."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"Your father works the mills. Debt-labor. Eleven years now, isn't it?"

The question landed somewhere below her ribs. "Yes."

"That's a long time to pay for a man's mistakes." His voice stayed soft, almost tender. "You must love him very much. Or resent him very much. Those often wear the same face." He tilted his head. "Tell me about the night of the seal. Walk me through it. You're a scribe—you're good at exact things. Be exact."

So she told the lie.

She told it smoothly, better than she'd rehearsed, the Corvane ledgers and the Psalter of Ashes, the last bell, the locked door, the cold walk back to the dormitory. She heard her own voice saying the words and marveled distantly at how reasonable they sounded, how *true*, and thought: maybe I'm not built for guilt after all, maybe I'll survive this—

And the mark woke up.

It started as warmth, low on her forearm, like sunlight through a window. Then it was heat. Then it was a coal laid lengthwise along her vein, and every word she spoke stoked it, and the words kept coming because stopping would be worse, wouldn't it, stopping would be confession—and the ink burned through cloth, through skin, into the bone, and somewhere very far away Vane was still speaking in that soft voice, asking if she was quite well, Scribe Halloran, you've gone pale—

The floor came up and met her with the traditional Christian humility of flagstone.

* * *

Vane watched the girl faint and felt something twist in his chest that he had spent nine years learning to ignore.

He crouched beside her while the others fluttered. Her sleeve had ridden up. Just for a moment—half a heartbeat—he saw the inside of her forearm, and there, in lines of black that were not quite ink and not quite scar, was writing. He didn't need to read it. He knew it the way a man knows his own reflection in a dark window. He had carried those same words on his own skin for nine years, in the place where his soul used to be.

*The thorn wakes.*

He straightened, and his face did what it was trained to do, which was nothing.

"She's overworked," he announced to the room. "Debt-families always push their children past sense. Give her water and a day of rest. No one is in trouble here." He smiled at them, the full kindly smile, and watched them relax, and hated himself a little more efficiently than usual. "I'll want to speak with Scribe Halloran privately when she recovers. Tomorrow will do. There's no hurry."

There was every hurry. There had been hurry for nine years. But men who hurried looked frightened, and frightened men were dismissed, and Vane had no intention of being dismissed before he understood what had come awake in this valley of dead air and obedient hearts.

That evening he walked the temple grounds alone, as he always did in strange places, counting exits out of habit. The millpond steamed in the cold. Somewhere beyond the eastern hills, past the border stones no one crossed, the world presumably went on being full of fire and light and all the things Dunlow had been built to forget.

An old groundskeeper nodded to him as he passed. "Cold night, Inquisitor."

"Cold everywhere," Vane said.

"Aye, but it's a *clean* cold here. No bite to it. Never has been." The old man squinted at the sky. "My grandfather used to say the valley keeps the weather polite. Like a guest that never leaves."

Vane almost smiled. *A guest that never leaves.* The valley itself, described perfectly by a man who would be punished for knowing it. Everything in Dunlow was like that—the truth sitting in plain sight, wearing laborer's clothes, waiting for someone with the rank to hear it.

He had been twenty-three when he'd found the book. A junior archivist in the capital, ambitious, devout, certain the world was a machine that ran on rules. He had opened the folio because opening forbidden things was what ambition did, and he had read the final page, and the words had climbed off the page and into him like they were coming home.

And then he had done the only wise thing available to a clever coward.

He had refused.

He remembered the moment precisely: standing in the archive with the book open, feeling the Thorn settle into him like a seed into furrow, feeling it *ask*—and saying no. Saying it aloud, alone, at midnight, to a book. And the seed had withdrawn, unhurried, unoffended, the way a creditor withdraws from a man with nothing to seize. It had left the words behind, though. Burned them into his arm as a receipt. *This account remains open.*

The temple had known within a month. They always knew. They hadn't killed him—that would have been wasteful. Instead they had taken everything else. Nine years of service, of obedience, of being sent wherever the temple's quiet fears pointed, hollowed out one refusal at a time until there was very little left inside the grey coat except the waiting. They called him their hound. Hounds, Vane had learned, were not rewarded for pointing at the quarry. They were rewarded for not running away.

But tonight, watching a debt-scribe's daughter collapse under the weight of her first lie, Vane had felt something he had not felt since that midnight long ago.

Hope. Ugly, selfish hope.

Because the words had gone into *her*. The Thorn had chosen—or been chosen—and she was young, and stubborn, and clearly a liar of recent vintage and poor quality, which meant she still had a conscience left to burn. And if she ran—if she did what he had been too afraid to do, if she took the thing outside the border where it could live—then perhaps the account would close. Perhaps the temple would release its hound. Perhaps there was a version of his life that started after this week instead of ending in it.

Or perhaps she would die in the marshes, and he would carry her name in his reports, and the waiting would continue.

Either way, tomorrow he would sit across from her, and he would ask his soft questions, and he would watch her choose between the truth and the burn. And whatever she chose, he intended to be standing closer to the door than she expected.

Vane turned up his collar against the clean, polite cold, and went in to write a report that said nothing, which was the most honest document he'd produced in years.

* * *

Oscar woke in the infirmary with a wet cloth on her forehead and Sister Odile keeping vigil with the focused energy of a woman who had been dying to gossip for hours.

"Fainted dead away," Odile said, with relish. "Right in front of the Inquisitor. Fenwick says it's your blood. Pell says it's your father's debts catching up. I say it's neither." She leaned in. "I say it's a secret."

Oscar looked at the ceiling. Beneath the bandage Odile had thoughtfully wrapped around her forearm—*you scratched yourself in your sleep, dear, you must stop worrying so much*—the mark throbbed dully, like a bruise with a memory.

"Odile," she said carefully, "if a person had done something foolish, and couldn't undo it, and telling the truth would destroy people she loved—what would you advise?"

Odile considered this with the seriousness it deserved, which was considerable.

"I'd advise her," she said at last, "to get a very good night's sleep. Because people who ask questions like that at dusk are usually gone by dawn." She patted Oscar's hand briskly and stood. "There's bread in the tin. Eat it before Fenwick finds it. And child—" she paused at the door, not turning around, "—the east marsh path floods at high water but it's passable at moonrise, if a person were foolish enough to need to know that. Old Maren's hut is past the third stone marker. Tell her Odile still owes her for the feverfew."

The door closed.

Oscar lay in the dim infirmary, heart hammering, and unwound the bandage. The words on her arm had changed. Where yesterday they had read *the thorn wakes*, tonight a single line had added itself beneath, in the same impossible hand:

*She runs at moonrise.*

Oscar stared at it for a long time. Then, very quietly, in the voice of a scribe arguing with a text—which was, after all, the only argument she'd ever been trained for—she said:

"I haven't decided yet."

The ink seemed, smugly, to wait.

Outside, the moon was already climbing over the eastern hills, and the marshes beyond them had begun, patiently, to flood.
