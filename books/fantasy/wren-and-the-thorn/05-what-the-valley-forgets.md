## 5. What the Valley Forgets

Maren Osgood had lived in the marshes long enough to know that the valley forgot things on purpose.

It forgot her name within a season of her exile, which had suited her fine. It forgot the road that once ran to her door, so that travelers passed her hut by without ever quite seeing it. It forgot old songs halfway through their choruses, so that even the oldest singers in Dunlow hummed a tuneless nothing where the verses used to be. The valley was not cruel about its forgetting. It was simply thorough, the way a careful housekeeper is thorough, and after eleven years Maren had made her peace with being one of the things swept under the floorboards.

She was mending a fish trap on her stoop when she saw the girl come stumbling out of the reeds.

This was unusual. Nothing came stumbling out of the reeds. The reeds gave things back—herons, mostly, and once a very indignant goose—but they did not deliver strangers. Yet here was a stranger: young, scribe-pale, dressed in temple grey with her sleeves rolled up as though she'd been running from someone who wanted very much to keep her sleeves down. Her boots were soaked past the ankle. Her hair had surrendered entirely.

Maren set down the fish trap and reached for the iron poker she kept beside the door for exactly this sort of occasion, though in eleven years the occasion had never once arrived, and the poker had been demoted to stirring porridge.

"Evening," Maren said pleasantly, in the tone of a woman who could be friendly or lethal depending entirely on the answer to her next question. "You're a long way from the temple."

The girl looked up. She had ink on her fingers and terror on her face, and the combination was so familiar that Maren felt it like a hand pressed flat against her own chest, thirty years deep.

"I need—" The girl's voice cracked. She tried again. "I was told you could help me. A woman at the mill said to follow the drainage cut until I saw a hut with blue bottles in the window, and then to be polite."

"Did she?" Maren glanced at her window, where six blue bottles caught the last of the light. "And are you?"

"Am I what?"

"Polite."

"I—" The girl hesitated, and Maren watched her decide, with visible effort, that lying was no longer among her available options. "I don't know yet. My name is Oscar Halloran. I work in the archive. Something happened to me three nights ago, and everyone who might explain it wants to burn me for it, and the mill-woman said—" Here the girl's composure finally went, all at once, like a dropped plate. "She said you were exiled for magic, and that if anyone would know what this is, you would."

She pushed back her sleeve.

Maren put down the poker.

* * *

Let it be recorded—and Maren Osgood, whatever else might be said of her, believed in keeping records—that she did not panic. Panic was for people whose lives still had shape left to ruin. What she felt instead was something older and more patient: the particular dread of a woman who has spent a decade hiding from a thing only to watch it walk up her path wearing a scribe's boots.

The mark ran from the girl's wrist to the crook of her elbow, black letters in no alphabet any living kingdom used, except that threaded through them—coiled around them the way ivy coils around a fence post—ran words in plain Dunlow script:

*the thorn wakes*

*she runs at moonrise*

"You've been copying prophecy texts," Maren said. Not a question.

"The archive. Restricted section." Oscar swallowed. "There's a book. Nobody's catalogued it in a century—the seal dates to the founding. The last entry is dated tomorrow. And it has my name in it, which is impossible, because whoever wrote it died before my grandmother was born." She laughed, a thin unhappy sound. "Except it gets worse. I copied the page. Just copied it, ink and paper, ordinary transcription, I've done it ten thousand times. And when I woke up it was *on me*. And when I lie, it burns. Inquisitor Vane asked me yesterday whether I'd entered the restricted stacks without authorization, and I said no, and I fainted in front of four acolytes. Do you know how hard it is to faint convincingly? I didn't even try. The mark did it for me. Like a conscience with teeth."

"And now you're running," Maren said.

"I haven't decided yet." The girl said it quickly, defensively, the way people repeat a phrase they've been using to argue with themselves for days. Then her shoulders sagged. "Yes. I'm running. Tomorrow night. Moonrise. Apparently even my own body has opinions about my schedule."

Maren stood, joints cracking their usual complaints, and went inside to put the kettle on. This was partly hospitality and partly because some conversations could not be held sitting down. When she returned with two chipped cups, Oscar was reading the titles off the jars on the windowsill—*nettle salt, grave-moss, foxfire tincture, DO NOT*—with the particular hunger of a scholar offered an uncatalogued shelf.

"That last one's for warts," Maren said. "Sit."

Oscar sat.

"Now," Maren said, "I'm going to tell you three things, and you're going to want to interrupt after each one, and I'd take it as a kindness if you saved your interruptions up for the end. First thing: everything the temple taught you about the valley is wrong, but not in the direction you think. They teach you the valley is blessed, yes? Set apart. Protected. Magicless because it's pure."

"Magic is sin," Oscar recited, automatic, then winced at herself. "That's the catechism. I wrote it out forty times as a girl."

"Mm. Second thing: there is no such thing as a place where magic can't live. Magic grows everywhere, child, like grass. Like bindweed. You cannot make ground where it will not grow. What you *can* do—" Maren held up her cup, and let herself feel, just for a moment, the old ache of the working she had not done in eleven years, the power pressing against the inside of her like water against a dam, present and useless and hers. "What you can do is build a lid. This valley has a lid. A null field, a hundred years old, laid down over every field and hearth and cradle in Dunlow. Nothing magical can survive inside it. Including me. Including you, now, which brings us—"

"To the third thing." Oscar's voice had gone very small.

"The third thing," Maren said gently, "is that whatever lives in your arm is stronger than the lid."

* * *

To do the girl credit, she did not interrupt. She sat with her cup cooling in her hands while Maren told her the rest, or as much of the rest as Maren knew, which was less than Maren pretended and more than anyone else in the valley knew at all.

She told her about the war—not the tidy schoolroom version with its righteous houses and its grateful commons, but the real one, the one her own grandmother had hummed fragments of into the dishwater: the age when magic belonged to everybody the way weather belongs to everybody, wild and common and unownable; and the slow ugly centuries when the great houses learned to breed it true, to bind it to bloodlines, to make of it a property, a title, a deed; and the commons who refused to sell, whose power stayed loose and strange and could not be collected.

"And you can't tax what you can't hold," Maren said. "So the victors built the vault. Only they called it a refuge. Dunlow, the safe valley, the pure valley—where the dispossessed could live free of magic's corruption." She snorted. "Free of it. Imprisoned by it. Every man and woman in this valley is descended from people whose power was confiscated at the border and poured into the working underneath our feet. The valley isn't sheltering us from the storm, girl. The valley *is* the strongbox. We're the coins."

Oscar looked down at her hands. Scribe's hands, ink-stained, careful. "My father," she said. "He works the mill debt. His father before him. We always thought—we were told we were being kept *safe*."

"You were being kept," Maren said. "Which is different."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Outside, a marsh bird made its idiot remark and thought better of it.

"And this Thorn?" Oscar asked at last. "The book says it wakes. The mark says—I say—it runs at moonrise. What is it? What did I read into myself?"

Maren took a long breath, because the honest answer required climbing over several of her own lies first, and she had grown fond of her lies; they kept the porch warm.

"There was a tree," she said. "Before the houses, before the war, before names for things. The oldest stories agree on almost nothing, but they agree on that. A tree at the root of the world, and all magic grew from it like fruit, and when the houses began fencing the fruit, someone—no story agrees who—cut a seed from the living wood and hid it. Primal magic, unbound. Uninheritable. The one thing in creation none of the great houses could breed into their bloodlines or tax out of anyone else's. Every house that ever found it tried to own it. That's what the stories say, anyway, and the stories also say every house that tried rotted from the crown down, so perhaps take the ambition with a warning."

"And the book in the archive—"

"Was the seed's alarm bell. Its watchdog. Somebody bound it to the vault, to this valley, and set it to sleep until the Thorn woke again. And when a foolish, dutiful, *polite* young scribe opened the book and transcribed its last page with her own honest hand—" Maren spread her palms. "Well. The lock doesn't care whose hand turns the key. The Thorn needed a vessel inside the null field, someone the lid would hold fast until she could be carried out. Someone who couldn't lie. Someone with debts that kept her exactly where the vault wanted her."

Oscar was quiet for a moment. Then, in the voice of a woman arguing with a text, which Maren would learn was the only argument she'd ever been trained for: "My father's debt. Even that. Even the debt was—"

"No," Maren said, and surprised herself with the force of it. "No, child, listen to me, because this part matters and the stories get it wrong on purpose. The vault may have arranged the board. It did not write your heart. You love your father. You honor your debts. Those are yours, and no prophecy gets to claim them. Whatever uses you, it uses what's *true* in you—and what's true in you is better than most people manage in a whole life untouched by destiny." She paused, then added, because honesty was the discipline of her exile: "Though I'll grant you that's cold comfort when the mark burns you for telling a fib."

"It's not cold comfort," Oscar said, and to Maren's eye it was even true; the girl held her fear the way she must have held difficult manuscripts, respectfully, reading all the way to the end. "It's terrible comfort. But comfort." She looked at her arm, at the coiled letters, and asked the question Maren had been dreading. "Can you take it out?"

"Out of you?"

"Out of me. Cut it, draw it, bleed it—I don't know how these things work. Can you give it to someone else? Put it back?"

Maren rose and went to the window. Dusk was settling over the marsh like a lid of its own, and the water channels were filling with the evening tide, patient as everything else out here. Eleven years she had lived in the valley's blind spot, invisible and forgotten, and she had told herself it was peace. It had been hiding, which resembles peace the way a painted door resembles a door.

"I could no sooner take it from you than I could take your pulse with these hands," she said. "I'm a hedge-witch in a null zone, Oscar Halloran. Everything I know is in those jars, and half of them are labeled *DO NOT*. If I could pull primal magic out of a living vessel, do you truly believe I'd have spent a decade teaching geese to leave my garden?"

"They clearly respect you."

"They fear me moderately. It's not the same." Maren turned back. The girl was smiling, faintly, and Maren hated how easy it would be to grow attached to her, and did it anyway, immediately, the way she'd done with every stray thing the marsh had ever delivered. "No. The Thorn goes where it goes, and what the book seems to be telling us is that it means to go *outward*, tonight or tomorrow night, at moonrise, across the border. Which brings us to the question you've been too polite to ask since you walked in."

"Which is?"

"Whether I'm coming with you." Maren sat back down and wrapped her hands around her cup, and found them steadier than she expected. "Because here is what I know about vessels carrying seeds of world-trees across hostile borders: they run badly. They fall in ditches. They trust smugglers. They need somebody who knows where the wardens' lanterns go dark and which channels flood deepest, and there is precisely one person in this valley who knows both, and she is drinking tea with you."

"You'd leave," Oscar said softly. "The null field. If you cross the border, your magic comes back. After eleven years."

"Yes, and I expect it will be like standing up too fast after a long illness, and I expect I'll cry, and I expect you'll have the good manners not to mention it." Maren drained her cup and stood. "Fetch your sleeve down, child. And eat something. Running is hungrier work than anyone expects."

But Oscar had gone still, her head cocked toward the window, toward the east.

"Moonrise isn't until tomorrow," she said. "The mark says moonrise."

"Mm," said Maren, who had heard it too.

Down the drainage cut, faint but closing, came the sound that the marshes never made: boots in rhythm. Lantern-light swung among the reeds, four points of it, maybe five, moving with the unhurried certainty of people following something that could not hide its glow. And under the lantern-light, thin as a wire, Oscar's forearm had begun to shine through her sleeve like a page held up to a flame.

"Well," said Maren, reaching for the poker, which was at least good for hitting things. "It appears the moon has decided to rise early. Get behind me, child, and mind the third plank—it's a liar."
