## 6. The Gate at Dusk

Maren woke her by throwing a shoe.

Not at her—at the wall beside her head, which was somehow more insulting. "Up," the old woman said, already moving through the dim hut, snuffing candles and pulling shutters. "They're coming."

Oscar sat up in the narrow cot, her heart still half-drowned in sleep. The mark on her forearm throbbed, a slow red pulse beneath her sleeve, as if it had heard before she had. "Who's coming? How do you know?"

"Because I've lived on this bog for thirty years, girl, and I know what it sounds like when the temple remembers I exist." Maren was stuffing things into a satchel with the brisk violence of someone who had done this before—more than once, judging by the practiced way she swept jars of dried nettle off a shelf into a cloth without looking at them. "Boots. Now. And whatever you do, don't argue with me while I'm packing. It makes me forget things."

"What kind of things?"

"Important ones." She paused, held up a small clay pot of greenish salve, considered it, and put it back. "See? Already lost one."

Outside, the light was going strange. Dusk in Dunlow always came down like a lid—gray thickening to slate, the valley's dead air pressing close—but tonight there was something under it. A tremble in the ground that Oscar felt in her teeth. Far off toward the causeway road, torchlight moved in formation. Not two torches. Not six. She counted the bobbing lights and stopped counting because the number kept growing.

"That's not a patrol," she whispered.

"That's an Inquisition," Maren said. "Full dress. They only do that for heresy or tax arrears, and I've paid my taxes." A beat. "Mostly."

"Maren." Oscar's voice cracked. "How did they find me? I didn't tell anyone. I didn't *write* anyone. I—"

"You walked out here through open marsh carrying a lit candle made of prophecy," Maren said flatly. "You think the valley doesn't notice? This whole place is a lock, child, and you are the key rattling around inside it. Something out there—" she jabbed a thumb at the darkening hills, "—felt you coming the moment you crossed my threshold."

The mark burned. Oscar pressed her palm over it, hard, as if she could hold it down like a lid on a boiling pot. "Then we run. Together. You know the marsh—you said so—"

Maren laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as reeds snapping. "Run where? I'm seventy years old and my knees sound like the mill gates. No, love. This part isn't yours to carry." She thrust the satchel into Oscar's arms. It was heavier than it looked, packed with the particular density of a life reduced to essentials. "There's a path. Follow the white stones—they're old, older than me, older than the temple, and don't ask how I know about them because I'll lie and then we'll both feel awkward. The path goes to the border."

Oscar's stomach dropped. "The border's a story. Nobody crosses. The guards say—"

"The guards say the world outside is poison and magic is sin, and you believed them for twenty-three years, which shows you're capable of believing anything." Maren caught her face between weathered hands, and her eyes—milk-pale, unsettling, kind—held hers. "Listen to me. When you cross, it will feel like dying. It isn't. It feels like dying because everything you are has been living in a room with no windows, and windows are loud. Breathe through it. Count. Scribes are good at counting."

"I can't leave you here." Her voice came out very small. "They'll—"

"They'll question an old marsh-witch about a runaway debtor and get nothing but complaints about my roof." Maren smiled, and it almost reached her eyes. "I've been nobody for thirty years, girl. It's the safest thing I've ever been. Go be somebody. It's more dangerous, but the food's better."

Somewhere behind the hut, a dog barked—the temple hounds. Then another. Then the barking became a chorus, and torchlight bloomed orange through the marsh fog like a sunrise arriving from the wrong direction.

"*Go,*" Maren said, and pushed her.

* * *

Oscar ran.

She ran the way she imagined rabbits ran—all instinct and no plan, satchel banging against her hip, boots finding the white stones by luck and prayer. The marsh took her in like an accomplice. Fog closed behind her footsteps; reeds whipped at her arms; the mud sucked and released her boots with a sound like wet applause. Behind her, voices rose—sharp, official, disappointed.

"Hut's empty!"

"She can't have gone far. Sweep the fen. Torch the reed-beds if you have to."

And then, closer, colder, a voice she knew from the archive corridors, from polite interrogations and gentle questions that were never really questions:

"Do not burn anything. I want the book intact, and I want the girl breathing. Inquisitor's order. Anyone who damages either answers to me personally."

Vane. Her stomach turned over. She pressed deeper into the fog, one hand on the mark, counting her steps because counting was all she knew how to do. *Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and twelve.* The white stones glimmered ahead like a spine of old bone, leading her out into the deepest black of the fen, where even the temple's long fingers had never built a drainage ditch.

The ground began to rise. The reeds gave way to heather, the heather to bare stone, and the air changed—it grew thin and clean and hungry, like the first breath after a fever breaks. Ahead, maybe fifty paces, stood a line she could not see but could feel, the way you feel a wall in total darkness: the edge of Dunlow. The edge of the only world she'd ever known.

Behind her, the torches crested the ridge of the fen. Hounds bayed. And Vane's voice carried across the water, clear and terrible, not shouting at all, which was worse:

"Oscar Halloran. You read something you were never meant to read. Come back now, and I promise the temple will be merciful."

Her father's face rose in her mind—stooped at the mill gate, flour-white, smiling his broken smile. Her little room above the scriptorium. Brother Aldous's terrible puns. The smell of ink and lamp oil. Twenty-three years of a life assembled from small, decent things, standing behind her in the fog like a lit window in winter.

The mark flared so hot she gasped—and then it did something new. It pulled. Gently, insistently, like a hand taking hers. Toward the line.

"It wants me to go," she whispered. "Of course it does. Everything wants something. That's all magic ever seems to be."

From behind: the ring of drawn steel. The baying turning eager. Vane's voice again, softer, and somehow that softness decided her: "Miss Halloran. There is no version of tomorrow in which you are still yourself. There is only a version in which you are ours."

"No," Oscar said aloud, to him, to the mark, to the whole gray prison of a valley. "There's a version where I'm mine."

She counted three steps—one, two, three, like Maren taught her, like every ledger she'd ever balanced—and crossed.

* * *

Dying, Maren had promised, was the wrong word for it.

It was still a very good word.

The border took her the way deep water takes a diver—not violently, but totally. Sound went first. The hounds, the shouts, the whole audible world of Dunlow simply ceased, as if someone had closed a great door. Then color drained out of the grass under her hands, out of the sky, out of her own skin, and for one endless heartbeat Oscar Halloran existed nowhere at all—a scribe-shaped absence, a page with the words scraped off.

Then the other side poured in.

It came as light. Not sunlight—something older and less polite, light that had opinions. It flooded through her eyes and her ears and her teeth and settled into her bones like wine into bread, and Oscar dropped to her knees on foreign grass and *screamed*, or laughed, or possibly both, because the world was singing.

That was the only word for it. The wind had a voice. The earth had a hum, low and vast, like a choir heard through a cathedral floor. Every blade of grass around her shimmered faintly at its edges, as though each one were remembering it was alive. Above her, stars—real stars, not Dunlow's flat painted dots—burned in colors she had no names for, wheeling in slow, deliberate spirals, as if the sky itself were thinking.

"Oh," she said. And then, because she was still, underneath everything, a scribe: "*Oh.*"

The mark on her arm had gone quiet. Not silent—quiet, the way a cat is quiet when it finally gets into the sunbeam it's been demanding all day. Warm. Content. Home, some treacherous part of her supplied, and she told that part firmly to be silent.

She knelt there for a long time. Long enough for her legs to stop shaking. Long enough for the vertigo to fade from a hurricane to a strong sea-breeze. Long enough to notice, distantly, that she was crying, and that the tears were for everything at once—for Maren alone in her hut with the Inquisitors, for her father who would learn by morning that his daughter had fled the debt and taken her name into the dark, for the valley shrinking behind her like a country seen from a departing ship, small and gray and beloved and false.

"I'm sorry," she told all of them, uselessly. The wind carried it away, and the wind, at least, seemed to accept the apology. It ruffled her hair almost kindly, and somewhere in its rustling she thought she heard—not words, exactly, but the *shape* of words. An accent. The world outside spoke a language she'd been born without and suddenly, desperately wanted to learn.

She got up. She made herself inventory, because inventories were safe, and scribes survived by making lists. One satchel, Maren's. One runaway debtor, hers. One seed of apocalyptic wild magic, lodged somewhere behind her ribs, currently purring. Zero coins, zero allies, zero idea where the white stones led now that they'd delivered her to the edge of a world that could kill her in a thousand inventive ways her temple tutors had described in loving detail.

Ahead, the land rolled away in silver-dark waves of heather and stone, descending toward a distant smudge of light that might have been a village, or a campfire, or a trap. The white stones continued—she could just make them out, threading downhill like a dropped rosary—and beside the first of them, half-buried in turf, stood a marker post with letters cut deep and worn shallow:

**DUNLOW — CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE CONCORD — TRESPASSERS WILL BE ANSWERED FOR**

Answered for. Not punished. Answered, like a question. Like a summons.

Oscar stared at it for a long moment, and then, because her whole life had been spent correcting other people's errors in other people's books, she drew a breath, crouched down, and added—with her finger, in the dust of the post's base, where any rain would erase it and no law could call it vandalism—a single word beneath the warning:

**Why?**

The mark pulsed once, warm against her wrist. Approval, or laughter. With the Thorn, she suspected, it would take years to learn the difference.

She shouldered Maren's satchel, took one last look at the gray line of the border behind her—at the faint orange bloom of torches, still searching a fen that no longer held her—and started downhill toward the light.

She was halfway there when she saw she wasn't the only one moving toward it. Two figures, further down the slope, walking fast and low: a big man with a lantern hooded against discovery, and beside him, stumbling, wrapped in an adult's cloak several sizes too large—

a child. A small, exhausted, unmistakably *glowing* child, faint gold light leaking from between their fingers like they'd been caught holding fireflies and couldn't let go.

The man's head came up. He'd seen her. His hand moved to something at his belt.

"Oi," he called across the darkening hillside, wary and sharp. "You lost, friend? Because nobody crosses that border by accident, and nobody walks this road at night unless they're running from something—or toward something worth stealing."

Oscar tightened her grip on the satchel, aware of the mark burning bright enough now to light her own silhouette, aware that hiding it was no longer an option, and called back the truest thing she'd said all day:

"Both!"
