## 7. A Kingdom of Inherited Fire

Tam Vessel had a rule about roads: never walk one you can't explain. A man with no cart, no horse, and no honest reason to be anywhere was a riddle, and soldiers loved solving riddles with sticks.

Which was why he was crouched in the ditch beside the Corvane high road at dawn, chewing on the last heel of yesterday's bread and watching a girl walk down the center of it like she'd never seen a road before.

She probably hadn't, he realized. Not one like this.

There was something wrong about her gait—the careful, flat-footed way she moved, as if the ground might do something. And there was something wrong about the way the road itself behaved around her. Every lamplighter's spark, every wisp of heat off a passing wagon's ward-stove, every stray thread of the road-wardens' patrol-glamour bent slightly toward her, the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone. Small things. Things nobody but a smuggler who'd spent ten years learning to see magic move would ever notice.

Things that made Tam's neck prickle.

"Down," he hissed, before his brain caught up with his mouth. "Soldiers at the bend."

The girl dropped into the ditch beside him with more obedience than sense, which immediately put him in mind of a startled heron. She was maybe nineteen, thin as a psalm, with ink stains up both arms—actual ink stains, or what looked like ink stains until you saw them *move*, faint and dark under her skin like veins in marble. Her eyes were doing the thing country eyes did when they first came out of the null valleys: wide, wet, overwhelmed, trying to count everything at once.

"You're not from here," Tam said.

"No," she said. "Is it always—" She gestured vaguely at everything. The sky. The hedgerows, threaded faintly silver with growth-charms gone feral. The distant spires of a tithe-station, glowing a soft, greedy amber against the morning gray.

"Always what?"

"Loud," she said. "Magic is so *loud* out here."

Tam blinked. Nobody had ever described it to him as loud. Heavy, yes. Expensive, certainly. Loud was new. Loud was interesting.

"I'm Tam," he said. "And you're about to get us both hanged if you keep glowing like a festival lamp, so let's get you walking normal."

"I'm not glowing."

"You're a little bit glowing. Come on. Keep your head down, breathe through your mouth, and if anyone asks, you're my cousin Ossy from the eastern holdings, and you've got the wasting sickness, which is why you look terrible."

"I don't look terrible."

"You look like a heron that lost a legal dispute. Walk."

* * *

Oscar Halloran had spent three days in a world that ran on fire.

That was how Maren had explained it, back in the marsh, in the last hour before the temple horns sounded: *Out there, girl, magic isn't a story. It's property. It's inherited like land and taxed like grain, and the folk who have none pay for the ones who have plenty. You've crossed out of the cage. Mind you don't admire the bars.*

She had not fully understood until the second morning, when their road passed a village where the tithe-wagon stood in the square.

She had thought, at first, that someone was being punished. The villagers stood in a line, and light was coming out of them—a thin, bluish shimmer rising from their cupped hands, drawn up out of their chests like breath in winter, funneling into a lead-lined barrel on the wagon. An old woman near the end of the line swayed as hers left her, and a boy of perhaps eight wept without sound, and the wagon's collector, robed in Corvane scarlet, tapped his slate with a stylus and called, "Next," in the bored voice of a man weighing flour.

"What are they taking?" Oscar had whispered.

"Their spark," Tam said. He hadn't whispered. He never seemed to whisper about any of it; he spoke of the tithes the way other men spoke of weather, steady and flat and furious underneath. "Everyone's born with a little. Most folk's is small enough to live without—warmth for a sickroom, luck for the crops, that kind of thing. The houses take it. Have taken it, six generations. They call it *contribution*. My mother called it bleeding, but then, my mother had opinions."

"And they use it for what?"

"Lighting their halls. Warming their gardens so roses bloom at midwinter. Running their wards." He'd jerked his chin toward the far hills, where a nobleman's estate sat inside a shimmering dome of pale gold, beautiful as a soap bubble. "Old Lord Corvane's granddaughter wanted a cloudless wedding day once, so they pulled the weather-magic out of four villages to give her one. Rained dust in those villages till harvest failed. But the wedding was *lovely*. There's a song about it. The song doesn't mention the dust."

He'd smiled when he said it, a crooked, bitter smile, and Oscar had understood that the smile was load-bearing. That behind it lived a man who could go one of two ways at any moment, and chose, daily, deliberately, to be funny instead of terrible.

Now, walking the high road with her head down and her borrowed cough rattling in her throat, she watched him work. Because he was working. Every mile, some small sleight: palming a charm off a milestone and slipping it into his coat, murmuring a counter-word that let a chained gate swing open an hour early, pausing to scratch out the house-mark on a boundary stone so a tenant farmer's pigs could graze the lord's verge for a season before anyone noticed. None of it large. All of it theft, by the law of this kingdom. He did it the way she'd once copied psalms—automatically, devotedly, one letter at a time.

"You're smiling," Tam observed.

"I'm cataloguing," Oscar said. "You'd be surprised how much of it I recognize. The temple did half of this too, just slower. We didn't pull magic out of people because we couldn't. But debt—that's our tithe. My father's been paying the Halloran debt with his back in the mill-yard since I was nine." She paused. "I used to think it was fair. Honest labor for honest weight. Now I'm looking at that woman's hands coming out of the tithe-line and I can't make it sit straight anymore."

"That's the trick of crossing the border," Tam said. "Everything you believed comes loose in its frame. Give it a week. You'll either get angry or get used to it, and I'll tell you now, only one of those keeps your soul."

"Which one kept yours?"

"The angry one. Obviously." He grinned. "It's the fun one."

* * *

They slept that night in the loft of a tithe-barn—not a contradiction, Tam explained, because the barn belonged to a widow who paid her tithe in stored grain and hated House Corvane with the clean heat of a struck flint, and hiding smugglers in the loft was the only revenge available to a woman with no magic and no sons.

Oscar lay on the rough hay, arm throbbing its low familiar ache, and listened to the estate lights beyond the hills. She could feel them now. That was the part nobody warned you about. Out here, with the valley's deadness no longer pressing on her like a hand over her mouth, she could *feel* magic the way you feel a fire in a cold room—each great house a hearth burning stolen wood, each drained village a hearth swept bare. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse in her forearm, the Thorn turned over in its sleep and leaned toward all that brightness like a dog scenting home.

*Not yet,* she told it silently. *Whatever you are. Not yet.*

Below her, Tam sat cross-legged by the loft door, whittling a child's toy out of fence-scrap—a little wooden wren, wings half-raised.

"Who are the children?" Oscar asked. She'd pieced it together across two days: the coded knocks, the barns, the way he counted heads in his mutterings—*four at Halden's, two more past the ford, the twins are seven now, gods.* "The ones you're moving. Who are they?"

"Unlicensed," Tam said, not looking up. "That's the legal word. Means they were born with too much. A farmer's daughter who sets the thatch alight when she has a nightmare. A miller's son who can hear what people mean instead of what they say. Small miracles in poor cradles. And the moment a house finds out—and there's always a finder-out, they pay schoolteachers and midwives to watch—those children go on a list. Then the list goes to the tithe-office. Then the tithe-office sends a wagon." His knife paused. "Officially, they're taken to the house for 'fostering and training.' Unofficially, strong raw spark burns brighter than old worn spark, and a great house's lights need feeding. Some of them really are fostered. The clever ones, the docile ones. The rest go below stairs and aren't spoken of, and their parents get a letter with a wax seal and nothing else, ever again."

"So you steal them first."

"I *relocate* them. Very fast. At night. To folk in the hill-country who owe me favors and hate houses even more than they fear them." He blew a shaving off the wren's wing. "It's not rescue, exactly. Rescue implies you can put things back how they were. It's more... rerouting. The river wants to flow somewhere, so you dig it another channel before somebody builds a mill on it."

"How many?"

"Hm?"

"How many children have you moved?"

He considered the question with the seriousness of a merchant counting stock. "Forty-one that I know reached safety. Two I don't know about—one wagon I lost on the coast road three winters back, storm season. I think about those two more than the forty-one. That's just arithmetic of the heart; the losses weigh double." He glanced up at her, sharp and sudden. "Why? Thinking of volunteering? Fair warning: the pay is nothing, the beds are hay, and everyone with authority wants you dead."

"I was thinking," Oscar said slowly, "that I spent six years cataloguing a restricted archive and believed I was serving something. Keeping knowledge safe. Orderly. And I've been out here four days and I've already helped steal two eggs from a tithe-coop, and it's the most useful thing I've done since I took my vows."

Tam laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him, loud enough that the widow's dog barked below. "Oh, you *are* going to be trouble. Scribe, are you? Actual letters-and-ledgers scribe?"

"Eleven years."

"Can you forge a seal impression?"

"...Yes."

"Then the gods have finally answered somebody's prayers, though I suspect they meant it for me." He tossed her the wooden wren. "Here. For the road. It's tradition—I carve one for every job. Don't ask what happens to the others."

Oscar turned it in her fingers. It was crude and warm and perfect, wings half-raised, ready to fly or ready to fight, impossible to say which.

"It's a wren," she said.

"They're lucky," Tam said. "Small birds. Too small to bother catching, and they sing louder than anything their size has a right to. My mother's favorite." Something crossed his face, brief as a shadow of a cloud, and was filed away. "Sleep, cousin Ossy. Tomorrow we cross the ford at Millbrook, and Millbrook's got a problem."

"What kind of problem?"

"The interesting kind." He lay back, hands behind his head, entirely too pleased. "A tithe-wagon, due through tomorrow noon, carrying collection from three villages. Guarded by four men. And riding in the back of it, per the list I read off a drunk clerk in Halden's tavern—two unlicensed children from the Weepford holding. Ages six and nine."

Oscar sat up so fast the hay rustled. "You want to rob a tithe-wagon."

"I want to *relocate its contents*," Tam said primly. "The wagon robbery is incidental. Entirely incidental. Say it enough times and even the Inquisition believes you." He closed his eyes. "Probably."

She should have said no. She heard herself not saying no—the silence stretching out, filling up with the memory of a blue-shimmering old woman in a tithe line, and a boy of eight weeping without sound, and a name written a hundred years ago in a book that had chosen her, of all the world's hands, to carry something wild into this kingdom of inherited fire.

"Two children," she said. "Six and nine."

"Six and nine," Tam agreed.

"My hands are scribe's hands," Oscar said. "What exactly would I have to do?"

Tam opened one eye. And the crooked smile that spread beneath it was, she would think later, the exact moment her old life finished dying—not at the border-flare, not at the marsh, but here, in a hayloft, over a wooden bird and a plan that was going to go wrong in every way that mattered.

"Well, cousin," he said, "can you drive a wagon?"
