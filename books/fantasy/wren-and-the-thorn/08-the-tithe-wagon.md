## 8. The Tithe Wagon

The smuggler's camp smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, and—unexpectedly—onions.

Oscar had been with Tam's crew for four days now, long enough to learn that the onions were Nonna's doing, and that complaining about them was a form of prayer. She sat on an upturned crate near the fire, a bowl of onion stew in her hands, watching Tam draw lines in the dirt with a stick.

"So," he said, "the tithe wagon."

The crew leaned in. There were seven of them besides Tam and Oscar: Nonna, who cooked and threw knives with equal indifference; the brothers Wick and Sorrel, twins who finished each other's sentences and occasionally each other's thefts; a broad, silent woman called Hesk; a boy named Pip who was maybe sixteen and lied about being nineteen; and two others whose names Oscar was still learning.

"House Corvane runs it every season," Tam went on, scratching the map into the mud. "Goes through the river villages, collects the tithe children. The ones born magic-touched. They take them to Corvane Hall for 'assessment.'" He made the word sound like something you'd scrape off your boot. "Assessment is a polite word for bleeding."

Oscar's stomach turned. In the valley, she'd read ledgers. Dry things. Columns of grain and candlewax and debts owed to the temple. She had never once imagined that somewhere, children were entered into columns like wheat.

"How many children?" she asked.

"Eleven this year, by our count." Tam glanced at her, and his voice gentled in a way that told her he'd noticed her hands shaking around the bowl. "We've done this before. We're good at it. Nobody dies if we're careful."

"Nobody dies," Pip repeated, like a charm.

"Right." Tam straightened up and clapped the dirt from his palms. "Wagon leaves Millbrook at dawn tomorrow, takes the ford road. One driver, two guards, plus whatever escort Corvane sends when they get close to Hall lands—which they won't have yet. That's our window. We hit them at Widow's Bend, where the road narrows and the trees come down to the water."

"And me?" Oscar asked.

Tam looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. "You're our scribe. You'll ride with the wagon after we've taken it. If any of those kids are carrying marks—family sigils, ownership brands, anything written into their skin—I want it read and recorded before we scatter them to safe houses. Corvane tracks their property by ink. We need to know what ink we're stealing."

*Property,* she thought. *They write their names on children.*

"All right," she said. "I can do that."

"You can also drive a wagon?"

"No."

"Can you throw a knife?"

"No."

"Can you do anything useful in a fight?"

"I once won an argument with a bishop."

Nonna snorted into her stew. "Keep her," she said.

Tam grinned—that crooked, lopsided grin that Oscar had decided, over four days of careful observation, was either genuine warmth or excellent camouflage, and which she suspected was both. "Fine. You ride shotgun on the bench next to Hesk, and if anyone comes at the wagon, you do what Hesk tells you. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Hesk, who had not previously spoken in Oscar's presence, and whose voice was so deep that Oscar felt it in the crate beneath her.

She should have been afraid. Some part of her noted, distantly, that a temple scribe from Dunlow—a valley without so much as a hedge-charm to its name—had no business sitting in a smugglers' camp planning armed robbery against one of the great houses of the realm. But the mark on her forearm hummed faintly under her sleeve, the way it did near strong magic, and eleven children were going to be bled like livestock tomorrow, and fear seemed like a luxury someone else could afford.

Besides, Tam had given her a spoon and a seat by his fire, and in Oscar's experience that was most of what trust amounted to.

* * *

Widow's Bend earned its name honestly. The road ran along the river there between black pines, narrow enough that two carts could barely pass, and the locals had hung little offerings in the branches—ribbons, bird skulls, a child's shoe—against the drowning.

Oscar crouched in the ferns above the road with Tam, waiting for dawn, and discovered that her body had opinions about fear that her mind hadn't authorized. Her knees shook. Her mouth tasted of copper.

"First time?" Tam asked quietly.

"Is it obvious?"

"You've checked your sleeves four times in ten minutes."

"That's—the mark. It does that."

"Mm." He didn't push. He was good at not pushing; it might have been his defining talent. After a while he said, "In the valley, did you ever see magic? Real magic, not stories?"

"No. Nothing grows there. Nothing burns wrong, nothing heals fast, nothing whispers. My whole life I thought the stories were exaggerations." She looked down at her arm, hidden under linen. "Then I crossed the border and a moth flew past me and it left a trail of light. A *moth*, Tam. I sat down in the road."

He laughed, soft, careful of the silence. "What was it like? Coming out. Everyone asks me that and I never have an answer."

"It was like—" She thought about it. "You know how a room smells when you've been in it too long, and then you step outside and realize the smell was there the whole time? It was like that. Except the room was my whole world, and the outside was *everything*."

Down in the bend, the river muttered over its stones. Somewhere east, faint as memory, the sun began to consider rising.

"When we take those kids," Tam said, "they're going to cry, and some of them are going to fight us, because they don't know we're the rescue. They think everyone who grabs them is the tithe." His jaw worked. "So you stay calm, and you talk to them like you'd talk to a frightened cat, and you remember that being feared is worse than being hurt. Can you remember that?"

"Yes," Oscar said. And because she was a scribe, because precision mattered to her even now, especially now: "I'm scared out of my wits, but yes."

"Good." He squeezed her shoulder once, briefly, through two layers of borrowed coat. "Scared means honest."

The wagon came at first light.

* * *

It went wrong in the middle, the way plans do.

The taking of the guards went almost beautifully—Wick and Sorrel dropped from the trees like ripe fruit onto the escort's rear riders, Hesk simply walked out of the mist in front of the lead guard, who took one look at her and appeared to reconsider his entire career—and for perhaps ninety seconds Oscar allowed herself to believe it would be easy.

Then the driver whipped the horses.

The wagon lurched forward, toward the bend, toward the drop where the road crumbled above the river, and Tam shouted, and Oscar—who was supposed to be hiding in the ferns until called—did the single stupidest thing of her life, which was saying a great deal, considering the rest of it.

She ran onto the road.

"Hesk!" she screamed, absurdly, uselessly, and then, because the driver wasn't stopping and the horses were panicking and the wagon was swaying toward the broken edge, she did the only thing her body knew how to do. She planted herself in front of the lead horse the way she'd seen farmers stop runaway oxen in Dunlow—arms wide, voice low, standing in the animal's eye-line rather than its path—

"*Whoa.* Whoa. Easy. Easy now."

And the mark on her forearm woke up.

She felt it like a struck bell. Something poured out of her—not thought, not intention, just a warm flood of *stillness*—and the horses stopped. Not gradually. Instantly, all six legs locking, steam rising off their backs, ears pricked toward her as though she'd spoken a word they'd been waiting their whole lives to hear.

The wagon rocked to a halt three feet from her chest.

Silence. River-sound. Her own heartbeat, enormous.

"*Oscar.*" Tam's voice, hoarse. "Oscar, get away from—what did you *do*?"

"I don't know," she said honestly. Her arm burned under the sleeve. "I don't know, it just—it heard me, or I heard it, I—"

"The kids," Hesk rumbled, already climbing onto the wagon bed. "Look at the kids."

Oscar turned.

Eleven faces looked back at her from between the slats of the covered bed, and every one of them was glowing. Faintly, unevenly, like lamps behind shutters—the magic-touched, the reason for the tithe, children of six and nine and twelve years old with light leaking out of their skin because nobody had ever taught them to hold it in. One little girl, maybe seven, with straw-colored hair, was holding the hand of a smaller boy and whispering to him, and where she touched him the light steadied.

Oscar's mark answered them, humming under her sleeve like a tuning fork set among bells.

"Well," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and Pip—arriving breathless at her elbow—finished for her:

"Well *indeed*."

* * *

The half of it that succeeded, succeeded wonderfully.

They got the wagon off the road and into the trees. They got the guards trussed and stashed in the root cellar of an abandoned croft, alive, because Tam's rules were Tam's rules. They cut eleven children loose from a wagon bed that stank of hay and old blood—old blood, dried into the boards, and Oscar understood then exactly what "assessment" meant, and had to stand with her hand pressed flat against a tree trunk for a full minute, breathing, while Hesk pretended not to notice.

By noon they were moving in three groups along three different paths, the children split up, bound for three different safe houses. The little girl with the straw hair—Wren, she was called, Wren with a ferocious scowl and light in her skin like a lamp behind paper—had been assigned to Oscar's group, ostensibly because Oscar was the least frightening adult present, and possibly because Wren had decided so herself and informed everyone, including the horses.

"You glowed," Wren accused her, marching beside her on the forest track. "On the road. You went all gold and the horses listened to you."

"I don't know what that was."

"Yes you do."

"I genuinely—"

"You *do*. I can tell when people know things. I'm magic." She said it with the tremendous authority of a seven-year-old who has recently been told she is going to be harvested for it, and who has elected to treat the diagnosis as a promotion.

Oscar looked down at her and felt something crack open in her chest, warm and terrible. "All right," she said. "Maybe I do. When we stop, I'll show you. But it's a secret, all right? Even from the other kids."

"I'm extremely good at secrets," said Wren, at a volume audible in the next county.

The trouble came at the ford.

* * *

Later, Oscar would go over it a hundred times. Later, in the small hours, in whatever barn or ditch she slept in, she would replay every decision and find the place where it could have gone differently, and there was never a place, and that was the worst of it.

One of the guards had gotten loose. Not the big one, not the one Hesk had stared into submission—one of the rear riders, a wiry man with a crossbow, whom Sorrel had tied badly in the dark and the dark had forgiven. He'd tracked them by the noise of the children, because children cannot be quiet, because they are children, and he came out of the alders at the ford just as the last group was crossing.

Oscar saw him first. She saw him raise the crossbow. She saw—this was the part that lived behind her eyes forever after—that he wasn't aiming at her, or at Tam, or at any of the adults. He was aiming at the wagon bed, at the huddle of glowing children, at the largest target, because his orders were about the cargo and a dead tithe-child was still worth more than an escaped one, and some cold clerk in House Corvane had probably explained that to him with a ledger line.

She shouted. She threw up her arm.

The mark flared—and *missed*. Whatever it had done to the horses, it did not do here; whether it needed touch or time or a calmer heart than hers, she never learned, because there wasn't time. The bolt went wide anyway, high, thudding into a pine—but wide was not harmless, and the little boy beside Wren, the small one she'd been holding hands with on the wagon, jerked sideways out of instinct, out of panic, off the crossing-stones—

Into the water.

The ford was knee-deep where the adults crossed. But the channel curved, and rain had come in the night, and the boy went in where the current ran, and he was six years old, and he was gone under before anyone reached the bank.

Tam reached him first. Tam dove in boots and all and came up with him and dragged him onto the shingle and pressed his chest and breathed into his mouth and did everything right, everything, everything right, and it did not matter. The river had him. The river kept him.

His name was Colm. Oscar learned it afterward, from Wren, who sat on a stone with her knees drawn up and told her in a flat, careful voice, the way children do when they have decided that crying is for later: "His name was Colm. He was my cousin. He could make flowers open early. That's all he could do. Just flowers."

Oscar knelt in the shallows afterward and washed the mud from her hands, mechanically, thoroughly, the way she cleaned her pens at the end of a day's copying, and realized what she was doing and could not stop doing it. Her scribe's hands. Hands that had copied psalms and debt-rolls and the birthdays of minor clerks. They had come out here clean, more or less. They were not clean now.

"We couldn't have known," Tam said behind her. His voice was wrecked. He was wringing out his coat and looking at nothing. "Sorrel's knot was bad, but I've run a dozen of these and—Oscar. Look at me. We could not have known."

"I know." She kept washing. The water was freezing. She deserved the freezing. "I raised my arm and I thought—if I just *reach*, the way I did with the horses—"

"You can't fight bolts with wishes."

"I know that too." She stood. Her hands dripped. Somewhere upstream a bird sang, indifferent and lovely. "But knowing it doesn't put him back on the stones, does it? Knowing things has never once fixed anything. I spent six years in an archive learning things, and the valley starved, and my father broke himself paying interest, and a boy who could make flowers open early is dead because a house wanted what's in his blood." Her voice rose and cracked and she let it. "Knowledge is just the *price list*, Tam. Somebody still has to pay."

He came and stood beside her in the shallows. He didn't touch her. He looked at the water running past their ankles, brown and quick and carrying nothing back.

"Then we pay," he said. "That's the whole job. That's all it's ever been."

Behind them, on the bank, Wren took Oscar's abandoned cloak—Oscar saw her do it, saw the small deliberate hands gather it up—and folded it around the empty space where Colm's bundle used to sit on the cart, and patted it once, and did not cry until much later, and then only into Oscar's shoulder, in the dark, where she had asked permission first.

* * *

They buried Colm at dusk above the ford, under a rowan, because Maren had once told Oscar that rowans keep watch, and Oscar wanted someone keeping watch.

Tam spoke a few words. Hesk laid a river-smooth stone on the grave. Pip cried openly and dared anyone to comment. And when it was done, Oscar knelt and did the only thing left in her power to do: she took out her writing kit, and on a strip of good vellum, by the last of the light, she wrote:

*Colm. Six years old. Cousin of Wren. Could make flowers open early. Stolen by House Corvane. Lost at the ford on the [date].*

Not a prophecy. Not a ledger entry. A record. Testimony, in a scribe's fair hand, that he had existed and that it had been wrong.

Tam read it over her shoulder. For a long moment he said nothing. Then: "Corvane keeps records too, you know. Their own version. In their version, this is a shipping loss."

"Then they can keep theirs," Oscar said, "and I'll keep mine. And someday somebody's going to lay the two side by side, and I intend for mine to be true."

She rolled the vellum and tucked it inside her coat, against the mark, against the thing that had woken in her on a road and saved six horses and failed one child, and she was still trying to understand what that failure meant when Hesk came up the slope at a pace that meant bad news.

"Rider coming," she said. "Fast. Flying Corvane colors—and he's not riding for the ford." She pointed east, toward the hills, toward the road Oscar and Tam's group would take at first light. "He's riding for *you*. Someone's told them what you are."

The mark burned, once, like a knock at a door.

And far off across the darkening fields, in the direction of Corvane Hall, every window of every watchtower lit at once—not with lamplight, but with the same gold glow that had leaked from a seven-year-old's skin, dozens of lights burning in perfect unison, like a message.

Like a summons.
