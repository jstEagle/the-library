## 9. Corvane's Offer

They did not bind her hands.

Oscar noticed that first, before the velvet of the carriage seat, before the smell of cedar and lamp oil and something richer underneath—money, she supposed, made into a scent. Her wrists were free in her lap, ink-stained and rope-burned from the marsh road, and no guard sat across from her, and the door was not even locked. The message was as clear as any ledger line: *You could run. It would be pointless.*

The carriage rolled through hills she had never seen, because she had never been permitted to see anything. Outside the window the world was green and gold and stitched with light—actual light, thin ribbons of it rising off the fields like heat-shimmer in winter, like breath off a pond at dawn. Magic, loose in the soil here, unhidden. In Dunlow nothing glowed but the temple candles, and those only because someone had lit them.

Her forearm ached under her sleeve. The words there had been quiet since Tam's crew scattered into the treeline, quiet since the moment rough hands pulled her from the wagon and a voice above her said, almost gently, "Careful with that one. She's carrying."

She had asked what he meant. No one answered. That, too, was a kind of message.

* * *

House Corvane did not look like a house. It looked like a decision the landscape had been forced to make—a keep grown out of the hillside in pale stone, its towers crowned with lamps that burned without flame. As the carriage climbed the switchback road, Oscar counted eleven of these cold lights and then stopped counting, because counting was a scribe's reflex and this place was designed to make you lose count. To make you feel small and arithmetic about it.

She was led through halls where servants moved without speaking, past tapestries showing battles she'd only read about in censored summaries—the War of the Sundering, the temple called it, though the tapestries showed very little sundering and a great deal of winning. In the last one, a field of common soldiers lay broken beneath a sky split by light, and above them stood seven figures robed in colors she recognized from her heraldry lessons. The founders of the great houses. Their faces were painted so they caught the lamplight wherever you stood, so their eyes followed you down the corridor.

One of them had green eyes and a jaw she knew. She'd seen that jaw an hour ago, attached to a living man, telling his guards to be careful with her.

"Lord Corvane will receive you in the record room," said the servant, and opened a door.

* * *

It was a library.

That was the first betrayal, because some traitorous part of her heart leapt at it—the shelves rising two stories, the ladders on brass rails, the long reading table scarred by centuries of pens. It looked like home. It looked like the archive in Dunlow, if the archive had been loved rather than merely guarded, if someone had dusted it, if the books were allowed to breathe instead of being chained shut behind the restricted seal.

A man stood at the far end of the table with his back to her, pouring wine into two glasses. He didn't turn when she came in. He let her stand there, let her look, let the silence do its work. Scribes were taught to fear silence; silence was where errors hid.

"You're the archivist," Lord Corvane said at last. Not a question.

"Scribe," Oscar said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Archivists have rank. I copy things."

"Mm." He turned. Up close he was younger than his reputation, forty perhaps, with his father's painted jaw and tired eyes that had seen too much of whatever powerful men see before breakfast. He slid one glass of wine across the table toward her. "I imagine you copy things very well. You found a book in your valley's vault that four generations of my family's agents failed to locate. That is either brilliance or providence, and I've learned to bill for both."

"I wasn't looking for it. I was cataloguing."

"Yes. That's how it always finds people." He drank, watching her over the rim. "Sit, Scribe Halloran. Please. This will go easier sitting."

She sat. She did not touch the wine.

"The mark," he said. "Show me."

"No."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Interest, sharpened to a point. "You refused a lord in his own hall. Good. That means it hasn't started eating you yet. When it has, you'll agree to anything just to make the burning stop, and I'll get a bearer who's already half-rotted, which is worthless to me. So—" he set down the glass, folded his hands— "we are going to talk first. And you are going to listen, because unlike the temple, I don't punish questions. I answer them. It's disarming, I'm told."

"My father's debt," Oscar said. Because it was the thing in her chest, the splinter, and better to name it than let him find it himself. "Your letter said you could erase it. That's a lie. Debts to the temple can't be bought. They're sacred obligations."

"They're paperwork." Corvane rose and walked along the shelves, trailing one finger over the spines. "Everything is paperwork, Scribe. That is the first lesson of power, and the temple teaches it to everyone except the people it papers against. Your father owes four hundred crowns and eleven years of labor for a loan signed under a law written by the temple, enforced by the temple, adjudicated by the temple. I hold charters older than the temple's right to lend. One signature from me and your father walks out of those mills tomorrow morning a free man. Not pardoned. *Free.* There's a difference. Pardon implies guilt."

"And what does it cost me?"

He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm around the edges, and that frightened her more than Vane's politeness ever had. "Now we're negotiating. Excellent."

* * *

What he brought her was not a contract. It was a box.

He set it on the reading table between them—old wood, iron corners, sealed with wax stamped by a crest she didn't recognize: a tree, split down the middle. "Do you know what this is?" he asked.

"A document case."

"It's the reason your valley exists." He broke the seal with his thumb like it was nothing, like it hadn't held for a hundred years, and began laying out pages on the scarred wood with the care of a man handling relics. Which, Oscar thought, he was. "You were taught, I assume, that Dunlow is a refuge. A blessed hollow where the war never reached. Where magic died naturally, like a fire burning out, and the faithful huddled in the ashes until it passed."

"That's what the catechism says."

"The catechism was written by the same seven families who won the war." He turned a page toward her. "Read."

She read.

It was a requisition ledger. That was the horror of it—that was the thing that made her stomach drop through the floor and keep dropping—it was formatted exactly like every ledger she had ever copied in eleven years of service. Columns. Dates. Signatures in the margin. *Received, from the commons of the Aldervale district, one hundred and forty bound volumes of hearth-magic, title vested in House Corvane per the Settlement Act.* Hearth-magic. Bound. Like grain. Like sheep.

Page after page. District after district. Names—actual names, farmer's names, miller's names, names with the same cadence as the names in Dunlow's parish rolls, because they *were* Dunlow's names, or their grandmothers'—

"What am I looking at?" she whispered, though she knew. She had known since the second page. She was a scribe; she could read a theft in any language, in any hand, however beautiful the script.

"The inventory," Corvane said quietly, "of everything taken. Every scrap of working magic in the borderlands, harvested from the common folk who'd held it for a thousand years—hearth-craft, weather-sense, mending-charms, midwife-knowledge, all of it, inherited in their blood like my family inherited ours—and bound into a single vessel. The Thorn. Then the vessel was buried in a null-field so deep that no one could ever draw it out again, and the empty land around it was declared holy, and the people whose magic had been poured into that hole were resettled inside it to be its living lock."

Oscar's mouth was dry. "The valley isn't a refuge."

"The valley is a *vault*, Scribe. Your whole life—the temple, the debt-laws, the mills, the catechism, the silence—you were born inside the lock mechanism. Your father's debt keeps him at the mills. The mills keep the town at the temple. The temple keeps everyone watching everyone else. Nobody leaves, nobody wonders, nobody digs." He tapped the ledger page, gently, almost tenderly. "My grandfather wrote the settlement act. He called it mercy. The alternative, after the war, was killing every magicless peasant who might someday remember what had been taken from them. Instead we gave them a valley and told them they were blessed."

"Why show me this?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why would you show me the crime?"

"Because I'm offering you justice." He leaned forward, both hands flat on the table, and for a moment the polished lord fell away and she saw something rawer underneath—a man who had clearly rehearsed this speech in front of mirrors and hated himself for it. "Not charity. Justice. Think, Halloran. You've read the ledgers. You know what was stolen, and from whom. Now ask yourself: who is positioned to give it back? Not the temple—they built the vault. Not the peasants—they have nothing, that's the point of them. Not the other houses—they'd start a war inside a year, and the last one burned half a kingdom. But House Corvane holds legal title to the Thorn's resting-place. Legal, documented, unbroken title. If the Thorn passes to me—lawfully, by sale, witnessed and sealed—then I can open the vault under color of law instead of conquest. I can restore magic to the commons *gradually*. Managed. With structure. Without burning the world down a second time."

"And your family keeps the deed."

"Our family administers the trust." He said it smoothly, and then—she had to grant him this—his eyes flicked away, just slightly, and came back. "Yes. We profit. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. Power doesn't launder itself, Scribe. Someone has to hold the dirty end while the cleaning gets done. My family has held dirty ends for six generations. We're practiced."

* * *

She should have said no immediately. She knew that. Some part of her, the part that had watched Tam argue with his smugglers about risking their necks for stranger's children, the part that had felt the child's death in the tithe-wagon raid sit in her chest like swallowed river-stone—that part was already composing refusals.

But another part, a part she hated, was doing arithmetic.

Four hundred crowns. Eleven years. Her father's hands, which had been a joiner's hands once, fine and quick, now cracked and chemical-burned from the mill vats. The way he hadn't met her eyes at Debtors' Row, the way he'd said *good girl* like a man apologizing. If she sold the Thorn—if she sold a seed that had been *stolen in the first place*, returned to the family that had organized the theft—her father would walk free. And the magic would go back to the people, slowly, safely, under law.

Under the law of the thieves.

"The offer," she said carefully, "is that I sell you something that isn't mine to sell."

"It was never anyone's to sell. That's the entire history of the world, Halloran." Corvane poured more wine he didn't drink. "Every inheritance is a theft with good posture. Mine included. I'm not offering you a clean transaction. There are no clean transactions left. I'm offering you the least filthy one available, and I'm the only buyer who can guarantee your father's freedom in writing, tonight, before witnesses."

"And if I refuse?"

His face did something complicated. Pity, maybe, or its expensive imitation. "Then I keep you here, comfortably, indefinitely, until the Thorn's hunger does what hunger does. I told you—I need an unrotted bearer, and rot takes months. We have time, you and I. Time is the one thing my family owns outright." He softened it with the good smile again. "But I don't think you'll refuse. I've read your file. Temple records are wonderfully thorough about debts and remarkably silent about people, but your instructors' marginal notes survived. *Halloran asks why.* Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else learns to stop asking why. You haven't. That's why the book chose you. And it's why, when you weigh your father's hands against a lord's promise, I think you'll find the scales tip where I want them to."

Oscar looked down at her sleeve. Beneath it, faintly, the transferred words had begun to warm—not burning, not yet, just warming, like a hand laid gently over hers. As if the Thorn were listening. As if it, too, wanted to know what she'd decide.

"You said witnessed and sealed," she said. "Tonight."

"If you agree."

"And my father hears it from me first. Not from a bailiff. From me. That's my price, above whatever's written."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he nodded slowly, and reached for fresh paper, and set it before her with a scribe's own reverence—an offer of a pen, ink, the tools of her trade turned to the trade of herself.

"Then let's write it plainly," he said. "You'll find I keep honest contracts. It's the honest ones that hold up when the world burns."

Oscar took the pen. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her; eleven years of copying had trained the shake out of it, apparently, even for this.

She had written three words—*Let it be*—when the lamp flames in the record room all bent sideways at once, toward the door, as if inhaling. Somewhere below them, deep in the hill, iron rang against iron, once, twice, and a voice she knew—rough, marsh-country, impossible—shouted her name.

Maren. Here. Inside Corvane's walls, where no hedge-witch should have drawn breath.

The pen snapped in Oscar's fingers.
