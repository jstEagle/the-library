## 10. The Mark Speaks

The cell was kind, as cells went.

That was the first thing Oscar noticed about her imprisonment in House Corvane: the kindness of it. The walls were pale stone, not the black basalt she'd imagined. There was a bed with a wool blanket, a washbasin that refilled itself—*magic*, her scribe's mind noted, cataloguing even now—a window too high to reach but wide enough to show a slice of Corvane's gardens, all clipped hedges and fountains that sang instead of splashed.

They had taken everything else. Her boots. The copy of the prophecy page she'd sewn into her hem, though they had missed it, which meant someone hadn't looked very hard, or someone wanted her to keep it. Even her father's little brass stylus, worn smooth from forty years of temple ledgers.

But not the mark.

She lay on the bed and rolled up her sleeve for the hundredth time. The words sat on her forearm in ink-black script, elegant as scripture:

*On this day the thorn wakes, and it wakes in Oscar Halloran.*

Three days since Lord Corvane had shown her the war records. Three days since he'd made his offer with that terrible gentleness—the debt erased, her father freed, the Thorn "legally acquired" through a contract no doubt already drafted by men who billed by the hour. She hadn't said yes. She hadn't said no. She'd said, *I need time,* and he'd smiled like a man who had already won and merely enjoyed the waiting, and they'd locked her in a room that was nicer than anywhere she'd ever slept in Dunlow.

That was the trap of it, she thought. Not the bars. The comfort. You could refuse a dungeon. It was much harder to refuse a soft bed and a view of singing water.

Sleep came anyway. Sleep always comes eventually, even to the guilty, even to the hunted. And when it came, it did not come gently.

* * *

She dreamed of the archive first.

Her own archive, the restricted stacks of the Dunlow temple, candlelight and dust motes and the smell of vellum gone soft with age. She walked the aisles knowing them the way you know your own hands. But the shelves went on longer than they should have. Aisle seven became aisle eight became aisle nine, and there were only supposed to be seven, and the candles burned without shortening, and somewhere far off she could hear water dripping in a rhythm almost like speech.

At the end of the ninth aisle stood a table. On the table lay an open book.

She knew before she touched it that it was not the prophecy book. This one was older. The cover was leather gone dark as dried blood, and the pages inside were not paper at all but something thin and veined, like leaves pressed flat, like skin.

"You're reading me wrong," said a voice behind her.

Oscar turned. The woman standing between the shelves was ordinary in every way that mattered and impossible in every way that didn't. She wore clothes from no century Oscar could name—a long coat embroidered with faded sigils, boots crusted with old mud, hair silver-streaked and cut practically, the way you'd cut it if you expected to run. Her age was a riddle: young eyes in a face that had been tired for a hundred years.

"Who are you?" Oscar asked.

"Elowen Vasska." The woman said the name the way you'd set down something heavy. "Last bearer of the Thorn. Before your Lord Corvane's grandfather. Before the valley." She tilted her head, studying Oscar with an expression that was half pity and half recognition, and both halves were unbearable. "You've got my chin. Isn't that strange? The seed likes certain shapes."

"I don't want it," Oscar said. "I never asked for it. I read a book. That's all I did. I *catalogued* it."

"Yes," Elowen said. "That's how it gets everyone worth having. Nobody worth anything goes looking for power. It has to find you mid-chore." She gestured at the book on the table. "Go on. You were going to anyway."

Oscar looked down. The veined pages turned themselves, and what was written there was not text but memory—images rising off the leaf-skin like heat off summer stone. She saw:

*A hall of nobles in Corvane colors, generations of them, and in each generation one figure slightly brighter than the rest, glowing faintly at the collarbone—and each bright figure, decade by decade, growing colder-eyed. She watched a Corvane matriarch sign orders with one hand while her other hand, the marked one, blackened slowly from fingertip to wrist. She watched the same woman weep over the signature and sign it anyway.*

*She saw a merchant house, nameless, that bought the Thorn's guardianship with gold, and within thirty years the family spoke to each other only through lawyers, and the last heir died alone in a house of mirrors because he could no longer stand the sight of what he'd become.*

*She saw—*

"The ones who built the valley," Oscar said quietly. Because she'd seen them now too, in the turning pages: six founders, three noble houses and three common-born mages, standing in a circle around a pit of darkness, pouring the commons' stolen magic into it. Sealing it. Hiding it. *Protecting the world from it,* the record insisted.

"They believed it," Elowen said. "That's the part people get wrong. They weren't monsters. They were good people doing an unforgivable thing for excellent reasons." She laughed, and it was a dry, awful sound, like pages tearing. "The Thorn doesn't corrupt you with evil, scribe. Evil is easy to spot. It corrupts you with *reasons*. Every house that held it rotted from within—not because they wanted bad things, but because holding it makes every compromise feel necessary. You start burning things to save things. And you are always, always saving something. There's always a village on fire somewhere that only you can put out."

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. In dreams, apparently, mouths did that. "And you? What did you burn?"

Elowen's face did something complicated. "A town called Merrow's Ford. Four hundred people. Plague, they said afterward. The records say plague." She met Oscar's eyes. "It wasn't plague. They were going to give me to House Corvane—I was the bearer, and bearers can be harvested, did they tell you that part? The Thorn lives in blood, and blood can be drawn. So I burned the ford so no one could follow me, and told myself it was survival, and survival felt like a reason." She held out her arms, and Oscar saw that beneath the sleeves the skin was scarred in branching patterns, black as ink, like a tree grown under the flesh. "I carried it forty years. Look what carrying does. Not using. *Carrying.*"

"That won't be me," Oscar said, and heard how young she sounded.

"No," Elowen agreed mildly. "Probably worse. You've got a conscience. Conscience just gives the rot more places to enter." She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were the exact brown of old ink, and Oscar understood suddenly that this was not a ghost haunting her dream but the opposite: the dream was Elowen's, and Oscar was the intruder in it, a guest in a memory kept alive on purpose. "I'm not here to frighten you. I'm here because you're going to make a choice soon—destroy it or plant it, those are the doors, there are only ever two—and I need you to walk through whichever door you choose *with your eyes open*. Do you know why the valley works? Why the null holds?"

"It was built to contain it. The stolen magic. The vault."

"A vault is just a box," Elowen said. "Boxes break. No—the valley holds because the founders gave it a heart, a mechanism, a living lock, and the lock runs on a simple fuel." She smiled, and there was grief in it, real grief, ancient and worn smooth. "*Debt.* Every soul born inside those borders owes a price for existing, paid in labor, in years, in silence. Your father's debt. Your debt. The whole valley is a machine that grinds obligation into containment. That's why the Thorn couldn't live there. Not because magic dies in Dunlow, child. Because nothing free can."

The floor of the archive shivered. Somewhere above, muffled through stone, came the sound of boots—many boots, moving with purpose.

"They're coming for you again," Elowen said, unsurprised. "Or for the network that's coming for you. Either way, wake up. But first—" she caught Oscar's wrist, and where her fingers touched, the mark flared white-hot, and the words on Oscar's forearm rearranged themselves, letters crawling like ants, reforming into a single new line beneath the prophecy:

*Ask the vault what it remembers about mercy.*

"What does that mean?" Oscar demanded. "What vault? What—"

"Wake up, scribe," Elowen said, and her voice was already fading, already becoming the drip of water, the rustle of pages. "And when the moment comes—when you're standing over it with your hands full of everything—remember Merrow's Ford. Remember that I was *sure*. Certainty is just rot wearing its good coat."

The boots grew louder. The candles guttered. The last thing Oscar saw was Elowen Vasska walking back into the ninth aisle, into the dark between the shelves, carrying her scars like a lantern that gave no light.

* * *

She woke gasping, sitting bolt upright, the mark burning cold instead of hot—which was somehow worse, a frostburn along the veins of her arm.

The new line was there. Of course it was there. She checked twice, three times, hoping the dream had been only a dream, but the ink-black script sat beneath the prophecy, neat as a ledger entry: *Ask the vault what it remembers about mercy.*

Outside, the slice of garden visible through the high window had gone grey with pre-dawn. The singing fountain had stopped. In fact—

Oscar went still, listening.

Everything had stopped. The fountains, the nightingales Corvane kept in the hedges, even the ever-present hum of wards she'd only half-noticed until now, the way you stop hearing a river you live beside. Silence, total and deliberate, the silence of a held breath.

Then, faint but unmistakable, from somewhere below and outside: a whistle. Two notes, low then high, repeated.

Tam's signal. The one he'd used in the marsh camps to call the children home.

He'd come back for her. After she'd been dragged out of the tithe wagon raid in chains, after he'd had every reason to cut his losses—he'd come back, which meant either the bravest rescue in the history of smuggling or the stupidest, and knowing Tam it would somehow be both.

Oscar pressed her hand flat against the cold mark and thought of Elowen's warning. *Certainty is just rot wearing its good coat.*

Well. She had never been certain of anything in her life except the Dewey decimal order of temple archives and the exact weight of her father's debt. Perhaps that would be enough.

Below her window, in the grey dark, two notes whistled again—low, then high—and this time, answering them from deeper in the house, came the ring of steel being drawn.
