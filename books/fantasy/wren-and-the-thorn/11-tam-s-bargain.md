## 11. Tam's Bargain

Tam had always believed there were three kinds of men in the world: those who sold things, those who bought things, and those who got sold. He'd spent thirty years making sure he stayed firmly in the first category.

Tonight he intended to sell the only thing he had left that was worth anything, and he hated himself for how easily his hands had stopped shaking.

He stood in Lord Corvane's audience chamber with wet boots and a smuggler's coat that had seen better decades, watching steam rise off a brazier while a man with too many rings poured wine he didn't offer to share. The chamber smelled of cedar oil and old money. Somewhere below them, in the cells under Corvane Hall, Oscar Halloran sat in the dark waiting for a blood-harvest she didn't understand and couldn't survive.

"You came alone," Corvane said. Not a question. A test.

"I came alone," Tam agreed. "Because what I'm selling doesn't have witnesses."

The lord's smile was thin and pleased. "And what would that be?"

"Every route." Tam kept his voice level, bored even, the way you did when haggling over something enormous. "Nine years of routes out of six cities. The safehouse names. The faces of every fence, every ferryman, every midwife who's ever passed an unlicensed child through my hands. Names, Lord Corvane. I'll give you the names."

Corvane set down his glass. The rings on his fingers clicked against it like small teeth.

"The children themselves are of no interest to me," he said. "Touched brats without bloodlines. They're worth nothing until they're grown, and half of them don't grow." He studied Tam with the flat patience of a man appraising livestock. "But networks. Networks are worth a great deal. House Corvane has spent two generations trying to map where the unlicensed magic of this kingdom flows, and here you are, offering me the whole river."

"I'm offering you a tributary," Tam said. "You want the whole river, you give me her."

"And why does a smuggler care about one scribe?"

Tam thought about the honest answer — that Oscar Halloran had looked at a wagon full of terrified children and started writing down their names, all of them, in beautiful temple script, because *someone should know who they were* — and discarded it. Honesty was a luxury. He sold other things.

"Because she's leverage," he said. "Whatever's in her blood, everyone from here to Ashfall wants it. You're not the only house moving on this. Sell me her freedom now, quietly, before your rivals know she's in your cellar, and you've traded nothing but paper for the thing everyone will kill each other over by spring."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly true. Corvane's eyes narrowed anyway, because lords could smell desperation the way dogs could smell fear, and Tam knew his own reeked.

"You love her," Corvane said, with mild contempt, as if naming a disease.

"I owe her," Tam said. "Which is worse. Love you can walk away from. Debt sits in you like a splinter."

For a long moment the only sound was the brazier popping. Then Corvane laughed — a short, genuine bark that made Tam flinch more than any threat would have.

"A splinter," the lord repeated, delighted. "Yes. All right, smuggler. The routes and names, sworn and sealed. And the woman walks out of my gate at dawn, debt-free, unmolested, with no mark of sale on her. If your information proves false or incomplete, I'll have her back within the week, and I'll harvest you both slowly enough that we become friends first."

"Charming," Tam said. "Do we shake on it, or is there a fancy piece of paper?"

"There is a very fancy piece of paper," Corvane said, ringing for his steward. "There always is. That's rather the point of people like us, isn't it? We make the paper, and people like you live inside it."

Tam signed his name four times. His hand didn't shake until the fourth signature, and then only a little, and nobody saw.

* * *

Oscar knew something was wrong the moment they cleared the gates, because Tam wouldn't look at her.

They walked the high road east through thin grey morning light, frost smoking off the ditches, and Tam talked the entire time — about the weather, about a horse he'd once owned that hated him, about the proper way to cook a marsh hen — a bright, relentless stream of nonsense that she recognized, after a mile, as the sound of a man drowning loudly.

"Tam." She stopped in the middle of the road. "What did you do?"

"Nothing. I haggled. It's what I do. You've seen me haggle."

"Yes, and when you lose a haggle you complain about it for a day and a half." She stepped closer. Her forearm ached under her sleeve, the ink-mark warm as a held coal, the way it always warmed when truth was nearby and being avoided. She'd learned that much about it: it burned when *she* lied, but it seemed to itch around other people's lies too, like a dog that smelled smoke before anyone else did. "What did you give him?"

"Nothing that matters."

The mark flared. Oscar hissed and gripped her wrist.

"Don't lie near me," she said. "I mean that literally, apparently. Something in me knows. So either tell me the truth or walk ten paces ahead and keep lying at a respectful distance."

Tam looked at her then — really looked — and whatever he saw in her face made him sigh the way men sighed when a bridge went out behind them.

"He wanted my network," he said. "Names, routes, safehouses. Nine years of work. Every family that ever trusted me with their children." He kicked a stone off the road with more force than the stone deserved. "I gave it to him. Sealed and sworn. In exchange for you walking out of that cellar."

The road was very quiet. Somewhere behind them, Corvane Hall's chimneys were waking up, sending up threads of smoke into a sky the color of dirty wool.

"All of them," Oscar said. Not a question.

"All of them." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "That's the joke of it, see. Nine years I told myself I was clean. Smuggler, yes, but the good kind — the kind that moves children instead of cargo, the kind whose crimes all have happy endings. And it turns out the ledger balances just fine either way. One scribe for forty families. Corvane did the arithmetic right in front of me and I couldn't argue with the sum."

"You could have argued with the sum," Oscar said. Her voice came out strange and cold, and she realized it was the same voice she used for cataloguing errors in the archive. "You could have let him keep me."

"Oh, certainly. And then he'd have opened your veins in a ritual basement and I'd have gone home rich in self-respect and you'd have gone nowhere at all." He spread his hands. "I'm not going to apologize for choosing you, Oscar. But I'm not going to pretend it was clean, either. That's the thing nobody tells you about desperate measures. They don't stay desperate. They settle in. They get comfortable. They start redecorating."

She wanted to hit him. She wanted, absurdly, to hug him. She did neither; she stood in the road with the wind pulling at her hair and felt the Thorn turn over inside her like something waking in a warm bed.

"The midwife in Lowmarsh," she said. "Sera. With the bad hip and the honey cakes. You're giving her to Corvane."

"Sera can run. Sera's been running from one house or another since before I met her. That's the other thing I keep telling myself." He rubbed his face. "Doesn't work. I know it doesn't work. Honey cakes don't outrun writs."

"So we go faster than the writs." It came out of her before she'd decided anything, and once it was out she found she meant it completely. "We warn them. All of them. Every name you sold, we reach first. You spent nine years building that network, Tam — you know its rhythms better than Corvane ever will, even with the paper in his hand. We spend it. Spend it all. Empty every safehouse, scatter every family, and by the time his men knock on doors they'll be knocking on empty rooms."

Tam stared at her. "That's — do you know how far that road runs? Six cities. Months."

"We've got months." She touched her sleeve, over the burning mark. "I've got a prophecy growing into my arm and every noble house in the kingdom sniffing after me. I wasn't planning on a quiet season anyway."

Something moved across Tam's face — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might eventually be planted. "You're furious with me."

"Furious," she agreed.

"But you're still —"

"I'm still here, aren't I?" She started walking east again, toward the low hills and the long road. "Furious and here. Learn to tell the difference. It's an important distinction and I suspect you'll need it."

He caught up in four strides, because his legs were longer, and after another quarter-mile he said, quietly, "The honey cakes were almond. Sera's were almond. Yours were the plain ones, because you said you liked plain, but I saw you looking at the almond ones."

"I did like the almond ones," Oscar said, and after a moment, despite everything, she smiled. "*This* doesn't fix anything, you know."

"No," Tam said. "But it's a start. Everything I've ever ruined started smaller than this."

* * *

They made twelve miles before dark and slept in the lee of a fallen boundary stone so old its inscription had worn to a suggestion. Tam built a fire small enough to cup in your hands and cooked marsh hen badly, on purpose, because he claimed perfectly cooked food attracted bandits, which Oscar was fairly sure was a lie invented to excuse his cooking.

She was nearly asleep when she heard him talking. Low, rhythmic, half-whispered — a list.

"—Lowmarsh, Sera, the crooked chimney by the tannery. Ferren's Cross, the Odell brothers, they'll be on the barley roads this time of year. Cael Harbor, Mother Brannock, tell her it's the tide-table code, she'll know—"

"What are you doing?" she murmured.

"Learning it," Tam said. "The list. All of it. If I'm going to spend nine years of work saving it, I ought to carry it somewhere safer than paper." He fed a twig to the fire. "Paper burns. Heads are harder."

"That's very nearly wise."

"I have my moments. Usually about twice a year. This is the second one — you're lucky."

Oscar closed her eyes. The Thorn pulsed gently under her skin, and for once it didn't feel like a warning. It felt almost like a heartbeat keeping time with someone else's.

"Tam," she said, into the dark. "Ashfall. When we get there — whatever the Thorn turns out to be, whatever it wants — promise me you won't trade anything else for me. No matter how bad it gets."

The fire crackled. For a long time he didn't answer, and she thought he might have fallen asleep, which would have been a mercy for both of them.

"I promise," he said finally, "to try. It's the best I've got. Clean promises are for people who haven't already spent theirs."

"That," Oscar said, pulling her coat tighter, "is the most honest thing you've ever said to me."

"Aye. Twice in one day. Don't get used to it."

Far to the west, behind them, a beacon kindled on Corvane Hall's highest tower — a thin red thread of fire against the night — and then, one by one, like stars answering a signal, beacons lit along the whole spine of the hills between them and Ashfall.

Tam sat up slowly. "That's not a pursuit signal," he said. "Pursuits use white. Red means—" He stopped, and she watched him do the arithmetic, and watched the answer arrive in his face. "Red means the houses are calling each other to war. They're not hunting you anymore, Oscar. They're mustering. Every house that's heard the name Thorn is marching for Ashfall, and we're standing on the road to it."

Oscar looked east, where the dark hills hid the ruin of the old capital, and felt the seed in her arm lean toward it like a plant toward sun.

"Then we'd better walk fast," she said, "and sleep even faster."
