## 12. The Road to Ashfall

The road out of the lowlands was paved with the dead.

Oscar understood that within an hour of crossing the river, though it took her longer to understand what she was seeing. The paving stones were old—older than the temple, older than Dunlow itself, older than anything she'd ever catalogued—and they had been laid in a pattern she recognized from her scribe training: interlocking spirals, the kind of work that took a guild of masons a lifetime. Except no masons had laid these. She could tell because between the stones, in the seams where mortar should have been, there were bones.

Small ones, mostly. Finger bones and toe bones, worn smooth by two hundred years of cart wheels and boots.

"Stop staring at your feet," Tam said, not unkindly, from ahead of her on the track. "You'll trip."

"They're *in the road*, Tam."

"I know."

"You've been walking on them like they're nothing."

"They're not nothing." He adjusted the strap of his pack without breaking stride. He walked the way he did everything, Oscar was learning—economically, like a man who'd learned early that wasted motion was wasted bread. "They're the reason we're going where we're going. But staring at them won't change them, and tripping over them will only add yours to the collection."

She looked up sharply to see if he was joking. With Tam you never could tell; his humor lived somewhere below the surface of his face, surfacing rarely and then only at the corners of his mouth. But he wasn't joking this time. His eyes were fixed on the road ahead, and his jaw had the set of a man chewing on something old and hard.

"The war," she said. "This is from the war."

"This is from *after* the war. This is what victory looks like when the winners are still alive enough to enjoy it." He nodded toward a hillside off the eastern edge of the road, where a farmhouse stood with its roof caved in and its walls blackened. "The Corvanes built their fortune on this stretch. First harvest after the valley was sealed, they came through the borderlands collecting. Magic-touched commoners who hadn't fled fast enough. They didn't waste them." Another nod, toward the road. "Roads need paving. Blood holds a binding better than lime."

Oscar made herself keep walking. It was that or sit down in the middle of two centuries of other people's worst day, and she refused to give the road the satisfaction.

The Thorn stirred under her skin as if it agreed with her refusal. It had been quiet since the marshes—quieter than she liked, actually, because quiet felt like a held breath—but now it warmed along her forearm where the words lived, a slow pulse like a second heartbeat learning its rhythm. She'd stopped trying to hide it from Tam days ago. There was little point. He'd watched her sleep through the first night out of Maren's hut with one hand hovering near his knife, and when she'd woken and asked him why, he'd said, "Because I wanted to see if it moved when you dreamed." And then, when she'd gone pale: "It does. Just so you know."

That was Tam. He told you the frightening thing flatly, like reporting weather, and somehow that made it bearable.

* * *

They made camp that first night in the shell of a waystation—a coaching inn once, by the look of it, though the stable doors had been burned and the well was choked with rubble. Someone had camped here before them; there were old fire-scorches on the hearthstone, layered like tree rings.

"How many travelers come through here?" Oscar asked, unwrapping the hard travel bread Tam doled out. It tasted the way bark sounds.

"Smugglers, mostly. Pilgrims going to gawk at Ashfall. The occasional scholar with more curiosity than sense." He glanced at her. "And fugitives. It's a good spot. One road in, visible for a mile, and the innkeeper's ghost doesn't ask questions."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be. Well." The corner of his mouth twitched. "A little bit meant to be."

She laughed despite herself—a short, surprised sound, and then a longer one when she realized how long it had been. Since before the archive. Since before Vane's polite voice and the burning mark and the word *thorn*. Her body seemed startled by its own noise, and the Thorn pulsed warmly, almost pleased, which was a thought she firmly declined to examine.

Tam watched her laugh with an expression she couldn't read, and then looked away too quickly, busying himself with the fire.

They ate in silence for a while. Then Oscar said, "Tell me about Ashfall. Really tell me. Not the smuggler's version—the true one. You know I'll find it in the archives eventually anyway."

"There won't be archives. That's rather the point of Ashfall." He fed a stick to the fire. "Before the war, it was the capital. Not of a kingdom—a *commonwealth*. Every house had a seat there, high and low alike. The commons' guilds had halls on the same streets as the noble houses. Magic wasn't inherited then, the way it is now. It was... I don't know the word. Learned? Shared? My grandmother called it 'a craft,' the way you'd call carpentry a craft. Some people had a gift for it. Most could learn enough to light a lamp, mend a fence, keep the fever out of a well."

"And then?"

"And then the houses decided that if magic could be learned by anyone, it would be owned by no one, and they couldn't abide that. So they fought a war about it, and when they'd half-killed each other, they did something cleverer than fighting." His voice went flat. "They cut the source. There was a tree—don't ask me what kind, the records burn conveniently well—at the center of everything. All magic came up through it, like water through a taproot. The victors felled it at Ashfall and sealed what remained of its heart in the valley. Your valley. Dunlow isn't a refuge, Oscar. It's a *vault*. They built a null zone around the stolen goods and filled it with debtors and dissenters so no one would ever think to look."

"I know," she said quietly. "Maren told me."

"I know she told you. I'm telling you because you flinch every time I say the word *Corvane*, and I want you to understand that flinching is exactly what they're owed." He poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Ashfall's been a ruin since the felling. The tree's stump is still there. Big as a cathedral. The nobles won't build on it—they're superstitious about the one atrocity they bother to remember. So it sits there, empty, in the middle of the kingdom, waiting."

"For what?"

He looked at her across the fire, firelight catching the scar through his eyebrow—the one she'd never asked about, the one she suspected had a story with children in it, given his trade.

"For whatever walks into it carrying a piece of the heart," he said.

* * *

Three days out, the land began to change, and Oscar began to understand why the smugglers' route bent the way it did.

They crossed a district where the fields grew in perfect geometric rows—not planted that way, but *grown* that way, the wheat standing in flawless lines as if combed by a giant's fingers. Tithe-land, Tam called it. Land worked by bond-farmers whose yearly payment to their House was drawn not in coin but in vitality. The crops flourished unnaturally because they were fed unnaturally. Oscar saw the farmers themselves only once, at a distance: thin figures moving through the gold with the slow, deliberate care of people conserving every ounce of themselves.

One of them straightened as their party passed on the far side of a hedgerow, and even across forty yards, Oscar saw the moment the man registered what she was—or rather, that she was *wrong*, that something about her rang against the drained quiet of the place like a struck bell. The Thorn flared hot beneath her sleeve. The farmer's mouth opened.

"Don't," Tam murmured. "Keep walking. If he calls it in, the tithe-riders come, and we can't outrun riders."

"But he might need—"

"He might. And you can't help him by being harvested in his field. Walk."

She walked. It was one of the hardest things she had ever done, harder than lying to Vane, harder than fleeing the marshes with the border flaring behind her, because this was not terror driving her forward but simple arithmetic: one farmer's hope weighed against every child Tam's network might yet pull clear. Justice, she was learning, was less often a sword than a series of small, sickening calculations.

That night she couldn't sleep. She sat up in the lee of a fallen boundary wall, arms around her knees, and found Tam already awake, keeping the middle watch as always.

"Say something distracting," she said.

He considered. "I once smuggled a pig."

"...What?"

"A pig. Across three counties. A magic-touched pig—well, I say magic-touched, but really it was just an enormous pig with opinions. Belonged to a widow whose landlord claimed all livestock born on his land, per the inheritance laws, and this pig had been born on his land and therefore legally belonged to House Ferren regardless of the fact that it had been raised on the widow's milk and slept in her kitchen." He shook his head at the memory. "Worst cargo of my career. Bit me twice. Screamed like a titled lady at a wedding when we crossed the ford. But we got her to the freeholds in the north, and last I heard she'd had piglets, and every single one of those piglets was born on free soil."

Oscar laughed until her ribs hurt, quietly, helplessly, tears leaking out the sides of her eyes, and Tam sat there looking deeply satisfied with himself, and when she finally caught her breath she realized she had leaned against his shoulder somewhere in the middle of it and neither of them had moved away.

The Thorn pulsed gently, like a cat settling. She chose, again, not to examine that.

"Do you ever think," she said, after a while, "that we're going to fail? That we're carrying the most dangerous thing in the world across a country full of people who want it, on a road paved with bones, and that the odds are—" she gestured vaguely at the ruined landscape, the tithe-fields, the whole architecture of a century of theft, "*that*?"

"Every day," Tam said. "Several times daily. Usually while eating."

"Doesn't that frighten you?"

"Of course it frightens me." He said it simply, without bravado, and somehow that was braver than any boast. "But fear's just information. It tells you what matters. And what matters is that somewhere behind us there's a wagon full of children headed for a blood-harvest, and somewhere ahead of us there's a vault full of stolen magic, and you're the only person alive who can decide what happens to both. So we walk." A pause. "Also I refuse to be killed by House Corvane specifically. It would be undignified. They wear *velvet*."

* * *

On the sixth day they reached the ash line.

Oscar felt it before she saw it—a wrongness in the air, a hush like the inside of the temple archive, like the null of Dunlow, but thinner, older, worn. The green country simply ended. Ahead of them the earth turned grey and fine, drifts of it banking against broken walls, and nothing grew. Nothing had grown here, she understood, in two hundred years. The felling of the world-tree had salted the capital of the commons with its own ruin.

They walked into it as evening came on, and the silence pressed close. Their footsteps left prints in ash that would still be there, Tam said, in a hundred years. The wind didn't move it. It didn't move anything here.

Oscar had prepared herself for grief. What she hadn't prepared for was recognition.

Because the ruins weren't ruins, exactly. Streets remained. Doorways remained. Fountains stood dry in little squares where, you could still see, market stalls had once clustered, where someone had carved a hopscotch grid into a flagstone, where a baker's shopfront bore a faded sign shaped like a pretzel. People had lived here. Ordinary people, with ordinary days—children playing games, bakers rising early, neighbors arguing about fences. And then one day the most powerful families in the world had needed their future secured, and had cut down the tree that fed everyone equally, and this was what equality's funeral looked like.

Her father was a debtor in a mill inside a vault, and he thought his poverty was his own failure. Her whole life she had thought Dunlow's stillness was peace. It had been a lid.

The Thorn woke fully then—no gentle pulse now but a rolling heat up her arm, across her shoulder, into her chest, and the words on her forearm glowed faintly through her sleeve, and Tam grabbed her elbow.

"Easy," he said. "Easy. It knows where it is."

"*It* knows where *it* is?"

"It grew here, didn't it?" His voice was careful. "Or the part of it that's left. We're walking into its memory, Oscar. Try not to take it personally."

Ahead, rising out of the ash-haze, she saw it: the stump. Vast beyond sense, wider than the temple in Dunlow, its severed top weathered into a plateau, its roots arcing out of the ground like the ribs of some buried leviathan. Someone had built stairs up one root, centuries ago, for pilgrims or scholars or the merely morbid. At its base, half-swallowed by grey drifts, stood the broken archway of a great hall, its lintel stone carved with words worn nearly smooth.

Oscar climbed the steps to read them, because she was a scribe, and scribes read. She brushed the ash away with her fingertips, and the Thorn hummed against her palm like a tuning fork finding its note.

*HERE THE COMMON TREE OF THE WORLD WAS CUT BY THE HANDS OF THE FEW FOR THE PROFIT OF THE FEW. LET NO ONE SAY THEY DID NOT KNOW.*

Below, Tam had gone still, his head cocked, listening for something she couldn't hear.

Then she heard it too. Hooves. Many of them, back along the road they'd traveled, moving fast, and beneath the drum of them another sound—fainter, wronger, like a bell ringing underwater. Temple bells. Or something that wore their voice.

"Riders," Tam said quietly. "More than riders. And that second sound—that's a null-cage being carried. They brought equipment." He looked up at her on the root-stair, ash swirling faintly around the stump's vast shoulders, and his face in the dying light was grim but steady. "They knew we'd come here. Which means either Corvane is smarter than I gave him credit for—"

"—or someone told him," Oscar finished.

The hoofbeats grew louder. Somewhere in the dark beneath the stump, in the hollow roots where the world-tree's heart had been torn out, something answered the Thorn's humming—and began, very softly, to glow.
