## 13. Maren Returns

Maren had always believed she would know her father's people by their smell before anything else. Corvane riders smelled of tallow and iron and the particular sweetness of blood that has been paid for rather than spilled. She'd been smelling it on the wind for two days now, faint but persistent, like a debt collector standing politely at the edge of a property.

So when she crested the ridge above the old drover's road and saw the two small figures huddled under a dead oak—one of them radiating enough wild magic to light a cathedral and doing her best to pretend otherwise—Maren was not surprised to find herself thinking: *they'll be dead in three days unless someone teaches them.*

She was slightly surprised by how much that thought annoyed her.

"Forty years," she muttered to the marsh behind her, which had followed her out of the valley in the form of damp boots and a permanent smell of rot-sweet reeds. "Forty years I keep my head down, and then a scribe walks into my hut with the Thorn in her arm, and now I'm hiking through Ashfall country like a girl with more sense than sense."

The oak's roots made a decent windbreak. Maren settled against one, pulled her cloak around her knees, and watched.

The tall one was moving. That would be the smuggler—Tam, if the marsh gossip was worth anything, and it usually was; reeds heard everything. He was pacing the perimeter like a man who believed worry was a form of labor and ought to be performed visibly. The other sat with her back against the trunk, sleeves pushed up, staring at her forearm the way one stares at a snake one has foolishly decided to keep as a pet.

Oscar Halloran. Maren had felt her coming for days—a warmth in the null-scarred places of the world, a place where the silence itself had developed a heartbeat. Forty years since Maren had felt anything like it. Forty years since she'd cut the feeling out of herself with her own hands, more or less, and called the bleeding finished.

"You're staring," Tam said suddenly, without turning around. "There's someone on the ridge."

"I'm always on ridges," Maren called down. "It's the marsh that moves."

He had a knife out fast—quick, honest reflexes, the kind you can't teach—and Oscar was on her feet with her arm half-raised like a shield, which was exactly the wrong instinct and confirmed everything Maren needed to know about how badly this was going.

"Don't," Maren said, standing, palms open. "If I wanted you dead I'd have let the riders find you yesterday. They were four hours behind you at the ford. You left tracks a blind mole could follow, and your friend here—" she nodded at Oscar "—leaves a trail of *warmth* wide enough to drive the tithe wagon through. I could have found you from Dunlow. So could they, eventually."

Tam's knife didn't lower. "Who are you?"

"Maren. Hedge-witch, exiled, unlicensed, unimpressed." She came down the slope with the unhurried gait of a woman who had long ago stopped caring whether men with knives approved of her approach. "And you, scribe, are carrying something that is going to get everyone within twenty miles killed unless you learn to stop shouting with your skin."

Oscar's eyes went to her forearm. To the ink-dark lines that showed even from here, faintly luminous, like veins of lightning trapped under paper.

"You know what it is," Oscar said.

"Girl, I've known what it is since before your grandmother learned to write her name." Maren reached them and stopped an arm's length away, close enough to feel it—the heat rolling off the mark, wild and unshaped, a bonfire burning inside a library. She winced despite herself. "Well. That's worse than I expected."

"Worse how?"

"Worse in the way that matters." She looked Oscar over properly now: hollow-eyed, mud to the knee, hands held carefully away from her body as though they belonged to someone else. A scribe's hands. Callused in the shape of pen-grips instead of sword-hilts. Somewhere behind those exhausted eyes was a woman who had spent her whole life believing the world was a thing that got *recorded*, not a thing that got *decided*.

Maren had been that woman once. Different archive, different cage. Same look.

"We can't stay here," Tam said. "You said there were riders."

"There are. Corvane outriders, sweeping toward Ashfall because their lord has finally worked out where his prize is walking." She jerked her chin eastward. "But they won't sweep this stretch until tomorrow's dawn. Which gives us tonight." She looked at Oscar. "One night to teach you the first lesson. It may be the only one we get."

"The first lesson of what?"

"Of being quiet," Maren said. "Sit down, both of you. This will take a while, and my knees are older than this tree."

* * *

They made a poor fire—Tam insisted on it, small and shielded, and Maren let him because fear needs its rituals—and while he kept watch with the particular vigilance of a man who trusts no darkness ever again, Maren sat across from Oscar and studied the mark in the firelight.

Up close it was beautiful, which was the cruelest thing about it. The lines moved. Slowly, like frost forming on glass, like something growing because growing was all it knew how to do.

"Does it hurt?" Maren asked.

"When I lie." Oscar said it flatly, the way people recite things they've decided not to cry about. "Or when I'm frightened. Or angry. Or—" she paused "—when I want something too much."

"Mm. And how often do you want things?"

A ghost of a smile. "I used to be very good at not wanting anything. I'm getting worse."

"That's the Thorn talking. It feeds on wanting. Not evil, mind—it doesn't whisper to you, doesn't tempt, nothing so tidy as your temple stories. It just *amplifies*. Whatever's strongest in you, it waters. Fear becomes terror. Hope becomes hunger. Love becomes—" Maren stopped herself. "Well. Love becomes large."

"And what happens to the person holding it?"

Maren fed a twig to the fire. "Depends on the person. The houses that held it—Corvane, Vail, the old Ashfall line—they held it like property, and it rotted them like property rots. But it's older than property. It grew on a world-tree once, before anyone thought to own a tree." She looked up. "Do you know why they call it the Thorn?"

Oscar shook her head.

"Because the first bearers tried to prune it. Cut it away from its root, make it portable, make it *theirs*. And a cut branch still lives, for a while. But everything it grows after the cutting comes out crooked." Maren spread her hands. "You're holding a crooked piece of a holy thing. The question isn't whether it will change you. It already has. The question is whether you'll grow crooked with it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere off in the dark, a marsh bird made its idiot complaint.

"How do you know all this?" Oscar asked quietly. "Really. A hedge-witch in a border marsh doesn't learn the genealogy of the Thorn."

"No," Maren agreed. "She learns it at her father's table, over supper, while the servants top up the wine." She watched the understanding move through Oscar's face—slow, then all at once.

"Corvane," Oscar breathed. "You're—"

"My father held the Thorn for eleven years before the war's end. My grandfather before him. I was raised on stories of our *great inheritance*, how our blood was chosen, how magic was the wall between our house and the animals." Maren's voice stayed level, which cost her. It always cost her. "When I turned nineteen, my father began preparing me for the rite. Blood-letting at the solstice, the family tithe, the honor of feeding the line." She rolled up her own sleeve.

Old scars. Long-healed, deliberate, straight as ledger-lines. Dozens of them, laddered up her forearm from wrist to elbow.

"They never told you the tithe went both ways, did they? The house takes magic *from* its children to feed the vaults. That's what inheritance means, girl. You don't receive power. You owe it. From birth. Forever." She let the sleeve fall. "I ran the night before my final letting. My father sent riders. I got to the marshes, and the marsh hid me, because the marsh belongs to no one. First thing in my life that did."

Tam had gone very still by the fire. Even keeping watch, he was listening.

"So when I tell you what the Thorn does to houses," Maren said, "I'm not reciting prophecy. I'm reading a family account book. Every generation, a little more owed. Every generation, the children a little emptier. By my father's time there was hardly a person left in Corvane Hall at all—just appetite wearing a man's face." She met Oscar's eyes. "That is what you're carrying. Not a weapon. An *account*. And everyone who touches it starts paying."

Oscar looked down at her arm. Her voice, when it came, was very small. "Then how do I put it down?"

"Ah." Maren smiled, without much humor in it. "There's the lesson."

* * *

The teaching, it turned out, was mostly unteaching.

"Everything your temple taught you about discipline is wrong," Maren said, pacing in front of the fire while Oscar sat cross-legged in the dirt looking like a student who suspected the exam had been rigged. "Your scribes train to suppress. Press the thought down, hold the line, white knuckles and clenched teeth. That works on thoughts. It does not work on the Thorn. You squeeze it, it squeezes back. You fight it, it grows stronger roots. Understand?"

"Then what do I do with it?"

"Nothing. That's the trick. Nothing is the hardest thing you'll ever do."

"That sounds like something a charlatan says right before taking your purse."

Maren barked a laugh. "Good. Suspicion will serve you better than faith out here." She crouched opposite her. "Listen. The Thorn amplifies what's strongest. So the first lesson is knowing what's strongest—not what you wish were strongest, not what flatters you, what *is*. Most people never find out. They go their whole lives mistaking their loudest feeling for their deepest one." She tapped Oscar's marked arm, gently, one fingertip. "You don't get that luxury anymore. The Thorn will find your deepest thing whether you show it or not. So you'd better meet it first."

"And if my deepest thing is fear?"

"Then you'll be magnificent and terrible, briefly, and then dead or worse." Maren said it kindly. "Now. Breathe. Not the scribe's breath—you know the one, little controlled sips, tidy as margins. Breathe like a marsh. In slow, out slower. Let the bottom of the breath be muddy. Don't tidy it."

Oscar closed her eyes. Maren watched her try—watched the shoulders climb, the jaw set, the whole body performing calm the way a bad actor performs grief. The mark brightened. Of course it did. Suppression was wanting-not-to-want, and the Thorn drank contradiction like wine.

"Stop," Maren said. "You're strangling it."

"It's *burning*—"

"Yes. Because you're treating it like a debtor who won't leave your parlor. Girl, it's not leaving. It's in your arm, your blood, probably your dreams by now. You cannot evict it. You can only stop feeding it." Maren took Oscar's hand—those careful scribe's fingers, cold as archive stone—and pressed it flat against the ground. "Feel that? Dirt. Wet dirt, old dirt, dirt that doesn't care about prophecies or debts or noble houses. When the burn rises, give it to the dirt. Not fighting it. *Pouring* it. Like water off a table edge. The earth has swallowed bigger fires than yours."

For a long moment nothing happened. Then Oscar's breathing changed—dropped out of her chest into somewhere lower, muddier—and Maren felt it happen, felt the wild heat bank itself like a fire settling into coals, felt the mark dim from lightning to ember.

"Oh," Oscar said softly. Her eyes were still closed. "Oh, it's—it likes that. It doesn't want to burn. It wants—"

"Rooting," Maren said quietly. "Yes. It's a seed, child. Seeds only want one thing." She released the hand. "Don't let it forget you're the gardener, not the soil."

Oscar opened her eyes, and for a moment—just a moment—Maren saw her clearly: not the fugitive, not the vessel, but the woman underneath, stubborn and scared and ferociously alive, sitting in the dirt of a ruined kingdom holding a piece of a broken god and *choosing*, minute by minute, to remain a person.

Renouncing power was also a choice with a price. Maren knew the price by heart. Forty years of scars, of exile, of watching her family's banners from a distance and feeling nothing she could safely name. She had paid it gladly, most days. Some days she paid it again anyway, the way you pay a debt that keeps finding new ledgers.

This girl would pay it too. That was the mercy Maren couldn't say aloud yet: that learning to be quiet would cost Oscar almost as much as learning to roar. Quiet meant watching Corvane's wagons pass without striking. Quiet meant letting some injustices walk past because intervening would wake the thing in her arm. Quiet meant a lifetime of swallowing fire and smiling.

"Well done," was all she said. "Again tomorrow. And the day after. Quiet is a craft, like any other. Nobody inherits it."

Tam came and sat down across the fire, knife finally sheathed, studying Maren with the frank appraisal of a man re-pricing goods he'd misjudged. "You ran from Corvane. And you've spent forty years helping people run *to* safety. The marsh, the smugglers' waystations—the reed-cutters talk about a witch who leaves lanterns lit."

"Reed-cutters talk too much."

"Yet it's true."

Maren shrugged. "I couldn't take the Thorn out of my blood. I could take other people out of theirs' reach. Seemed a fair trade." She poked the fire. "Besides, spite keeps better than wine."

Oscar laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her, brief and warm and human—and the mark didn't flare at all. Small victory. Maren hoarded it.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, and with it, far off along the eastern road, the sound Maren had been dreading: hoofbeats. Not many. Three riders, maybe four, moving at a canter—not a sweep pattern. A direct line.

Straight toward them.

Tam was up before she finished counting. "How did they—"

"They didn't find you." Maren was already on her feet, scanning the treeline, her gut going cold in a way that had nothing to do with morning. "This isn't a search. It's an arrival. Someone told them exactly where you'd be."

"Corvane has informants everywhere," Oscar said, gathering their few belongings with shaking hands.

"No." Maren's mouth had gone dry. She knew this road. She knew who traveled it alone, on foot, in temple grey, and she knew—had known since last night, had felt it like a splinter in the world's silence—that the null-scar in the east had been walking closer for days, patient as a verdict. "Riders don't bother me. Riders are just Corvane's hands." She looked at Oscar, and saw the same realization dawning in the girl's face a half-second later. "It's what the riders are escorting that should frighten you. There's a man on this road who read the same book you did, scribe. Years ago. And refused."

Oscar went white. "Vane."

The hoofbeats rounded the bend.
