## 14. The Prophecy's Second Reader

Inquisitor Vane had been following the smell of woodsmoke for two days, and he hated himself for how good it felt to finally sit down.

The fire was small and mean, the kind travelers built when they wanted warmth but not attention, and it crackled in a ring of stones at the mouth of an overhang where the old road to Ashfall climbed out of the river valley. Two bedrolls. A dented kettle. A lean young man with a smuggler's build sitting up fast, hand going to a knife he was too honest to pretend he hadn't reached for. And the scribe—Halloran—on her feet already, back against the rock, one arm half-raised as though she could hold off the temple the way you'd hold off weather.

"Sit," Vane said. "Please. My knees are seventy years older than the rest of me, and if I'm going to disappoint you both, I'd rather do it from the ground."

He lowered himself onto a stone at the edge of the firelight, laid his staff across his lap, and did not draw the seal-iron from his belt. He watched them notice that. He watched Halloran notice that she noticed, and something in her face shift from fear toward the more dangerous territory of confusion.

"You're alone," Tam said. It wasn't a question so much as an accusation wearing a question's coat.

"I am alone. Which is either a mercy or an insult, depending on your opinion of me." Vane held his hands toward the fire. They were long hands, ink-stained at the cuticles even now, even after everything—a scribe's hands that had learned other work. "I'd offer to make tea, but your kettle is empty and I've been drinking stream water since Dunlow, and I refuse to be pitied on top of everything else."

Halloran hadn't sat. "Where are the others?"

"There are no others." He said it plainly, because plainness was the only currency he had left. "There haven't been others since the border marshes. I told the temple I could track you because I'm the only Inquisitor who ever read the archives cover to cover. Both things are true. The second thing is why there are no others."

Tam glanced at Halloran. Vane watched the glance—the quick, silent negotiation of people who have been running together long enough to develop a shorthand—and found, to his mild surprise, that it didn't sting. He had stopped being a man who belonged inside anyone's shorthand years ago.

"You're not here to arrest me," Halloran said slowly.

"No."

"Then you're here to kill me, which would at least be honest."

Vane laughed before he could stop himself—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge in a house no one lived in. "No. Though I appreciate that you think I'm capable of honesty. Most of the temple doesn't believe I am anymore. They may be right." He looked into the fire. The smoke smelled of pine and wet ash, and beneath it, faintly, of her—the Thorn had a scent, once you knew it; green and sharp, like sap from a cut branch, like the first day of spring happening somewhere it shouldn't. He had smelled it through the archive door eleven years ago and never entirely stopped smelling it since. "Miss Halloran. Oscar, if I may. May I tell you a story? It's long, and it's about me, which is normally unforgivable, but I promise it ends with information you need."

Tam said, "We don't owe you anything."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's rather the point of my whole life."

* * *

He began, as all his stories began, with the archive.

"I came to Dunlow nineteen years ago," he said. "A novitiate with a gift for cataloguing and no family name worth mentioning, which in the temple is nearly the same as having no soul. I was given the restricted stacks because no one else wanted them. Dust, cold, and books nobody reads—that was my parish." He smiled at the fire. "You know it. You worked it off, debt by debt. I worked mine off ambition by ambition. I thought if I catalogued enough of the past, someone would notice me and give me a future.

"One winter—I was twenty-three, maybe twenty-four—I found a book shelved wrong. Or shelved right, depending on how you look at it. Black binding, no title, chained to the shelf with a chain so corroded it broke when I breathed on it. As though it had been waiting for exactly one careless novitiate with cold hands." His fingers moved on his knee, tracing a shape Vane didn't let them finish. "You know the book. Final entry. A date. A name."

"The date is tomorrow," Halloran said quietly. "Or—it was three weeks ago, now."

"Yes. And the name is yours. And beneath it, the words that ended my sleeping: *the thorn wakes.*" He looked up at her. Firelight made her look very young and very stubborn, which he suspected was accurate. "I read it, Miss Halloran. I read every word of every entry. Do you understand what that means? The prophecy doesn't choose its reader at random. It chooses whoever *reads*. Whoever takes it into themselves. I stood in that freezing stack with a lamp guttering and I read the whole cursed book, and then I waited to burn. I waited to wake with ink under my skin. I waited to become whatever it is you became."

"But you didn't," Tam said.

"No." Vane turned his palms up. "And I have spent nineteen years learning why, and the answer has ruined me, so do forgive me if I deliver it badly." He drew a breath. "The Thorn isn't a seed looking for soil. It's a seed looking for a *yes*. It asks. That's what nobody understands—not the temple, not Corvane, not Maren, bless her stubborn marsh-drowned heart. It asked me, in the dark, in the silence after the last page. And I said no."

The wind moved through the pines above the overhang. Somewhere down the slope, water talked to itself over stones.

"It asked you," Halloran repeated. Her hand had drifted to her forearm without her seeming to notice—the sleeve, the mark beneath it. "How? What did it—" She stopped. Started again. "What did refusing feel like?"

"Like closing a door on a child standing in the snow," Vane said, and the words came out level because he had rehearsed them for two decades, in the dark, in the silence, until they were smooth as river stones and just as cold. "It didn't argue. It didn't threaten. It simply... withdrew. Like a tide going out. And the room got colder, and the book went dull in my hands, ordinary, just paper and dead ink, and I remember feeling—this is the shameful part—I remember feeling *relieved*. I put it back on the shelf. I mended the chain with wire from my lamp. I went to compline and sang the responses and thought: I have escaped something terrible. I thought I had won.

"Three days later, the hollowing began."

"Hollowing," Tam said.

"The temple has a punishment for those who touch the restricted archive and are not chosen by it." Vane kept his voice pleasant, conversational, a man discussing grain prices. "They don't write it down, of course—they're scribes, they write down everything, except the things that matter. It's called quieting. It's done with rites and fasts and certain prayers spoken backward into the ear while you sleep, and it takes years, and it doesn't hurt. That's the cruelty of it. It doesn't hurt at all. You simply lose things. First the memory of the book itself—you'll notice I can describe every entry except the ones between the first and the last; there's a hole in me shaped like forty pages. Then smaller things. My mother's voice—I remember that she sang, I remember that it annoyed me, I cannot for the life of me recall the tune. The taste of the food I loved at twelve. Why I became a scribe in the first place. Ambition went early; I barely miss it." He flexed his ink-stained fingers. "They hollow you out and fill the space with doctrine, and doctrine is lighter than a soul, so you float. You become very good at your work. You rise. Everyone says what a steady man Inquisitor Vane is. What a reliable man. What an empty—"

He stopped. Smiled. Adjusted nothing on his face, because there was nothing left to adjust.

"They couldn't quiet the reading itself," Halloran said. She had sat down at some point; Vane couldn't say when. "If they took the whole memory, you wouldn't know why—"

"—why they were punishing me, yes, precisely. So they left the shape of it. A hole with edges. I know there was a book. I know I refused. I know what I refused *became*, because the prophecy was written down elsewhere, in fragments, and I am very good at research. But the center is gone." He tapped his own breastbone, lightly, the way you'd knock on a door hoping no one answered. "Do you know what I feel when I stand near you, Miss Halloran? Right now?"

She shook her head.

"Cold," he said. "Not the fire's fault. Cold, the way you feel cold standing where a wall used to be. Your Thorn remembers me. It remembers that I said no. And it holds a grudge the way only patient things hold grudges." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the pleasantness fell away entirely and what was underneath was so tired it seemed geological. "I didn't come to arrest you. Arresting you is what the *me* the temple built would do, and I confess I'm not always sure where he ends and I begin. I came because I read the fragments. I came because I know what Corvane knows, and what Ashfall is, and what the valley really is, and I knew you'd find out eventually and I wanted—"

He paused. Searched for the word among the shelves of himself, most of them bare.

"I wanted to warn you," he finished simply. "That's all. Nineteen years, and the sum of my rebellion is a warning delivered beside a borrowed fire."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke, and Vane occupied the silence the way he occupied all silences now—like a tenant who knew the landlord was coming.

It was Tam who broke it, because Tam was the sort of man who couldn't leave a wound unprobed. "Why should we believe any of this? You tracked us across three provinces. You knew our route. If the temple hollowed you, they still aimed you at her like an arrow."

"They did aim me." Vane nodded, unoffended. "They gave me the assignment because I'm the only one who might recognize what she carries. And I accepted it, because refusing an assignment accelerates the quieting—they take something bigger each time, and next on their list, I suspect, is my sister's face, and I find I'm not ready." He said it without self-pity, reporting facts. "But arrows are permitted a certain drift, Master Tam. Especially old ones."

"And if we send you away?" Halloran asked. "Report you? Leave you here?"

"Then I'll report that I lost you in the hill country, and I'll pay for the lie however they choose to bill me." He shrugged. "I've an inventory of what's left. It's short. Mostly it's this: I remember being twenty-three and afraid, and choosing wrongly, and I remember that the choosing was the last free thing I did for a very long time." He looked at her across the fire, and let her see all the way down into the hollows, because pity was useless unless it was honest. "Listen to me, girl, because this is the only sermon I have left. The Thorn will ask you, too—not once, at the beginning, but again and again, at every step, in every form: use me, spend me, be the vessel, be the sword. And everyone around you will tell you what a responsible bearer you are, what a good heart, and each time you say yes it will feel like justice, and each yes will carve out a little more of whoever you were before. Refusal is not safe. I refused once, cowardly, in the dark, and it cost me everything anyway. But refusal done *well*—refusal done with your eyes open, on your own terms, for reasons you chose—" his voice caught, or perhaps it was only the smoke, "—that's the only way I know of to keep the person who started this journey alive at the end of it."

Halloran was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was gentle, and gentleness, Vane thought, was the most dangerous thing anyone had aimed at him in nineteen years.

"What was your mother's song?" she asked. "Can you hum it?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Something moved behind his eyes like a man pacing a room that has been bricked shut.

"No," he said at last. "But I know it was in a minor key. I know that much. They missed that much."

"Oh," said Halloran softly, "you poor man," and Vane discovered that after two decades of doctrine, of steadiness, of floating, those four foolish words undid him more thoroughly than any rite the temple had ever performed. He turned his face to the fire and was silent for some time, and the other two had the grace to let him be.

When he could speak again, he was briskly practical, because briskly practical was armor and he still owned some. "Ashfall. You're making for Ashfall—don't bother denying it, it's the only place left that makes sense. There's a road along the ridge that avoids the shrine at Coldmere; the temple has watchers there, and one of them owes me money, which means he watches *exactly* as instructed, no better. Take the ridge. And when you reach the ruins—" he hesitated, weighing how much of himself the next sentence would cost, and paid it, "—there's a door beneath the fallen bell tower. It opens for temple seal-iron. Mine." He drew the iron from his belt, considered it a moment—an object that had defined him for nineteen years—and tossed it across the fire to Halloran, who caught it against her chest like something hot. "Consider it my resignation. They'll take my memories of tonight regardless, so I intend to make tonight worth the loss."

"Why?" Tam demanded. "Why throw away everything for her?"

Vane smiled at him, and for the first time it reached his eyes, and made them briefly, startlingly young.

"Because somebody read the book before her," he said, "and said no in the dark where no one could see, and has regretted the *manner* of it ever since. She'll refuse the Thorn too—I'd stake my remaining memories on it; she has a scribe's soul, and scribes know that some things should never be written in blood. But she'll do it at the vault, in the light, for the right reasons, having *chosen* it. She'll succeed where I failed. And someone ought to be alive to see the version of events where the frightened boy in the archive finally gets it right." He rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and settled his cloak on his shoulders. "Second chances are wasted on the young, Master Tam. They haven't broken enough yet to know what they're for."

He walked to the edge of the firelight, then paused, struck by one last thing—one small, human thing surfacing from the flooded rooms of himself like a chair bobbing up in a drowned house.

"Miss Halloran. The entry above yours. The one dated ninety-nine years ago." He didn't turn fully around. "There was a name there too. I've carried it a long time, in the part of me they couldn't reach. It was *Maren of House Corvane.* Tell her—" he searched again, and this time found it instantly, "—tell her the boy who read her page is sorry he was afraid. And that her page was the reason I understood yours."

Then he went, down into the dark along the stream, an old arrow drifting home, and the fire cracked, and the pines sighed, and Oscar Halloran sat turning the seal-iron over in her hands while Tam stared after the Inquisitor and said, quietly, "Well. I'll be damned."

Behind them, far to the north, where the ruins of Ashfall slept under a century of ash and grass, torchlights were moving along the ridgelines—many torches, in ordered lines, converging. House colors glinted at their heads.

Corvane had not waited to be found.

"We have two days," Oscar said, standing, tucking the seal-iron into her coat like a heartbeat she'd been entrusted with. "Maybe less. Walk fast, ask questions later."

Tam groaned, shouldered the packs, and followed her into the dark.
