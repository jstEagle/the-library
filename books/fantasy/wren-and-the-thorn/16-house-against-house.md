## 16. House Against House

The first thing Tam felt was the ground humming.

He'd been asleep against a fallen column in Ashfall's outer ring, one arm around his pack, dreaming of nothing much—a river, a boat, no debts—when the stone beneath him began to shiver like a plucked string. He'd grown up in the smuggling warrens under Vellmark, where you learned to read vibrations the way priests read scripture. Cart wheels. Marching feet. A lot of marching feet.

"Up," he said to the dark. "Everyone up."

Maren was already awake. She was always already awake; Tam suspected she didn't so much sleep as stand down for a few hours out of politeness. She stood on the broken wall with her head tilted, listening to the hum.

"How many?" Tam asked.

"At least four houses," Maren said. "I can taste the banners from here. Corvane's iron and ash. Vess's salt. The Marrow twins' sickly-sweet rot. And something old coming up from the coast." Her mouth went thin. "They didn't come to negotiate."

"They came because they heard the Thorn's awake."

"They came because they heard *everyone else* might get it," Maren corrected. "That's worse. Fear makes nobles generous with other people's blood."

Below them, in the shelter of the ruined archive's entrance, Oscar sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the horizon where torchlight was beginning to bloom like a rash. She looked, Tam thought, like someone doing arithmetic and not liking the answer.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

Tam squinted. Counted fires. Did the smuggler's math he'd been doing since he was nine years old, when the difference between an hour and two hours had been the difference between living and being hanged.

"Until dawn," he said. "Maybe less if they're eager. Nobles are always eager when they think someone else is about to be richer than them."

"And we're what, exactly? Bait? Prize?"

"You," Maren said gently, "are the only thing in this kingdom that isn't already owned. That makes you either priceless or intolerable, depending on who you ask."

Oscar laughed—a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "My father used to say I'd make something of myself. I don't think this is what he meant."

"It never is," said Tam. "Come on. If we're going to be besieged, let's do it somewhere with two exits instead of none."

* * *

Inquisitor Vane watched the noble army arrive the way other men watched weather.

He had taken position on the ridge above Ashfall three days ago, ostensibly to observe, actually because his orders from the temple had stopped making sense and he'd found that standing very still confused his handlers less than moving did. Behind him, the temple's escort waited with the patient menace of men paid by the hour. Ahead of him, below, the armies of four great houses were arranging themselves around a ruin that contained, as far as any of them knew, the seed of godhood.

Vane had read the prophecy book twenty years ago. He remembered every word. He also remembered what the temple had done to him afterward—not with knives or fire, but with smallness. Little duties. Little humiliations. Twenty years of being hollowed out like a gourd until only obedience rattled around inside.

And now, watching the banners unfurl in the pre-dawn gray—the black thorn of Corvane, the white wave of Vess, the twin serpents of the Marrows—he understood something that made him want to sit down in the dust and laugh.

They hadn't come for the Thorn.

Well. They thought they had. But look at them. Look at how they deployed. Corvane's pikes angled not at the ruins but at Vess's flank. Vess's skirmishers were positioned to cover the road home, not the approach. Every general here had planned for betrayal by his allies before planning for victory over their common enemy, because that was how power worked—it could not trust, and so it could not rest, and so it marched to war against itself while telling itself it marched toward glory.

The Thorn didn't need to corrupt anyone. It just needed to exist, and the powerful would corrupt themselves scrambling toward it, like moths who burned each other's wings before ever reaching flame.

"Sir," said his lieutenant, a young woman named Pell whose face still had some softness left in it. "Should we move to secure the site? The Archon's instructions—"

"The Archon's instructions assume we serve the temple," Vane said. "Look down there, Pell. Tell me what you see."

She looked. She was smart enough; that was why he'd brought her. He watched her face change as the arrangement of the armies resolved into its true shape.

"...They're pointed at each other," she said slowly.

"Four hundred years of holy doctrine, and the truth fits in one sentence." Vane folded his hands. "We will wait. And when they have finished demonstrating exactly why no one should ever hold that kind of power, we will see what's left worth saving."

Pell hesitated. "And if what's left is a girl with a burning arm?"

Vane thought of Oscar Halloran's face when she'd lied to him in the archive corridor—thought of the way her voice had shaken, and the way she'd fainted, and the strange, awful kinship he'd felt watching her suffer for the sin of reading. He had spent twenty years believing he'd refused the Thorn rightly. Standing on this ridge, watching four armies prepare to butcher each other for it, he wasn't sure anymore. Refusal that leaves the world unchanged—is that virtue, or just cowardice with better posture?

"Then," he said, "we decide what we actually believe. For the first time in our lives."

* * *

Sergeant Brakka of House Vess had been a soldier for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once seen a battle plan survive contact with breakfast, let alone contact with another army.

The trouble started an hour before dawn, when one of the Marrow twins—I never could tell them apart, Brakka thought, and I've killed men for both of them—decided to seize the high ground of the western terrace before Corvane could. This was sensible. Unfortunately, the Marrow scouts mistook a herd of Ashfall goats for Corvane outriders and loosed a volley into them, and the goats, being goats, stampeded directly through Vess's camp, and Vess's pickets, being jumpy, opened fire on the goats, and the noise carried across the valley like a declaration of war, which functionally it became, because by sunrise nobody remembered whose fault the goats were, only that arrows had flown.

Brakka stood in the mud of the forward trench and watched three centuries of noble strategy dissolve into a screaming, smoking melee around a ruin full of dead kings, and she thought: *this is what we are. This is what we've always been.*

Her house's magic lived in her blood—generations of it, banked like coin, a warmth behind her ribs that could harden her skin to leather and put the strength of two men in her arms. She'd been proud of it once. Then she'd been drained by tithe after tithe, her inheritance siphoned upward to fuel Lord Vess's ambitions, and pride had curdled into something more like tenancy. She was a field her lord planted and harvested. The warmth in her chest wasn't hers. Nothing was hers.

A Corvane pikeman came over the trench wall, face painted with ash, eyes wild. Brakka hardened her skin and broke his pike across her forearm and then, because he was nineteen at most and shaking, shoved him back instead of killing him.

"Go home!" she shouted at him.

"There's no—" he started, and then a Marrow fireball took the top off the barricade behind him, and whatever home meant to him now, it wasn't behind those lines either.

By midmorning the battle had stopped resembling anything but a riot with heraldry. Houses fought houses; soldiers deserted and looted both sides; the goat herd, having caused the apocalypse, grazed placidly on the eastern slope, unbothered. Magic lit the smoke in colors that didn't exist anywhere else—Corvane's iron-gray lightning, Vess's blue salt-fire, the Marrows' green rot-light crawling along the ground like spilled poison.

And underneath it all, Brakka noticed—and she would carry this observation out of the battle and tell it to anyone who'd listen, which eventually turned out to be historians—the ruin itself seemed to be *listening*. The old stones drank the spilled magic. Every death fed something in the ground.

*This place remembers,* she thought. *This whole kingdom is built on a grave, and we're dancing on it.*

* * *

Oscar felt each death like a pebble dropped into water.

She couldn't explain how she knew. There was no sound, no vision—just a faint ripple through the mark on her arm, a tiny cold pulse, over and over, faster now. The Thorn was aware of the battle. The Thorn was *counting*.

"Stop that," she whispered to her own forearm, and felt slightly insane saying it.

They had retreated into the archive's upper galleries, a honeycomb of collapsed reading rooms where Tam swore he'd stashed supplies during a previous run. It smelled of dust and old vellum and, faintly, of the sea, though the coast was fifty miles away. Through the shattered windows they could see the battle as a smear of light and motion, like watching a storm through frosted glass.

"Here." Tam pressed a strip of dried fish into her hand. "Eat. You can't save the world on an empty stomach. Believe me, I've tried."

"I'm not trying to save the world."

"You're carrying the only piece of it that matters, and you haven't slept in two days, and you keep apologizing to your arm. Eat the fish, Oscar."

She ate the fish. It was terrible. She was grateful for it.

Through the wall, muffled, came the boom of siege-magic hitting the outer walls. Dust sifted down between the floorboards like slow snow. Somewhere below them, in the buried vault where the heart of everything slept, Oscar could feel the Thorn turning over in its long dream, and she understood—with a clarity that felt less like thought and more like temperature—that the armies outside weren't fighting over the Thorn at all.

They were fighting over the *right* to fight over it later.

"That's the trick of it," Maren said quietly, appearing beside her with the uncanny silence she possessed. Oscar was starting to suspect the woman moved through the world the way rumors did. "You don't need to hold power to be corrupted by it. Wanting it does half the work. Watching others reach for it does the rest. My family—" she paused, and Oscar heard, for the first time, something like grief in her voice, "—my family spent nine generations perfecting the art of wanting. We called it ambition. We built statues to it. And when I fled, do you know what my mother said to me?"

"What?"

"She said, 'Then you'll be nothing.' As if that settled it." Maren smiled, thin and real. "It took me thirty years to understand she was giving me a gift. Nothing is a door, child. Everything is a cage."

Outside, something enormous struck the eastern wall, and the gallery lurched, and Tam caught Oscar's elbow as books rained from a broken shelf—a century-old treatise on drainage law, tumbling open at her feet, absurd and ordinary amid the end of the old order.

"Two exits," Tam reminded her, hauling her toward the stairwell. "Remember? Time to use one."

* * *

Lord Aldous Corvane arrived fashionably late, which was to say precisely when the other houses had bled themselves enough to be manageable.

He observed the battlefield from the command rise with the satisfaction of a man watching competitors ruin each other at auction. His retainers flanked him, their inherited magic banked and gleaming; his personal guard wore armor forged by six generations of bound smiths, and it drank light rather than reflecting it. He was sixty-one years old and had never lost anything except, once, briefly, a daughter—which was not loss but theft, and theft could be corrected.

"My lord," said his marshal, "the Marrows are breaking. Vess holds the terrace but her left is collapsing. Shall we commit the reserve?"

"In a moment." Aldous studied the ruin's central massif, where the old archive burrowed into the earth like a wound. Somewhere down there was the girl. Somewhere down there was the Thorn. His spies had cost him a fortune and delivered certainty: the Halloran woman had crossed the border, met the hedge-witch, and come to Ashfall. Everything the prophecies promised was within his grasp, and better—all the rival claimants were busy destroying each other's capacity to contest it.

There was a proper way to take possession of a thing like the Thorn. Legally. Formally. With contracts and witnesses and the girl's consent, purchased fairly—her father's debt erased, her safety guaranteed, a life of comfort in exchange for what she carried. He had drawn up the papers himself. Justice, administered properly, was simply purchase conducted with dignity.

That was the lie he told himself, and it was a well-made lie, load-bearing, and he had lived inside it for so long that he had forgotten it had a door.

"Commit the reserve," he said at last. "Take the archive intact. And find me the girl—unharmed. She is a party to a contract, not a casualty."

"Yes, my lord."

As the reserve swept down the hill, fresh and terrible, Aldous permitted himself one moment of anticipation—one breath in which he imagined the Thorn in his bloodline, Corvane ascendant for a thousand years, his name carved into the world-tree's stump.

He did not notice, then, that the stones beneath the battlefield were glowing faintly. None of the lords did. Only the soldiers close to the ground saw it, and they were too busy dying to mention it.

* * *

From the gallery window, Oscar watched the Corvane reserve advance and felt the Thorn stop counting deaths.

It began, instead, to *wake*.

Heat poured up her arm—not the burning of lies this time, but something older and stranger, like sunrise happening under her skin. She gasped. Across her forearm the transcribed words of the prophecy shifted, rearranged, and for one clear moment she read a line she had never seen before, inked in no book, written in no century:

***when the houses gather against the seed, the seed shall choose.***

"Maren," she said. Her voice sounded far away. "Maren, it says it chooses."

Maren took her arm, read the line, and went pale in a way that Tam had never seen in all their years of narrow escapes.

"Downstairs," Maren said. "Now. Both of you. Before they finish breaking the walls—or before the Thorn finishes waking up. Whichever comes first."

Above them, the roof split with a crack like the world clearing its throat, and through the gap poured light—not sunlight, not fire, but the deep gold of the vault below, rising, answering.

Somewhere in the smoke and the shouting, the battle faltered. Soldiers on all sides stopped, staring at the ruin that had begun to shine.

And Oscar, pulled between two people who loved her in their prickly, imperfect ways, ran down into the earth toward the choice that had been waiting for her since a scribe a century ago dipped his pen and wrote tomorrow's date.
