## 17. The Choice in the Vault

The stair down had no bottom.

That was Oscar's first thought, and her second, and by the third hundred step it had stopped being a thought and become simply the truth of her body: she was descending into a place that did not wish to be found. The walls were not stone, exactly—they were stone that remembered being a tree, grain swirling in pale wood-color under her sputtering lantern—and the steps themselves were roots, worn smooth by a century of nothing walking on them.

Tam went ahead of her with the better light. Maren came behind, one hand resting between Oscar's shoulder blades like a paperweight on a restless page.

"You're humming," Tam said over his shoulder.

"I am not."

"You are. Something from the temple liturgy. The boring one, the one they sing at harvest accounting." He grinned back at her, and even now, even here, the grin worked. "You count things when you're frightened. You've been counting steps since we started."

"One hundred and forty-one," Oscar admitted.

"See? A scribe to the marrow. You'd tally your own funeral."

"Someone would have to," she said. "No one else's figures would be legible."

Maren made a sound behind them that might have been a laugh or might have been an old woman clearing marsh-water from her lungs. "Both of you hush. We're nearly there. Can't you feel it?"

Oscar could. She had been feeling it for an hour—the Thorn stirring under her skin like a bird turning in its sleep, then waking, then beating. Not burning. Burning had been the valley's doing, the mark's protest against lies. This was different. This was recognition. Whatever waited below knew what she carried, the way a lock knows its key, or a wound knows the blade that made it.

At step three hundred and six—she counted, because Tam was right, she always counted—the stairs ended.

* * *

The vault beneath Ashfall was round as a held breath.

Lantern-light couldn't reach its ceiling; the dark up there was soft and total, like the inside of closed eyelids. The floor was a mosaic of pale root-wood, and set into its center was a basin perhaps two paces across, carved in the shape of a cupped hand so large and so ancient that the fingers had cracked and half-sunken, so that it looked less like a hand now than like the idea of one—a thing the earth had dreamed of holding.

And above the basin, suspended in nothing, hung a tree.

Not a living tree. An impression of one. A shape of silver-green light no taller than a sapling, branches bare, roots trailing downward into the basin without touching it—held forever a finger's width above the soil, the way a held note is held, the way a scream is held when you wake from the dream too late.

Oscar's knees went out from under her.

She didn't faint. She knelt, deliberately, because it seemed the only honest posture available. The Thorn inside her chest flared once, wildly, and then—settled. Went quiet. Went *attentive*. She felt it turn toward the hanging tree the way a child turns toward a mother's voice in a crowded room.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's the world-tree. Or—" She stopped, because her eyes were doing something her vocabulary couldn't support.

"The heart of it," Maren said. Her voice had gone strange—flat, careful, the voice of a woman handling something that had already broken her once. "What's left of it. When they cut the Thorn free, this is where the cutting happened. And this is where they brought everything they took." She gestured, and Oscar followed her hand to the walls.

She had taken them for shadows. They were not shadows.

The walls of the vault were lined with doors. Hundreds of them, thousands, small and plain and shut, each no bigger than a bread-box, each carved with a single name. As Oscar's lantern passed over them, the names caught the light and gave it back wrong—not reflected but *remembered*, glowing faintly from within like coals banked for a very long winter.

"Whose names?" Tam asked. His voice had lost all its smuggler's ease.

"Everyone's." Maren walked the wall slowly, her fingertips hovering an inch from the little doors without touching them. "Every commoner whose magic was confiscated during the war. Every hedge-witch, every weather-worker, every child born with fire in their palms in some mud village no lord ever bothered to learn the name of. They didn't kill the magic. Killing it wasn't possible—that's the joke, if you want one. Magic can't be destroyed by people who have it. So they *banked* it. Sealed each inheritance behind a door with its owner's name, and built the valley on top as a null field to keep the doors quiet, and told everyone left alive that the war was over and the age of wonders had ended for the good of all."

"And the nobles kept theirs," Oscar said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "They didn't put their names on doors."

"They put their names on *deeds*." Maren turned. In the lantern light her face was old and tired and furious in the deep, patient way of someone who has been angry longer than most people have been alive. "Inheritance, they called it. Property. Bloodline. A hundred years later nobody even remembers it was theft."

Oscar looked down at her own forearm, where the words lived under her skin: *the thorn wakes*. She had copied them onto paper once, secretly, guiltily. Then the paper hadn't needed them anymore. Now she understood why. The book had never been a prophecy, not really. It was an *alarm*. A mechanism. The vault had written itself a letter and mailed it a hundred years into the future, and the address had been whoever was foolish enough or fated enough to be cataloguing the restricted archive on the correct night.

Her whole life. Dunlow. Her father's debt, the mills, the temple, the ink-stained hours of her childhood—all of it a clock's inner workings. She had spent twenty-two years being a gear.

"I need a moment," she said, with tremendous dignity, and was sick into the corner of the vault like a person of considerably less dignity.

Tam held her hair back and said nothing, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in weeks.

* * *

When she could stand again, they gathered at the basin's edge, and Maren explained the choice, because apparently the vault explained it to whoever carried the Thorn—it spoke in images, Maren said, in pressure behind the eyes, and Oscar felt it even now: two shapes turning slowly in her mind like coins spinning on a table, waiting to fall.

"You can end it," Maren said. "The basin will take the Thorn back into the heart-tree and close. Unmake it. And here is the part your temple never taught you, girl, because temples never teach the arithmetic that matters—" her voice hardened— "magic cannot be unmade while any of it lives. The Thorn is primal. First-stock. The seed everything else grew from. Kill it, and every door in this room goes dark. Every spark in every noble bloodline gutters out. Every hedge-charm, every healing, every hearth-rune. All of it. Everywhere. Forever."

Silence. Even the hanging tree seemed to lean closer.

"And the other coin?" Oscar asked.

"Plant it." Maren crouched, laid her palm flat on the soil in the great stone hand. "Here, in the vault soil, where the stolen magic sleeps behind its doors. The Thorn takes root, the doors open, and everything that was hoarded comes home—but changed. Wild. It won't answer to bloodlines anymore. It won't be property. It'll be like rain, or like reading—something anyone can learn and no one can own. Corvane would lose his empire overnight. Every house would. Half the kingdom's power structure would collapse within a year, and I promise you the lords will not go gently into being ordinary men."

"And me?" Oscar asked quietly. "Either way. What happens to me?"

Maren didn't answer. Which was an answer.

Tam answered instead. "We find another way. There's always another way. That's practically my trade—there's always another road through the marsh." He took her hand. His palm was warm and calloused and shaking slightly, and he let her feel him shaking, which she suspected cost him more than the words did. "Oscar. Listen to me. We didn't come all this way—through the tithe wagons and Vane and the burning road and that truly horrifying inn outside Graywater—so you could kneel down in a hole under the world and do arithmetic with your life."

"It's not arithmetic," Oscar said. "It's a choice. That's worse."

"Then make it slowly."

"There isn't time to make it slowly." She said it gently, and hated how true it was. She could feel the vault listening. Could feel the doors along the walls paying attention, a thousand small attentions pressing in like the silence of a full congregation. Somewhere overhead, distantly, through a hundred yards of root and stone, she could almost hear Ashfall holding its breath. "Corvane knows where we've gone. Maren's ravens saw his column leave at dawn. However long I take to decide, he's spending the same time getting here."

"Then decide wrong," Tam said fiercely. "Decide fast and wrong rather than slow and dead. Plant it. Break them. Make the bastards ordinary. You've seen what they do—the harvest wagons, the drained villages, the children sold by the pound of blood. You owe them nothing."

"I don't owe *them* anything," Oscar agreed. "But I'm not deciding about them, Tam. That's the trap. Everyone wants this choice to be about the lords. It isn't. It's about everyone else." She turned, slowly, and looked at the doors. At the names. "If I plant it, magic comes back wild—to farmers and fishwives and every cruel stupid human being in the world, because cruelty doesn't check bloodlines before it picks up a tool. If I destroy it, the lords lose their whips, yes. But so does the midwife in some village who charms fever out of infants. So does every small good thing the small people have smuggled out of a hostile century. I'd be burning down the house to spite the landlords, and the tenants freeze first."

Tam opened his mouth. Closed it. Ran both hands through his hair.

"This is why I hate clever people," he announced to the ceiling.

"You love one clever person."

"I'm suffering," he confirmed, "tremendously."

Despite everything—the sick taste in her mouth, the weight of a thousand sealed names watching her—Oscar laughed. A short, cracked, real laugh. And in the laughing she felt the Thorn stir again, softly, not hungry but *curious*, and she realized with a lurch that it had been waiting for exactly this: not her courage, not her despair, but her attention. Her whole self, gathered into one question.

She knelt at the basin's edge and put her hands in the soil.

* * *

The vault showed her everything at once, and she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it.

She saw the world-tree whole—vast beyond sense, its crown lost among stars, its roots drinking from every river of the world. She saw the first cut. She saw hands—noble hands, afraid hands, hands that had convinced themselves they were saving the world—carving the seed free, and the tree's light going out ring by ring like windows darkening in a city at dusk. She saw the gathering of the confiscated sparks, the naming of the doors, one name at a time, patient as a plague. She saw the valley raised over it all: not a refuge, not a sanctuary, a *lid*. She saw the last bearer of the Thorn, generations back, standing where Oscar knelt, choosing to carry it out rather than end it or free it, because he could not bear either grief—and she saw what he became, what the carrying made of him, and she understood at last the echo who had visited her dreams, warning her in a voice like ash.

And then the vision narrowed, kindly, mercifully, to a single image:

A garden.

Two gardens, actually. Side by side. In one, a blackened circle where a seed had been burned out of the world—sterile, safe, silent, and above it a sky in which nothing glowed, ever again, and people bent to their lives like people in a story with the last page torn out. In the other, a shoot of silver-green breaking soil, wild and branching and utterly ungovernable, growing in directions no gardener had planned, into hands that had never held anything, for purposes that would not be clear for a hundred years—and also, threaded through its beauty, unmistakably, thorns.

The vault did not tell her which to choose. That was the horror and the honor of it. It had waited a century for a reader, and having gotten one, it did precisely what a good archive does: it presented the record and withheld its opinion.

"Some help you are," Oscar whispered.

The vault, being a vault, said nothing. But the soil under her fingers was warm, and she thought—though she would never swear to it, never write it down—that it felt a little like forgiveness in advance. Whichever way she chose.

She lifted her head. "I know what I'm going to do."

"Do you?" Tam crouched beside her. "Because I've known you since the tithe wagon, Oscar Halloran, and you have the most changeable mind of any stubborn woman I've ever met."

"I'm going to plant it," she said. "Not for the reasons anyone wants. Not to break Corvane—though I confess the thought has a certain flavor. And not because wild magic is safe; it isn't; it's a storm we're choosing to walk into. I'm going to plant it because a hundred years ago a lot of people had something taken from them, and it was never returned, and everything rotten in this kingdom grew in that gap. You can't fix a theft by burning what was stolen. You return it. That's the whole of justice, Tam. It's embarrassingly simple. It's the first thing they teach you in the archives." Her voice shook. "Return what isn't yours. Let the owners decide what to do with it. Even if the owners are everyone. Even if 'everyone' terrifies me."

Maren had gone very still. When Oscar glanced at her, the old woman's eyes were wet, and she was looking at the wall of doors with an expression Oscar couldn't name until she realized she could: it was the look of a daughter at a grave she'd been told all her life was somebody else's.

"And the cost?" Maren asked quietly. "Do you know yours yet?"

"No," Oscar said honestly. "But I know whose fault it isn't. It isn't Tam's. Get him out of here. Both of you, when it's done—get up the stairs, get clear, because whatever happens when this takes root, Corvane's column is—"

The lanterns guttered.

All of them, at once, though there was no wind. The hanging tree's silver-green light flickered—and steadied—and in that steadying Oscar felt the Thorn lurch against her ribs like a startled heart.

Then, rolling down the stairwell from the world above, faint but unmistakable: horns. Many horns. And beneath them, muffled by a hundred yards of root and stone, the sound she had been dreading since dawn—

Boots. Thousands of boots, arriving at the ruins of Ashfall, marching in step toward the buried vault where a scribe, a smuggler, and an exile stood around an open choice.

"He's early," Tam breathed.

"No," Oscar said, rising, wiping the vault soil from her hands, feeling the Thorn rise with her, awake now, blazing, ready. "We're late."

She reached out and took the seed of the world in her hands—and the doors along every wall began, softly, one by one, to glow.
