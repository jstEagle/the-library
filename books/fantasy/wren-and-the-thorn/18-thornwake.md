## 18. Thornwake

The first sign was the birds.

They had been roosting in the ribs of Ashfall's ruined bell-tower all week—crows, starlings, a pair of fat pigeons Tam had named Chancellor and Vice-Chancellor because they spent all day preening and accomplishing nothing. At dawn they rose together, every one of them, a single dark breath leaving the tower, and flew south.

"They know something," Tam said, squinting up at the empty sky. He was mending a strap on his pack with his teeth and his knife at the same time, which struck Oscar as an ambitious way to lose a lip. "Birds are terrible gossips but excellent cowards."

"Corvane's coming," Oscar said.

She didn't know how she knew. The Thorn had been quiet for three days—Maren's lessons were working, or seemed to—and it sat beneath her breastbone like a banked coal, warm and patient. But this morning it had shifted, the way a sleeper shifts when someone opens the door of the room. Not afraid. *Aware.*

Tam lowered the strap. "How far?"

"A day. Maybe less." She turned her hand over and looked at her palm, where the mark lived now permanently, a thorned vine inked in no ink she could name. "It can feel him. It felt him before I did."

"That's cheerful."

"It's honest." She tried for a smile. "You always say honesty is the cheapest armor."

"I say that about jokes," Tam said. "Jokes are the cheapest armor. Honesty is expensive as hell, which is why so few people wear it." He stood, brushed off his knees, and looked down the long broken avenue of Ashfall toward the horizon, where the morning mist still hung in gray sheets over the drowned streets. "Right. Wake Maren. And then wake everyone else. If we're going to be murdered by a noble house in a ruin, I'd like the courtesy of being upright for it."

* * *

By midmorning the whole strange little company of them stood in the great sunken square before the buried archive: Oscar, Tam, Maren, Vane, and the scattering of smugglers and runaways who had drifted to them over the weeks like leaves catching in a hedge. Forty-two souls, give or take. Corvane was bringing six hundred.

"We don't fight," Maren said. Her voice carried the flat calm she used when the truth was too large for decoration. "We cannot win a battle. We are not trying to win a battle. We are trying to buy Oscar time to reach the vault."

"And after?" asked one of Tam's people, a wiry girl called Finch who had been rescued from a tithe wagon two years ago and had never quite stopped running. "After she does whatever she's going to do down there. What happens to us?"

Nobody answered. Oscar felt forty-two pairs of eyes find her without anyone moving, the way a congregation finds the altar.

"I don't know," she said. It cost her something to say it plainly, and she saw it cost the others something to hear it. "Either way, the world changes today. If I destroy the Thorn, magic dies everywhere—instantly, permanently. Every glowing lord goes dark. Every child born touched goes quiet. If I plant it—" She stopped. Swallowed. "If I plant it, everything changes and nobody knows what 'everything' means yet. Both roads go off the edge of the map. I'm sorry. I wish one of them were safe. They aren't."

Finch chewed on that. Then: "Well. Off the map is better than on Corvane's map."

There was a ragged murmur of agreement, and someone laughed—a short, startled bark of laughter, the kind people make when fear and hope collide—and Oscar thought, absurdly, of the archive at Dunlow, of dust motes in lamplight, of her father's hands folded on the mill table. Whatever happened in the next hour, she wanted it recorded somewhere. Someone should write it down. That was what scribes were for. Even if the only thing left to write on afterward was memory.

* * *

Corvane came at noon, under a banner that had once meant justice.

Oscar watched from the rim of the sunken square as the column emerged from the western road—six hundred soldiers in lacquered scale, their armor veined with inherited light so that the whole force moved like a river of slow green fire. Above them floated the house standard: a black key on white. House Corvane had held the keys of the kingdom's tithe-courts for nine generations. They opened things. They rarely closed them.

At the head rode Lord Aldous Corvane himself, and beside him, to her horror, rode Inquisitor Vane's former second, Brother Cassel, in temple grays—the church and the crown arrived together, as they always did when there was inheritance to protect.

"He'll parley first," Maren murmured. "He wants you willing. Blood given takes cleaner than blood taken. Old law."

"Everything he does is old law," Oscar said. "That's the whole problem with him."

Lord Corvane reined in at the far edge of the square, close enough that his voice reached them easily—it was amplified by some patient old spell woven into his gorget, a voice that had been talking down to commoners for so long it had forgotten any other register.

"Oscar Halloran!" it boomed, genial as a festival herald. "Scribe of Dunlow! I come not as your enemy but as your purchaser! Name your price—your father's debt, your freedom, a house, a title, lands in the green country—and the Thorn passes legally into my blood, and you walk away rich and whole! Refuse, and I am legally obliged to take it from your corpse, which I promise you neither of us will enjoy!"

"You enjoy everything," Tam shouted back. "I've seen your tax ledgers!"

A ripple went through the Corvane ranks. Somewhere in it, a soldier snorted and hastily disguised it as a cough.

"Master Tam." Corvane's voice cooled by several degrees, the warmth of a hearth banking down toward coals. "Your network sold itself to me once already. Shall I tell these good people what you traded?"

Oscar felt Tam go rigid beside her. She put her hand on his arm. "They know," she said quietly. "They've known since the road. You bought my life with your secrets, and they decided you were worth more than your sins. That's rather the point of everything we're doing, isn't it? People being worth more than ledgers."

Tam exhaled. "I keep forgetting you're a nice person," he said. "It's very disorienting."

Below, Corvane waited out the pause, then shrugged—a small, economical motion, the shrug of a man closing a ledger page.

"No bargain, then. So be it. Take the scribe alive. The rest—" he waved one gauntleted hand, casual as brushing crumbs, "—are outside the law. Do as you like."

The river of green fire began to move.

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles have lines and formations and some pretense of order. What followed was a breaking.

Maren had prepared, in her marsh-witch way, for exactly this: pits of soft ash under glamoured crusts, tangle-charms knotted into the ruins' doorframes, a dozen small cruelties of terrain that turned the Corvane charge into a stumbling flood. Tam's people fought the way smugglers fight—never standing, always fading, striking from doorways and dropping through collapsed floors, appearing behind a soldier just long enough to take his sword-arm's attention and vanish again. It was beautiful, in its desperate improvised way. It was also hopeless, and everyone knew it, and they did it anyway, because the only thing being purchased with their courage was minutes.

Oscar ran for the archive entrance with Vane and Maren flanking her.

Behind her, the square came apart. She heard Finch scream a war cry with laughter buried in it. She heard Chancellor—or possibly Vice-Chancellor—caw furiously from somewhere overhead, having apparently returned to watch the disaster. She heard the deep groan of thousand-year-old stone taking wounds it would not survive.

Then the light found her.

Not Corvane's men—not yet. It came up out of the ground itself, out of the seams of Ashfall, pale gold and ancient, and it rushed toward her like water finding a drain, like everything the valley had ever swallowed recognizing its heir. The Thorn woke fully for the first time, and Oscar dropped to her knees on the archive steps with her arms wrapped around herself, gasping, because it wasn't pain exactly. It was *recognition*. A hundred years of stolen magic pressing against the inside of her skin saying *here, here, here you are.*

"Don't let go of her!" Maren's voice, very far away. Hands on Oscar's shoulders. "Oscar. Look at me. Quiet the vine. Breathe with me—slow, slow, you're a door, not a window—"

"I'm a door," Oscar whispered. The words were almost funny through her chattering teeth. "I'm a door and there's a flood on the other side and the hinges are—"

Boots on stone. Green firelight spilling down the archive stair.

Vane met them alone at the top of the steps, and Oscar, curled and half-blind with light, heard the impossible sound of the Inquisitor drawing steel with a prayer on his lips—not a temple prayer, she realized dizzily, but something older, something he'd learned before the hollowing, back when he'd refused the Thorn and been punished for it. She heard steel ring on steel, twice, three times. She heard Vane grunt, and fall, and get up, which she would think about for the rest of her life, however long that proved to be.

Then hands seized her hair, wrenched her head back, and Lord Aldous Corvane looked into her face from a distance of six inches, his eyes full of green fire and appetite.

"There you are," he breathed. "Nine generations. Nine generations of ledgers and laws and patience, all for the seed in your chest. Hold still, little scribe. This will only ruin you."

His blade came around—not to kill, never to kill, a pommel-strike to stun, careful, *careful*, because damaged goods fetched less—

And Oscar Halloran did the last thing anyone expected, including herself.

She stopped holding the door shut.

It wasn't strategy. She would have liked to claim, later, that it was strategy—that she understood in a flash of genius that the vault below could channel the eruption, that timing was everything. But the truth was simpler and more human: she saw the blow coming, and she was tired, and angry, and grieving, and she thought of her father's bent back in the mills and Finch's laugh and Vane's grunt as he fell getting up, and the Thorn surged up to meet her anger like a dog feeling its master's leash slip, and she *let it.*

The world broke open.

* * *

Later—much later, in fragments—she would remember it in pieces, the way you remember lightning.

The pommel stopping a hand's width from her skull, frozen, trembling, as though the air itself had turned to glass.

The green fire guttering out of six hundred sets of armor at once, draining downward into the stones of Ashfall, into her, into the seed—nine generations of hoarded inheritance flowing home like a river remembering its bed. Soldiers crying out, clutching at suddenly dim metal, feeling for the first time in their lives the cold where the family fire had always been. Some of them wept. Some of them laughed. One man near the front pulled off his helmet, stared at his bare ordinary hands, and sat down in the ash and did not get up for a long while.

Corvane screaming—not rage, worse, *bargaining*—"Wait, wait, we can share it, we can draft terms—"

The vault doors below her feet opening, not with force but with relief, the way a jaw unclenches.

And the Thorn erupting upward through her, through the ruins, through the whole wounded skeleton of the old capital, wild magic flooding into a world that had spent a century believing magic was property. It came up out of every crack and grave and foundation-stone. It came up out of the mass graves of the drained. It came up green and gold and colorless, shapes without names, light that tasted like rain. It did not ask anyone's permission and it did not check anyone's bloodline. It landed on nobles and smugglers and crows alike, and Chancellor—definitely Chancellor—caught a mouthful of raw creation and turned briefly, gloriously, iridescent, before shaking it off in disgust.

Oscar stood in the middle of it, at the eye of the breaking world, and felt the blade enter her.

She barely registered it—one of the few soldiers whose power hadn't fully drained, panicking, striking blind at the source of the catastrophe, the point sliding under her ribs with terrible professional economy. She looked down at it almost curiously. Red, spreading across her scribe's smock. Of course. Ink. She had always known it would end in ink somehow.

The Thorn screamed.

Not in sound—in *everything*. The eruption doubled, tripled, grief and fury pouring out of her wound into the world, and the last thing she saw clearly was Corvane's banner tearing loose from its pole and burning without fire as it fell, and Tam's face somewhere in the chaos, white as paper, screaming her name—

* * *

She was walking.

She didn't remember starting. The battlefield was behind her—silent now, or perhaps her ears were simply past silence—and ahead lay the western road, the long road, the road home to a valley that had kept her safe and kept her prisoner and was, right now, at this very moment, beginning to crack along its borders as the Thorn's flood battered against a century-old cage.

The excess was in her. She could feel it—the overflow, the part of the eruption that had no place else to go, enough wild magic to level kingdoms, riding in her chest alongside the blade. If she fell here, in the open, it would spill. Everyone fleeing—Tam's people, Corvane's broken soldiers, the children, all of them streaming east away from Ashfall—would be caught in it.

But the valley could hold it. The valley was built to hold it. That was the joke at the bottom of everything, the one the universe had been telling at her expense for a hundred years: the prison was also a vessel, and she was the only one who knew the way in.

Her feet knew the road. Her blood was leaving her steadily, marking the way like a scribe's line.

Somewhere behind her, faint and frantic, she heard Tam shouting her name again, and Maren's voice rising over his, and she smiled, because of course they'd noticed. Of course they'd come after her. People being worth more than ledgers.

"Go back," she said softly, to no one, to everyone, and kept walking west with the Thorn blazing in her chest and the sunset bleeding red across a world where, as of today, magic belonged to no one.

Behind her, hoofbeats. Behind her, voices refusing, in the stubborn human way, to obey even the plainest instruction.

Ahead, the mountains, and the valley, and the border that was already beginning, high on the ridgeline, to burn like a curtain drawn aside.
