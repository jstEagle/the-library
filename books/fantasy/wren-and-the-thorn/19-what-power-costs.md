## 19. What Power Costs

Tam had spent his whole life learning how to read a crowd in danger, and the crowd pouring out of Ashfall's ruins was screaming all the wrong things.

Not *run*. Not *help*. They were shouting each other's names—mothers calling children, smugglers calling crew, a Corvane footman, of all people, calling for his horse—and beneath it all there was a sound Tam had never heard before and hoped never to catalog: magic, loose, unowned, singing through the air like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

The Thorn had erupted. He'd seen it happen from the ridge, seen Oscar go down under Corvane's men, seen the sky over the vault split open like wet paper. And now every drop of power that had been bottled in noble bloodlines for a century was coming untethered, and it wanted somewhere to go, and the someones standing between it and anywhere were mostly farmers.

"Tam!" Maren's voice cut through the din. She was half-carrying, half-dragging Oscar up the slope from the vault road, and even at forty paces Tam could see what was wrong. Oscar glowed. Not the pretty hearthlight glow of a well-bred mage. She burned like a lamp with its glass cracked—light leaking out at the seams, along her wrists, her throat, the corners of her eyes, as if she'd become too small for whatever she was holding.

"I've got her," Tam said, and took her weight.

She was lighter than she should have been. That frightened him more than anything else.

* * *

They got as far as the old toll house on the north road, where the crowds thinned and Maren finally let herself sag against the wall. Oscar lay on a table that had probably seen better centuries. The light under her skin pulsed in time with nothing—not her breath, not her heart. It pulsed in time with the bell-sound rolling across the valley behind them.

"She's dying," Maren said. No ceremony to it. Maren didn't waste ceremony; she'd told Tam once that she'd been raised in House Corvane and had developed an allergy to it. "The Thorn is defending her. That eruption—that was it spending everything it had to keep her alive. And now the excess has nowhere to go. It'll burn her out from inside by morning unless it finds a vessel."

"A vessel." Tam wiped blood off Oscar's forehead with his sleeve. It came away faintly luminous. "Meaning another person."

"Meaning anyone who takes it into themselves. Or—" Maren stopped. Tam watched her look north, toward the border marshes, and he watched her face do something complicated.

"No," he said.

"The valley's null field is collapsing. Vane's people broke it during the battle—I felt it go. But it isn't gone gone. There's still a hunger in it. A hundred years of eating magic, and it hasn't lost the appetite." She turned back to him. "If someone carried the excess into what's left of the null zone, the valley would drink it. All of it. Every last spark."

"And the carrier?"

Maren said nothing, which was an answer, which was worse than any answer she could have said out loud.

Oscar opened her eyes.

It shouldn't have been possible. Tam had seen enough dying people to know what dying looked like, and this wasn't it, exactly—it was worse, because she looked calm. Scribes, he'd learned, had a particular kind of calm. His mother had worked for a records-house in Corvane City, copying bills of sale, and she used to say the scribes were the only people in the building who slept at night, because they knew exactly what everything cost.

"How much did you hear?" Tam asked her, uselessly, because she'd heard all of it.

"All of it." Her voice was rough but steady. "You're both doing that thing where you talk about me in front of me. I hate that thing."

"We noticed," Maren said dryly.

Oscar tried to sit up. Tam pushed her down, gently, and she let him, which scared him more than the glowing did. Oscar Halloran did not let people push her down. He'd known her three months and she hadn't once let anybody push her down about anything.

"It doesn't have to be you," he said.

"Yes, it does." She said it the way she read aloud from ledgers. Factually. "The Thorn chose me when I read the prophecy. Whatever else it is, it's mine. I'm not handing my debts to somebody else. I spent my whole life watching my father do that in the other direction—letting the temple take his labor so his daughter wouldn't have to pay. Look what it made of him." She smiled, and there it was, the gentle crooked humor that had gotten them through the tithe wagon and Corvane's dungeons and the long grey road to Ashfall. "Besides, I'm the fastest walker of the three of us. Ask anyone."

"You're not walking anywhere," Tam said. "I'm carrying you."

"That's sweet. You can carry me to the border. Then I walk in alone."

"Maren." He turned to the hedge-witch. "Tell her there's another way. You're the one who knows things. Tell her."

Maren crouched beside the table and took Oscar's hand, and for a moment Tam saw the two of them framed against the burning sky—the exiled daughter who had fled her inheritance, and the debtor's child who'd had hers thrust upon her—and he understood that there was no argument left in the world that would change what happened next. Some choices get made long before the moment arrives. This one had been made in a dusty archive, a hundred years ago, by whoever wrote tomorrow's date in a book and left it for a girl to find.

"There might be another way," Maren said slowly. "But it would need a bearer strong enough to hold the excess while we found it a home. Weeks. Maybe months. And Oscar has hours." She looked up at Tam. "You know I'd lie to her if lying would help."

"I know," Oscar said. "That's why I trust you."

* * *

They walked north through the refugee columns, and Tam carried her, and it was the longest mile of his life.

He talked the whole way, because Oscar was fading in and out and he'd decided—if you could call it deciding; it was more like a reflex, like flinching—that if she slipped away in silence he'd never forgive himself, whereas if she slipped away mid-argument she'd come back just to correct him. He told her about the first run he'd ever made, age fourteen, moving a magic-touched miller's boy over the Corvane border in a turnip cart. He told her about the worst night of the smuggling years, the one he'd never told anyone, the night he'd left a family behind because the wagon couldn't stop and he still heard the mother's voice in his sleep. He told her about his mother's scribes, about how he'd always thought record-keeping was the most cowardly trade going until he met one who'd copied out a forbidden prophecy by lamplight knowing full well it would burn itself into her arm.

"You thought I was brave," Oscar murmured, from somewhere near his collarbone.

"I thought you were insane."

"Both, probably. Tam?"

"Yeah?"

"When we met, you told me I smelled like a library."

"You did. Dusty and disapproving."

"And you still carried me out of the tithe yard."

"You were unconscious. Carrying unconscious people is basically free."

Her laugh was small and crackling, literally crackling, sparks jumping off her lips, and Tam held her tighter and kept walking and did not let his voice shake. "What are the terms?" she asked.

"What?"

"My debt. If I'm walking into the valley to pay it off, I want the terms read to me properly. You're the honest criminal. Read me the terms."

Tam thought about it. Really thought about it, the way you think about a contract when someone's life is the collateral. "Terms are as follows," he said. "One: you pay your own debts, no one else's, which you're doing. Two: nobody ordered this payment—you chose it, freely, which makes it not a sacrifice, it makes it a settlement. Three—" his voice did shake here, and he let it, "three: the creditor is a hole in the world, and holes don't get to keep what they take forever. Someday the valley opens. Someday everything it swallowed comes back out. So really, you're not paying the valley. You're lending to it. And I intend to collect."

"That," Oscar said, "is the worst contract law I've ever heard."

"I never claimed to be a scribe."

"No," she said softly. "You claimed to be a friend. Terms accepted."

* * *

The border, when they reached it, was a ruin you could see.

For a hundred years the edge of Dunlow Valley had been invisible—a line where birds went quiet and hedges grew strange. Now it stood exposed like the skeleton of a whale: a shimmering wall of absence running from hill to hill, flickering, guttering, full of gaps where the battle's wild magic had torn it. Through the gaps Tam could see the valley beyond—the mills, the temple spire, Debtor's Row, the little grey houses where people had lived their whole lives without a single spark of wonder, believing that was simply what the world was.

People were fleeing out of those gaps. Temple folk, mill families, scribes with their ledgers strapped to their backs, staring in horror at the burning sky they'd never known existed above their heads.

And the surge was following them. Tam could feel it now that he knew to feel—Oscar had taught him, in the weeks on the road, how to notice magic the way you notice weather. The excess boiling off the Thorn was flowing downhill after the crowds like floodwater after a village, drawn to every living thing, hungry to fill empty vessels whether the vessels wanted filling or not. Unclaimed magic poured into unwilling people. He'd seen what that did, back at the vault road. It didn't make mages. It made wreckage.

"It'll reach the crowds in minutes," Maren said. "The null zone will drink it if you can get inside far enough. Past the third marker stone. Deeper than anyone's ever gone and come back."

"Then I'd best be going," Oscar said, in Tam's arms, "because I've never been much good at coming back anyway."

He set her down. Her feet held, barely. She was nearly transparent with light now, a person-shaped lantern, and she looked at the valley she'd been born in and bound to and fled from, and Tam watched her say goodbye to it without saying a word, because that was Oscar—she put things in ledgers other people couldn't read.

"Hey," he said. "Halloran."

She turned.

"The turnip cart," he said. "Year I was fourteen. The miller's boy cried the whole way and I gave him my coat and he threw up on it and I have never once regretted it. That's the whole speech. Go on."

"You're a terrible orator," she said, tears cutting bright channels through the light on her face, smiling anyway. "Wren would be ashamed of you."

"Wren," Tam said, "would write it down better than either of us could say it. Go."

And she went.

* * *

He meant to watch until she passed the third marker stone. He lasted until she passed the first.

The null zone took her the way a river takes a dropped coin—one moment visible, the next absorbed, the light of her sinking into the grey air of the valley and spreading outward, outward, a slow bloom of borrowed dawn rolling across the mills and the fields and Debtor's Row. The surge chasing the refugees hit the border and stopped, caught, drunk down, gallon upon impossible gallon of wild power vanishing into the hunger of the place that had eaten a century of magic and still said *more*.

Behind Tam, the crowds stopped screaming. He heard a woman say, wonder-struck, "the lights went out," and a man answer, "no—look—they went *in*."

Then the border shuddered. The whole shimmering wall groaned like an old ship in a storm, and Tam, who understood borders and crossings and the price of passage better than any man alive, understood in one cold flash what was happening: the valley, glutted at last, was letting go. The null field wasn't collapsing anymore. It was dissolving. Coming down like a debt called due.

And Oscar was inside it.

"No," Tam said, and ran.

He heard Maren curse behind him—he'd never heard her curse like that, in old Corvane tongue, the language of the houses she'd renounced—and then her footsteps pounding after him, and together they went through the gap in the dying border, into Dunlow Valley, where no magic had lived for a hundred years and where Tam had once sworn, loudly, over drinks, that he would never set foot because nothing there was worth stealing and no one there needed saving.

He'd been wrong twice.

They found her at the second marker stone, face-down in the frost-killed grass, dark and silent and ordinary-looking for the first time since the vault. The light was gone out of her. Tam rolled her over with hands that wouldn't behave, and Maren pressed two fingers to her throat, and closed her eyes, and said, "She's breathing."

"Barely."

"Barely counts." Maren's voice cracked clean down the middle, the mentor mask slipping off entirely, and underneath it was just a woman who had buried too many people and refused to bury this one. "Help me lift her. We're getting her out before the border finishes falling, and if you argue with me I'll hex you with whatever's left in these old bones."

Together they lifted her between them—Tam under her shoulders, Maren under her knees—and carried her back through the flickering remains of the wall, past the first marker stone, out of the valley that had taken her payment and was, even now, opening its long-shut gates behind them.

The last thing Tam saw, looking back over his shoulder, was the temple spire catching the first true sunrise Dunlow had seen in a hundred years.

"Hold on," he told the weight in his arms. "Debt's paid. Now you owe me a conversation. And I collect."

Far away, deep in the dissolving dark, something that might have been a heartbeat—or might have been a seed, settling into new soil—answered.
