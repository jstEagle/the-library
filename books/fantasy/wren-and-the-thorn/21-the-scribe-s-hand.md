## 21. The Scribe's Hand

The scar on my forearm itches when it rains.

It has been eight months since the Thorn woke, and the mark that once burned when I lied is now only a pale silver branching across my skin, like frost on a windowpane, like a river seen from very high up. The healers of Dunlow — my Dunlow, which is no longer magicless and no longer quite mine — say the scarring runs deeper than skin. They say it the way they say everything now: with wonder in their voices and fear underneath, because half of them had never seen a wound made by raw magic before, and the other half had never seen one *survived*.

I have learned to like the itching. It reminds me I am alive, and that my body, for all its failures, still keeps its own records.

My hands, though. My hands are the real archive of what happened.

The left one aches in cold weather where Corvane's chains bit into the wrist. The right one trembles sometimes, late at night, when I try to write and remember instead that these same fingers held the Thorn as it tore itself out of the world's old order and scattered magic like a farmer sowing barley. Both of them are stiff in the mornings now, and both of them are, slowly, learning their trade again.

Because I am writing. Of course I am writing. It is the only prayer I know.

* * *

The new archive lives in what used to be the tithe-house.

There is an irony there that Maren appreciates far more than I do. She stands in the doorway most mornings with her tea, watching the carts arrive — not grain carts now, not wagons hauling children to blood-harvest, but carts of paper. Reams and reams of it, donated by houses that once measured their wealth in glowing heirlooms and are only now learning what paper costs when nobody can conjure more of it out of the air. House Corvane sent the first shipment. Lord Corvane did not sign the letter that accompanied it. His steward did. Some apologies are too large for a signature; they arrive as pallets of blank pages instead, and you accept them because the pages are needed, and because refusing them would be its own kind of theft.

"Careful with that corner," I called out this morning, as two volunteers wrestled a crate through the doors. "That's the good rag paper."

"Everything's the good rag paper," said Tam, who was leaning against the doorframe on the other side, eating an apple he had absolutely not paid for. "You've become a snob about stationery, Oscar Halloran. I've watched you turn away a shipment because it smelled of smoke."

"It *did* smell of smoke. Smoke means damp storage means foxed pages means testimony rotting before anyone reads it." I set down my pen long enough to glare at him properly. "These aren't prophecies, Tam. Prophecies keep. Testimony doesn't. It has to be read while the people who lived it are still alive to be believed."

He looked at me for a moment over the apple, and his grin went softer at the edges, the way it does when he forgets to perform being careless. "And here I thought you'd gotten boring," he said, and stole a second apple from the crate on his way past, because some natures even the fall of noble houses cannot amend.

Tam is not what he was either. The smuggling network dissolved itself, mostly — when magic became something anyone could learn, the traffic in unlicensed children simply ran out of road. There was nothing left to smuggle them *from*. He spent a bitter fortnight insisting he was unemployed before Maren pointed out, drily, that a man who knows every hidden road between here and the coast might be useful to a kingdom trying to rebuild its roads honestly. Now he runs messages and supplies for the new council, and complains about it constantly, and would die before admitting he sleeps better than he has in years.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Mornings are for cataloguing. Let me tell you what we are building, because I want it written down somewhere other than my own head, in case my own head proves less durable than I hope.

* * *

We call it the Book of Witness, though officially it has no name yet. The council argued about naming conventions for three weeks — the council argues about everything for three weeks; I believe it is in the charter — while I quietly began shelving volumes under plain numbers so the work wouldn't wait on the politics.

The premise is simple, and it took me months to trust its simplicity. We do not record the future. We record what happened.

Every person who comes to Dunlow may give testimony. What they saw during the war of the houses. What was taken from their families — land, magic, children, names. What was done to them in the valley while the null field hummed and the rest of the kingdom forgot we existed. What they did themselves, if they can bear to say it; several former Inquisitors have come, gray-faced, and given accounts that made the transcription clerks go quiet, and I have entered every word without comment, because that is the rule I made and the rule I will keep: *the archive does not judge. It remembers.*

Judgment belongs to courts and to time. Memory belongs to everyone.

It was Vane, oddly enough, who taught me the distinction. He came to the archive in the spring — walking with a cane now, the temple's hollowing having left him frail as winter paper — and asked to see my procedures. I expected mockery. Instead he read through my guidelines twice, corrected three inconsistencies in my citation format, and then sat for a long time with his hands folded on his knees.

"You understand what you've done," he said finally. "The temple kept prophecy because prophecy could be controlled. A hundred years, and only ever one copy, only ever readers the temple chose. But testimony—" He shook his head. "Testimony breeds. Every witness makes more witnesses. You've built something that cannot be owned."

"I built shelves," I said.

"Yes," he agreed, with the ghost of the polite menace that once made my blood run cold, repurposed now into something almost like humor. "So did every revolution. Most of them burn theirs within a decade. Don't let yours be flammable." And he gave his own deposition, four hours of it, everything the temple knew about the vault beneath Ashfall, and left before I could decide how I felt about any of it.

His testimony is Volume Three, Sections Eleven through Forty. I checked the binding myself. It will hold.

* * *

My father came to visit in the autumn.

I should explain about the debt first, because the debt was the spine of my old life and I have had to learn to stand without it. When the Thorn woke and the null field collapsed, every contract written against valley labor came up for review — and then for annulment, when the new council ruled that debts contracted under a century of enforced helplessness were not debts but leashes. The Halloran obligation to the temple mills was struck from the rolls on a gray Tuesday morning. My father stood in the mill yard while a clerk read the proclamation, and according to Tam, who was there delivering supplies and claims to have been merely passing by, my father did not cheer or weep. He simply took off his work gloves, folded them, and put them in his pocket, as though setting down a tool at the end of a shift.

Then he walked forty miles to find me, because apparently that is where I get my stubbornness.

He arrived at the archive on a day I was elbow-deep in a misfiled crate, ink to the elbows literally as well as figuratively, arguing with a volunteer about chronological ordering. I heard his voice before I saw him — that low, worn-down voice I had spent years hearing only through mill-fence conversations, asking a question at the desk — and I came around the shelving too fast and knocked over a stool, and there he was.

Smaller than I remembered. Grayer. But standing straighter, somehow, without the debt bent inside him like a rod.

"Papa," I said, brilliantly. Twenty-eight years old, survivor of the Thorn and the war of the houses, and my entire vocabulary deserted me.

He looked at my scarred arm, and my ink-stained hands, and the shelves behind me stretching back into the dark full of ordinary human voices, and he said, "Your mother always said you'd end up surrounded by books. She didn't think you'd build the library first, though."

We talked until the lamps burned low. About the mills, about the valley waking up green and strange and crackling at its edges with new wild magic that the farmers don't know whether to curse or court. About my mother, whom I had not spoken of aloud in years, and whom the debt had silenced in our house as surely as the null field silenced everything else. He told me things he had never been free to tell me, small things, terrible ordinary things — how he had sold our family's one heirloom, a grandmother's ring with no magic in it at all, just love and gold, to buy me my first slate. How he had applied three times to have my indenture transferred somewhere kinder and been refused each time by men whose names he remembered with perfect, patient accuracy.

I offered to enter all of it in the archive. He considered it seriously, my father, turning it over the way he turned over everything.

"Not yet," he said. "Maybe next year. Right now I'd like to just be your father visiting, instead of a source." And I understood, because I had spent a hundred days being the vessel of a seed of primal magic and what I had wanted, the whole time, was for someone to ask me how I slept.

He stayed a week. On his last evening he inspected my handiwork — the ledgers, the transcriptions, the careful plain numbering — and pronounced, in the tone he had once reserved for well-plowed furrows: "Straight rows. Good soil." I have received finer praise from councils and kings-in-all-but-name since the world changed. None of it warmed me like that.

* * *

Some nights I still dream of the vault.

Not the horror dreams — those come rarely now, and Maren taught me the quieting before she left for the coast, so the dreams arrive gentler, more like memories than hauntings. But sometimes I stand again in the heart-mechanism beneath Ashfall, in the place where I held destruction in one hand and planting in the other, and I feel again the exact weight of that choice, and I wake with my heart going like a mill-hammer and lie in the dark asking the ceiling whether I chose rightly.

I planted it. You know this; everyone knows this; the world outside my window glows faintly at dusk with a thousand small learnings, hedge-craft and hearth-sparks and the first stumbling cantrips of children who will never be property. Magic untethered, uninheritable, wild as weeds and nearly as hard to kill. I planted the Thorn in the vault soil and let the eruption carry it everywhere, and then I carried its excess into the collapsing border of the valley and let the null zone drink the surge that would have burned the fleeing crowds to ash.

Mostly powerless, the healers called what remains in me. Mostly. There is a little left, a residue, and it does one thing only: when I lay my scarred hand flat on a page of testimony, I can tell whether the witness is telling the truth. Not the whole truth — truth is layered, and people believe their layers sincerely — but the core of it, the load-bearing beam. The Thorn's last courtesy, or its last joke. The scribe who cannot be lied to.

I use it sparingly. Truth, Maren warned me, is a tool that cuts its holder too, if gripped wrong. But I use it. Because an archive that can be poisoned is worse than no archive at all, and because somewhere in these volumes, someday, someone will bring me a testimony that matters enormously, and I will need to know.

I did not expect that someone to be a child. But I am getting ahead of myself again, and it is raining, and the scar is itching, and there is one more thing I must write down before the lamp gutters.

* * *

Maren writes, sometimes. From the coast, where she is teaching — renounced daughter of House Corvane turned schoolmistress, teaching farmwives and fishermen the craft their great-grandmothers were drained to feed. Her letters arrive smelling of salt and are exactly as dry and devastating as she is. The latest one sits open on my desk:

*"You asked whether renouncing power counts as using it. Yes. That is the lesson and it is the cruelest one. Every refusal shapes the world as surely as every seizure — it just shapes it slower, and nobody writes songs about it. Keep your archive anyway. Slow shaping is still shaping. — M."*

Below it, in her smaller hand: *"P.S. Tell Tam his routes are sloppy. I found three of them blindfolded."*

I showed the postscript to Tam. He spent the rest of the afternoon loudly planning a fourth route specifically to spite her, and then spent the evening actually drawing it, carefully, tongue between his teeth. They write to each other more than either of them writes to me, I suspect. I have decided to find this charming rather than conspiratorial, mostly because I have no evidence and no desire to gather any.

Here is what I know, on this rain-softened evening, with the itch in my arm and the lamp burning steady: the world is not fixed. I spent twenty-eight years believing it was — that debt was bedrock, that bloodlines were law, that a girl born in a dead valley would die in a dead valley with her father's ledger balanced and nothing else. Then I read one page I should not have, and everything I believed was proven to be a mechanism, and mechanisms can be rebuilt.

Justice, I wrote in the archive's founding preface, is not a treasure to be seized. It is a garden. It wants tending. It wants straight rows and honest soil and someone willing to pull weeds in the rain without applause. I stole that image from my father's fields and I am not sorry; the best ideas in the new kingdom are stolen from common places.

The garden is young. Some days it looks like nothing at all — just shelves, just numbers, just tired people saying true things into the quiet while I write them down. But last week a woman from the eastern farms testified about the tithe wagon that took her brother, forty years ago, and her granddaughter listened from the public benches with her mouth open, and afterward the girl asked me, shyly, whether ordinary people's stories really got kept forever now.

"As long as the roof holds," I said, "and longer than that if the roof doesn't. We'll rewrite them as many times as it takes."

She nodded gravely, the way children nod at important contracts, and went home, and I sat for a while in the lamplight feeling something I had almost forgotten the shape of.

Hope. Quiet, unspectacular, tended-daily hope. Not the blazing certainty of the night the Thorn woke — nothing about that night was quiet, and I bear the scars to inventory it. This is the other kind. The kind that gets up in the morning. The kind that files things correctly.

Tomorrow there are three crates of rag paper to shelve, a deposition from a reformed Inquisitor to transcribe, and a letter to answer from a schoolmistress who is probably right about everything. Tonight there is rain, and an itching scar, and a pen that fits my hand the way it always has, scar tissue and all.

I dipped the nib, and began the evening's entry the way I begin them all now, plainly, in a hand that shakes only a little:

*"Today, the eleventh of Thaw-month, the following persons gave witness..."*

Outside, far off toward the archive doors, I heard footsteps — small ones, light ones, the quick patter of a child running errands after dark, which the streets of Dunlow permit again and which still makes me smile every single time.

I did not look up. There was ink to lay down straight, and the garden does not tend itself.

But the footsteps stopped at my door. And knocked.
