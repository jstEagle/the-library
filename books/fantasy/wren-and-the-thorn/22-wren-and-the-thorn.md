## 22. Wren and the Thorn

The rebuilt archive smelled of cedar shavings and fresh ink, which Oscar had decided was the correct smell for a place where truth was kept. The old archive had smelled of dust and secrets, and she had learned the hard way that those two things were not the same.

She was fifty-one now, or thereabouts—the years after Ashfall had blurred together like a page left out in rain—and her knees had opinions about stairs. But she still climbed them every morning, because the new archive of Dunlow sat above the temple proper rather than beneath it, and she had insisted on that detail with a stubbornness that three successive temple heads had eventually stopped arguing about.

"Nothing true should live in a basement," she'd told them. "Truths in basements grow strange."

The morning light came through tall windows now, unshuttered, because the archive had no restricted section anymore. That had been the hardest fight of her life—harder than Ashfall, harder than the vault, harder even than the long gray year when she hadn't known if Tam would live—and she had won it not by argument but by simply refusing to build the shelves. She had stacked the lumber in the corner where it remained to this day, a monument to shelving never built, and children used it as a climbing frame, which she privately considered its highest purpose.

Oscar was cataloguing—a scribe's habit dies hard, if it dies at all—when she heard the small feet on the stairs.

She knew before she turned around who it would be. There were perhaps forty children in Dunlow under the age of ten, and only one of them climbed the archive stairs the way Wren did: too fast, slightly sideways, with the breathless urgency of someone pursued by an idea.

"Wren Halloran-Ashe," Oscar said without looking up from her ledger. "You are supposed to be at lessons."

"Lessons ended." Wren appeared at her elbow, eight years old, gap-toothed, with her mother's dark curls and what everyone agreed was Oscar's own unfortunate talent for finding things that were meant to stay found. "Mistress Ilsa said we could go. She said it *loudly*, so it counts."

"It always counts," Oscar agreed gravely. "That's the trouble with saying things loudly."

Wren leaned over the ledger, hair falling across Oscar's arm. "What's that one?"

"A record of grain shipments from the year you were born. Thrilling stuff. Forty barrels of barley, two disputes about a fence line, one wedding."

"You wrote down a *wedding*?"

"I write down everything now. That was the whole idea." Oscar set down the pen. It was true, and she liked saying true things; they sat easily in her mouth these days in a way they never had when she was young. "A kingdom that forgets its weddings is as badly served as one that forgets its wars. Possibly worse. Wars make better stories, so people remember them anyway."

Wren considered this with the ferocious seriousness of an eight-year-old being handed actual philosophy before breakfast. Then her eye caught on something behind Oscar, and everything else fell out of her face.

"The new book," she whispered.

*Ah,* thought Oscar. *So it's today.*

* * *

The book sat on the reading table by the east window, exactly where it had appeared four days ago, which is to say: it had not appeared at all. It had simply been there one morning, the way the first book had been there a hundred years ago in a locked archive that no living hand had opened. Some things, Oscar had concluded, did not arrive. They *ripened.*

It was bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, and it bore no title. When Oscar had opened it—because of course she had opened it; she was fifty-one, not dead—she had found pages of clean paper and a single inscription on the first leaf, in a hand that was not hers but felt uncomfortably familiar anyway, the way a stranger's laugh can feel familiar:

*A date. A name.*

The date was eleven years hence. The name was **Wren**.

Below it, in smaller letters, the old formula, unchanged across a century of terrible history:

*Whoever reads it carries it.*

Oscar had closed the book, sat very still for a long time, and then done something the young woman she had been would have found unthinkable: nothing. She had not copied the page. She had not hidden the book. She had not fled into marshes or consulted hedge-witches or started composing her will. She had made tea, drunk it while it was still hot for once in her life, and then walked to the schoolhouse to have a quiet word with Mistress Ilsa about which children might be ready, someday, for a conversation about choices.

And then she had waited, knowing—with the certainty of someone who has read a prophecy about herself—that Wren would find it. Not because fate dragged children up archive stairs. Because Wren was Wren.

Now the child stood before the green book the way people stand at the edge of cold water.

"Is it real?" Wren asked. "Tam says books don't just *happen*. He says everything comes from somewhere and somebody, and if you can't find the somebody you haven't looked hard enough."

"Tam is usually right," Oscar said, "and I have looked very hard indeed. Come here. Sit."

Wren sat, folding her legs under her on the chair in the way that drove Mistress Ilsa to despair. Oscar pulled her own chair around so they faced each other across the corner of the table, the book between them like a fourth guest.

"Do you know what this archive used to be?" Oscar asked.

"Grandmamaren says it was a cage with books in it."

Your grandmother, Oscar thought, has never once softened a story in her life, and the world is better for it. "Your grandmother is correct. There was a room underneath this one, sealed, that only certain people could enter, and the rule wasn't *are you wise enough* or *are you kind enough*. The rule was *were you born to the right people*. Everything in that room was power, and power belonged to bloodlines, like a cow or a field." She tapped the table. "I grew up believing that was simply how the world worked. The way weather works. You don't argue with weather."

"What changed?"

Oscar smiled. "I read a book I shouldn't have."

"That's why we're not allowed to touch things without asking?"

"That is one reason among several thousand, yes." She turned the green book gently, so its cover faced Wren. "But here's the part that matters. Nobody made me read it. Nobody forced my hand open. I chose, because I was curious, and curiosity is not a crime—it's the best thing any of us have—but choices have weight. That's not a punishment, Wren. It's just physics. The magic kind."

Wren's fingers hovered over the cover without touching it. "There's a name inside. I saw it when you opened it last week. I wasn't spying."

"I know you weren't. You were standing directly behind me humming, which is the opposite of spying. Spies hum *quieter*."

The girl giggled, then sobered again with the whiplash speed of childhood. "Is it my name? Really?"

"It's your name."

"And a date?"

"A date eleven years from now."

Wren frowned. "That doesn't make sense. How could a book know?"

"How could it know?" Oscar leaned back. "Child, I have spent thirty years trying to answer that question, and I'll give you everything I've got, free of charge, because that's the other thing this archive does now—everything free, everything in the open, everything argued about in public where anyone can shout at it. Here's what I know." She held up one scarred finger. Her hands were mapped with old marks now, faded script ghosts that surfaced in cold weather like old bruises; the Thorn's writing had never entirely left her skin, though the Thorn itself had gone wherever seeds go. "One: the world is older than our explanations of it, and some of those explanations were written by people who wanted things. Two: prophecies are less like instructions and more like doors. They don't tell you where to go. They just stop being shut. Three—" she hesitated, because the third thing was the expensive one— "three: whoever reads it carries it. And carrying is not the same as keeping. I carried mine for two years, and it nearly hollowed me out, and in the end I gave it away with both hands and it cost me—"

She stopped. Outside the window, Dunlow went about its morning: carts on the lane, the mill turning where her father had broken his back for debts that no longer existed, and beyond the fields the hills where the border had been, green and ordinary now, holding nothing in and nothing out. Somewhere out there, magic moved through the world like weather through an open country—learnable, teachable, belonging to whoever put in the years to learn it, the way music belonged to musicians and bread to bakers and no one at all. Noble houses still existed. Some of them were even decent. But their glow was gone, their inheritance spent and scattered the day a dying scribe had walked into a collapsing null-field carrying more power than any bloodline had ever held, and let the valley drink it.

Her father had lived to see it. That remained the single fact she treasured most, the coin she kept in the pocket of her heart: he had lived six years past that night, working the mills by choice instead of debt, and he had died in his bed with his daughter's hand in his, in a valley that was no longer a prison. Whatever else the Thorn had cost—and the ledger was long, oh, it was long—there was that line, entered in black ink, balanced.

"—it cost me," Oscar finished quietly, "more than I had, and less than I feared. Both things were true. Most true things come in pairs like that. You get used to it."

Wren was watching her with eyes far too old for the face, the way children do right before they say the thing that undoes you. "You're scared for me," she said.

"Yes," Oscar said, because lying had burned her skin once, and she had never taken it up again. "Terrified. Every day since I read that name. I've had eleven years' worth of terror saved up in advance and it hasn't even happened yet, which strikes me as poor planning on the universe's part."

"But you didn't hide the book."

"No."

"Why not?"

*There it is.* Oscar took a breath. "Because hiding it wouldn't keep you safe. It would only keep you ignorant, and ignorance isn't safety, it's just fear wearing a sensible coat. Because the old world hid things from children like you, and called it protection, and built a prison around a whole valley while it did. And because—" her voice caught, and she let it, because the archive had no restricted section and neither, anymore, did her heart— "because a very brave man once told me that the difference between an inheritance and a trap is whether anyone asked you first. Tam said that. He stole children out of tithe wagons because nobody had asked them, nobody had given them the choice, and he believed—I believe—that the single most sacred thing one person can give another is a real choice, with the whole truth attached, and no hand on the scale."

She slid the green book across the table until it rested under Wren's hovering hand.

"So here is your choice, Wren Halloran-Ashe, and I swear to you on this archive and everyone in it that there is no wrong answer. Open it, and you carry whatever's inside, and we will carry it with you—all of us, openly, in the daylight, with arguments and tea and probably shouting. Close it, and you walk out of here and play in the yard, and the book will wait for you or for someone else, and either way the world will not end today." She smiled, and meant it, and let the smile carry the fear alongside it, paired truths, as ever. "Eleven years is a long time. I was seventeen when I read mine, and I would have given a great deal for one honest adult to say: *you may also say no.* So I'm saying it. To you. Thirty years early. No is a complete sentence, and it is yours."

Wren looked at the book for a long moment.

Then, with the terrifying practicality of the young, she looked at Oscar instead.

"What's inside it? Do you know?"

"I don't. I looked. Blank pages, except for your name and the date. Which means whatever it holds hasn't been written yet—or it's waiting to be written by whoever reads it. The scholars argue about which. Your grandmother says it's both, and then she makes a face like she's bitten a lemon, which means she knows and isn't telling."

"Grandmamaren is *very* mysterious."

"Your grandmother is a former noblewoman who lives in a marsh and teaches frogs to answer to their names. Mysterious is the polite word."

Wren laughed—a bright, unguarded sound that bounced off the cedar shelves and came back warmer—and Oscar thought, not for the first time: *whatever comes, at least she will not meet it alone in a basement. At least she will meet it in the light, with people.*

The child put one finger on the cover. The leather was warm, Oscar noticed. It was always warm.

"Can I think about it?" Wren asked.

Oscar blinked. In a century of prophecies, across every account she had gathered from every land, no reader had ever asked that question. Vane hadn't. She herself hadn't. Even Maren, fleeing her inheritance, had run *from* the choice rather than sitting down beside it.

Every cycle, it seemed, could be changed. Even the smallest ones. Especially the smallest ones.

"Of course you can think about it," Oscar said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, and she did not care. "Think for a day. Think for eleven years. The book isn't going anywhere, and neither am I. I'll be right here, arguing with grain ledgers and drinking tea that's gone cold, same as every day."

Wren slid off the chair, paused, and then—quickly, the way children do when affection embarrasses them—threw her arms around Oscar's neck and squeezed.

"You're the best keeper," she announced into her shoulder. "Even if you write down boring weddings."

"Especially because of the weddings," Oscar managed. "Go on. Yard. Before Mistress Ilsa sends someone after you."

Small feet pounded down the stairs, sideways and too fast, chased by laughter. Oscar listened until the sound faded into the ordinary morning noise of a valley that had forgotten how to be a cage.

Then she looked at the green book, warm and patient on the table, blank pages holding a future that hadn't decided itself yet.

"Not yet," she told it, conversationally, the way she talked to all books now. "She's eight. Let her be eight."

The book said nothing, which was, Oscar reflected, the most encouraging thing it could possibly have done.

Outside, Wren's laughter rose up from the yard, wild and uninheritable, belonging to no one and everyone—and Oscar Halloran picked up her pen, turned to a fresh page, and began, as she did every morning, to write down the truth.
