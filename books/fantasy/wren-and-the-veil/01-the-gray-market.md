## 1. The Gray Market

The trick to stealing a memory is to be boring.

That's not something they teach you in the trade—there's no guild hall, no apprenticeship, no kindly old fence taking a waif under her wing. You learn it the way I learned everything: by starving until you figured it out. People guard their valuables. They guard their coin, their knives, their daughters' honor. Nobody guards the dull parts. Nobody thinks twice about the man who asks them what the weather was like on the march north from Harrow's Crossing, or whether army bread was really as bad as everyone said. Boring questions get honest answers, and honest answers are doors left unlocked.

So there I was at the Drowned Lantern, third pint deep in a conversation with a soldier named Aldric Vey, being the most forgettable man in the valley.

"Two years on the border," he was saying, gesturing with a mug that sloshed amber down his wrist. He'd stopped noticing where it went around the second pint. "You can't imagine it. The mud. The cold gets into your bones and stays, like a tenant who won't pay."

"Sounds miserable," I said, and meant it, which helped. Sympathy loosens people like heat loosens a jar lid.

"Miserable." He laughed, wet and sudden. "It was the best thing that ever happened to me. Can you believe that? Two years of freezing and digging latrines and watching men I ate breakfast with stop breathing—and I've never felt more *alive*. Out there, every day mattered." His face did something complicated. "Here nothing does."

I kept my hand loose around my own mug, fingers brushing the rim where I'd chalked the sigil an hour earlier, invisible unless you knew to look. The Veil doesn't allow magic—that's its one commandment, carved into every gatepost and preached by every gray-robed warden at every checkpoint. No spells. No enchantments. Nothing that glows or hums or bends the world out of true. What the wardens don't advertise, because it would collapse their entire economy inside of a week, is that the rule has a loophole shaped like a person. Magic can't follow you in. But memory isn't quite magic. Memory is just the shape experience leaves behind, and if you're born with the knack—the way some people are born double-jointed—you can reach into that shape and lift the whole thing out like a plum from a pie.

The sigil wasn't magic, strictly speaking. It was more like... a hook. A question asked in the only language forgetting understands.

Aldric leaned closer, confiding now, the way they all do when the ale hits the loneliness. "And here's the thing nobody tells you about coming home. They give you your discharge pay and your parade and then—" He snapped his fingers. "Nothing. You sit in a room that used to be yours and you realize the war took up all the space where a life should go."

"That's terrible," I said softly.

"It's unbearable, is what it is." His eyes were shining, and not from the drink alone. "Some nights I'd give anything to set it down. Just for a while. Just long enough to sleep."

There it is. There's always a there-it-is. Every mark eventually hands you the knife and thanks you for it.

I turned my mug a quarter-turn, the chalk sigil catching the lamplight for half a heartbeat, and asked him the last boring question: "What was the worst morning? The very worst one?"

He told me. God forgive me, he told me everything.

* * *

The extraction itself takes eleven seconds, if you're good, and I'm good. It feels like drawing a thread through fabric—one smooth pull, no snags, and the whole garment quietly comes apart in the middle where nobody's looking yet. Aldric would walk home tonight with two years of his life missing, replaced by a fog of vague unease he'd blame on drink or bad lungs or the valley itself. He'd feel lighter. That's the cruelty of it, and the mercy, and I have spent six years refusing to decide which it is.

The memories settled into my head the way stolen goods always do—heavy, foreign, humming faintly with someone else's frost. I could already feel them at the edges of my awareness: mud, whistles, a boy named Tammy laughing over a card game three seconds before a shell took the wall beside him. I'd sort them later, decant them into the neat little labeled compartments in my skull where I keep other people's lives, and tomorrow I'd take them to market.

Tonight I walked Aldric to the door of the Drowned Lantern and shook his hand and told him it had been a pleasure, and meant that too, which is the worst part.

"You know," he said, squinting at me through the parting fog of his old self, "you remind me of someone. Can't place it."

"Don't worry," I said. "Nobody ever can."

* * *

The Gray Market doesn't open until the lamps are lit, because the Brokers say darkness is good for trade, but the truth is they just like the theater of it. Down past the tanneries, where the fog pools thickest because the streets slope wrong, there's a square that isn't on any map worth trusting, ringed with stalls lit by oil lamps that burn without flickering—a tiny, deniable piece of magic the Brokers permit themselves and no one else. The rule of the valley, after all, has always been one law for the founders' heirs and another for the rest of us.

I passed the usual stalls on my way in. The woman who sells first kisses, bottled and warm, to romantics who never managed to have one. The twins dealing childhood summers by the hour. A nervous young man hawking the taste of his grandmother's stew, which sounds pathetic until you learn there are exiles in this valley—people who wandered in through the passes decades ago and can never leave—who would pay a week's wages to taste a kitchen that no longer exists anywhere on earth.

The Veil keeps magic out. It also keeps everything else out, including the people. Nobody talks much about that second part. The valley fills up like a trap fills up, and the exits are guarded by wardens who check your papers and your eyes and ask questions in voices too gentle, and somehow, year after year, the number of people leaving never quite matches the number arriving.

"Quentin Eriksen." The voice came from the broker's stall at the square's heart, silk over gravel. "Still breathing. The valley must be slipping."

Maren Voss presided over the central table like a spider who had decided webs were beneath her. She was small and immaculate and older than she looked in the way that people who consume other people's time tend to be, and she wore a coat the color of wet slate that I would have sold a year of my own memories to afford, if I'd had a year worth selling.

"Voss." I set my satchel on the table. "Still buying. The market must be thriving."

"Flattery." She smiled with excellent teeth. "Show me."

I drew out the soldier's war the way you'd draw out a bolt of cloth—carefully, letting her see the weave. She closed her eyes and ran her fingers through air that suddenly smelled, even to me, of gun smoke and frozen mud. Her expression didn't change, which was how I knew it was good. Brokers only react when they're trying to cheat you.

"Harrow's Crossing campaign," she murmured. "Frontline infantry. High-fidelity. Emotional residue intact." Her eyes opened. "This is quality work, Eriksen. Almost a shame."

"Almost?"

"There's blood in it. Real blood, recent. That narrows the buyers—most of my gentle customers want war the way they want storms in paintings." She named a price that was an insult, I named a number that was a fantasy, and we settled somewhere in the honest middle, which in the Gray Market means both of us walked away feeling robbed. Three silver bits, a copper, and a chit for bread at Ferro's bakery, redeemable against her account, because even the Brokers preferred to keep their ledgers tangled.

"The bread chit's generous," I said.

"You look thin. It disturbs the customers. Desperation makes them clutch their memories tighter." She slid the coins across. "Word of professional advice, since you insist on surviving: big game lately. Someone new asking questions in the low quarters. Stranger type. Not from the valley. Asking about old things." She examined her nails. "Old things like fire."

"Fire," I repeated.

"Dragon-fire," she said, and watched my face with the patience of a fisherman watching still water. "Which is a strange thing to be curious about, given that dragons are a story we tell children, and the only fire anyone's seen in this valley in a century comes from a stove." She smiled again, wider. "If such a stranger approaches you, Eriksen—being, as you are, our most talented little thief—I trust you'll bring the conversation to me. There's a bonus in it."

"I live to serve the market," I said, which we both understood to mean the opposite.

* * *

Ferro's bakery was dark when I got there, so I wedged the chit under his door and took my bread from the basket he leaves on the sill for exactly this arrangement, because trust between thieves and bakers is the closest thing the Veil has to religion. I ate walking. The fog was thick tonight, pressing close and soft as wool, muffling the whole valley into a held breath. Somewhere a dog barked and the sound arrived late, apologetic.

My lodgings were two rooms above a shuttered dyehouse, reached by stairs that complained in seven languages. Rent was paid through Saturday. I counted it twice anyway, an old habit from the years when counting once felt too optimistic to bear.

I sat on the bed and did the sorting.

This was the part of the trade nobody saw, and the part I hated least, which worried me sometimes. I laid the soldier's memories out in the dimness of my own mind like a peddler laying out ribbons: the enlistment, bright and stupid with patriotism; the first winter; Tammy and the cards; the shelling; the letter home he never sent because he couldn't find words that wouldn't be lies; the discharge; the terrible quiet. I filed each one where it belonged. Mud with the mud. Frost with the frost.

And underneath them, as always, the rest of the inventory stirred. Six years of lifting. A hundred lives in fragments, shelved in my skull like jars in a pantry. I keep them organized. It's the only tidiness in my existence, and I suspect it's also the sickness at the center of it—because when your own shelf runs empty, you start rearranging everyone else's.

I went looking, as I do most nights, for my mother's face.

I know I had one. I know this the way you know arithmetic: there was a woman, there was a room, there was a name she called me before Quentin, before the valley, before the knack showed itself and made me useful and therefore sellable. I remember knowing her. I remember remembering her, once—warmth, flour, a song in a language I no longer speak. But I sold the early years first, back when I was sixteen and hungry and hadn't learned that memories don't regrow, that you can't sell the same summer twice, that the self is not a field that replants. The brokers were gentle about it. That's the thing people never believe. They were kind. They explained the terms clearly. They let me keep the knowledge of her existence while selling me the contents, and I signed, because the alternative was winter.

What remains is a shape where a face should be. Warmth, flour, song. If I try hard I can almost raise an outline—cheekbones, maybe, or the idea of cheekbones—but it slides like fog through fog, and I'm left holding the certainty of love without a single feature to hang it on. Some nights I think that's worse than forgetting outright. At least the forgotten get peace. The half-remembered get homework.

I sat with the emptiness for a while, professionally. Then I banked the lamp, because lamp-oil is silver, and lay down in the dark with a stranger's war humming gently at the edge of my sleep, frost in someone else's bones leaking into mine, and told myself—as I tell myself every night, with decreasing conviction—that next season I'd stop. I'd save up. I'd buy back one memory, just one, the mother one, if any seller in this valley ever had it, and I'd retire rich in the only currency that matters.

The fog pressed at the window. The valley breathed.

And in the morning, when I came down the complaining stairs with bread crumbs still on my collar, there was a stranger standing in the dyehouse yard—a figure in travel-stained clothes, out-of-valley boots, asking my neighbor in a clear, carrying voice:

"—and I was told a thief worked here. Someone who deals in memories. I'm looking for someone who knows about *fire*."

They turned as my stair creaked, and looked straight at me, and smiled like a door opening.

I should have gone back inside.
