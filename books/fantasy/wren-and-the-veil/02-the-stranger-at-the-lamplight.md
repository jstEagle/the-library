## 2. The Stranger at the Lamplight

There are three rules to working the Lamplight district of the Veil, and I break all three of them before the night is out.

Rule one: never drink on a job. Rule two: never work a mark who asks questions instead of answering them. Rule three — and this is the one that keeps you alive in a valley where memory is currency and everyone's pockets are inside their skulls — never, ever let a stranger see you looking.

I broke rule one with my second glass of plum wine. I broke rule two within ten minutes of sitting down across from them. And by the time the lamps guttered low and the fog pressed its gray face against the windows of the Drowned Sparrow, I had broken rule three so thoroughly that I might as well have hung a sign around my neck: *Memory-thief, currently distracted.*

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Thieves always do. It's the memories we take that make us greedy for endings.

* * *

The stranger arrived in the valley the way weather arrives: suddenly, without paperwork, and everyone pretended they'd seen it coming. I heard about them before I saw them, because the Veil is small enough that anything new gets passed around like contraband. A traveler came through Graygate on the north road — no escort, no broker's chit, no fog-fever. That last part was what made people talk. The fog takes something from most outsiders when they cross in. A name, a face, the taste of their own childhood. They stumble into the valley half-erased, easy pickings for someone with my particular talents.

This one walked in whole.

"They asked Old Sefa about dragon-fire," Tamsin told me over the counter of the Sparrow that evening, wiping glasses that were already clean. She runs the place, or it runs her; after forty years it's hard to say which. "Dragon-fire. In *this* valley. Either they're mad, or they're from somewhere the maps forgot."

"Everyone's from somewhere the maps forgot," I said. "That's rather the point of the Veil."

"Not asking about the war, though." She leaned in, lowering her voice even though there was nobody to hear but me and the wine barrels. "Not asking about the Brokers, not asking which districts are safe. Asking about fire. Old fire. Sefa says they used words she hadn't heard since her grandmother died."

"What words?"

"She wouldn't say. Said saying them felt like standing too close to a stove."

I should have let it go. That's the professional instinct, and for eleven years in this trade it has served me well: curiosity is an expense, and expenses get you sold. The Brokers pay good scrip for thieves with tidy ledgers, and untidy ones end up in the memory-hollows below the Exchange, smiling at walls.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about stealing memories for a living: you develop a taste. Not for the big ones — love and grief are common as bread, and brokers pay accordingly — but for the strange ones. The woman who remembered being a lighthouse in a dream that lasted thirty years. The soldier whose happiest recollection was the smell of his enemy's cooking. You spend long enough rummaging through other people's heads and you start to believe the world is mostly made of things that shouldn't fit together.

A stranger walking through fog that unmakes people, asking about fire that hasn't burned here in a hundred years? That was a thing that didn't fit together.

So when they came into the Drowned Sparrow that night and took the stool two down from mine, I did something reckless.

I looked.

* * *

They were hard to look away from, which was itself suspicious, because everything in the Veil is designed to be forgettable. Gray stone, gray fog, gray faces under gray lamplight. But this one sat like the lamplight had been waiting for them. Dark coat travel-worn past any fashion, boots that had climbed real mountains, and eyes the color of banked coals — that specific amber-red you only see if you've stood near something burning down to its last.

They caught me looking. Of course they did. And instead of the usual arithmetic strangers do — *threat, value, threat again* — they smiled.

"You've been staring for six minutes," they said. Their voice had smoke in it. Not metaphorically. Or maybe metaphorically; it's hard to tell, in the Veil, where everything is a little bit on fire underneath.

"I'm admiring your boots," I said. "Genuine leather. Do you know how rare that is here? Half the valley wears reconstituted hide and pretends it doesn't itch."

"They were a gift." They turned on the stool to face me fully, and I felt the way you feel when a door you thought was painted opens onto a room. "From someone who owed me a great debt."

"People usually pay debts in money."

"People usually aren't interesting."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and the laugh came out wrong — too honest, too loud for the Sparrow's acoustics of murmured despair. Tamsin glanced over. I ordered another plum wine I didn't want, because buying drinks gives your hands something legitimate to do.

"Quentin," I said, offering my name the way you offer bait. Names are cheap. Everyone in the Veil trades theirs freely; it's everything attached to them that costs.

"Wren," they said.

And here is where I'll confess something, since confession is apparently the shape this evening was taking: I reached for their surface thoughts the way you'd brush someone's sleeve in a crowd. Light. Accidental-looking. Every thief in the valley can skim the top layer off a mind with a touch and eye contact — it's not theft, technically, more like reading a letter left open on a table. Most travelers' surfaces are all noise. Homesickness. Fog-shock. The dull panic of people doing sums about how much of themselves they have left.

Wren's surface was quiet.

Not empty — quiet. Like a lake at dawn, holding its breath around something enormous just beneath. I got exactly one image before they noticed and closed up like a fist: *fire. Vast, winged, wheeling against a sky I didn't recognize.* Then nothing but polite attention, warm as a hearthstone.

My hands went cold around the wine glass.

Eleven years in the trade. I have skimmed soldiers, smugglers, a Broker's own courier, and once, memorably, a man who turned out to be three children stacked in a trenchcoat running a con on the eastern markets. I know every flavor of mental static a human head produces. What I don't know — what *nobody* knows — is how someone keeps a dragon behind their eyes and smiles about the price of boots.

"Something wrong?" Wren asked. Mildly. Terribly mildly.

"Wrong?" My voice did something impressive and stayed level. "No. Just deciding whether you're worth a second drink."

"And?"

"You're expensive," I said. "But I'm feeling reckless."

Which is how I ended up agreeing to a date. They suggested it — dinner tomorrow, the little place on Candlemaker's Row where the candles are the only light and the food is mediocre and therefore nobody watches anyone. I said yes with the smooth ease of a professional accepting a contract, and inside, every alarm I owned went off in a chorus.

Because here's the ledger I was keeping, and I'm nothing if not honest with myself about dishonesty: I wanted their memories. Whatever was coiled under that still water — the fire, the sky, the words that made old women go pale — I wanted it. It would fetch more than a year of soldier's nightmares. It might fetch enough to buy me out of the gray market entirely, out of the rented room with the damp ceiling, out of the life where I sell pieces of myself between jobs to cover rent.

That was the plan. That was absolutely, completely the plan.

So why, walking home through the fog with the lamps dying behind me one by one, was I already breaking the oldest rule of all?

Thieves have a private taxonomy of memories. There's stock — the sellable stuff, bulk feelings and borrowed adventures. There's leverage — secrets you keep to trade upward. And then, at the bottom of every thief's inventory, there's the category we don't write down: *keepers*. The ones you steal almost by accident and then can't part with. I had three keepers in eleven years. A mother singing in a language I didn't speak — not my mother; my mother's face is gone, sold in a bad winter, and some nights I try to reconstruct her from other people's mothers the way you rebuild a shattered pot from the sound it made. A rainstorm over a city I've never visited. And the smell of bread from a bakery that burned down before I was born, lifted off a dead man who'd carried it sixty years.

Tonight, walking home, I realized I'd added a fourth keeper, and I hadn't stolen a thing.

The image of that fire, wheeling against an impossible sky. One glance through a crack in a stranger, and I was already hoarding it.

I stood outside my lodging until the fog beaded in my hair, and I made myself say it aloud, to the gray street, to no one:

"It's just a mark. It's just the biggest score of your life. You're going to have dinner, you're going to be charming, and you're going to lift whatever's under that lake, and you are *not* going to feel anything about it."

The fog, naturally, said nothing. The Veil never does. It just took my breath and hung it in the air like everything else it refuses to give back.

* * *

Candlemaker's Row smelled of tallow and rosemary, and Wren was already there when I arrived, early, seated in a corner with their back to the wall — a fighter's habit or a fugitive's, and I noted it the way I noted everything now, compulsively, helplessly. They'd dressed the same coat but cleaned the boots. Someone who cleans their boots for me. Dangerous. Everything about them was dangerous in ways I kept failing to itemize.

"You came," they said.

"I said I would. In the Veil, that's practically a blood oath."

We ordered the mediocre stew. We drank thin red wine that tasted of the effort of grapes. And we talked — or rather, they talked, and I fished, and the difference between conversation and larceny narrowed until I honestly couldn't tell you which one I was doing.

"So," I said, turning my spoon like a card sharp, "Old Sefa says you ask about fire."

"Do I?"

"That's what the whole street says. Dragon-fire, specifically. Which is funny, because there haven't been dragons anywhere in living memory, and the Veil has a very long memory. It's mostly what we do with it."

Wren watched me over the candle flame, and the amber of their eyes did something I decided not to interpret. "And what do you do with it, Quentin? Your memories. When you're finished with them."

Careful. *Careful.* "Same as anyone. Keep the good ones. Misplace the rest."

"Mm," they said, in the tone of someone filing a lie in a drawer they intended to reopen later. "Sefa also said you were the person to talk to. About old things. Things the valley forgot on purpose."

The candle flickered. Somewhere behind me a chair scraped, ordinary as breathing, and I flinched anyway.

"And why would a stranger care what the valley forgot?" I asked. "Forgetfulness is our best product. People cross the fog precisely to stop carrying whatever they were carrying. The Veil is a kindness, if you squint. A mercy with a toll booth."

"Is that what you believe?" Softly asked. Genuinely asked, which was worse. "Or is it what you sell?"

I put down my spoon. Under the table, my hand drifted to the little lead lozenge in my pocket — a focus-stone, thief's kit, the thing that lets me pull a full memory clean instead of skimming. Eleven years of habit said: *now, while they talk, while the wine loosens them, take the fire, take the sky, take the words that frightened an old woman.*

And my hand — my traitor, amateur, rule-breaking hand — stayed exactly where it was.

"Forget the valley," I said. "Tell me something true instead. One true thing. That's my price for dessert."

Wren leaned back. Considered. The candlelight moved on their face like water over stones.

"All right," they said finally. "One true thing." A pause, long enough for me to lean in despite myself. "You've already taken something from me, and I let you."

The bottom dropped out of the evening. "I don't—"

"The fire," Wren said quietly. "At the Sparrow. You reached, and I opened the door one inch, and you took a look. I felt it like a draft." They weren't angry. That was the horrifying part. They sounded almost *gentle*. "Most thieves in this valley would have taken the whole lake and left me a puddle. You took one image and then spent the rest of the night pretending you hadn't. Why?"

Every answer I owned — professional restraint, greed for a bigger haul later, simple cowardice — arranged itself in a queue, and I dismissed the entire line, and heard myself say:

"Because I wanted it to be *mine*. For one hour. Before it became stock."

Silence. The candle bent in a draft from the door. And Wren looked at me the way you look at a lock you've been trying to open for a hundred years when it finally, softly, clicks.

"Have dinner with me again tomorrow," they said. "And the day after. Ask me anything. Take whatever you want — I mean it. But not tonight. Tonight I need you to walk home, sleep, and think very carefully about whether you want to know why a stranger came asking about dragon-fire." They set coins on the table, stood, and paused. "Because Quentin — the answer is going to change what you think the Veil is for. And once you know, you can't unknow it. That's not a threat. It's the only fair warning I'm allowed to give."

They left coins for the candles too, which nobody does, and the door closed behind them, and I sat alone in the small warm light with my heart doing something embarrassing and arrhythmic.

* * *

I didn't go straight home. Old habits. I cut through the alley behind the Row, checked my shadow twice, and was three streets from my lodging when I saw the lanterns.

Three of them, moving in formation through the fog. Broker-gray, Broker-silent, each one carried by a figure whose face I couldn't quite hold in my memory no matter how I looked — hollowed couriers, the kind the Exchange sends when it wants something found without leaving witnesses to the finding. They weren't hurrying. They were *quartering*, methodical as hounds, working the district outward from Candlemaker's Row.

Working toward wherever a certain stranger had slept last night.

Or — the thought arrived cold and complete, like ice down a collar — toward whoever had been seen sitting with them.

I pressed into a doorway and let the fog have me, and as the lanterns slid past close enough to touch, I understood that the choice Wren had offered me wasn't a choice anymore. The Brokers had already chosen. All that remained was whether I'd be standing beside the stranger with the fire behind their eyes when the knocking started —

— and whether, when it did, I'd finally learn what was buried under all that still water, or die curious.

I gave the lanterns a hundred-count, then slipped out of the doorway and ran, not home, but toward Graygate, toward the last place Wren had been seen, telling myself the whole four streets that it was about the score.

It was the worst lie I'd ever told, and I've told some masterpieces.
