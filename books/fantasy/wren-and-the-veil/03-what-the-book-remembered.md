## 3. What the Book Remembered

The Brokers' archive smelled the way old money dreams: leather, lamp oil, and just beneath it, the faint copper tang of other people's lives. I'd been fencing there for six years and had never once been invited past the front counter, which was how you knew you weren't important. Important people got tours. The rest of us got a bell to ring and a clerk with a face like a closed ledger.

That Tuesday morning — if it was Tuesday; the fog made days into rumors — the clerk's name was Pell. Pell had a voice like wet paper and a policy of making you wait exactly long enough to feel grateful when he didn't.

"Eriksen," he said, not looking up from his tally book. "You're early."

"I'm punctual. There's a difference. Early is a character flaw."

"Mm." He turned a page with one finger, savoring the delay the way other men savored brandy. "What have you brought me?"

"A soldier," I said. "Or what was left of him after I got through. Eleven years of war memories. Marches, battles, a winter in some pass where they ate their boots. High-quality misery. It'll sell to the nostalgia trade for triple what you'll pay me, which is why you'll pay me half of that and we'll both pretend the arithmetic is fair."

Pell almost smiled. In the gray market, that was practically a hug.

"Eleven years is a lot of shelf weight," he said. "Sit down."

I sat on the customer stool, the one with the leg worn smooth by a century of nervous sellers, and watched him do the ritual: the brass pan on the scale, the memory-vial uncorked, the little silver spoon of light that meant my merchandise was clean. No magic in the Veil — that was the whole point of the place — but memories behaved strangely here anyway. They glowed. They wanted to be remembered, even when nobody could afford to keep them.

"Boots," Pell repeated, tasting it. "They ate their boots."

"And each other's boots. And eventually, briefly, a mule named Duchess, whom he loved. That part's worth extra. Grief sells better than hunger."

"You're a poet, Eriksen."

"I'm hungry. Same thing, most mornings."

He counted out coins — bread money, rent money, a little over because eleven years of war is genuinely heavy stock — and I pocketed them and should have left. That's the professional discipline of my trade: take the coin, don't linger. Lingering is how sellers start browsing their own inventory, and browsing leads to buying back, and buying back your own memories at a loss is the fastest way to go broke in this valley.

But the archive was quiet. Pell had stepped into the back room with his tally book, and the door between the counter and the stacks — the one that's always locked, the one I've spent six years not staring at — stood open about four inches.

I want to tell you I hesitated. I want to tell you some better version of me weighed the risk against the reward and made a calculated choice, because calculated choices are what I sell myself instead of a personality. But the truth is my feet moved before my brain finished the sentence, and then I was through the gap in the door and inside the Brokers' archive proper, and the smell hit me all at once: ten thousand vials, ten thousand lives, dust and lamplight and the particular hush of a room where everything is somebody's yesterday.

The stacks went up farther than the building should have allowed. I've stolen enough memories of architects to know when a space is lying about its dimensions, and this one lied magnificently. Rows of shelving climbed into shadow. Vials sat in labeled bins, thousands of them, amber and blue and one disturbing shade of green I decided not to think about. There were sections. *Harvests, Domestic.* *Harvests, Foreign.* *Confiscations.* *Estate Lots.*

And at the far end, alone, a case with glass doors and a lock that had been broken so long ago the replacement chain was itself rusting. Inside: books.

Not vials. Books. Paper and ink, the pre-fog kind, the kind that remembers without being paid to.

Here's the thing you need to understand about me, so the next part makes sense. I am not curious. Curiosity is expensive. Curiosity is why travelers wander into the Veil asking questions, and asking questions is how they end up lighter by a decade and heavier by nothing at all. My entire survival strategy rests on knowing exactly as much as I need to and no more. I steal memories; I don't collect them.

But I opened the case anyway.

Later — much later, in the dark, in Wren's company or without it, I still haven't decided which version I prefer — I would try to explain it as instinct. A thief's nose. Something in the air of that archive had shifted the moment I walked in, the way fog shifts when something large moves behind it. But honestly? I think it was simpler and stupider than instinct. I think I was tired. I think I'd sold my mother's face three winters ago to cover a bad month, and I'd spent every day since walking around with a hole shaped like her where a person used to be, and some animal part of me was always, quietly, endlessly searching shelves for anything that might fit back in.

So I opened the case. So sue me. Everyone else already had.

The books were ledgers, mostly. Broker records going back decades — harvest tallies, buyer lists, prices per year of grief. I skimmed them with a professional eye and put them down. Then, at the back, flat under everything else, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, there was a book that wasn't a ledger.

It was bound in gray leather, fog-colored, the kind of binding that doesn't want attention. No title on the spine. When I unwrapped it, the oilcloth crackled like old ice, and the smell that came off the pages was different from the rest of the archive. Not leather and lamp oil. Smoke. Faint, ancient, woodsmoke-and-storm smoke, the smell of a fire that had been out for a hundred years and hadn't accepted it.

The first pages were script in a hand too elegant to trust, the kind of handwriting that belongs to someone who has never once been told no. Prophecies, apparently — the Veil's founding generation, writing down what they claimed the future had told them. Most of it was the usual prophetic soup. A river running backward. A king with two shadows. The fog lifting, the fog thickening, the fog doing various things fog does. I was already reaching to rewrap it when the page turned and I stopped being a person who understood the world.

There was a drawing.

An engraving, technically, done in that fine crosshatched style that costs a fortune, of a figure standing beside a dragon. Not a picture-book dragon — not the fat heraldic lizards on tavern signs. This thing was wrong the way weather is wrong, all coiled muscle and impossible length, its hide rendered in strokes that made the paper look hot. Its eye was the only part inked solidly black, and whoever cut that plate had understood something about eyes.

And standing beside it, one hand raised — not in command, not in fear, in *recognition* — was a person.

I knew the coat. I knew the lean frame, the set of the shoulders, the way the head tilted slightly, like someone perpetually listening for the price of things. I owned that coat. I was wearing a worse copy of it right now.

The face was mine. Not similar. Not "you look like someone I once met." Mine, down to the small scar through my left eyebrow that I got at fourteen falling off a roof I shouldn't have been on, a scar I have never once mentioned while selling a memory, because it's the only thing about my appearance I actually like.

Under the engraving, in that too-elegant hand, two words:

*Quentin Eriksen.*

My name. Spelled correctly. Nobody in the Veil spells it correctly. Pell writes it "Ericksen." My landlady writes it "Ericson." I have spent six years being misspelled by everyone who knows me, and a dead stranger a century underground had gotten it right on the first try.

I did what any reasonable professional would do. I checked the exits. Both of them. Then I checked whether my hands were shaking (they were), then I checked whether anyone had noticed me (the back-room door was still shut, Pell's pen scratching away in there like a small diligent insect), and then I looked at the page again, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming in the Brokers' archive tends to result in your memories being harvested *at a discount.*

The text beneath the image ran in tight columns, half of it in the founders' formal script, half in something older that looked like it had been copied from a source the copier hadn't enjoyed. I read the common parts twice, then a third time, moving my lips like a child, because reading it silently kept letting the meaning slide off.

*When the last fire gutters beneath the stone,* it said, *one will come whose hands remember what they never held. By theft shall the flame be fed or ended. The thief stands at the heart of the Veil, and what the thief chooses, the world becomes.*

*Quentin Eriksen.*

There it was again. Third time now. Like the book was enjoying it.

I want to describe the vertigo properly, because "vertigo" is the word everyone uses and it undersells the experience. Vertigo is a fear of falling. This was a fear of having already fallen — of discovering that the ground you've been standing on for thirty-one years was actually a ledge, and the drop had a name, and the name was yours, and some dead hand had written it down before you were born and then filed you under *Prophecy, Miscellaneous* in a locked case in the one building in the valley you definitely should not burgle.

My whole life, I'd operated on a single article of faith: *I'm invisible.* That was the gift of the Veil, if it gave gifts. Fog thick enough to lose a war in, a gray market where a person could be reduced to an inventory item, and me slipping through all of it unremarked, unrecorded, safe. Every memory I stole was a brick in that wall. Nobody looks for a man with no history. Nobody hunts a ghost.

Except apparently somebody had hunted me a hundred years ago. Somebody had sat down with a copper plate and a burin and carved my exact face next to the last dragon in the world, and then the Brokers had put me in a case and lost the key, and I had walked past that case for six years on my way to sell pieces of myself, humming.

The invisible life was never invisible. It was archived.

I heard the scratch of Pell's pen stop.

In my trade you learn to hear silences the way sailors learn weather, and that silence had changed pressure. He wasn't writing anymore. He was listening.

I did the math fast, the way I do everything fast except decisions. The case's broken chain could be explained — it had been broken for decades, rust on rust, obviously not me. The book, though. The book was in my hands, and a fence caught holding unlisted stock doesn't get arrested in the Veil. We don't really have arrests. We have *inventory adjustments*, performed on the person.

Options: one, put the book back and walk out and spend the rest of my life knowing my face was filed under prophecy in the basement of the people who bought my mother's face, which — no. Two, take it, which was theft from the Brokers, capital crime of the gray market, punishable by becoming stock. Three —

Three was still forming when the back-room door opened.

Pell stood in it, tally book in hand, and his wet-paper face did something I'd never seen it do. It sharpened. Behind the counter he was a clerk; here, among the stacks, with the door open behind him, I understood suddenly that Pell was a tripwire wearing a cardigan, and I had just tripped it.

"Eriksen." His voice was still soft. Softness, I realized, was the dangerous register. "That case is restricted."

"It was open," I said. "Chain's busted. Has been for years, by the look of it. I was going to report it."

"Were you."

"I'm civic-minded."

He came forward at a walk that wasn't hurrying, which was worse than hurrying. And here is the thing I will think about for the rest of whatever my rest turns out to be: he didn't look at me. He looked at the *book*. At the gray leather and the smoke-smell and the page that was, I realized with a fresh cold turn of my stomach, facing outward, engraved side up, my name catching the lamplight like a confession.

Pell looked at the book, and then at me, and then at the book, and I watched him do arithmetic. I know that expression. I wear that expression. It's the expression of a man calculating what a thing is worth versus what it will cost him, and in the gray market that calculation only ever ends one way.

"That's founder-era material," he said. "It shouldn't—" He stopped. Recalibrated. When he spoke again, the clerk was gone entirely, and what was underneath had a boss, and the boss had been summoned. "Wait here."

"No," I said.

His eyebrows went up. "No?"

"I'm leaving. With this. You can bill me." I was already moving, book under my arm, toward the counter and the door and the fog beyond, walking with the unhurried gait of a man with every right to be wherever he is, which is the single most useful skill my profession ever taught me.

"Eriksen." Pell's voice cracked like a vial. "*Eriksen!*"

I rang the front bell on my way out, because I'm courteous, and then I was in the street with the fog closing behind me like water, and the book was against my ribs, warm, absurdly warm, warmer than paper has any business being, and my heart was going like I'd run the length of the Underfog.

I made it nine streets before I understood the real problem.

Anyone could follow me. Anyone could see me carrying it. And more to the point — I had read the words, and you can't unread words, and somewhere in this fog-bound valley, in the last hour, I had stopped being a thief and started being a *prophesied thief*, which is a completely different animal with completely different predators.

Which was when I felt it: the prickle on the back of my neck of being watched by someone good at it. Not Pell. Pell watched like a clerk. This was patient, amused, close — the watchfulness of someone leaning in a doorway I'd passed twice without seeing.

"You read it, then," said a voice I knew. Warm, low, far too pleased with itself. The stranger from the lamplight. Wren. "I was wondering when you'd find the book."

I turned, slow, one hand on the gray leather, and asked the only question that mattered:

"How did you know it would be me?"
