## 4. Wren's True Name

I had rehearsed the confession forty times on the walk from the lamplight district. In the shower of a rented room I'd never sleep in. Against the fog itself, which is a terrible listener — it just takes your words and hangs them in the air like accusations.

None of the rehearsals survived contact with Quentin Eriksen eating toast.

They sat across the table from me in their narrow lodgings, buttering bread with the focused efficiency of someone who has learned that meals are fuel and not occasions, and they said, "You're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to lie to me. You did it twice last night. Once when I asked where you learned about dragon-fire, and once when the waiter brought the wine and you flinched at the label like it owed you money."

"It was a very bad vintage."

"Sure." They bit into their toast. "Go on, then. Lie to me properly. I've been stolen from by professionals; I can tell the difference between a bad vintage and a person deciding what to do with me."

And that was the thing about Quentin Eriksen. Everyone in the Veil thought of them as a pickpocket of the intangible — a small, gray, careful life, lifting memories from travelers and trading them for bread and rent. Nobody looked at them twice, because nobody looks at the invisible. But invisibility, I can tell you, is a discipline. It requires seeing everything and being seen by no one.

Quentin had seen me from the first minute. Which was inconvenient, because my mission required being exactly the kind of person nobody sees coming.

"The date," I said, "was a mission."

The knife stopped against the bread for half a second. Then it continued, smooth as anything, which told me more than screaming would have.

"There was an actual mission," they said. "Not just, you know, dinner."

"There was dinner."

"How romantic. Who sent you?"

I have been the last warden of a dying fire for longer than I want to count. I have walked out of the valley and back in through ways that don't exist on any map. I have carried a name in a dead tongue in my mouth for so long it's worn smooth, like a river stone, like a coin rubbed blind. And I found that what stopped me, in that moment, wasn't fear of the Brokers or the weight of a century's duty.

It was that Quentin was looking at me like I was still the person who'd made them laugh twice over cheap wine, and I knew that when I finished talking, I wouldn't be.

"My order is called nothing," I said. "Because names get stolen here, and things without names are hard to put in ledgers. We keep what the founders forgot. There were seven wardens when the Veil went up. There's one now."

"Seven wardens of what?"

"You know. You asked me about it over dinner with that hungry look. Dragon-fire."

"Dragon-fire." Quentin set down the knife. "There hasn't been a dragon in this valley since—"

"There's never been a dragon *in* this valley. That's the entire point of the valley." I pulled the chair around so I could face them square, and I took out the thing I'd carried against my ribs for eleven years: a scale, dull as a cinder, warm as a held hand. "The heart is under your feet, Quentin. Under the whole valley. The Veil isn't a place. It's a lid."

They looked at the scale for a long moment. Then they looked at me, and I watched them do the arithmetic they were built to do — the professional arithmetic, the *what is this worth and how do I lift it* arithmetic — and then I watched something rarer happen, which was them setting the arithmetic aside entirely.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because of a book."

"I know about the book."

That stopped me. "You—"

"I fence memories to the Brokers' archive. You think I've never browsed?" Their mouth did something bitter. "Third stack, restricted reading room, behind the tax ledgers because even the archivists found it too strange to file properly. A century-old prophecy journal with a sketch of my face in it. My face, Wren. My actual face, drawn by someone dead before my grandparents were born, with my name underneath like a caption." They laughed, and it was not a good laugh. "So either you're the fourth person this month to run a very elaborate con on a memory-thief, or I've been part of somebody's story since before I existed. Neither option makes me feel great."

"It's neither," I said. "It's worse than both, and I'm sorry, but you deserve the whole of it, so: the prophecy says the one in that drawing will either extinguish the last fire or rekindle it. The founding families wrote the Veil into being to hide the heart from a world that wanted to carve war-magic out of dragon-flesh. But hiding a heart means keeping a heart, and keeping a heart means feeding it, and feeding it meant—" I turned my hand over, palm up, the gesture of a man showing he holds nothing. "Bloodlines. A lineage seeded into the valley generations deep, so there would always be one person the fire would answer to. The founders didn't write a prophecy, Quentin. They wrote an instruction manual and disguised it as fate."

The toast sat untouched. The fog pressed gray against the window, the way it does, patient as a creditor.

"And the Brokers?" Quentin said quietly.

"Found the book sixty years ago. They've been trying to find the prophesied blood ever since. Not to help it." I reached across the table — slowly, the way you reach toward a bird — and touched the back of their wrist. "To use it. One drop seals the Veil forever. Closes the lid permanently. No fire, no waking, no outside world ever again. An eternal gray market with them at the top of it."

"And you were sent to find me first."

"Yes."

"When? When were you sent, exactly?"

Here it was. Here was the part I'd rehearsed forty times and botched in every version.

"Eleven years ago," I said. "But I found you three weeks ago, and I need you to believe me when I say those are different sentences. Eleven years of searching every registry, every parish roll, every Broker ledger I could steal. Three weeks ago I finally traced a bloodline through six generations of deliberately forgettable people, and it ended at a memory-thief in a garret who sells other people's Sundays for bread." I swallowed. "The date was cover. Getting close to you was the assignment. Asking you odd questions about dragon-fire was reconnaissance."

"And the laughing?" Quentin asked. Their voice was very level, which in a thief of their caliber is the loudest alarm there is. "Was that reconnaissance?"

"No," I said. "That was real, and it ruined my professionalism, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending otherwise."

* * *

Quentin stood, went to the window, and stood there with their back to me, and I let them stand there because some conversations you have to conduct with a person's spine.

"So let me assemble this," they said at last. "A secret order older than the fog. A dying god-heart under the market stalls where I buy onions. A prophecy with my face on it that the most powerful people in the valley would kill to own. And you. Sent to retrieve me like a parcel." They turned. "Say the part you haven't said yet. The part where you tell me what happens if the Brokers find me, because I've dealt with hunters before, Wren, and I want to hear you say it plainly."

"If they take you, they don't kill you. Killing you wastes the blood. They take a drop, they bind it into the Veil's roots, and then they keep you. Alive, comfortable, and hollowed. Every memory lifted until there's nothing left in you but obedience. I've seen their hollowed soldiers. They smile beautifully and remember nothing, and they do exactly as they're told forever."

"That's not death."

"No. It's worse. It's the thing you personally have spent your whole life dancing along the edge of, done to you on purpose, by professionals." I stood up. "Which brings me to the part I've been dreading more than all the rest, so I'll say it fast, like pulling a splinter: we have to leave. Tonight. Because I wasn't followed here, but I *was* noticed, and a Broker hunter named Corvane made me two days ago outside the archive, and men like Corvane don't notice people idly."

Quentin went very still. Then, with maddening calm, they began clearing the breakfast things.

"You knew," they said. "You came here knowing they might be behind you, and you sat down to toast anyway."

"I needed you to hear it from me before you heard it from a boot on the door. There's a difference between being rescued and being told the truth, and you strike me as someone who'd burn down a city over that difference."

"Correct assessment." They stacked the plates with a click. "How long do we have?"

I listened. Not with my ears — with the scale against my ribs, which had been growing warmer all morning, the way it always does when the Veil's attention turns somewhere, when the fog thickens its listening. The founders' wards ran on the heart's own heat, and the heart had opinions about its prophesied blood coming near it.

"Minutes," I said. "Maybe."

"Right." Quentin moved then, and I finally understood why the founders' bloodline had stayed hidden for a century, because what I saw wasn't panic. It was a professional at work. They swept a loose floorboard up with their heel, pulled out a canvas roll, and began selecting tools with the tenderness other people reserve for photographs. "Back window drops into the tanner's yard. The tanner owes me; I gave him back his daughter's christening, which I stole from him by accident two years ago, and guilt is excellent currency. From there—"

The lamp guttered.

Not the way lamps gutter. Lamps flicker when the oil runs low. This was different: the flame bent sideways, all at once, toward the door, like iron filings finding a magnet, and I felt the scale go hot enough to bite.

"They're here," I said. "Now. All of them, or the fog's attention at least — Quentin, the window, *now*—"

But Quentin had gone still again, head tilted, watching the bent flame with an expression I couldn't read. "It's pointing," they murmured. "The fire's pointing at the door."

"Quentin."

"Do you hear that?" they whispered. "Under the stairs. Listen."

And I did hear it — a sound I'd hoped never to hear inside the Veil's streets, a sound that belonged to the deep places and the drowned tunnels and should never, ever have come scratching up through a garret floor. A wet, eager, sniffing sound. Like hounds. Like hounds made of nothing but appetite and attention.

"Memory-hounds," I breathed. "Voss already knows. She's not sending hunters, she's sending trackers — they follow stolen thoughts, Quentin, they follow the *scent of memories*, and your head is—" I stopped.

Quentin was smiling. It was not a nice smile. It was the smile of someone whose terrible, crooked, thieving life had just become, for the first time, an advantage.

"My head," they said, slinging the canvas roll over their shoulder, "is full of forty strangers' memories, a soldier's war, a baker's grief, and apparently a dragon's dreams I didn't even know I had. So unless those hounds can sort one scent out of a crowd—" The door shuddered against its frame. Plaster sifted down like snow. Quentin grabbed my sleeve and hauled me toward the window, and above the groaning hinges they shouted, bright and fierce and alive:

"—then we run for the underfog, Wren, and we let them choke on everything I've ever stolen!"

The door burst inward. The window burst outward. And we went with the glass, down into the tanner's yard, into the gray and gasping morning, with the hounds of Maren Voss singing our names behind us.
