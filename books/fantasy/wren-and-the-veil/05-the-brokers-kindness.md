## 5. The Brokers' Kindness

The invitation arrived the way invitations from powerful people always arrive: without warning, without explanation, and with an implicit understanding that declining was technically possible in the same way that walking off a cliff is technically possible.

It came on gray paper—everything in the Veil came on gray paper—and it was delivered by a courier who didn't knock so much as materialize in my doorway like a debt I'd forgotten I owed.

"Quentin Eriksen." The courier read my name off the card as if confirming a diagnosis. "Broker-Head Voss requests your presence at the Ledger House. Tomorrow. Evening."

"Requests," I said.

The courier's face did something that on a less disciplined person might have been a smile. It wasn't. "Requests."

I looked at the card. Heavy stock, real ink, not the stamped charcoal the brokers used for summonses to minor offenders. At the bottom, beneath Maren Voss's name, was a single line written in a different hand: *Come hungry.*

*Come hungry.* Like I was being invited to dinner instead of summoned by the woman who effectively owned the fog I lived in.

I should have burned it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when everything they describe is over. I held it over my little iron stove, and I thought about burning it, and then I thought about what happens to people who burn Broker-Head correspondence, and I put the card in my coat pocket instead, next to my lockpicks, where at least it would be among friends.

* * *

I spent the day before the summons doing what I always did, which was pretending to be ordinary. I lifted a wool merchant's memory of his daughter's wedding—he'd been drinking at the Saltcellar and left his head unguarded, which is practically an invitation—and traded it to Doss at the Gray Market for three weeks' rent and a jar of pickled onions I didn't need.

Doss weighed the memory vial in his palm like a jeweler. "Wedding memories are soft goods," he said. "Sentimental. They move slow."

"It's a good one," I said. "Real love in it. You can feel it."

"Feeling it is your problem, Eriksen, not mine." But he paid full rate, because Doss was honest in the way that only men who've made their peace with dishonesty can be, and I took my coins and my onions and went home to decide what a person wears to dinner with the most dangerous woman in the valley.

Here is what nobody tells you about selling memories: you don't lose them all at once. You lose them the way a coat loses buttons. One morning you reach for your mother's face and find a smooth place where it used to be, and you can't even grieve properly because grief requires remembering what you're grieving for. I knew I had had a mother. I knew she had sung while she cooked—I had sold that memory years ago, to pay for a winter—and sometimes, in the space between sleeping and waking, I could almost hear the tune. Just the tune. Never her voice carrying it. Never her face above the stove.

I owned exactly one photograph of myself as a child. I kept it face-down in a drawer because looking at it felt like reading someone else's mail.

So when I say that Maren Voss's invitation frightened me, understand: it wasn't the danger. Danger I knew. I'd been dancing on Broker patience since I was fourteen, lifting memories out of travelers' heads with quick fingers and quicker excuses. What frightened me was the word *hungry*. Because Maren Voss was not a woman who fed people. She was a woman who fattened them.

* * *

The Ledger House sat at the top of Ashfall Street like a tombstone that had decided to become a mansion. Gray stone, black windows, and a door of dark oak carved with the Brokers' seal: two hands cupping a single drop. Everything the Brokers built looked like it was waiting to be buried.

A housekeeper with the flat, washed-out eyes of the lightly skimmed—people sell small memories to the Brokers too, little ones, to make rent; a first kiss here, a dead friend there—showed me up stairs I tried to count and lost track of twice, into a dining room where a fire actually burned. Not peat-smoke haze. A real fire, orange and alive, the first one I'd seen since coming into the Veil. Magic couldn't cross the fog, but apparently money could buy a convincing imitation.

Maren Voss stood at the window with her back to me, watching the fog press against the glass like something that wanted in.

"You're punctual," she said, without turning. "That surprises me. Thieves are usually late. It's part of the romance they tell themselves."

"I'm not late because I'm not a thief."

Now she turned, and I got the full effect, and I'll admit it landed. She was maybe sixty, silver-haired, dressed in plain charcoal wool that probably cost more than my building. Her face was the kind that photographs badly and devastates in person—all sharp planes and one deep crease between the brows, like a signature. And her eyes were the eyes of someone doing arithmetic about you constantly, and enjoying it.

"No," she agreed. "You're a *finder*. There's a difference, and we both know what it is. Finders locate things that have wandered away from their owners." She gestured to the table. "Sit. Eat. I meant what the note said."

The table was set for two. Roast bird, actual butter, bread that hadn't gone gray at the edges. In the Veil, flour came through the passes already stale, and everyone ate like the famine was a guest who'd stayed too long but might still leave. This table said the famine had never been invited in the first place.

I sat. I told myself I wouldn't eat until I understood the price, and then she poured wine—a red, dark as old blood, which I chose to interpret as coincidence—and I drank, because refusing a Broker-Head's wine is its own kind of statement, and statements get people followed home.

"You're wondering what this costs," Voss said, taking her own seat.

"The thought crossed my mind."

"Nothing. Tonight, nothing. Tonight I'm simply feeding a young person a decent meal, which is more than this valley has managed for anyone in thirty years, and I am ashamed of that, and I intend to fix it." She said it plainly, no performance in it, and that was the frightening part. Liars perform. Believers just talk.

We ate. The bird was absurdly good. I hated how much I enjoyed it, the way you hate catching yourself laughing at a con.

"Do you know why magic doesn't work here?" she asked, midway through, conversational, like asking whether I followed any sport.

"Everyone knows why. The founders sealed it. The Veil keeps it out."

"Mm. And do you know *why* they kept it out?"

I set down my fork. "Because magic is what started the border wars. That's school-taught."

"That's school-*fed*." She smiled, and it was almost warm. "Close enough, though. The founders were tired of watching fire-magic turn fields into ash and healing-magic into a weapon for whoever held the supply. So they built the Veil—not to imprison anyone, Mr. Eriksen. To protect. A hundred years of no wars. No plagues. No children conscripted at twelve. Can you name another place in the world with our record?"

I couldn't. Nobody could. It was the one thing every child in the valley learned before they learned their letters: *the fog keeps us safe.*

"And yet," Voss went on, softly, "you steal memories in the gray market, and the Brokers trade them, and half the valley walks around missing pieces, and we call this safety." She sighed, and for a moment the arithmetic behind her eyes showed its working, and she looked tired in a way that seemed genuine. "Safety that requires constant maintenance isn't safety. It's a machine, and machines need parts. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

"What parts do you think I have?"

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the fog leaned against the window, patient as tide.

"A week ago," she said, "one of my archivists was cataloguing a private collection—an estate sale, nothing sinister—and he opened a book. Prophecies, mostly nonsense, the usual century-old fever writing. Except one page stopped him cold." She reached beside her chair and brought up a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the table between us without opening it, the way you'd set down a snake you weren't sure was dead.

"He swears the man drawn on that page has your face. And the name beneath it—" she tapped the oilcloth once, "—is yours. Quentin Eriksen. Written down before your parents met."

My mouth had gone dry, and it wasn't the wine. "Forgery. Or coincidence. Faces repeat. Names repeat."

"Faces don't repeat *with that scar*, Mr. Eriksen. The artist even caught the notch in your left eyebrow. Which means either someone painted your future a hundred years ago, or someone painted your past and lied about when." She folded her hands. "I've spent two weeks finding out which. And I've come to believe the book is genuine. Which makes you the most important person in this valley, and quite possibly the most important person in the world, and you've been spending that importance on wedding memories and rent money."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below us, the Ledger House ticked and settled like a ship at anchor.

"So here is what I offer," Voss said. "Not a threat—you've noticed I haven't threatened you, and I won't. An offer. Come inside. A position. Real wages, real food, a room in this house if you want it. Protection from every collector and cutpurse who'd gut you for what's in your blood, because once the book circulates—and books *circulate*, Mr. Eriksen, that's the whole tragedy of books—there will be people far worse than me who come for you. People who don't feed you first." She paused. "And all I ask in return is one drop. One drop of blood, freely given, for the sealing rites. Civic duty. The same duty every founder's descendant has performed for a century. Your drop keeps the fog whole. Keeps the wars out. Keeps the machine running."

"One drop," I said.

"One drop."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you finish your meal, you keep the wine, and you walk home unmolested, and I will be genuinely sorry." She refilled my glass herself, which I suspect was choreography, and which worked anyway. "But I'd ask you to consider what you're saying no *to*. Quentin." My name, given gently, like a hand extended across a table. "You've spent your life selling pieces of yourself to strangers. Has anyone ever offered to give you something back? A place. A purpose. A seat at a table where the fire is warm?"

And here is the thing I'm not proud of, the thing I turned over for the rest of that night and many nights after: it nearly worked. It worked right up until the last inch. Because sitting in that warm room, with butter on my bread and my name said kindly by a powerful woman, some starved animal in my chest stood up and pressed its nose against the bars and *wanted*. Wanted the room. The wages. The belonging. I had been alone so long that loneliness had stopped feeling like a condition and started feeling like a personality, and Maren Voss was offering me an exit from my own self, and she'd dressed it as duty so I could take it without shame.

One drop. What's one drop, when I've already sold gallons of myself by the vial?

I looked at the oilcloth-wrapped book between us. She hadn't opened it. Hadn't shown me the page. And something in my trade-honed gut finally spoke up—the same instinct that knows a mark is too generous, that a locked box offered freely is never free.

"You haven't let me see it," I said. "The page."

Voss's expression didn't change, which was itself a change. "The archive is careful with its holdings."

"You want my blood for a prophecy you won't show me." I stood, slowly, keeping my hands visible and my voice level, the way you do around dogs. "In my trade, madam, when a buyer won't show the goods, it's because the goods aren't what they claim. I thank you for the meal. It was the best I've had in six years, and I mean that as a compliment to your kitchen and an indictment of everything else."

I got to the door before she spoke again.

"The stranger," Voss said quietly.

I stopped. The door handle was cold under my hand.

"The one asking questions about dragon-fire. The one you've been seen with twice now—at the Lamplight, and leaving the archive district Tuesday night, moving quickly, both of you glancing over your shoulders." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Friends are wonderful things, Mr. Eriksen. I recommend choosing them before someone chooses them for you. Goodnight."

I walked down those uncountable stairs with my heart going like a trapped bird, past the washed-out-eyed housekeeper, out into the fog, which had never once in my life felt like protection and tonight felt exactly like what it was: a wall, with me on the wrong side of it, and something on the other side scratching to get in.

Wren. She knew about Wren. Which meant Wren's odd questions about dragon-fire hadn't been idle curiosity—they'd been loud enough to echo all the way up to the top of Ashfall Street. Which meant either Wren was careless—and everything about Wren suggested the opposite—or Wren wanted the Brokers to hear.

I didn't go straight home. I went the long way, through the market stalls shuttered for the night, checking the reflections in dark windows out of pure professional habit.

And in the third window—in the glass of the closed apothecary, warped and silvered with age—I saw that someone had chalked a small sign on my own door, four streets ahead, where I could not possibly have put it myself:

*A single line. A wren, drawn in flight, and beneath it, in a handwriting I was meeting for the first time and somehow recognized:*

*Don't give them your blood. Meet me where the lamplight dies. Bring the book if you can.*

*I already knew where it was.*
