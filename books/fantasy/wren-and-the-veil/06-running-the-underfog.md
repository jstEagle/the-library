## 6. Running the Underfog

### Quentin

Here is something they don't tell you about running for your life through a valley that eats magic: it's exactly like running anywhere else, except your lungs give out faster and the scenery has opinions.

"Down here," Wren said, and I want it on record that I hesitated. The hatch they'd pried open in the alley behind the tannery breathed up at us like an open grave — cold air, wet stone, and that particular smell of water that has been sitting somewhere dark since before anyone's grandmother was born.

"That's a sewer," I said.

"It's the Underfog."

"It's a sewer with better branding."

Wren looked over their shoulder at the end of the lane, where lamplight was doing something wrong. Not flickering. *Focusing.* Three lamps' worth of light bending toward a single point, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone, and I understood — with the calm, detached clarity that has always been my least useful talent — that whatever was coming wore the light like a leash.

I went down the hatch.

The ladder was slick and went on longer than ladders should. Above us, the trapdoor clapped shut, and Wren dropped a rusted bar across it with the ease of someone who had done this before, possibly many times, possibly in another century, which — given everything I'd learned about them in the last two days — struck me as a live possibility.

Then we ran.

I'll be honest about the terror part, because a thief's memoirs that leave out the fear are just bragging with footnotes. The tunnels under the Veil are not empty. They are *full* — of old water, old brick, old names scratched into the walls by people who presumably also hoped the dark would hide them. Our footsteps came back to us doubled and tripled, so that it always sounded like more of us than there were, which was either comforting or a preview, depending on how you felt about being outnumbered by yourself.

And behind us — above us, through six feet of earth and a hundred years of silence — came the sound.

You've never heard a memory-hound if you've lived a decent life, so let me describe it badly: imagine a dog barking inside your own skull. Not hearing a bark. *Remembering* one. The hounds don't track scent or sound. They track thought. Voss's creatures hunt stolen memories the way sharks hunt blood — and I was carrying, at a conservative estimate, four hundred strangers' worth of pasts in my head, sloshing around like coins in a well.

"I'm going to ask a question," I said between breaths, "and I need you not to lie to me, because I've had enough revelations this week to last a lifetime, however long yours turns out to be."

"Ask."

"The hounds. When they catch up. What happens to the person carrying the memories?"

Wren didn't answer fast enough, and Wren, whatever else they were — dragon-warden, liar, the best kisser I have ever professionally surveilled — did not strike me as someone who needed time to think unless the truth was bad.

"They don't kill you," they said finally. "They *unpack* you. Every stolen memory comes out. All at once. Into the fog." A beat. "The Brokers call it harvesting."

"And what's left?"

"A person," Wren said. "Technically."

We ran faster.

* * *

### Wren

I have watched Quentin Eriksen pick pockets, pick locks, pick fights, and pick apart my cover story with the patience of a surgeon and the bedside manner of a tax collector. In three days I have seen them afraid exactly twice. Once when they saw their own face in a book older than their grandmother. And once, just now, at the words *what's left*, when they went very quiet in a way that told me everything about how they'd survived the Veil: by making sure there was never much left to take.

They were already running again before I finished answering. That's Quentin. Fear doesn't stop them; it gets filed, stamped, and put to work.

But I need to say plainly what I have been circling since the archive, because the tunnels have a way of forcing honesty — the dark down here is total, and lies need light to survive:

I lied to them first. The date was a mission. The questions about dragon-fire were real, but the flirtation was tradecraft, and every hour I spent wanting it not to be, I was choosing my orders over the person laughing across the table from me while lifting my watch. They know now. They haven't forgiven me, but they haven't left, and in the economy of Quentin Eriksen, *not leaving* is a currency worth more than gold.

The hounds were gaining. I could feel them through the stone — a pressure behind the ears, like weather coming. Voss bred them from the founders' own designs, dogs hollowed out and refilled with a single hunger. They couldn't smell us. They smelled what we carried.

Which gave me an idea so reckless that I knew immediately it would work, because all of Quentin's ideas work, and some of mine do too.

"Quentin. Stop."

They stopped. Barely. Their whole body kept running in place like a dog held back by a word.

"You said you can feel them getting louder."

"In my teeth, yes. Why?"

"How loud? Which memories?"

They frowned. Then their eyes changed — that shift I'd started to recognize, the one where the weary fence fell away and something quicker looked out. "The soldier's. The one from the market, the war memories. They're... hot. Loudest. Like they're ringing."

Of course. Hounds track stolen thoughts — and nothing in the Veil was stolen harder, or more recently, than a soldier's war. To a memory-hound, those memories wouldn't read as cargo.

They'd read as prey.

"Throw them," I said.

"Throw—"

"Not literally. You're a broker's cutter. You can shape a memory's surface, make it shout. Flood the tunnel behind us with the soldier's war — gunpowder, mud, the screaming. Make the hounds think their quarry went that way."

Quentin stared at me. Somewhere above, the trapdoor groaned under weight.

"You want me to do a *job*. While running. In the dark. For free."

"I want you to live."

Another beat. And then Quentin Eriksen smiled — the market smile, the one I'd mistaken for charm before I understood it was armor — and closed their eyes, and did the most frightening thing I have ever witnessed a human being do voluntarily.

They opened themselves.

* * *

### Quentin

Let me tell you what it feels like to deliberately spill other people's memories into the dark, since apparently I'm the only person alive who knows.

It's like opening a door you've spent your whole life keeping shut. Four hundred minds' worth of stolen past pressed against the inside of my skull, and I cracked the door an inch, and aimed.

I took the soldier's war and I *pushed*. Mud. Whistles. The taste of cordite. A boy named Aldric who joined up because his brother did and died because his brother did — I'd sold pieces of him a dozen times, and I had never once, until that moment, felt guilty about it. Now his fear poured out of me into the flooded dark, loud as a bonfire, and behind us the baying in my teeth swung, wavered—

—and turned.

The pressure in my skull lurched away like a hand releasing my wrist. Wren grabbed my arm and pulled, and we ran the *other* way, and above us the hounds' cry faded toward the far tunnels, chasing a dead man's worst day, which I had made louder than any living thought in the valley.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me ragged and slightly unhinged, the laugh of someone who has just discovered that their curse is also a weapon.

"I threw a war at them," I gasped. "Wren. I threw an entire *war*."

"Yes." Wren was grinning too — a real one this time, no tradecraft in it, and I hated how much I wanted to keep that expression, file it away, never sell it. "Congratulations. You're the only person in the history of the Veil who's ever confused a memory-hound."

"Somebody should have tried sooner. It turns out four hundred strangers make excellent decoys." The laugh drained out of me as I said it, because underneath the exhilaration sat a colder fact, settling in like silt: it worked because I am full of other people's lives. It worked because I am *hollow*, and hollowness, it turns out, has tactical advantages.

The tunnel ahead bent, and Wren slowed, and the mood broke against the wet stone.

"We can't keep this pace," they said. "There's a place ahead. Old founder-work, pre-Veil. We can breathe there. But Quentin—" they hesitated, and I braced, because hesitation from Wren meant the next sentence had weight. "The trick you just did. It cost you. I saw it."

"It didn't—"

"You went grey around the mouth. When you pushed the soldier out, something of *you* went with him. Just a thread. But the fire will ask for more than threads, eventually." They touched my elbow, light as a moth. "When we get to the heart, if you still mean to do this — I need you to understand what feeding it costs. Before you promise anything else."

"I promised you nothing," I said. "I stole your watch."

"You gave it back."

"I returned it to the *scene*. Legally distinct."

And Wren laughed — really laughed, startled out of them, echoing off the drowned bricks — and I stood there in the dark with a war's worth of borrowed terror draining out of my boots and thought: this. This is the memory I'd keep, if keeping were allowed. Not the prophecy, not the fire. This. Wet feet, terrible joke, their laugh bouncing down a sewer like it belonged there.

The tunnel widened. Ahead, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, something glowed — not lamplight, not foglight. Warmer. Older.

"What is that?" I asked.

Wren's grin faded into something I'd learn, later, to recognize as grief wearing its working clothes.

"The chapel," they said. "Where it all started. Where the founders wrote down what they'd done." They looked back the way we'd come, toward the distant, fading music of hounds chasing a ghost-war, then forward into the glow. "And where you're going to hear the rest of it. Quentin — what the Veil *is*. What your blood has to do with it."

The warm light touched our faces.

"After you," I said, and followed the dragon's warden into the dark that remembered everything.

* * *

Behind us, far off in the flooded dark, the hounds stopped.

They stood in the black water where a soldier's war had burned brightest, sniffing at echoes, and found nothing living there to hate. One by one, they lifted their heads.

And began, patiently, to cast about for a newer scent.

A fresher theft.

Something that smelled — faintly, unmistakably — of fire.
