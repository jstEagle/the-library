## 7. The Founder's Confession

I have knelt in a great many chapels in my long, strange career. Most of them were burning at the time, which tells you something about my line of work.

This one was drowning instead, which I suppose counts as variety.

The Underfog tunnels had spat us out into a hollow beneath the valley's western ridge—a cathedral sunk to its ribs in black water, its steeple poking up through the stone like a bone through skin. Stalactites hung where the bell should have been. Somebody, a century ago or more, had carved a dragon into the lintel over the door, and then somebody else had chiseled it half away, leaving only the eye and one enormous claw, so that the creature seemed to be reaching eternally for something just out of grasp.

I knew that carving. I had helped carve it.

Quentin didn't know that yet. They stood hip-deep in the water beside me, wringing out their coat with the grim efficiency of someone who has decided that complaining is a luxury they cannot currently afford, and said, "So this is where you tell me the truth, or this is where I drown you in a flooded church and sell the memory of doing it."

"You'd have to get the memory past the Brokers first," I pointed out. "And honestly? After tonight, I might let you."

"Comforting." Quentin sloshed toward the altar, where a stone basin sat above the waterline, dry as a beggar's cup. "You said the founders built the Veil. You said it like it was a confession. So confess. We've got maybe an hour before Voss's hounds pick up our trail again, and I would like to spend it knowing what I'm dying for."

"It's not a confession," I said. "Not mine. I'm just the messenger. The message is older than your whole valley, older than your language, probably older than bread." I hesitated, because there are things even a dragon's warden doesn't say lightly. "But yes. It's a confession. Just not mine to make. So I'll make it anyway, because the people who made it are dust, and someone has to."

Quentin sat on the edge of the basin—dry stone, worn smooth by generations of sitting—and looked at me with those gray-market eyes, the ones that appraise everything for resale value. Even now, soaked and hunted and betrayed, they were cataloguing me. I could feel it. *Warden, unknown age, unknown weight in memories, possibly priceless.*

"Start with the dragon," Quentin said. "Start with why everyone in this valley can't say the word 'magic' above a whisper."

* * *

Here is what I told them, standing in that drowned church with water lapping at my knees like a congregation that never learned the hymns:

Once—no. Not once. Still. The dragon is still alive. That's the part nobody remembers, because forgetting was the whole point.

Her name, in the old tongue, has no clean translation. The closest I can give you in yours is *Ember-That-Refuses*. She was old when the mountains here were young, and she was dying—not from any wound, but from the world itself. Because here is the secret under all of it, the thing the founders understood and the world did not: dragons do not breathe fire. Dragons *are* fire. Their hearts don't pump blood; they burn. Every flame that ever lit a forge, every wildfire that cleared a forest floor so new things could grow, every candle in every window of every home on the far side of those mountains—it all traces back, thread by thread, to dragon-hearts. Magic is just fire that remembers where it came from.

And men had learned to take it.

There was a war coming—I say "a war" as if wars come singly, like buses. There were always wars coming. But this one would have been different, because both sides had found the last dragon-heart, and both sides had learned that a dying heart burns brightest, that its fire could be siphoned, bottled, fed into weapons. One spark of Ember-That-Refuses could level a city. A handful could end a continent. And everyone who wanted that power told themselves they'd use it *defensively*, the way everyone always does.

The founders were not heroes. I want that understood. They were seven scholars and soldiers and one very tired priestess who looked at the coming war and decided the answer was theft. They stole the heart. They carried it into this valley, and they built the Veil around it—the fog, the dampening, the dead air where magic cannot follow—out of her own shed scales and spent flame. A cage woven from the prisoner's own feathers, if dragons had feathers. They hid the heart beneath the valley's roots, where it could gutter slowly, harmlessly, unseen.

And the price—the founders were honest enough about this, at least among themselves—was everyone inside the cage too. Every soul born or stranded within the Veil's reach would live magicless, gray, fogbound. Forever. Because the alternative was letting the world burn itself down with stolen fire.

"The Veil isn't a prison," I told Quentin, and my voice cracked on it, because I had watched these words be spoken with such pride a hundred years ago. "It was meant to be a mercy. A quarantine ward. They called it the Kindness, when they laid the first stones of that chapel. This place—" I touched the wet wall, the carvings of dragons and seven kneeling figures, "—was the first thing they built. A chapel to house the mercy. People came here to pray for the dragon's soul while they starved her."

Quentin was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped off the broken arches. Somewhere far above, the hounds were still hunting, but distant, and we both pretended otherwise.

"They didn't ask anyone," Quentin finally said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"The people living in the valley. The ones who'd be locked in. Nobody asked them whether they wanted to be sealed inside a dying god's sickroom."

"No," I said again. "They were farmers. Fishermen. A few hundred souls. The founders weighed their lives against millions and did the arithmetic."

"And got it wrong?"

"I think," I said carefully, "they got it right for about forty years. And then they died. And the arithmetic got inherited by people who never saw the war it was preventing. Who only saw the power sitting under their feet."

* * *

Because that's the rot in any mercy: it doesn't stay merciful on its own. Mercy requires tending, and tending requires remembering *why*, and the second generation of keepers remembered only the *how*.

The founders' order—the Wardens, my order—was meant to watch the heart, feed it what little sustenance a dying fire can accept, and wait for either its death or the world's wisdom, whichever came first. For a generation, they kept the vow. Then the wars outside ended—or rather, paused, as wars do—and the world forgot dragons entirely, because the Veil's fog leaks outward as well as inward, and the founders had woven forgetting into its fabric. No army came looking. No weapon-hungry kingdom clawed at the mountain passes.

And the keepers looked at each other across the quiet and asked the fatal question: *then what is all this for?*

Some of them said: nothing. Let the Veil thin. Let the heart die in peace. Let the valley open.

Others said: the heart still burns. Dying fires burn brightest. And we are its only keepers, which makes us its only rightful heirs.

You can guess which faction won. They always win, the ones with the better argument for keeping the treasure. They stopped calling themselves Wardens and started calling themselves Brokers, though it took generations for the name to settle. They discovered that a dragon-heart's fire, siphoned drop by drop, could do things in a magicless valley that looked suspiciously like miracles—preserve a memory perfectly, trade it, transfer it, hold it in a vault like a jewel. The memory-trade wasn't commerce that grew naturally in the Veil's gray soil. It was engineered. A machine built on top of the hidden fire, converting the last dragon's dying light into currency.

Every memory Quentin ever stole and sold passed through that machine. Every brokered recollection, every gray-market bargain in that fog-bound town—it was all Ember-That-Refuses, burned down into coin.

"They're not brokers," I said. "They're parasites on a dying god, and the whole valley is the wound they're feeding through."

Quentin stared at the water between us. When they spoke, their voice was flat in the way I'd learn meant the opposite of calm. "My mother sold memories. Before I was born. To pay for—whatever people pay for. And her mother before her. Everyone I know has sold something. Whole neighborhoods walking around with holes in their heads, and it was all—"

"Fuel. Yes."

"So when Voss offered me protection for a drop of blood—"

"She wasn't offering charity. She was offering you a place in the feeding chain. Closer to the heart than most ever get." I crouched, put my hand flat on the black water. Beneath us—far beneath, through stone and root and a century of silence—I could feel it. Faint. Fainter than it had been even ten years ago. A coal guttering in a windless room. "She believes it, you know. That's the worst part. Maren Voss genuinely believes she's the last honest keeper of the Kindness. That sealing the Veil forever, hoarding the heart, starving it slow—that's *mercy*, still, after everything. She thinks she's finishing the founders' work."

"Is she?"

I laughed, and it echoed wrong in the drowned nave, off the water and the broken bell-tower and the half-carved dragon reaching for something it would never touch.

"The founders stole a heart to save a world," I said. "Voss steals memories to save a cage. Somewhere in between, the mercy rotted, and nobody held a funeral for it. That's how it works, Quentin. Tyranny almost never arrives wearing its own face. It arrives wearing mercy's, and it wears it so long the face fits."

* * *

Quentin didn't answer right away. They pulled their knees up onto the dry basin rim and wrapped their arms around them, and for a moment they looked very young, and very tired, and like someone doing arithmetic of their own.

"When I was eight," they said, "I tried to remember my mother's face. I'd sold it—sold the memory, I mean, when I was younger, before I knew what I was doing. And I sat in my room trying to rebuild it out of nothing, out of other people's descriptions, and I couldn't. And I thought, well, that's the Veil, isn't it. That's just how life is here. Fog outside, fog inside." They looked up at me. "You're telling me it wasn't the Veil. It was a business model."

"I'm telling you it was a theft," I said, "committed by thieves who thought they were surgeons, maintained by thieves who know exactly what they are."

"And you?" Quentin's voice sharpened, and I deserved it. "Where were the Wardens while this happened? While generations of people sold pieces of themselves to keep the lights on? Where was the last warden, besides taking strangers on dates to pick through their heads?"

"Dead," I said. "Mostly. Scattered. Hunted—the Brokers were thorough about that part. I'm what's left, and I spent ninety years doing what Wardens do best, which is watching and doing nothing, because watching and doing nothing is practically our founding discipline." I held up a hand before Quentin could cut in. "Until I found the prophecy book. Until I found your face in it. And then I stopped watching, and started lying to you, which brings us to the present and my ongoing status as the worst date in the history of this valley."

That earned me something that was almost a laugh, strangled at birth. Progress.

"The book," Quentin said. "My face. My name. A century old. Explain that, if you're explaining things."

I looked down at the water again. At the faint warmth I could feel pulsing underneath us like a heartbeat heard through a hospital door. And I made a decision—the same kind of decision the founders made, probably, weighing what to tell against what to withhold, doing my own rotten little arithmetic. Except this time I chose the other side of it. Maybe that's how mercy gets unrotted. One withheld truth at a time, returned.

"The founders didn't just build a cage," I said. "They left a key. Prophecy is what magic looks like after it's been compressed for a hundred years—fire turned to ink. One of the seven was a seer, and she wrote down what she saw: that the heart wouldn't die quietly forever. That someday the fire would gutter so low it needed feeding, and that a child of the Veil—one born inside the fog, grown up trading in other people's light—would carry fragments of the dragon's own memory without knowing it. Memory moves through that trade like blood through a wound. Generations of it, drifting. Some of it hers." I nodded at the floor. At her. "It pooled somewhere, Quentin. In someone. The seer wrote down who."

The water went very still around us, as if it were listening too.

"And the ending," Quentin said quietly. "The page you wouldn't let me read."

"That page stays closed until you choose to open it," I said. "I mean that. Whatever else happens, whatever Voss does, whatever the hounds bring down on us in the next hour—that choice is yours, and no one takes it from you, including me. That's the difference between me and every Warden before me, and I intend to keep it."

Quentin studied me for a long time, rain-gray eyes in lantern-dim light, deciding—as always—what I was worth.

Then, from far back up the tunnel we'd come through, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading: the hounds. Not barking. Hounds that track stolen thoughts don't bark. They *hum*, a low harmonic note like a wet finger on glass, and when there's a dozen of them, it sounds like the valley itself beginning to sing.

Quentin's head snapped toward the sound. Mine didn't. I'd known they'd find us eventually. What I hadn't known—what I felt now, with cold certainty spreading through my chest—was the direction of the hum. It wasn't behind us.

It was below. Rising. From the direction of the heart.

"Quentin," I said, already moving. "We need to run. And not the way you think."
