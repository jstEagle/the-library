## 8. Memories That Aren't Mine

There is a ritual to taking inventory of stolen goods, and I perform it the way a priest performs mass: alone, at night, with candles I can't afford and don't believe in.

The lodgings Wren and I had fled to—Wren's contact's place, a cellar room behind a tannery that smelled like regret and tanning acid—had one table, one chair, and one lock that would stop a determined child for approximately four seconds. But it was dry, and it was dark, and it was ours until the Brokers found us, which by my estimate gave us somewhere between two days and never.

So: inventory.

I keep my stock the way other thieves keep coin. In my head, because heads are the only vaults in the Veil the Brokers haven't picked. Every memory I've lifted over eleven years sits folded and sorted in the back rooms of my skull, tagged with the date and the donor and what it fetched. It's not hoarding, whatever Wren says. A craftsman keeps his tools. The fact that some nights I open them up and walk through them like a man touring houses he'll never own—that's just quality control.

* * *

The soldier's war memories had sold well, back before everything went sideways. That was the trade that started this whole catastrophe, if you trace it far enough—the lift I made off a deserter named Corvan who'd fought in the border wars before the fog closed. Fire, mostly. Burning fields, burning villages, burning men. War runs on fire the way mills run on water.

I'd kept one fragment out of the sale, the way I always do. Not greed. Craftsmanship. You keep a sample of every batch so you can prove provenance if a buyer claims your goods are counterfeit. That was the rule I told myself, anyway, back when I still bothered telling myself rules.

That night, in the tannery cellar with Wren asleep—or pretending to sleep, which for Wren was apparently indistinguishable—I opened the sample.

And it was wrong.

Not corrupted. Not degraded, the way old memories go soft at the edges like fruit left too long. Wrong the way a key is wrong when it fits a lock you've never seen. I was standing on a hillside under a sky the color of hammered copper, watching something enormous move against the sun, and the memory knew things about fire that no human being has ever known, because no human being has ever *been* fire—

I came out of it gasping, both hands flat on the table, the candle guttering.

"Quentin." Wren's voice, low and immediate, from the cot in the corner. "What happened."

"I don't know." My mouth tasted of smoke. Actual smoke. I checked the candle. Still burning, small and innocent. "I looked at the Corvan sample. The fire one."

Wren sat up slowly. In the dark their eyes did that thing they sometimes did—caught light that wasn't there. "Show me."

"You can't just—"

"No," Wren said. "But you can describe it. Slowly. From the beginning."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about memory-work, because the Brokers like it that way: memories have texture. A soldier's fear feels like wet wool. A bride's joy tastes like honey cut with vinegar. A mother's lullaby has a grain to it, like wood. After eleven years I could blind-read a memory the way a cloth merchant reads fabric between his fingers—tell you whose life it was without opening my eyes.

This fragment's grain was nothing I had ever touched.

It wasn't human. I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had at three in the morning in a tannery cellar while my professional worldview quietly caught fire. The memory didn't feel *like a person*. It felt like weather. Like geology. It had the patience of stone and the appetite of a furnace, and underneath both, threaded through it like gold seam in rock, was something I can only call *grief*, except grief implies loss and this was older than loss. This was the grief of something that remembered when the world was young enough to be worth loving.

"The hillside," I told Wren, because they asked and because saying it aloud made it less impossible. "Copper sky. Something flying—huge, wings like ship sails, but I couldn't see it properly, the way you can't see your own shoulder. Because—" I stopped.

"Because you were seeing through its eyes," Wren said quietly.

"Because I was seeing through its eyes." My voice came out flat as slate. "Wren. This is Corvan's memory. I took it off him in a tavern four months ago. He was a deserter from the border garrison. He drank cheap gin. He had a tattoo of his regiment's crest and he cried when he talked about his horse. He was a *person*. So why does the inside of his head feel like—"

"Like the dragon."

I hadn't said it. Neither had they, really—not plainly. We'd been circling it since the archive, since the prophecy book with my face in it, since the word *dragon-fire* first crossed the lamplight between us like a coin passed hand to hand. But there it was now, sitting in the middle of the room, too big for either of us to pretend we hadn't seen it.

"The dragon," I agreed. And then, because I am who I am, even with hunters presumably combing the city for me: "Which raises a fascinating commercial question."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Quentin."

"Somebody sold this. Somebody *traded* it. Memories don't wander, they move along the same routes everything else moves—brokers, fences, couriers. Which means this fragment has a chain of custody going back—" I was already doing the arithmetic, the cold little abacus in my skull clicking away, "—decades. Centuries. Corvan bought half his vices secondhand; half the valley does. This could have passed through forty hands. A hundred."

"And each time it passed through someone," Wren said, "it left a copy behind. Or a residue. Or—" They stood, crossed to the window slit, looked out at fog. "Quentin, how many fire-memories do you hold? Total. Everything you've ever lifted or bought or fenced and kept samples of."

I closed my eyes and walked the shelves. "Fire-heavy stock. Forty-one lots. Maybe sixty fragments once you count partials."

"How many feel wrong?"

I walked the shelves again, slower, touching each one the way you touch a row of doors. Most of them were ordinary conflagrations—house fires, forge work, a baker's memory of his oven, a pyre. Human heat, human scale. But here, this one, the glassblower's memory where the molten glass had seemed to *want* to move—wrong. Here, the child's memory of a bonfire festival where she'd sworn the flames had turned to watch her pass—wrong. Here, deep in an old lot I'd bought bulk from a dead fence's estate, a fisherman's memory of sea-fire, phosphorescence burning green on black water, and the bone-deep certainty that the burning was *singing*—

"Seven," I said. "Maybe eight. They're scattered across years of stock. I thought they were just odd. Beautiful-odd. I kept them because—" I heard myself and hated it. "Because they felt like mine."

Wren turned from the window. Their face in the candlelight was doing something complicated. "They're not yours."

"No."

"They might be hers."

*I* hadn't said that either. But the pronoun settled into the room the way a bird settles on a branch, and neither of us moved to shoo it off.

* * *

We spent what remained of the night doing the only thing I'm actually good at: appraising.

I laid the seven fragments out in sequence, oldest lot to newest, and Wren watched me dive and surface, dive and surface, taking notes in the margins of a scrap of tannery paper because apparently even fugitives from Broker justice need documentation.

Fragment one: the copper-sky hillside. Flight. The wind under wings wide enough to shadow a village, and beneath the wind, a name—not spoken, *sung*, syllables in a language that predated every tongue in the valley, a name that fit in the mouth like a coal fits in the hand. I could almost hold it. It slipped every time I reached, the way dream-details slip.

Fragment two: the glassblower's kiln. Heat as craft. The memory of shaping fire instead of merely enduring it, and the strange tenderness of it—fire as something you raise, like a child, like a garden.

Fragment three: the bonfire festival. Humans, hundreds of them, gathered around flame not to burn anything but to *look* at it. Wonder. The dragon-memory underneath wore the wonder like borrowed clothing, ill-fitting, and there was longing in the seams.

Fragment four through seven, in order: less and less. Sea-fire singing. A hearth seen from outside a window, through rain, from very high up. An egg—warm, warm, warm, the single most concentrated warmth I have ever held in my skull, warmth like a heartbeat the size of a cathedral. And last, the newest lot, barely a sliver: darkness. Cold coming on. The sensation of folding inward, banking oneself down and down and down to a coal, to a spark, to a *maybe*, and around the maybe, walls going up. Stone walls. Fog walls. Someone building a cage and calling it, in the muffled voices outside, a mercy.

When I surfaced from that one, I discovered I was crying, which was professionally embarrassing, and that Wren had a hand on my arm, which was more than professionally embarrassing.

"It's dying," I said. "The seventh one. It's not a memory, it's a—it's a status report. It's *current*." I stared at them. "Wren, these aren't random. They're not scattered. They're a sequence. Flight, craft, wonder, love, egg, decline, entombment. That's not a life. That's a *story*. Someone curated these. Someone put them into circulation deliberately, piece by piece, generation after generation, the way you'd seed a river with fish."

"Why?" Wren asked, but their voice said they already knew, and mine answered anyway because the abacus wouldn't stop clicking:

"So someone would find them. So the pieces would drift through the trade the way silt drifts downstream, and pool somewhere. In a thief. In someone who keeps samples. In someone who's spent eleven years learning to read memories by their grain, who'd know, eventually, that these grains don't match any human hand." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Someone planted a message in a bottle and threw it into the gray market, and the bottle washed up in *my head*."

"In the prophesied one's head," Wren said softly.

"Don't say that like it's comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the window slit, the way dawn comes everywhere in the Veil, filtered through fog until it's less a light than an apology. I should have slept. There were Brokers hunting us, hunters who tracked stolen thoughts, a prophecy with my face in it and a price presumably on my blood. Sleep was the responsible choice.

Instead I sat at the table and held the egg-memory, carefully, the way you'd hold an actual egg.

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I will deny to my grave if I live to have one: I have been lonely in a way that isn't about people. Eleven years of lifting other lives and selling them, of knowing hundreds of strangers from the inside and being known by none, of renting rooms in other people's pasts because I couldn't afford one in anyone's present. I sold my mother's face when rent came due the winter I was nineteen, and I have spent every winter since trying to remember what I paid for.

But this. This creature I have never met, who died—or didn't die, who *banked*—a century before I was born, whose heart supposedly burns under this valley like a coal under ash. Its memories fit in me like my own. When I held the flight-fragment I felt the wind and thought: *oh, I know this.* Not remembered it. Knew it. The way you know your own name before anyone teaches it to you.

Uncanny doesn't cover it. Uncanny is a stranger wearing your coat. This was a stranger whose skin I'd apparently been wearing my whole life without noticing the seams.

"She seeded the trade herself," I murmured. "Or her wardens did. Generation after generation, dropping fragments into the current, waiting for the water to carry them somewhere they could mean something."

"To you," Wren said from the cot. They weren't sleeping either. I was starting to suspect Wren didn't, much. Wardens' privilege, maybe. Wardens' burden. "The prophecy names you, Quentin. The memories found you. Those aren't separate facts."

"Everything about me turns out to be arranged," I said. "My face in a hundred-year-old book. Dragon dreams in my skull. Next you'll tell me my mother sold her face to a Broker on purpose."

The silence that followed lasted exactly two seconds too long.

"Wren."

"They were careful records," Wren said, very quietly, in the tone of someone stepping onto ice and testing it. "The founders'. I've read them. Transactions, in the early decades—people *paid* to enter the Veil, and some of what they paid was memory. It was how the cage was funded. How the fog was maintained. There's a ledger entry, year thirty-one. A woman, name redacted, traded—" They stopped.

"*Traded what,* Wren."

"A face," Wren said. "One face, described in detail. To a broker named Voss. Grandmother, I think. The founder line. The payment was earmarked for the fog works." They met my eyes across the cellar. "Quentin, I don't know it was your mother. The dates fit. The description—there was a scar, here—" they touched their own cheekbone, "—and I've seen what your mother looks like, because you keep one fragment you never sold, the one you tell everyone is just provenance—"

I was already there. Deep in the one shelf I never inventory, the locked room behind the locked room, holding the fragment I'd carried for eleven years and never once examined with professional eyes, because examining it meant risking it, and it was the only thing I owned outright.

A kitchen. Steam. A woman with a scar along her cheekbone, laughing at something out of frame, turning toward whoever held the memory—

Toward whoever held the memory.

Turning toward the *camera*, as it were. Turning toward the person remembering. And I had never, in eleven years of keeping this fragment, asked the question that was now arriving like a knife: *whose eyes am I looking through?*

Because it wasn't the mother's memory. It was someone else's. Someone she was laughing at. Someone she was turning toward with her whole face open and glad, someone she loved, someone whose point of view this was—

Someone tall. Someone whose hands, at the edge of the frame, were not entirely— 

The fragment shuddered. For half a second the kitchen flickered and underneath it there was copper sky, and wings, and the name in the dead tongue rose up through the steam like a bubble through honey, and this time I almost caught it—

The candle went out. Both of them. All of them, every flame in the cellar, simultaneously, and in the sudden dark I heard Wren say, in a voice stripped of everything but awe:

"It knows you're reading its mail."

In the blackness, warm as a banked coal, the name finally surfaced—and slipped away again, leaving only its shape, the way a footprint leaves its shape in snow.

Somewhere above us, faint through the floorboards, dogs began to bark. Memory-hounds. Coming up the tannery lane.

"Quentin," Wren said, already moving, "whatever you just felt—we need it. And we need to run."
