## 9. The Village of the Unremembered

**Quentin**

The hounds lost us at the third junction, mostly because I fed them a memory of my own death.

It was a good one—drowning, cold water in the lungs, the particular panic of knowing you are about to stop existing and being unable to do anything about it. I'd lifted it off a fisherman two years ago and never found a buyer for it, because nobody in the Veil wants to remember dying. They want other people's weddings, other people's first kisses, other people's summers at the sea. Death is the one commodity with no market. So I'd been carrying it around like a stone in my pocket, and it turned out the stone had one use after all: I dropped it behind us in the flooded tunnel and the hounds swarmed it like flies on honey, tongues lapping at the taste of my ending while Wren and I climbed a ladder of rusted rungs into the dark.

"Did that hurt?" Wren asked, hauling me up over the lip of the shaft.

"Losing memories doesn't hurt," I said. "That's the whole problem with it. It's like paying with money you never counted. You just have less than you thought."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once watched a dragon die."

I was learning not to ask about the dragon. Every time I did, Wren got a look on their face like a door closing in a house I wasn't allowed into yet. We had been running for two days—through the Underfog, through the drowned chapel where the founders' confession still echoed off the tiles, through tunnels that smelled of old rain and older secrets—and I was tired in a way that sleep didn't touch. My feet hurt. My head felt lighter than it should, which for me is never good news. And somewhere behind us, Maren Voss was rebuilding her hunt with the patience of a woman who owns every clock in the valley.

Wren went first along the ledge, one hand on the wet stone wall, and I followed the sound of their breathing because I couldn't see anything else to follow. The tunnel narrowed. Then, without any warning at all, it opened.

I stopped walking.

Below us—not far below, maybe thirty feet, close enough to count windows—was a village. An actual village, with crooked rooftops and chimney smoke and strings of lamps hung between buildings like someone had decided the dark up here could be negotiated with rather than surrendered to. Terraced gardens glowed faintly green under glass cloches. A dog barked somewhere, which nearly made me laugh, because I hadn't heard a dog since I was small enough to be someone's problem instead of my own.

"There's no village on any map of the Veil," I said. "And I've stolen maps from people who steal maps."

"It's not on the maps because the Brokers erased it," Wren said. "The village, and everyone who ever lived here. There's a whole district of the archive that's just them. Empty shelves with names on the spines."

"You've read the archive?"

"I told you. I've been looking for you a long time." They started down the switchback path before I could decide whether that sentence was romantic or terrifying. With Wren it was usually both.

* * *

**Wren**

They came out to meet us before we reached the first gate, and I understood immediately that they had known we were coming, though I couldn't say how. Maybe the same way I had known. When you spend enough years around the edges of something enormous—the dragon, the fire, the long slow dying at the center of everything—you develop a sense for its weather. The dragon-memories in Quentin's head were weather. The whole valley must feel them, faintly, the way you feel a storm three fields away.

An old woman stepped forward from the gate. She was small and square as a teapot, with white hair cut practical-short and hands that looked like they had built most of what I could see. Behind her stood perhaps forty others, holding lamps, saying nothing, looking at Quentin with an expression I recognized because I had worn it myself the night I first saw them across the lamplight of the Gray Market: recognition without explanation.

"You're carrying fire," the old woman said. Not a question.

Quentin glanced at me. "I'm carrying a lot of things. Most of them aren't mine. That's sort of my profession."

"We know what you are," she said. "We knew before you crested the ridge. The whole valley dreams of smoke lately, and the smoke smells like you."

"My name is Quentin," Quentin said, and there was something in their voice I'd never heard there before—a wariness so old it had gone smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket for years. "Before we go any further, you should know I don't give anything away for free. Including introductions."

The old woman laughed. It transformed her face entirely. "Oh, I like this one," she said to me. "You didn't tell us they'd be funny."

"I didn't tell you anything," I said. "You sent word that the hounds were running and the fire was walking. Your words."

"Tam," she said, tapping her own chest. "Tam Osgood. I was a schoolteacher, before. Before is a long story and the tea is short, so come inside."

* * *

**Quentin**

Here is what I expected from the village of the unremembered: desperation. People the Brokers erased should be ghosts, should be hollow, should be clawing at each other for scraps of identity the way the memory-beggars in the Gray Market clawed at travelers' sleeves. I had built my whole understanding of the world on the idea that losing your past makes you smaller. It was a convenient belief. It justified my prices.

Instead they sat me down in a warm room that smelled of bread and woodsmoke and cedar, and they fed me stew with root vegetables I didn't know the names of, and they talked. That was the thing. They *talked*. About the harvest under the glass bells. About a wedding scheduled for spring. About a man named Aldous who had taught himself to play the fiddle even though none of them could remember teaching him, or being taught by him, or why the fiddle mattered, only that when he played it everyone cried and no one minded.

"We keep new records," Tam explained, refilling my bowl without asking, which nobody had ever done for me in my life. "Written ones. Daily. If the Brokers come back with their erasers, we'll lose the memories again, but the paper remembers differently. Paper is stubborn."

"You write down your own lives," I said. "Like accounting."

"Like gardening," she corrected. "A memory you tend grows back."

I looked at my bowl. I looked at the room full of strangers who had given me food and heat and a chair with a cushion on it, and I waited for the catch, because there is always a catch, because I am a thief and thieves assume the whole world is running the same con they are.

"What do you want from me?" I finally asked.

The room went quiet in a way that wasn't hostile. Tam set down the pot.

"Nothing," she said.

"People don't want nothing. Nothing is expensive."

"Then let's say we want what you'll give freely, when you give it. Which may be nothing. Which is fine." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her eyes were sharp and kind at once, a combination I had previously considered impossible. "Child, do you know how many of us there used to be? Four hundred souls in this village. The Brokers took our names into their archive and sold pieces of us until we were too thin to be worth hunting, and then they forgot to finish the job, and we crawled down here and built walls and grew food and learned to be people without pasts. Do you know what that's like?"

"Yes," I said, before I could stop myself.

She nodded slowly, like I'd passed a test I hadn't studied for. "Yes. I thought so. You have the look. The look of someone keeping accounts because the accounts are all they've got." She touched my wrist, briefly, her hand dry and warm. "You're carrying something that belongs to us. To all of us. Can you feel it?"

I could. I had been feeling it for days, ever since the chapel, ever since Wren said the words *dragon's heart* out loud in the dark. Fragments in my head that weren't human memories at all—heat without burning, the view of the world from very high up, a name in a language that tasted like copper. I had assumed they were just another theft, one more thing I'd lifted off some traveler without knowing its worth.

"They recognize it in you," Tam said, gesturing at the room. "The way you'd recognize a song you heard as a baby. Sit with us tonight. That's all we ask. Sit, and eat, and if you dream of flying, well—" she smiled, "—so does everyone here, some nights. We think it leaks."

* * *

**Wren**

I watched Quentin sit with them, and I will admit—I watched with the particular anxiety of a warden watching a guarded thing walk toward an open window.

This was not part of the mission. The mission was: find the prophesied one, keep them ahead of Voss, get them to the heart before the fire gutters out. Clean lines. But missions rot when you feed them, and I had been feeding mine on lamplit conversations and the specific way Quentin says *obviously* when they are about to do something reckless. Somewhere between the archive and the Underfog, the mission had grown a second pulse, and the second pulse was just a person I didn't want to see hurt.

And now this village was going to make everything worse, in the best possible way.

Because I could see it happening. I could see Quentin—thief, cynic, professional non-believer in kindness—slowly unbending by degrees, like a wire held near a flame. A girl of maybe ten brought them a cup of cider and Quentin thanked her, actually thanked her, with eye contact. An old man showed Quentin his garden records, decades of careful handwriting documenting cabbages, and Quentin read them with genuine attention, and I heard them say, quietly, "My mother might have kept books like this. I wouldn't know. I sold that part."

The old man patted their shoulder and said, "Then we'll write you a new book. First page starts today."

Quentin laughed. It startled them; I could see that. Laughing appeared to be something that happened to Quentin rather than something Quentin did.

Later, by the common fire, Tam took me aside while Quentin was surrounded by children demanding to know if the stories about fog-hounds were true. (Quentin was embellishing shamelessly. The hounds were not, as claimed, the size of horses.)

"The fire is dying," Tam said. It wasn't a question either. Nothing in this village was a question; they had learned to live on statements.

"Dying," I confirmed. "Weeks, maybe. Less if Voss gets there first. Her people are already smothering the vents."

"And the one carrying the memories knows the price?"

I looked across the fire at Quentin, mid-performance, arms wide, describing a hound the size of a barn while children shrieked with delight. "Not yet."

"Then you have a hard night ahead of you," Tam said, "and I'm sorry for it. But hear an old teacher out: whatever you decide to tell them, tell it soon. Belonging is a strange medicine. It makes the bitter pills worth swallowing—but only if you swallow them while the warmth is still in your mouth. Wait too long, and the warmth curdles into betrayal, and you'll lose them twice."

"I already betrayed them once," I said. "The date was a mission. I told them that."

"Mm. And did they believe you'd stopped?"

"No."

"Good," said Tam. "Distrust keeps a person honest. Now go. Your thief is telling lies to children and enjoying it far too much. That's a person waking up. Don't waste the morning of them."

* * *

**Quentin**

I dreamed of flying that night, and woke up crying, which was confusing on several levels.

Not sobbing. Just—tears, present on my face without my permission, the way frost arrives on a window. In the dream I had been enormous and warm and the ground was a rumor below me, and there had been a name in my mouth, my mouth, a name with too many consonants, and I had been singing it into the wind for no audience at all except the mountains, and the mountains had listened the way old things listen.

I lay in the borrowed bed—actual bed, straw mattress, wool blanket that smelled of lavender someone had died before remembering—and took inventory, because inventory is what I do instead of praying.

Mother's face: gone, sold years ago, price of rent.
First theft: gone.
The sea: gone, sold to a broker's clerk who wanted to feel something vast.
The fisherman's drowning: spent, feeding hounds.
Flying: present. Warm. Not mine. Or—here was the question that had been circling me for days like a moth—not mine *yet*.

There was a knock. Wren, wrapped in a blanket, hair doing something structurally ambitious, holding two cups of something steaming.

"You're awake," they said.

"Observant."

"You're crying."

"Also observant. It's the flying. Apparently I leak." I sat up and took the cup. Some kind of bark tea, bitter and grounding. "Wren. The fragments in my head. The dragon ones. Are they going to hurt me?"

Wren sat on the edge of the bed, and the mattress dipped, and I noticed I had begun cataloguing small facts about Wren the way I used to catalogue exits from rooms. "No," they said. "But they're going to change you. They're already changing you. Memory always does, even borrowed memory. Even stolen memory." A pause. "*Especially* stolen memory, maybe. You carry it more carefully than an owner would."

"Why are you telling me this now? At dawn. In a stranger's house. With terrible tea."

"Because today we rest, and tomorrow we walk, and the day after that we reach the heart, and I promised myself I would not lie to you again even by omission, and omission has been getting heavy." They looked into their cup. "The prophecy has an ending, Quentin. I've read it. I haven't told you what it says."

Outside, the village was waking. I could hear the dog again, and the fiddle—Aldous, practicing, badly, beautifully. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, and forty people who had been erased began another day of writing themselves down, tending themselves like gardens, refusing to be small.

I had lived twenty-six years in the Veil and no one had ever saved me a seat by a fire. One night here and I understood, with terrible clarity, exactly how much I had to lose.

"Tell me," I said.

Wren opened their mouth—

—and that was when the bell changed. Not the hour-bell. The gate-bell, fast and hard, the alarm rhythm, and Tam's voice cutting across the courtyard below, clear as a blade:

"Fog on the north road! Fog that walks! Everyone inside—they've found us!"
