## 10. Voss's Offer, Repeated

The first thing you learn in the Veil is that fog doesn't walk. Fog drifts. Fog loiters. Fog has the ambition of damp laundry.

So when Tam said *fog that walks*, my body understood before my mind did, and I was already moving—snuffing the candle, sweeping Wren's pack off the floor, grabbing the bread knife because apparently that was my arsenal now.

"Back door," I hissed. "Now."

Wren didn't argue, which scared me more than the bell did. They moved like someone who had drilled for exactly this, shoulder to the wall, one hand on the small of my back steering me through the dark as if I were the guest in my own house. Behind us, below us, the courtyard of the Unremembered erupted into controlled chaos—the kind of chaos people have practiced. Children went down a hatch. Old Sefa stood in her doorway with an iron poker and an expression that dared the fog to try something.

Then the hounds came over the north wall.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night. They were not dogs. They were shaped like dogs the way a rumor is shaped like the truth—right silhouette, wrong substance. They moved through the fog rather than on top of it, and where they passed, lamplight bent toward them, hungry. Memory-hounds. I'd sold enough stolen thoughts to Brokers' runners to have heard about them the way you hear about drowning: secondhand, and never believing it until the water's in your mouth.

One of them turned its head. It didn't have eyes. It had a *pull*, and the pull was aimed at Wren.

"They track thought," Wren said, low and fast. "Not scent. Quentin—the memories in your head, the ones you've stolen—they can smell those too. You're carrying a pantry and they're starving."

"Flattering."

"Run anyway."

We ran.

* * *

The Village of the Unremembered had been built by people who expected this night eventually, and it showed. Every third house was a decoy with cold hearths and empty rooms. Every real house connected—through root cellars, through crawlspaces under pig pens, through a rope-bridge strung between two rooftops that Sefa crossed at seventy years old without holding on, which told me everything about how long she'd lived here and what she'd survived to do it.

We went up. Hounds don't look up; they're made of fog and appetite and neither floats well. From the roofline I watched them tear through the decoy houses below, dissolving doors, unraveling bedsheets, finding nothing but absence—and then, one by one, going still. Turning their eyeless heads.

Toward the center of the village.

Toward the well.

"No," Wren breathed beside me, and I knew before they said it. "They're not tracking us anymore. They've found what they came for. A sample. Something of ours."

The well water, I realized. Our cupped hands. The bucket we'd both touched.

Or simpler than that: me. My whole reeking cargo of stolen lives, soaked into every surface I'd leaned against for two days.

Below, the hounds stopped searching. They sat in a perfect circle around the well, patient as furniture, and out of the walking fog itself came a woman.

I knew her face from memory-broker coins, from the seal pressed in gray wax on every licensed trade in the valley, from the murals in the archive where she stood taller than mountains holding scales. Maren Voss, Broker-Head of the Veil, and in person she was smaller than her legend, gray-haired, unhurried, dressed like somebody's kind aunt come to collect for the temple fund.

She looked up at our rooftop directly. Of course she did.

"Quentin Eriksen," she called, and her voice carried the way sound carries in dreams, arriving everywhere at once. "You've been busy. And you've been keeping company you shouldn't." A pause, mild, almost fond. "Come down. Nobody here needs to be hurt. I only want to talk."

Sefa appeared at my elbow on the roof, poker still in hand. "Don't," she said. "She talks beautiful. That's the whole trick of her."

"I know the trick," I said. "I run it myself for a living."

Which is why, God forgive me, I believed I could survive the conversation.

* * *

They gave us tea.

That's the detail I keep turning over, the one that won't sit still. Voss's hunters had dragged Wren down from the roofline—I was "invited" separately, which is to say escorted by four hollow-eyed soldiers who smelled of nothing at all, not sweat, not smoke, not life—and instead of chains we got chairs. A parlor in the Broker's waystation at the village edge, a room furnished with genuine taste: good fire, good carpet, tea in real porcelain. Someone had chosen the curtains. Someone had once cared whether this room felt safe.

Voss poured for all three of us herself. Her hunters stood along the walls like coatracks, and Wren sat rigid in their chair with two of the eyeless hounds pooled at their feet like spilled ink, close enough to touch, which was the message. The tea was chamomile. I will never drink chamomile again without tasting fear.

"You're not eating the pastry," Voss observed. "That's all right. I wouldn't either."

"Why am I here?" Wren asked.

"You're collateral, dear. Nothing personal." She turned to me, and her eyes were the pale gray of fog over slate, and they were kind, and that was the worst thing in the room. "Quentin. Do you know why the Veil works?"

"Magic can't cross in," I said. "Founders built it that way."

"Founders built it *that way*, yes, but do you know why it holds? Why the fog stays, why nothing burns, why no spell has ever cracked it in a hundred years?" She sipped her tea. "It holds because it's fed. Not with magic—with memory. The founders bound the Veil with human remembering. Every generation, the keepers of the valley gave their pasts to the fog to keep the walls thick. That's what the memory trade was, originally. A tithe. Beautiful, really. The valley forgets so the world can't find what's hidden inside it."

"And what's hidden inside it," I said, "is a dragon."

Something flickered across her face—not surprise. Disappointment, maybe, that we'd gotten there without her getting to tell me. Or relief. With Voss I suspect even she couldn't always tell the difference.

"The last dragon," she agreed. "Dying for a century. Its heart banked down to a coal beneath our feet. And its fire—" she set down her cup with a small, precise click "—is the last true magic left in the world. Do you understand what that's worth? What the wars outside would do for it? There are kingdoms out there that would crack the world open to hold a single flame of it. That's why the founders hid it. That's why we keep it hidden."

"That's why you hoard memories," Wren said quietly. "You found where the power pooled. In forgetting. In the trade."

"We *steward* it." For the first time her voice sharpened, and I saw the steel under the aunt. Then it smoothed again, deliberate as a hand smoothing a bedsheet. "But stewardship requires sacrifice, and lately the sacrifice required is specific. The prophecy book your friend stole from our archive—it's a thousand years of careful observation, Quentin, not superstition. The prophesied blood can renew the binding. Permanently. One drop, freely given, and the Veil seals forever. No more tithes. No more generations of forgetting. The valley becomes self-sustaining. Safe. Forever."

"And the dragon?"

"Sleeps on. Peacefully. As it has for a century." She smiled at me, gentle as snowfall. "You'd be a hero of the valley. You'd want for nothing. House, income, protection—you've clearly developed a taste for protection, given whose roof you've been sleeping under." Her gaze slid to Wren, almost playful. "And them? They walk away. Whole. Unhurt. I give you my word."

"There it is," I said.

"There what is?"

"The price list. Blood from me, dragon-memories out of my head—that's the part you didn't say out loud, but the book says the blood must be given *knowing*, doesn't it? Aware of what it carries. So I'd have to surrender the fragments too, so there's no ambiguity in the giving. Two payments wearing one coat." I wrapped my hands around the teacup because they wanted to shake and I wasn't going to give anyone the satisfaction. "Here's my question, Broker-Head. If the drop is freely given, why does the giver have to be alone first? Why did your hounds circle the village for hours before you walked in? Why does *freely given* need this many soldiers?"

Voss regarded me for a long moment. And then, infuriatingly, she laughed—a real laugh, short and warm.

"Oh, you're exactly what the book promised. Sharp." She leaned forward. "Let me tell you something true, Quentin, since you've earned it. Yes, the ritual needs willingness, and yes, willingness is easier to arrange than you'd think. But I would genuinely prefer you said yes awake. Because I've read the other ending too. The one where the fire gets fed instead of smothered. The one where the fog burns off." Her kindness dropped away entirely, just for a breath, and underneath it was something old and terrified and vast. "Do you know what's out there, beyond the passes? Do you have any idea what the world did with magic, back when magic was everywhere? I've seen the founders' records. Cities that ate themselves. Children conscripted at six to feed war-engines. The founders didn't build a cage, boy. They built an ark. And you—" her voice softened again, terribly sincere "—you were born holding the key, and you've spent three days with a stranger whispering that you should use it."

I looked at Wren. Wren looked back—bruised at the cheekbone where the hunters had handled them, hands bound in iron-thread cord, sitting very still the way a person sits when they're calculating odds and don't like any of them. They shook their head, barely. A fraction.

*Don't,* that nod said. *Whatever she's offering, don't.*

And the awful thing, the thing I've had a hundred years of nights to chew on since, is that some traitor part of me wanted to say yes. Not because I believed her—though she was good, she was so good—but because of the shape of the offer itself. Safety. Belonging. A chair kept by a fire, guaranteed, in perpetuity, signed in official wax. I had spent twenty-six years renting myself out in pieces so that someone, somewhere, might let me stay. And here was the most powerful person in the valley sliding the contract across the table and saying: *stay.*

That's what cages are, I understand now. Not bars. Offers.

"I have a counteroffer," I said.

Voss raised an eyebrow.

"You leave the village alone. All of them—the erased people your ledgers say don't exist. You let Wren go. And I'll consider your terms."

"You'll *consider* them." She sounded more amused than angry, which frightened me. "That's generous negotiating for someone with no cards. Look around, Quentin. The hounds have already mapped every thought you carry. If I wanted to simply take—"

"Take the blood of a prophet unwilling," Wren said, "and watch the binding fail. The book cuts both ways, Voss. You know it does. That's why you're pouring tea instead of holding knives."

Silence. The fire popped. One of the hounds rippled, restless, and Voss quieted it with two fingers, absently, the way you'd soothe a dog.

"Well," she said at last. "Well." She stood, smoothing her skirts, and the aunt returned, warm and terrible. "Then we'll do this the slow way. I'll make you a promise instead of an offer, Quentin Eriksen: I will not hurt the stranger. Today. Whatever you choose, they walk out of this room breathing, because I keep my word—it's the only currency I've ever valued. But I'm taking them with me. Insurance. And every day you spend deciding is a day they spend in my keeping, and every day after that, the accommodations get humbler. You have until the moon turns." She gathered her gloves. "You know where to find me. Everyone does. That's rather the point of being a Broker."

At the door she paused, half-turned, and for a moment she wasn't looking at me at all but at Wren, and her expression was one I couldn't read—grief, maybe, or recognition.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I'm sorry it's you. I've read the book more times than anyone alive. I know how the last page goes."

And then they were gone—Wren between the soldiers, the hounds folding into the fog behind them, the whole procession swallowed by gray within ten steps, as if the valley had inhaled them.

I sat in the parlor alone with three cups of chamomile going cold, and I did not cry, and I did not scream, which surprised me. What I did was look at the room properly for the first time. The good fire. The tasteful curtains. The comfortable chairs.

A trap doesn't have to look like a trap. The best ones look like mercy. The best ones pour you tea.

I thought about the founders' ark, and the wars outside, and the dragon sleeping on a century while a valley forgot itself one memory at a time so nobody would ever go looking. I thought about Voss's face when she spoke of the world beyond the passes, and how her terror had been real, and how real terror in powerful people builds the cruelest cages of all—because they truly believe they're keeping you safe in them.

And I thought about the last page. *I know how the last page goes.* She'd said it like a threat. But I had seen the book too, and there was a version of the ending she hadn't read right, I was sure of it, the same way she'd misread everything else—seeing only the burning, never the light after.

The moon had twenty-nine days left in it.

I intended to need about four.

Because I knew a vault with locks older than the Brokers, and I knew the gray market's back channels better than anyone breathing, and I knew—this was the part that made me smile, cold and small, in that warm and terrible room—I knew every trick of the thieving trade, and I had just been handed the richest target in the valley's history:

Maren Voss's own certainty that I would come to her, begging terms, on her schedule.

Nobody suspects a thief who's supposed to be waiting.

I finished her tea. It seemed rude not to. And then I walked out into the fog that had caged my whole life, and for the first time I saw it exactly as it was—not weather, not mystery, just walls, worn thin in places nobody had checked in a hundred years.

Time to steal my dragon back.
