## 11. What Quentin Steals for Love

There are three rules to stealing a memory, and I'd learned them all before I learned my mother's face, which is not the boast it sounds like.

One: nobody guards what they've forgotten. Two: the best lock is a person who believes they're doing you a favor. Three: never steal anything you want to keep.

I was about to break rule three so thoroughly that I half hoped some future thief would come and lift the memory of me breaking it. It would be kinder than living with it.

The Brokers' vault sat beneath the Gray Market like a stone heart under gray ribs, down past the counting rooms where clerks weighed recollections on brass scales—two years of first love for one of surviving a shipwreck, that kind of arithmetic—and past the cold room where memories too dangerous to sell were kept in jars that hummed when you walked by. I had sold in that market for eleven years. I knew its rhythms the way a pickpocket knows the sway of a crowd. Tonight the Market was holding its monthly Reckoning, when Voss's brokers tallied the quarter's takings, which meant every clerk, guard, and memory-hound handler would be upstairs arguing over ledger entries while the vault below held nothing but its own smug silence.

Mostly.

"You're certain about the rotation?" I asked.

Wren didn't look up from the map they'd inked across three stolen napkins and, at some point, the back of my hand. "The hounds change shift at the second bell. The handlers take them through the east gallery because it's warmer. And Voss keeps the prophecy book in the inner case because she thinks paper can't lie."

"Paper lies constantly," I said. "It's people who tell it what to say."

"That's why we're taking it."

They were crouched beside me in the crawlspace above the salt cellar—a disused storage room that smelled of a hundred years of brine and neglect—and they were wearing the gray coat I'd stolen for them off a laundry line two nights ago, and their hands were steady, and mine were not. That was new. In eleven years of lifting other people's pasts out of their heads, my hands had never once shaken. Apparently all it took was wanting something for myself.

Rule four, which I'd never taught anyone because it was really just rule three wearing a disguise: fear is just greed with its collar up.

* * *

Getting into the vault proper was the easy part, because the easy part had been arranged months ago, by me, against exactly this contingency, back when my biggest ambition was dying warm. Every fence in the Veil kept a favor banked with the vault's night-warden, a hollow-cheeked man named Odell who had sold so many of his own memories that he'd begun renting out the empty rooms in his head to whoever paid. I had once sold Odell a memory of his daughter's wedding—the real one, lifted from her cousin—which he played behind his eyes every night before sleep. He believed I'd given it to him free. He believed this because I'd told him so, gently, while he wept.

That was the trade, you understand. That was always the trade. You took someone's past and left them a story, and if you were very good, the story held until morning.

I found Odell at the second landing, where he stood his post with a lantern turned low and a face turned lower. When he saw me his whole body did the thing bodies do when they're relieved and ashamed at once.

"Quentin." A whisper. "You shouldn't be here."

"No," I agreed. "You should walk me to the third door and forget you saw me. That's the safest thing. If Voss finds out you let me pass without a fight, you're ruined. But if you fought me—if you *chased* me and lost—you're a warden who did his duty against a master thief. She might even promote you."

I watched the idea move through him like weather. This was the oldest trick in my book and it worked because it wasn't entirely a lie: Odell *would* be safer having fought me. The Veil forgave failure. It never forgave complicity.

"She'll ask why you came," he said.

"For Wren." Let her chew on that. "And for a book."

His brow creased—the small honest confusion of a man whose head has too many rented rooms. "A book?"

"The one she's been reading aloud to the inner circle. Paper can't lie, Odell." I smiled the smile that had paid my rent for a decade. "But it can be stolen."

He walked me down. At the third door he grabbed my sleeve, and for one terrible moment I thought he was going to remember something true, some fragment of the wedding still lodged in him like a splinter. Instead he said, "Your mother would be proud of you," which was the cruelest thing anyone had said to me in years, since neither of us knew whether it was true or even what she looked like, and then he shouted an alarm into the empty corridor with convincing anguish, and I hit him—softly, professionally, a love-tap, a stage fall—and he went down snoring honestly enough to fool God.

I hated that I was good at this. I hated it the way you hate a scar that's saved your life.

* * *

The inner vault was cold enough that my breath showed, which was deliberate; memories keep better in the chill. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, and on them sat the jars, thousands of them, each labeled in a clerk's careful hand: *harvested 3rd of Thaw, male, 40s, grief, high grade*. Grief, high grade. Somewhere in those shelves was a jar containing everything I'd ever sold, including the last of my mother, bought back by the Brokers at market price and shelved like preserves.

I could have taken her. That was the thought that ambushed me between the second and third aisle—that I could crack my own jar open right now, pour my mother's face back where it belonged, and walk out of this story a smaller and happier thief. My hand was actually moving toward the shelf marked ERIKSEN, Q. — PERSONAL HOLDINGS before I caught it.

Not tonight. Tonight there was a person in a cell above us and a page in a book that had my name on it, and both of those things had a claim on me that predated my own.

Wren touched my shoulder. I hadn't heard them come down the service stair. Their eyes went to the jar shelf, then to my face, and whatever they saw there made them say, quietly, "After."

"After what?"

"After we burn the fog off this valley, I will personally help you rob this entire room." A pause. "Starting with your shelf."

It was such a Wren sentence—larceny offered as tenderness—that I laughed, which nearly got us killed, because the laugh echoed, and somewhere in the dark above us a hound whined in its sleep.

* * *

Wren was in a holding cell two floors up, behind a door that cost more than my building, and getting them out required the single most expensive trick in my repertoire: I gave a memory away.

The cell block was tended by a junior broker named Pell, young, ambitious, terrified of Voss, and—critically—recently promoted past a rival named Cassmer, whom he suspected of scheming against him. I knew this because I had picked it out of his head six weeks ago while selling him a soldier's courage-memory, which is the sort of thing that sounds monstrous when I describe it and felt merely like weather when I did it. You stop noticing the moral weight of theft when theft is your climate.

I came around the corner of the cell block limping, bleeding artistically from a scalp wound I'd given myself against a doorframe (rule one: nobody guards what they've forgotten, but everybody guards a wounded stranger), and collapsed at Pell's feet with a sealed ledger clutched to my chest.

"*Cassmer* sent me," I gasped. "He's moving Wren tonight. Quiet transfer. Voss doesn't know. Here—" I shoved the ledger at him. "Transfer orders. His seal. He wanted it deniable, so he hired outside help, and now I want out, because when Voss finds her prize gone, the outside help hangs."

Pell read the forged orders—I'd copied Cassmer's seal from a wine bill, seals are only ever as good as the vanity of the man who commissions them—and I watched him arrive at the conclusion I'd built for him: that here, at last, was the knife to gut his rival. All he had to do was verify the prisoner was gone, and report it, loudly, in front of witnesses.

"Open the cell," I whispered. "Confirm it's empty. Then raise the alarm on *Cassmer*, not on me. I'll vanish. You'll be the hero."

He opened the cell.

The cell was not empty. Wren sat inside it looking mildly bored, which was acting, because I'd spent the previous evening coaching them through exactly this: *when the door opens, don't lunge, don't thank me, look inconvenienced.* They stood slowly, stretched, and said, "Took you long enough," in a tone that made Pell's suspicion pivot beautifully away from rescue toward conspiracy.

Then I took Pell's memory.

Not all of it—just the last ninety seconds, which is a delicate extraction, like lifting the cream off milk. I have done it hundreds of times. I did it now with my fingers at his temple and my breath steady, and I felt his recent past come loose into my hands: a bleeding stranger, a forged ledger, an opened door. What remained in Pell's head was a gap, and gaps get filled. By morning he'd remember confronting Cassmer's plot and reporting it, and the story would hold until—

Well. Until it didn't. Until Voss, who was not a hollow-eyed warden or an ambitious boy but a woman who had built an empire on knowing what people forgot, traced the seam in Pell's evening and pulled.

Which meant we had perhaps until dawn. Maybe less. I took the ninety seconds anyway. Some prices you pay knowing the bill comes due.

We went down the service stairs together, Wren's hand in mine, both of us running, and I want it recorded that in that moment—wanted, hunted, mid-heist, the alarm bell beginning to toll above us—I was happier than I had ever been in the Veil. That should have warned me. Joy is the most expensive thing in the valley and nobody ever reads the tag.

* * *

The prophecy book was in the inner case, as Wren had promised, and the inner case was locked, and the lock was the reason I'd saved it for last, because the lock was not a lock.

It was a memory-trap. The Brokers' finest work: a mechanism keyed to thought itself. Touch the case with intent to steal, and the case would read your intention and take payment—one of your own memories, ripped out at random, screaming, as the price of entry. Half the thieves who'd tried the vault had been found wandering the Market afterward, rich in everyone else's pasts and bankrupt of their own.

But here is what the Brokers' engineers had never understood, because understanding it would have ended their business: a memory cannot be taken from someone who is giving it.

I pressed my palm to the cold glass and I thought, deliberately, clearly, offering it up like bread on water: *Take the night of the lamplight. Take the first time I saw Wren.*

The case drank. I felt the tug behind my eyes, the old familiar cold hook, and then—nothing. The glass stayed shut. Because the trap couldn't harvest what I'd already surrendered; intent to lose a memory isn't theft, and the mechanism, elegant and merciless, had no purchase on a gift.

Then I did the actual breaking, which was mundane: I'd palmed the case's true key off a drunk assistant curator three nights ago, because every grand magical defense in the Veil was bolted onto a perfectly ordinary keyhole by a workman who wanted to go home to dinner.

The case sighed open.

The book was small. That was the insult of it—my whole prophesied life, bound in calfskin no bigger than a hymnal. I took it, and Wren took the lantern, and we ran, and the running was glorious, alarms tolling, hounds baying somewhere behind us, Wren laughing breathlessly as we burst up through the salt cellar and out into the fog-choked street where the gray air closed over us like water.

Triumph. Real triumph. I had broken into the impregnable heart of the valley and stolen back the two things I loved, and we were alive, and the fog swallowed our footprints.

It wasn't until we reached the safehouse—an abandoned tannery by the Underfog gates, reeking of old lime—and lit a candle, and Wren barred the door, and we finally, finally stopped moving—it wasn't until then that I opened the book to see how my story ended.

I'd read the earlier pages a hundred times in my head since the archive: the face that was my face, the name that was my name, the dragon coiled in ember-dark around a figure standing unburned in fire. I'd let myself believe the ending would match. Prophecies are generous in the middle chapters. It's the endings where they collect.

The final page was newer than the rest. Different ink. A hurried, faithful hand.

It showed the heart of the valley, the coal beneath the roots, and a figure kneeling before it with arms open, pouring light into the dark—not light like a lamp. Light like a body burning. And around the figure, drawn in strokes so fine they might have been smoke, were small falling shapes: pages, maybe. Or feathers. Or memories, leaving.

Underneath, in letters that had been written by someone who knew:

*The fire takes what feeds it. The one who wakes the dragon will not remember waking it.*

The candle guttered. Wren read it over my shoulder, and I felt them go still in a way that had nothing to do with fear of pursuit.

"Quentin—"

"It's fine," I said, which is what I say instead of crying. I closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "It's fine. We stole it. That was the job. Everything after this is extra."

Outside, far off across the fog, the hounds began to sing again—and this time there were more voices than there had been, and they were not behind us anymore.

They were ahead. Between us and the Underfog. Between me and the coal at the bottom of the world where, according to a book I'd just committed a felony to possess, I was going to burn myself down to ash and a blank page.

Wren's hand found mine in the dark.

"There's another way down," they said. "Through the drowned chapel. It's longer. It's flooded. It's—" They stopped. "It's the way I came, the first time. Before you were born, maybe."

"Then we take it," I said, and pocketed the book next to my heart, where it lay like a coal of its own. "I've got until dawn before Voss unpicks Pell's head. Show me the water."
