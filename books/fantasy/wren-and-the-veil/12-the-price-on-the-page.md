## 12. The Price on the Page

I have read the book four times now, and each time I hoped the last page would say something different.

Books are stubborn that way. They do not revise themselves out of kindness. I know this better than most — I spent a hundred years being the subject of one, more or less, and nobody asked my permission about how the story went either.

We had made camp in the drowned chapel's vestry, which sounds grander than it was. The chapel itself was a stone ribcage half-swallowed by the Underfog, its bell tower tilted like a drunk leaning against a friend who has already walked away. The vestry still had a roof, though, and a dry corner behind the altar where Quentin could sleep without the damp getting into their lungs. They were asleep now, finally, curled around their satchel of stolen memories the way other people curl around a pillow. Even unconscious, they guarded it. A thief to the marrow.

The prophecy book sat open on my knees.

It was not a beautiful book. The founders had bound it in plain gray leather, the color of everything in this valley, as if even their prophecies had been afraid of drawing attention. The pages were foxed at the edges and the ink had gone brown in places, but the words held. Prophecy ink always holds. It is written by people who know exactly how long it needs to last, which is the only kind of foresight they ever actually have.

I turned to the final page for the fifth time.

I should explain something about wardens. We are trained to read hard things aloud. There is a discipline in it — if you can speak a grief, you have measured it, and a measured grief cannot ambush you. My teacher, Sef, used to make me read the casualty lists after every skirmish with the fire-hungry kingdoms, standing straight, voice level, until the names stopped tasting like ash. *A warden who flinches,* she said, *teaches the flame to flinch.*

So I did what I was trained to do. I read it aloud.

"*And the one foretold, whose blood remembers what was stolen, shall feed the last flame with their own remembering.*"

My voice came out steady. Good. Keep going.

"*What they have lived, they shall give. What they have loved, they shall lose from within. Mother's face and stranger's kindness, every name they ever learned to say—*"

Stop there. That's enough.

I didn't stop. That's the thing about reading aloud. Once your mouth knows the shape of the sentence, it finishes it whether the rest of you consents or not.

"*—and they shall walk out of the fire whole of body and hollow of past, a stranger wearing their own face, and they will not know why the world weeps for them, nor why one stands beside them weeping loudest of all.*"

Below that, in a smaller hand, cramped and hurried as though the writer had wanted it over with:

"*This is the mercy of it: they will not grieve. Only those who remember them will carry the weight. Let the prophesied one be told before, not after, so that their choice may be a true one. A sacrifice made blind is no sacrifice. It is theft.*"

*Let them be told.*

Well. Someone in that century-old room had a conscience. It clearly hadn't extended to writing the price anywhere except the very last page, buried under a hundred years of dust and a Brokers' archive catalog number, but it was there. It counted. I decided, sitting alone by a dead altar with a sleeping thief ten feet away, that it counted.

Then Quentin said, from the dark, "Read it again."

---

They weren't asleep. Of course they weren't asleep. Quentin Eriksen sleeps like a cat in a house full of dogs — technically resting, functionally listening. I have watched them nap upright against a tunnel wall while hounds bayed two bends away. I once saw them wake because a memory-broker three stalls down changed the pitch of his haggling.

"Go back to sleep," I said, and closed the book, which was the single least subtle gesture of my entire long life.

"It's the last page." They sat up. In the chapel's thin gray light their eyes caught what little there was, and I remembered — not for the first time, not for the last — that the prophecy had drawn that face surrounded by fire, and that I had spent decades telling myself drawings lie. "You've been turning pages all night, Wren. You think I don't count? You've read the ending five times and every time you got to the last leaf you went quiet like someone walking past a grave."

"That's dramatic."

"You're dramatic." They held out a hand. "Give it."

"No."

"Wren." Their voice did the thing it did in the Gray Market when a broker tried to lowball them — pleasant, patient, and absolutely immovable, a river deciding about a rock. "You read it out loud knowing I was awake. Some part of you wanted me to hear it. So give me the book, or tell me plainly, but don't do the thing where you decide what I can survive knowing. I've had brokers do that to me my whole life. I'm not dating one."

"We're not — " I started, out of pure reflex, and then stopped, because we had kissed twice in the last day, both times mid-crisis, and arguing taxonomy felt like rearranging furniture in a burning house. "That's not the point."

"It's entirely the point." They crawled over, took my wrist — gently, which was worse than firmly — and turned the book toward themselves. "Let me see what my ending costs."

I could have fought them for it. I want that on the record. I am a warden; I was trained to fight things larger and older and more stubborn than I am, and Quentin was none of those things, merely determined, which is honestly the worst of the three. But Sef also taught me that a secret kept *for* someone becomes a cage built by the keeper, and I had just finished explaining to Quentin, not two days ago, that the Veil was exactly that — a cage built by people who called it protection right up until it rotted into tyranny.

Hypocrisy is heavy. I let go of the book.

Quentin read.

I watched their face instead of the page, which is a coward's habit and an honest one. I saw their eyes move down the lines I knew by heart now. I saw the moment they reached *what they have loved, they shall lose from within*, and I saw — this is the part I will carry longer than any wound — I saw them understand it, calmly, completely, the way they understood lock mechanisms and broker tells and which travelers carried memories worth taking. No flinch. Just the quick, competent assessment of a professional encountering a new kind of price.

"Huh," they said.

"*Huh?*" My voice cracked on it. "That's — the book says you forget *everything*. Your mother's face. Every name. Me. It says you walk out of the fire not knowing why anyone is crying, and — and it says the ones who remember carry it, Quentin, it says *me*, specifically, it wrote me in, a hundred years ago, weeping beside a stranger —"

"I know. I read it." They looked up. "Did you notice it also says I get told *before*? Whoever wrote this wanted me to choose with my eyes open. That's more courtesy than this valley has ever shown anybody." They turned back a page, scanning. "Where does it say I die?"

"It doesn't."

"The vault copy showed burning. You said — in the archive, you said the last page showed the prophesied one burning."

"Burning *awake*, maybe. Or burning away. The ink smears there — water damage, a century of it." I heard my own voice doing the thing I hated, reaching for comfort like a drunk reaches for a bottle. "There's room in it for you to live."

"There's room in it for anything. That's what makes it a prophecy and not a contract." Quentin set the book down between us, carefully, the way they handled other people's memories. Then they said, very quietly, "You were going to leave, weren't you."

I am a century-old warden, the last of my order, sworn to a dying dragon and fluent in six dead tongues, and that question undid me faster than any of Voss's hunters ever had.

"When?" I said, which was a confession shaped like a deflection.

"Just now. When you finished reading. You did the breathing thing you do before you lie to me — you think I haven't noticed you breathe differently? — and then you closed the book and you looked at the door. Not the tunnel we came in through. The *other* door. The one going deeper." They tilted their head. "You were going to go feed the fire yourself. Weren't you. Wardens can feed it. That's the whole job, isn't it? That's why there aren't any of you left."

I said nothing, which in the acoustics of that ruined chapel was a shout.

"Don't," Quentin said.

"You don't understand what it asks of — "

"I understand *exactly* what it asks. I just read it. It asks everything I remember. And you were about to pay it instead, with a hundred years of memories, with — " their voice finally frayed, just at the edge — "with every version of me you've decided I am since the lamplight. You'd walk into the fire and come out hollow and I'd never even know what you'd done. I'd just find you gone and some coal roaring and spend the rest of my life thinking I drove you off." They laughed, wet and short. "That's not protection, Wren. That's just theft. You'd be stealing my ending. It's the only thing I've ever had that was actually mine."

"The fire needs remembering," I said. "It doesn't care whose. Mine is heavier. A hundred years of watching, Quentin — the wars outside, the founders' faces, the dragon when it was *alive* — it would burn hotter on mine. It would work better. That's not heroism, it's arithmetic."

"It's arithmetic the way Voss does arithmetic." They leaned forward. "'For the greater good, therefore I decide for everyone.' You told me her whole tragedy was that she believed she was kind. Don't stand in a drowned church telling me you're going to be kind *at* me."

"That is not the same — "

"It's *adjacent*." They grabbed my hands then, both of them, and theirs were shaking, which frightened me more than the hounds had, more than Voss, more than the page. "Listen to me. I sold my mother's face years ago. Did you know that? First winter I was starving and a broker paid triple for childhood memories and I told myself I'd keep the best one, the important one, and then rent came due. I don't remember her. I have been hollowed out already, piece by piece, by choice, for bread. Everything the fire wants from me, I've been losing in installments my whole life." Their grip tightened. "The difference is this time I get to choose what it's *for*. This time it buys something. Do you understand? It's the first trade I'll ever make where I'm not the one getting robbed."

"And if what it buys isn't worth — "

"It buys the sky." Simple. Certain. "It buys the fog off this valley. It buys every hollowed soldier in Voss's army waking up and remembering their own names. It buys a village full of erased people becoming real again. And yes — " quieter now — "it apparently buys you, standing there crying, which frankly seems like a downgrade for you, but the book says you stay, and I've noticed the book is rude but not usually wrong."

"You're not allowed to make jokes about this."

"I'm absolutely allowed. Jokes are the only currency I've never sold." They exhaled. "Here's what happens now. We sleep — actually sleep, both of us. At dawn we take the deep tunnel down to the heart. Together. You walk in front because you're the warden and you know the way, and I walk behind because that way you can't sneak off to immolate yourself, and if you try, I will pick your pockets so thoroughly you'll be lucky to have lint left. I know a hundred tricks you haven't seen yet. I've been saving them."

"For what?"

"For exactly this." A ghost of their market smile, the one that had hooked me under the lamplight a lifetime ago — eleven days ago, in truth, but the heart keeps its own calendar. "You said the date was a mission. Fine. Consider this one mine."

I wanted to argue. I catalogued the arguments — that the fire might take me anyway, that a warden's death was accounted for and a civilian's wasn't, that love was precisely the thing that disqualified Quentin from making clear-eyed choices here. I lined them all up like soldiers.

Then I looked at their face, and I thought about what the anonymous scrivener had written in his cramped, guilty hand. *A sacrifice made blind is no sacrifice. It is theft.* He had understood, a hundred years ago, that you cannot protect people by editing them. That the founders' great sin hadn't been building the cage — it had been deciding, generation after generation, what the caged were allowed to know.

I had spent a century despising that decision. I was not going to make it myself in a vestry at dawn.

"All right," I said.

Quentin blinked. "All right? Just — that fast? No speech? No 'as the last warden, I forbid — '?"

"As the last warden," I said, "I formally note my objection, log it in the order's records — " I tapped my own temple — "and yield to the prophesied one's judgment, because the alternative is becoming Maren Voss with better hair. Sleep. We leave at first gray."

They lay back down, and within minutes their breathing slowed into genuine sleep this time, the guarded kind loosening at last. I sat with the book in my lap and did not read it again. Some sentences you only need to hear once for them to live in you permanently, like a name, like a debt.

But near dawn, half-dreaming myself, I heard Quentin murmur something into the dark. I leaned close.

They were repeating it. The line. *What they have loved, they shall lose from within* — over and over, soft, the way you rehearse a speech or learn a spell, committing it to the deepest shelf of themselves where brokers couldn't reach and neither could I.

Making sure, I realized, that whatever else happened — whatever the fire took — they would at least go into it having chosen to memorize the cost.

Outside the chapel, somewhere far above the flooded dark, the fog began to stir, and beneath our feet, faint as a pulse through stone, something ancient shifted its weight — as if the heart below had heard us coming, and was waiting to learn what we would bring it.
