## 13. The Heart Beneath the Valley

*Quentin*

The tunnel stopped being a tunnel somewhere past the drowned chapel and started being a throat.

That's not a figure of speech I'm proud of. I've spent my whole life describing things in ways that make them easier to sell — a soldier's terror becomes *a vivid account of the northern campaigns*, a widow's grief becomes *a rare vintage, gently aged*. But there was no way to dress up what we were walking down into. The walls had gone from stone to something older than stone, ribbed and dark, warm to the touch in a way that made my teeth ache. The air tasted like the second before a lightning strike.

"Tell me that's normal," I said.

Wren walked ahead of me with the prophecy book strapped across their back and their hand trailing along the wall like they were greeting an old horse. "It's a root system. Or a blood vessel. The founders' records disagree."

"The founders' records disagree about whether the valley is sitting on top of a tree or an artery."

"The founders disagreed about most things. It's why they built a cage instead of having a conversation." Wren glanced back at me, and even in the near-total dark I could see the shape of their worry. It was becoming familiar. That was its own kind of terrifying. "How are your memories?"

I took inventory the way I used to take inventory of coin after a bad night at the market. The dragon-fragments were still there — fire, flight, that name in the dead tongue that sat behind my teeth like a stone I kept almost swallowing. My own memories were still there too, thinner than they'd been a week ago, the way a coat gets thin at the elbows. Mother's face. The first theft. Wren at the lamplight, saying my name like they'd practiced it.

"All present," I said. "Ask me again in an hour."

We descended for what felt like days and was probably six hours. The Underfog thinned as we went — that was the strange part. Down here, beneath the valley, the fog that ruled everything above simply *stopped*, cut off at a boundary so clean it might have been drawn with a knife. Below the line: clear air, warm air, air that moved. Above it: the gray forever.

"It can't reach the heart," Wren said when I pointed this out. "The fog is the Veil. The heart is what the Veil exists to hide. They don't mix."

"And if someone puts the fog and the heart in the same room?"

Wren didn't answer, which was an answer.

The last stretch of the descent opened without warning. One moment we were squeezing through a crease in the world barely wide enough for the book on Wren's back; the next, the crease exhaled us into a cavern so large that my eyes refused to process it and offered me, instead, a series of smaller lies: a cathedral. A buried sky. A wound.

And at the center of it, on a shelf of black glass that might have been rock or might have been something cooling out of a forge ten thousand years ago —

A coal.

Not a fire. Not a dragon. A coal the size of a fallen man, guttering, pulsing light the color of the last coals in a hearth the morning everyone has overslept. Each pulse lit the cavern roof, which was not stone but scale — miles of it, vast as weather, the ribs of a creature grown into the bones of the earth so long ago that the earth had learned to call the ribs its own.

"Oh," I said. Very eloquent. Very Quentin Eriksen.

"That's her," Wren said quietly. They'd gone still in the way people go still around wild animals, or churches. "That's the last of her."

The coal pulsed again, slow as a sleeper's breath, and I felt it in my chest — felt something behind my sternum lean toward it like a plant toward a window. And inside my head, the stolen fragments turned over. Fire, yes. Flight, yes. But underneath those now, closer to the surface, something else: a feeling of being *watched over*. Of being small and warm while something enormous breathed above you.

She wasn't just dying down here. She'd been *kept*. And some buried shard of her knew it, because the shard lived in my head.

"We should go down," Wren said.

"Wren." I caught their arm. "Look."

Because I'd seen what they hadn't yet, my eyes being better trained for this particular skill: noticing other people before they notice you. You don't survive in the memory trade by being unobservant. You survive by spotting the mark, the watcher, the broker's runner, three seconds before they spot you.

At the far edge of the cavern, where the black glass shelved up toward the tunnels on the south side, there were lights. Lantern-light. Orderly lantern-light, spaced at even intervals, the kind of light that comes from people who have been issued their lanterns by an institution.

And moving between the lights, figures. Dozens of them. Descending in files, silent, unhurried, taking up positions around the coal's shelf the way soldiers take a hill — not rushing, because no one was going anywhere.

"Hollowed," Wren breathed.

Every one of them. I could see it even at this distance, the particular quality of their movement — no wasted motion, no hesitation, no *self*. I'd sold memories to men who came back hollowed. I'd watched it happen to a pickpocket named Sef who got on Voss's bad side; he still walked and worked and ate, but the thing looking out of his eyes had been evicted, and whatever moved in after paid no rent. There were forty of them at least. Maybe more, still coming down the southern tunnels.

And at their center, standing at the edge of the coal's shelf with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman admiring a garden she'd finally gotten around to weeding —

Maren Voss.

* * *

*Wren*

I had imagined this moment perhaps two hundred times across the long years of my wardenship: arriving at the heart, at last, with the prophesied one beside me. In most versions there was kneeling. In several there was singing. In none of them — not one — was Maren Voss already there with an army of the emptied, unrolling instruments of smothering across the dragon's deathbed.

"They're ash-casters," Quentin whispered. Their voice had gone flat and professional, which I was learning meant fear. "See the canisters? Gray market make. Memory-salt packed in kiln clay. You crack one open over a flame and it eats the heat right out of the air. Poachers used them to smoke out — "

They stopped.

"To smoke out dragons," I finished.

"Ancient history," Quentin said. "Nobody's needed them for a hundred years."

"Somebody kept the molds," I said.

Below us, hidden behind a spur of rib-bone wide as a ship's hull, we watched Voss's people work. They moved with the terrible efficiency of the hollowed — no complaints, no rest, no error. Four of them carried a canister the size of a coffin to the shelf's edge and set it down with a gentleness that made my skin crawl. There must have been twenty such coffins already, ringed around the coal at careful intervals.

"She's not going to burn it," Quentin said slowly. "She's going to *starve* it. Salt the air. Smother the last flame and keep it smothered." They laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "You know what that makes her? The best broker in history. Because that's all the Veil is, isn't it? A vault. And she's not sealing the vault shut — she's putting the treasure permanently under lock. Eternal scarcity. Nobody gets the fire. Not the war-mages outside. Not us. Not even the dragon. Forever."

"Yes," I said. Because there was no point pretending otherwise. The founders had built the cage as mercy — a dying creature hidden from a hungry world, a hungry world protected from its own appetite. But mercy rots when it's administered by the ambitious. A century of Brokers had turned *protect* into *possess*, and Maren Voss had turned *possess* into a religion.

"How many canisters before it's done?" I asked.

Quentin's eyes did the arithmetic. I'd noticed they did that — flicked left, quick, when calculating. "Twenty around her now. If each covers a third of the shelf... she needs maybe thirty. Thirty-five to be safe." A pause. "They've placed nineteen."

"So we have minutes."

"We have minutes," Quentin agreed. "Or we have nothing, depending on how you count."

I counted nothing. I counted a century of watching, a decade of searching, a lamb at the lamplight whose face had been drawn in a dead woman's book before either of us was born — all of it come down to nineteen canisters and forty hollowed soldiers and minutes.

Then I looked at Quentin, and I revised my count.

Because Quentin wasn't looking at the soldiers. Quentin was looking at the *canisters* — reading them, the way they read everything, for resale value and weakness. And their expression was not the expression of a person counting nothing.

"What are you thinking?" I asked.

"I'm thinking," Quentin said, "that memory-hounds track stolen thoughts. That hollowed soldiers are people with all their thoughts stolen. And I'm thinking about what happens to an empty house when you throw a lifetime of somebody else's memories through the front door." Their jaw set. "I've got a dragon in my head, Wren. Fire and flight and a name nobody's spoken in a thousand years. And every one of those soldiers down there is running on nothing."

"You'd wake them," I said. "All of them. At once. In the middle of a battle, underground, with Voss standing right there."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"It *is* reckless."

Quentin grinned at me — that specific grin, the one from our first evening, the one I now understood had been aimed at me like an arrow since the moment we met. "Good. Reckless is the only currency I've ever had that spends clean."

Below us, the twentieth canister touched down on the shelf.

Voss raised her hand.

* * *

*Quentin*

Here's a thing nobody tells you about dread: it's boring. All that buildup in stories about the trembling knees and the cold sweat, and sure, fine, all true, but underneath the theater it's just waiting. Dread is the world's longest pause. And I have never once in my life been good at pauses.

So when Voss raised her hand over the twentieth canister, and the signal-horn down on the shelf gave its low moan, and forty hollowed faces turned in perfect unison toward the tunnels — including ours, because of course she had scouts in the north passages too, because of course she did — I did the only thing I know how to do with a pause.

I broke it.

"Now," I said, and pulled the stopper off myself.

I'd spent the whole descent doing something I'd never done before: not hoarding. Every trick of my trade runs on keeping the goods close, parceling them out, controlling the flow. Instead I'd been doing the opposite — gathering every dragon-fragment in my skull, stacking them against the inside of my forehead like cordwood, ready to throw.

Fire. The first fire, seen from below, from very small and very new.

Flight. The muscle-deep joy of a body that owns the wind.

The name. Her name, in the dead tongue, which I now spoke aloud into the cavern, and which rang off the ribs overhead like a bell struck in a church:

"*Ashveth.*"

The word went out of me like blood leaving a wound, and with it went everything stacked behind it — a century of a dragon's living, hurled across the black glass shelf, through the warm dead air, into forty empty houses with their doors hanging open.

What happened next, I will be explaining badly for the rest of my life.

The nearest soldier — a big man, farmer's shoulders, Voss's livery stretched across them — stopped mid-stride. His head came up. His mouth opened. And out of him came a sound I'd heard exactly once before, from a hound-handler in the Underfog: the sound of a person remembering they exist. It isn't a word. It isn't a scream. It's somewhere between a sob and a gasp, like surfacing from deep water.

He turned. Not toward the coal. Toward *Voss*.

Then the second soldier remembered. Then five. Then a dozen — staggered, uneven, some dropping to their knees, some clutching their heads, one woman laughing, actually laughing, a cracked and rusty laugh like a gate opening after years shut. The orderly lantern-ring of Voss's army dissolved into forty separate human emergencies.

And through the middle of it, calm as Sunday, Maren Voss turned her face up toward our ledge, found us in the coal-light without any trouble at all, and smiled.

"There you are," she called, and her voice carried the way voices do in cathedrals. "I wondered how long you'd hide behind the ribs. Come down, Quentin. Both of you. You're just in time to watch me save the world."

"You're going to smother it!" Wren shouted back. "You're going to bury the last fire in the world under salt and call it salvation!"

"I'm going to end the *appetite* for it." Voss spread her arms, and the surviving hollowed — the ones my storm hadn't reached, maybe fifteen of them, still empty, still hers — closed ranks around her. Around the canisters. Around the guttering coal, whose pulse had begun, faintly, to quicken. Had it heard its name? Was that possible?

"Outside this valley," Voss said, "they are already building engines that run on burned memory. Armies that fight for the pleasure of forgetting what they've done. You think the founders caged her out of cruelty? They caged her out of *mercy*, and the world has only gotten hungrier since. I am the last adult in the nursery, child. And I am *not* letting you feed the fire."

She snapped her fingers.

Fifteen hollowed soldiers turned toward us as one, and began to climb.

Beside me, Wren drew the warden's blade they'd carried folded for a hundred years. Below us, the freed soldiers — the remembering ones — were finding their feet, and some of them, God help us, were picking up stones.

And deep beneath everything, slower than a heartbeat but faster than despair, the coal pulsed once, twice —

— and did not gutterso much as *lean*, the way a sleeper leans toward a voice calling them home.

"Quentin," Wren said quietly, "whatever you're about to do —"

"Do it fast," I said. "I know."
