## 14. Battle of Borrowed Selves

I want it noted, for the record I am fairly certain no one will ever keep, that when the hollowed soldiers came down the last stair into the heart-chamber, my first thought was not *I must save them* or even *we are going to die*. My first thought was: *that's a lot of coats.*

There were thirty of them. Maybe forty. They filled the cavern mouth like smoke poured backward into human shapes, and every one of them wore the same gray Broker-issue coat, buttoned to the throat, and behind their eyes was nothing at all — that particular polished absence you see in men who have sold so much of themselves that what's left is just a well-lit room with the furniture removed. I knew the look. I'd seen it in mirrors on bad years.

Voss stood among them like a matron at a school recital, hands folded, pleased with her choir.

"Quentin," she called across the coal-light. "Last chance to do this the kind way."

"You've used that line before," I shouted back. "It's wearing thin. Like everything else about you."

Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Their fingers were cold as river stones and shaking, which was somehow worse than if they'd been calm. Dragons' wardens, apparently, shake too. Good to know the mystique has cracks.

"What's the plan?" Wren whispered. "Tell me there's a plan."

"There's a plan," I said.

"Is it a good plan?"

"It's the only plan I have, so functionally yes."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a thief of memories: after enough years, you stop thinking of them as things you take and start thinking of them as things you carry. A warehouse with bad shelving and no inventory list. For most of my life that had been a curse — it's hard to know who you are when your head is a lost-and-found for strangers.

But we had spent three days underground learning what else lived in my head. The dragon's memories. Fire, flight, a name in a dead tongue that made my teeth ache when Wren said it aloud. Generations of Brokers, skimming the valley's memory-trade without knowing it, had been passing the dragon's own past along like a coin nobody could spend — until it landed in the pocket of a petty thief with nowhere to put it.

And the hounds had tracked us by those memories. Which meant the memories were loud. Which meant —

"They're hollowed," I said to Wren, watching the soldiers advance, boots crunching on bone-dry stone. "Not empty. Hollowed. There's a difference."

"I don't follow."

"A room that's been emptied still has walls." I pulled the satchel off my shoulder — the one I'd packed in the vault, the one full of everything I'd stolen back, including the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against my spine. "A hollowed man isn't gone. He's just been *evicted.* And I have spent my entire miserable career learning how to break into occupied heads." I grinned, and felt my face do something feral with it. "Time to try the reverse."

Wren stared at me. "You're going to *pickpocket an army* — "

"I'm going to give them a refund."

* * *

You should understand how memory-work actually feels from the inside, because the books get it wrong. It isn't light and it isn't sound. It's pressure. When I lift a memory from a drunk soldier in a fog-bound tavern, there's a moment where his whole self leans toward me like a door opening, and I slip through with whatever I'm stealing held flat against my palm so it doesn't snag on anything. Do it wrong and you tear something. Do it right and neither of you bleeds.

What I did next was none of those things. What I did next was stand in front of forty hollowed soldiers and open myself like a floodgate.

I reached for the dragon first, because the dragon was loudest. Fire. Not the idea of fire — the *fact* of it, heat as a native language, the sky seen from above the clouds where the clouds look like a second country. I took that memory and I didn't hold it flat against my palm. I threw it.

The air over the soldiers *bent.*

I can't describe it better than that. The fog that leaks down here from the Veil's roots, the damp gray breath of the valley — it rippled outward from me in a ring, and where it touched the front rank of soldiers, they stopped.

All of them. At once. Forty pairs of boots going silent mid-stride, forty gray coats suddenly very still, and in forty hollow faces something moved. Not thought exactly. More like a man asleep turning toward a window because the sun found him.

"*Burn,*" one of them said. His voice came out rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose. "*I remember burn—*" And then he frowned, confused at his own mouth, because it wasn't his word. It wasn't even a word. It was the dragon's, worn like an oversized coat.

"More?" Wren breathed beside me.

"Much more."

I opened wider.

* * *

Let me tell you about some of the people inside those soldiers, because I met them that night, all at once, the way you meet everyone at a party where someone's knocked out a wall between rooms.

There was a woman whose head I flooded with the smell of bread — not the dragon's memory, one of mine, stolen off a baker in the market two winters ago, kept because I couldn't bring myself to sell the smell of yeast and morning. She dropped her rifle. Actually dropped it, clattering on stone, and pressed both hands over her mouth, and through her fingers she said, "My mother baked on Thursdays. Why do I know that? *Why do I remember Thursdays?*"

There was a boy — he couldn't have been more than nineteen under the coat, nineteen and hollowed so young it made something in my chest crack sideways — who caught the memory of rain. Just rain, a summer storm off a farmer's mind, the drum of it on tin roofing. He looked up at the cavern ceiling as though he could see through a hundred feet of rock to a sky that had never once rained on the Veil, and he began to cry without changing expression at all, tears sliding down a blank face, like his body remembered crying before his self did.

There was a man who got a fragment of flight and fell to his knees. Not wounded. *Homesick.* For a sky he'd never owned, borrowed off a dying dragon through six generations of careless brokers and lodged by accident in a thief's skull.

And underneath all of it, threading through every one of them, I pushed the dragon's name. The dead-tongue name, the one that burned. I didn't say it. You can't say it; the mouth won't hold it. But I pushed it the way you push a thought, and forty minds that Voss had swept clean as new-fallen snow heard it, and snow started sliding off roofs all across that cavern.

"That's enough," Maren Voss said.

She didn't shout it. That was the terrible thing about Voss — she never shouted. She said it the way she'd offered me tea and protection and civic duty, reasonable as rent, and forty soldiers flinched toward her voice like puppets feeling their strings remember them.

"Enough," she repeated, and raised one hand, and I felt it then — the counter-pull. She was doing what I was doing, but inverted. Where I gave, she took. I watched the baker-woman's face go slack again, the Thursday-morning light draining out of her eyes, and rage hit me so hot and fast I understood, for one vertiginous second, why dragons burn things.

"No," I said. "You don't get to un-people them twice."

"Quentin." Her voice softened, which was worse. "They chose to forget. Most of them. Grief, debt, shame — they came to us and asked to be emptied, because forgetting is a mercy. I have built a valley on mercy."

"You built a *warehouse* on mercy and you live in the office."

I threw more memories.

* * *

This is the part I'll be proud of until the day I die, however many days the fire leaves me, and I want to be precise about it, because it wasn't cleverness. Cleverness I had plenty of. This was something I'd only ever read about in other people's hearts.

I stopped fighting like a thief and fought like a *giver.*

Every trick I knew ran in reverse. The misdirection that slips past a mark's guard became a doorway held open. The gentle extraction that lifts a war-memory out of a soldier's skull without tearing anything became a *gentle insertion* — I set whole lives down inside those hollowed heads like a landlady returning furniture to storage units, piece by piece, this is your chair, this is your window, this is your name.

I gave a gray-coated sergeant the memory of a daughter learning to whistle. I gave a scout the taste of river water, cold and mineral, from a fisherman I'd robbed four years ago. I gave a big slab-faced man with a scarred chin the memory of dancing badly at a wedding while everyone laughed *with* him, and he stood in the middle of a battle and laughed, out loud, alone, remembering laughter.

And the dragon's memories I saved for last, and I spent them like a miser breaking open the strongbox. Flight to the ones who dreamed of leaving. Fire to the ones whose anger needed somewhere honest to live. And to every single one of them, the name. The burning name. *You had a self once,* the name said, in the wordless way names say things. *It is still yours. Nobody else's claim on you outranks your own.*

The front rank turned around.

I want that sentence to sit there a moment. The front rank of Maren Voss's private army, hollowed men and women in gray coats, turned their backs on her — slowly, joints creaking like doors long shut — and faced the thing they'd been marching to smother, the guttering coal that was all that remained of the last dragon, and they *bowed.* Some of them. The ones furthest awake. The baker-woman knelt. The boy with the rain on his face raised one hand toward the ember-light like a man greeting a sunrise.

"He remembers us," someone whispered. "The fire remembers us."

"The fire doesn't—" I started, and then stopped, because Wren had gone rigid beside me, staring at the coal, and the coal was — brighter. Faintly. Pulsing in a rhythm that was almost, almost breathing.

*Oh,* I thought. *Oh, it's been listening the whole time.*

* * *

"Kill them," Voss said quietly. "The ones that turned. Kill the thief. Take the warden alive."

The rear ranks advanced — maybe twenty still hers, still dark behind the eyes, though I could see the light leaking in even there, faces twitching, mouths moving around half-remembered words. She'd hollowed them deep. Or they'd asked to be hollowed deeper than most. Either way they came on, and the ones I'd woken rose up to meet them, and it was a battle now, ugly and close, gray coats grappling gray coats in the ember-light, and I couldn't tell friend from foe except by the eyes.

So I stopped looking at coats and looked at eyes, and I kept working. Sweat ran down my spine. My head ached like a struck bell. Every memory I gave away left a gap, and I was starting to feel the gaps — little holes in the fabric of me, places where a summer storm or a whistling child used to be. I'd spent years terrified of losing memories. Here I was, spending them like a drunk spends wages.

Worth it, I told myself. Worth it worth it worth it.

Then Voss herself came at me through the melee, and I learned what a woman looks like when she has hoarded ten thousand stolen lives and knows how to use every one of them.

She moved like a soldier, a dancer, a duelist — three skills from three different donors, layered smooth. Her hand closed on my wrist and her mind hit mine like a door slamming, and suddenly I was drowning in *her* takings: a hundred griefs, a thousand betrayals, the whole gray ledger of her career pressing down on my thoughts, trying to shove me out of my own head.

I did the only rude thing I know how to do in a stranger's mind.

I stole from her.

Right there, hand to hand, eye to eye, I reached past her attack and *lifted* — one memory, snatched blind off the top of her stack, and it hit me even as I took it:

A girl. Small, dark-haired, standing in a Broker's parlor, maybe eight years old, saying in a careful rehearsed voice: *"I want to forget my village burned. Please."* And a kind-faced broker leaning down, gentle as Sunday, saying: *"Of course, little one. We're here to help."*

Maren Voss staggered back as if I'd struck her. Because I had. I was holding her oldest wound up between us like a lantern, and her face — her controlled, reasonable, merciful face — came apart around it.

"That's mine," she whispered.

"Yeah," I said. "Funny how that works, isn't it? How it feels when somebody takes."

Behind her, the battle was turning. Half her army remembered itself now, and the other half was faltering, and the coal beneath the valley pulsed brighter with every memory I set loose, drinking them in like — like a heart remembering blood.

Voss looked at the coal. Then at me. And I watched her make the calculation I'll be dreaming about for the rest of my life, whichever side of the fire I end up on: she turned from the fight entirely and ran — not away, but *down*, toward the heart, toward the last flame, arms outstretched, screaming words in a dead tongue that I realized with a sick drop of my stomach she'd stolen from someone too.

Smothering words. Sealing words.

"WREN!" I shouted. "THE FIRE — SHE'S GOING TO — "

And the whole cavern went white.
