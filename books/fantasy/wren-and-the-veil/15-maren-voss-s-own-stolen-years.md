## 15. Maren Voss's Own Stolen Years

I want it noted—I would note it myself if there were anyone left to take minutes—that I did not enjoy watching Quentin Eriksen kneel on the floor of the heart-chamber. There is a difference between satisfaction and pleasure, and I have spent forty years learning the difference because no one else in this valley bothers to.

The coal guttered beneath us. That was the whole of everything, right there: a dying ember the size of a sleeping dog, cradled in a bowl of black stone, breathing its slow red breath into the roots of the world. The Veil fog above us was its exhalation. Every gray morning every citizen of this valley had ever complained about was dragon's breath, thinned and cooled and stretched over a hundred years of hiding.

And kneeling beside that ember, held by two of my hollowed soldiers, was a pickpocket who thought they'd come here to save the world.

"Get up," I said. "You'll bruise your convictions."

Quentin looked up at me. Even now—cornered, outmaneuvered, Wren in my custody one level above and their prophecy book locked in my vault—they had the gall to look *amused*. That's the thing about thieves. They think the whole world is a mark. They think everyone has an angle, so when they meet someone honest about theirs, they assume it's a performance.

"You're not going to kill me," Quentin said.

"No."

"Because you need my blood."

"Because you're useful," I corrected. "Killing is what people do when they've run out of imagination. I hoard imagination. It's rather my entire business model."

One of my soldiers shifted behind them—a big man, former garrison, face smooth as a peeled egg where his memories used to be. I'd taken twenty-six years off him personally. He didn't remember his name. I did. It was Aldric Fenner, and he'd once cried at his daughter's christening, and now he'd hold a stranger's shoulder for as long as I told him to, and feel nothing about any of it.

That is not cruelty. I want that minuted as well. That is *mercy*, of the only kind this valley can afford.

* * *

Let me tell you how I got here, since no one else will remember it after tonight, and I have always believed a woman should deliver her own eulogy.

I was born under the fog like everyone else, in the year the Brokers still called themselves Custodians, back when the lie was fresh enough to have a shine on it. My mother sold memories—not stole them, *sold* them, hers, legally, in the old days when you could sign yourself down to nothing for a sack of flour. She sold her wedding day when I was four. She sold my father entirely when I was six; after that she'd look at me sometimes like she was trying to place me, the way you squint at a word you know you know. By the time I was eight she'd sold so much of herself that the brokers stopped paying. Nobody buys a house that's been gutted. She sat in our rooms on Candlemaker Row and smiled at strangers and could not tell you why.

The Custodians came for me when I was nine. Not because I'd done anything. Because erased children make excellent material—we had no families left to object, no neighbors who remembered us, no paper trail worth the ink. They took me to the archive and they tested me, the way you test ore, and they found my talent: I could hold other people's memories without dissolving into them. Most handlers burn out in five years, go mad or vacant or both. I could carry forty years of a stranger and walk out of the vault whistling.

Do you understand what that made me? Not powerful. Powerful came later. First it made me *useful*, and usefulness, in the valley, is the only currency that compounds.

I climbed. Of course I climbed. Everyone climbs or drowns; the only question is whether you pretend you're not climbing. The men ahead of me pretended. They wore their hoards like modesty garments—oh, these few memories are merely for safekeeping, oh, we keep the valley's past out of civic duty—and all the while they slept on beds of other people's weddings and woke up richer in *lived* than any ten citizens combined. I watched Broker-Head Corvin Hale, my predecessor, spend an afternoon inside a dead vintner's memory of his daughter learning to walk, and then come out and vote against the bread subsidy with a straight face.

So yes. I took the chair from him. And yes, I took his memories while I was at it—all ninety-one years of them, including the ones he'd stolen from others, including a summer in the southern passes that he'd bought from a smuggler at obscene cost because it contained sunlight, actual unfiltered sunlight, which none of us born under the fog had ever seen.

I kept that one. I'm not ashamed. I opened it maybe twice a year, alone in the head-office, and stood in light that wasn't mine and never would be, and I told myself: *this is why the work matters. So that someday no one has to rent the sun.*

* * *

Here is what Quentin doesn't know, standing there with their thief's shrug and their prophesied blood, and what Wren certainly doesn't know, glowing faintly like a lantern that's read too much poetry about itself:

I have read the founders' letters. All of them. The originals, not the sanitized copies the archive shows to visitors. Ellery Vane wrote to her co-founders in the last winter of the war, and I have her actual hand in front of my mind's eye whenever I choose, because I took it from her descendant along with three generations of guilt:

*"They are burning the sky again. The northern coalition has found a way to bottle dragon-fire. If they get the last heart, they will not use it to warm their homes. You know what they will do with it. We must hide it where fire cannot be found—under fog, under forgetting, under a valley that does not appear on maps because maps require memory and we will take the memory ourselves. God forgive us. Better a small cage than a burned world."*

A small cage than a burned world. That is not tyranny. That is arithmetic. Horrible, unbearable, *necessary* arithmetic, done by people who had watched cities become weather.

The founders meant the Veil to be temporary. They thought the war would burn itself out in a decade and they could open the fog like opening windows after a long illness. But wars don't burn out. Wars bank, like coals. And the world outside never got less hungry—it just changed which flag the hunger flew under. Every generation, some envoy or refugee or mad pilgrim found the valley's edge and pressed their face against the fog, and every generation the Brokers turned them away, and I will tell you what those travelers carried in their pockets, because I've robbed enough of them: requisition orders. Munitions manifests. Letters home describing what flame-weapons do to a hillside full of people.

The world outside wants what sleeps under this valley. It has always wanted it. The only reason your bread is cheap and your streets are quiet and your children have never heard an air-raid horn is that Maren Voss keeps the lid on.

And now a pickpocket with a dragon's memories sloshing around in their skull wants to pull the cork.

* * *

"You're staring at the coal," Quentin said.

"I'm auditing it," I said. "There's a difference."

"It's dying."

"It's *resting*. There's a difference there, too, though I notice your generation has trouble telling them apart." I walked closer to the ember-bowl. The heat came off it in waves you felt in the teeth more than the skin, a low animal patience. "It has been dying for a century. Slowly. Gracefully. The fog holds because the fog is its breath, and its breath grows thinner every year, and in perhaps another fifty years it would exhale its last and the Veil would fail on its own—quietly, gently, with the heart already dead, so that whatever the world found when the fog lifted was ash and a story." I turned back to them. "That was the plan. That was always the plan. Patience. Let the fire die of natural causes, seal the chamber, keep the valley forgotten, and the weapon dies in its sleep instead of waking up in someone's arsenal."

"And now?"

"Now *you*." I let myself smile. It's a good smile; I paid a memory-broker extra for a diplomat's charm to wear it with. "Now there's a person walking around my valley with fragments of the dragon's own memory in their head, and a warden of the old blood whispering prophecies in their ear, and a book in my vault that says the fire can be *rekindled*. Fed. Woken. Do you understand what rekindling means? It means the heart starts beating again. It means the breath comes strong. It means the fog lifts—and the moment it lifts, every government, every guild, every ambitious bastard with a grudge and a map learns that the last dragon-fire in the world is alive and located." I shook my head. "You wouldn't be saving the world, Eriksen. You'd be mailing it an address."

"The world has a right to—"

"The world has a right to nothing." My voice came out harder than I'd intended, and I noticed, distantly, that the coal-light had gone orange in the hollows of the soldiers' faces. "The world had its chance. The world spent three centuries turning dragon-fire into artillery. The founders hid it because the alternative was genocide by escalation, and you—you were not even born, you have never seen a city burn, you have never held a soldier's memory of what burning does—you want to crack the cage because the cage *offends* you. Freedom is a luxury of people who aren't standing next to the powder store."

Quentin was quiet a moment. Then, in that maddening mild tone: "Your mother. What was her name?"

The question went through me like a splinter finding old wood.

"That's not relevant."

"What was her name?" They couldn't stand, not with Fenner's hands on their shoulders, but they leaned forward, and their eyes had that awful thief's attention, total and warm, the look that makes marks feel like the only person in the room. "You remember everyone's memories. Everyone's mothers. Everyone's weddings. You're wearing a dead vintner's afternoon somewhere in that head of yours. So what was your mother's name?"

I knew it. Of course I knew it—I keep meticulous records, even of things that hurt, *especially* of things that hurt, because pain is data. Her name was Sable Voss. She liked salt plums. She hummed when she cooked. She sold my father's face for flour and mine for medicine and in the end she smiled at strangers on Candlemaker Row until winter took her, and I was nine, and no one claimed me for eleven days.

"Sable," I said.

"And you remember her face?"

Silence. The coal breathed. Somewhere above us, through a hundred feet of root and stone, the fog moved over the valley, gray and endless and safe.

"No," I said. "I remember forty thousand faces that aren't hers. I am the finest archivist this valley has ever produced, and my own mother's face is a hole shaped like a hole." I stepped closer, close enough that the ember-heat touched us both. "That is what you don't understand, Eriksen. I am not standing here despite the cost. I am standing here *because* of the cost. I know exactly what forgetting buys and what it charges. I've paid the bill my whole life. And I am telling you, as the only person in this valley qualified to give an opinion—the Veil is worth it. Whatever it costs. Whatever it cost *me*. A small cage than a burned world. The founders were right. Hale was right. I am right."

"You believe that," Quentin said softly.

"With everything I have left."

"Yeah," they said. "That's what scares me. Because a year ago, I'd have taken your deal. Bread and rent and somebody calling it belonging. I nearly took it from you in that office, you know. Your kindness. It was good kindness. Warm all the way through." Their eyes flicked to the coal, then back to me, and there was something in their face I recognized and hated, because it was my own face, forty years ago, before the climbing started. "You're what I'd be if I'd said yes."

"I'm what you'd be if you'd *survived*," I said. "There's a difference."

"Not as much as you think."

* * *

That was when the messenger came down the spiral stair—one of my archivists, white-faced, holding a slate with the vault-seal broken across it in the old alarm-script.

I read it twice.

Wren was gone from the holding room. Not escaped—*gone*, in the sense that the guards remembered putting them in the cell and could not remember anything after, a clean surgical absence, three hours wide. And the prophecy book was missing from its case, and in its place lay a single gray feather that my archivists swore had not been there an hour before.

And the vault's ledger showed one further irregularity. Someone had accessed the deep stacks. Someone had browsed, sampled, and returned—with perfect professional courtesy—a single memory from the founders' collection.

Ellery Vane's letter. The one about the burning sky.

I stood very still, holding the slate, while the coal breathed and the soldiers waited and Quentin knelt there watching me with those thief's eyes that missed nothing, and I understood, with the cold clarity of forty years in the trade, that I had finally met a better thief.

"Well," I said. "Clever girl."

Quentin's mouth curved. "She gets it from me."

Above us, faint through the stone, something vast shifted in its sleep—and for the first time in a hundred years, the coal under the valley brightened.
