## 16. The Choice at the Coal

The heart of the dragon looked, up close, like nothing so much as a dying hearth.

I had imagined—God help me, I had imagined *wings*. Scales like hammered bronze, a voice that shook the roots of the mountain. What I got instead was a coal the size of a dinner table, sunk in a basin of black stone beneath the valley's deepest root, guttering with a light the color of old honey. Every few seconds it dimmed. Every few seconds I thought: *that was it, that was the last of it,* and then it rallied, stubbornly, the way an old man rallies between sentences he knows will be his last.

Wren stood beside me, ash-smeared, one arm cradled against their chest where a hunter's blade had found them back in the vault. They didn't say anything. There wasn't anything left to say that we hadn't already said badly on the way down.

Behind us, sealing off the tunnel mouth like a cork in a bottle, came Maren Voss.

She had brought forty soldiers with her, which would have been impressive if they'd been soldiers. They weren't, not anymore. They were the hollowed ones—the ones whose memories she'd stripped out over the years and stored like preserves in her vaults, leaving behind bodies that walked when she told them to walk and stood very still when she didn't. They fanned out across the cavern floor with the terrible patience of furniture arranging itself.

"Quentin." Voss's voice carried in the stone chamber the way voices do underground—flat, close, intimate, no matter how far away she stood. "You've had a long night. We all have. Let's not make it longer than it needs to be."

I turned around slowly. Old habit. Never give a buyer your back; they'll talk themselves into haggling while you're facing the wall.

"Maren," I said. "You look tired."

"I am tired." She came forward alone, hands open at her sides, the picture of reasonableness. She'd dressed down for the occasion—no broker's sash, just a gray coat, practical boots. A woman of the people. She'd been rehearsing that costume for days, I suspected. Maybe years. "Do you know what I was doing the night the Veil went up? No, of course you don't. Nobody does. It doesn't matter. What matters is what happens next, and next can go two ways."

"Two ways," I said. "Brokers always count in twos. It keeps the arithmetic simple."

Something flickered across her face—not anger. Recognition, almost. The look of a woman watching a younger version of herself be clever in exactly the way she used to be clever. It should have frightened me more than it did. At the time, mostly, it made me sad.

"Way one," she said, ignoring me, or filing my voice away for later dissection. "You step aside. My people put out the fire. It takes ten minutes and a shovel apiece. The Veil thickens, seals permanent, and this valley goes on being safe. Small, yes. Safe. No war comes through fog that never lifts. No dragon-fire feeds some foreign general's engines. Children here grow up and grow old and die without ever learning there's a world worth burning down."

"And way two?"

"Way two is you feed it." She nodded past me, at the coal. At Wren. "You pour everything you've stolen—and everything you are—into a creature that hasn't flown in a hundred years, and the Veil burns off, and the world comes *in*, Quentin. Everything out there. The wars you've read about in travelers' heads. The men who salt cities because a map has a line drawn crooked. That's what you're proposing to invite through the front door. For what? A sunrise?"

The coal guttered. Rallied. Guttered again.

And here's the thing I need you to understand, because I need someone to understand it, even now, even memoryless: I *wanted* to say yes.

Not to Voss—to safety. I wanted it the way a starving man wants bread, simple and total and without shame. I had spent my whole life in the fog. I knew its rules. You steal, you sell, you eat, you pay rent on a room where the damp comes through in shapes like countries nobody's ever conquered. The Veil was a cage, but it was *my* cage, and I knew every bar of it by touch, and there is a horrible comfort in bars when you've leaned on them your whole life.

Voss saw that wanting. Of course she did. Reading want was her trade the way reading memory was mine.

"You've sold everything, haven't you?" she said, softer now, coming closer. The hollowed soldiers stood like statues behind her. Wren shifted their weight beside me, hurt arm and all, ready to do something stupid and brave, and I put out a hand without looking—*not yet*—because I needed to hear the rest. "Your mother's face. Your first winter. Whatever you were before you were a thief. You're standing there telling yourself you've got nothing left to lose. But you have. You've got the only thing left. You've got *now*. The next hour. The taste of whatever you eat tomorrow morning. Why should that belong to a lizard?"

"She's not a lizard," Wren said quietly. "She's the last of something."

"The last of something that burned cities," Voss said. "Yes. I've read those memories too, warden. I own copies."

The coal pulsed. And underneath my ribs, the stolen fragments pulsed with it—I'd been carrying pieces of her for weeks by then without knowing what they were. Fire seen from above, from a throat's height, the world a smear of green and smoke. A name in a dead tongue that lived in my mouth like a stone I kept almost swallowing. The feeling—this is the one that undid me, this is the one I still reach for and find only a smooth blank wall—the feeling of loving something enormous and mortal all at once, of knowing your own death was coming and finding it acceptable, *acceptable*, because the thing you loved would go on breathing heat into the cold.

She hadn't wanted to burn anything. That was the joke at the bottom of all of it. She'd wanted to *warm* things. The burning was just what happened when the small creatures kept taking and taking and calling it peace.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," I said to Voss. "You said safe and small like they're the same word. They're not. I've been safe. This whole valley's been safe for a hundred years. And you know what we did with our safety? We built a market where people sell each other's Tuesdays. Where a soldier can't remember why his hands shake. Where—" my voice cracked, traitor, and I pushed through it, "—where I sold my mother's face for bread rent, Maren. Bread *rent*. That's what your sealed cage grew. Safety didn't protect anything. It just made the rot polite."

"Better polite rot than open war."

"Says the woman standing in front of a dying heart with forty empty men and a shovel."

Her jaw tightened. For one unguarded second I saw the other woman under the gray coat—not the broker, the erased child, the girl somebody had scraped hollow before she learned to scrape back. She believed it, is the awful thing. She believed every word. Somewhere in that hoard of stolen years she'd assembled a philosophy the way misers assemble coins, one borrowed certainty at a time, and she would die on it, and she would bury the valley under it, and she'd call it mercy the whole way down.

"The fire dies either way," she said. "That's the truth nobody wants. It's guttering now. In a year, in five, it's ash, and the Veil falls anyway, and we're not ready, and the world walks in on us like wolves into a pen. I'm offering you the chance to choose *when*. To choose *how*. Sealed forever. Stone. Silence. Sleep. Isn't rest better than risk?"

And the coal flickered, low, lower—

and in that flicker I felt her. Not the dragon. The *heart*. One beat left in a long ledger of beats, and it was asking, not begging—it had too much dignity left to beg—just asking, the way you ask a stranger on the road for directions. *Which way. Which way from here.*

I thought about my mother's face, which I could not remember, and which I had nonetheless spent eleven years refusing to sell the last fragment of—a sliver of warmth, a smell of cedar and rain, nameless now, sourceless, but *mine*. The one thing in my head I'd never fenced. The one thing I'd lied to brokers about, cheated buyers over, nearly bled for.

If I fed the fire, I lost that too. I understood that. The book had made it plain enough: *the one who feeds the flame pays with everything they remember.* Every theft. Every Tuesday. Wren's laugh, Wren's hand, the lamplight, the date that started all of this. All of it, into the coals, gone, and Quentin Eriksen walking out of the smoke a stranger wearing their own skin.

Voss was offering me survival. Survival, and a room with the damp in familiar shapes, and bread, and the fog, forever.

And I stood there, at the actual moral center of my entire stupid life, and discovered that the question was never *safe versus free*. Voss had framed it that way because framing was her gift. The real question was meaner and smaller and truer:

*A self worth surviving as—or just surviving?*

Because I had already survived as nothing. I'd done it for years. I was an expert. I knew exactly how much of a person you could sell off before what remained stopped answering to the name. I was the valley's leading authority on the subject, and the answer was: less than you think. Less than anyone thinks. There's a line, and I'd crossed it once, in a broker's back room, trading away a summer I couldn't even identify anymore, and what walked out of that room had been wearing me like a coat ever since.

The fog took everything gradually, so gradually it felt like nothing. That was its genius. That was Voss's genius. Nobody chooses the cage; they just wake up one day unable to imagine outside it, and call the inability wisdom.

"No," I said.

"Quentin—"

"No." Louder. My voice bounced off the black stone and came back at me doubled. "You want to know the difference between us, Maren? You hoarded other people's pasts because yours was taken. Fine. Terrible. True. But I'm going to give mine away. On purpose. To something that needs it more than I do. And that—" my hand found Wren's, and held, and I still didn't look away from her, "—that's the whole difference. You take. Even your mercy takes."

The coal flared.

Just once—like a struck match, like a held breath—and in that flare the dead-tongue name rose in my throat unbidden, three syllables I had never learned and had always known, and I understood that she had heard every word. Understood that the fragments in my skull weren't cargo anymore. They were a door, and I was standing on the threshold, and the house beyond was burning down unless somebody carried the firewood in.

The firewood was me.

"Last chance," Voss said, and there was real grief in it, which was somehow worse than the threat. "Step aside, Quentin. Be safe. Be *small*. I'll even leave you your name—you can keep the name."

"That's the most generous offer anyone's made me in my life," I said. "And it's still a cage with my name painted on the door."

I turned to the coal.

I turned to Wren.

And Wren—who had lied to me, saved me, bled for me, read me my own death sentence out of a century-old book and stayed anyway—looked back at me with an expression I will describe even though describing it costs me nothing now, because I don't have it to lose. It was the expression of someone watching a person they love walk toward a door only one of them can fit through.

"Don't," Wren whispered.

"Someone has to remember you were right," I said. "Might as well be everyone else."

I opened my hands over the dying heart, and I began—to the horror of forty hollow men and the last Broker and the whole rotten merciful cage of the Veil—to take out my keys.

Every theft. Every stolen Tuesday. Every face, every war, every summer, every name.

Everything I remember.

* * *

The first thing I gave her was the cedar-and-rain warmth. My mother's last unsold fragment. It went out of me like a breath in winter, visible for a moment, then not.

The coal drank it and brightened.

"Oh," Voss breathed behind me. Not a command. Not a threat. Just *oh*—the sound of a woman seeing, after a hundred years of arithmetic, a number that wouldn't balance.

I reached deeper. The soldier's trembling hands. The baker's wedding morning. The traveler's crooked-map wars, given up freely now instead of fenced, every stolen thing poured home or burned or both, and the fire climbed my arms like ivy, and it didn't hurt, that's the part nobody believes—it *warmed*, it warmed like the thing I'd been reaching for my entire gray life had finally turned around and reached back—

"Stop him!" Voss's voice, cracking now. "Put it out! PUT IT OUT!"

Shovels. Boots. Wren shouting my name in a voice that broke in the middle.

And I, Quentin Eriksen, thief of the Veil, prophesied extinguisher-or-kindler, stood at the coal with my whole life in my cupped hands and made the only choice I have ever been certain of.

I gave her Wren next. Lamplight. The odd questions. The laugh.

I gave her everything I remember.

And the dark came in sweet as sleep—

* * *

—but before it closed, one ember refused the dark. One small, stubborn, unsold thing curled at the bottom of me, too deep for even me to find, and glowed.

Somewhere above, the fog began to scream.
