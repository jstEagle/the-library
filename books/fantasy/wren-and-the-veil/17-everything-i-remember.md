## 17. Everything I Remember

The heart of the dragon was smaller than I expected.

I want you to understand what I mean by that. I had spent three weeks running through the drowned dark of the valley with a warden who turned out to be a dragon's keeper and a book that had my face in it, and in all that time I had built up some image of the thing we were dying to reach. A furnace. A sunken cathedral of fire. Something with architecture.

What was actually there, at the bottom of a shaft beneath the roots of the world, was a coal. One coal, glowing like the last ember in a grate on the last cold morning of the year, no bigger than my two fists cupped together. It sat on a bed of black glass that had once been stone, and it pulsed—slowly, so slowly—like something breathing through a long illness.

"That's it?" I said. "That's the last dragon-fire?"

"It's enough," Wren said.

Wren always said things like that. Flat, certain, as if certainty were just another item of equipment they'd packed for the journey, right next to the rope and the knife. They were kneeling at the edge of the glass, and the light of the coal moved across their face and made them look, for one unguarded moment, exactly like someone I might have fallen in love with on purpose, rather than by accident, during a date that was supposed to be a theft.

Behind us, up the shaft, came the sound of Maren Voss's army: hundreds of boots on wet stone, and worse than the boots, the silence between them. The hollowed soldiers didn't talk. They didn't breathe loud. They climbed down toward us the way water climbs down a wall—patient, total, without opinion.

"They're close," Wren said.

"I know."

"Quentin." They turned to look at me, and their voice did something I hadn't heard it do before. It cracked. Just slightly, just at one seam, the way good pottery cracks before it breaks. "You don't have to do this. We can find another way. There's always—"

"There isn't," I said. "You read the page. You read it out loud in your terrible dramatic voice, which I'll note you only use when you think it'll make me change my mind, and it won't."

"The fire takes everything," they said. "Everything you remember. Quentin, your mother's face—you told me you barely have it left as is. If you feed this thing—"

"If I feed it, it lives. If it lives, the Veil burns off. If the Veil burns off, Voss has nothing left to be queen of." I crouched by the coal and felt its heat on my knuckles, faint, apologetic, a fire that had learned to take up less space. "And if I don't, she smothers it, seals the fog forever, and everyone in this valley spends eternity trading each other's childhoods for bread. Which, speaking as a professional, is not a market I want to see cornered."

"You're making jokes."

"I'm always making jokes. It's how you know I'm terrified."

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being a memory-thief: you get very clear, very early, on the difference between having a thing and keeping it.

I've held a soldier's wedding in my head—the rain on the tent, the bride laughing with her mouth full of cake—and then handed it over to a broker for eleven coins, and felt nothing but the weight leaving. I've carried a fisherman's whole life for two days like a passenger, his father's boat, his daughter's crooked drawings, and given it away at the Gray Market stall before breakfast. Memory is inventory. That's what I told myself for ten years. Inventory goes out; bread comes in; you survive; survival is the whole trick.

Except.

Except there was one memory I never sold, even in the worst winter, even the year I ate boiled bootlace moss and called it soup. My mother, standing in a doorway with flour on her hands, saying my name—not Quentin, the other one, the small name, the home name—and smiling at whatever I'd done, which I no longer remember, because the smile was the part I kept and everything around it went to pay rent.

That one I never sold. And now the fire wanted it. The fire wanted all of it, every shelf and cellar of me, and the awful thing—the transcendent, horrible, wonderful thing—was that some deep thief-instinct in my chest already understood the trade. I had spent my whole life taking memories that belonged to other people. Here, finally, at the bottom of the world, was a buyer offering the only fair deal I'd ever been shown: everything I had, for everything there would ever be.

"Tell me again why it has to be mine," I said, stalling, because Wren's face was doing something and I needed one more minute of looking at it.

"Because the prophecy chose you. Because you carry the dragon's own memories—they woke when you touched the coal upstairs in the chapel, you felt it. Because the fire doesn't take fuel from wardens or brokers or soldiers." Wren swallowed. "It takes fuel from someone willing. And apparently, in the entire history of this cursed valley, that's only ever going to be you."

"Lucky me."

"Quentin—"

"Wren." I said their name and watched them flinch, because they knew—I could see them knowing—that this was the last time I'd say it and know what it meant. "How many times have you imagined this? The fire waking. The fog burning off. The sky coming back."

"Every day since I was nine years old."

"And in all those imaginings, did anyone stay? Did the person who lit it stick around to enjoy the weather?"

They were quiet. The coal pulsed. Somewhere above us the first hollowed soldier's foot found the glass floor of the shaft, a sound like a coin dropped in an empty church.

"No," Wren said finally. "In none of them."

"Right." I stood up. My knees cracked. Even at the end of the world, my knees crack; there's no dignity in prophecy. "So here's what happens. I feed the fire. It wakes. The Veil goes. Voss goes with it, because her whole empire is built on fog and forgetting, and sunlight is hell on both. And I—" I looked at my hands, the thief's hands, quick and clever and suddenly just hands. "I become a stranger. Probably sitting somewhere dumb. Probably hungry. So somebody's going to have to come find me and tell me my name, and I'd really prefer it wasn't Voss."

"It'll be me," Wren said. No hesitation. None. As if they'd rehearsed it, and maybe they had, maybe they'd been carrying that sentence since the lamplight, since the first date, since before the date, back when I was a mark and they were on a mission and neither of us knew we were auditioning for each other.

"Good," I said. "Then we have a plan."

* * *

The first soldier came over the lip of the shaft while I was still kneeling, and I'll give Voss's people this much: they're efficient. He walked straight for the coal with his empty face tilted down at it, and behind him came the second, and the third, and the sound of more, a whole staircase of them filling the dark.

Wren moved to meet him and I grabbed their wrist.

"No. You hold them. Not to win—to buy minutes. I need minutes."

"How many?"

"All of them you can steal."

That got the ghost of a smile out of them. Thieves' humor. Our whole courtship had been built on it. Then they let go of me, drew the warden's blade—a plain thing, notched, older than the valley—and stepped between me and the army of the hollowed, and I stopped watching after that, mostly, except sideways, constantly, the way you can't stop watching a fire you love.

I knelt at the coal and put my hands around it.

And it recognized me.

I don't know how else to say it. The heat changed. It went from the dumb warmth of a dying thing to something with attention in it, something turning over in its sleep and finding my fingerprints familiar, because they were—it had left them there, generations ago, scattered through the valley's gray market in fragments, waiting. Fire, flight, a name in a dead tongue. All those years I thought I'd stolen a dragon's past along with soldiers' weddings and fishermen's daughters. I hadn't stolen anything. It had been mailed to me. Postage due.

"Well," I said to it, conversationally, because if you're about to die you might as well be polite. "You picked a strange courier."

The coal brightened. It liked that. Or it liked my hands, or it liked being spoken to after a century of smothering silence under Voss's fog. Hard to say. Dragons, even dead ones, even one-coal-sized ones, are not forthcoming.

Above me steel rang against steel. Wren fought the way they did everything—flat, certain, economical—but there were too many, and the minutes they were buying were getting expensive, and I understood that I could not wait for a clean moment because there was no such thing. There was never a clean moment. There was only the moment you were in.

So I opened my shelves.

* * *

You want to know what it's like to pour yourself into a fire? Fine. But you won't keep it either, so listen fast.

It starts easy. The inventory goes first—the stock, the trade goods, the war memories and wedding days and stolen summers that were never mine anyway. Those leave almost gratefully, like birds out an opened cage, and the fire drinks them and grows, and the glass floor under me begins to glow, and the first soldier stops mid-stride and makes a sound I will spend no time describing because I won't remember it, but it was the sound of a man remembering he had a daughter.

Then it asks for more. The fire is polite the way hunger is polite. Mother's doorway. Flour on her hands. The small name. I hesitated—one breath, one whole lifetime in a single breath—and then I gave it, because I had spent ten years selling strangers' mothers to buy bread and it would be obscene, obscene, to hoard mine at the end.

The fire roared.

Not metaphorically. The shaft filled with light and heat and a sound like a word in a dead tongue finally pronounced correctly, and the black glass went orange under my knees, and up above, Wren shouted something—my name, probably, or theirs—and the soldiers were stumbling back, clutching their heads as the dragon's memories tore loose from me and flooded the air, and every hollowed man in that shaft was suddenly, catastrophically, himself.

And still the fire asked.

Every theft. Every lie. The first coin I ever earned and the first person I ever cheated to earn it. The lamplight, the date, Wren leaning across the table asking their odd, dangerous questions while I catalogued their mind like a shopkeeper pricing stock—and then the moment I decided, appalled at myself, that this one I wanted to keep. The archive. The book with my face in it. The Underfog, the chapel, the village of the unremembered where an old woman fed us stew and called me *little ember*, and I had felt—for the first time in my life—like I belonged somewhere, and I gave that too, gave the belonging, gave the stew, gave the old woman's face, because a fire this size needs everything and I had promised everything.

Wren last.

I saved them last. I don't know if that was mercy or cowardice. Their face at the lamplight. Their hand on my wrist in the dark. Their terrible dramatic voice reading prophecy aloud. *Tell me your name,* they'd said once, teasing, early, back when names were cheap. I gave the fire the sound of my voice saying theirs instead, and the fire took it the way the tide takes a footprint—gently, totally, without malice—

and then there was a great deal of light,

and then there was morning,

and I was sitting on a hillside above a green and steaming valley with no fog anywhere, none, just sky, enormous and blue and rude about it, and my hands were empty, and a stranger was walking up the hill toward me with tears all over their face, and I had absolutely no idea why that should matter to me at all.

Except.

Somewhere under all that ash, in the cellar of me, in the place where I'd once kept a smile with flour-dusted hands above it—something stayed. An ember. A name-shaped warmth.

The stranger stopped in front of me and opened their mouth, and though I couldn't have said why, I found myself hoping—hoping the way you hope before you know there's anything to hope for—that whatever they said next, it would start with *tell me*.
