## 18. Ash and Morning

I have watched a dragon die by inches for a hundred years, and I will tell you the truth no one wants carved on any monument: dying slowly is louder than dying quickly.

The heart beneath the valley had been guttering so long that its silence had become a kind of weather. I had grown up inside that silence, in the way you grow up inside your own name—without noticing it until someone takes it away. And then Quentin Eriksen, thief of other people's yesterdays, liar of small convenient lies, poured everything they were into that coal like wine into a beggar's cup, and the silence *ended*.

What rose out of the roots of the valley was not the dragon of the founders' paintings. The paintings showed majesty: wings like cathedral doors, scales the color of hammered gold. What rose was gaunt and gray and half its old size, smoke leaking from seams in its hide like a kettle past patience, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever seen, because it was alive, and it was awake, and it looked at me with one ancient eye and knew me.

"*Little warden,*" it said, in a tongue my grandmother taught me in fragments before she forgot them, and I am not ashamed—I was ashamed later, in the daylight, when there were witnesses—but in that moment I dropped to my knees in the ash like a boy meeting a story he'd stopped believing in.

The Veil tore.

I don't know how else to describe it. The fog that had sealed this valley for a hundred years did not lift; it *tore*, the way a bandage tears off a wound that has finally healed enough to breathe, and beyond it—beyond a century of gray—I saw sky. Real sky. Blue at the top where the fog had never let blue live, and clouds doing their slow idiot work, and a sun coming up over mountains I had only ever known as shapes behind milk-glass.

People all across the valley stopped in the streets and looked up, and I could hear them from the hillside where I stood: hundreds of voices making the same sound at once, a sound with no word for it, the noise a person makes when the ceiling comes off their whole life.

* * *

The dragon circled the valley three times before it flew east.

I counted. Of course I counted. A warden counts; it is the whole of our useless sacred job. Three circles, low, its shadow sweeping over the rooftops of Graymarket and the Brokers' archive and the drowned chapel where I had once confessed the truth to a stranger I'd been sent to manipulate and had instead—well. That's another chapter, and mostly Quentin's to tell, or was, which is a sentence I still can't hold without my hands shaking.

On the third circle, it passed directly overhead, close enough that the wind of it knocked me sitting, and every person in the valley felt what I felt: a memory that was not theirs, settling into them gently, like snow.

That was the dragon's parting gift, and it was also the debt paid. For a hundred years, the Brokers had hoarded memories in their vaults, and the founders before them had hoarded the world outside the fog, and everyone born under the Veil had been poor in exactly one currency—their own pasts. Now the dragon, rising on the last warmth Quentin had fed it, opened its hoard. Every stolen memory in every Broker's ledger came home. Not gradually. All at once, at dawn, like birds returning to a roost.

I saw it happen to the soldiers first. Voss's hollowed army, the ones who hadn't turned when Quentin flooded the air with the dragon's remembering—they stood in the ash field below the heart-chamber's mouth, and they woke. You could see it move through them like wind through wheat. One moment they were standing at attention with nothing behind their eyes but orders; the next, a woman near the front put her hand over her mouth and said, "*Marta? Marta, I had a sister, where is my—*" and then she was running, weeping, toward a village she suddenly remembered loving.

A man knelt down in the cinders and picked up a child's shoe that had no business being there and held it and said nothing at all for a long time.

And above the whole valley, faint as breath on glass, names began to appear—not written anywhere, just *known*, the way you know your own name. The erased villagers, the ones the Brokers had unwritten over the decades, surfaced in the world's mind again. Somewhere far outside the valley, I would learn later, archivists in coastal cities found records that had been blank for generations now bearing ink. Letters arrived addressed to people who had spent years believing they belonged to no one. The world remembered. It remembered clumsily and incompletely and sometimes cruelly late, but it remembered.

The dragon went east into the sunrise and did not look back, because dragons are wise, and looking back is for wardens.

* * *

I found Maren Voss at noon, or what I guessed was noon; the sun was still an astonishment we all kept checking on, like a coin we expected someone to demand back.

She was walking.

That's all. Walking. Down the long road that led out of the valley through the torn Veil, past the boundary stones the founders had set, moving with the steady purpose of a woman going to market, except there was no market anymore and no Broker-Head either, and her arms swung empty at her sides.

I fell into step beside her, because someone should, because I had spent my life as the keeper of last things and it seemed wrong for the most powerful person in the valley to walk out of history unaccompanied.

"Maren," I said.

She glanced at me. Her eyes were the worst thing I saw that day, and I had seen a dragon rise from a grave. They were not cruel. Cruelty requires a self to be cruel *from*. Hers were polite, vacant, the eyes of a house after the family has moved out—swept, orderly, waiting for occupants who are never coming back.

"Do I know you?" she asked. Genuinely asked. No irony left in her to sharpen into a blade.

"You did," I said. "You offered me tea once, and threatened me twice, and once you did both at the same time, which took talent."

"That sounds like something I would do," she said, and smiled faintly, pleased, as if I'd told her she'd once been tall. "I seem to have lost—" She patted her pockets. Both pockets. Then she stopped patting, because her hands apparently forgot what they were looking for mid-motion. "I had a great many things."

Here is what happened to her, as best anyone could piece together afterward: when the dragon's hoard flew home, it flew home *out* of wherever she had hidden it. Voss had built herself from other people's pasts the way a magpie builds a nest from theft—shiny, structurally unsound, all of it borrowed. The memories she'd swallowed to make herself Broker-Head had never been hers to keep, and when the true owners reclaimed them, they took the mortar out of her walls. What remained was a very capable, very disciplined emptiness wearing a good coat.

Some said she deserved worse. Some said worse was impossible, because worse requires a person to suffer it, and there wasn't one left. The villagers whose names came back that morning voted, in the loose furious way crowds vote, to let her go. Marta's sister said, "If I take anything from her now, I'm only doing what she did," and that settled it, the way one clean sentence can settle what a hundred angry ones cannot.

"Where will you go?" I asked Voss.

"I think there was a place," she said slowly, feeling for it like a tongue probing a missing tooth. "Before. There was a fire somewhere, and I was small, and someone—" Her face worked. For one second—one—one fragment surfaced, some original girl from before the hoarding began, some child the Brokers themselves had erased and repurposed—and then it sank again, and her expression smoothed back to pleasant blankness. "It doesn't matter," she said, kindly, as though comforting *me*. "None of it matters. Isn't that restful?"

And she walked on, down the road, out of the valley, into a world that no longer had any record of her, and I watched until the road bent, and I did not follow. I want to say I felt triumph. What I actually felt was cold, the specific cold of seeing your own possible future take a walk without you. Quentin had fed a fire with everything they loved. Voss had starved herself of everything she might have loved, one purchase at a time. Same valley, same trade, two directions.

I stood at the boundary stone for a while. Then I went to find Quentin.

* * *

I should tell you about the searching, because the searching was the longest morning of my life, and I have lived through several centuries' worth of mornings compressed into one year.

The heart-chamber was a ruin of light. Where the coal had guttered, there was now a scar of fused stone, glassy and warm, shaped vaguely like a sleeping animal, and around it the ash lay knee-deep in drifts. Quentin was not among the waking soldiers. Not among the freed villagers streaming down into the valley, touching their own faces, saying their own names out loud like prayers they'd forgotten the words to. Not at the chapel, not at the lodgings on Candle Lane, not—though I checked, hating myself for checking—at the Brokers' vault, whose doors stood open forever now, its shelves bare, every stolen yesterday gone home.

I found them at dusk, on the hillside above where the fog used to sit.

They were sitting cross-legged in the ash, watching the sunset with the absolute attention of someone who has never seen one, and I suppose they hadn't—not really, not knowing what it was, not knowing sunsets came in series, one after another, a thing you could rely on.

"Quentin," I said.

They turned. And I want to describe their face to you, because everyone always asks, and because I need to say it once, plainly, so it stops ambush-ing me at night: it was Quentin's face. Exactly. The crooked nose, the ash smudge, the wary set of the mouth that used to soften only when they thought I wasn't looking. Nothing was missing.

Everything was missing.

"Hello," they said, politely. The way you speak to a stranger who has approached you on a hillside at dusk. "Were you looking for someone?"

I sat down in the ash next to them. My legs simply declined to keep holding me up, and the ground seemed as good a place as any to find out whether grief has a floor.

"Yes," I said. "But I think I'm a bit late."

They considered that with a slight tilt of the head, and then—they did this, they actually did this—they scooted sideways and patted the ground beside them, offering me a better seat, courteous to a weeping stranger, because Quentin Eriksen at their absolute core, underneath every theft and every memory, had apparently always been kind, and the kindness had burned too bright for even the fire to take.

"The sky's different here," they said, looking west. "I keep thinking that. Like someone cleaned a window I didn't know I was behind." A pause. "Do you know why I'd think that?"

"I do," I said.

"Everyone keeps looking at me strangely," they went on, conversational, tired. "Since I woke up in the ash. A woman cried when she saw me. Grabbed my hands and cried and thanked me for something, and I didn't have the heart to tell her she had the wrong person." They looked at me then, directly, and their eyes were clear and empty as washed glass. "Did I do something? Before? Everyone acts like I did something enormous."

"You did," I said.

"What?"

And I opened my mouth to tell them—to begin, at the beginning, the lamplight and the odd questions and the date that was a mission and the mission that became the only true thing I've ever done—and found that I couldn't. Not because the words weren't there. Because telling a person their whole life in one sitting felt like another kind of theft, and I had sworn, kneeling in a drowned chapel, that I was done stealing.

"Tomorrow," I said. "I'll tell you tomorrow. And the day after. It's—it's a long story."

"How long?"

"A hundred years, give or take. Plus one date."

They frowned, and rubbed the smudge of ash on their cheek, making it worse, and something in my chest broke and re-set itself slightly differently, the way bones do.

"All right," they said. "Tomorrow. But you'll have to start with your name. I don't know it. Do I?"

"No," I said, and the lie and the truth weighed exactly the same on my tongue. "Not yet."

The sun finished setting—the first full sunset this valley had seen in a century, and it wasted no drama on us, just went about its ordinary gorgeous business—and the first stars came out over an open sky, and beside me a stranger who had once been the whole point of my existence shivered in the cooling ash and asked, almost shyly,

"It's Wren, isn't it? Someone said it earlier. I've been repeating it so I wouldn't lose it."

I stared at them.

"Why?" I managed. "Why that name?"

"I don't know," Quentin said. "It just... stayed. Like a coal that wouldn't go out."

Above us, far to the east, something vast beat its wings once against the dark, and I made a promise to the stars, to the dragon, to the ash, to the person sitting beside me who was and was not the one I'd loved:

*I'll tell you everything. As many times as it takes.*
