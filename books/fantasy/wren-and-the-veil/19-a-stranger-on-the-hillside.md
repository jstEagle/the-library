## 19. A Stranger on the Hillside

I have watched a dragon die and a dragon be born, and I will tell you which is harder to witness: neither. The hard thing is the morning after, when the sky is impossibly blue and the person you love is sitting in the wet grass like they were planted there.

I came over the hill at dawn because I couldn't sleep, and because some warden's instinct in me — old, half-rotted, mostly useless for centuries — kept whispering that the fire had left something behind. It had. It had left Quentin.

They were sitting where the fog used to pool thickest, at the lip of the valley where the Veil's gray breath had rolled up against the mountainside for a hundred years like a tide that never went out. Except now it had gone out. All of it. You could see the whole valley from up there, every roof and road and river bend, naked under the morning light, and the people down in it were standing in their doorways blinking like moles dragged into the sun, touching each other's faces as if checking whether they were real.

And Quentin sat above all of it with their hands in their lap, watching nothing.

"Quentin," I said.

They turned. That was the first knife: they turned politely. Quentin Eriksen had looked at me a thousand ways by then — suspicious, flirtatious, furious, terrified, and once, on the worst night of both our lives, like I was the only solid thing in a world coming apart. This was none of those. This was the look you give a stranger who has addressed you on a road. Courteous. Slightly wary. Already calculating how far away I was.

"Hello," they said. "Do I know you?"

* * *

Let me be honest about what I wanted to do in that moment, because honesty is the only inheritance I have left worth anything. I wanted to sit down beside them and say: you are Quentin Eriksen, you steal memories, your mother's face is gone from your head but her laugh isn't, you take your bread with too much butter, you hum when you're nervous, you once told me that the fog was the only honest thing in the valley because it never pretended to be anything but fog. I wanted to pour myself into them like water into a cracked cup until something held.

Instead I said, "A little. Not enough."

Because here is what a century as the dragon's warden teaches you, if you're slow enough to need a century to learn it: you cannot give someone back themselves. Memory isn't an object. It doesn't sit in a vault waiting to be repossessed. Quentin hadn't lost their past the way Voss hoarded other people's pasts, locked in jars and ledgers. They had spent it. Fed it, coal by coal, into the last fire in the world, and the fire had done what fires do. There was no drawer where it all sat folded. There was ash, and there was morning, and there was this stranger in my beloved's skin asking me polite questions.

"You look tired," Quentin said. "Were you in the fire too? Everyone keeps smelling like smoke."

"A little," I said again.

They nodded, satisfied, and turned back to the valley. And I stood there in the grass feeling like the last page of a book that had been torn out and burned, holding the shape of the missing thing and nothing else.

* * *

I want to tell you about the valley that morning, because it deserves telling, and because I needed a few minutes before I could look at Quentin again without my chest doing something undignified.

The fog was simply gone. Not lifted — gone, the way a debt is gone when someone else pays it. For a hundred years the founders' working had hung over this place, a gray ceiling and gray walls, and we had all told ourselves it was weather. Then Quentin fed themselves into the dragon's heart and the Veil burned off in under an hour, and the sky that appeared was so blue and so enormous that grown men and women fell to their knees in the market square, and not one of them from grief.

The Brokers' hall had emptied overnight. Their memory-jars — thousands of them, glinting in their vaults like a swamp of trapped fireflies — had cracked or gone dark when the heart woke, though nobody yet understood why, least of all me. Maren Voss was nowhere to be found. Some said they saw a woman walking out through the western pass with her arms full of jars, muttering names. Some said she was still in her tower. I knew better than most what happens to a person built entirely out of stolen years when the theft stops working, and I did not go looking for her. Mercy has limits, and mine end at the woman who put hunters on Quentin's trail.

But the villagers — the remembered ones, the ones whose names had always been safe inside the fog — came up the hillside all morning. They brought bread. They brought a blanket. An old woman brought wildflowers and set them next to Quentin with the reverence you'd give a shrine, and Quentin thanked her warmly, and asked her name, and forgot it before she'd taken three steps down the slope, and she didn't mind. She'd heard what it cost. Everyone had heard. The story traveled faster than the sunrise: the thief who stole everything back and paid with the only thing they couldn't steal again.

"They keep looking at me," Quentin said, after the fourth such pilgrim. "Like I did something."

"You did."

"I don't remember doing anything." A pause. Then, with a flicker of something that was almost the old dry wit: "That's inconvenient. I've met people who claim to remember things they never did. Usually they want money. What does it mean, that everyone remembers me better than I do?"

I sat down in the grass beside them. Slowly, so as not to startle. The way you approach any wild creature, or any wounded one; I couldn't tell which category applied.

"It means you're famous," I said.

"For what?"

"Saving us all."

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. Their eyes were the same eyes. That was the second knife, and worse than the first: everything about them was the same. The crooked way they sat. The habit of tilting their head when assessing whether a person was lying. Even the little crease between the brows, the one I had privately catalogued as *suspicion, professional grade*. All the furniture was intact. The house was just empty.

"That's a strange thing to say to someone you barely know," they said finally.

"It is," I agreed. "I'm a strange person."

* * *

Here is what I did not do that first day, and every day after, and why.

I did not tell them they loved me. Because they didn't. Not anymore. Whatever Quentin and I had built in those frantic, treacherous weeks — the date that was a mission, the mission that became a date, the night in the Underfog when they held my hand not for cover but because they wanted to — all of it lived in the memories they had poured into the fire, and the fire had eaten it, and no amount of my telling could resurrect it. If I said *you loved me*, I would only be teaching them a fact. Facts are cheap. I deal in facts; I've dealt in them for centuries, prophecies and histories and the names of dead dragons. Love isn't a fact. Love is a thing that happens, and you cannot make it happen to someone by reciting its history at them. Ask any prophet.

So instead I stayed. And I learned the discipline of staying, which is the hardest magic I know, harder than warding a dying dragon for a century, and let me explain what that discipline consists of, since you may someday need it:

It consists of making soup without saying *you always liked it with more pepper*, and letting them discover the pepper themselves, and watching their face change when they do, and saying nothing.

It consists of walking behind them on the hillside paths, not leading, because Quentin Eriksen had spent a lifetime navigating the Veil alone and would navigate the open sky alone too, and following too close would turn them into a patient instead of a person.

It consists of hearing them say, on the third evening, "You again," with a tone caught between irritation and relief, and understanding that *you again* was the closest thing to welcome they could currently produce, and accepting it like a gift, because it was one.

It consists of not crying in front of them, and doing it anyway once, at dusk, quickly, facing away, and when they asked what was wrong, saying "smoke in my eyes," which was even true, in a way, because the whole valley still smelled faintly of burnt fog and dragon-fire and the end of a century.

* * *

On the fifth day they found me sitting by the cold hearth of the cottage the villagers had lent them, and they stood in the doorway with their arms crossed and said, "All right. I'll bite. Who are you?"

"Wren."

"Just Wren?"

"Just Wren."

"And what am I to you, Just-Wren?" They came and sat across from me, and their eyes had that thief's sharpness I remembered, hunting my face for the seam, the false bottom, the trick. "Because you don't leave. You bring bread you pretend you didn't bake. You flinch every time I say your name, like it costs you something to hear it. And yesterday, when I laughed at the goat knocking over the well bucket, you looked at me like—" They stopped. Recalibrated. "Like you'd seen a ghost wearing my clothes."

I should have lied. I was, historically speaking, excellent at lying; it comes with the warden's trade, guarding secrets until they fossilize. But I was so tired, and the firelight was doing something kind to their face, and I thought: *they deserve one truth, even if it's the wrong one.*

"You died," I said quietly. "Or near enough. To save everyone. And the person who came back is — new. Unwritten. And I loved the person who was written, and I miss them, and I stay anyway, because you're here and they're not, and mourning you to your face seemed rude."

Silence. The hearth ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, the goat committed another crime against infrastructure.

Then Quentin said, "That's the stupidest, saddest thing anyone's ever said to me," and I braced for the door slamming, for being sent away, for the long empty centuries I deserved —

— and instead they leaned forward, elbows on knees, studying me with that tilted head, and asked, "What was I like? The written one."

"Not like this," I said honestly. "This one is softer. Slower to trust, somehow, even though you trust easier. They were all edges. They'd have picked my pocket by now just to see if they could."

"Hm." A pause. "Could they have?"

"Once. Early on. Before I caught on."

"And after?"

"After," I said, "they stopped stealing from me. Said I wasn't worth the price." I smiled at the floor. "They were lying. I was worth exactly that much, and they'd already decided to keep me."

Quentin was quiet for a long time. When I looked up, their expression had shifted into something I recognized with a jolt — not memory, nothing so cruel as memory, but its shadow, its echo off a far wall. Curiosity with warmth underneath it. The same look they'd given me across a lamplit table in the Gray Market, the night they decided to pick a stranger's brain over dinner.

"Tell me about them," Quentin said. "From the beginning. Tell me everything."

And I understood two things at once, the way you sometimes understand things in fires and floods, all at once and too late to be useful. First: that this was going to hurt, and that I was going to do it anyway. And second — arriving a half-second behind the first, like thunder after lightning — that somewhere in the ashes of everything Quentin had burned, something had survived. Not a memory. Smaller than a memory. An ember. The ember was this: given a blank page and a stranger full of stories, Quentin Eriksen's first instinct was to lean in and ask for more.

You can build a person out of that. I resolved, sitting there in the cooling light, that I would.

"All right," I said, and pulled my chair closer to the hearth. "From the beginning, then. There was a fog..."

* * *

We talked until the candle drowned in its own wax. I told them about the Gray Market and the brokers and the taste of stolen soldier-memories, and Quentin winced at the right places without knowing why. I told them about the prophecy book with their face in it, and they touched their own cheek absently, as if checking. I told them about the Underfog and the hounds and the moment they'd realized the memories in their head weren't all theirs — and there, mid-sentence, they held up a hand.

"Wait," they said slowly. "Say that part again. The dragon's memories. Fire, and flying, and a name in a language that's dead."

I said it again.

And Quentin went very still, staring into the dead hearth, and said, in a voice that wasn't quite their own: "*Vaelthyrre.*"

The name of the dragon. The true name, the one in the dead tongue, the one that had existed nowhere in the living world except in Quentin's stolen stock and in the oldest pages of the prophecy book. I had never spoken it aloud to them. I had never spoken it aloud to anyone in ninety years.

My heart stopped, started again, badly.

"How do you know that word?" I whispered.

Quentin blinked, and the stillness broke, and they frowned at me with the mild puzzlement of a person waking from a dream they can't hold onto. "Know what word?" they said. "You were saying something about tunnels."

But I had heard it. I had heard it land in the room like a struck bell, and I knew — the way a warden knows, in the marrow, in the century-old ache of the warding — that the fire had not eaten everything. That some coals survive the burning. That somewhere under all that blankness, small and stubborn and utterly itself, the heart of Quentin Eriksen was still smoldering.

And I thought: *then it isn't over. None of it. We begin again, and this time I get to watch them choose.*

"Well," I said, steadying my voice, folding my hands like a storyteller settling in for the long night. "Where was I? Ah. The tunnels. You hated the tunnels..."

Outside, the first stars came out over a valley with no fog and no ceiling, and inside, I told the story from the beginning, and this time, I noticed, Quentin didn't ask me who I was.
