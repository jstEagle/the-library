## 20. Tell Me Your Name

There is a particular cruelty in knowing someone completely and being known by them not at all. I had spent my whole warden's life learning to hold things—names, oaths, the weight of a dying fire—and none of it prepared me for the weight of Quentin Eriksen looking at me like a stranger. Which, to be fair, I now was. A stranger with excellent posture and a great deal of unsolicited history.

We sat on the hillside where the fog used to live. The grass there was new, thin and stubborn, the color of an apology. Below us the valley lay open to the sky for the first time in a century, and people were still coming out of their houses to stare upward like the clouds owed them money.

Quentin sat with their knees drawn up and their hands loose in their lap—the hands of a person who had forgotten they'd ever stolen anything, which was almost true. They watched a bird cross the blue with the naked astonishment of someone seeing flight for the first time. Maybe they were. Birds had never come into the Veil. Nothing came into the Veil. That had been rather the point.

"Your name," I said.

They looked at me. Their eyes were the same. That was the thing I hadn't braced for properly—I had braced for everything else, the blankness, the fumbling, the terrible politeness of strangers—but I hadn't braced for those eyes, which held nothing of me and were still, unbearably, Quentin's.

"Quentin," they said slowly. "I think. It was written somewhere." They touched their own collarbone as if checking. "Quentin Eriksen. There's a feeling when I say it. Like a shoe that fits."

"That's your name," I agreed. "It fits."

"And you're—" They squinted at me, and I felt the look go through me like a pick through a lock, old habit outliving its owner's memory of the trade. "You've been telling me things all morning. Wren. You said you were called Wren."

"I did."

"It doesn't fit," Quentin said. "When you say it. It fits *you* fine, but it doesn't snag on anything. In my head, I mean. Everything you tell me just—" they made a gesture like water passing through a net, "—goes through."

"All right," I said, and kept my voice level, because if I let it shake they would see what it cost, and Quentin—my Quentin, the one who was gone—had hated being pitied more than they'd hated the Brokers. "Then we'll do it differently. We'll start at the beginning, and you'll tell me what snags."

"The beginning of what?"

"Everything. Well." I considered. "The beginning of us, which is a smaller story than everything, but a better one."

Quentin's mouth twitched. Not a smile. The ghost of a place where a smile had once been installed. "Go on, then."

* * *

"You lived in Graymarket row," I said, "in a room above a chandler's shop that smelled so strongly of tallow you could never tell if you were hungry. You stole memories for a living. Lifted them right out of travelers' heads in the fog, quick as pickpocketing, and sold them to the Brokers for bread and rent."

Quentin frowned. "That sounds like a terrible way to live."

"It was a living. You were good at it. You used to say—" and here my voice caught on the wire of it, because I remembered the exact tilt of their grin, "—you used to say you'd stolen so much of other people's lives you didn't have room left for your own."

"Did I have anyone? Family?"

"A mother," I said gently. "Whose face you sold. Or lost. The trade took it either way. You couldn't remember her face. You told me that on our first date, which is a genuinely deranged thing to tell someone on a first date, and it's one of the reasons I fell in love with you."

Quentin absorbed this. The bird came back and crossed the sky the other direction; Quentin tracked it the whole way, greedy for it, and I understood they were hoarding sky the way they'd once hoarded other lives.

"A first date," they said. "With a stranger who walked into a valley no stranger enters. I must have robbed me some of your memories. To sell."

"You tried," I said. "It didn't work. My head doesn't open that way. And then you got curious instead of greedy, which—for you—was far more dangerous."

"What was I curious about?"

"Me," I said. "And the questions I was asking. Dragon-fire. The old prophecies. You thought I was eccentric. You thought—" I smiled despite myself, "—you thought I was mysterious in an attractive way, and that you might relieve me of a few interesting memories over dinner and keep the interesting ones for yourself. For once you wanted to *keep*. That's what you told me later. That for the first time in your life, you wanted to keep something instead of selling it."

"Was it me?"

"It was you. Lamplight table, bad wine, you wearing a coat you absolutely could not afford. You asked me what I was running from, and I lied badly, and you knew I lied badly, and you liked me anyway. You had a terrible weakness for liars with good intentions. You said we were a matched set."

Quentin laughed—a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody. They pressed their hand flat against their sternum.

"What?" I asked, careful, so careful. "What happened?"

"Nothing. Nothing, it's—" They shook their head. "Keep going. What happened after dinner?"

* * *

So I told them about the archive. About the prophecy book a century old with their own face inked beside the last dragon, and how they had gone white as unlit candles, and how I had confessed under the lampposts that I was the dragon's warden and the date had been a mission and I had chosen them before I'd ever meant to choose anyone.

"They hunted us," I said. "Maren Voss and her Brokers. They wanted your blood to seal the Veil forever—a cage around a dying heart, built as mercy by founders who feared what the world would do with dragon-fire, rotted into tyranny by people who feared losing their power over it. We ran the Underfog. You confused their memory-hounds with stolen thoughts. You fought hollowed soldiers by giving them back pieces of themselves until they turned on Voss. You broke into the Brokers' vault with nothing but your own two hands and every trick of a very dishonorable trade."

"Sounds implausible," Quentin said. "Me, heroic."

"You weren't heroic," I said. "You were brave, which is different and worse. Heroism is a temperament. Bravery is a decision you make with your hands shaking. Yours shook constantly. You complained the entire time. You complained about the tunnels, the hounds, my personality—"

"My personality?"

"—your word was 'insufferably noble'—and the fact that the drowned chapel had eels. But you never once suggested turning back. Not even at the end, when the book showed you what it would cost."

Quentin went very still. "What did it cost?"

And there it was, the question I had been circling for three days like a hound that wouldn't settle. I had told them about the fog, the vault, the battle. I had not yet found the courage for the coal.

I looked at the open sky. It seemed obscene, somehow, that the sky got to be beautiful while I had to do this.

"The fire was dying," I said. "The last dragon-fire in the world, guttering under the valley's roots, and Voss was there to smother it and rule an eternal Veil. Someone had to feed it. The dragon's own memories would feed it for a while. But the prophecy said—" I made myself look at Quentin as I said it. "The prophecy said the one who feeds the fire pays with everything they remember. Everything they are."

Quentin's hand rose slowly to their chest again. "I fed it."

"You fed it everything. Your mother's face. Every theft. Every year of your life worth the name." My voice failed on the next part, and I let it fail, because lying to them now would have been a worse theft than any they'd ever committed. "You fed it me, Quentin. You poured me into the fire with both hands, and the fire woke, and it burned the Veil away, and it saved everyone, and you forgot why you wanted to save them. You forgot me."

The wind came up the hillside, smelling of rain and open world and green things growing where gray had been. Quentin sat with it. I let them.

"So the person I was," they said finally, "chose this. Chose to become me."

"Yes."

"Knowing." They weren't asking about the mechanics. They were asking whether I was certain, and I was, and they saw that I was.

"Knowing," I said. "You wouldn't let me take your place. You wouldn't let me protect you from your own choice. You said—" and here I stopped, because I had kept that sentence like a coal in my own chest for three days, warming my hands on it, and taking it out meant it might gutter. But Quentin deserved their inheritance, even the burning parts. "You said, *Don't you dare make this smaller. I've spent my whole life borrowing other people's selves. This is the only thing I've ever done that was entirely mine.*"

Quentin closed their eyes.

For a long moment there was only wind, and the distant sound of the valley below—a hammer, a child, someone laughing at something, sounds the Veil had starved a century of—and I sat in the wreckage of my own patience and waited for whatever Quentin would decide I was to them: stranger, liar, or ghost.

"Tell me the rest," they said at last. "Not today. Not all at once. But tell me the rest."

"I will."

"Every day. Whatever survives." They opened their eyes, and there was something fierce in them, some ember-tempered hardness that no blankness could fully erase, and I thought: *there you are.* "If the person I was could pay that much, the least I can do is keep the receipts."

A laugh broke out of me, wet and unplanned. God, I'd missed them. Both of them—the one gone and the one here, who were the same person wearing different amounts of themselves.

* * *

We started small. Names, mostly. I gave them the village of the Unremembered, and the word *shelter* snagged—just barely, a fish-nibble of recognition, gone in a breath, but real. I gave them the Underfog, and the eels, and they gagged convincingly at the eels, though I suspected courtesy rather than memory. I gave them Maren Voss, and Quentin's lip curled at the name with pure animal distaste, no history in it, just instinct. Instinct, I was learning, survived the fire. The self beneath the memories had been the best part of Quentin all along, and the fire hadn't wanted that part. Only the past.

By late afternoon we had worked our way to the vault, and the escape, and the final descent to the heart beneath the valley, and Quentin listened to all of it with their chin on their knees like a child at a hearth.

"And then the fire," they said. "And then you carried me out here. And then—" they gestured at the hillside, the grass, the three days between then and now, "—this. You stayed."

"I stayed."

"You could have gone back to being a warden. Guarding a dragon that doesn't need guarding anymore. You could have—" They hesitated, choosing words with a thief's precision even now. "You don't have to sit on a hill teaching a stranger their own life. That's penance, not friendship. You could be free of me."

I looked at them for a long moment.

"Do you know what I am?" I said. "What I actually am, underneath the warden title?"

"Something to do with dragons."

"I'm the last of an order whose entire purpose was one long vigil. Centuries of watching and waiting and keeping, and never once—never once in all that keeping—did anyone keep *me*." I pulled a blade of grass and shredded it, because it was easier than their eyes. "And then a memory-thief in a stolen coat tried to rob me over bad wine and asked me what I was running from, and for the first time in four hundred years, someone wanted my answer. So no. This isn't penance. This is the first thing in my long, dull, dutiful life that I have ever gotten to choose."

Quentin was quiet.

Then, softly, they said: "Say your name again."

"Wren."

"Say it slower."

"Wren," I said. Just the word, laid down gently, like a coin on a counter.

And I watched it happen—I saw it cross their face, the flicker, the snag. Their brow creased. Their mouth shaped the syllables without sound, testing them, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Something moved behind their eyes, deep down where the fire's reach had ended or faltered or perhaps simply missed.

"There," they whispered. "Wait. There's—it snags. It snags on *Wren*."

My heart stopped doing its job. "What snags? Tell me exactly."

"A sound," Quentin said, frowning, digging. "No—not a sound. A *feeling*. Like—" they pressed their fist against their breastbone again, harder this time, as if trying to reach through, "—like hearing a door open in a house you've never been in but somehow know the layout of. Wren. When you say it, something in me says *yes, that one, that's the one I paid for.*"

The blade of grass fell out of my fingers.

Because that was it. That was proof. Not a memory—memories burn—but something older and deeper than memory, some seam of recognition stitched below the place the fire could reach. The prophecy had taken everything Quentin remembered, but it had not touched what Quentin *knew*, and apparently, at the bedrock of them, beneath mother and thefts and every borrowed self, Quentin knew me.

"That's enough," I said, and my voice cracked clean in half, and I didn't care. "That's more than enough. That's everything."

"It's hardly anything," Quentin protested. "It's a name and a feeling."

"It's a coal," I said. "Trust me. I know fires. Everything alive starts as a coal."

Quentin looked at me—at my ruined voice and my shredded grass and whatever my face was doing that I couldn't control—and something in their expression shifted, softened, became less wary. Not remembering. Choosing, maybe. The first draft of it.

"Wren," they said deliberately, tasting it, and the snag caught again—I saw it catch—and they almost smiled, that ghost-smile finding its old installation. "All right. Wren. Tomorrow, tell me the part about the wine again. And don't skip the part where I was charming."

"You were insufferable."

"Don't skip it anyway."

"I won't," I promised. "Tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that, for as long as it takes. I have centuries of practice at waiting for things."

But that night, as the valley's new lamplights bloomed below us—one for every window, in a valley that had lived a century in gray—Quentin paused at the door of the cottage the villagers had given us and said, quietly, without turning around:

"There's one more thing. It's probably nothing. But all evening there's been a shape in my head I can't put a name to. Not a memory. More like... an errand. Like I'm supposed to be *doing* something, and I've forgotten what."

The wind moved through the new grass.

"What kind of errand?" I asked.

Quentin turned, and in the lamplight their eyes were Quentin's eyes, and troubled, and awake.

"I don't know," they said. "Only that it has something to do with a shop. And something that isn't mine."
