## 22. Wren and the Veil

### Quentin

The shop opens at dawn because that's when the fog used to lift, and some habits outlive their reasons. There's no fog anymore — hasn't been for three seasons — but I still find myself standing in the doorway every morning, watching the light come down the valley walls like it's checking for something.

"Still there?" Wren asks from behind me, two chipped mugs in hand.

"Still there."

We could put up a sign that says what the shop is, but nobody in the valley needs one anymore. The Returned Memory Exchange, the villagers call it, though I didn't name it that and wouldn't have. Names matter differently now. I know this the way you know a scar: intellectually, secondhand, by touching the edges of where something used to be.

Here's what I know about myself. My name is Quentin Eriksen. I was born somewhere I can't picture, to a mother whose face I paid away before I ever knew what I was doing with my life. For years I stole memories from travelers and sold them to brokers in a valley sealed under fog, and then I burned all of it — theirs, mine, everything — into the last dragon-fire so that the sky could open and the world could remember us again.

I know all of this because Wren told me. Repeatedly. Patiently, mostly, though there was a stretch in early autumn when they got a certain tightness around the eyes, telling it for the hundredth time, and I learned to ask better questions instead of the same ones. That's a kind of memory too, maybe. Not the thing itself, but the shape of caring about it.

The first customer of the morning is a woman named Odile who lost her wedding day to a Broker's ledger eleven years ago. She doesn't remember being married. She remembers her husband only as a stranger who kept being kind to her at confusing intervals, which she says was either the worst or best thing that ever happened to her.

I keep her memories in a jar on the third shelf. Not magic — there's precious little true magic left in the valley now that the Veil is gone, and what remains belongs to the dragon, and the dragon belongs to itself — but craft. Old Broker technique, turned inside out. Voss's people knew how to pull a memory out whole and store it in glass and salt. They used it to make people empty. I use it to make people full again. Same tools, different hands. I try not to think about how much that proves.

"Ready?" I ask Odile.

She sits in the chair by the window and nods, and I press my palms to her temples the way Wren taught me — gently, the way you'd hold a bird, not the way the Brokers held things, clamped and greedy.

The memory goes home. I feel it leave the jar like water finding its old channel. And then Odile makes the sound. They all make the sound. It's not crying, exactly, or laughing, exactly — it's the sound a person makes when a door they'd bricked over swings open and the room behind it is exactly as they left it.

"He proposed," she whispers. "Under the plum tree. He was so nervous he dropped the ring in the well."

"There's a version of you that never forgot," I tell her. "This is just her catching up."

She grips my hand too hard on the way out and presses coins into my palm, and I give them back, because that's the rule, the only rule: nothing sold, nothing bought, everything returned. She leaves them anyway, on the counter, the way they all do. I use them for flour and lamp oil and, once a month, honey, which Wren claims is medicinal.

### Wren

There are mornings I wake and forget, for one merciful half-second, that Quentin doesn't remember me. Then the half-second ends, and I lie there learning it again, the way you relearn a death each dawn for the first year.

Except it isn't death. That's the thing I have to keep teaching myself. Death doesn't make tea. Death doesn't argue with me about whether the shop should open on rest-days (Quentin says yes; the village has nowhere else to be). Death doesn't look at me across the breakfast table with those clear gray eyes and say, "Tell me about the date," which is what Quentin said last Tuesday, apropos of nothing, fork paused midair.

"The date," I said.

"The first one. You've told me about the prophecy and the Brokers and the tunnels and the chapel and Voss, gods, you've told me about Voss enough times that I dream about her. But you always skip the date."

"I skip it because—" Because it was the night I fell in love with them, and they don't remember it, and describing it feels like showing someone a photograph of a house that burned down with them inside it. "Because it's complicated."

"We have time." Quentin smiled. "That's the whole point of the valley now, isn't it? Everyone keeps saying we have time."

So I told them. About the lamplight, and the questions about dragon-fire that were supposed to be tradecraft and were actually nervousness, because wardens aren't trained for flirting and I had rehearsed my cover story so many times I'd begun to believe it. About how I'd sat across from Quentin planning to read their memories like a ledger and instead spent four hours laughing at a joke neither of us could later reconstruct. About how, walking back through the gray streets, I'd thought: *this person, of all people, this pickpocket of other lives* — *this is who the book chose*, and how strange and right that had felt even then.

Quentin listened with the particular stillness they have, chin on hand, watching my face like I was weather worth forecasting.

"And did you steal anything that night?" they asked at the end.

"You mean, did I pick your memories?"

"I mean, did you take something of mine you weren't supposed to?"

I thought about lying. I've gotten worse at it; living among the remembered does that. "You took mine first," I said. "I never got them back."

Quentin nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a suspicion rather than confessed a theft, and reached across the table and took my hand, and said, "Good. Keep it."

I am a warden of the last dragon. I was raised in oath and isolation for a duty older than the valley. I have stood in drowned chapels and read the confessions of founders and watched a fire that predates nations gutter and roar. None of it prepared me for *keep it*. None of it prepared me for how much I would want to.

* * *

### Quentin

Afternoons, when the shop empties, I do the inventory. Rows of jars, each holding somebody's stolen life: a fisherman's first storm, a twin's shared childhood, an entire wedding, a lullaby in a language the singer's granddaughter thought dead. The Brokers took these from the valley's people over generations — payment for protection, taxes, punishments, or simply because Voss's collectors liked the taste of other lives. When the Veil burned, the vaults cracked open, and what wasn't destroyed came to me, because apparently burning down a tyranny qualifies you to run the untying of it.

Some jars I'll never return. Their owners are gone — hollowed soldiers who died in the battle beneath the valley, or erased villagers who walked into the mountains afterward and never came back. For those, I've built a shelf near the window where the light hits. Wren calls it the listening shelf. Once a week we open the jars and let the memories air, so they don't spoil, and sometimes the room fills briefly with laughter in dead voices or the smell of bread baked sixty years ago, and we stand there like museum visitors in a country of one room.

Today, going through the newest crate — salvaged from a collapsed Broker waystation in the western cliffs — I found a jar labeled in Voss's own cramped hand. The label said: **M. VOSS — AGE 9 — DO NOT OPEN**.

I sat with it a long while.

Wren found me like that, cross-legged on the floor, the jar in both hands. They crouched beside me without asking. They're good at that — arriving at the exact moment the silence needs company.

"She was one of them," I said. "An erased child. She told me, at the coal. Said she clawed her way up out of nothing by hoarding everyone else's pasts. But this—" I turned the jar. Inside, faintly, a child's summer moved: running feet, tall grass, someone calling a name I couldn't quite hear. "This is hers. Before. She kept herself locked up in her own vault."

"Do you return it?"

"To whom? There's no Maren Voss anymore. Just a gray woman the villages don't remember, walking the high passes. We tried giving her back pieces, last spring. She looked at them like they belonged to a stranger." I set the jar down carefully. "Maybe that's the answer, though. Maybe belonging to a stranger is still better than belonging to no one. Maybe someday she walks past a farmyard and smells cut grass and stops, and doesn't know why, and stays anyway."

Wren was quiet. Then: "You could have become her. You know that."

"I count on it being close," I said. "It keeps me honest. Every jar here is a temptation wearing a label. Every one is a life I could sell, or hoard, or wear. Instead I get to hand them back and watch doors open." I looked at the shelves, all those small glass suns. "Voss thought memories were power. They're not. They're weight. The only question is whether you carry yours or make everyone else carry it for you."

"That sounds like something you'd have known before," Wren said softly. "Before the fire."

"It sounds like something I'm choosing now." I bumped their shoulder. "Which, I'm given to understand, is the family tradition."

### Wren

In the evenings, the dragon comes down from the high crags.

Not often — she is old, and grief made her, and grief keeps her hours — but once or twice a week she circles the open valley in long slow spirals, and the children run out of their houses screaming with joy, and the adults stand in their doorways with their hats over their hearts. Her fire is small now. A candle where there was once a sun. But it is *hers*, fed by her own returned centuries, and it will grow, Warden's oath or no, the way all true things grow: slowly, and against expectation.

Sometimes she lands on the ridge above our cottage and lets me climb up and sit between her shoulder blades, the way I did when I was young and the world was only fog below and oath above. She doesn't speak in words. She speaks in warmth, in the slow tide of her breathing, in memories she sends me unasked — flight, mostly. Always flight.

Tonight she sends me one I haven't seen before: a hillside at dusk, three seasons ago. A figure sitting alone where the fog used to be, blank-eyed, hands empty in their lap. And another figure climbing the hill toward them, heart hammering, carrying nothing but a name they hoped was still true.

*I know this one,* I tell her. *This is the day I found Quentin.*

*Yes,* she answers, in the way she answers, which is not words but certainty. *Look closer.*

And I do, from her height, from her ancient patient sight — and I see what I could not see that day. I see the ember. A single spark of red buried deep in Quentin's gray-and-empty gaze, banked like a coal in ash. The fire had taken everything, but it is a fire, and fires do not consume what they love. It had hidden one thing. One small unburnable thing.

Me. Or not me, exactly. The *name*. Wren. Just the word, glowing faintly in the dark of Quentin, waiting.

I slid off the dragon's back that evening with my legs shaking and walked down to the cottage where Quentin was lighting the lamps, and I didn't say anything, because what would I even say — but Quentin looked up and frowned at my face and said, "What? What happened?" and I said, "Nothing. Everything. Tell me your name," which had become our joke, our ritual, our nightly liturgy, and Quentin rolled their eyes and said, "Quentin Eriksen, memory-monger, retired," and I laughed until I had to sit down.

They never asked why I laughed. But sometimes, when I say *tell me your name*, I catch it — a flicker, a hitch, a half-second where Quentin's eyes go distant and warm, like someone hearing a song from a house they lived in long ago. I think the ember stirs. I think, some mornings, it almost catches.

I don't tell Quentin about the dragon's gift. Some knowledge is a weight, and I have learned whose shoulders are for carrying.

* * *

### Quentin

Winter came early this year and hung around like a guest with nowhere better to be, and the shop grew quiet, and I grew restless, and Wren noticed before I said anything, because Wren always notices first.

"Go," they said one frozen morning. "Take the sled. The mountain villages haven't seen the return-cart since autumn."

The return-cart. That's the traveling version of the shop — jars packed in straw, a folding chair, me knocking on doors in villages that the maps forgot for a century. It's the work I love most now, if I'm honest, more than the shop, more than the inventory. Because out there, past the valley mouth, people don't know what I was. They only know what arrives: a stranger with warm hands and a sledge of second chances.

I was three days out, in a village called Harrowmere that smelled of woodsmoke and sheep, returning a blacksmith's memory of his late father, when a man caught my sleeve outside the inn. Gray coat, gray face, travel-worn. Something about him snagged — the careful emptiness around his eyes, the way his glance went to my sledge and stayed there, hungry and afraid at once.

"You're the one who gives them back," he said. Not a question.

"I am."

"I have—" His hand went to his chest, pressed flat, as if holding something in. "There are things missing. Gaps. Years. Someone said— they said if anyone could fix it, it'd be you."

I studied him. Under the grayness, under the hollow, something flickered. Familiar, somehow. The set of the jaw. The way he flinched from kindness like it might be a trap.

"What's your name?" I asked.

He opened his mouth. Closed it. His eyes went bright and panicked. "I don't— I had one. I know I had one."

And here is the thing about the person I am now, the person assembled from other people's accounts and my own daily choices: I felt no fear. Only recognition. I have stood where he stands. I wake up there most mornings, truthfully, in the country of the blank, and I have learned it is survivable if someone walks with you.

"Then we start there," I said. "Names are easy. Come inside, it's freezing. I'll buy you dinner, and tomorrow we'll see what's in the gaps. No charge. That's the rule."

He stared at me. "Why free? Nothing's free."

"Somebody paid for all of it already," I said, and thought of a hillside, and a voice saying *tell me your name* to a stranger in their own skin, and a fire that takes everything except what it loves. "The least I can do is pass it on."

He followed me in. Outside, snow began to fall, soft and unhurried, the first I'd seen since the sky opened, and I realized I was grinning at it like a fool, and that somewhere east of here Wren was probably grinning at the same snow, and that tomorrow I would start digging through a broken man's gaps with everything I had.

* * *

### Wren

Quentin comes home on the fourth evening, snow in their hair, smelling of woodsmoke and other people's kitchens, and drops the sledge in the yard and finds me at the door as if the door had been waiting all day, which it had.

"Miss me?" they say, which is new — they've started saying it lately, testing it, and I've started answering honestly, which is *yes, always, constantly, it's the whole architecture of my days* — but tonight I just say, "Obviously," and pull them inside.

Over supper they talk about Harrowmere, and the man with no name, and the gap in him they mean to mend. Their hands move when they talk now; their face is a weather system. The blankness burned away to something vivid and unfinished, and I have stopped mourning the polished, wary thief I fell in love with by lamplight, because this person — this loud, generous, stubbornly kind person who gives everything away for free — is not a lesser draft. They're the final one. They're who Quentin was always becoming underneath the fog and the fences and the fear.

Later, banked fire, two chairs angled together, the valley dark and star-struck outside windows that no longer need shutters against any fog. Our nightly liturgy. I take Quentin's hand.

"Tell me your name," I say.

Quentin sighs the sigh of the long-suffering. "Quentin Eriksen. Retired memory-thief. Current proprietor of a business model that appalls economists." A pause. Their thumb moves over my knuckles, absently, like it knows the road. "Tell me yours."

"Wren," I say. "Last warden of the last dragon. Terrible at undercover work. Hopeless at leaving."

"Mm," Quentin says. And then, softer, in the tone they save for the edge-of-sleep moments when the ember must be closest to the surface: "I don't remember falling in love with you. I want you to know that I know that. You've told me the story enough times that I can recite it — the lamplight, the bad tradecraft, the joke we can't reconstruct. But knowing isn't remembering. What I have isn't the fall. It's just..."

They trail off. Outside, far off, a dragon-cry threads through the stars, low and contented.

"It's just what?" I ask, though I know. I ask because hearing it is the closest thing I have to being remembered.

"It's just that every morning I wake up and there you are, making tea wrong — you always scald it, how many times have I told you — and I think: *oh good, him again.* And I choose it. Whatever the fire left me, it left me that much: I look at you and I choose it. Every day. On purpose." Quentin turns, eyes clear and gray and lit from somewhere deep and red. "Maybe that's better than remembering. Remembering just happens to you. This I *do*."

The fire settles. Snow ticks softly at the window. Tomorrow there will be jars to return, a nameless man to sit with, honey to buy, a dragon to wave at from the ridge. Tonight there is this, which is not a memory and never will be:

Morning coming. Both of us choosing. Again.
