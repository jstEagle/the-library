## 1. The Charm Seller

The fog came off the canal at dawn the way it always did in Vell — thick as spilled milk, smelling of tallow and cold iron — and by the time Ulyana Pemberton had unlocked her stall's three locks and lit the single permitted candle, the market of Saltrow was already filling with the day's first mourners.

That was what they were, though no one said so. The city called them customers. The crown called them citizens. But Ulyana had spent six years watching them come down the narrow lane between the fishmongers and the coffin-maker's, hunched into their coats like people arriving at a graveside, and she knew grief when she saw it walk.

She arranged her wares with the care of a priest dressing an altar. Small glass vials, each no longer than a thumb, each stoppered with wax the color of old honey. To any passing constable they were perfume samples, or remedies, or nothing at all — trinkets sold by a thin woman with ink-stained fingers who kept her eyes down and her voice soft. The law of Vell was painted on every corner post in letters tall enough to read from horseback: *MAGIC IS FORBIDDEN. THE GIFTED WILL BE TAKEN.* Beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic: *Memory is property. Trafficking in memory is theft.*

Ulyana had committed that particular crime eleven thousand times, give or take. She had never once been caught, because the trick of selling stolen memories was not the stealing. Anyone with the gift could pull a recollection out of a sleeping head like a thread from a hem. The trick was making sure no one ever knew what you'd taken — least of all the person you took it from.

She set the last vial in its velvet groove and looked up to find her first customer already standing at the stall, and felt the familiar lurch in her chest, half hunger and half shame.

Maren Ostwick had been a widow for two years, four months, and — if the bells across the canal could be trusted, and they rang the hour with the smugness of something that had never lost anyone — eleven days.

"You have it," Maren said. Not a question. Her knuckles were white around her purse, and beneath her shawl her shoulders trembled with the effort of appearing calm. In Vell, appearing calm in public was a survival skill ranked just below knowing how to read.

"I told you I might," Ulyana said quietly. "I never promise."

But she did have it. Of course she had it. She had been cultivating this one for months, patient as a gardener, because Maren Ostwick's grief was deep enough to water anything.

"May I—" Maren glanced over her shoulder at the lane, empty except for a boy hauling baskets of eels, then leaned close. Her breath smelled of chamomile and sleepless nights. "Is it him? Truly him? Not one of your — your fabrications?"

Something in Ulyana twisted, sharp and small, at the word *fabrications*. As if she needed help inventing sorrow. As if there weren't more than enough of it already lying around the city, free for the taking.

"Sit," she said, and pulled out the stool kept beneath the counter for exactly this purpose. "And don't look at my hands."

* * *

Here is what the constables of Vell believed about memory-thieves: that they crept through windows at night, that they drugged wine at parties, that they were monsters with black eyes and hungry mouths. Here is what was actually true: most of the time, the best memories were given away freely, by people desperate to be rid of them, and all the thief had to do was listen.

Maren talked. That was the ritual, though neither of them named it. She talked about her Bram while Ulyana pretended to sort vials, and her hands drifted up from the counter without seeming to move at all, hovering an inch above Maren's folded ones, and the gift went out of her fingertips like warmth off a stove.

She didn't take from Maren. She reached *through* her — along the fine silver threads of love still anchored to the dead man — down into the place where the living keep their dead: not in the grave, but in the body. In the muscle-memory of a hand held. A kitchen shared. The specific weight of someone leaning against your back while you both pretend to watch rain.

There. A morning, eight years old and bright as new coin. Bram Ostwick at the canal door in his shirtsleeves, flour on his forearms, laughing at something Maren had said before the memory begins, so that his laugh arrives like sunlight through a curtain. He crosses the room. He puts his floury hand on the back of her neck, right where the neck meets the skull, the way he always did, and says — not words, exactly, the memory keeps only the shape of words, warm and low — and Maren, twenty-nine years old and not yet a widow, turns her face into his chest and breathes him in.

Ulyana gathered it gently, gently, the way you lift a moth without touching its wings, and drew it down through her own mind — tasting it, always tasting it, that was the cost of the craft, you carried everything you stole somewhere behind your teeth — and into the waiting vial. The liquid inside, which had been clear river-water that morning, clouded and swirled and settled into a slow amber gold, like whiskey, like honey, like late afternoon light in a kitchen where no one has yet died.

"There are rules," Ulyana said, when she was sure her voice would hold. She stoppered the vial with fresh wax and pressed her thumb into it, sealing it. "You open it when you're alone. You hold it in both hands and think of nothing else — that part matters, thinking of nothing, the memory needs somewhere to go. And it lasts as long as it lasts. Some women get ten minutes. Some get a whole morning." She hesitated. "Some only get the smell of him. Flour, in his case. Bread baking. It varies."

"How much?" Maren asked, and her voice cracked on the second word, and Ulyana hated herself with a clean and familiar hatred.

"Eight crowns."

"That's — Ulyana, that's a month of—"

"Six, then. Because you waited politely while I served the Halloran woman last week, and because your husband mended my awning frame two winters ago and refused payment." This was true. She checked these things. She made herself check them, a penance disguised as bookkeeping. Every charm she sold, she tried to tie back to some kindness received, some debt acknowledged, so that the transaction resembled something other than what it was: a woman selling other people's love back to them, piece by piece, until the well ran dry.

Maren counted out six crowns with shaking hands, coins worn smooth by other people's desperation, and took the vial like it was a newborn. "Will he know me?" she whispered. "In there. Will he—"

"He'll be glad to see you," Ulyana said. "He always was."

Maren wept then, briefly, furiously, pressing her shawl to her mouth, and Ulyana looked away and studied her candle flame and let the woman have her privacy in public, which was the only kind Vell allowed. Then Maren straightened, thanked her in a voice scraped raw, dropped one extra coin on the counter despite everything — *they always did that, they always tipped their dealer, what did it say about a city where grief left tips* — and walked back up the lane with her spine straight and her treasure cupped against her sternum, and Ulyana watched her go and thought, as she did every time:

*I should stop.*

She thought it the way other people thought *I should eat better*. It had stopped meaning anything years ago.

* * *

The rest of the morning passed in the small honest commerce she maintained as camouflage: needles, thread, buttons cut from horn, bundles of dried lavender that smelled of nothing after the first week. Two children bought licorice root. A dockhand haggled over pins and won, because letting the dockhands win kept Saltrow fond of her, and fondness was armor in a city where a neighbor's accusation could empty your house by nightfall.

Because that was the other thing about Vell, the thing under everything, like the canals under the streets: everyone was afraid, and fear made the whole city a congregation of informants. The witch-hunts came in waves — there had been one when Ulyana was nine, one at fifteen, a bad one four years ago that had emptied three streets in Coppersmith Ward. They always began the same way. A proclamation. A bounty. Then the quiet knock at doors, and the carts, and the neighbors who hadn't seen anything, officer, truly, though their curtains twitched all night long.

Ulyana had survived by being unremarkable. Thin, plain-dressed, forgettable-faced — she'd learned to slouch, to mumble, to laugh a beat too late at jokes. The gift itself helped; a light touch on a constable's mind could make her face as memorable as a fencepost. She had done it so many times she sometimes worried she'd rubbed herself smooth, that underneath all the borrowed dullness there was no original Ulyana left, just a shape where a person used to be.

At midday, old Tam the pie-seller brought her a pasty on credit and gossip instead of coin.

"Hall of Records posted new boards yesterday," he said, chewing, watching her with the frank nosiness of the very old. "Big ones. Wax seals thick as my thumb."

"New tax schedule?" She bit into her own pasty. Mutton, mostly gristle. She ate it anyway.

"Crown's calling in gifted." Tam said the word the way people said *plague* — softly, glancing about. "Not just traffickers this time. Gifted. Anybody feels things too sharp, knows things they shouldn't, heals quick, dreams true. They've got a name for the sweep." He lowered his voice with relish. "*The Quiet Harvest.*"

"Poetic," Ulyana said, and kept her face bored, and inside her the ice-water feeling began, slow and cold, spreading out from her sternum through her arms.

"Girl I know saw them hanging the boards at midnight. Soldiers. Dozens. And ash everywhere on the ground come morning, like somebody'd burned something at the foot of every board. Offerings, or warnings." Tam shrugged, wiped his mouth, shuffled off with his tray.

Ulyana stood in her stall among her buttons and her lavender and her eleven thousand crimes, and listened to the bells ring noon, and thought about how many days she had, now. A season? A month? The waves always started broad and got narrow. Broad: everyone suspicious. Narrow: lists. Names. And Ulyana Pemberton, who had spent six years making herself impossible to remember, would find out whether she'd really erased herself or only dimmed the lamp she lived behind.

* * *

She closed early. She told herself it was to inventory stock, and it was, but it was also because her hands wouldn't stop moving unless she gave them work.

The inventory was short. Forty-one charms, sorted by depth of feeling — she kept them in boxes labeled in plain trade-script: *JOYS, SMALL*; *SORROWS, COMMON*; and one box with no label at all that held the pieces too strange or too heavy to price. Selling them all before the sweep arrived was the sensible move. Cash out. Run. She'd run before; she knew the roads south.

She got as far as taking down the JOYS, SMALL box when the knock came at her shutters — three raps, pause, two raps — and her heart performed a full revolution before she recognized the pattern.

"It's open," she called, and hated how high her voice came out.

It was the widow. Maren Ostwick. Standing in her doorway at dusk with her shawl dripping canal-fog, holding the amber vial in both hands like a reliquary, and her face —

Her face was wrong. Not grief-wrong. Ulyana knew every weather of grief; it was her native climate. This was something else. This was awe with terror underneath it, the look of a woman who had seen something true and couldn't decide if it was a miracle or a wound.

"I opened it," Maren said.

"You're meant to wait until—"

"I opened it." She stepped inside without invitation, dripping. "Ten minutes. You said ten minutes, maybe a morning. Ulyana — I lived a whole day in there. A whole day. He burned the bread and we laughed and we went to his mother's and it rained and I could feel the rain, I could feel it on my arms, and when it ended I was on my kitchen floor and I'd wet myself and three hours had gone by." She thrust the vial forward. Her hands were steady now, which was worse than the shaking. "What did you sell me? What *is* this?"

"A memory," Ulyana said carefully. "His. Yours. Yours of him. It doesn't—"

"Don't." Maren's voice dropped low and flat. "Don't lie to me, girl. I sat with my dead husband for a whole day. I know what that costs. Nothing costs nothing, not in Vell. So I'm asking you plain, and I'll ask once." She leaned in. She smelled of bread flour, still, faintly, impossibly — the charm had marked her, bled a little of Bram into her skin, and the sight of it made something in Ulyana's chest crack sideways. "Where does it come from? When you bottle a thing like that — where do you get it?"

And here was the moment. Ulyana had rehearsed answers for this moment for six years. *Distillations. Herbal. Old techniques from before the ban, harmless, folk-craft, nothing forbidden.* Lies with good bones, lies that had held under worse pressure than a weeping widow.

Instead she heard herself say: "From the living. From the places people keep their dead. I don't take anything you'd miss. I couldn't — I won't. What I sell is overflow. Love leaves more behind than a body can carry. I just... catch what spills."

Silence. The candle guttered. Somewhere out on the canal, a boat knocked against its mooring, patient as a heartbeat.

"You're one of them," Maren said at last, wonderingly. "You're *gifted*. Right here in Saltrow, selling at market next to the eel-boy, and nobody—" She stopped. Ulyana watched the thought arrive in her face, watched it land: *nobody has reported her, and I am now the person who knows.* Fear moved through the widow's features like a shadow of a bird.

"Maren." Ulyana kept her voice level, gentle, the voice she'd use with a spooked animal. "You will forget this shop sells anything but buttons."

"I can't forget, I—"

"*You will forget this shop sells anything but buttons.*" And Ulyana reached out — not touching, never touching skin, skin made it permanent and permanence was a cruelty she reserved for enemies — and laid her gift over the widow's mind like a cloth over a birdcage. It was delicate work, gentler than stealing, harder too: smoothing the evening down, folding the terrible knowledge small, tucking it into the deep shelves where a mind keeps things it has decided not to have decided. She left Maren the memory of buying a charm — that she'd allow, that was mercy, let the woman keep her day with Bram — but the rest went soft and grey, the vial becoming simply a purchase, Ulyana becoming simply a shopkeeper, the danger becoming a vague unease she'd attribute to the weather.

When she withdrew, Maren Ostwick blinked, smiled a small confused smile, thanked her for the buttons, and walked home through the fog carrying a day with her dead husband and no idea of its price.

Ulyana stood alone in her stall with her hand still raised, trembling.

*This,* she thought. *This is what I am.* Not a thief of purses. A thief of truths. She robbed people even of the knowledge of being robbed, and called it mercy, and charged six crowns.

She blew out the candle. In the dark she began packing the charms away, fast now, faster — forty-one vials wrapped in wool, boxed, buried under button stock. All but one.

She didn't remember packing that one. That was the thing that stopped her, there in the dark with her heart going hard against her ribs. At the bottom of the unlabeled box — the strange box, the heavy box — lay a vial she had no record of making. No entry in her ledger. No price scratched on the wax. The liquid inside wasn't gold or grey or any color her craft produced; it was deep mountain blue, blue as high altitude, blue as cold starlight, and when she lifted it to the window and the last grey light of the sky came through it, she saw movement inside. Snow falling, maybe. Or ash.

And threaded through the fog of her own exhaustion, rising up from wherever she kept the things she'd hidden from herself, came a fragment — not a memory, nothing so complete, just an impression, a splinter: *a boy, standing at a gate carved into the side of a mountain, turning back to look at her, mouth opening to say something she could not hear.*

She had never seen that gate. She was nearly certain she had never seen that gate.

Outside, far off toward the center of the city, a bell began to ring that had no business ringing at this hour — the proclamation bell, the one that summoned criers, the one that preceded every hunt — and its voice rolled across the rooftops of Vell like the first stone of an avalanche.

Ulyana stood in the dark, holding the blue vial, and listened.
