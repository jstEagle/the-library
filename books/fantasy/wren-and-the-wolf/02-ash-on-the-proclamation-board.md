## 2. Ash on the Proclamation Board

The paper went up before dawn, while the fog still hung in Vell's throats like something swallowed wrong.

Ulyana saw it from her window on Cinderglass Lane: two crown men in grey livery, faces pinched against the cold, nailing a broadsheet to the proclamation board outside the tannery. They worked quickly. They always did when they thought no one was watching — as if speed could make the thing less real. One of them kept glancing over his shoulder at the shuttered windows, and when he finished he rapped his knuckles twice on the board itself, some old soldier's superstition, and then they were gone into the white.

She dressed without hurry. That was the trick of it, she'd learned. Hurry was a confession. She braided her hair, put on her plainest coat, the one with the mended elbow, and went down into the street carrying an empty market basket like any woman with any errand.

The broadsheet was already ringed by neighbors, standing at the careful distance of people who want to read and don't want to be seen reading. The Widow Marsh was there. Old Fennick the cooper. The boy who swept the tanner's step, pretending to sweep.

**BY ORDER OF THE LORD PROTECTOR**, it began, in letters fat enough to read from a moving cart, **A GENERAL HUNT IS DECLARED AGAINST ALL PERSONS PRACTISING UNLICENSED ARTS WITHIN THE WALLS OF VELL.**

*Unlicensed arts.* The phrase had been invented by a lawyer to mean magic without saying magic, because even the crown's own clerks flinched at writing the word. Beneath it came the list of what counted: scrying. Charm-craft. Memory-work — *the theft or sale of recollection*, written out in full, and here Ulyana felt the cold walk up her spine and settle between her shoulder blades like a hand.

Memory-work was named first, actually. Before scrying. Someone had decided it mattered most.

And beneath all of that, the reward line, which was how you knew the crown meant business this time and not merely theater: **TEN CROWNS FOR EVERY GIFTED PERSON TAKEN. TWENTY FOR ONE WHO RESISTS.**

Ten crowns. Half a year's honest wages for most of the people standing around this board. Enough for a family to eat through winter. Enough, if you were desperate, to forget that the person you were selling had a face.

"Disgraceful," said Widow Marsh, loudly, to no one and everyone.

"Mm," said Fennick.

They'd both bought charms off her within the last month. The widow had wept at her stall over a bottled evening — her husband laughing at something stupid, rain on the awning, his hand warm on the back of her neck. Fennick had bought a memory of being young, because his knees hurt now and youth was the one country he couldn't walk back to. And here they stood, performing outrage at the board like two actors who'd forgotten which play they were in.

Ulyana didn't say anything. She looked at the broadsheet a long moment — long enough to seem like ordinary civic dread, the kind anyone might feel — and then she moved on down the lane with her empty basket, and felt their eyes slide off her the way eyes slid off every unremarkable woman in Vell.

That was the whole art of survival, really. Be furniture. Be weather. Be nothing worth ten crowns.

She almost believed it, walking home.

---

The screaming started at noon.

It came up Cinderglass Lane from the direction of the wash-houses, thin at first, the way screams are when distance gets its hands on them, and then closer, and then unmistakably human and unmistakably next door. Ulyana was at her worktable, sorting stock — little glass vials wrapped in waxed linen, each labeled in her small neat hand — and she set down the vial she was holding very carefully, because her fingers had begun to shake, and shaking hands drop things.

Marta. It was Marta Quill who lived below and across, the dyer, with the burn-scarred forearms and the laugh like a cart going downhill. Marta, who had never once asked where Ulyana got her charms but had traded her a bolt of saffron cloth last spring and called her *little sister* with a dye-stained finger jabbed affectionately at her chest.

They brought her past Ulyana's window bound in a witch's net — iron-threaded mesh that made the gifted feel like their skin was being flayed in slow motion, that was the point of it, cruelty dressed up as restraint. Marta's mouth was open. What came out of it wasn't words anymore, hadn't been since the wash-house; it was just sound, the sound a person makes when the world has stopped being the world.

There was blood on Marta's cheek. Ulyana would think about that later — not the net, not the guards, not the crowd, but the specific fact of the blood, a bright thread running from her hairline down past her jaw, and how no one wiped it away, and how Marta herself didn't seem to notice it, because everything else hurting her was so much larger.

A neighbor asked one of the guards what she'd done. The guard, young, unhappy, said: "Someone said she had the gift. That's all it takes now." He said it flatly, the way you report weather. Then he saw Ulyana in the window and looked away first, and that look-away told her everything about him she needed to know: he wasn't sure yet. He wasn't sure yet, and unsure men are the most dangerous kind, because they have something left to prove.

The cart took Marta toward the Hall of Justice, and the screaming faded by degrees until it was a memory, and then it wasn't even that.

Ulyana stood at her window for a long time after the street emptied. Somewhere below, someone started sweeping. Life in Vell was like that. It swept around the holes people left.

Then she went to her hearth and began to feed it her life's work.

---

She burned them in batches, because burning them all at once would make too much smoke and smoke drew questions. Charms first — the harmless ones, the ones she sold honestly enough, a grandmother's kitchen, a first snowfall, a wedding dance seen from the edge of the floor. These were memories people had *sold* her freely, or fragments she'd lifted from those who wouldn't miss them, and they went up with soft pops of released light, little ghosts escaping the flame. The fire turned briefly gold each time, then settled back to orange, greedy again.

Then the inventory she'd never shown anyone. The strong stuff. A nobleman's entire childhood, lifted from him at a party while he slept in his cups — that vial fought her, hissing when the wax broke, spitting a smell like cut grass and someone else's mother's bread. A judge's memory of taking a bribe; she'd kept it as insurance, and it burned with a greasy black flame and a stink like spoiled meat, and she was glad to see it go.

Vial by vial. Label by label. Her hands knew the work and hated it. Each charm was money, yes, but it was also proof — proof that she existed as what she was, and proof burns faster than paper.

By dusk the hearth held nothing but ash that glittered faintly, the way magical residue does when it dies. She raked it out and scattered it in the slop bucket, doused it well. In Vell, even your ashes could testify against you.

And then there was the box under the floorboard.

She'd been saving it for last without admitting that's what she was doing. Inside, wrapped in wool, lay the one charm she hadn't dared sell, hadn't dared examine, hadn't dared anything except keep — three years now, through two moves and one bad winter when she'd eaten nothing but onions for a week and could have sold this single vial for more than a month's rent.

She'd taken it years ago, in her early days of thieving, from a client whose name she'd never learned — a pale man in court colors, come to her old fence in secret, wanting to be rid of something. People sometimes paid to *forget*. That was the ugly half of her trade that she kept in the back of her mind behind a locked door: for every grieving widow buying back a dead husband, there was someone paying to bury something alive. The pale man had paid very well. She'd drawn the memory out of him with her gift the way you draw a splinter, and bottled it, and never once looked inside.

Because the moment she had touched it, drawing it free, she had *seen* — a flash, the thief's toll, the way every stolen memory shows the thief its surface before it settles — and what she'd seen made no sense.

She sat on the floor with the box in her lap and the fire burned down to embers behind her, and finally, with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, she unwrapped the wool and took out the vial.

Small. Ordinary glass. The memory inside shifted like smoke underwater, faintly luminous, the color of late afternoon in high mountains.

She should burn it. Every instinct honed by twelve years of surviving Vell said burn it, burn it now, drop it in the coals and rake the ashes into the river. A charm she couldn't explain was a charm that could hang her. The hunt had named memory-work *first*.

But her hands didn't move toward the fire.

She pressed the vial to her temple instead — the old way, the dangerous way, opening yourself to a memory that isn't yours — and let it in.

*A mountain gate.*

Not Vell. Not anywhere near Vell. Stone underfoot worn smooth by centuries of feet, air thin and clean and cold in a way lowland lungs complain about. A gate of black iron taller than a church, set into the living rock of the mountain itself, worked with sigils that hurt to look at directly — wards, old ones, fed by something that glowed far below like a heart behind the stone. A dull red pulse. A fire seen through miles of rock.

And a boy standing before the gate.

He was maybe fourteen, thin, dark-haired, dressed in the rough good clothes of a family that had dressed him carefully for this because there would be no other clothes after. He wasn't crying. That was the detail that cracked something in Ulyana's chest — he stood very straight, chin up, the way children stand when they've been told to be brave and have decided, with terrible seriousness, to comply. An adult in court colors held his shoulder. Clerks with ledgers murmured somewhere behind. And the boy was looking up at the gate, and then — the memory turned, the way memories do, following the gaze of the one who owned it —

— and the boy looked *back*, over his shoulder, directly at her. Directly at the man whose memory this was supposed to be. Except it couldn't be, because the boy's face was—

The vial fell from her temple. She caught it against her knee. Her whole body was ringing like a struck bell.

She knew that face.

She knew it the way you know your own reflection — not by study but by the absence of surprise. The same narrow jaw she washed every morning in her own mirror. The same crooked canine tooth. The same eyes, brown shot through with green, that had looked back at her from every looking-glass of her childhood beside her own.

*Tomas.*

Her brother. Who had died — no. Not died. Who had been *sold*, eight years ago, when she was sixteen and the debts had come due and their father's creditors had circled like gulls, and the story the family had agreed to tell itself was that Tomas had gone to work for a merchant house in the lowlands and simply never written. She had been young and grief-stricken and practical, the Pemberton way, and she had folded that loss up small and put it away and built a life on top of the grave of it.

And this memory — this memory that belonged to some pale court official, some clerk or broker present at the sale — had lived in her floorboard for three years, and she had never opened it, and she understood now with a horror that arrived slowly, like floodwater rising past ankle and knee and waist, why she had never opened it. Why she had paid a memory-witch — she could not remember which one, could not remember *when*, the transaction itself was gone, smoothed over, sealed — a great deal of money to take something *out of her*.

She had stolen this memory from herself.

She had paid to forget her own brother, and the forgetting had been imperfect, because grief doesn't drown, it only goes under, and it had surfaced as this: a charm she couldn't explain, kept through two moves and a starving winter, guarded by the part of her that never signed the contract.

The ember-light breathed in the hearth. Down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up.

Ulyana sat on the floor of her room above Cinderglass Lane and held her brother's last afternoon in her fist, and the dread that had walked up her spine at dawn — the broadsheet, Marta's blood, the guard's uncertain eyes — changed shape in her chest. Dread is a thing that wants you frozen. This was not that. This had heat in it now, and direction.

Somewhere under that mountain, past the black gate and the hungry red glow, her brother was alive — alive the way fuel is alive, the way a candle is alive — because she had glimpsed it in the memory's depths before it closed: the sigils drinking light, the wards fed from below, the whole vast machinery of the court running on something that flickered.

On someone.

She stood up. Wrapped the vial back in its wool. But she didn't put it back under the floorboard. She put it inside her shirt, against her skin, over the place where her heartbeat had gone strange and steady.

Tomorrow she would need things she didn't have: a map of the mountain road, a disguise, money, a plan, and knowledge of the court beneath — its gates, its wards, its officers. Knowledge of the kind that only two kinds of people possessed. The priests of the dying flame. And the men who had hunted the gifted for the crown, and knew exactly where such souls were sent, because they had sent them.

One of those men had quit. Two winters ago. Corvin Ashe, the inquisitor who walked out of the Hall of Justice mid-trial and never gave a reason, and drank now in a dockside tavern called the Drowned Bell, and flinched, according to rumor, at the smell of smoke.

Ulyana took down her coat with the mended elbow and put it on.

Outside, the fog was coming back in off the river, swallowing the lamps one by one, and somewhere in the city a bell tolled the hour — and beneath the bell, faint, imagined or not, she could have sworn she heard a fire roaring, far away under stone, getting hungrier.
