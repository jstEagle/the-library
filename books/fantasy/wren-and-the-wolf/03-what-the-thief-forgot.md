## 3. What the Thief Forgot

Sleep did not come to Ulyana Pemberton that night.

She lay in the narrow room above the dye-shop on Tanner's Row, in a bed she rented by the week from a landlady who asked no questions because Ulyana paid in coin and never in conversation. The ceiling had a water stain shaped like a closed hand. She had stared at it many nights before. Tonight it seemed to stare back.

The burning was still in her nose. Char-smoke clung to her hair, her sleeves, the creases of her palms, no matter how she scrubbed. She had fed eleven charms to the brazier behind the shop — eleven bottled memories gone up in a hiss of silver flame — and the ash of them had drifted down Tanner's Row like gray snow, settling on doorsteps, on shutters, on the proclamation board with its fresh crown seal and its list of crimes that could now be written against any soul in Vell who saw too much or knew too little of forgetting.

One charm remained. It sat on the table beside her bed in its little stoppered vial, turning slowly as if it were alive, the memory inside it catching the lamplight like a fish turning in dark water.

She had meant to burn it with the others. Her hand had been around it, her thumb already working at the wax seal, when something in her own chest had slammed shut like a door. Not fear. Fear she knew; fear was a familiar tenant. This was older and stranger — a refusal rising up out of some cellar of herself she had not visited in years, saying *no*, saying *not this one*, saying it with a voice that did not sound entirely like hers.

So she had kept it, and now she could not sleep for looking at it.

*What is this?* she thought, for perhaps the hundredth time since the brazier. *Whose is this?*

Because here was the thing about stolen memories: they came labeled. Not with words — nothing so convenient — but with texture. A widow's grief had a certain weight, a wet heaviness, like wool after rain. A merchant's pride was brittle and bright, sugar-glass. The memories of soldiers tasted of iron and long roads. After six years in the trade, Ulyana could hold a vial to the light and read its contents the way a jeweler reads a stone — provenance, flaws, the little signatures of the life it had been pried out of.

This one told her nothing. Or rather, it told her something impossible: it felt like *hers*. Like reaching into her own pocket and finding a key to a door she'd never seen.

That was the trap of it. That was why her body had refused the fire. Somewhere beneath the craft and the commerce and the careful armor of her trade, some part of her had recognized what her waking mind could not name.

She sat up. The bed creaked. The lamp guttered and steadied.

"Fine," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the stain on the ceiling, to whatever stubborn thing lived under her ribs. "Show me."

She took the vial in both hands. The glass was cool, then warm, then cool again, like a living thing breathing shallowly. She worked the wax loose with her thumbnail — a skill performed ten thousand times, always on other people's heads, other people's pasts — and the stopper came free with a small sigh, a breath of air that smelled impossibly of pine and cold stone and woodsmoke.

Then she pressed the open mouth of the vial to her temple, the way she'd done a thousand times in reverse, and let it pour in.

* * *

The world went white, then gold, then —

— snow. Snow falling through a light that has no business existing so far north, a honeyed late-afternoon light that turns every flake to drifting ember. A mountain fills half the sky, black rock streaked with old fire-veins, and cut into its base is a gate. Not a city gate. A *throat*. Iron-banded, tall as three men, carved over with script that hurts to look at directly, the way the sun hurts.

He is standing before it. A boy. Fourteen, maybe fifteen — all elbows and restless feet, hair the same unmanageable brown as hers, a coat two sizes too big because coats are bought for the boy you'll become, not the boy you are. His breath steams. His hands are shoved deep in his pockets to hide that they're shaking.

There is a man beside him. Gray-robed, gray-faced, with the soft well-fed hands of someone who has never carried anything heavier than a ledger. He is speaking in the low, soothing tone people use on animals and children being led somewhere they don't want to go.

"—a great honor," the gray man is saying. "You must understand that. There are perhaps a dozen souls in all Vell considered worthy, and the court chose *you*."

"I didn't say I wanted to be chosen." The boy's voice cracks on *wanted*, and he hates it, she can hear him hating it, hating the crack, hating the snow, hating the enormous indifferent beauty of the gate.

"The debt doesn't ask what you want." The gray man's tone does not change. That is the horror of it, later, when she replays this — the tone never changes, never sharpens, never threatens. It simply states, the way winter states itself. "Your sister signed. Your sister's debts became yours when the guild called them due. This is mercy, boy. The alternative was the river."

And the boy — Tomas, the name arrives whole and terrible, *Tomas*, and the recognition hits her like a fall onto ice — Tomas looks back down the road. Just once. Down the long white road toward the city he will never see again, toward the smoke of its ten thousand chimneys, toward a garret room where a girl of seventeen is sitting with her head in her hands surrounded by ledger pages she cannot make balance.

He looks back, and though there is no one on the road, though she is nothing in this moment but a memory watching itself be remembered, his eyes find the place where she stands inside the remembering, and he says:

"She thinks I don't know. She thinks I signed without reading it." His jaw works. His eyes are bright and furious and fourteen years old. "I read every word."

The gray man makes a small sound that might be sympathy in a man who has none left. "Then you understand—"

"I understand she'd have sold herself first." Tomas turns to the gate. Lifts his chin. Does the thing he always did when he was terrified and determined not to show it — plants his feet like a tree deciding to be a wall. "So don't dress it up. Don't call it an honor. Open your door and write down what actually happened: Tomas Pemberton, sold to pay his sister's debts, price of one life against four hundred crowns, buyer the court beneath the mountain, purpose—" and here his voice finally breaks clean through, and he lets it, lets them hear it, makes it their problem, "—purpose, fuel."

The gates begin to open. Light comes out of them that is not sunlight, not lamplight, not any light that belongs to the surface of the world — a deep red-gold pulsing light, rhythmic, patient, *breathing*, and Ulyana understands with a lurching, sickening certainty what she is seeing, what he is walking toward, what has been burning under the mountain all these years with her brother's soul for kindling—

*Tomas—*

He steps forward. And just before the dark takes him, just before the throat of the mountain swallows the last of the honeyed light, he says one more thing, quiet, almost to himself, almost to her across the impossible distance between a memory and the woman holding it:

"It's all right, Wren. You'll forget. I made sure of it."

* * *

Ulyana came back to herself on the floor.

She did not remember falling. Her cheek was against the boards; the empty vial had rolled under the table; the lamp had burned down to a stub and the room was mostly shadow. Her whole body was shaking in a slow, deep rhythm, like something seismic passing through her, and her face was soaked, and she could not account for a single minute of the last hour except that she had been somewhere else, somewhere with snow and a gate and a voice she would have known anywhere, anywhere, even stripped of everything, even drowned, even dead.

"Tomas," she said. The word came out ruined.

She got up by way of the table, the chair, the wall. Her reflection hung ghost-gray in the dark window: a thin woman, twenty-three years old, with smoke still in her hair and her eyes gone huge and wrong.

*Wren.* He had called her Wren. No one had called her that in — how long? Six years? Seven? It surfaced with the memory like a body surfacing in a flooded cellar, bloated and unmistakable. *Wren.* Because when they were small she had been small and quick and always in the rafters of things, up in the eaves of the market stealing apples, up in the loft of the chandlery counting candles, and Tomas, eight years old and solemn as a magistrate, had pronounced that she wasn't a girl at all, she was a wren, and the name had stuck until the world got big enough to crush it out of use.

Six years. Six years since anyone said it. Six years since—

Since what?

She stood in the middle of the floor and made herself do the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one discipline she trusted, the one honest religion left to her.

Tomas Pemberton. Born in the spring, the year of the double comet. Their mother dead of the lung-rot when he was nine. Their father gone before that — conscripted, or fled, depending which neighbor's version you believed. Two children alone in Vell, and Vell ate children unless they learned to eat first. So she had learned. She had found out she could *take* — that her fingers on a sleeping man's temple could draw out the color of his childhood, the smell of his mother's kitchen, the whole warm weight of a day he loved, and bottle it, and sell it to someone starving for exactly that. Magic, the crown called it now. Then, it had only been a talent, like a good singing voice. It paid the rent. It paid the debts. It paid, eventually, for the mistake — the one bad season, the borrowed money from the guild at rates that moved like living things, the four hundred crowns that grew instead of shrank no matter what she poured into the hole.

Four hundred crowns. The number in the memory matched the number in her bones. She had carried it so long it had stopped being money and become anatomy.

And then — what? What happened after the debt came due?

Nothing. That was the answer. Nothing happened, because there was no *after*. There was a gap in her life the size of a brother, a gap she had walked around for six years the way you walk around a missing stair, and she had never once wondered at it, because wondering required noticing, and noticing had been taken from her.

*I made sure of it.*

She crossed to the cracked mirror above the washstand and looked at herself properly, the way she hadn't dared in days. And there it was, once you knew to look. A scar behind her left ear, thin and pale and precise — the mark every thief in her trade carried, the entry-point scar, the little door they opened in themselves to take from others. Except hers was wrong. Hers was deeper. Older. Hers was not the neat keyhole of a practiced hand but the ragged signature of a desperate one, done fast, done alone, done by candlelight in a garret room by a girl of seventeen with a ledger full of ruin and a brother's coat already packed by the door.

She had done it to herself. She had reached into her own head and torn out the worst hour of her life and buried it in a vial, because a person cannot survive knowing what she had traded, and she had needed to survive, and survival was the whole of the law she lived by.

But she had been careless, or merciful, or both. She had hidden the memory instead of destroying it. Bottled grief keeps. Bottled truth keeps better. For six years it had sat in her stock like a coal under ash, waiting for the night the rest of her life burned down around it.

"Oh, Tomas." She pressed her forehead to the cold mirror. "You stupid, brave, unbearable boy. You *read* it. Of course you read it. You never signed anything in your life without reading it — you wouldn't even sign for the milk—"

The laugh that came out of her was the worst sound she had ever made.

He had known. All along, standing at the gate, he had known exactly what she'd done and exactly what it would cost her to know it, and he had spent his last free minutes on the surface of the earth making sure she would forget him — not out of cruelty, not even out of kindness, exactly, but out of that fierce, stubborn, maddening protectiveness that had been his since he was small enough to carry. He had taken the guilt off her the way he used to take the blame for broken crockery, for stolen apples, for everything, *everything*, always stepping in front of her with his chin up and his feet planted.

And she had forgotten him. Not lost him — *forgotten* him, which was worse, which was a violence she had committed with her own hands. Every year since, she had walked past boys of a certain age in the market and felt a pang she couldn't source. Every winter she had bought two of something — two bread rolls, two pairs of gloves — and stood baffled at her own purse. Every year on the first snow she had wept without reason and blamed the wind.

The wind. The snow. The gate.

She pushed away from the mirror and began, mechanically, to move — dressing, pulling on boots, gathering her few tools into the worn leather roll she'd carried since she was fifteen. Her hands knew the motions. Her mind was elsewhere, down a long white road, at a gate of black iron, doing new arithmetic.

The court beneath the mountain. The court that bought souls. The gray-robed brokers who came down to Vell twice a year, whose transactions appeared in no ledger she had ever seen, whose buyers paid in coin minted from no kingdom she could name. She had heard whispers — everyone in her trade had — of vessels, of vaults, of a fire that never went out. She had filed it all under *not my business*, the way she filed everything that might get her hanged.

Not anymore.

They had her brother. They had had him for six years, burning. And the crown — the crown that today nailed proclamations to boards and dragged neighbors screaming into wagons — the crown had sold him, or brokered him, or at minimum known, and she would find out which if it was the last act of her life.

At the door she stopped. The room behind her held the ash-smell of eleven destroyed charms, the ghosts of eleven strangers' happiest hours, and one empty vial rolling gently under the table like a spent shell casing.

"You'll forget," she repeated softly. "You made sure of it."

Well. She was a thief. Taking things back was the family trade.

She blew out the stub of the lamp, and went down the stairs into the dark, toward the one man in Vell who would know the road beneath the mountain — a witch-hunter who had quit, which meant a witch-hunter with debts of his own, and debts, Ulyana Pemberton thought grimly, were the one currency she had never once failed to collect.
