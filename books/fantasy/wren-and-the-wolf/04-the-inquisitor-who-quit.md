## 4. The Inquisitor Who Quit

The house on Candlemaker's Row had once been white.

Ulyana could see it in the bones of the place — the way the lintel still held a carved rose beneath its coat of soot, the way the windows kept their shape even through grime. Now it was gray like everything else on the Row, and the man who lived inside it was gray too, or trying to be.

She watched from across the street for an hour before she moved.

Corvin Ashe came out at dusk to buy bread, which was the kind of habit that got careful men killed. He was taller than his reputation, thinner than his portraits — the wanted-notices had made him broad and terrible, a tower of a witch-hunter with a face like a closed door. In person he looked like a door someone had abandoned. His coat was good wool gone shabby at the cuffs. He carried no sword. That was either humility or arrogance, and Ulyana had learned to price both.

He paid for the loaf with copper, counted twice. He nodded to the baker. He did not look at the shadows, because men who had stopped looking were men who had decided they deserved whatever found them.

*Good*, Ulyana thought. *That's a debt I can work with.*

She crossed the street and fell into step beside him, close enough that their shoulders almost brushed.

"Bread's stale by morning," she said. "You should know that. You've bought from him four days running."

Ashe stopped walking. He didn't reach for a weapon — there wasn't one to reach for — but something shifted behind his eyes, the old machinery waking up. He turned and looked down at her with the flat assessment of a man reading a confession.

"I know you," he said slowly.

"No."

"You do. The charm-seller. Pemberton." His mouth twisted. "Eleven charms burned yesterday, according to my old colleagues. A pity. Those were honest work, as your trade goes."

"Your colleagues," Ulyana said, "are dragging a lamplighter's daughter out of her bed tonight. I heard them drawing up the warrant while I was burning my stock. She has fits. Falling sickness, since she was six. They'll call it possession." She let that sit between them like a coin on a table. "You'd have signed that paper once."

Something moved in his face — quick, buried, real. "I don't sign papers anymore."

"No. You buy stale bread instead." She smiled without warmth. "Walk with me, Inquisitor. Or stand here, and let me say the rest of this where the baker can hear it. Your choice. I hear the crown pays well for word of where disgraced witch-hunters sleep."

For a long moment he said nothing. A cart rattled past. Somewhere a child was being called in to supper, the sound thin and ordinary against everything Ulyana knew was coming for this city.

Then Ashe walked, and she fell into step beside him, and neither of them spoke until the Row gave way to the tanner's quarter, where the smell covered conversations the way snow covers graves.

"Say what you came to say," he said finally.

"I need the road beneath the mountain."

His stride didn't break, but it changed — a hitch small enough that only a thief would catch it. "There is no road beneath the mountain."

"There's always a road. That's the first thing they teach you in your order, isn't it? *Every lock wants opening; every wall was built by hands.*" She'd read his training manual. Stolen, third-hand, pages missing — but the missing pages had been about the court under Mount Ceren, and the missing pages themselves had told her plenty. "The Salt Court. The soul-brokers. The fire that never goes out. You wrote your resignation letter the week after you came back from there. Everyone assumed it was about the Halloway girl you burned."

"It was about the Halloway girl."

"The Halloway girl was the last straw. The mountain was the first." Ulyana stopped walking and turned to face him fully, letting him see her eyes in the light of the tannery lamps — letting him see how much she knew, or thought she did. "You went up as an escort. Six brokers, forty souls in lead-lined cases, and one witch-hunter to make the crown's deliveries look righteous. You saw what they feed the fire. And you came back down and burned a girl anyway, because the machine was still running and you were still a gear in it, and then one day you weren't."

The silence stretched. Down the lane, a dog was barking at nothing, or at something.

"You've been talking to ghosts," Ashe said quietly.

"I've been talking to ledgers. Yours are better guarded than most, but I'm very patient." This was a lie built on a truth, which was the only kind of lie she trusted. What she actually had was a memory that wasn't hers, a boy at a gate, and a brother whose name she hadn't been able to say aloud in three years. But Ashe didn't need to know the size of her arsenal. Only that she had one. "Here's what happens now. You tell me how to get into the Salt Court. You come with me, because I'll need someone the wards still remember. And when we're done, I burn every page I copied from your file, and you go back to your stale bread and your white house going gray."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow the Hall of Records receives an anonymous account of Corvin Ashe, former Inquisitor Second Rank, witnessing the tithe of souls and saying nothing. Signed in your hand — I'm very good at hands." She tilted her head. "You quit the hunt, Inquisitor. Quitting isn't innocence. It's just desertion with better posture."

He laughed then. It was a terrible sound, dry and short, like something breaking in a locked room.

"They warned us about people like you," he said. "First day of training. *Beware the thief with a cause,* the old instructor said. *A thief with a cause will spend anything. Herself included.*"

"Wise man. Was he right?"

"He was right about everything except which side of the desk I'd end up on." Ashe looked past her, up toward where Mount Ceren stood invisible beyond the city's rooftops, a darkness deeper than the sky. When he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere else entirely — back three years, back up the mountain. "You want to know what's under there. Fine. But you don't want to know it the way I know it. Nobody does."

"Try me."

He studied her for a long moment — the same flat assessment as before, but slower now, turning her over like a page he suspected had writing on the back.

"All right, Pemberton," he said at last. "But we leave before the gates open. And if I say run, you run, and if I say stop, you stop, and if I put a knife in your hand you use it exactly where I tell you and nowhere else. Because the thing you're walking toward—" He shook his head. "It doesn't bargain. It collects."

"So do I," Ulyana said. "We should get along fine."

* * *

Corvin Ashe could not sleep in the house on Candlemaker's Row, and had not truly slept in it for three years.

He lay on the narrow bed with his boots off and his coat folded over the chair, listening to the house settle, and told himself the same thing he told himself every night: *you did what the law required, and then you stopped doing what the law required, and both of those things cost what they cost.*

It was a poor prayer. It was the only one he had.

The Halloway girl came to him in the dark the way she always did — fourteen years old, wrists bound with the red cord of formal accusation, asking him not in words but with her eyes: *You're the one who decides. Aren't you? You're the one who looks.*

He had looked. That was the whole of his office, the heart of the inquisitor's craft — not the pyre, not the cord, but the looking. *Discernment*, the manuals called it. The sacred burden of telling true magic from false, guilt from desperation, the wolf from the dog that merely barked at shadows. And Corvin Ashe had been the best discerner of his generation. They'd said so at his elevation. *Ashe sees what others miss.*

He had seen a frightened girl with shaking hands and a fever, and he had seen it wrong, and the fire had taken eleven minutes to kill her, and he had counted every one of them because counting was the only penance the order allowed.

Afterward he'd requested reassignment. They gave him the mountain instead — a promotion dressed as exile, an escort duty to the Salt Court, delivering the crown's annual tithe. Forty cases. Lead-lined, warded, cold to the touch in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

He had told himself they were empty. Ceremonial vessels. Symbols of the tithe, nothing more.

Then, in the vaulted dark beneath the mountain, he had watched the soul-brokers unseal one — just one, a demonstration for the visiting dignitary, cruelty performed as courtesy — and he had heard what came out of it.

Not screaming. That was the worst of it. Everyone imagined screaming. What came out was *humming*. A tuneless, wandering hum, like a child alone in a nursery, searching for a song it had half-remembered. It lasted nine seconds before they fed it to the fire, and the fire flared green-white, and every warden in the chamber sighed with satisfaction like men settling into warm baths.

Corvin had resigned by letter from the foothill inn before dawn. He had never told anyone why. The order hadn't asked. Asking questions was how you ended up in the tithe yourself.

Now there was a knock-pattern at his door — two, pause, one — and he was out of bed with the knife from under his mattress before his mind caught up with his hands.

"It's midnight," he said through the wood. "We said before the gates opened."

"We did. Which is why I'm here at midnight." Ulyana's voice, low and amused. "Gates open at dawn. Dawn searches happen the night before, Inquisitor. Two hunters already asked the baker about you this evening. You're popular again."

He dressed in the dark. He took down the loose brick above the hearth and retrieved the things he had sworn he'd never need again: a short sword in an oilcloth wrap, a seal-ring with the inquisitor's eye stamped in black jade, a travel pass signed by a man who was now ash. The ring he weighed in his palm for a long moment. Then he pocketed it. Some doors only opened for monsters, and you carried the monster's face with you.

When he opened the front door, she was leaning against his doorframe as if she owned it, hood back, arms crossed, watching him with those quick appraising eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"You keep a knife under your mattress," she said. "Left side. Amateur. Anyone searching assumes left side."

"Noted. I'll move it to the ceiling."

"See, that's the spirit." She pushed off from the frame and started down the street, expecting him to follow, because people like her always expected that, and the hell of it was that he did.

They went down through the sleeping city, along the canal road where the water moved black and slow, and Corvin found himself talking — not because she pried, but because the night was dark and the road was long and some part of him had been waiting three years for a witness.

"The court takes souls," he said. "Not metaphorically. Not the way the Church means it. Actual souls, bottled, catalogued. The crown sells them the condemned, the nameless, the gifted who die in custody. And the court feeds them to the dragon-fire under Mount Ceren, and the fire holds up the wards, and the wards hold up—" he gestured vaguely at the world, the city, everything— "whatever the wards hold up. That's the catechism, anyway. Feed the fire, save the walls."

"But you don't believe the catechism anymore."

"I believe the fire is fed. I've seen it fed." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Whether it needs feeding — whether any of it needs doing at all, or whether it's just a furnace with a priesthood sitting on top of it — that I couldn't tell you. Discernment was supposed to be my gift. Up there, I discerned nothing. I stood in a room full of humming bottles and I thought about my career."

Ulyana was quiet for several steps. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge, which somehow made it worse.

"What did they look like," she said. "The bottles. On the shelf."

"Like anything. Like wine. Like preserves. Little lead-cased vials with wax seals and paper labels." He glanced at her. "Why?"

"Because I'm going to be reading those labels soon," she said, "and I'd rather not flinch when I do."

He wanted to ask. Every instinct he owned wanted to ask — the old instinct, the discerning one, the one that looked and looked and looked. But he had learned what his looking cost other people, and so for the first time in his life, Corvin Ashe deliberately did not ask, and walked beside her in silence instead, and found that the silence had a strange weight to it, almost companionable. Two thieves on a night road. One stole memories. The other had stolen his own conscience back from an empire, and was still not sure the theft had taken.

At the river gate, where the wall met the water and the watch-tower lamp swung its blind arc overhead, they crouched in the reeds while he picked the postern lock with tools he'd confiscated from a smuggler seven years ago and never returned.

"One condition," he said, working the pick. "Before we're past the wall and it's too late for you to change your mind."

"You're in no position to set conditions, Inquisitor."

"Humor me." The lock clicked. He eased the door open an inch and looked back at her, and what he said next surprised him by being true. "Whatever name it is you're going up the mountain for — whoever's label you're going to read on those shelves — when you find them, don't do what I did. Don't count. Counting doesn't save anyone. It only teaches you the exact size of what you failed to stop."

Ulyana went very still.

For a heartbeat he thought she would strike him, or laugh, or lie — and he braced for all three. Instead she looked at him with an expression he had no training to read at all, something raw and young underneath all that thieving polish, and said:

"Tomas," she said. "His name is Tomas. My brother. They took him eight years ago, and I sold the memory of it to survive the grief, and I only just remembered that I remembered." Her jaw tightened. "So no, Inquisitor. I won't count. I'm going to take him back."

Beyond the postern door, the canal waited, black and patient, running north toward the mountains the way all water eventually runs.

Corvin Ashe held the door open, and understood — finally, completely, with the clarity of a man arriving late at a fire — why he had agreed. Not blackmail. Blackmail was just the key she'd used. But doors only open from the inside, and he had been standing at this one for three years, waiting for someone to give him a reason to walk through.

"After you," he said, "Pemberton."

And the two of them slipped out of Vell together, into the dark that leads beneath the mountain.
