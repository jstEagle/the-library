## 5. Guild Debts

The guildhall hadn't moved, but everything around it had grown up like weeds around a headstone.

Ulyana stood across the lane from the Drowned Bell for a long while, hood up, hands in her coat pockets, watching the lamplight leak through its shutters in the familiar crooked pattern. Three years. Three years since Sable had stood on those worn stone steps and pronounced her name struck from the rolls, since forty thieves had looked at her with the particular blankness of people pretending not to know you. Three years since she'd walked out of Vell's underworld with nothing but the clothes she stood in and the skill in her fingers, and built a life in the cracks above — a stall, a name, a modest trade in other people's yesterdays.

Now she needed them again. The thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She crossed the lane before her pride could argue. The Bell's door was the same — black oak, iron-banded, with the brass knocker shaped like a drowned man's fist. She lifted it and let it fall twice, the old signal, the one that meant *friend, business, no blades*. Her knuckles knew the rhythm without consulting her.

The viewing slit opened. One eye, brown and bloodshot, appraised her.

"Closed," said the eye.

"Open for old friends."

"We don't have any of those."

"You do," Ulyana said, and pulled her hood back just enough. "You just buried the memory of them."

The eye blinked. The slit closed. A long minute passed, long enough for her to consider the alley to her left and the rooftops above, mapping exits out of habit, before the bolts began to draw back — three of them, then a fourth she didn't remember being there. The door swung inward on warmth and lamplight and the smell of tallow and fried onions, and the guild of Vell's underworld looked out at her through the face of a boy she half-recognized, grown taller and harder since the last time she'd cuffed his ear for picking her lock.

"Pemberton," the boy said, and there was something in his voice — not welcome, exactly. Recognition. The guild remembered her even if it had disowned her. Memory was currency everywhere in Vell, and the Drowned Bell had never been able to afford forgetting anyone.

"I need to see Sable."

"He's seeing no one."

"He'll see me." She stepped past the boy into the hall before he could decide otherwise, and the door closed behind her with the soft, final click of a trap.

* * *

The main room was fuller than she expected. Bad times drove thieves indoors, she supposed — the crown's hunt had emptied the streets of anyone with a gift, and the guild's ordinary criminals were huddling closer to their fire. A dozen faces turned as she crossed the floor, and she read them the way she read everything: the card-sharp Wren's-finger Marta, still cheating at solitaire against herself; the twins, Hodge and Hob, grown from wiry boys into wiry men; old Cutter Vane by the hearth, whittling a piece of wood into something that was probably a knife and had been, for twenty years, probably a knife.

None of them spoke to her. None of them looked away, either. That was the guild's way — exile didn't erase you, it just froze you, a figure in the window of a house you used to live in.

Sable was at the far end, at the long table under the Bell's one good chandelier, counting coins with the methodical patience of a man who loved the sound more than the sum. He hadn't changed. That was the cruelest thing. He was still lean and grey-stubbled, still dressed in the guildmaster's uniform of expensive plainness, still wearing the silver ring — a bell, tiny and tarnished — that marked his office. His hands didn't stop moving when she approached. He counted through her silence, made her wait through the last of a purse, and only then looked up with eyes the color of wet slate.

"Ulyana Pemberton." He said her whole name, the way you'd read a charge sheet. "The Bell must be haunted. I could have sworn we struck this name from our rolls."

"You did. I'm not here to be unstruck."

"No?" He leaned back, lacing his fingers over his vest. "Then what does an exile want in her old house? Come to beg? Come to steal?" His mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I hear that's a thing you do now. Memories. Little bottles of other people's lives, sold at a stall like fish. Half the city says you're a witch. The other half says you're a fraud. Which are you tonight?"

"The one who needs passage," she said. "Out of Vell. Quietly, before the hunters close the roads. I'm told the Bell still moves people through the mountain passes when the price is right."

"It does." He turned a coin over his knuckles, caught it, turned it again. "And the price is right for friends. For exiles, it's steeper."

"Name it."

"Money's no good to me." He gestured, and the room began, subtly, to empty — Marta first, then the twins, then the boy at the door, until only the two of them and old Cutter Vane remained, and Vane was deaf as a post and whittling and counted as furniture. "The hunt's bad for trade. The crown's squeezing every road out of the city, and the roads are my arteries. I can get you through, but it'll cost me favors I'd rather not spend. So you'll pay in kind."

"I don't have anything you want."

"You have exactly what I want." He stood, and up close she could smell the pomade in his hair and, under it, the ink and paper smell of a man who'd spent the week with documents. "Three months ago, the crown's men came through that door with a writ and took everything off my shelves that had writing on it. Ledgers. Rolls. The names of every debtor, every fence, every smuggler who ever owed the Bell a favor. Forty years of accounts, Ulyana, sitting in the Hall of Records right now, in a strongbox with a crown seal on it, being read by clerks who are building a case to hang half my people."

"Then that's a crown problem."

"It's a *Bell* problem, which means it's mine, and I can't touch it." His voice stayed pleasant, which she knew from old experience was the most dangerous register he owned. "The Hall of Records is watched by the Inquisition's own. If I send guild hands, they'll be marked before they reach the second floor. But you —" the coin appeared between his fingers again, vanished — "you're not guild anymore. You're not anyone. A ghost with a stall and a talent. If a ghost is caught, the Bell feels nothing. If a ghost succeeds —" the coin appeared again — "the Bell remembers its debts."

"You want me to steal it back for you."

"I want you to *earn* your passage. The ledger for the road. One job, one night, and by morning you're a ghost with a horse under her and my word that no gate between here and the passes opens for the hunters on your name." He smiled then, fully, and it was the smile she remembered from her first year in the guild, when he'd been kind to her in the way a fisherman is kind to bait. "You were the best fingers I ever trained, Pemberton. It would be a shame to let all that go to waste on widows' keepsakes."

She should have walked out. She knew it the way you know a stair is missing in the dark — a certainty in the gut, arriving a half-second too late to matter. Because under the humiliation, under the cold arithmetic of it, something in her had already said yes. Something that had missed the smell of tallow and the click of bolts and the company of people who knew her name and her whole history without needing to buy it.

That was the trap of the Drowned Bell. It didn't hold you with chains. It held you with belonging, and belonging was the one currency Ulyana Pemberton had never learned to counterfeit.

"The Hall of Records," she said. "Crown seal. Inquisition guards."

"Second floor, strongroom, east wing. The seal's wax over iron — you'll want a heat-knife, and I'll give you one." He slid a folded paper across the table. "Floor plans. My last clerk drew them before the clerks stopped being mine."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you can take your chances at the gates with every other gifted soul in Vell, and I'll light a candle for you at Saint Verity's." He picked up the next purse and began to count it, dismissing her, and then — as she turned — added, softly, "Oh. And Pemberton. The hunters came by yesterday asking after a woman who sells memories. I told them I'd never heard of you. Consider that a free favor. The next one's on account."

She stopped with her hand on the door.

"You knew," she said, without turning. "You knew what they'd do. You knew I'd have nowhere else to go."

"I knew you were the best I ever trained," Sable said, "and that pride is a door that opens from the inside. I just waited for you to push."

* * *

She didn't go straight home. She walked the long way, through the tanners' quarter where the river stank of lime and the washing lines crossed overhead like rigging, and she let herself feel it — all of it, the humiliation sitting in her throat like bile and, worse, underneath it, the treacherous warmth. The lamplight through the shutters. The boy who'd known her name. Old Vane, whittling his probably-knife, who had looked up at her — just once, just for a second — with an expression she'd spent three years pretending she didn't need: *you came back*.

Three years ago they'd cast her out for a theft she hadn't committed. That was the version she told herself, the version she'd polished smooth as river glass: Sable had found the guild's winter take short by two hundred crowns, and someone had to hang for it, and the orphan girl with no family and no friends was the easiest weight to throw overboard. She'd left with nothing and sworn she'd never come back, and she'd kept that oath the way you keep an oath when the alternative is admitting it costs you something to keep.

And now she'd pushed the door open from the inside, exactly as he'd said. Exactly as he'd planned. Because Sable didn't waste moves. He'd known about the hunters. He'd known about her stall. He'd known, with the patience of a man who counted coins for the sound of it, that the crown's witch-hunt would eventually drive her back to the only family she'd ever had — the family that had sold her to save its own purse.

She climbed the stairs to her rooms above the tannery and lit the lamp, and the small familiar space looked back at her: the shelf of bottled memories, the blanket on the chair, the crack in the window she'd never fixed because the whistle of wind through it was the closest thing she had to a voice in the house. She'd built all this out of nothing. She'd built a life that didn't need the Bell.

And she'd trade a piece of it back tonight, gladly, for a horse and an open gate.

She sat at her table and unrolled Sable's floor plans by lamplight. The Hall of Records, east wing, second floor. The strongroom marked in a clerk's careful hand, with a note in the margin: *guards changed at the ninth bell. Two on the stair. One within.* She studied it until the lines were printed on the inside of her eyelids, the way she'd studied locks as a girl, the way she'd studied faces. Then she set the plans aside and looked at her shelf.

Forty-odd bottles glinted in the lamplight, each one a life in miniature, each one stolen fair and sold or kept. Her stock. Her livelihood. And at the back of the shelf, wrapped in a scrap of black cloth she hadn't unwrapped since the night she'd burned the rest, the one she couldn't sell and couldn't throw away: the memory of a boy at a mountain gate, standing in the snow with his breath steaming, looking back over his shoulder at someone the memory didn't show.

She'd been carrying that memory for years without knowing whose it was. Three days ago she'd learned. And the road to the mountain, the road to the court beneath it, the road to Tomas — it ran through the Hall of Records, through a crown seal, through the very strongroom where the guild's ledger sat beside, perhaps, a hundred other documents. Manifests. Purchase records. The mountain court bought souls; Corvin Ashe had told her that much before she'd blackmailed him into silence about the rest. Souls bought from the crown directly, he'd said. Which meant somewhere in that building, on some clerk's shelf, there was paper that said *Pemberton*.

Her brother's name. Her debts. The sale that had fed a child to a furnace under a mountain so that a girl of nine could eat through the winter.

"I'm coming," she said to the wrapped bottle, quietly, the way you speak to the dead or the almost-dead. "Whatever it costs. I'm coming."

Then she blew out the lamp, because a thief works in the dark, and in the dark she let herself plan — the stair at the ninth bell, the two guards, the one within, the heat-knife and the wax seal and the iron box. She planned it the way Sable had taught her, years ago, in this same city, in a room with a chandelier: *every lock is a promise, and every promise can be picked.*

The ninth bell would ring tomorrow night. She'd be under it when it did.

And if the ledger held what she thought it held — if somewhere in the crown's careful accounts there was a line, a date, a sum, a name — then Sable's little errand would become her own, and the Drowned Bell could keep its passage. She'd take the road to the mountain on her own terms, with her own prize, and let the guildmaster count his coins to the sound of nothing.

She smiled in the dark, and it was not a nice smile. It was the smile Sable had trained into her, all those years ago, before he'd decided she was worth more as a sacrifice than a daughter.

*Every lock is a promise,* she thought, pulling the blanket over her shoulders and closing her eyes. *And tomorrow night, I'm going to break one of yours.*
