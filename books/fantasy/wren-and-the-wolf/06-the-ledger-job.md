## 6. The Ledger Job

The Hall of Records did not look like a place where secrets lived. It looked like a place where paper went to die.

Ulyana crouched in the shadow of a buttress across the square, watching the great bronze doors catch the lamplight — doors wide enough to admit a funeral procession and carved with the crowned face of King Aldric's grandfather, whose stone eyes had watched Vell's citizens shuffle in with their grievances for sixty years. Above the doors, in letters meant to be read from horseback: *ALL DEEDS ARE KNOWN TO THE CROWN.*

*All deeds are known,* she thought. *Except the ones I'm about to do.*

Beside her, Corvin Ashe shifted his weight and made a small sound of disapproval. He'd been making them all evening — at her choice of clothing (a courier's gray, unremarkable), at her habit of chewing the end of a lockpick between jobs, at the fact that she had eaten an entire meat pie while he laid out his diagrams.

"You're going to tell me what's wrong with my plan now?" she said.

"Your plan is that there is no plan."

"My plan is that you have a plan, and I follow it until it breaks."

Corvin exhaled through his nose. In the lamplight she could see the new lines around his mouth, the ones that hadn't been there three years ago when his face had hung on every garrison wall in the city — *Inquisitor Corvin Ashe, Shield of the Faithful* — back before he'd torn the shield off his own chest and walked out of the witch-hunt mid-purge. She'd memorized that face as a girl in the guild, the way other children memorized saints. Now it was just a tired man with a sword callus and eyes that flinched away from hers.

"The Hall closes its shutters at dusk," he said. "Six clerics sleep on-site, in the east wing. Two night watchmen walk the circuit — one on the ground floor, one on the galleries above the reading room. The Sealed Annex is behind the iron gate at the north end. That gate has no lock."

"No lock?"

"It has a ward. A truth-ward." He said the word flatly, the way a man says the name of a disease that killed someone he loved. "It reads intent. You can't pick it, because there's nothing to pick. It opens for hands carrying honest business and stays shut for anything else. And if you force it—"

"It knows what I am." Ulyana turned the lockpick over in her fingers. "So we don't go through the gate."

"There is no way into the Annex except the gate."

"There's always another way." She nodded up, past the galleries, past the clerks' dormers, to the roofline where a lead rain-gutter ran like a silver vein toward a small dark window set high in the Annex's outer wall. "That window. What is it?"

"A light-shaft. It's nine inches wide and drops thirty feet onto the archive floor."

"Nine inches." She smiled. "I've been thinner."

Corvin stared at her. "You're not fitting through a nine-inch shaft."

"I fit through smaller when I was fourteen, and Sable hadn't yet decided pies were worth eating." She tucked the lockpick away and stood, knees cracking, and looked at him until he looked back. "You said you'd get me in, Inquisitor. So get me in."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he pulled something from inside his coat — a flat brass disc on a chain, stamped with the sunburst of the Office of Inquiry — and hung it around his neck where the old shield used to sit.

"Follow me," he said. "And Ulyana. Whatever happens in there — whatever you see me do — don't stop walking."

She wanted to ask what that meant. The bell over the Hall began to toll the hour, and there was no time.

* * *

They went in through the front.

It was, Ulyana admitted, the most beautiful thing about Corvin Ashe: he understood that the boldest lie was often the truest-looking one. He straightened his coat, lifted his chin into the old authority, and knocked on the bronze doors like a man who had spent ten years being answered on the first knock.

A slot slid open. A bleary eye.

"Office of Inquiry," Corvin said, holding the brass disc to the gap. "Audit of sealed records. Warrant of the Second Seal."

The eye read the disc. Then it read Corvin's face — and here was the thing Ulyana had understood about fear since she was small enough to hide under stalls: people were afraid of magic, yes, but they were more afraid of the men who hunted it. The eye widened. The slot snapped shut. Bolts turned like knuckles cracking, and the doors swung open on a young watchman who bowed so fast he nearly folded.

"Inquisitor. Sir. We weren't told—"

"You weren't told because audits aren't announced." Corvin swept past him without slowing, and Ulyana followed two steps behind, hood up, carrying the document case they'd prepared — warrants copied from genuine ones, ink aged with tea, seals pressed from a mold Corvin still kept out of some stubborn professional nostalgia. "I'll need the Annex opened. My clerk will handle the retrieval."

"Sir, the Annex requires the under-clerk of records, and Master Hollen doesn't come in until—"

"Then fetch him."

"He—he lodges across the city—"

"Then you'll open it yourself," Corvin said pleasantly, "or you'll explain to the Second Seal why the crown's audit waited until morning. Which would you rather write down?"

The watchman wrote nothing down. His hands shook so badly getting the annex key off its board that Ulyana almost felt sorry for him — almost, because his keys were also opening the door to the room where the crown kept lists of people like her, people to be found and dragged and burned, and pity was a luxury she'd stopped affording the crown's employees years ago.

But the key only got them to the iron gate. And the gate did not care about keys.

The truth-ward woke as they approached, a faint shimmer along the iron bars like heat off summer cobblestones. The watchman stopped three feet short, sweating. "It—it has to be carried through honestly, sir. If you're lying about your purpose, it'll know, and it'll ring the alarm-bell, and—"

"Thank you," said Corvin. "Wait by the doors."

He waited until the man's footsteps faded down the marble hall. Then he stood before the gate, and Ulyana saw him gather himself the way a diver gathers breath.

"Corvin," she whispered. "What are you going to do? Because if you're thinking of forcing it—"

"I'm not going to force it." His voice was very quiet. "I'm going to mean it."

"What?"

"The ward reads intent. So the intent has to be true." He looked at her then, and his eyes were terrible and steady. "When I walk through that gate, I have to believe, all the way down, that what I'm doing is honest work for the crown. And so do you."

"That's impossible. I'm stealing their ledger."

"Are you?" He stepped forward, and the shimmer brightened against his coat. "From where I stand, the crown's own law says a soul cannot be bought or sold. That law sits in this building. The men who broke it hid the proof of it behind this gate. So as far as I'm concerned—" his voice dropped, roughened, "—as far as I'm concerned, I am an officer of the crown, executing a lawful audit of unlawful records. I resigned my office, but I never stopped believing in what it was supposed to be. That's the one true thing left in me. Let it be enough."

He walked forward.

Ulyana held her breath. The shimmer touched him, crawled over him like searching fingers — and parted. He passed through the gate as if it were morning mist, and stood on the other side, breathing hard, looking half-triumphant and half-sick.

"Your turn," he said.

*I am a clerk's assistant,* she told herself, stepping up. *I am here on lawful audit business. I am exactly what I appear to be.*

The shimmer touched her and laughed. She felt it laugh — felt it slide through her like cold water finding every hidden thing, the stolen memories sewn into her coat lining, the gift coiled in her fingers, the forged warrant, the whole architecture of lies. It pressed against her skull and asked, gently, *and what are you, really?*

*Thief,* it answered itself. *Witch. Liar.*

The gate began to glow red.

"Ulyana." Corvin's voice, sharp, low. "Don't fight it. Don't push back. Give it a truth."

*A truth.* Her mind raced through everything she was, hunting for one clean honest thing, and found it — absurdly, horribly — standing right in front of her.

*I am here to take back what was stolen from me,* she thought, and meant it with her whole scarred heart. *Whatever else I am, tonight, that's true.*

The red faded. The shimmer hesitated — probing, doubtful — and then, grudgingly, like a customs official waving through a traveler with the wrong papers but the right face, it let her pass.

She stumbled through the gate, shaking. Corvin caught her elbow.

"What did you give it?" he murmured.

"Doesn't matter." She steadied herself. "Get me to the P-section."

* * *

The Sealed Annex at night was a cathedral built for paperwork. Shelves rose forty feet into darkness, iron catwalks stitching them together, and the air smelled of dust and lamp oil and something older underneath — something like the mountain, like deep stone keeping its own cold time. Their lantern threw a small trembling circle, and beyond it the shelves went on forever, holding every deed the crown had ever done and never wanted spoken aloud.

P. Pemberton. She found the drawer at the third hour, after two near-misses with the gallery watchman (once hiding beneath a reading table, once flattened against a shelf while his boots crossed the catwalk directly overhead, close enough that she could hear him humming a drinking song). The drawer stuck; Corvin eased it open with the flat of his knife.

Inside: ledgers. Dozens. She pulled the one marked with the double-S of the Sealed Court — *Sub Terra, Sub Sigillo* — and spread it on the reading table, and Corvin held the lantern close, and they read.

The first pages were dull. Grain shipments. Salt quotas. Payments to wardens, to masons, to a chandler who supplied four hundred candles a month to rooms that officially did not exist. Ulyana's finger moved down columns of figures, and then stopped moving.

"*Acquisition, live subject, male, age eleven,*" Corvin read, his voice gone strange. "*Delivered by crown escort, winter road. Consideration paid to seller per schedule B.*"

He turned the page. There was a name.

*Tomas Pemberton.*

The hall tilted. Ulyana put her hand flat on the table to keep the world level, and Corvin's lantern hand came up as if to steady her, and then thought better of it and withdrew.

"There's more," he said quietly. "Look at the schedule."

Schedule B was a price list. Not for grain. For souls — though the ledger, being a careful document, called them *vessels*, *acquisitions*, *live considerations*. Eleven souls purchased that year. Nine the year after. The buyers' column held only one name, repeated down the decades like a heartbeat: *The Court Beneath, per Warden Vess.* And the sellers' column — the sellers' column was the crown itself. Not kidnappers. Not brokers. The crown, selling its own subjects, wholesale, with receipts.

"They're not hiding it from the court," Ulyana heard herself say. "The court isn't stealing from them. They're partners. The witch-hunt—" She stopped. Started again. "Every gifted person the hunt drags in. Where do they go? The ones nobody sees burned?"

"Nowhere," Corvin said. "Or rather — down." He closed his eyes briefly. "I signed transfer orders. I thought I was sending them to the penitentiary at Harrowmere. I never asked why Harrowmere's wagons always traveled south, toward the mountain, and came back empty."

The lantern flame shivered. Somewhere far above, the gallery watchman hummed his drinking song, oblivious, and Ulyana stood in the cold heart of the Hall of Records and understood, finally and completely, what she had known since the boy at the mountain gate first surfaced in her sleep: that her brother had not been taken. He had been *sold*. By the crown. With paperwork. At a negotiated rate.

And somewhere in these same shelves, she realized with a fresh chill, were the seizure lists for every gifted soul in Vell. Names. Addresses. Children.

"We're leaving," she said. "Now. Before I burn this whole building down with everyone in it."

"You're not going to burn it down."

"No," she agreed. "Because burning it tells them what we found. But Corvin—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Eleven souls a year. For how many years?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The ledger's spine said the answer for him: *Volume Forty-One.*

They repacked the drawer. They took nothing — nothing except what Ulyana had already taken, days ago, from a different shelf entirely: the pages she'd palmed during their forged-warrant rehearsal, the ones listing the mountain court's delivery schedule. The next shipment south left in nineteen days. They would be on the mountain before it arrived, and Tomas's name would not be added to Volume Forty-Two.

At the gate, the truth-ward let them out without complaint. Perhaps it had decided they were honest after all. Or perhaps, Ulyana thought grimly, wards only ever asked whether you believed in what you were doing — and grief, properly aimed, counted as belief.

They crossed the dark reading hall. The watchman at the doors bowed them out, thanked the Inquisitor twice, and locked the bronze doors behind them with the profound relief of a man who would sleep better knowing the audit was someone else's problem.

Outside, the square was empty and silvered with frost. They made it half a block before Corvin stopped walking.

"You used it," he said.

Ulyana's blood went cold. "Used what?"

"At the gate. The ward pushed back against you and you — bent. Just slightly. Like heat coming off a road." He wasn't accusing. That was the worst part. He was stating a fact in the flat voice of a man who had spent ten years cataloguing facts about people like her, most of which ended in ash. "I've seen a thousand gifted people use their gift, Ulyana. I know what it looks like."

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the square, the fountain dripped. She could run. She could deny it. Three years ago she would have denied it, and been good at it.

Instead she said, "And you're not going to report me."

It wasn't a question, but he treated it like one. "No."

"Why?"

"Because I reported a woman once." His hand rose unconsciously to the hollow of his throat, where the sunburst disc hung. "She was selling charms at a market stall. Widow's comforts. Memories of dead husbands, bottled pretty. I wrote her name on a list, and six weeks later I stood in a square and watched them burn her, and she looked at me the whole time, and she never once screamed." He turned to face Ulyana fully, and the lamplight caught the lines around his eyes, and she saw that they were not age lines at all. They were the lines of a man who had looked at something too long. "Her name was Maren Kettle. She was fifty-one years old. And every night since, I have wondered what memories she was carrying, and whose they were, and whether anyone in this world is poorer now that hers are gone."

The frost crackled under Ulyana's boots. She thought of the widow Marta, and the bottled husband, and the coin in her palm that never quite felt like payment.

"She sounds like she deserved better," Ulyana said carefully.

"They all did. That's the whole point. That's—" He stopped. Laughed, once, without any humor in it. "That's what I told myself when I quit. And then I spent three years doing nothing with it. Nothing. Until a thief blackmailed me with my own resignation letter and dragged me halfway across my principles."

"So why come? Really?"

"Really?" He looked up at the sky, where the mountain was a darker shape against the dark. "Because I put a hundred names on those transfer orders, and I'd like to take at least one of them back." He started walking again, then paused. "Also, you make an excellent meat pie disappear, and I find that restful."

Despite everything — the ledger, the price list, the cold climbing her spine — Ulyana laughed. It startled her, the sound, like a bird flushing from cover. She couldn't remember the last time.

They walked south through the sleeping city, two shadows among shadows, and neither of them mentioned that something had shifted between them in the Hall of Records. Neither named it. Trust was like that, she suspected — it didn't announce itself. It just quietly picked up your bags and started walking beside you.

They were nearly at the safehouse when Corvin stopped so abruptly she bumped his shoulder.

Ahead, pinned to the safehouse door, a notice gleamed wetly in the lamplight — fresh paste, fresh ink, the crown's seal still shining:

*BY ORDER OF THE SECOND SEAL: REWARD OF FIVE HUNDRED MARKS FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO THE APPREHENSION OF THE THIEF WHO ENTERED THE HALL OF RECORDS THIS NIGHT. THE GATES OF VELL WILL BE SEALED AT DAWN UNTIL THE AUDITOR IS FOUND.*

Below it, someone had already chalked a single word on the doorframe, in a hand Ulyana knew as well as her own heartbeat, a hand she'd last seen signing her exile papers three years ago:

*Sable knows.*
