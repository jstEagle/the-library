## 7. Sable's Bargain

The guildhouse had not changed. That was the cruelest thing about it.

Three years, and the door in the tannery wall still opened at the same knock — two quick, one slow, the rhythm Sable himself had taught her when she was fourteen and hungry enough to learn anything. The same smell of wet stone and lamp oil. The same boy lounging against the same table, whittling the same kind of useless wooden bird, and when he saw her he dropped it, and his face did something complicated before settling into nothing.

"Ulyana Pemberton," said Wren Halloway, who had once been her closest friend in the world and was now just a name she said to herself sometimes, late, like a prayer she'd stopped believing in.

"Wren."

"You're supposed to be dead." He said it without accusation, almost wistful, as if death were a country he'd considered emigrating to.

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things." She pulled back her hood. The lamplight found the scar at her temple, the one she'd earned on her last night here, and Wren looked away from it the way you look away from a wound you caused.

Behind him, down the long corridor lined with the guild's trophies — locks picked, safes cracked, a framed set of crown seal-stamps taken off dead magistrates — voices murmured. She heard the word *hunt* twice, the way you hear your own name in a crowd: instantly, involuntarily, with a cold drop in the stomach.

"Sable in?"

Wren hesitated. That hesitation told her everything about how the last three years had gone. "He'll see you. He always said he would." A bitter little laugh. "He talks about you, Uly. Did you know that? Every new thief gets the story. The girl who could take a memory out of a sleeping man's head so gently he dreamed better after. The girl who got greedy."

"I didn't get greedy."

"No," Wren agreed quietly. "You got honest. Worse crime."

He led her down the corridor, past the trophy cases, past the door with the iron bolt, and knocked once, which was itself a message — one knock meant trouble walking in on its own feet, uninvited and known.

* * *

Sable had grown old the way rivers cut canyons: slowly, and all in one direction. His hair had gone from black to ash-grey, his hands were knotted where they used to be quick, but his eyes were exactly the same — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who counted everything, including you, and always kept the tally.

He sat behind his desk with his fingers laced over his belly, and he looked at her for a long time before he spoke.

"Ulyana."

"Sable."

"The prodigal thief." He gestured to the chair across from him. It was the chair she'd sat in at fifteen, taking her oath: *the guild is my hand, my hand is the guild.* "Sit. You look tired."

"I look hunted. There's a difference."

"There is." He nodded slowly. "And I imagine that difference is why you've come crawling back to the people who put a knife-scar on your head and threw you into the street."

She sat. She made herself sit the way she'd learned to sit across from wealthy clients — spine straight, hands still, grief folded up small and tucked somewhere out of sight. "I need passage out of Vell. East road, quiet cart, no questions. I know the guild still runs the night freight."

"The guild runs many things."

"And I'm asking to buy one of them."

"With what?" He spread his hands, and she saw the gold rings had multiplied — three on each hand now, heavy, vulgar, the rings of a man who'd stopped being a thief and become an owner. "You have no coin worth mentioning. No favors banked. Your debt wasn't cleared when you left, girl. It *accrued*."

"My debt was paid when you took everything I had and marked my face."

"That was business."

"That was *betrayal*, and we both know why you did it." She leaned forward. "Because the crown came asking about a memory-thief working the Hill district, and instead of hiding me you sold them a story — that I'd gone rogue, that the guild disavowed me — and bought yourself three more years of comfortable blindness while they dragged every other gifted child in the Warrens out of their beds."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes. Not guilt. Guilt required a conscience, and whatever organ beat in Sable's chest had been replaced decades ago by an abacus. It was calculation, recalculating.

"You always did think in stories," he said. "Martyrs and monsters. Me, I think in ledgers." He opened a drawer — slowly, deliberately, letting her watch his hands the whole time, because Sable never let you forget he was armed even when he wasn't — and took out a slip of paper, sliding it across the desk. "Passage east. One cart, one driver who doesn't ask questions. Cost: the ledger in the Hall of Records. The one the crown seized from us in the spring raids. You bring it back by tomorrow midnight, and you ride out clean."

She stared at him. "That ledger has four hundred names in it."

"It does."

"Every thief, every fence, every safehouse in Vell."

"It does," Sable said again, pleasantly. "Which is why I want it back. And which is why the crown wants it *not* back. Consider the difficulty reflected in the price."

"And if I say no?"

"Then no cart. No road. And the hunters find you by week's end, because—" he smiled, and it was the smile from her oath-day, warm as a hearth and exactly as controlled— "you're staying under my roof tonight, Ulyana. Guild hospitality. Sleep well."

* * *

They gave her the little room above the kitchen, the one that had been hers at sixteen, with the same crack in the ceiling shaped like a river delta and the same loose floorboard third from the window. Some habits of a house die hard; some are kept alive on purpose.

She lay awake under the wool blanket that smelled of tallow and old smoke and did not sleep, because she had spent three years learning what betrayal smelled like, and this house reeked of it.

It was past the deepest hour when she heard the boots. Two pairs, trying to be quiet on stairs that sang to anyone who'd lived on them. One pair heavy, one light. And beneath the boot-heel song, fainter, a third sound: the dry whisper of oilcloth being unwound from steel.

Ulyana was moving before her mind finished the arithmetic. Out through the window, onto the kitchen roof, down the drainpipe she'd climbed a hundred times at fourteen — except her body was three years older now and her hands shook, and halfway down her grip slipped and she fell the last ten feet into the frozen mud of the tannery yard, wrenching her ankle, biting her own wrist to keep silent.

Above her, the window banged open. Lamplight spilled out. Wren's face, white in the glow, and behind it another man's — a stranger, big, with the cropped hair of a crown man.

"She's going for the wall," the stranger said.

Wren said nothing. Wren only looked down at her, and in the lamplight she watched him do the arithmetic Sable had done at his desk, and come out with a different number. He turned away from the window. Deliberately. Slowly. Giving her the seconds.

She ran.

* * *

The Warrens at night, hunted, was a lesson in how much of yourself you'd already lost. Every alley she'd owned like a coin purse was suddenly foreign territory; every shadow might be a hunter's cloak. Her ankle sang with every step. Behind her, whistles — the crown's three-note hunting call, answered from two streets over, then from ahead. They were folding the net closed, and they knew these streets better than she remembered knowing them.

She cut through the chandlery, over the drying-yard fences, and came out into Tanner's Row — and there was Corvin Ashe, leaning against a wall in the dark like a man waiting for a coach that would never come, sword bare and low, and his face when he saw her was not surprised.

"How—" she gasped.

"Wren sent a boy. Paid him with a wooden bird, apparently." Corvin pushed off the wall. "Your old friend has a sense of humor."

"He has a sense of *survival*. It's not the same thing." She grabbed his sleeve. "The gates, Corvin. If Sable sold me, the gates are already sealed. Names given to the harbor watch, the east post—"

"Both. Which is why we're not using either." He started running, and she ran with him, ankle screaming, through streets that grew wider and cleaner as they left the Warrens behind. "There's a corpse-gate under the Almsfield. Dead-cart gate. It opens twice a night for the plague carts, and the men who open it are paid in coin that smells of the guild, which means Sable bought their silence years ago — which means tonight, of all nights, it's the one gate in Vell that isn't watching for you."

"Because he thinks buying the gate means owning my escape either way."

"Because Sable," Corvin said, "thinks three moves deep and no further. It's his tragedy. Come on."

* * *

The Almsfield at night was a field of shallow graves and deeper silence, and the corpse-gate was an iron mouth in the city wall, low and narrow, breathing out the smell of lime and cold earth. Two men stood beside it with a lantern turned down to a coal. When Corvin stepped into the light with his former-colleague's stride and his inquisitor's papers held up like a talisman, they actually bowed.

The cart was already there — the plague cart, its boards stained dark with history, its driver muffled to the eyes.

Corvin checked the gate bolts himself, then turned to her. "East road, three miles to the ferry at Marsh Crossing. Then north, then up. You know where."

"The mountain." She looked back once, at the city of Vell lying black and vast under the stars, its thousand chimneys breathing smoke, its proclamation boards papered with her face by morning. Somewhere behind those walls was a room above a kitchen with a crack in the ceiling like a river delta, and a man counting rings on his fingers, and a boy whittling birds who had turned away from a window when it mattered. "Corvin. Why did Sable sell me? Really. He could have just refused me passage."

Corvin was quiet a moment. Then he reached into his coat and handed her a paper — a hunter's circular, fresh ink, a description of her, a reward figure that made her breath catch.

Underneath the description, in the neat hand of the crown's bounty office, a single line:

*Subject believed traveling toward the mountain court. Prior association with subject T. Pemberton, soul-brokered to the Undercourt eight years past, payment received and receipted.*

She read it twice. The world went very quiet, the way it does right before something breaks.

"He knew," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Eight years. He's known where Tomas went, what they — he *took a cut*."

"A broker's fee," Corvin said gently. "Ten percent, standard rate. Your brother's soul has been paying Sable's rent for eight years, Ulyana. And tonight he sold you for the same reason he sold Tomas — because keeping you alive cost more than selling you was worth."

She folded the paper. Folded it again. Put it inside her coat, over her heart, where she kept the things she intended to turn into weapons.

The driver clicked his tongue. The corpse-gate groaned open, iron teeth parting on the darkness beyond, and Ulyana climbed up onto the plague cart among the shrouded shapes and the lime-dust, and did not look back again.

Behind her, with a sound like a held breath finally released, the gates of Vell began to close — all of them, every post and portcullis, sealing the city shut against the morning and the hunt and everything she had been.

She rode out through the corpse-gate into the dark with her brother's name in her mouth and a traitor's arithmetic in her fist, and the road bent north, toward the mountain, toward the fire that burned on borrowed souls — and somewhere far above the tree line, faint as a dying heartbeat, she thought she saw it flicker.
