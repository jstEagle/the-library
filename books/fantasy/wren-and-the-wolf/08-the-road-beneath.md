## 8. The Road Beneath

The mountain road did not begin so much as it gave up on being a road.

Corvin Ashe had walked the first mile of it in silence, boots crunching over gravel that the crown had once paid masons to lay flat and had since forgotten. Behind him, Ulyana Pemberton picked her way down from the treeline with the particular care of someone who had spent three days learning not to trust the ground under her feet — or the man walking ahead of it.

They had left Vell before the gates closed behind them, and the sound of those gates shutting — iron on iron, a bell tolling twice for lockdown — still lived somewhere in his back teeth. He kept chewing on it. The city was sealed now. Every gate watched. Every name that mattered written in a ledger they no longer possessed, which meant every name that mattered would be guessed at instead, and guessed-at names made sloppy hunters, and sloppy hunters burned houses with people still inside them. He knew because he had been the sloppy hunter once.

He stopped where the road bent around a granite shoulder and let the wind off the peaks hit him full in the face. It smelled like snowmelt and pine resin and something older underneath, something mineral and faintly warm, as if the mountain itself had a hearth lit somewhere deep inside.

"You keep stopping," Ulyana said, coming up beside him. Her voice was flat with fatigue. Three days of hard walking will do that to anyone; three days of walking after watching your only ally nearly die holding a door shut against guild knives would do worse. "Either you know where you're going or you don't."

"I know where I'm going." He pointed with his chin, up and east, where the tree line broke against grey scree and above the scree hung a smear of woodsmoke too thin and too high to be a village. "Shrine's another day. Maybe two if the pass is iced."

"And if it's iced?"

"Then we walk carefully."

She looked at him the way she'd been looking at him since the Hall of Records — like she was still deciding what he was worth, and the arithmetic wasn't coming out in his favor. Fair enough. He'd done the same arithmetic about himself plenty of times, and it never came out clean either.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of an old waystation, one of the crown's relay posts abandoned when the mountain court decided it preferred its privacy. The roof held over half the room; Corvin built the fire under that half, small and shielded, out of habit. A man who had spent eleven years finding other people's hidden fires learned to build discreet ones himself.

Ulyana sat across the flames turning a strip of dried venison over and over without eating it. She'd barely spoken since noon. He knew the shape of that silence. It was the silence of someone carrying a question they hadn't figured out how to ask yet.

"Ask it," he said.

She didn't pretend not to understand. That was one thing he'd learned about her: she didn't waste effort pretending. "In the Records hall," she said. "You saw what I did. To the lock."

"Yes."

"The crown calls that witchcraft. You were the crown for eleven years." She set the venison down on the flat stone beside her. "Why aren't you dragging me back in chains?"

Because I'm tired. Because chains are all I ever produced. Because the last person I chained is ash outside a village you've never heard of. He said none of that. What he said was: "Because the hunt isn't about magic. You've seen that by now. If it were about magic, they'd have come for me years ago."

Her eyes narrowed. "What's that supposed to mean?"

He fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. There were ways to answer this question and none of them were painless, so he supposed the choice was just a matter of picking his poison. Directness, at least, had the virtue of being quick.

"It means," he said, "that I have the gift too. Whatever you want to call it. The crown's word, my old commander's word, the word painted on the doors of everyone we dragged out at dawn — that word applies to me."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, a bird complained once and went quiet.

Ulyana stared at him for a long moment. "You're lying."

"I wish I were."

"No. No, you're lying, because—" She stopped herself. He watched her rebuild the sentence brick by brick, the way she must have rebuilt a hundred lies in a hundred market stalls. "Because the inquisitors test each other. Everyone knows that. They're tested twice a year, blood-rite and candle-rite, before witnesses—"

"Before witnesses who want their careers," Corvin said. "I passed both rites for eleven years. Do you know why?" He held up his hand, palm toward the fire, and let a little warmth gather there — nothing showy, nothing that would light the dark, just enough heat to make the skin glow faintly amber, the way coals do through a grate. Then he pulled it back, swallowed it down, smothered it. "Because I got very good at hiding it. Better than you, probably. You steal memories in a cellar; I hid mine in plain sight, in uniform, while using it to find people exactly like us."

The silence that followed had weight. He let it sit.

"That's how you found them," she said finally. Not a question. "The gifted. You could feel—"

"Their fires. Everyone's gift burns a little different. Yours tastes like cold iron and river water, if you're wondering. I felt it the first time you shook my hand." He almost smiled. It didn't take. "A memory-thief feels like a draft under a door. Like something being carried out of a house that should stay put. Eleven years, and I never once failed to identify a gifted subject. That was my reputation. The hound of Vell. They gave me a medal."

"And the ones you found." Her voice had gone very quiet. "What happened to them?"

"You know what happened to them. You saw your neighbor dragged away. You saw the proclamation boards." He turned the twig over in the coals until it blackened. "Pyre, mostly. Some were transported to the court beneath the mountain, if they had useful gifts. Useful meaning harvestable."

"Harvestable," she repeated, and something in her face shifted — he couldn't read it fully, but he recognized grief rearranging itself into purpose. She'd been doing that all week.

"There was one," he said.

He hadn't planned to say it. It simply came up, the way things do when a man has been chewing on them for six years and finally finds the road long enough and the dark forgiving enough.

Ulyana went still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"A girl named Maren Ostrow," Corvin said. "Nineteen. Lived in Lowbridge, worked a laundry, and on the side she sold charms at the fish market. Little glass beads with a memory folded inside. First kiss. Last summer. A mother's voice singing. Nothing stolen — she only ever sold her own memories, which is more than most of us can say." His throat tightened around the next part, but he'd promised himself honesty on this road, or something near enough to honesty to serve. "I found her stall on a Tuesday. Felt her gift across the whole square, bright as a struck match. I reported her Wednesday. She was tried Friday."

"And burned Saturday."

"Burned Saturday morning," he said. "I attended. We always attended. Standing honor guard in white cloaks while—" He stopped. Restarted. "She didn't scream. That's the detail I can't get rid of. They always screamed, the innocent ones, because screaming was the only testimony left to them. Maren just stood there and looked at me. Through me. And right at the end, when the kindling caught, I felt her gift go out. Not extinguished — *released*. Everything she'd ever folded into glass, everything she'd saved up, all of it leaving her at once, and for half a second the whole square could smell bread baking and hear someone laughing, some joke from her childhood, and then it was gone and she was gone and the crowd applauded the purity of the flame."

Ulyana's hand had come up over her mouth at some point. Above it, her eyes were wet and furious at once.

"She was you," Corvin said. "Do you understand? She was you, exactly you, down to the fish-market charms and the careful little life. And I lit the match myself, because the commander handed it to me and that's what honor guards do, and I did it, and I told myself for six years that the law was the law and mercy was weakness, until one morning I woke up and couldn't remember a single reason the law deserved me and every reason I didn't deserve it." He looked into the fire. "So I resigned. Which is a coward's version of justice. I know that. Don't bother telling me."

* * *

But she bothered telling him anyway.

"Justice," Ulyana said, later, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and the cold had crept in past the ruined walls. She sat with her knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, and her voice had lost the shock and gained an edge. "You want to talk justice? Let's talk. You burned a girl for selling her own memories, and then you resigned, and then you came with me. Is that the sum of your penance? One dead girl, one resignation, one road?"

"It's not penance," Corvin said. "Penance would be easy. Penance would be mine to pay and done."

"Then what is it?"

"Honesty? I don't know. Maybe I'm trying to find out whether the law I served was ever anything but a machine for making men like me feel righteous while we did monstrous things. Maybe I'm trying to find out if there's a version of justice that doesn't run on pyres." He pulled his cloak tighter. "Maybe I'm just too cowardly to hang myself and too honest to go home."

"Justice outside the law," she said slowly, tasting the phrase. "That's a thief's doctrine, inquisitor. Every cutpurse in Vell believes it. The law is for other people; my need is the true law. I stole for years on exactly that logic."

"And was it wrong?"

"Some of it." She said it without flinching, which he respected. "Stealing from the fat merchants who bottled their own cruelty and sold it as vintage — no. Stealing from the widow who needed to believe her husband died thinking of her — yes, maybe. I don't know. I've never known. That's the truth nobody in Vell wants said out loud: most of us are guessing. The law guesses with a sword. I guess with a pickpocket's hand. You guessed with a torch."

"And Maren?"

"Maren didn't guess anything," Ulyana said. "Maren was certain. Certain and good, from the sound of it. That's what makes it unforgivable — not that you were confused, but that you weren't. The law was clear, and you obeyed it, and the clarity itself was the crime." She unfolded slightly, looking at him across the embers. "So here's my question, Corvin Ashe, and think carefully, because I've trusted exactly no one for three years and I'd like a reason to trust you: when it comes time — and it will come time, up there, in that court — when it comes time to choose between the rule and the person, which will you choose?"

He thought about the warden they'd learn to fear soon enough. He thought about the ledger, and the names in it, and the machinery of the hunt grinding on behind sealed gates. He thought about a girl who smelled like bread and laughter for half a second before the smoke took her.

"I don't know," he said.

Honesty again. It cost him something to give it, and he watched her weigh the cost, and decide — not that it was enough, but that it was at least real.

"Good," Ulyana said. "If you'd answered fast, I'd have walked out of this camp tonight."

"That's a terrible standard for trust."

"It's the only one I have left." She lay back against her pack and pulled her hood down over her eyes. "Get some sleep, inquisitor. Tomorrow we climb."

* * *

He did sleep, eventually, badly, and dreamed of white cloaks and glass beads scattered on cobblestones, each one holding a tiny moving picture of a life he'd helped end. He woke before dawn to find Ulyana already up, standing at the broken wall, looking east.

He joined her. The sky was going grey-gold behind the peaks, and there — high on the mountainside, where yesterday there had been only smoke — a light was burning. Not a beacon. Not any signal he knew. It flickered with a rhythm too slow and too vast to be human, rising and falling like breath, like the pulse of some enormous sleeping animal.

Ulyana's hand found his sleeve without her seeming to notice she'd done it.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Corvin had seen the shrine-light described in confiscated texts, in testimonies taken under seal, in the feverish margins of books the crown had burned along with their authors. He'd never believed the descriptions. He believed them now.

"That," he said quietly, "is the dragon-fire. Or its reflection. Ysolde keeps the vigil on the mountain's flank, and the fire answers her." He watched the great slow pulse swell and dim, swell and dim, and thought of everything the court beneath had built on top of that heartbeat — the vaults, the ledgers, the bottles, the lies. "It's weaker than it should be."

"Weaker how?"

He hesitated. On the road, he'd promised himself honesty; here was the first bill come due. "The texts say a healthy fire burns steady. This one stutters. Weeks, maybe. Perhaps less."

Beside him, Ulyana Pemberton went very still, and Corvin Ashe understood — with the same terrible clarity he'd once used to find the guilty and had lately begun using to find the truth — that whatever brother she was climbing this mountain to save, the clock above his head had just gotten shorter.

She started walking before the sun cleared the ridge.
