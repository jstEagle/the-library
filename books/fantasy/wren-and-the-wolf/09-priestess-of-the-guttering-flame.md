## 9. Priestess of the Guttering Flame

The shrine announced itself by silence.

They had climbed for two days through pine and scree, the road beneath them narrowing until it was less a road than a suggestion the mountain tolerated. Corvin had said little since the night he told her about the girl he burned — the memory-thief with no name in any record but his own — and Ulyana had let him keep that silence, because she recognized its weight. It was the same weight she carried when she thought of Tomas. Some things you could not set down without dropping them on someone else.

Now the pines thinned, and the wind dropped all at once, like a door closing behind them, and Ulyana understood they had crossed into something's keeping.

The shrine stood in a hollow where three slopes met: low-walled, round, roofed in slate gone green with lichen. No bell tower. No gate. Just a ring of standing stones around it, each one scorched black at heart-height, as if a fire lived inside every one of them and had been pressing outward for centuries. The stone was warm when Ulyana laid her palm against the nearest. Warm as skin.

"Ysolde," Corvin said quietly, to himself, to the mountain. "I'd heard she was dead."

"People hear what's convenient," said a voice from the doorway.

She was small. That was the first thing — smaller than Ulyana expected, smaller than the stories made room for. A woman somewhere past sixty, wrapped in a priestess's grey robes gone brown at the hem, hair the color of ash braided tight against her skull. Her eyes were the surprise. They were amber, but not evenly so; light moved in them the way light moves in coals, settling and shifting, and when she blinked, Ulyana could have sworn the afterimage lingered a half-second too long.

"You're the witch-hunter," Ysolde said, looking at Corvin with neither fear nor welcome, the way one looks at weather. "And you're the thief." Her gaze settled on Ulyana and did something strange — narrowed, softened, sharpened all at once, like a hand testing an edge. "You carry more than you know you carry."

"I get that a lot," Ulyana said.

"You don't." Ysolde stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before dark. The mountain listens better after dark, and I'd rather it didn't hear you yet."

* * *

Inside, the shrine was one round room and smelled of resin, cold iron, and something older underneath — a mineral smell, like the air in deep cellars, like struck flint. A fire burned in a central hearth, but it burned wrong. The flames were white at their cores, pale as milk-glass, and they gave almost no heat. Ulyana felt it as a pressure rather than a warmth, a hum behind her teeth.

Around the hearth stood seven iron braziers, and in each one lay a single object: a river stone, a bird's skull, a knot of red thread, a child's wooden horse, a wedding ring fused to a finger-bone, a scrap of cloth embroidered with a name worn nearly away, and — seventh, nearest the fire — a small glass bottle, empty, stoppered with wax.

Ulyana knew bottles. She knew that shape the way a fisherman knows hooks.

"What is this place?" Corvin asked.

"A listening post." Ysolde knelt by the hearth and fed it something from a pouch at her belt — not wood; it looked like dried petals, or scales. The white flames leaned toward her hand. "There were forty-one shrines once, ringing the court beneath the mountain like a ring of candles around a bed. Priests and priestesses in each, tending the fire from afar, keeping the wards sung. I am what's left of forty-one." She said it the way people report a harvest total. "This one still hears the mountain. The others have gone deaf, one by one, as the fire dims."

"Dims," Ulyana repeated.

Ysolde looked up at her, and the coal-light in her eyes turned, briefly, pitiless.

"Sit down, thief. You've come a long way to learn how your brother burns."

* * *

Ulyana did not sit. She stood very still while the words went through her — *your brother burns* — and found, to her own horror, that some part of her had already known. Had known since the dream of the boy at the mountain gate. Since the charm she couldn't explain. Knowing had been arriving in her by degrees, the way dawn arrives: not suddenly, never suddenly, but absolutely.

"Tomas Pemberton," Ysolde said. "Sold to the crown at eleven years old. Entered the mountain through the Salt Gate on the third day of Frostwane, fourteen years ago. He was weighed, catalogued, and given to the fire." She recited it like scripture because, Ulyana realized, it was scripture — the liturgy of a thing Ysolde had watched happen ten thousand times and been powerless to stop. "His soul has fed the dragon-fire ever since. It is why the wards of this valley hold. It is why the mountain does not wake."

"The mountain," Corvin said slowly. "Wake."

Ysolde rose, joints cracking, and beckoned them to the hearth. "Sit. Both of you. This is a long story and it ends worse than it begins, which is the only kind worth telling."

Ulyana sat. Corvin sat beside her, close enough that she felt the tension running through him like a wire.

"The dragon under Vell is not asleep," Ysolde began. "It is *contained*. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. When the first kings built their city above it, they could not kill it — nothing kills such a creature — so they bound it instead, and the binding required fuel. Not wood. Not coal." She nodded at the hearth. "A soul burns longer than any timber. A soul burns clean. And a soul that is given willingly—" her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in the liturgy— "a soul given freely burns brightest of all. But the court learned long ago that willing souls are rare, and unwilling ones burn almost as well, if you take them young enough that hope hasn't hardened."

"The wards," Corvin said. "The ones over Vell. The dampening."

"Now you begin to see the shape of it." Ysolde's mouth twisted. "The same binding that cages the dragon smothers magic across the whole valley. Every gift in Vell, every stolen memory, every charm sold in a market stall — all of it leaks through cracks in the ward, thin as light under a door. The crown calls magic outlawed. The truth is simpler and crueller: the crown *built* a machine that makes magic scarce, then criminalized the scarcity's victims. The witch-hunt isn't piety, hunter. It's inventory control."

Corvin said nothing. His hands were flat on his knees, and Ulyana saw his knuckles whiten.

"And the fire?" Ulyana asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You said it's dying."

Ysolde reached out and held her palm above the white flames. The fire leaned up toward her like a plant toward sun, and for a moment Ulyana saw — or dreamed she saw — shapes in it. Faces. Hundreds of faces, flickering, dissolving, reforming.

"Fourteen years ago, the fire burned high enough that we priestesses could hear it singing through the stones. Ten years ago, it dimmed enough that half our shrines went deaf. Five years ago, the court stopped taking souls in ones and twos and started taking them in dozens — whole debtors' prisons emptied into the vaults, entire families who owed the wrong taxes." The coal-eyes closed. "And still it gutters. Souls are not infinite, and the court has grown greedy — they bottle far more than they burn, hoarding the rest against some future only the warden believes in. A fire fed irregularly burns irregularly. The wards are fraying. In the capital they blame smugglers for the magic seeping into the streets. There are no smugglers, thief. There is only a dying flame, and the seams of the world coming apart around it."

"How long," Ulyana said. Not a question. A demand.

Ysolde opened her eyes.

"Weeks," she said. "Six, perhaps eight, if the winter is kind. When the fire dies, the binding dies with it. The wards over Vell will fall in an afternoon — every gift will wake at once, every bottled soul in the vaults will wake with it, and the mountain—" She paused, and Ulyana watched a woman who had spent decades delivering terrible truths fail, for the first time, to deliver one. "The mountain will wake. And it will be *hungry*, and it will be under a city of two hundred thousand people who were taught that magic was a crime and dragons were a fable."

The silence afterward had texture. Ulyana sat inside it and felt it press against her like water.

* * *

Later — she couldn't have said how much later; time moved strangely in the shrine — Ysolde put barley soup in front of them, thick with wild garlic, and Ulyana ate because grief and fear both demand fuel and she had learned that long ago.

Corvin stood at the ring of braziers, studying the objects in them. "These are offerings?"

"They are *anchors*," Ysolde corrected. "Each shrine holds relics of souls already given to the fire. Through them, I listen. Sometimes—" she hesitated— "sometimes, if the soul was strong and the fire is quiet, I can hear them. Fragments. A name. A song. Once, a joke — a terrible joke, told by a cooper's son from Harrow Fen, about a barrel with ambitions. I laughed aloud alone in this room, and then I wept until morning." She looked at Ulyana. "Your brother's relic is in the sixth brazier."

Ulyana was on her feet before she decided to move.

The sixth brazier. The scrap of cloth, embroidered with a name worn nearly away. She lifted it out with both hands, careful as lifting a bird, and turned it toward the hearth-light. The stitching was crude, childish. She could read most of it. *T-o-m-a-s*. Below it, half unraveled, a second word she couldn't make out.

"He sewed it himself," Ulyana whispered. "He was always sewing things. Patching my sleeves when I wasn't looking. He'd hide the needle if I caught him at it." Her thumb traced the letters. "Our mother taught him before she—" She stopped. "Where did you get this?"

"It came with him to the Salt Gate. The court allows each soul one token. They believe it improves the burning." Ysolde's voice was flat with contempt. "Superstition dressed as procedure. But the tokens pass through the shrines for cataloguing, and I steal what I can. This one I kept fourteen years."

Ulyana held the scrap and felt nothing rise from it — no vision, no whisper, none of the gift's usual pull. Just cloth. Just thread. Just the fact of a boy who patched his sister's sleeves and was taken through a gate and burned.

Except.

Except there *was* something. Faint as heat off stone at dusk. A rhythm, almost. Like breathing heard through a wall.

"I can feel him," she said. "Barely."

"Then the fire hasn't taken all of him yet." Ysolde crossed the room and stood beside her, and the coal-light of her eyes reflected in the embroidery. "Souls don't burn all at once, thief. They burn the way candles do — top down, slow, the newest memories first. What goes into the flame last is what was laid down deepest. First loves. First fears. The people we loved before we understood love was a thing that could be lost." She touched the cloth, lightly, over Ulyana's fingers. "If your brother still burns at all, it is his oldest self that remains. Whatever he remembers of you now would be a child's remembering. A girl who needed patching."

Ulyana hadn't cried in the shrine. She wouldn't start here. She folded the cloth along its old creases and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where the unexplainable charm hung on its cord, and felt the two of them — cloth and glass, past and present — settle against each other like hands finding each other in the dark.

"Then I'll go down and take him out," she said. "Before the weeks run out. Before anything wakes."

"You speak of the vaults of the court of salt and smoke as if they were a pantry," Ysolde said.

"I've broken into places with worse locks."

"You haven't." Ysolde's tone did not change, which somehow made it worse. "But that is not even the true obstacle. Listen to me now, thief, because this is the part that will cost you sleep." She took Ulyana's wrist — her grip was stronger than her size suggested — and drew her toward the hearth, toward the white fire, close enough that the pressure of it pushed against Ulyana's chest like a hand.

"Look," Ysolde said. "Really look."

Ulyana looked. And the gift she had spent her whole life controlling — the gift that opened other people's memories to her like doors — opened *involuntarily*, for the first time since childhood, and showed her the fire.

She saw the mountain from below. She saw the great chamber where the dragon lay coiled in chains of salt and iron, each link tall as a church, and above it the fire — not a hearth-fire but a sea of it, white and patient, pouring down channels cut into the rock like veins. And in the fire, souls. Not consumed, exactly. *Spent*. Burning down the way a candle burns, from the top, from the newest, and what remained sank deeper and deeper into the oldest self, and around each sinking soul the fire drew strength the way roots draw water.

And she saw — this was the part she would carry afterward like a splinter — she saw that the fire did not distinguish between souls freely surrendered and souls taken screaming. Fire was fire. But the *binding* knew. The binding sang sweeter, held tighter, when what fed it had been given.

She pulled back gasping. Corvin caught her elbow. She shook him off, mostly.

"So I find a way to give myself," she said. "One soul for one soul. Trade me for him."

"No," Corvin said instantly.

"You don't get a vote."

"*No.*"

"Neither of you understands," Ysolde said, and the weariness in her voice stopped them both. "The vaults hold hundreds of bottled souls. The warden holds the keys. You cannot trade yourself in — the court does not want your soul, thief, they want *supply*. They want the ledger of names. They want every gifted citizen of Vell catalogued and collected, generation upon generation, forever. One girl's devotion means nothing to men who think in centuries." She turned back to the fire, and the white light made her look, for a moment, like something carved rather than born. "And there is worse. If the fire dies before it is refed — truly dies, gutters to ash — the souls within it die too. There is no remainder. No fragment. Your brother would not be freed. He would simply end."

The word *end* fell into the room and did not echo. Shrines eat echoes.

Ulyana stood with the cloth over her heart and the charm on its cord and the taste of ash in her mouth, and she thought of the widow Marla, who had bought a bottle of her husband's laugh and slept with it under her pillow, and she thought: *this is what I have been selling all along. This is what everyone in Vell has been drinking like wine. Burnt souls, thinned to flavor.*

"Weeks," she said aloud. "You said weeks."

"Six. Perhaps eight."

"Then we go down now. Tonight."

"Tonight," Corvin echoed, and when Ulyana glanced at him she saw that whatever argument had been building in him had burned off, leaving something harder and colder and more useful. "The Salt Gate. How do we get in?"

Ysolde smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "That," she said, "is the question I have waited fourteen years for someone brave enough, or foolish enough, to ask."

She crossed to the seventh brazier — the empty bottle — and lifted it down.

"But first, thief, tell me what you're carrying around your neck. Because the fire has been staring at it since you walked in, and fires stare at very few things."

Ulyana's hand rose to the cord before she could stop it. The little bottle, the charm she couldn't explain, the one she'd saved from her own bonfire. The one holding a memory that was not hers: a boy at a mountain gate, turning back to wave.

Ysolde uncorked the empty vessel, held it up to the white light, and read whatever priests read in glass.

"Oh," the last dragon-priestess said softly. "*Oh.* You've been carrying a piece of him all along."
