## 10. The Court of Salt and Smoke

The mountain did not want them.

Ulyana felt it in her teeth from the first switchback above Ysolde's shrine — a low, grinding resistance, like walking against a river's current. The wards Ysolde had described were older than the crown, older than Vell itself, and they tasted every soul that climbed the pilgrim road looking for something the mountain kept below.

But they did not stop her.

That was the first obscenity of it. She had expected to be turned away, scorched, unmade — anything but *welcomed*. Yet when she reached the gate cut into the mountainside, all iron teeth and salt-crusted stone, the two wardens in grey livery looked at the seal on her traveling case, at the broker's marks stitched into her coat collar, and stepped aside with the bored courtesy of men admitting a tradesman.

"Soul-broker Pemberton," the taller one read from a ledger he did not offer her to see. "Expected. Third bell, the Exchange."

"Then I'll keep my appointment," Ulyana said, and was proud that her voice came out flat and bored, the voice of a woman who had sold grief by the dram for six years.

Inside, the gate swallowed the daylight whole.

---

Corvin had wanted to come in with her. She had refused him twice, once gently and once not.

"You're known," she'd said, the night before, in the shrine's smoke-stained antechamber where Ysolde kept her vigil over a fire she could only feel, never see. "A disgraced inquisitor walks into the court beneath the mountain, and half the wardens will recognize your face before you clear the vestibule. They gave you medals, Ashe. Medals get remembered."

"And what do they remember of you?"

"Nothing," she'd said. "That's the entire point of me."

It wasn't entirely true. The court had bought Tomas Pemberton's soul through channels — through brokers, through the crown's own clerks — and somewhere in those ledgers there might be a line item with her family name on it. But names on paper were not faces at gates, and Ulyana Pemberton had spent years learning how to be no one in particular. She'd stolen the broker's seals off a dead woman's estate in Vell three months ago and had been waiting, without knowing why, for a use for them. Now she knew. There was a horrible satisfaction in it. Every sin she'd committed in her old life had apparently been rehearsal.

So Corvin stayed on the mountain with Ysolde, nursing his ribs and his guilt in equal measure, and Ulyana walked down alone.

---

The descent took hours, and it took things from her as she went.

Not memories — she checked constantly, turning inward the way she did before sleep, confirming the inventory: the smell of the widow Marta's kitchen, the sound of Tomas laughing at nine years old, the weight of her first stolen charm still warm in her pocket. All present. All hers. What the mountain took instead was color and warmth and the sense that the world above continued to exist. The stair wound down through salt-veined rock, past niches where votive candles burned without flickering, and the air grew thick enough to chew. By the time the stair opened out, her lungs had learned a new rhythm and her eyes had learned to accept light that came from everywhere and nowhere — a pale amber glow rising from below, sourceless, patient.

She came out onto a gallery of black stone and stopped, because no amount of Ysolde's warnings had prepared her for scale.

The Court of Salt and Smoke was built inside a cavern so vast that its ceiling was a rumor, lost in haze and hanging chains. Terraces descended in concentric rings toward a central pit, and from the pit rose the glow — the dragon-fire, banked low behind screens of salt-glass, breathing its long dying breath. Around the fire, tier upon tier, stood the offices of government. Not the crude bureaucracy of Vell's Hall of Records but something older and more elegant: clerks in bone-white robes moving between desks of black wood; couriers descending spiral stairs with cases like her own; a tribunal of five seated on a dais near the fire, their faces lit from underneath in a way that made them look drowned.

And threaded through everything, carried on the warm updraft from the fire, was the sound.

Singing, almost. A murmur of ten thousand voices just below audibility, rising and falling like wind through wheat. Ulyana had heard bottled souls whisper before — a charm here or there, a memory with too much life left in it, murmuring its little fragment of self. This was that, multiplied past bearing. The whole cavern hummed with it. The clerks didn't seem to hear. They had grown around it the way a body grows around a splinter.

"Broker." A clerk materialized at her elbow, young, precise, with ink stains to the second knuckle. "Your case, please. Inventory at the Exchange, standard tariff. You're expected at the third-bell sitting — the tribunal is reviewing outside acquisitions this season."

*Outside acquisitions.* Souls bought from beyond the mountain. Her stomach turned slowly over, and she smiled.

"Of course," she said, and handed over the case.

---

The Exchange occupied an entire terrace: a hall of counters and brass grilles, where brokers from a dozen distant places queued to have their wares weighed, graded, and priced. Ulyana watched the process with the detached horror of a woman seeing her own trade performed at cathedral scale.

At each grille, a case was opened and its vessels laid out in rows — small glass bottles, some no larger than her thumb, glowing faintly with whatever color the soul inside remembered being. An assessor would lift each one to a lens of smoked crystal, murmur a grade — *whole, fractured, residual* — and set it on one side of a brass scale against a counterweight of salt. Then a price. Then a receipt in triplicate. The vessels went into wheeled cages, and the cages went down ramps toward the fire.

Nobody wept. That was the detail that lodged under her skin like a splinter. In Vell, when Ulyana sold a charm, someone wept — the buyer, remembering; sometimes herself, watching. Here, souls changed hands with all the emotion of grain futures. One broker ahead of her haggled cheerfully over a batch of *residuals*, arguing they were worth more than the assessor's grade, and won.

"Fresh stock?" the clerk asked her, when her turn came.

"Not exactly." She slid the case open a hand's width, showing the empty vessels she'd packed as ballast — clean glass, no souls, because she would sooner have died than carry inventory into this place. "I'm here on acquisition. I understand the tribunal reviews outside buyers this season. I'm interested in private purchase."

The clerk's manner shifted — not warmer, but more attentive, the way a fisherman's hands go quiet when the line pulls. "Private purchase is by approval only. Category?"

"Specific," Ulyana said. "Named."

Something flickered behind the clerk's eyes. Interest, or its professional counterfeit. "Named retrieval requires provenance documentation. How did the soul come to your attention?"

"A family matter." She let a fraction of weariness show, the exact measure a real broker might permit herself. "My brother sold himself south of here, years ago. Through crown channels. I've only recently learned where he ended up."

"Ah." The clerk softened into something almost human — pity, filed neatly under procedure. "Consignment sales are unfortunately common. The war levies, before the hunt. If you have the consignment number—"

"I don't. I have a name. Pemberton."

The clerk wrote nothing down, which told Ulyana he wouldn't need to. "Pemberton," he repeated. "I'll need to consult the registry. It will take until after third bell — the tribunal sits then, and the archives close during session." He produced a chit of pale bone and pressed it flat with a small brass stamp that bit a seal into it. "Present this at the lower archive after session. You may observe the Exchange in the interim, if you wish. Most brokers find it... instructive."

*Instructive.* She took the chit and thanked him, and hated him, and hated herself more for the thanks.

---

Third bell rang from somewhere deep in the rock, a tone more felt than heard, and the tribunal convened at the fire's edge, and the Exchange emptied of clerks the way a tide empties a shore. For perhaps an hour, the terraces belonged to the couriers and the caged vessels and the singing.

Ulyana used the hour.

She had told Corvin she would look, catalog, and get out — reconnaissance, nothing more, until they understood the ground. But the ground kept pulling. The archive was closed, yes, yet the *vaults* — she could see their mouths from the gallery, dark arched doorways on the terrace nearest the fire, each one guarded by a single warden who stood far too still, in the way of things warded rather than things watchful.

And the vaults were where the cages went.

She followed the last courier of the hour at a distance, down the ramp, keeping to the shadowed inner curve where the firelight failed. The cage trundled ahead of her on its iron wheels, whispering. Inside, maybe forty vessels rocked gently with the motion, and their murmur rose as they neared the vault, quickening like birds smelling home — or a slaughterhouse; from this distance she couldn't tell which.

The vault mouth breathed cold when she reached it, cold that had no business existing a hundred feet from a furnace. The warden at the door did not turn his head. He looked, Ulyana realized, less like a man than a man-shaped absence — a thing the wards wore for appearance' sake. His eyes were fixed on the middle distance, and when the cage rolled past him into the dark, he did not so much as blink.

She should have turned back. She catalogued, instead, the fact that she was going to keep walking, and found she could summon no argument against herself.

---

The vault was a cathedral built by accountants.

Racks rose in ranks into the gloom, floor to unseen ceiling, iron shelving bolted into living rock, and on every shelf stood bottles. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Each one labeled in a fine clerical hand: a name, a date of consignment, a grade, a lot number. Some glowed bright as embers; some had dimmed to the color of old honey; a few, in the deepest rows, were nearly dark, their labels curling, their contents reduced to a sediment of light that stirred only when Ulyana's footsteps passed — a tiny turning-toward, a hope so worn down it could only manage a flicker.

The singing was unbearable here. Up close it resolved into fragments, and the fragments were *people*. A woman's voice repeating a child's name. A man counting, endlessly counting, some debt he'd died owing. A girl humming a tune that Ulyana recognized from a market in Vell, from a fruit-seller's cart, from ordinary sunlight, and hearing it in this salt-dark place nearly broke her cover right there, because her hands started shaking and her breath went ragged and she had to press her palm flat against the cold iron of a rack and breathe through it like a woman riding out a fever.

*They're alive,* she thought. *They're not fuel. They're alive in there, and they know.*

Ysolde had said the fire needed freely given memory. Ysolde had believed it, grieved it, tended it from afar with a priestess's terrible faith. But standing among the racks, Ulyana felt the wrongness of the whole architecture the way she felt a forged seal — something subtly, fundamentally off. Freely given things were not stored in cages. Freely given things did not have lot numbers.

She put the thought away. There would be time to be right about it later, if she lived.

She moved deeper. The lots grew older as she went — dates climbing backward through the years, consignments from the war levies, from the famine, from the purges before the purges became fashionable again in Vell. And then the dates passed a year she knew intimately, the year of her father's debts, the year the collectors came twice in one winter, the year Tomas—

The label stopped her mid-stride.

It was on the third rack from the vault's back wall, eye level, exactly where an archivist's tidy mind would put a recent-ish consignment in a section organized by year. Small bottle, thumb-sized, glowing a steady gold that she knew — she would have known it blind, drunk, dead — because it was the color of the light in their mother's kitchen on bread-baking days, the color Tomas had loved best, the color he'd said heaven must be if heaven was anywhere at all.

The label read:

**PEMBERTON, TOMAS. Consigned per crown transfer, year of the double levy. Grade: whole. Lot 114.**

*Whole.*

He was whole. Not fractured, not residual, not worn down to sediment like the poor voices on the outer racks. Whole, and burning, and labeled, and filed.

Cold went through Ulyana — not fear, though fear was in it, but something older and harder, a fury with no heat in it at all, the kind that doesn't shake the hands. She had imagined rage as fire her whole life; now she learned it was ice, and that ice held better than fire ever had. She stood in front of the bottle and her face, she was quite sure, showed nothing whatsoever, because showing nothing was the first trade she'd ever learned and the only one that had never betrayed her.

*Hello,* she thought at the bottle, absurdly, uselessly. *You idiot. You brave, stupid, generous idiot.*

Because she knew — she had known since the dream on the mountain road, since Corvin's confession by the dying fire — that Tomas had not been seized. He had *sold*. Sold himself, at eleven years old, to settle the debts of a sister who never learned the full accounting. And here he was. Lot 114. Priced, weighed, and burning to keep a lie alive beneath a mountain while the city above hunted people like her for the crime of the same gift that had gotten him eaten.

Her hand rose toward the bottle.

She caught it at the last instant — Ysolde's voice in her memory, sharp as a slap: *the wards read intent, girl, and intent is the one thing you cannot forge* — and let it fall back to her side, trembling now, the ice cracking along its length.

Around her, the singing went on. Somewhere in the dark, the girl from the fruit-cart hummed her market tune. Somewhere a mother's voice said a child's name, and said it, and said it.

Ulyana memorized the rack. Row, shelf, position — the way she'd once memorized the patrol routes of Vell's tax collectors, the way she memorized everything she intended to take back. Lot 114. She etched it into herself deeper than any label.

Then she turned to leave, calm-faced, empty-handed, a broker with business concluded—

—and found the young clerk from the Exchange standing at the end of the aisle, bone-white robes pale as a shroud in the bottle-light, watching her with an expression of mild, terrible interest.

"You're a long way from the public galleries, broker," he said softly. "And I don't recall issuing you a vault pass."

Behind him, at the vault mouth, the warden who never blinked had begun, very slowly, to turn his head.
