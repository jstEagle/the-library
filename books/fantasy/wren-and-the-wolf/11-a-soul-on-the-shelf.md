## 11. A Soul on the Shelf

The vaults beneath the court smelled of cold iron and something sweeter underneath — a faint, cloying scent like overripe plums left in the sun. Ulyana had spent three days learning the rhythm of the place before she dared come this deep. Three days of playing the soul-broker Vess Karim, with her borrowed seals and her forged letters of introduction and her face remade by a charm she'd bought from a hedge-witch in the low town: cheekbones sharpened, eyes darkened, a scar drawn along her jaw that itched maddeningly whenever she spoke.

Three days of watching clerks in salt-white robes carry vessels down these stairs, and carrying nothing back up.

Now, past midnight, when the corridors emptied and only the lamps burned in their niches like patient yellow eyes, she walked the long gallery alone.

The shelves rose on either side of her, floor to ceiling, cut from grey stone that sweated moisture in the mountain's chill. And on those shelves, ranked in their thousands, stood the vessels.

They were small things, most of them. Bottles no larger than a sparrow's egg, blown glass gone cloudy with age, each one stoppered with wax and sealed with a lead tag stamped with a number. Some were jars, some were urns, some were boxes banded shut with copper wire. A few — the oldest, at the far end of the gallery — were great clay amphorae taller than a man, and Ulyana did not let herself wonder what was in those. She kept her eyes forward and her breathing even and she counted.

Row forty-one. Row forty-two. The tags ran in sequence, thousands upon thousands, and somewhere among them —

*Pemberton.*

She'd seen the name on the broker's manifest two nights ago, copied it onto her wrist in ink disguised as a beauty mark, and scrubbed it away the moment she was alone. But she could still feel where the letters had been, the way you can feel a word written on your skin long after the ink dries.

*Pemberton. Row 47, niche 312. Acquired: winter court, eleven years past. Source: private sale. Status: active.*

*Active.* As if her brother were a ledger entry still drawing interest.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. All through the infiltration — the gates, the guards, the endless performance of being Vess Karim, a woman who traded in souls the way grocers traded in flour — her hands had trembled whenever she let herself think of Tomas. But now, walking the aisle where he actually was, close enough to touch, they were calm as stones. Grief, it seemed, had a bottom. Below the grief there was simply work, and she knew how to do work.

Row forty-seven began with the letter P. She found the niche numbers climbing — 298, 300, 306 — and then, at eye level, behind a thin grille of blackened bronze:

312.

The vessel inside was small. Smaller than she'd imagined. A bottle of green glass, no bigger than her fist, its surface fogged as though breath had been breathed against it from the inside and never wiped away. The wax seal was cracked with age but unbroken. The lead tag swung gently on its wire, turning, turning, as if something within the niche stirred the air.

*Pemberton, T.*

Ulyana stood very still.

Eleven years. Eleven years since the night the debt collectors came to the tenement on Candler's Row, since the men with the crown's writ took everything — the stove, the blankets, her mother's wedding ring — and told her that what remained of the family's obligations would be settled *otherwise*. She had been fourteen. Tomas had been nine. She remembered screaming. She remembered the neighbors' doors staying shut. She remembered waking the next morning in the charity ward with no brother and a debt marked PAID in someone else's handwriting, and she had never known — never once in eleven years — what *otherwise* had meant.

Now she knew. He was here. He was here, and he was small enough to hold in one hand.

"Forgive me," she whispered, though there was no one to hear, and no god in this place but fire. "I'm late."

She reached through the grille.

Her fingers had not yet closed around the glass when the ward woke.

It made no sound. That was the horror of it — no alarm, no clangor, no rush of boots down the corridor. There was only a sudden pressure, like diving into deep water, and then the world folded sideways and Ulyana Pemberton was no longer standing in a vault at all.

* * *

She was nine years old again, or rather she was seeing through the eyes of someone who was nine, and the snow was falling on the mountain gate.

*Tomas's* snow. *Tomas's* gate.

She understood it instantly, the way you understand a thing in dreams — this was memory, living memory, still warm inside the vessel, and the ward had opened a window into it because that is what wards on soul-vaults do: they show you what you are trying to steal.

The gate was black iron taller than the tenements of Candler's Row, set into the mountainside, and it was carved all over with words in a script that hurt to look at. Torches guttered in brackets on either side, and their flames bent toward the gate as if being drunk. Before the gate stood a clerk in salt-white robes, and beside the clerk stood a man Ulyana recognized with a sickness that went all the way through her: Master Sable, younger by a decade, his grey-streaked hair still dark, his rings fewer but just as gaudy.

And between them, small and shivering in a coat too thin for the mountain, stood a boy.

Tomas.

He looked so much like her that it caught in her chest like a fishhook — the same sharp chin, the same dark brows, the same way of holding his shoulders up as if braced for a blow. But his eyes were dry. Whatever crying he had done, he had finished doing it, and what was left was a terrible, adult steadiness.

"You understand the terms," the clerk was saying. His voice was bored, the voice of a man reading a bill of lading. "The vessel will be prepared. The transfer is permanent. The crown's debt against the Pemberton household — four hundred crowns, eleven shillings — is discharged in full."

"I understand," the boy said.

"The payment goes to your guardian of record." The clerk glanced at Sable. "You are the guardian of record?"

"I am," Sable said smoothly. "Guildmaster of the Vell thieves' guild, acting on behalf of the family. The girl can't sign. She's not of age."

*The girl.* Her. Ulyana watched herself appear in the memory's edge — a thin, wild-eyed girl of fourteen being held at the arm by one of Sable's people, struggling, mouthing words the memory would not give her sound for. She remembered this now, or she remembered the shape of it: the drugged tea Sable had given her that evening, the waking in a strange room, the ride up the mountain road in a cart under sacking. She had thought, all these years, that she had failed him. That she had slept while he was taken. That it had been done *to* them both.

But Tomas turned, in the memory, and looked at her — at the girl she had been — and his face did something no nine-year-old's face should know how to do. It softened. It forgave her in advance.

"It's all right, Ulya," he said, and his voice was clear across the snow, clear as a bell, clear as if he were speaking directly into her chest eleven years later. "I said yes."

The memory shuddered. The clerk was speaking again, the ritual words, and young Ulyana was screaming now, actually screaming, and Sable's man had his hand over her mouth, and Tomas — Tomas walked forward on his own legs. No one dragged him. No one carried him. He walked to the gate with his chin up and his fists in his pockets the way he did when he was scared and didn't want anyone to know, and at the threshold he stopped and looked back one last time.

"They were going to take you instead," he said. Not a question. He had heard it — overheard it, she realized, overheard Sable and the collectors arguing in the guildhall while she slept off the tea. Four hundred crowns, or a gifted child. And the collectors had wanted the elder one. The one whose gift was already showing. "So I fixed it. I'm the one who fixed it, Ulya. You remember that. Don't you dare remember it wrong."

Then the gate opened, and the light that came out of it was orange and hungry, and the memory folded shut like a hand closing.

* * *

Ulyana came back to herself on her knees on the vault floor, the green bottle untouched in its niche, her palms flat against cold stone, her whole body shaking.

*Don't you dare remember it wrong.*

All these years. All these years she had built her life on the foundation of that night — the guilt of it, the failure of it, the engine of every theft and every charm and every careful, hidden day. She had believed she'd slept while her brother was sold. She had hated herself for it in the quiet way you hate a scar, without looking at it directly, and the hatred had been useful. It had kept her moving. It had kept her hard.

And it had been a lie. Not her lie — his. A nine-year-old boy had walked into the fire on his own feet so that his sister wouldn't be taken, and he had asked her, with the last full breath of his free life, to carry the blame for him. Because he knew her. Because he knew she couldn't live with the truth — that her little brother had paid her debts, had bought her life with his — and so he'd handed her the gentler weight instead. *You failed me,* he'd let her believe. When the truth was: *you were worth saving, and I saved you.*

A sound came out of her that was half laugh and half sob, and she pressed her fist against her mouth to keep it from echoing down the gallery.

Above her, the lamps flickered. Somewhere far off, through leagues of stone, the dragon-fire breathed, and every flame in the vaults leaned toward it the way the torches at the gate had leaned. Feeding. Always feeding. Eleven years of her brother, poured spoonful by spoonful into a fire to keep the wards alive, to keep the lights burning, to keep the comfortable machinery of this rotten court turning — and he had *chosen* it, chosen it at nine years old, and that choice changed nothing about what she meant to do and changed everything about how she would do it.

She got up off her knees. Her legs held. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had finally begun to shake — now, when it no longer mattered.

Because here was the thing the ward had taught her, the lesson written in her brother's own voice: the vessel could not simply be taken. The moment her fingers touched glass, the vault would know, and the warden would know, and whatever passed for law in this place would come down on her like the mountain itself. She'd need more than quick hands. She'd need the wards dead, or the guards blind, or the whole rotten system in flames — and she found, examining herself honestly, that she no longer much cared which.

Guilt had carried her this far. It would carry her no further. What filled her now was something hotter and cleaner, something with edges.

Resolve.

She memorized the niche. She memorized the seal pattern, the make of the glass, the exact height of the shelf, the route back to the stair. Then she did the hardest thing she had done in eleven years: she stepped back from the bottle. Away from Tomas. Leaving him one more night in the dark, because rushing now would lose him forever, and she had promised him — silently, furiously, across eleven years and a wall of glass — that she would remember it right.

*I'm coming back for you,* she told him. *And this time nobody fixes anything but me.*

She turned toward the stair.

The corridor lamp nearest the junction had gone out.

Not guttered — *out*, snuffed clean, and as she watched, the next lamp down the gallery died the same way, and the next, darkness walking toward her along the wall at an unhurried pace, and with it came footsteps. Measured. Unafraid. The footsteps of someone who owned the dark and had merely come to collect what was hiding in it.

Ulyana's hand went to the knife at her hip and found, with a lurch, that the shadows ahead already held a shape — broad, robed in salt-white, wearing the warden's chain of office like a man wearing a leash he'd learned to enjoy.

"Well," said the Warden of the Vaults, into the dying light. "Vess Karim. Or should we try your other name?"
