## 12. The Warden's Price

They took him in the gallery above the vaults, and Corvin let them.

It was not courage. He wanted to be honest with himself about that, at least, because honesty was the one discipline the Order had never managed to beat out of him. It was arithmetic. Ulyana had gone down the eastern stair toward the soul-shelves with a broker's seal on her coat and a lie in her mouth, and every second he remained free in the upper corridors was a second the warden's people might spend noticing her instead of him. So when the salt-cloaked guards came around the corner with their hooked staves leveled, Corvin Ashe — formerly Inquisitor Second Class, formerly a great many other things he did not care to name — dropped his hand from his knife and said, "I'll come quietly," and meant it the way a man means a bet.

They bound his wrists in wire that bit. They took the knife, the lockpicks sewn into his collar, the vial of ash-oil in his boot. One of them struck him across the mouth with the back of a gauntleted hand, casually, the way a man swats a fly, and Corvin tasted blood and thought: *good*. Anger aimed at him was anger not spent searching the lower galleries. He spat red onto the salt-white floor and smiled with what teeth he had left, and the guard who had hit him did not meet his eyes again for the rest of the walk.

They took him up, not down. That interested him. He had expected the dungeons — every court beneath every mountain had dungeons, it was practically a law of architecture — but instead they climbed through winding corridors lined with niches, and in each niche stood a bottle.

Not charms. Not the little stoppered memories Ulyana sold in the gutter markets of Vell, each one small as a tear and worth a night's bread. These were vessels the size of a man's forearm, blown in shapes that hurt to look at too long, and inside each one something moved. Light, mostly. But light with a *weight* to it, light that pressed against the glass like a face against a window, and as Corvin passed, vessel after vessel turned its brightness toward him — not all at once, but in a slow ripple, like wheat bending under wind, like a crowd turning to watch a prisoner go by.

He had burned a memory-thief once. He had stood in a square in Vell with a torch in his hand and told himself the woman had been dangerous, that her gift was a corruption, that fire was merely the law taking its natural shape. He had watched her memories leave her in the smoke — he hadn't known that would happen, that the burning of a thief would spill her stolen hoard into the air for anyone nearby to catch the edges of — and for one unguarded moment he had *seen* them. A mother's kitchen. A lover's hands. The smell of rain on a specific street on a specific afternoon. All of it rising off her like steam, all of it gone, and her looking at him at the end not with hatred but with a kind of terrible curiosity, as if she were memorizing *him* to take wherever she went.

He had resigned eleven days later. He had told himself it was conscience. Some nights he suspected it was only that he could no longer bear the smell of smoke.

The bottles rippled as he passed, and Corvin understood, with a cold settling in his chest, that he was walking through a prison where every cell held a person who could not scream.

*Hold on,* he thought at them, absurdly, and kept walking.

* * *

The warden's audience chamber sat at the crown of the mountain's hollow, directly above the fire shaft, and the heat came up through the floor grates in slow breaths, like the building itself was sleeping. The room was round, ringed with salt columns, lit by a chandelier of vessels far grander than those in the corridors — souls the size of fists, souls the size of hearts, arranged in ascending spirals so that their combined glow threw shifting shadows across a desk of black stone.

Behind the desk sat the Warden of the Vaults, and he had already removed his chain of office and laid it flat, which Corvin recognized as theater. A man who loved power wore it. A man who *understood* power set it aside while negotiating, so you'd remember he didn't need it in this room.

"Corvin Ashe." The warden did not look up from a ledger. His voice was mild, dry, the voice of a provincial tax assessor. "Second Class. Eleven years' service. Commendations for the Harrow Street action and the Purification of Coldharbour Market. Resigned rather than accept transfer to the Northern Circuit." He turned a page. "You've been difficult to find."

"I've been easy to find," Corvin said. "I've just been dull company."

That earned him a glance. The warden's eyes were pale gray, unhurried, and entirely without cruelty, which was somehow worse than cruelty. Cruelty wanted things. This man simply wanted everything, quietly, forever.

"Sit," the warden said, though no one had offered Corvin a chair. Two guards resolved the ambiguity by forcing him into one, and wired his ankles to its legs, and withdrew to the walls. The heat breathed up through the grate beneath him. Somewhere far below, the dragon-fire made a sound Corvin felt in his teeth rather than heard — a long, subterranean sigh, like a bellows running down.

"You're here with the Pemberton woman," the warden said. "Don't bother denying it. Vess Karim is a serviceable forgery — I compliment whoever cut her papers — but the court buys its soul-brokers from three families, all of whom report to me, and none of whom sent her. So." He folded his hands. "She has come for the vessel marked Pemberton. And you have come with her. Which raises an interesting question, Inquisitor, because you are the only man in this mountain positioned to answer it honestly: *why*?"

"Wrong priest," Corvin said. "I quit."

"No one quits," the warden said, without emphasis, as if stating that no one escapes gravity. "The Order of the Flame is not an employment. It is a shape your life was poured into. You may crack the mold, but you stay the same shape." He rose, crossed to the chandelier's winch, and turned it a half-circle with one gloved hand, lowering a single vessel to the level of his eyes. Inside it, light pressed and pressed. "Do you know what these are, truly? Not the doctrine. The doctrine is for the priests and the pamphlets. Do you know?"

"Bottled souls," Corvin said. "Sold by the crown. Burned in the fire below to keep the wards standing."

"That is what the fire *used* to be for." The warden set the vessel back into its spiral with the tenderness of a man shelving wine. "The wards are real, yes. The old wards that keep the deep things down and the mountain whole — they want feeding, and for six hundred years we fed them, and called it holy. But the wards are masonry now, Inquisitor. Old masonry. Cracked. Whatever sleeps beneath us stopped caring about our arrangements some centuries ago, and the fire keeps burning anyway because the fire does not know the difference between feeding a ward and feeding nothing at all." He returned to his desk and sat, and laced his fingers. "We are pouring souls into a hearth that heats an empty house."

"Then why keep burning them?"

"Because the fire is *dying*, Mr. Ashe. Weeks left. Perhaps less — you may have noticed the lamps failing tonight; the fire draws on everything connected to it when it's hungry. And when it dies, the ritual dies with it, and the crown's entire claim to legitimacy dies with the ritual, and every gifted soul in Vell currently cowering from the witch-hunt will discover there is nothing behind the curtain but old men and inventory." He said it all pleasantly. "Unless someone manages the transition."

Corvin kept his face still. *Manage the transition.* He turned the phrase over the way he'd been trained to turn evidence, looking for the print underneath.

"And the inventory," he said slowly. "The vessels. When the fire dies?"

"They don't burn, if that's your worry. The fire consumes what is fed to it freely, through the rite. Left in the bottle?" The warden shrugged. "Souls keep, Mr. Ashe. That is rather the point of bottling them. Kept cold, kept dark, kept *ordered*—" he touched the ledger on his desk, almost fondly, "—they are the most stable form of power yet discovered. A soul in a vessel can be traded. Leased. Spent in units. It cannot lie, cannot flee, cannot organize. It is capital in the purest sense ever devised." He leaned back. "When the fire gutters out, the court will hold several thousand vessels and a monopoly on the only currency that matters. The wards will fall or they won't; that will be the next generation's problem. My problem is ensuring that when the change comes, the inventory stays *here*. Consolidated. Understood."

*Hoard,* Corvin thought. The word arrived in his mind wearing plainer clothes than any the warden had offered: *he is going to let the fire die, and he is going to sit on a mountain of bottled people, and he calls it consolidation.*

Aloud, he said, "And the Pemberton vessel? Is that part of the inventory, or does it have sentimental value?"

Something moved behind the gray eyes — not warmth, but recognition of an opponent's move. "Ah. She told you, then. About her brother."

"She didn't have to tell me much," Corvin said. "I read the seizure ledgers before I burned half of them. Twelve years ago, crown collectors took a boy from the Pemberton household under a debt writ. Sold onward to the court. I assumed he'd been made a servant." He met the pale gaze steadily. "I assume differently now."

"The fire was healthier then," the warden said. "Younger souls burn longer. It was good stewardship." He said it without shame because, Corvin realized, he genuinely felt none; shame required believing there was another standard to measure against, and this man had built his entire self out of ledgers precisely so there wouldn't be. "But you haven't answered my question either, Inquisitor. Why are you here? You resigned over one thief. One. Out of hundreds. What makes the Pemberton woman worth throwing your obscurity away for?"

And there it was. The question he'd been carrying up a mountain, and the question he'd been refusing to answer even in the privacy of his own skull, because every honest answer had teeth.

"I owed a debt," Corvin said.

"To her?"

"To someone," he said, and left it there, and watched the warden decide not to press — which told Corvin the man already knew. Of course he knew. The Order filed everything. The warden had likely read the Coldharbour report before Corvin had finished writing it, including the addendum about spilled memories, including the part where the inquisitor conducting the execution had stood too close to the pyre.

*She looked at me like she was memorizing me.*

"Here is my offer," the warden said, and Corvin braced himself, because the man's voice had shifted into a different register — still mild, but now precise, the voice of a man laying cards face-up. "Your reinstatement. Full rank, restored, backdated — your pension accrues from the day of resignation, not today. The Coldharbour incident sealed and struck from the record. And a commission: Warden's Attaché for the City of Vell. Which means, in practical terms, that when the fire dies and the hunt ends, you will be the man who decides how the transition is managed in Vell. Who among the gifted is processed. Who is overlooked. There will be a list, Mr. Ashe, and you will hold the pen." He unfolded his hands. "In exchange, you give me the woman. Her name, her purpose, her method of entry, and whatever arrangement she believes she has with the guild or the priestess. She goes into a vessel herself — a fair trade, a soul for a soul, and hers is evidently a strong one — and the Pemberton boy burns in the last of the fire, which he was purchased to do regardless. Nothing changes for him. Everything changes for you."

The heat breathed up through the grate.

Corvin sat with it. That was the discipline, the one thing the Order had given him that was worth keeping: the ability to sit inside temptation without moving, to let it fill the room like smoke and simply breathe it and wait. Because the offer was *good*. That was what made it obscene — it wasn't a fool's bargain, it was a real one. The Coldharbour record, gone. Eleven years of sleeping in doorways and flinching at the smell of smoke, retroactively erased. And the pen. God, the pen. A list of names, and his hand on it, and every name he failed to write down was a life quietly saved — he could hide the gifted inside the bureaucracy itself, bury them in paperwork, do more good with a quill in that room than ten years of hiding in ditches—

He caught himself doing it, building the palace of rationalization brick by brick, and admired its architecture, and then looked underneath it at the foundation, which was a woman in a vault full of screaming light, trusting him to be upstairs making noise.

"If I refuse," Corvin said, "you'll have me questioned. I know how the Order questions. I wrote two of the manuals."

"You wrote the manuals," the warden agreed. "So you know that men break. It isn't personal; it's mechanics." He picked up his pen and made a small note in the ledger, unhurried. "But I notice you haven't refused. You've asked about refusal the way a merchant asks about a price he intends to haggle. So let me make the arithmetic plain." He looked up. "The fire is dying, Mr. Ashe. Every day it burns weaker, and every day I consolidate. The Pemberton boy goes into the fire within the week whether you speak or not — his vessel is already scheduled. The only variable in this equation is what happens to *her*. Speak, and she becomes leverage I no longer need, handled cleanly, painlessly, as an asset. Stay silent, and I must treat her as an intruder in a facility holding several thousand souls, during a period of instability, with guards who are frightened and armed." He set down the pen. "Silence doesn't protect her, Inquisitor. Silence is just cruelty with better posture."

*He's lying about half of it,* Corvin thought. *Which half?*

That was the trap within the trap. The offer might be genuine. The threat might be genuine. Both, neither. The warden dealt in certainty the way other men dealt in coin, and he was spending it lavishly right now, and Corvin had learned in eleven years of interrogations that the man most certain of his own honesty is the one selling the finest lies.

But there was one thing the warden had said that rang true precisely because it was ugly, and Corvin held onto it like a nail in a collapsing wall: *the boy burns within the week regardless.* If that was true — and the scheduling ledger open on the desk suggested it was; there was a line item, fresh ink, *Pemberton, T., final allocation* — then every hour Corvin spent in this chair was an hour Ulyana had to reach the shelves.

So he gave the warden what the warden had actually asked for, though the warden didn't seem to know it yet: time.

"You want me to denounce her," Corvin said slowly, "and you want me to believe it won't matter to her fate either way. Fine. Then it costs you nothing to give me until morning. I've spent eleven years not trusting my own judgment; I'll not sign a man's sister into a bottle after a single conversation in a hot room. Give me the night. Show me the schedule. Let me read the allocation rites myself — I'll want to verify the fire's decline independently, since you've just told me the entire ritual is theater. If your numbers hold, you'll have your statement by dawn, signed, in my own hand, with my full name and rank."

The warden regarded him for a long moment. The chandelier-light moved across his face, souls pressing at their glass, and Corvin could not tell whether the expression on him was doubt or delight.

"You realize," the warden said at last, mildly, "that everything you've asked for is also everything you'd need to build an escape."

"Yes," said Corvin. "That occurred to me."

A silence. Below them, the dragon-fire sighed again, fainter this time, a sound like a great animal turning its face to the wall. One of the vessels in the chandelier flickered — dimmed, guttered, steadied — and the warden glanced up at it with the briefest flicker of something that, on a lesser man, might have been worry.

"Guards outside the door," he said. "Wire stays on. You'll have the allocation ledgers brought to you at first bell, and Master Renn to supervise your reading." He picked up his pen once more, and paused, and added, without looking up: "For what it's worth, Inquisitor, I hope your numbers convince you. Men who cooperate live longer, and I've grown tired of filling vessels from this city. There are only so many strong ones left."

Two guards walked Corvin down out of the heat, past the rippling niches, to a cell carved from the salt itself — smooth white walls that gave back lamplight like frozen moonlight, and a barred vent in the ceiling through which, very faintly, came the smell of smoke.

They shut the door. Bolts fell. Corvin sat down against the wall and listened until the guards' footsteps faded, and then listened longer, to the mountain itself: the tick of cooling stone, the far-off sigh of the dying fire, and — beneath it, so soft he might have invented it — footsteps overhead. Quick ones. Moving east, toward the vaults. Toward the shelves. Toward a bottle labeled with her own name.

*Take your time,* he prayed to no one, to the salt, to whatever was left of the flame. *Every hour, Ulyana. Buy me every hour.*

Then he took the small brass button from the cuff of his coat — the one thing the searchers had missed because it was not a tool, it was a *keepsake*, and keepsakes were the one category of contraband no searcher in eleven years had ever thought to check — and began, silently, patiently, to unscrew it.

Inside, packed in wax, was a thimbleful of ash-oil he'd been carrying for eleven years.

Not enough to burn a door. Barely enough to burn a lie.

It would have to do.
