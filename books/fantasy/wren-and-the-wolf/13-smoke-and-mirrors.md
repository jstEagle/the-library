## 13. Smoke and Mirrors

The warden's chambers smelled of salt and old smoke, and Ulyana hated how much she liked them.

She had been inside fine rooms before — she had robbed half the merchant houses along the Amberway in her years before the guild cast her out — but she had never been inside a room that *breathed* wealth the way this one did. Not gold, exactly. The court beneath the mountain did not flaunt its riches; it wore them like skin. The walls were veined with pale mineral that caught the lamplight and held it. The floor was warm underfoot, warmed by the fire below, by the fire that was fed, so everyone said, by souls. Her brother among them.

Tomas.

She kept his name behind her teeth like a coin held in the cheek. If she said it aloud here, even to herself, she was afraid it would crack her open in front of the wrong person. So she walked the warden's outer chamber as she had been taught to walk it three days ago when they'd fitted her with a broker's grey sash: unhurried, bored, entitled. A woman who belonged. A woman with business.

Her business, tonight, was real. That was the joke of it. In her left sleeve she carried six bottled memories she had stolen from Vell's wealthy over two desperate years, and the warden's steward had agreed to appraise them at midnight. Souls were currency down here, but *memories* were raw stock — the court refined them, or so the brokers' gossip claimed, pressing the bright ones into vessels and letting the dull ones burn. Ulyana had spent two days learning the gossip, and two days was enough to know that the gossip was a curtain, and curtains existed to be pulled back.

And behind the curtain, she suspected, stood a man who knew exactly what the fire ate, and why.

The steward arrived at the stroke of the water-clock, a narrow man with ink-stained fingers and the flattened courtesy of someone who had long ago stopped seeing people as people. He weighed her bottles without comment, wrote figures on a slate, and led her through an arch of black stone into the inner chamber, where Warden Hesse Marlowe sat behind a desk of dragon-bone — actual bone, yellowed and faintly warm, and Ulyana's stomach turned at the size of the creature it must have come from, and at what else the desk might have come from.

"Ulyana Pemberton," the warden said.

Her blood went to ice, then to boiling, then back to ice, all in the space of a heartbeat. *He knows my name.* Then she remembered: the ledger. The guild ledger she and Corvin had stolen from the Hall of Records had carried names, and names traveled. Every broker who came down the mountain road came with a history. It meant nothing. It meant everything. She could not tell which, and so she smiled the smile she'd worn for widow Marsham, the one that said *I am trustworthy because I am tired.*

"You have me at a disadvantage, Warden."

"I read dossiers for breakfast." He gestured at the chair across from him. It was a small cruelty, making her sit while he remained standing, looking out the window that wasn't a window — a sheet of polished obsidian that reflected not the room but something deeper, a red glow far below, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. "Six vessels. Unrefined. Street-grade theft, if your dossier is honest." His eyes came around to her at last. Grey eyes. Patient. The eyes of a man who had never once needed to hurry. "You want to sell to the court directly. Cut out the middlemen."

"That is generally why one comes to a buyer."

"Mm." He crossed to the desk, lifted her first bottle — the memory of a countess dancing at her own daughter's wedding, purchased from the countess herself in a moment of drunken sentiment — and held it up to the lamp. The light moved inside it like a fish. "You know what I admire about your trade, Pemberton?"

"My discretion."

"Your *arithmetic*." He set the bottle down. "Every gift has a cost. You steal a man's wedding day, he weeps at the altar without knowing why, and you eat well for a month. Clean exchange. No gods, no guilt." Something flickered behind his face, there and gone. "Most gifted people I've met are sentimentalists. They believe their talent means something. You don't. I find that refreshing."

*He thinks I'm a monster,* Ulyana thought, and found, to her private horror, that the assessment stung less than it should have. Three days in this place and the arithmetic was already getting under her skin. She thought of Tomas burning somewhere below her feet and made herself keep smiling.

"The appraisal," she said.

"In a moment." He sat, finally, folding his hands. "First, a question. Curiosity, nothing more. Your file says you sold charms in the Saltmarket for two years. Charms of *dead men*, mostly. Widows bought them." He tilted his head. "Why do you suppose the crown never taxed you?"

Because the crown was buying, she nearly said. Because the ledger showed the court purchasing souls straight from the witch-hunters' cells. But that ledger was ash now, shattered in the vaults during the riot, and Corvin was in a cell somewhere beneath her feet because of it, and she could afford no slips. "I paid my tithes like anyone."

"Like anyone," Marlowe repeated softly. "Yes. Well." He drew the slate toward him, wrote a figure, turned it toward her. It was triple what the bottles were worth in Vell. Generosity, or bait. "For all six. And for a seventh, if you can produce one."

"A seventh of what?"

"Anything of yours." His voice did not change at all. "The court pays best for freely given memories, you see. There's a quality to them the stolen ones lack. Brightness. We can't manufacture it." A pause, thin as a blade. "Though heaven knows we've tried."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far below, the pulse of the fire seemed to quicken, or perhaps that was only her own heart.

"I didn't come down the mountain to give anything away," Ulyana said. "I came to sell."

"Naturally." He waved the slate away, and the steward stepped forward to collect her bottles, and the transaction was done, and none of it — not one word — had been the reason she was here.

* * *

She had planned the theft of the warden's memory the way she planned all her work: obsessively, in the dark, lying awake in the brokers' dormitory listening to strangers breathe.

It was not like taking from widows. Widows wanted to forget; their grief loosened the memory's roots until it practically fell into her hands. Merchants guarded their ledgers but not their heads. The wealthy of Vell wore their secrets like loose jewelry, snatchable in passing, in a handshake, across a card table. But a warden of the mountain court would have wards in his skull the way other men had locks on their doors. Touching his mind uninvited could burn her gift out entirely, or worse — could announce her, could ring some bell she couldn't hear, could bring guards running before she finished drawing breath.

So she would not touch his mind uninvited.

That was the trick, and she had built it from the oldest rule of her trade: *the hand takes nothing that the owner does not offer.* Widow Marsham had offered her grief freely, cupping it out to Ulyana like water. People offered things constantly, carelessly, to anyone patient enough to stand still. And wardens — she had learned this from watching the brokers, from listening to the apprentices complain — wardens *rehearsed*. They practiced their lies the way priests practiced liturgy, alone, nightly, turning each deception over until it shone. A rehearsed lie was a memory being handled. Being handled meant being held loosely.

Being held loosely meant it could be taken.

She waited three hours after the appraisal, sitting in the cold gallery outside his chamber with her sash and her boredom, until the corridor emptied and the water-clock dripped past midnight. Then she knocked.

"Pemberton." Marlowe stood in the doorway, unsurprised, which frightened her more than suspicion would have. "Something wrong with the price?"

"I want to renegotiate." She let a fraction of desperation show, measured to the drop. "A seventh memory. Freely given. You said the court pays best for those."

His interest sharpened so fast she felt it like a change in air pressure. "Yours?"

"If the figure's right."

"Come in."

He poured wine — one cup, then, after a visible hesitation, a second — and talked while he poured, and this too was according to plan, because a talking man was a remembering man, and a remembering man was a man whose doors stood open whether he knew it or not. He spoke of the fire, casually, the way landowners speak of weather. Its appetite. Its moods. The wards it sustained, the great lattice of protection woven through the mountain, older than the court itself, older than Vell.

"It gutters, you know," he said, settling across from her. "Everyone downstairs pretends otherwise. The priestess faction wrings their hands. The brokers raise prices." He smiled thinly. "But fires are honest things. This one is dying, Pemberton, and everything we've built on top of it dies with it unless we feed it properly."

"And what does it eat properly?" She kept her voice idle, curious, a broker's voice. Beneath the table, out of his sightline, her right hand lay open against her knee, palm up, fingers curled slightly inward — the receiving posture, the shape her hand made when she took.

"Souls," he said, and drank. "Refined. Willing ones preferred, though the crown's cells supply adequately."

The word went through her like a spear. *Tomas.* Somewhere below her feet, her brother's soul burned in a lattice of ancient magic, keeping the mountain safe, keeping *Vell* safe, keeping every smug citizen above who had never once wondered where their safety came from. Her hand trembled against her knee. She steadied it. And in that instant of contact — his mind open, rehearsing, handling his certainties like loose coins — she reached.

Taking a memory was not like the stories said. There was no wrenching, no flash of light. It was closer to eavesdropping through a wall, if the wall were made of time. She slid her attention along the surface of his open thoughts, found the rehearsed thing — she always knew it by its polish, by the way it gleamed with handling — and lifted.

The lie came away whole.

* * *

There was no ritual.

That was the first thing the stolen memory showed her, and it broke something in her understanding of the world so cleanly that she nearly gasped aloud, there in the warden's chair, wine untouched in her hand.

No ritual. No ancient covenant. No necessity.

She saw it through Marlowe's younger eyes, forty years deep: a chamber beneath this one, a council of robed figures around a table, and on the table a dying fire in a brazier — the last coals of the true dragon-fire, scraped from the corpse of the last true dragon, the mountain's old wards already fraying around them. And an old woman's voice, dry as paper: *The wards drink memory. Any memory. It need not be a soul. It need not even be willingly given, though willing burns brighter and longer.*

Any memory. Any at all.

She saw the council look at one another. She saw the moment — she would remember this moment, she realized, for the rest of her life, however long that proved to be — she saw the exact moment when one man, young then, sharp-faced, already ambitious, said: *"Then think what a soul is worth. Think what people will pay, what they will fear, what they will surrender, if they believe the fire demands nothing less than souls."*

Think what a monopoly on the miraculous is worth.

They had invented the hunger. The fire had never demanded souls. It would have accepted anything — a childhood, a love affair, a name — any memory pressed close enough and given close enough. But memories were cheap. Memories were everywhere, in every head in Vell, worthless until someone made them scarce. So the court had made them scarce. The court had written the liturgy of sacrifice, trained the priests to tend it, priced the vessels, licensed the brokers, and told the world above that the mountain's safety cost souls, when the truth was that the mountain's safety cost *nothing* — and the lie cost everything.

She saw forty years of it unfold in the space between two drips of the water-clock. The witch-hunts, reframed: not protection against dangerous magic, but harvest. The hunters swept the gifted into cells, and the cells fed the court, and the court refined the caught souls and fed the fire just enough to sustain the fiction — a starvation diet, a managed decline — while the rest of the taken souls went into the vaults. Hoarded. Bottled. *Power.* Because a soul in a bottle was leverage, was wealth, was a weapon, and the warden had understood before any of them that the fire was never the treasure.

The vault was.

She saw Marlowe, older now, standing before the council with his grey eyes patient: *Let it gutter. Let the wards fail slowly, district by district, and watch what the city above will pay us for salvation. Fear is the only tax that collects itself.*

And underneath all of it, threaded through every scene like wire: the knowledge, cool and certain, that the fire did not need a single soul more. That every soul in those vaults — hundreds, thousands, her brother among them — was not fuel.

Was inventory.

Ulyana opened her eyes.

Marlowe was watching her over the rim of his cup, and for one vertiginous second she thought: *he knows, he felt it, it's over.* But no. His gaze held only the mild appraisal of a businessman awaiting an answer about price. He did not know what she had just done, because no one in forty years had thought to guard against the one theft that mattered — the theft of the lie itself. Why ward a secret that everyone below already knew? Secrets were only warded from outsiders, and outsiders never got this far.

Except one had.

"You've gone pale," Marlowe observed. "Is my figure insufficient?"

"No." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because everything inside her was screaming. She set down the untouched wine. "The figure is generous. Almost suspiciously so."

"We pay for brightness." He smiled. "Freely given, remember. The fire prefers it."

*The fire doesn't prefer anything,* she thought. *The fire is a dog you've taught to beg so the neighbors believe it's starving.* She looked at this man — this tidy, patient, gray-eyed man who had spent four decades manufacturing hunger so that he might sell relief from it — and she understood that she was looking at the engine of everything. The hunts. The fear in Vell's streets. The neighbor dragged away screaming. The widow buying back her dead husband piece by piece because memory had been made into scarcity, and scarcity into law.

Her brother was not fuel. Her brother was *stock*.

The fury that rose in her then was unlike anything she had ever known — not hot but cold, absolute, a fury with a floor to it, a place to stand. For three years she had believed her gift was a curse that made her a thief of the grieving. Now she knew better. The curse wasn't hers. The curse was a story, told by men like this one, to make the miraculous scarce and themselves rich. Her gift was just a key. And these people had built a prison around the lock.

"Broker?" Marlowe prompted. "You drifted."

"Forgive me." She stood, smooth as pouring oil, and gathered her composure around her like the grey sash. "Long journey. Thin air. I'll return tomorrow with my answer on the seventh memory."

"Do." He raised his cup to her, pleasant as Sunday. "The court rewards decisiveness, Pemberton. And punishes its absence. You'll find we're very traditional that way."

She bowed the broker's bow and walked out of his chamber on legs that wanted to run.

In the corridor, the obsidian windows glowed with the fire's slow pulse, and she stopped before one, alone, and let herself feel it — all of it, Tomas alive in a bottle labeled inventory, Corvin rotting in a cell, Ysolde's shrine waiting on the mountainside with whatever truth the old priestess tended. The fire didn't need souls. Which meant the souls could be taken out. Which meant—

Which meant the fire would go out anyway, unless something fed it. Unless someone gave.

She pressed her palm flat against the warm black glass, and far below, the light pulsed against her hand like a heartbeat asking a question.

Behind her, soft as a knife leaving a sheath, a voice she knew said: "Well. Isn't this a picture."

Sable.
