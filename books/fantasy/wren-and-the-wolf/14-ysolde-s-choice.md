## 14. Ysolde's Choice

The shrine smelled of tallow and old snow.

Ysolde had kept it for sixty years, sweeping the ash that drifted down from the mountain's throat every dawn, feeding the altar-flame with splinters of her own past — a name here, a face there — so the small fire in the brazier would remember what the great fire beneath could not: how to burn without devouring. She was the last of an order that had once numbered nine hundred. The others were dust, or worse, and the mountain above her was dying by inches, and she had made her peace with all of it long ago.

Then the girl came down the goat-track with a witch-hunter at her back, and Ysolde's peace went out like a snuffed wick.

She felt them before she saw them. That was the first thing. Magic answered magic across distance, the way two tuning forks wake each other with a touch, and the girl's gift rang through the stone of the shrine like a struck bell — a thief's gift, a memory-eater's gift, rare as dragon's teeth and twice as dangerous. Ysolde set down her broom and stood very still in the doorway and waited.

They crested the ridge at dusk. The girl walked like someone carrying something breakable inside her chest; the man behind her walked like a blade that hadn't decided which way to point. Ysolde catalogued them both in the old way, the priestess way, reading the shape of their souls through their shadows on the snow. The hunter's shadow was torn at the edges, half-burned, but whole underneath. The girl's shadow flickered — gaps in it, holes where memories should have cast their weight — and one hole was enormous, ragged, freshly torn.

*Oh,* Ysolde thought. *Oh, child. What did you already give away?*

"You're the priestess," the girl said. Not a question. Her voice was rough from the road. "Ysolde. Last of the Order of the Undying Flame."

"Last of everything," Ysolde said. "Come in before the wind decides otherwise."

* * *

Inside, the brazier burned low and gold, and Ysolde fed them barley soup because travelers lie better on full stomachs, and listened.

They told it badly and out of order, the way true stories always come — the girl, Ulyana, talking fast about the court beneath the mountain, the vaults of bottled souls, the fire that ate them; the man, Corvin, cutting in with the cold details, the warden, the wards, the numbers of guards. Ysolde let them run themselves out. She had learned patience from a fire that took centuries to say anything.

When they finished, the silence held for a long moment, filled only by the tick of the brazier.

"You want to free your brother," Ysolde said to the girl. "And you want the fire not to die when you do. Because if the fire dies—"

"The wards fail," Corvin said. "I've seen the crown's surveys. The mountain holds back something. Nobody says what."

"The deep cold," Ysolde said simply. "The thing that was here before the dragons, before Vell, before names. Your kings built their city on a lock and called it geography." She turned her hands over the flame, letting its warmth soak into knuckles gone thin. "For six hundred years my order tended the fire, and for six hundred years the court has lied about what feeds it. Souls, they tell the brokers. Souls, they write in their ledgers. It is a convenient lie. Souls are things you can take."

Ulyana leaned forward. "Then what does feed it?"

And there it was — the question Ysolde had carried alone for sixty years, the question she had sworn never to answer aloud, because answers have consequences and she was tired of being the last one left to bear them.

But the girl's shadow had holes in it. And the fire had weeks, maybe less. And Ysolde was old, and tired of hoarding truth like a miser hoards coins he can never spend.

"Memory," she said. "Freely given memory."

* * *

Ulyana went white. Corvin's hand moved toward his belt, toward a weapon that wasn't there anymore — Ysolde noted the absence, filed it away — and then went still.

"That can't be right," Ulyana said. "The vaults are full of souls. I saw them. Bottled, labeled, sold—"

"They work," Ysolde agreed. "Poorly. A soul burns slow and dirty, like wet peat, because a soul does not *want* to be fuel. Every soul in that vault is screaming, quietly, forever, and the fire must waste most of its strength smothering the scream before it can use the heat. That is why the fire gutters now. That is why the wards thin. The court has been starving a furnace with the wrong food for three hundred years, and calling the famine holiness." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "A memory given freely burns clean. One memory, truly surrendered — loved, cherished, released without reservation — will feed the fire for a hundred years."

"How do you know?" Corvin asked. His voice was flat, careful. Inquisitor's voice. *Give me the source or give me nothing.*

"Because I have fed it myself." Ysolde pulled the collar of her robe aside, and showed them.

Across her collarbone ran a scar like a brand — not flesh-seared but *memory*-seared, a pale smoothness where sixty years ago there had been texture, warmth, a life. The order's final rite. When the dragons died and the last fire began to fail, the nine hundred priests of the Undying Flame had each given freely of their most precious memory to keep it alive. Ysolde had given her wedding day. She knew this only because the rite's records survived; the memory itself was a smooth blank in her mind, a word rubbed off a page. She remembered that she had been happy once, on one particular morning, sunlit and perfect and worth a hundred years of fire. She could not remember one second of it. She never would.

"What did you give?" Ulyana asked softly. Thieves always asked that. They wanted to weigh the loss, price it.

"I couldn't tell you if I wanted to." Ysolde laughed, and the laugh came out older than she was. "That is the mercy and the cruelty of it, child. The fire takes clean. No residue, no fragment, no dream to haunt you. You know *that* you gave. You will never know *what*. Forever means forever. There is no charm, no spell, no god that gives it back."

Corvin spoke into the quiet. "So the court could have done this at any time. Volunteers. Freely given."

"The court does not ask," Ysolde said. "Asking costs time, and taking costs nothing — to them. And a taken soul cannot refuse, while a memory must be *surrendered*, wholly, with the heart open. You cannot trick a memory out of someone. Believe me. My sisters tried, in the early years, when we were desperate and stupid. Coerced memories burn black and foul the wards worse than souls." She looked at Ulyana, and made herself say the rest plainly, because the girl deserved plainness after a lifetime of everyone lying to her. "There is more. Listen well, because this is the part that will tempt you, and the temptation is a trap.

"A stolen memory can be returned. Sold back, gifted back, pressed into the mind again like a flower into a book. Your brother's soul can be freed and made whole — souls mend, given time and love. But a memory given to the fire is ash. There is no vessel in any vault holding what the fire consumes. It becomes light. It becomes warmth. It becomes the ward between Vell and the deep cold. If you give, child, you give the way I gave. Permanently. Irretrievably. You will stand in the ashes of your own past and know only the shape of the hole."

* * *

The fire ticked. Outside, wind worried the shutter loose and let it fall.

Ysolde watched the girl's face and read it the way she read flames — flicker by flicker. Hope rising. Horror rising behind it. And underneath, moving quick and bright as a fish under ice, the thief's arithmetic: *a memory. Only a memory. I steal memories every day. I know what they cost.*

*You don't,* Ysolde thought, sorrowing. *You know what other people's cost. You've never paid yourself.*

"There's another way," Ysolde said, before the girl could speak. "One other way. It's why I'm telling you any of this."

Corvin's eyes narrowed. Ulyana went still, the stillness of prey scenting weather.

"I am the last priestess," Ysolde said. "When I die, the rite dies with me, and no one living knows how to surrender cleanly — how to open the heart, how to give without the giving tearing loose pieces of the self along with the gift. An untrained giver loses more than they mean to. Sometimes much more. Sometimes—" she thought of a novice, eighty years ago, who had meant to give one afternoon and had emptied herself of her entire childhood instead, and had sat afterward in the snow, smiling, asking who she was — "*everything.*"

"So let me go up the mountain with you," Ysolde said. "Let me stand at the fire. Let me give what remains of mine. Sixty years of small things — faces, songs, the taste of bread, the color of this brazier. It will not be one treasured day. It will be enough. Enough to relight the fire for a generation. Enough to buy your brother free without cost to you." She spread her scarred hands. "I am seventy-three years old, child. My memories have mostly finished their work. Yours haven't even started. It is simple arithmetic. Let me pay."

"No."

Ulyana said it immediately, flatly, the way people refuse things they've already refused inside themselves a dozen times. But Ysolde had argued with fire for sixty years, and fire always got the last word eventually, and she was not done.

"Child—"

"No." Ulyana stood. The brazier-light caught her face, hollowed it, lit the fury banked in it like coal. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to hand me absolution wrapped in a bow. I didn't walk three hundred miles to trade one stranger's past for my brother's future. That's just the court's arithmetic with kinder handwriting — somebody pays, somebody burns, and I walk away clean." Her voice cracked and she hated it, Ysolde could see her hating it, and pushed on anyway. "He chose to be sold. For *my debts*. He paid for me once already. I'm not going to balance that ledger with your wedding day, or whatever smooth little blank you've got left under that scar."

"It was my wedding day," Ysolde said quietly, and watched the girl flinch. "You see? I know the shape of the hole, even if I can't see through it. I am offering you a wound I have already survived."

"That's exactly why I'm saying no!" Ulyana's voice rose, and somewhere in it was a younger voice, a girl's voice, selling bottled grief to widows in a market square, hating herself and doing it anyway. "You've already paid once. Sixty years ago. You gave them your happiest day and you've spent every day since tending the thing that ate it — and you want to give it *more*? What's left? You'd be walking up that mountain to give away whatever small warm thing is still keeping you *you*. I've seen what forgetting does to people. I sell it in bottles." She pressed her fist against her sternum. "I won't be the reason you forget the color of that fire. Or the sound of the wind. Or your own name. Not for Tomas. Not for anyone."

For a moment neither of them breathed.

Then Ysolde did the hardest thing she had done since the last of her sisters died. She let it go.

Not entirely. Never entirely — pride is the last fire to gutter. But she bowed her head, an inch, a concession measured in millimeters, and when she raised her eyes again they were wet and level.

"Then hear the rest," she said, "and understand what you're choosing. Because you will go up that mountain, girl — I see it in you, I saw it the moment you came over the ridge — and you will stand before that fire, and the fire will look at you the way fire looks at everyone: patiently, hungrily, waiting to see what you'll feed it. And in that moment you will have to choose what to give, and you will have to mean it. Completely. The fire knows the difference between a gift and a payment. It always has. Give it a sacrifice and it will spit your memory back in your teeth, and take something else instead — something you didn't choose. That is the law of the flame, and no priestess, no king, no warden beneath the earth has ever overturned it." She leaned forward, and the old authority of nine hundred voices moved in her throat. "Whatever you carry up there — your brother, your guilt, your grief — leave it at the threshold. Carry only the thing you love most, and carry it open-handed. Can you do that? Do you even know yet what it will be?"

Ulyana opened her mouth. Closed it.

And Ysolde watched the answer move behind the girl's eyes and saw, with a priestess's terrible clarity, that she did not know — that the question had just been born in her and would grow for days, gnawing, until the fire forced it out of her.

It was Corvin, then, who broke the silence — the torn-shadowed hunter, staring into the brazier with the face of a man doing sums he dreaded.

"The warden," he said slowly. "In the cells, before I got out — he talked. About the fire failing. About hoarding what's left." He looked up, and his eyes had gone cold and bright. "If freely given memory feeds the fire, and souls barely do — then the warden doesn't need the fire alive at all. He needs it dead. The vaults, the wards, the whole ritual — it's not worship. It's inventory." His jaw tightened. "And if he finds out what you two just told me — that one good memory outlasts a thousand souls — he'll burn every memory-thief in Vell to keep the secret buried."

Ysolde rose, joints cracking, and went to the window.

Above the shrine, the mountain stood black against the stars, and deep in its throat, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the last dragon-fire pulsed — dimmer tonight than yesterday. Dimmer than any night before.

"We leave at first light," she said. "All three of us. And pray the fire keeps its patience longer than the warden keeps his hunger."

Behind her, she heard Ulyana whisper, almost to herself:

"*What do I love most?*"
