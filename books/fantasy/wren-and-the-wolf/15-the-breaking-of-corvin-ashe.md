## 15. The Breaking of Corvin Ashe

They took his boots first.

Corvin never understood why, in eleven years of service to the crown's Inquisition, until he found himself on the wrong side of the salt-iron table and felt the cold of the mountain floor climb through his bare feet like a living thing. It was not cruelty for its own sake. It was procedure. The warden of the Court of Salt and Smoke did nothing without procedure. Strip the prisoner of every comfort, every dignity, every small familiar weight — even the leather around his ankles — until there was nothing left of him but the questions.

The cell was a vault of grey stone lit by a single lantern that burned without flame. Soul-fire, the priests called it. Corvin had carried a dozen such lanterns into condemned houses himself, back when his hands were steady and his conscience was something he could fold away at the end of a shift like a warrant. Now the light sat on the table between him and the warden's interrogator, and it made everything look drowned.

Her name was Vess. She had introduced herself with perfect courtesy, an hour ago or a year ago — time moved strangely under the mountain — and she had not raised her voice since.

"Again," she said. "Where is Ulyana Pemberton?"

"I don't know."

She sighed, the way a clerk sighs at a misfiled ledger, and turned the small brass wheel beside her hand. The chains on Corvin's wrists drew taut. His arms rose until his shoulders burned, until his heels barely kissed the stone, until breathing became arithmetic — cost against gain, cost against gain.

Pain, Corvin had learned, came in dialects. There was the blunt shout of a broken bone. There was the low mutter of cold working into old scars. And there was this: the slow, patient grammar of the rack's gentler cousin, the one the Inquisition had never put in its manuals because it left no marks worth photographing. His joints spoke first. Then his tendons. Then, eventually, when Vess turned the wheel another quarter-turn, something deeper began to speak — something that did not belong to muscle at all.

He thought about Ulyana Pemberton, and lied to himself about why he wouldn't give her up.

It wasn't loyalty. He'd known her three weeks. It wasn't love; he was too old and too ruined for that particular fever. It was arithmetic of another kind. If he gave her to Vess, then everything he had done since resigning his commission — the road, the shrine, the lie he told at the gates of the court with a forged broker's seal sweating in his palm — would become what it had always threatened to be: a performance. A man playing at redemption the way children play at soldiers, all noise and no consequence.

If he stayed silent, it was real. That was all. Silence was the only honest thing he owned.

"Your pulse changed," Vess observed. She said it mildly, almost kindly, watching the little glass instrument on the table where a thread of quicksilver trembled. "You thought of something pleasant. Guilt, perhaps. You inquisitors always mistake guilt for virtue."

"You were never an inquisitor," Corvin rasped.

"No. I read your kind the way you read ledgers." She turned the wheel again.

* * *

They gave him water when he stopped being able to answer, which was a mercy of a bureaucratic sort — a dead prisoner answered nothing, and the warden wanted answers. While he drank, they let him hang, arms down now, and he watched the soul-lantern and tried to think of anything except the sound his own body had begun to make, a thin wet creak like rigging in a storm.

He thought of the fire he'd set in Marrow Lane, four years ago. Not the warden's fires, not the clean official burnings — his own. A tenement on Marrow Lane, a tip from an informer, a woman named Odile Fen who kept forty stolen memories in jars beneath her floorboards and sold them as charms to grieving widows. Exactly like Ulyana. Exactly.

Odile had run. Of course she'd run. They all ran, and running was confession, and confession was ash. Corvin had chased her up three flights of stairs with a torch in one hand and a writ in the other, and when she'd turned at the landing — a small woman, ink-stained fingers, eyes wide with a terror he understood only years later — the stairwell had been full of lamp oil someone had spilled, hers or his, it didn't matter anymore, and the flame had jumped the gap like a hungry dog slipping its leash.

She hadn't screamed. That was the detail that had unmade him. She had looked at him through the fire — through *his* fire, his torch, his writ, his law — and she had pressed both ink-stained hands over her mouth, and she had not screamed, and then she had been gone.

The inquiry had cleared him. Procedure, followed. An accident, ruled. A promotion, offered. He had resigned instead, and everyone in the Hall of Records had assumed it was grief, and grief was respectable, and so they let him go with honors.

But it hadn't been grief. It had been recognition. Because in the moment before the flame leapt, Odile Fen had looked at him the way a person looks at a mirror they are about to break — and some part of him, some part he had spent thirty-nine years nailing shut behind his ribs, had looked back.

"Resting?" Vess asked, returning.

"Contemplating my sins," Corvin said. "There are so many I have to keep a list."

"How industrious." She sat, folded her hands, and regarded him with the patience of a woman paid by the hour. "Let me tell you how tonight ends, Inquisitor Ashe. There are two versions. In the first, you give me the Pemberton woman's location, and the warden restores your name, your rank, your pension, your boots. You walk out of this mountain a man again. In the second—" she glanced at the lantern, and its light guttered, just slightly, like a held breath "—in the second, we keep asking until you stop being a man at all, and then we take what's left of you downstairs, and you warm the city above us for a century. Either way, the court wins. The only question is whether *you* win anything first."

"The fire doesn't need souls." He said it before he could weigh it. Her face didn't change, but her hands did — a tiny stillness, a held breath of their own. So Ulyana had been right. So the theft from the warden's memory had been true. The court knew the ritual was a lie, knew the dragon-fire burned on freely given memory alone, knew it, and fed it people anyway.

"There it is," Vess said softly. "The thief told you. Or you stole it out of her head, one or the other — the warden suspects she has talents beyond bottling charms." She leaned forward. "Say it again."

"The fire doesn't need souls. It needs memory. Freely given. Everything else is theater."

"And who would believe you? A disgraced witch-hunter, tortured past sense, babbling in a vault no one leaves." She smiled, and it was the first true expression he'd seen on her. "You're not a witness, Corvin Ashe. You're inventory."

She turned the wheel all the way back and started over from the beginning.

* * *

Somewhere past the fourth hour, Corvin stopped feeling his body as his own.

This was a thing the Inquisition taught its recruits, in the first year, in the windowless classroom beneath the Hall: *the body will betray you, so live in the mind.* He had been good at it. He had lived in his mind for thirty-nine years, in fact, ever since he was a boy in the fen-country who could make candle flames lean toward him when he was angry, and his mother had caught him doing it, gone white as milk, and told him that if anyone ever saw, men with grey cloaks would come and take him away, and that he must bury it, bury it *deep*, deeper than anything, and so he had. He had buried it so deep he'd forgotten which drawer he'd put it in. He had joined the men with grey cloaks instead. He had hunted his own kind for eleven years and told himself the hunt was penance when it was really camouflage — the deepest hiding place of all.

Now the wheel turned, and the pain climbed, and the place where he had nailed the door shut began, very quietly, to creak.

"Where is she?"

"In a hole you'll never find."

"That's not an answer. Where. Is. She."

Heat, he thought absurdly. Why is there heat? The vault was cold. The lantern was cold light. But heat was gathering along his forearms the way sweat gathers, the way blood gathers in a bruise, rising from somewhere below the wrists, below the elbows, below the ribs, below the nails, below the boy in the fen-country with the leaning candles — rising toward the surface after thirty-nine years underground like water finding a crack in a cellar wall.

Vess saw it before he did. He knew because her hand stopped on the wheel, and her head tilted — the precise, clinical tilt of an inquisitor observing an anomaly — and her voice, for the first time, lost its clerical calm.

"What are you doing?"

"Nothing," Corvin said. And then, because it was true, and truth was the last weapon left to him: "Nothing I've ever allowed."

The quicksilver instrument shivered. The lantern flared — not guttered, *flared*, drinking something from the air — and the chains on his wrists grew hot, not from any mechanism, not from any wheel, but from themselves, from the iron remembering what iron did near a furnace.

Vess stepped back. Smart woman. Eleven years too late for Odile Fen, but smart.

"Suppressant," she snapped at the door. "Get me a suppressant, now—"

The door didn't open fast enough. Nothing opened fast enough. The door inside Corvin Ashe — thirty-nine years of nails, of grey cloaks, of writs and warrants and careful, careful silence — came off its hinges all at once, and what came through it was not rage, though rage was in it, and not fear, though fear had built it. What came through was simply *himself*, whole, undivided, the self he had amputated to survive his mother's warning and the Inquisition's embrace, and it filled the vault the way dawn fills a valley.

Every flame in the room stood up straight.

The lantern's false fire stretched tall as a child. The brazier by the door roared green-white. Even the soul-lantern — bottled, sealed, dead — threw light like a struck bell throws sound. And Corvin hung in his chains at the center of it, barefoot, bleeding, laughing, because it didn't hurt. That was the joke. Thirty-nine years of dread, and it didn't hurt. It was the first time since Marrow Lane that *anything* hadn't hurt.

"Gods," Vess whispered, backed against the far wall. "You're one of them. All these years — the hunts, the lists — you were on your own list."

"Yes," Corvin said. His voice came out doubled, resonant, a voice with a fire behind it. "Item four hundred and six. Corvin Ashe. Gifted. Unregistered. At large." He flexed his hands, and the chains slagged — not broke, *melted*, running off his wrists like golden tears and pooling on the stone, glowing. "I resigned before anyone noticed. I thought that was cowardice. I'm beginning to revise my opinion."

"Stand down," Vess said, and there was a blade in her hand now, salt-iron, ward-forged, and her voice had found its procedure again because procedure was all she had. "The warden offers you reinstatement. That offer stands. Put it away, whatever it is, and—"

"He's going to let the fire die." Corvin dropped to the floor. His legs nearly failed him — the magic was carrying more than his flesh wanted to — but he stayed upright through sheer spite, and spite, he reflected, had always been his strongest discipline. "He's going to hoard the remaining souls upstairs while the wards fail and the mountain goes dark, and he'll call it stewardship. You know it. You read the same ledgers."

"Stand. Down."

"No." He smiled, and felt the smile glow. "I find, Vess, that I've stopped following procedure."

She lunged — quick, professional, salt-iron aimed at the meat of his thigh, a disabling cut, textbook — and Corvin caught the blade in his bare hand.

He heard her gasp. He felt it too, distantly, the way one feels weather through a window: the steel going soft in his grip like taffy, the ward-forging unraveling into ordinary metal and then into less than that, a drip of cooling slag on the stone between them. His palm was scorched. He would carry the brand forever, a red star in the lines of his hand, and he found he didn't mind. Marks were honest. Marks were the opposite of the Hall of Records.

"You should run," he told her, gently. "I mean that. Whatever he's promised you, he's lying, and when this vault comes down — and it is coming down, Vess, because I can hear them, I can hear every jar on every shelf upstairs starting to wake—" and that was true too, and it frightened him more than anything else, because he *could* hear them, faint as distant bells, hundreds of bottled souls stirring as the mountain's great wards loosened their grip, "—he will feed *you* to the lanterns before he admits a single error."

For a long moment Vess looked at him — at the melted chains, the slagged blade, the burning palm, the man — and Corvin watched eleven years of his own former life pass behind her eyes: the certainty, the procedure, the terrible economy of believing the state's arithmetic.

Then she ran. He let her. Some debts you pay by choosing, once, not to collect.

* * *

The corridor outside the vault was empty, alarm-bells just beginning their dumb clatter far above, and Corvin walked into it barefoot with his fists still smoking and realized he was crying.

Not from pain. From the sheer stupid relief of it — thirty-nine years of holding a door shut, and here on the other side had been nothing monstrous at all, only himself, tired and furious and free. He thought of Odile Fen's ink-stained hands over her mouth. He thought: *this is what she was hiding. This is what we burned.* He thought of Ulyana Pemberton somewhere above him or below him, walking into the dark with her thief's grace, and he sent the thought after her like a prayer to no god in particular: *hold on. I'm coming. And I am done being quiet.*

Behind him, the vault's lantern burst its glass, and freed flame went roaring up the corridor wall like a written sentence finally spoken aloud.

Ahead, the whole mountain began, very softly, to shake.

And in his ears, clearer now, closer — hundreds of small voices waking in their bottles, asking the same question in a hundred different tones:

*Who's there? Who's come to let us out?*
