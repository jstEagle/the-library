## 16. Riot in the Vaults

The pain had stopped being pain an hour ago. That was the first mercy the warden's instruments allowed, and Corvin Ashe had learned, in the long grey years of his service to the crown, that mercy from such hands was never mercy at all. It was a door left open on purpose. They wanted him to walk through it — to say her name, to give up the thief with the charcoal eyes who had trusted him just enough to sleep near him on the mountain road. All he had to do was speak.

He hung from iron rings in a chamber of white salt-stone, and the light came up through the floor in slow amber pulses, like the mountain itself was breathing. Each pulse of the dragon-fire below, Ysolde had said. Each pulse meant the fire still lived. Corvin counted them the way a drowning man counts waves.

"Former Inquisitor Ashe." The warden's voice arrived before the man did, round and reasonable as a magistrate reading a tax roll. He came into view carrying a small glass vessel, no bigger than a plum, and its contents moved. Not liquid. Something that *wanted*.

"You were the crown's best hound for eleven years," the warden said. "You burned forty-one of the gifted. Then one girl with stolen memories in her pockets, and you wept in front of your own pyre and walked away from everything. Sentiment is a disease, Corvin. But it responds to treatment."

"I don't know where she is," Corvin said. His voice came out in ribbons.

"That's the beautiful part." The warden held the little bottle up to the amber light, and inside it something turned over, slow and blind, like a fish under ice. "You don't have to know. This is a soul — a small one, a cooper's apprentice who died owing us money. Souls remember heat. Souls remember fear. And when I press this against your skin, you will remember everything she ever told you, whether you meant to keep it or not."

Corvin looked at the thing in the glass and felt something in his chest come loose — not fear exactly. Recognition. Eleven years he had hunted people whose blood hummed with power he claimed not to believe in, and every pyre he lit had lit something in him too, some low coal he'd smothered with procedure and doctrine and, at the end, with drink. He had told himself the coal was only shame.

It wasn't.

"The fire is dying," the warden said conversationally, setting the bottle down on a steel tray with a click. "Did the priestess tell you that? Weeks left, perhaps less. When it goes, the wards go, and everything in these vaults wakes up at once. So I've been making arrangements. Downsizing. Consolidation." He smiled the way ice smiles. "Your thief has been stealing from my shelves. I want her name, and where she sleeps."

The amber pulse behind the wall stuttered. Just once. Just slightly.

The warden noticed. Corvin watched him notice — saw the flicker of something that even a man made of salt could feel: dread. The warden picked up the bottle faster than he meant to.

And Corvin stopped holding the coal down.

It didn't feel like power. It felt like a cough he'd been suppressing for thirty years. It went out of him in all directions at once — through the iron rings, through the salt-stone, through the tray — and the bottle in the warden's hand burst like a struck bell. The soul inside it didn't spill; it *left*, tearing free in a ribbon of pale fire that screamed without sound and vanished upward through the stone.

The rings melted. Corvin hit the floor on hands that were still smoking, and the smoke was coming from him, from between his fingers, from the old scars on his forearms lighting up like seams of ore. Every pyre he'd ever lit rose in his memory at once and then reversed, all that fire flowing backward into him, and he understood with terrible clarity what he had been all along: a lantern pretending to be dark.

The warden was shouting for guards. The salt-stone walls rang like the inside of a drum.

Corvin stood up. The chamber door was warded — he could see the ward now, could see all wards now, gold threads stitched through the air — and he put his burning hand through them and they came apart like wet paper.

Then he opened the door, and let the mountain hear him scream.

* * *

Three levels above, Ulyana Pemberton heard the scream come up through the floor and knew, the way you know a knock on your own door, that it was Corvin.

She had been in the vaults for an hour, moving through corridors lined with niches, and each niche held a bottle, and each bottle held a person. She had made herself stop looking after the first twenty. Looking was how you died down here — you looked at the label *Widow Marran, age 61, sold by her son*, and you thought of the widow from your stall, and your hands started shaking, and shaking hands could not pick locks.

But the vaults had changed in the last few minutes. The amber pulse in the walls had gone irregular — quickening, guttering, quickening again — and the bottles had begun to do something she had never seen bottled memory do.

They were waking up.

It started as a glow. Then a vibration, a faint chime like a wet finger on glass. Then, in a niche near her shoulder, a bottle containing a grey-haired ferryman began to rock against its cork, deliberately, rhythmically, the way a moth throws itself at a window. Down the corridor, another joined it. Another. A thousand tiny lights blinking awake in their glass tombs, and beneath her feet the whole mountain shivered.

"The wards are failing," she whispered, and the words came back to her doubled off the glass.

She ran.

Tomas's niche was on the seventh level, according to the broker's badge she'd stolen two days ago off a fat man who smelled of cloves — *Pemberton, T., acquired year 12, court purchase, fuel-grade*. Fuel-grade. Her brother, graded like coal. The stairs between levels were guarded on a rotation, but the rotation had broken; she met only one guard on the fifth landing, and he wasn't watching the stairs, he was watching the wall, where the amber light was pulsing so fast now it strobed. She put her sap against the base of his skull and lowered him down gently, because she was done, she had decided somewhere on the road beneath the mountain, with being someone who left people crumpled.

The sixth level was flooding.

Not water — light. The failing wards were bleeding their stored power out through the lowest points, and it ran along the floor in shallow sheets of gold, and where it touched the bottles they woke faster. Ulyana waded through it. It wasn't warm. That was the horror of it; it should have been warm, it was made of souls, and it was cold as ditchwater, and it clung to her boots and dripped off her hem in threads of light that crawled instead of fell.

*Pemberton, T.*

Seventh level, east gallery, niche forty-one. She counted as she ran. Thirty-seven. Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine —

The ceiling came down.

Not all of it — a section of the gallery ahead, salt-stone crashing into the golden flood with a hiss like a hot iron in a trough, and behind the collapse she heard running feet, shouting, the ring of drawn steel. Guards, coming from the far end. Behind her, more shouts, echoing down the stairwell. Between the two, a corridor of rocking, chiming, waking bottles, and somewhere among them, her brother.

Ulyana did the arithmetic she was always doing, the cold little sums that had kept her alive since she was nine years old. Front exit blocked. Back exit blocked. Time: none.

So she did the thing she never did.

She reached out with her gift — not toward a pocket, not toward a sleeping mark, but toward the vault itself, toward ten thousand bottled souls — and she *opened* herself to them the way she opened pockets, and they poured in.

They came as fragments. A woman's wedding on a canal boat. A boy falling from a roof, over and over. The taste of bread in a city she'd never seen. A hundred lives in a heartbeat, too many, far too many, filling her up like floodwater filling a cellar — and she took all of it, every fragment, and she *held* it, and then she pushed.

The push went outward as pressure, as presence, as a voice made of everyone: *wake up. Wake UP. WAKE UP.*

Every bottle on the sixth and seventh levels burst at once.

The sound was not glass. It was choir. Ten thousand souls tore free of their vessels in a single instant and filled the corridor like a blizzard blown indoors, and the guards — she saw them through the whirl — dropped their swords and stood with their arms open, weeping, because whatever came pouring out of those bottles touched them and gave back memories they'd sold or lost or never known they were missing. One guard fell to his knees and called a name, over and over, a name that no living person should have known.

Through the blizzard of light, Ulyana ran to niche forty-one.

There it was. Smaller than she had imagined, uglier — a workman's vessel, squat, corked with lead, the label written in a clerk's bored hand. *Pemberton, T.* No age. No note of what he'd been. Fuel-grade.

Her hands were shaking after all.

She got her nails under the lead seal, and it burned her and she didn't care, and as she worked it loose the bottle grew warm in her palms — the first warm thing in the whole cold mountain — and through the glass she saw not liquid, not light, but a room. A kitchen. A boy sitting on a stool with his feet hooked on the rungs, laughing at something off to the side. Laughing the way only Tomas laughed, head back, eyes shut, all in.

*Tomas.*

The seal gave. The cork sighed out. And the whole blizzard of freed souls in the corridor stopped moving at once — every single one turning, like iron filings to a magnet, toward the small warm weight in Ulyana Pemberton's hands.

* * *

Ysolde felt the mountain change from three miles away, kneeling at her cold shrine with her hands pressed flat to the stone altar, reading the fire's pulse through the rock the way other priestesses once read scripture.

For six hundred years her order had tended the flame. For six hundred years the flame had eaten. They had called it sacrifice; they had called it necessity; they had built a beautiful theology around a lie told by the first warden to the first priestess, and Ysolde had known it was a lie for forty years and tended it anyway, because the alternative was the wards failing and the mountain's hoard waking unloosed on Vell.

Now the wards were failing anyway. She could feel them going, thread by golden thread, and beneath the failure the fire itself was changing — not guttering. *Feeding*. Something had gone into it that was not a soul, and the fire had seized it like a starving dog seizing bread, and it was roaring.

And the souls — the thousands of souls the court had hoarded, banked, shelved like wine — were leaving. Pouring out of the vaults and up through the mountain and out, home, wherever home was. She felt each departure as a small cooling in the stone, hundreds of them, then thousands, the great cold hoard of the court of salt and smoke emptying itself like a purse with a cut seam.

Ysolde began to laugh. She was an old woman alone in a shrine above a dying order, and she laughed until tears cut lines through the ash on her face, because the fire had waited six hundred years for honest food, and tonight, at last, somebody had brought it some.

She rose. Her knees cracked. There was nothing left for her at the shrine — the tending was done, the tending was *over* — and there was everything left for her down in the dark, where a girl with a talent for theft was about to meet the last warden of the last lie.

Ysolde took up her censer, and started down the mountain road at a pace no old woman should have managed, and the wind at her back smelled, for the first time in her long life, of morning.

* * *

The warden ran.

He would not have described it as running. In his own account, later, to no one, he was *proceeding decisively toward the primary vault*. But his shoes rang on the salt-stone stairs in the rhythm of a man who had not run anywhere in thirty years and had forgotten how, and the amber light that had always pulsed for him — respectful, obedient, his — was now strobing wild and uneven, and everywhere he looked, his hoard was escaping.

His hoard. His life's work. Forty years of acquisitions. He had told himself, in the language he used for himself, that he was a steward — that when the fire died, someone must keep the souls safe, and that safety required authority, and authority required him. The fact that the souls were safer in people had never once been a sentence he permitted himself to think.

He burst into the primary vault, the deep one, where the oldest acquisitions slept behind triple wards, and stopped.

The wards were already broken. Not failed — broken, torn open from outside, and standing among the emptied niches was the former Inquisitor Ashe, wrapped in pale fire to the elbows, looking at him with an expression the warden recognized from the other side of a hundred interrogations. It was the expression of a man who has decided something.

"Ashe," the warden said, and made his voice a magistrate's voice, level, unhurried. "Think about reinstatement. Think about—"

"I've thought about everything," Corvin said. "That's my whole life. I thought about everything and did nothing."

Behind him, from the corridor, came footsteps — quick, light, uneven, the gait of a woman carrying something precious and running. The warden turned, and saw her come through the doorway into the strobing light: the thief, Pemberton, with a small glass vessel hugged against her chest like a child, her face smeared with gold light and salt-dust and something else, something ferocious.

The warden's mind, which had spent forty years pricing things, priced her in a heartbeat and found the number.

"The ledger," he said. "I still have the ledger, girl. Every gifted citizen in Vell, every name the crown collected — including yours, including your dead brother's sale record, signed and sealed. You want his history? You want to know what he was promised, what he was paid? Trade me. His vessel for the ledger. Your brother's past for your brother."

And Ulyana Pemberton looked at him — at the last warden of the court of salt and smoke, standing alone in a vault of empty shelves while his empire poured out through the cracks in the world — and she shifted her brother's vessel into the crook of one arm, freeing her right hand.

"You're holding a ledger," she said. "I'm holding my brother. You genuinely don't see the difference, do you?"

Down through the mountain, deep below the vaults, the dragon-fire roared like something waking from a six-hundred-year fast — and both of them turned toward the sound, because only one of them could feed it, and only one of them knew yet what it wanted to eat.
