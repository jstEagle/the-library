## 17. The Last Dragon-Fire

The corridor ended where the world's warmth did.

Ulyana had walked through vaults of glass and salt, past shelves that hummed with the small imprisoned lives of strangers, and still the cold had been a merchant's cold — the chill of cellars, of coin kept in dark places. This was different. This was the cold of the space between stars, pressing against her skin like a hand testing whether she was worth crushing.

Beyond the final arch, the fire burned.

It was smaller than she had imagined. All her life in Vell she had heard of the dragon-fire beneath the mountain — *the fire that warms the crown, the fire that keeps the wards*, mothers said it the way they said *the king is good and the roads are safe*, words worn smooth by repetition until no one thought to question them. She had pictured something vast: a lake of flame, a chained beast the size of a cathedral. Instead it was a hearth-flame no larger than a bonfire, crouched in the center of a bowl-shaped chamber of black stone, burning a color she had no name for — gold shot through with veins of blue, like lightning trapped in amber. It made no sound at all. That was the worst of it. A fire that big should have roared. This one only breathed.

And around it, on a ring of obsidian steps, sat the warden.

He had been waiting for her. Of course he had. Corvin's escape had torn through the lower vaults like a lit fuse, souls waking in their bottles, corridors collapsing under the weight of their own released magic — and still the warden sat with his hands folded, a thin man in grey robes trimmed with tarnished silver thread, his face the kind of face you forgot while looking at it. Only his eyes were memorable: pale as frost on a window, patient as winter.

"Ulyana Pemberton," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like an entry in a ledger. "You've caused me a great deal of expense tonight."

She stopped at the edge of the archway, one hand braced on the salt-crusted stone. Her lungs ached. Somewhere behind her, deep in the vaults, something screamed — a bottle breaking, perhaps, or a person. She could not let herself think about which.

"I'm not here for you," she said.

"No." The warden smiled without warmth, the way a knife smiles. "You're here for the boy."

The fire guttered. It was such a small motion — a flicker, a lean, like a candle deciding whether to die — but Ulyana felt it in her chest, a tug behind her ribs as if something had hooked her heart and pulled. In her satchel, wrapped in cloth, the vessel she had carried from Shelf Eleven went cold against her side. Tomas's bottle. His soul, dimming.

"He's fading," she said. Her voice came out flat. She had learned long ago that grief sounded too much like weakness in this city, and old habits held even here, at the bottom of the world.

"They all fade when the fire fades." The warden rose, unhurried, brushing ash from his sleeve. "That is the cruelty of the arrangement, if you haven't worked it out yet. The fire must be fed. When it starves, everything bound to it begins to starve too — the wards, the mountain, the little lights we keep in jars. Your brother has been burning in that flame for eleven years, Miss Pemberton. Every ward over every city gate in Vell, every charm of protection the crown sells to its frightened citizens — your Tomas is in the smoke of them. And now—" he gestured at the trembling dark above them, where dust sifted down like grey snow— "now he is nearly spent."

Ulyana's hand found the strap of her satchel and gripped it hard enough to hurt. *Nearly spent.* Eleven years. He had been nine years old when he signed himself away to pay what she owed, and she hadn't known, hadn't let herself know, had stolen the memory out of her own head because living with it would have killed her. And he had chosen it. That was the wound that would not close: he had chosen it for her.

"You lie," she said. "Ysolde told me the truth. The fire doesn't need souls."

Something flickered across the warden's face — annoyance, quickly folded away, like a coin slipped back into a purse.

"The priestess," he said, "is a romantic. Yes, the fire can burn on other things. Grief. Devotion. Memory freely given. But those are slow fuels, and thin. Souls are rich. Souls are *efficient*. Do you know how many wards the crown demands these days? How many gates, how many borders, how many frightened little towns that pay for protection they don't need because we tell them they need it?" He began to walk the ring of steps, circling the dying fire, and Ulyana tracked him the way she'd once tracked cutpurses in Vell's markets — sideways, always keeping him at the corner of her eye. "Fear is the best business in the world, Miss Pemberton. I merely hold the supply."

"The supply is people."

"The supply is *gifted* people." He stopped. Turned. The pale eyes fixed on her with sudden, terrible interest. "Which brings me to why you are still alive."

The words dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well. Ulyana heard them fall and fall and never land.

"You came through my vaults," the warden continued softly. "Past three wards that should have flayed the memory from your skull. You carry a stolen soul in a bag on your hip. Your friend — the disgraced inquisitor — just tore a hole in the eastern stacks using magic he has spent thirty years pretending he doesn't have. You are, both of you, walking evidence of everything my court exists to suppress and monetize." He spread his thin hands. "And I find I am tired of pretending otherwise."

Above them, the mountain groaned. A shelf somewhere in the darkness toppled; glass shattered; a sound like a sigh ran through the walls, and the fire shrank another finger's width. Ulyana felt the pull again, harder this time. Tomas. Hurry. *Hurry.*

"You're going to offer me something," she said. "So offer it. I don't have time for your theater."

For the first time, the warden looked genuinely pleased.

* * *

"A ledger," he said.

He drew it from his robes as though producing a dove from a hat — a book bound in white leather gone yellow at the edges, its spine cracked, its pages swollen fat with writing. Ulyana knew it instantly. She had held its twin in her hands not two weeks ago in the Hall of Records, had felt the same greasy weight of other people's names.

"This is not the seized ledger your guild wanted back," the warden said. "That one was a copy. An accounting of debts, petty thefts, taxes paid in stolen memories. This is the original. The master record." He set it on the obsidian step beside him, and the firelight caught the pages and made them glow like something rotting. "Every gifted citizen in Vell. Names, addresses, the nature of each gift, the strength of it. Three thousand and forty-one entries. Children included. We've been collecting since before your grandmother was born."

Ulyana's mouth had gone dry. "The hunt—"

"Was never about cleansing the city." The warden's smile returned, thin as a blade's edge. "The hunt is a harvest. Every gifted soul dragged off by witch-hunters goes to a holding cell, then to a broker, then here. To the shelves. To the fire. The crown calls it justice. I call it inventory." He tapped the cover of the ledger with one long finger. "But the times are changing. The fire dies. The wards fail. My position depends on maintaining the illusion that all of this—" he gestured at the chamber, the fire, the mountain itself— "is necessary. It is becoming difficult. So I am diversifying."

"Diversifying," Ulyana repeated. The word tasted obscene in that place.

"A woman of your talents," the warden said, "could be very useful to me in Vell. No more hiding in alleys selling bottled ghosts to grieving widows. You'd have license. Protection. Wealth. You would run the collection yourself — decide who comes to the mountain and who doesn't. Think of it, Miss Pemberton. Power over the very machine that took your brother." He paused delicately. "And of course, first, before anything else—"

He nudged the ledger forward with his foot, so that it slid one step closer to her, and stood aside, and gestured at the fire.

"—the trade. The ledger for the boy. You walk out of here with his soul in your bag and your city's gifted safe in your keeping, and I walk out of here with the means to rebuild my inventory in more... cooperative hands." He tilted his head. "Or you refuse, and we all burn together. The fire has minutes, not weeks. When it dies, your brother dies with it — permanently, utterly, no afterlife, no return. The choice seems simple to me. Blood versus paper. Your brother versus three thousand strangers whose names you don't even know."

The chamber was very quiet. The fire breathed its soundless breath. Dust fell like snow.

And Ulyana Pemberton, who had survived Vell since she was twelve years old by understanding exactly what people were willing to buy and what they were willing to sell, stood at the heart of the last dragon-fire and understood, finally and completely, the shape of the bargain laid before her.

He was right. That was the horror of it. It *was* simple. Take the ledger. Trade it for Tomas. Walk away with blood intact and let the paper do its work — let the hunts continue, let the harvest roll on, let three thousand and forty-one names go on feeding the fire one by one, year by year, children going into bottles so that her brother could come home whole. No one would ever blame her. Who could? She had saved her family. Everyone understood family.

Her thumb brushed the clasp of her satchel. Through the cloth she could feel the vessel, faintly warm again, as if Tomas sensed her near. *He chose to be sold,* she thought. *To pay my debts. He chose it when he was nine years old because someone had to, and no one else would.*

And what would he say, if she traded three thousand strangers to get him back?

What kind of man would she be handing back to the light?

"There's a flaw in your arithmetic," she said.

The warden raised an eyebrow.

"You think I'm choosing between blood and paper." Ulyana unslung the satchel from her hip, slowly, and held it in both hands. "But you made one mistake, warden. You told me the truth. Ysolde told me the truth. The fire doesn't need souls. It never needed souls. It needs *something freely given* — and you've been feeding it theft for a hundred years and calling it necessity." She began to walk toward him, toward the fire, one step and then another, and the cold broke apart around her like ice on a spring river. "You didn't build a temple. You built a market. And I have spent my whole life robbing markets."

The warden moved faster than a man his age should have — his hand darting into his sleeve, silver light gathering there, the wards of the chamber waking with a sound like a struck bell—

But Ulyana had already opened the satchel.

And already, with the steadiness of every theft she had ever committed turned inside out at last, she reached past her brother's vessel, past the charms and the broken bottles and the shredded remnants of her stock, and closed her hand around the thing she had guarded hardest of all: not the memory of Tomas, not the boy at the mountain gate —

but the three years after. Every small charm sold in the rain. Every night she had almost given up and hadn't. The widow Marta's face when she heard her dead husband laugh again. The fragile, stubborn, hard-won warmth of the life she had built alone in the cracks of a cruel city.

Everything she had. Everything she was, outside of blood.

She gave it to the fire.

* * *

The flame took it the way the sea takes a dropped coin — instantly, totally, without malice.

Light exploded outward from the hearth, gold and blue and a third color underneath, a color made of every morning Ulyana had ever loved. The black bowl of the chamber blazed like day. The warden's wards snapped apart with a sound like the sky cracking, and he was thrown backward down the obsidian steps, his silver spell scattering into sparks, his ledger tumbling from where it lay — pages tearing free, names lifting into the air like ash in a gale, three thousand and forty-one souls' worth of secrets whirling upward into the light.

And the fire roared.

Not the small, starved, soundless breathing of before — a true roar, the voice of something ancient waking from a long illness, filling the chamber, filling the mountain, rolling up through the vaults where bottled souls woke and sang in their shelves and the collapsing corridors blazed with dawn. Ulyana stood at the fire's edge with empty hands and an emptiness opening inside her where three years used to be, and watched the flame climb tall and clean and free.

She could not remember why she had come.

She knew that she had come for something. There was a shape in her chest shaped like purpose, like urgency, like a name — but the name was gone, the urgency was gone, the three warm years that should have told her who she was and why she fought were gone, fed to the flame forever, unrecoverable, as Ysolde had promised and she had chosen anyway. She stood in the blazing heart of the last dragon-fire holding a satchel with a warm bottle in it and did not know, could not remember, whose soul she carried.

But the fire was rising. And somewhere above her, the mountain was coming down.

Through the roar she heard footsteps on the stairs — Corvin's voice shouting her name, hoarse, desperate — and beyond him, from the wrecked doorway of the eastern vault, a sound that stopped her heart for entirely different reasons:

Singing. Hundreds of small voices, rising from the waking shelves.

The bottles were opening.
