## 18. What Ulyana Gives

The fire pit at the heart of the mountain court was older than the city above it. Older than the crown that claimed to own it. Older, perhaps, than memory itself.

Ulyana stood at its edge with the ledger clutched against her chest like a shield, and she understood, finally, why they had built everything else around this one room. The vaults of bottled souls, the salt-crusted corridors, the warden's smoke-wreathed throne — all of it was scaffolding. All of it existed to keep hands away from this: a circle of black stone, and in its center, a flame no larger than a candle, burning a color she had no name for. Not gold. Not red. Something between amber and grief.

It was so small. That was the cruelest part. Three hundred years of souls — brothers, daughters, mothers, sold by the crown by weight and measure — and all that stolen life had fed down to this: a flame the size of her thumb, guttering like the last lamp on a dead street.

"You're hesitating." The warden's voice came from behind her, smooth as poured tallow. He had not moved from his chair since Corvin's escape had torn the court open; he didn't need to. His guards were converging through the collapsing corridors, and the wards were failing, and none of it mattered, because he still held the only two things in the world Ulyana wanted. "That's wise. Hesitation is what separates brokers from thieves."

"I'm not hesitating." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — flat, distant, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth. "I'm deciding which of us is lying."

"Then let me make it simple." He rose, and the smoke that clung to him stirred like something waking. He gestured, and a guard — one of the few still standing after the riot had swallowed the lower vaults — stepped forward bearing a shallow dish of black glass. In it, small as a wedding ring, sat a vessel of pale crystal. It caught the firelight and threw it back wrong, the way ice throws light, the way bottled souls always did.

She knew it before he said the name. She had known it for three years without knowing it, the way you know a scar under a glove.

*Tomas.*

"Pemberton," the warden said, savoring it. "The ledger for the boy. Every gifted citizen in Vell, every name the crown has gathered in twenty years of hunting, delivered into my keeping. And your brother walks out of this mountain whole. You have my word."

Ulyana almost laughed. Almost. What stopped her was not doubt — doubt would have been a mercy — but arithmetic. She had spent her whole life doing sums other people couldn't see. A widow's coin for a dead man's laughter. A merchant's silver for the smell of his mother's kitchen. She knew exactly what things cost, and she knew exactly what this thing cost, and the warden's word was worth precisely nothing because he had already told her, in his own stolen memories, that the fire did not need souls at all.

The ritual was a lie. The hunger was a lie. The court had invented a god out of a dying flame so that it could keep a monopoly on magic, and every soul in those vaults — every soul in *this* dish — had been burned not for the mountain's sake but for the warden's ledgers, his power, his beautiful hoarded fear.

He thought she didn't know. He thought she had come here blind, a thief reaching for a shelf.

*Let him think it,* she told herself, and found that thinking it steadied her hands. *Let him think it right up until the moment it stops mattering.*

"You're offering me my brother," she said aloud, "in exchange for the names of everyone like me. Everyone the hunters can burn in the squares of Vell while you sit down here growing fat on their fear."

"I'm offering you a choice," the warden said. "You of all people should appreciate choices. You sell them by the bottle."

"No." The word came out of her before she had decided to say it, and once it was out she felt lighter, the way she always felt when a lie left the room. "No, you're not. You're offering me a trade dressed up as a choice, and I've seen enough of those to know the difference. A widow chooses between starving and forgetting her husband. A father chooses between his daughter and his debt. Those aren't choices. They're just cruelty wearing a polite face."

The warden smiled. It did not reach anything behind his eyes; she wasn't sure there was anything behind his eyes anymore but inventory. "Then you choose the ledger."

"I choose neither."

She lifted the ledger off her chest.

It was heavier than a book had any right to be. Twenty years of names, written in the crown's iron-gall ink, each page a list of the hunted: the hedge-witch on Tanner's Row, the boy who could quiet dogs with a look, the midwife who sang fevers down, the widow who dreamed true. Names she had read by candlelight in Corvin's rooms and memorized against her will, names that had followed her down the mountain road like a procession of ghosts. Somewhere in those pages, if she looked, she would find her own.

The warden saw what she meant to do one heartbeat too late. "Don't—"

She tore it.

Not gently. Not ceremonially. She tore it the way you tear bread when you're hungry, the way you tear a bandage when you can't bear the slow way, and the sound of it — wet, fibrous, final — rang through the fire chamber louder than any bell. Pages came free in fistfuls. She hurled them toward the pit, and the updraft took them, and for one suspended moment twenty years of the crown's careful cruelty hung in the air like black birds.

Then the flame touched them.

The fire flared — briefly, greedily, the way it flared for everything it was given — and the ledger burned. Names curled into ash. Ink went up in threads of violet light. The hedge-witch, the dog-quieting boy, the fever-singing midwife, the widow who dreamed true: gone, unfindable, unwritable. The warden made a sound then that Ulyana would carry in whatever was left of her memory, a sound like a man watching his own heart being weighed on scales and found wanting. He lunged forward across the black stone, robes smoking, hands grasping at scraps of ash that dissolved even as he seized them.

"It's done," Ulyana said. "Burn the rest of your books. It won't grow back. Fear doesn't keep."

"You've killed them." His voice cracked into registers she hadn't heard in him before — and beneath the fury, horribly, there was panic, real panic, the panic of a man whose entire architecture was coming down. "Every one of them. Without the lists the hunters will—"

"The hunters will find nothing," she said, "because there is nothing left to find. That was the point."

Guards were pouring into the chamber now — she could hear them, boots on salt-crusted stone, the ring of drawn steel — but they slowed at the threshold, uncertain, because their master was on his knees at the edge of the pit scrabbling at embers, and the wards overhead were shedding light like a cracked lantern, and something in the room's oldest deep silence had begun to change.

Ulyana turned back to the flame.

This was the part she had decided in the dark hours of the road, in Ysolde's shrine, listening to the old priestess describe the price with hands that shook. *Any freely given memory,* Ysolde had said. *But the giver forgets what they give forever.* Forever. Not sleep-forgotten, not drink-forgotten, not stolen-and-sold forgotten, where somewhere in some bottle the truth sat waiting to be bought back. Forever-forgotten. The memory didn't go into a vessel. It went into the fire, and the fire ate it, and it was nowhere ever again.

Ysolde had offered to carry it herself. Ulyana had refused, and the refusal had cost her more than she'd let show, because refusing kindness was a skill she'd had to learn young and never got better at.

But now, standing here, with the ash of the ledger still drifting down like dirty snow, she understood something she hadn't understood at the shrine. The warden had asked her to trade Tomas for the future of everyone like her. A terrible bargain, an honest monster's bargain. But there had been a third option all along, the one nobody priced because nobody stood to profit from it.

Give the fire what it actually needed. Give it freely. And pay in the only coin that was truly hers.

Her hand went to the charm at her throat.

Three years. That was what hung there, bottled in cheap green glass behind a wire cage she'd soldered herself: three years of her life, distilled. Not the big things — the big things were mostly wounds, and wounds she could spare. This was the small stuff. The morning light through her stall's awning in the Saltmarket, honey-colored, catching dust. The widow Marta's hands, trembling, closing around a bottle that held her husband's laugh. The first coin anyone had paid her that didn't feel like theft. Rain on the canal. Sable's voice, before the betrayal, teaching her the lockpick's rhythm — *one-two-three, breathe, turn* — back when belonging had still been possible. Corvin's face the night he'd shown her the warrant he'd refused to sign, the first honest thing she'd ever seen a hunter do. The taste of plum brandy on a rooftop with the whole city spread out below, and someone beside her, and the astonishing, ordinary thought: *I am not alone.*

Three stolen years. No — not stolen. That was the lie she'd been telling herself since the guild cast her out, that everything good in her life had been taken rather than lived. These years were hers. Earned in small coins, day by day, charm by charm. The widow's gratitude. The work of her own two hands. The slow, stubborn construction of a self out of the wreckage of the family she'd lost.

And she was about to feed all of it to a flame the size of her thumb.

"Do you know what happens," the warden said from the floor, watching her, reading her the way she read marks, "when the fire dies? The wards fail. The mountain opens. Everything in those vaults wakes up at once — every soul we ever bottled, screaming, loose, looking for bodies that rotted centuries ago. Your brother among them. Is that your mercy? Chaos? Is that justice?"

"It's not mercy," Ulyana said. "It's just not yours."

She opened the cage at her throat.

The green glass was warm from her skin. Three years of warmth, stored in the only vault she'd ever trusted. She held it up to the firelight one last time — the widow's smile, the rooftop, the rain — and she let herself look, really look, the way you look at a house you're leaving, knowing you will never stand inside it again.

*I'm sorry,* she told it. *I'm sorry, and thank you, and it was mine, and I give it.*

She had expected it to hurt.

It didn't. That was the strangest thing. She tipped the vial over the pit and the memory poured out in a ribbon of soft gold-green light, and it fell into the flame the way a child falls asleep, easily, trusting, and the fire —

The fire *roared.*

Not the greedy flicker-flare it gave for stolen souls. Something else entirely. The flame stood up. It went from the size of a thumb to the height of a child to the height of a man in the space of three heartbeats, and its color changed — the amber-grief burning away, burning *clean*, into a white so bright the whole chamber lit like noon, and the heat that rolled off it carried no hunger at all. Only warmth. Only light. The black stone drank it and glowed like a coal. The failing wards overhead caught the new fire and knit themselves whole with a sound like a struck bell, a great low tolling that Ulyana felt in her teeth, in her ribs, in the floor beneath her feet.

Freely given. That was the secret Ysolde had guarded for a hundred years and the court had buried under three centuries of ritual and lies: the fire had never wanted souls. It had wanted a gift. It had been starving in a vault full of stolen goods, choking on everything it was never offered, and one small bottle of honestly lived days had fed it more than a thousand bottled dead.

The guards at the threshold dropped to their knees. She watched it happen — hardened men, soul-brokers, wardens-in-training, folding down onto the salt-white stone with their heads bowed as if the light itself had pressed them there. Even the warden had stopped clawing at ash. He knelt too, though his face was twisted, though he stared at the roaring flame with an expression like a man watching his religion burn and be reborn as something he could no longer own.

"You fool," he whispered. "You absolute — do you know what you've done? The fire answers to no one now. Not the crown. Not the court. Not *me.*"

"Yes," Ulyana said. "That was the point."

The light was everywhere now, and inside it, she felt it begin.

It started at the edges, the way dusk does. The rooftop. The plum brandy. The rain on the canal. She felt them lift out of her — not torn, not stolen, nothing like the ugly wrench of her gift used on another person — but released, the way you release a bird you've kept too long, and each one as it left left a small clean emptiness behind, a socket where a jewel had been. The widow Marta's hands. The morning light through the awning. Sable's voice teaching her the picks. Corvin's face over the unsigned warrant.

*One-two-three, breathe, turn.*

She held on to the last one longest — the thought on the rooftop, *I am not alone* — not because she could save it, but because she wanted it to know, in whatever half-second of going it had, that it had been true. That it was still true. That whatever she became on the far side of this emptiness, the truth of it would hold even without the remembering.

Then that went too.

And Ulyana Pemberton stood at the edge of a blazing, liberated fire, holding a broken ledger's ashes and a small empty vial of green glass, and she could not have said, for the life of her, why she had come.

She knew there had been a reason. She could feel the shape of it the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth — a purpose-shaped absence, aching faintly, important beyond all telling. There was a boy. There was a vessel. There was something she had promised someone on a long cold road, and a priestess with shaking hands, and a hunter who had followed her into the dark for reasons she could no longer weigh. All of it was there, just past the edge of reach, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

Gone. All of it, gone, forever, and the fire burning white and free above the black stone, and the wards singing, and the vaults below beginning — she could hear it now, rising through the floor — beginning to wake.

The warden was laughing. Kneeling in the ash of his empire, smoke-stained, stripped of everything, he laughed like a man who had finally been told the punchline of a joke played on him for thirty years. "It's waking up," he said, gesturing at the floor, at the depths, at the sound. "Do you hear it? The vaults are *waking.* Every soul in this mountain, and no wards left to hold them, and no ledger left to sell them, and no fire hungry enough to want them. You've unmade the court, girl. Well done. Now survive what comes next."

The floor shuddered. Cracks ran out from the pit like lightning frozen in stone, and through them, from the dark levels below, came a sound Ulyana's body understood before her emptied mind could: hundreds of voices, waking at once, rising.

Somewhere down there, in a vault labeled with a name she could no longer place, a small pale vessel had begun to glow.

* * *

The light was still pouring upward when Corvin Ashe found her.

He came through the shattered eastern arch half-carrying, half-dragging a figure she didn't recognize — a young woman in a dragon-priestess's faded vestments, ash-white hair loose, one arm bound in scorched bandages. Ysolde. Though the name meant nothing to Ulyana now; she registered only *stranger, wounded, ally-by-the-way-she-held-a-blade.* Behind them the corridor smoked, and the ringing of the wards had become a continuous chord, and the whole mountain groaned around them like a ship changing course.

Corvin stopped when he saw her. Saw the empty vial in her hand. Saw her face — blank in a way faces should never be, wiped clean down to the bone — and something crossed his expression that she couldn't read and wouldn't have understood if she could.

"Ulyana." He said her name carefully, testing it, the way you test ice. "Do you know who I am?"

She looked at him. Tall. Burn-scarred hands. Eyes that seemed, absurdly, to be bracing themselves.

"No," she said honestly. "Should I?"

Something in him broke and reformed in the same instant — she saw it happen, a crack and a setting, like a bone. He crossed the chamber to her in four strides, took the empty vial gently from her fingers, closed her hand back around it instead, and held on.

"Yes," he said. "You should. Come on. We're getting out of this mountain, and there's someone upstairs you need to meet."

Above them, the ceiling split along an ancient seam, and the first of the waking souls rose into the firelight — a thread of silver, climbing, free — and the mountain began, at last, to come down.
