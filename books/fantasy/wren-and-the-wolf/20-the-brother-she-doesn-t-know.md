## 20. The Brother She Doesn't Know

Tomas woke before dawn, the way he always did now, and lay still in the gray half-light listening to his own heart.

It was a strange thing, a heart. He had gone years without one — or without knowing he had one — burning instead in that vault of salt and smoke, a warmth with no name attached to it, a candle-flame that had once been a boy. He remembered the fire. He remembered being *in* it, which no one should remember, which he suspected was the price of coming back: you carried the burning with you like a stone at the bottom of your chest, and some mornings it flared, and some mornings it only smoldered.

But this morning it was quiet, and so he rose, dressed in the too-large clothes Ysolde had given him on the road down from the mountain, and went to check on Ulyana.

She slept in the corner room of the shrine's guest house, curled small beneath a blanket, her back to the door. Even asleep she looked guarded. Tomas had watched her for three days now — three days since the mountain came down, since Corvin Ashe carried them both out of the smoke on a cart borrowed from a farmer who asked no questions — and he had learned her sleeping habits the way he had once learned everything about her: patiently, hungrily, from a distance she didn't know existed.

She talked in her sleep. Not words — sounds. Small frustrated noises, like someone arguing with an invisible opponent and losing.

He had a sister who did that. He remembered that much.

That was the cruelty of it, and Tomas had spent three days cataloguing the cruelty with the methodical care of a man counting coins he'd never spend. He remembered *her*. He remembered all of her — the gap between her front teeth when she grinned, the way she hummed off-key while working, the winter their mother died when she was nine and she hadn't cried until she found a dead sparrow in the gutter and then couldn't stop, weeping over a bird because the bigger grief had nowhere to go. He remembered selling himself to the crown's brokers, signing the paper with a hand that shook, telling himself it was only memories they were buying, only memories, not knowing — or refusing to know — that the soul went where the memories went.

And she remembered none of him. Worse than none: she remembered nothing of the years since, either. The fire had taken what she gave it, whole and clean, and what remained of Ulyana Pemberton was a woman with a hole in her life shaped like three years, standing in a stranger's kitchen asking, politely, warily, "Who are you again?"

Ysolde had told her the truth, or most of it. That Tomas was her brother. That Ulyana had traded something precious to save him. That the something could not be returned — or rather, that it *could*, that the fragment existed, bottled, but that to pour it back into her would be its own kind of violence, a memory forced into soil that hadn't chosen to receive it.

Ulyana had listened to all of it with her arms crossed and her face carefully blank, and then she had said, in a voice Tomas would have known anywhere, flat and steady the way it went flat and steady right before she lied: "I'm sure that's very moving. I don't feel anything."

And she had walked out to the shrine garden and sat there until dark.

Tomas understood. That was the worst part. He understood better than anyone alive what it was to be a vessel with someone else's contents. He knew what she felt — or didn't feel — looking at him. A stranger with her family's jaw and her father's crooked nose, claiming a history she had paid dearly to lose. If someone told him *you loved this man once, here is the proof* about a face he couldn't place, he would want to burn the proof too.

So he didn't force it. That was his one resolution, made the first night, renewed each morning like a vow: he would not hold the memory up to her face and say *look, look, this is us.* He would simply stay near, and be findable, and let whatever grew between them grow wild instead of planted.

It was astonishing how much of love was just proximity, done honestly.

* * *

On the fourth morning she appeared in the shrine kitchen while he was making porridge, and stood in the doorway with the wary look of a cat entering a room held by dogs.

"You cook," she said.

"I learned recently." It was true. Everything he knew he had learned recently; his entire self was a recent acquisition. "The priestess says I make it too plain."

"Ysolde has expensive tastes." She came in, hesitated, then sat at the table, as far from him as the room allowed. He set a bowl in front of her without comment and turned back to the pot, giving her the gift of not being watched.

She ate in silence. Then, grudgingly: "It's good, actually."

"My one skill." He kept his eyes on the dishes. "I'm told I used to have others, but they're all in a bottle somewhere under a mountain, so I'm starting over."

The silence that followed had a texture. He heard her spoon stop against the bowl.

"That's not funny," she said.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But if I don't laugh at it, I'll have to grieve it, and I've only just gotten a body, and grief seems like a lot to ask of it so soon."

Something happened to her face then — not softening exactly, more like a crack in ice, there and gone. She looked at him properly for the first time, he thought. Searching. He let her search. He knew what she'd find: nothing. A stranger's eyes. He bore it without flinching, because flinching would tell her he wanted her to find something, and wanting things from her was the one debt he refused to put on her account.

"Do you remember me?" she asked finally. "At all? Before."

"Everything," he said. "That's my particular misfortune. We're opposites that way."

Her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the hollow of her throat — where, he'd noticed, she wore a small charm on a cord. Glass on a leather thong, cheap glass, the kind sold at fairs. He had seen her touching it the same way for three days, an absent, repeated gesture, like worrying a loose tooth.

"What's that?" he asked, though he knew.

She looked down at her own fingers as if surprised to find them there. "It was in my pocket. After the mountain. Nobody knows how it got there." Her voice flattened again — the pre-lie flatness. "It's probably nothing."

"It's probably something," Tomas said gently, "but it doesn't have to be today."

She stared at him. And there — he saw it — a flicker, a ghost of expression crossing her face like a shadow of a bird over water: *recognition without content.* The feeling of knowing someone arriving ahead of any reason why. He'd felt it himself since waking, toward smells and songs and the particular way light came through eastern windows. The body remembers what the mind surrendered. Hers was trying to tell her something about him, and he could watch it failing, and it was the most painful thing he had ever witnessed, including his own burning.

She stood abruptly. "I'm going to help Ysolde with the tending."

"The tending is sitting alone in a cold room staring at a brazier."

"Yes," said Ulyana, "that sounds about right," and left, and Tomas washed the two bowls and smiled at the window, because she had taken her bowl to the basin herself instead of leaving it, and she had never once done that as a child, and some things survived even in the ash of a person.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the following week, the way water finds a channel.

Mornings, she tended the dying fire with Ysolde — the great hearth at the shrine's heart, fed now on freely given memory, kept alive out of devotion rather than need, since the wards it once powered had fallen with the court. Afternoons, Tomas worked the shrine's small vegetable garden, badly, learning vegetables the way an infant learns the world. Evenings they ate together, and she talked to him the way people talk to strangers they've decided to trust provisionally — guardedly, testing, circling.

He learned her tells all over again. The new ones layered over the old ones. When she lied, her voice went flat. When she was frightened, she got precise. When she was moved and hated being moved, she became sarcastic about small domestic things — the shrine's leaky roof, Ysolde's taste for incense, the quality of the bread.

"You hold the trowel wrong," she told him one afternoon, appearing at the fence around the garden plot with her arms folded.

"I've been holding it for eight days. I'm an expert."

"You're strangling it. Like it insulted you." She climbed over the low fence instead of using the gate, and he bit the inside of his cheek, because she had done exactly that at every fence and wall between our house and the river, every gate, every stile — *why walk around when over is faster* — and here it was, surviving in her like a fossil in stone.

"Show me, then."

She showed him. She crouched beside him in the dirt and took the trowel from his hand and demonstrated the angle, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and some cheap soap, and her hands were quick and certain and she frowned the exact frown of a ten-year-old teaching him to whittle, brow furrowed, tongue pressed to her teeth, and Tomas looked at the turnips and breathed through the ache.

"You're staring at the turnips like they owe you money," she said.

"They do. They're growing terribly."

"Because you strangle them." She stayed a while longer, adjusting his grip twice, correcting nothing else, and then left the way she'd come, over the fence, and he sat in the dirt among his terrible turnips and let himself feel all of it, privately, completely, the way you're allowed to feel things no one can see.

Corvin Ashe came through on the ninth day, gaunt and moving carefully, his burns mostly healed into shiny patches he clearly hadn't finished making peace with. He'd been staying in the village below, he said, deciding what to do with a life he'd planned to end on that mountain. He brought news and salt pork and an awkwardness around Ulyana that Tomas recognized as guilt wearing armor.

"The hunt's collapsing," Corvin reported, over the evening meal. "Without the ledger there's no list. The crown's arresting names off old rumor and getting laughed at. Vell's remembering it's bigger than its kings." He glanced at Ulyana. "Your guild's tearing itself apart. Sable lost the floor vote three days ago. Word is he'll be lucky to leave the city with his teeth."

"Good," Ulyana said, and meant it, and didn't know why the word tasted strange in her mouth, and Tomas watched her notice that it tasted strange, and file it away, and say nothing.

Later, walking Corvin out to the road, Tomas asked quietly: "Does she know? What Sable did — that he knew about me all along, that he sold the information to the brokers in the first place?"

"She knows what Ysolde told her. She just doesn't feel the shape of it yet. Grief needs somewhere to stand." Corvin looked back at the shrine, at the small figure of Ulyana silhouetted against the firelight in the doorway. "You know she gave up three years for you. Her whole life since. Everything she built, everyone she—" He stopped. "And she doesn't even know what she paid. She thinks she's a woman with a hole in her. She doesn't know she's a woman who bought something enormous."

"I know," Tomas said.

"And you're not going to tell her."

"Not with words."

Corvin studied him for a long moment. "When I burned an innocent," he said, "her brother begged me to remember her. Wanted me to carry her name out of the fire. I refused — thought it was mercy to forget her, mine and hers both." His voice caught and leveled. "Maybe it was. Maybe carrying a name nobody says is worse than losing it. I don't know anymore. But you—" he nodded at Tomas— "you're doing the other thing. Carrying it alone. Is that better?"

Tomas looked out at the dark hills, at the faint glow of the shrine fire behind them, at the woman in the doorway who had crossed a kingdom for a brother she couldn't name.

"It's not better," he said. "It's just mine to do."

* * *

The change came small, the way real changes do.

It was raining on the twelfth day — a soft autumn rain that turned the shrine paths to slick clay — and Ulyana found him in the guest house mending a torn sleeve with clumsy stitches, and instead of passing by she sat down across from him and said, "Show me how."

"Mending?"

"You clearly do it badly enough that I can't make it worse."

He laughed, and handed her the needle, and she worked beside him while the rain ticked against the shutters, and after a long time she said, without looking up:

"I keep having this feeling. Around you." The needle paused. "Like when a word's on the tip of your tongue. You know you know it. You can almost feel the shape of it. But it won't come, and the harder you grab at it the further it goes." She pulled the stitch tight, too tight, and made herself loosen it. "Ysolde says the memory exists. In a bottle. That you could give it back to me." Her eyes came up, and they were sharp and frightened and fierce, all at once, and she was so entirely his sister in that moment that Tomas had to look at the mending. "Why haven't you? Why does everyone keep *waiting*?"

"Would you want it?" he asked carefully. "If it were offered?"

"I don't know. That's the problem. How do I want something I can't remember?" She set the sleeve down. "But I hate this. I hate being a stranger at my own life. Everyone keeps treating me like glass — *rest, Ulyana, eat something, Ulyana, don't strain yourself remembering, Ulyana* — and meanwhile there's a bottle under a mountain with three years of me in it and everyone tiptoes around whether I should have myself back."

"Under a collapsed mountain," Tomas said. "The vaults are buried. Ysolde thinks the vessels survived — souls are durable things — but reaching them would take months, and crews, and permission from a crown that would very much like to get its property back." He met her eyes. "And there's another problem. One nobody's said plainly, so I'll say it. If you took those memories back — all of them, whole — you'd take back the choice too. The reason you gave them. And I don't think you gave them lightly. I think whoever you were before chose to become whoever you are now, and I think that choice deserved respect."

The rain filled the silence.

"You sound like you knew her," Ulyana said at last. "The before-me."

"I did."

"Was she—?" Ulyana stopped. Started again, smaller: "Was I worth it? Whatever it cost?"

Tomas set down the needle. He thought of the boy at the mountain gate, the one in the fragment she wore against her throat without knowing why — a boy turning back for one last look, memorizing a girl's face because he already knew he'd need it later. He thought of the answer he'd carried through fire and vault and waking, the one thing the fire hadn't touched because it wasn't a memory at all, it was just true.

"Yes," he said. "She was worth everything. Ask anyone who knew her."

"That's not an answer. That's nepotism."

"That," said Tomas, "is the first joke you've made that sounded like *us*, and I'm going to treasure it, and you'll never know why."

She stared at him — startled, almost angry, and then something in her gave way, not to tears but to a long shaky exhale, like a knot loosening after years. "You're infuriating," she said. "You talk like you've known me my whole life."

"In a sense," said Tomas, "I've known you my whole life. Both of them."

* * *

On the fifteenth day she knocked on his door after dark, which she had never done.

He opened it and she stood in the corridor holding the little glass charm on its cord, cupped in both palms like an ember she was afraid of dropping.

"This," she said. "It's warm. It's been warm since the mountain, and glass shouldn't stay warm, and I know — I *know* — it matters, and I can't make it matter in the right way. Every time I touch it I feel—" Her jaw tightened. "There's a boy in it. Just a feeling of a boy. At a gate. Turning around. And I don't know who he is, and I don't know why he's looking at me like that, and it wakes me up at night, Tomas, it sits in my chest like a coal, and I am *tired*—"

"Sit down," Tomas said softly.

She sat. He sat opposite her. For a moment neither spoke, and the rain came back, gentler now, a hush on the roof.

"I can't give you the memory back," he said. "Not tonight, maybe not ever — the vaults are buried and the crown is circling and it wouldn't be safe for months regardless. But I can tell you one thing about the boy at the gate, and you can decide whether it helps or hurts."

She went very still. "Tell me."

"He was looking back because he was leaving someone," Tomas said. "Someone he loved more than he was afraid. And he was making himself remember her face on purpose, because he already knew that someday she might not remember his, and he wanted to be easy to recognize when she came looking."

The charm glowed faintly in her cupped hands, warm as a held bird.

Ulyana looked down at it for a long, long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough and level and did not break, because it was hers, and it never broke when the stakes were highest — some things survived even the fire.

"If I came looking," she said, "what would I have been looking for?"

Tomas smiled, and felt the stone in his chest go quiet at last.

"His sister," he said. "She was late. But she came."
