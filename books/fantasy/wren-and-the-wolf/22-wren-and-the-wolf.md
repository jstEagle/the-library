## 22. Wren and the Wolf

### Ulyana

He stopped three paces from her stall, the way a man stops when he isn't sure of his welcome, and up close he was younger than she'd first thought — twenty, perhaps twenty-one, with a scar along his jaw like a stitch in cloth and eyes the color of tea left too long in the cup.

"You're the charm seller," he said. His voice had road dust in it, a long way traveled.

"Everyone in Vell is something," Ulyana said. "I'm the honest one."

His smile flickered at that, as if the word *honest* were a coin he was turning over to check for clipping. "I heard that too. That you don't sell memories anymore."

"Memories are stolen goods," she said, and heard how it sounded — like scripture she'd learned from someone else's mouth. She'd been saying it for a year now and still didn't know where she'd gotten it. "I sell what people make themselves. Knot-charms against bad luck. Salt-bottles for grief, empty ones, so the grieving has somewhere to go. Little glass birds that hold a wish until it hatches." She lifted one of the birds from its row, green glass with a fleck of gold in the belly, and turned it in the light. "That's the whole stock. Nothing in them but air and intention."

"They're good work," he said, and there was something in his voice then that made her look at him again — not admiration exactly. Recognition, maybe, though of what she couldn't say. It was like hearing a song you half-knew played on an instrument you'd never seen.

"Thank you," she said slowly. "Have we met? You have a face I feel like I should be charging."

The laugh came out of him before he could stop it, startled and bright, and for a moment he looked so young, so *familiar*, that the door in her chest creaked wider and showed her a room full of light she couldn't enter.

"No," he said. "We haven't met. Not — not properly." He looked down at his hands. They were a laborer's hands, a traveler's hands, knuckles nicked and one finger slightly crooked, as if it had been broken and set by someone who loved him more than they knew about bones. "I'm new in the city. I'm looking for work. And lodging, if you know anywhere cheap."

"The Drowned Bell takes two coppers a night and doesn't ask questions," she said. "Which is either a recommendation or a warning, depending on your constitution."

"I've got a strong constitution."

"I can see that." She found herself cataloguing him without meaning to — the way he stood with his weight even on both feet, ready; the way his eyes kept returning to the stall, to the little glass birds, to her, with an attention that felt less like a stranger's curiosity than a habit. As if watching her were something he'd practiced. "You'll want references for real work. The guilds are fractured since the hunt collapsed, but the masons are taking on—"

"Actually," he said, and stopped. Started again. "Actually, I was hoping — do you need help? At the stall. Anyone can see you're doing the work of three people. I'm good with my hands. I don't drink. I—" He exhaled. "I'd work cheap."

Ulyana leaned back against her cart and studied him. A year ago — no. She couldn't remember a year ago clearly, which was itself a strange thing, a hole-shaped thing, like a tooth missing from a comb. But she imagined a year ago she would have sent this boy off with a laugh. She'd kept everyone at arm's length then. She knew that about herself the way you know about a scar: the wound is gone but the shape remains, and sometimes, in certain lights, it aches.

Now there was just the stall, and the square, and Mrs. Aldery coming by every morning for tea and gossip, and old Fenwick who paid in buttons, and the long evenings after market when she walked home alone past the fountain and felt the city settle around her like a coat that almost fit.

"Why?" she asked. It wasn't a merchant's question. It came out softer than she intended. "You don't know me. Why would you want to spend your days selling glass birds?"

He was quiet for a moment. The fountain murmured behind him. Somewhere across the square, children were racing hoops, and their shouting rose and fell like weather.

"Because," Tomas said — and he said his own name inside his head like a key turning, like a door he'd been carrying locked for years finally opening onto a room full of light — "it looks like important work."

* * *

### Tomas

She didn't know him.

He had known, going in. Ysolde had told him plainly, sitting by her guttering shrine-fire with her ruined hands folded: *The fire took what she gave, and the fire does not give change. If you put her memory back into her, it will burn her the way a flood burns a dry field. Let her come to it or let her not. Love is patient, boy. Even when it's got nothing left to be patient with.*

So he had waited. Three hundred and sixty-four days of waiting, working the stall she'd finally — after six weeks of his showing up every morning with bread and excuses — agreed to let him help with. Six weeks of arranging glass birds and learning which customers haggled and which needed gentleness, while she taught him things he already knew and he pretended, badly, not to notice how she hummed when she worked, off-key, the same three notes, a tune he'd known before he'd known anything.

*Wren*, she called herself now. Not Ulyana — Wren, because when the fire took her years it had taken the name with them, and the name she'd built afterward was small and brown and quick, a bird that survives winter by staying close to the eaves. She'd told him that once, laughing, wine-warm at the Drowned Bell after a festival: *A wren's not much to look at, but it outlives the fancy birds. It stays.*

He was the wolf. She'd decided that too, another festival night, pointing at him across the table with terrible gravity. *You've got those eyes. You watch everything and you're loyal to one thing and God help whatever threatens it.* He hadn't argued. He hadn't trusted himself to speak.

And today was the day. One year since the mountain came down. One year since he'd stood in the ash-field above Vell's north road with Corvin Ashe — gray-faced, half-dead, free — and watched a girl with no reason to trust anyone walk down out of the smoke carrying a stranger who slept like the dead, and heard her say, *He followed me. I don't know why. He seemed like he needed somewhere to be.*

Tomas had needed somewhere to be. He had needed it so badly that he'd bitten his tongue bloody keeping his sister's name behind his teeth.

Ysolde had given him the fragment months ago. One piece, salvaged from the edge of the fire's feeding, the last thing Ulyana had held before she gave herself away: a boy standing at a gate beneath a mountain, turning back once to wave at a girl on a ridge who was too far away to see his face. It wasn't much. It wasn't the brother. It was the shape of a door, nothing more — but doors matter, Ysolde said. Doors are how rooms get entered.

He'd had it set in glass by the only craftsman in Vell who owed Corvin a life. A small round charm, the size of a coin, the image caught inside like a fly in amber: a boy at a gate, waving.

He carried it in his pocket all through the anniversary market, feeling it against his palm like a heartbeat that wasn't his, and all day he almost gave it to her and didn't. In the morning, when she was arguing cheerfully with Fenwick about the price of a salt-bottle. At noon, when she fell asleep sitting up behind the stall, chin on chest, worn out from the festival crowds, and he stood guard over her sleep like a dog and hated himself for the sweetness of it. At dusk, when the lamps were lit and the square went gold and quiet.

It was only when she started packing up, when the day was ending the way days ended now — together, ordinary, unremarkable, which was the whole miracle of it — that he understood there would never be a better moment, because there was no such thing as a better moment. There was only this one, and the next one, and whether you were brave in it.

"Wren," he said. "Before you pack the awning. I have something for you."

* * *

### Ulyana

"For me? It's not my birthday. I checked. I don't have one anymore, which is very convenient, I celebrate whenever I like."

But she stopped packing, and something in her stilled, some animal part of her that had survived the years she couldn't remember — the part that knew how to read a room, a street, a man's hands. Tomas was nervous. She'd learned all his tells over a year of working beside him: he rubbed his crooked finger when he lied, he hummed her three notes when he was happy without knowing he did it, and when he was frightened of something soft — not danger, never danger, he moved through danger like it was furniture — but frightened of something tender, he stood very straight, like a soldier at a review.

He stood very straight now.

"It's a charm," he said. "Not one of yours. Older. It came to me a long way back, and it was never mine to keep, and I think—" His jaw worked. "I think it was always supposed to be yours."

He held it out on his open palm. A small disc of glass, green-gold, and inside it —

Inside it, a boy. Standing at a great dark gate set into living rock, a traveler's pack at his feet, caught mid-turn, one hand raised in a wave. Frozen forever in the act of looking back.

The bottom dropped out of the world.

Ulyana didn't faint. She'd fainted once in her remembered life — at Mrs. Aldery's, over burnt onions — and this was nothing like it. This was the opposite of fainting. Every sense she had came up sharp and screaming, and the square, the lamps, the gold light, all of it receded as if drawn back through a keyhole, and there was only the boy at the gate, and the ache.

It wasn't memory. She knew it wasn't memory the way you know a dream from waking. Memory has edges, weight, a place in the architecture of your life, and this had none of it — it floated loose in her, homeless, howling. It was a wound in the shape of a person. It was grief with no funeral attached. She loved whoever was behind that gate, whoever the boy was waving to, with a love so large and so old it predated everything she was, and she did not know his name, and she did not know why, and the not-knowing was a hand closing around her heart.

"Tomas," she said.

The name came out of her from nowhere, from the loose floating thing, from wherever homeless love keeps its records — and she saw him flinch as if struck, saw his eyes go bright and wrecked, and she realized she had called him by a name he had never given her. He was Tom to the neighborhood. Tom the helper. Tom who didn't drink.

"How do I know that name?" Her voice came out strange. Steady and strange. "How do I know *you*?"

For a moment she thought he would tell her. Everything in him leaned toward telling — she could see the words backed up behind his teeth like water behind a dam. And she braced for it, whatever it was, the truth like a flood.

Then he breathed out, slow, and shook his head, and smiled — that crooked, hesitant smile, the boy at the gate smiling — and said:

"When you know," he said, "you'll know. And if you never do — that's alright too. I'm not here for the knowing. I'm here for the *staying*. That's the whole job, Wren. That's all of it."

She looked at the charm in her hand. The boy waved. Somewhere under the world, a door stood ajar, and light came through it, and she could not cross, not yet, maybe not ever — but she could stand near it. She could keep it. She could let it be hers without understanding it, the way you keep a key to a house you half-remember loving.

"Where did you get this?" she asked quietly.

"A long way back," he said. "From someone who loved you enough to wait."

"*Waited* for what?"

"For you," he said simply. "It turns out that's most of what love is. Nobody warned me. I'd have practiced."

And Ulyana Pemberton — Wren, the charm seller, the woman with the hole-shaped year and the homeless grief and the stall full of glass birds that held wishes until they hatched — laughed, wet-eyed and helpless, and closed her hand around the boy at the gate, and put the charm in her pocket over her heart.

"Alright, Tom," she said. "Help me with the awning. And then you're coming to the Bell, because it's my unbirthday and Mrs. Aldery will be insufferable if we're late, and—" She paused, hand on the cart, and looked at him sideways, and her voice gentled into something neither of them named. "Stay close tonight. I don't want to lose track of you."

"Not a chance," said Tomas, her brother, her wolf, and took the other end of the awning in his crooked-fingered hands.

They folded it down together, easy as breathing, two shapes against the lamplight, and the square held them a moment longer — the wren and the wolf, the keeper and the kept promise — and then the lamps burned low, and the fountain murmured on, and in her pocket, over her heart, the boy at the gate waved and waved and waved, patient as glass, certain as love, at a girl on a ridge who was walking, slowly, toward the light.
