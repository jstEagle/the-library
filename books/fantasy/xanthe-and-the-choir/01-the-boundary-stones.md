## 1. The Boundary Stones

The thyme was sulking again.

Petra knelt in the third row of Maren's herb garden, pressing her fingers into the soil around the pale-stemmed plants, and decided — not for the first time, and certainly not for the last — that the thyme had the right idea. Everything in this valley sulked. The soil grew things well enough, fat carrots and bitter greens and Maren's precious feverfew, but there was a flatness to it all, a stubbornness, as though the earth itself had been told to behave and had never quite forgiven anyone for asking.

She pinched a sprig of thyme and crushed it between her fingers, breathing in the sharp green bite of it. Even the smell seemed smaller here. Diminished. As if the scent itself had been asked to keep its voice down.

Beyond the garden's low stone wall, the valley spread out in its usual perfect stillness: the millpond lying like a coin someone had dropped and never picked up, the slate roofs of Hollowmere village smoking gently into the morning air, the orchards heavy with apples that would ripen on schedule and be harvested on schedule and pressed into cider on schedule. Somewhere beyond all of that, past the pastures and the alder woods and the grey shoulders of the fells, stood the boundary stones.

Petra had never seen them up close.

No one had, not really — not since before she was born. They were a day's walk away at the valley's narrowest point, and no one walked there anymore. There was no need. The stones did their work silently, invisibly, the way a roof does, and you only thought about the roof when it leaked.

Except Petra thought about them constantly. She thought about them the way other girls thought about the sea, or about boys, or about whatever lay just out of reach. She was seventeen years old and she had spent every one of those years inside the world's most comfortable cage, and she knew — *knew*, with the same certainty she knew her own heartbeat — that there was something wrong with a place where nothing ever changed.

Not nothing-ever-changed the way villages were always complaining about, either. Not the ordinary stillness of country life. Something stranger than that.

"Thyme wants talking to," Maren had told her once, years ago, when Petra was small enough to carry in one arm. "All herbs do. But especially thyme. It's a proud plant."

"Does talking work?" Petra had asked.

Maren had gone quiet in a particular way she sometimes went quiet, the way she went quiet whenever Petra asked anything with the word *magic* in it, even sideways. "Nothing works here, child," she'd said finally. "That's rather the point."

Petra had been six. She hadn't understood then. She understood now, mostly. Understood it the way you understand a rule you've never seen broken but have never once seen explained.

Here is what everyone in Hollowmere knew, in the vague, unexamined way people know the shapes of clouds:

Magic didn't cross the boundary stones. Spells wouldn't light inside the valley. Charms curdled, hexes fizzled, blessings landed like dead birds. A hedge-wizard from over the mountains had come through when Petra was nine, all robes and rings and promises, and had stood in the market square and tried — genuinely tried, sweat pouring down his face — to conjure so much as a dancing light above his palm. Nothing. He'd laughed it off, called it a curiosity, sold three charms to gullible farmers that promptly did nothing at all, and left looking like a man who had been robbed in broad daylight by an invisible thief.

And that was the second thing everyone knew, the second cloud-shape: memories of the outside didn't survive the stones either. Travelers came through — rarely, these days, but they came — peddlers and pilgrims and the occasional lost hunter, and they could tell you about the world beyond while they stood in the square. They spoke freely enough. But ask them a week later, after they'd left, and they remembered nothing of the valley at all. And the people of Hollowmere, who heard everything those travelers said, found that the details slid off them like rain off slate. Try to hold onto a description of the capital, or the mountains' far side, or the king, and it slipped away within days, leaving only a greasy residue of *there-is-something-beyond* and a faint headache.

It was, everyone agreed, very restful.

Petra found it unbearable.

* * *

Maren was in the house, grinding valerian root, and the sound of the mortar carried through the open window in a slow, patient rhythm: *grind, lift, grind, lift.* It was the sound Petra had fallen asleep to for as long as she could remember. It was, she suspected, the sound she would fall asleep to until she died, unless something changed.

She pulled weeds with more violence than the weeds deserved.

Apprentice. That was what she was. Hedge-witch's apprentice, keeper of the feverfew and the comfrey and the sage, learner of poultices and tinctures and the hundred small domestic arts that Maren called "the true craft" whenever Petra pushed too hard about the other kind. Petra had learned to read, to write, to reckon; she had learned which mushrooms killed and which merely blinded; she had learned to set bones and birth calves and talk a frightened farmer's wife down from a fever-terror at three in the morning.

What she had not learned was anything at all about why.

Why the stones. Why the silence. Why Maren — who had hands that moved like a woman who had once known power, who hummed melodies under her breath that Petra had never heard anywhere else and that made the hair on her arms stand up for reasons she couldn't name — why Maren lived in this drowsy valley growing cabbages when there was a whole world outside where, presumably, things *worked*.

"Why won't you ever answer me properly?" Petra muttered at the thyme. The thyme declined to comment.

There were questions she had stopped asking aloud years ago, because Maren's silences had developed edges. What's beyond the mountains? Who was my mother? What did she look like? Where did she go? Each one had been met with the same gentle, immovable wall: *Your mother loved you. Your mother is gone. The valley keeps us safe. Ask me something useful instead — how much yarrow for a poultice?*

Her mother. Even the thought of her felt like reaching into dark water. Petra had been four when her mother left — or died; Maren was maddeningly inconsistent on the subject, sometimes saying one, sometimes neither, sometimes both in the same sentence, as if the words themselves couldn't hold still. What remained was almost nothing: a name — *Liria* — and a scarf of deep blue wool that still smelled, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke, though Petra had long ago stopped trusting her own nose on that count. Memory embellishes. Memory lies. Memory, in this valley especially, was an unreliable servant.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the house.

Maren's cottage squatted at the garden's edge like a toad that had decided to become architecture: stone walls two feet thick, a roof of turf gone mossy and gold, windows deep-set and small. Smoke curled from the chimney in a thin, obedient line. Everything about it said *safety*. Everything about it said *forever*. Petra had loved it her whole life, the way you love your own hands, without thinking about it.

And lately — these past months, this whole slow autumn — she had begun to notice that loving a thing and being suffocated by it are not opposites.

Because here was the other thing. Here was the thing she hadn't told anyone, the thing she turned over at night like a stone in her pocket:

The stillness was getting worse.

Small things. The kind of things you could dismiss individually and only fear collectively. The millpond, which had always rippled at least a little in the evening wind, now lay perfectly flat for hours at a time, mirror-still, wrong-still. The birds — and this one truly troubled her — the birds had begun flying in patterns too neat, arcs and spirals of such geometric precision that watching them made her teeth ache. Last week, old Tam Wrensworthy had sworn that he'd seen the sun hesitate coming over the fell at dawn. Everyone had laughed at him. Petra had not laughed. Because she had seen it too, or thought she had, that half-second stutter at the horizon like a skipped stitch, and she had felt something in the world's fabric pull taut.

The valley was holding its breath. Petra didn't know how else to put it. Something that had been relaxed for a very long time was tightening, slowly, patiently, the way a hand tightens.

* * *

By late afternoon the light had gone amber and long, and Petra's hands were stained green to the wrist, and Maren called her in to help sort the dried herbs for winter. This was the rhythm of their days, worn smooth as river stone: garden, kitchen, mortar and pestle, the ledger of remedies, supper, sleep. Petra moved through it with her hands and let her mind wander where it always wandered — out, past the fells, past the stones, to the unimaginable elsewhere.

Maren's almanac sat on its shelf above the hearth.

Everyone in Hollowmere knew about the almanac. It was part of Maren's legend: a big, battered book bound in cracked black leather, brass corners gone green with age, kept locked with a small iron clasp that had no visible keyhole. Farmers brought it their troubles the way they brought sick animals — "just a glance, mistress, just a peek" — and Maren always refused with the same mild smile. Weather, planting days, fevers, births: the almanac supposedly held all of it, the year's pattern written out ahead of time. Whether that was true or whether Maren simply enjoyed her mystique, no one knew, because no one had ever seen inside it.

Petra had asked to see it exactly once, when she was twelve. Maren had looked at her for a long moment — a look Petra still hadn't fully decoded, something beneath gentleness, something almost like grief — and said, "When you're ready, you won't need to ask."

Which was the kind of answer that sounds wise right up until you realize it means *never*.

But tonight, tonight of all nights, Maren was distracted. She'd been distracted for days, actually — humming less, staring out windows, checking the eastern sky with a frequency that had stopped being casual. Tonight she'd sent Petra to the loft for the good twine and had stayed below, muttering over the winter stores, and the ladder creaked, and the fire popped, and the almanac sat on its shelf not ten feet from where Maren bent over the grain bins, her back turned.

Petra took the twine down slowly. She stood in the loft's dimness and watched the grey crown of Maren's head bobbing among the sacks. Her heart was doing something unhelpful in her chest.

*You're an apprentice,* she told herself. *He's her book. Trust is the whole craft.*

*And she's been lying to me my whole life,* answered the other voice, the newer one, the colder one. *About the valley. About my mother. About whatever's making the birds fly in circles.*

She climbed down without deciding to. That was the terrible thing about wanting — it didn't wait for permission. One moment she was on the ladder; the next her feet had carried her across the floorboards, silent out of pure practice, and her hand was on the almanac's spine. The leather was cool and slightly tacky, like the skin of something dormant.

The iron clasp was cold. She turned the book over in her hands. No keyhole — she'd known that — but now, close, with her breath loud in her own ears, she saw what she had never seen before: a seam so fine it might have been a scratch, running along the spine's edge. And along the seam, faint as breath on glass, a shimmer. Not metal. Not light, exactly. Something like heat-haze, like the air over a summer road.

Like a ward.

Petra's mouth went dry. *Wards don't work here.* That was the whole law of the valley. Nothing worked here. And yet here was a lock that was locked with something that wasn't iron, glowing faintly against every rule Maren had ever taught her—

"Petra."

She didn't jump. That was somehow worse. Maren stood among the grain sacks, a scoop of barley forgotten in her hand, watching her with an expression Petra had never seen on that weathered face: not anger, not surprise. Recognition. As if she'd known this moment was written somewhere, and had hoped, vainly, for a longer page.

"I was just—" Petra began.

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "I expect you were."

She crossed the room and held out her hand, and Petra gave up the book, feeling like a thief and a fool. Maren weighed it in her palm, looked at the shelf, looked at the girl. Outside, the light was dying; the firelight caught the deep lines of her face.

"There's a storm coming," Maren said, apropos of nothing. It had not been in her voice all day, but it was in it now: a flat, careful tone, the tone of a woman standing in a doorway deciding whether to bar it. "Tomorrow night. Equinox. You'll want to be indoors early."

"Maren—"

"Bed," Maren said, not unkindly, and that was the end of it, the way it was always the end of it.

But Petra lay awake in her loft bed long after the house went dark, listening to the wind rise over the fells, and the cold voice in her chest kept turning one thing over and over, worrying it like a dog with a bone.

Because Maren had taken the almanac and put it away — but she had put it away *unlocked*. The clasp hung open. Deliberately, Petra was almost certain. An invitation, or a test, or a trap.

And Petra had never once in her life been able to leave a door closed.

She waited until Maren's breathing below went slow and deep. She counted to five hundred. Then she climbed down into the cold dark of the kitchen, where the banked fire breathed its red glow, and took the almanac from the shelf, and carried it to the window where the last moonlight fell.

The clasp opened under her thumb without resistance, warm now, almost eager, like a held breath released.

The pages were dense with Maren's small, slanted hand — columns of dates, of remedies, of notes in that cramped cipher Petra half recognized from the margins of her lesson books. She turned pages quickly, hunting, hardly knowing what she hunted for. Years of entries. Decades. The handwriting aging across the seasons, ink shifting from brown to black to a fine faded violet.

Then her eye caught on a name, and everything in her stopped.

*Liria.*

Her mother's name, written in the margin beside an entry dated twenty-two autumns past, on the eve of an equinox — this equinox, this very date. Petra's heart slammed. She dragged the page closer to the moonlight.

Someone had inked the name out.

Not scribbled — *inked out*, carefully, thoroughly, with layer upon layer of black so dense the paper beneath had gone stiff and shiny. And yet, because the ink was old and the candle-moonlight caught it at an angle, the letters showed through like bones under skin: *L-i-r-i-a*, and after the struck-through name, in a different hand, a single line Petra had to tilt the page three times to read:

*She paid. Do not wake what sleeps in her debt.*

Below that, older, in Maren's own hand, half a sentence had been scraped away with a knife — but the last word survived, ghost-faint in the damaged paper:

*...the stones are not a wall. They are a—*

The rest was gone.

Petra sat in the dark kitchen with her mother's name blacked out in her lap and the wind rising outside, and every comfortable certainty she owned tilted beneath her like a floor becoming a tide. The valley was safe. The valley was a prison. Her mother had paid — paid *what?* — and something slept, something that must not be woken, and the boundary stones were not a wall but something else entirely, something Maren had cut out of her own book with a knife.

Above the fells, the first clouds of the coming storm slid across the moon, and in the silence of the kitchen Petra heard — or thought she heard, or desperately needed to have heard — a sound from beyond the eastern ridge.

A knock. Faint. Patient. Like small knuckles on wood.

But there was no door out there. There was nothing out there but darkness, the stones, and the whole wide sleeping world.

The knock came again.
