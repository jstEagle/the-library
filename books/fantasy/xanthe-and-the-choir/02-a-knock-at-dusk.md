## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The equinox came in on a wind that smelled like apples going soft.

Petra felt it first in the garden, kneeling between the rows with dirt to her wrists and a trowel she'd been meaning to sharpen since midsummer. The air had changed its weight — the light, heavy sweetness of late summer giving way to something thinner, more honest, the smell of things ending. She pulled the last of the feverfew and laid it in her basket, and she did not look at the boundary stones.

She was very good at not looking at them. She'd had a lifetime of practice.

They stood at the valley's mouth like a row of bad teeth: eleven grey monoliths, taller than a man, older than any story Maren would tell straight through. Moss grew on their north faces. Lichen wrote its slow silver sentences across their flanks. Birds nested in the cracks of them without fear, which Petra had always thought was either a compliment or an insult, depending on how you felt about being safe enough to be ignored. Between each stone the world simply stopped. Not a wall, not a gate — just a place where the valley's green gave way to the grey scrub of the outer lands, as clean-edged as if someone had drawn it with a knife.

Nothing crossed. That was the whole of it, the whole of everything. Nothing crossed, and nothing remembered crossing. Travelers who came down from the pass arrived at the stones and then, by every account anyone could give, turned around and went home, cheerful about it, unable to say why. Merchants' letters spoke of a pleasant valley they'd meant to visit once. Maps showed a blank space where the valley sat, annotated with a cartographer's shrug. And magic — magic most of all — died at the stones like a moth against glass. Maren could brew, distill, poultice, and charm with herbs and hands and patience, but no spell of hers would light inside the boundary. Petra had watched her try, exactly once, years ago: a simple cantrip to warm water, the kind any hedge-witch's apprentice learned before she learned to read. Maren had spoken the words and the words had gone out of the air like breath on a window, leaving nothing, and Maren had looked at her own hands for a long time afterward and said nothing at all.

That silence had taught Petra more than any lesson.

She carried the basket up the path to the cottage, hip-checking the gate open because her hands were full, and stopped dead in the yard.

Maren was standing at the window.

Not working. Not stirring, not grinding, not doing any of the hundred small motions that made up the old woman from dawn to dark. Standing. Both hands flat on the sill, looking east toward the pass, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing — yellow-green at the horizon shading into violet, the equinox dusk arriving early and arriving wrong. Her white braid hung loose over her shoulder, coming unwound, and she hadn't noticed.

"Maren?" Petra set the basket down inside the door. "What is it?"

"Wind's from the east." Maren said it the way other people said *the well's gone dry* or *the cow's foundered*. A fact with a funeral in it. "It's been from the east all day."

"So? It's autumn."

Maren didn't answer. She was still watching the pass when the light failed, and she was still there when Petra had banked the fire, fed the hens, and put the kettle on, and by then Petra's skin had begun to crawl in a slow, prickling way, like the moment before a summer storm when the hair on your arms stands up and you don't know why you know what you know.

The knock came after full dark.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes with the side of a fist when knocking properly hurts too much.

Maren moved faster than Petra had ever seen her move. One moment she was at the hearth; the next she had crossed the room and put herself between the door and Petra, one arm out flat like a bar, and her other hand had closed around the iron poker, and none of it looked sudden — it looked rehearsed, worn smooth by use, the motion of a woman who had done this before and more than once.

"Stay behind me," Maren said quietly. "Whatever knocks."

"It's the size of a sparrow," Petra hissed back. "I heard it."

"I heard it too." Something in Maren's voice made the hairs on Petra's neck lie down flat instead of standing up. "That's what worries me."

The knock came again. Softer this time. Almost apologetic.

Maren lifted the latch.

The child fell into the room.

There was no other word for it. She came through the doorway the way water comes through a crack — all at once, sideways, already collapsing — and Maren caught her under the arms, and the poker clattered to the floorboards, and Petra got a single clear look at the girl before the firelight made everything flicker and strange.

She was small. Seven, maybe eight; it was hard to tell, because hunger and road-dirt had edited her down to essentials. Mud had dried in layers on her, geological, cracked like a drought-bed — mud on her bare feet, mud to her knees, mud in her black hair, which was cut short and raggedly, hacked rather than trimmed, as though someone had taken a knife to it in haste or grief. Her clothes had been fine once. That was the thing Petra noticed second, after the mud, and it unsettled her more than the mud had: the shirt was linen so finely woven it shone, and the little jacket had been dyed a deep, unmistakable violet, and even ruined — torn at the shoulder, scorched along one hem, stained with what Petra chose not to identify — it was worth more than everything in Maren's cottage together.

Royal purple. Petra knew it the way valley children know things they've never seen: from a scrap of ribbon in a peddler's pack, years ago, held up to the light while the peddler talked about the capital, about the court, about the king whose name everyone in the valley said carefully, the way you'd step around a sleeping dog. *Aldric's purple,* the peddler had called it. *Worth more than your house, girl.*

The child's eyes were open. That was somehow the worst part. She was awake, and aware, and looking at Maren with an expression of desperate, furious concentration — the look of someone trying to say something that mattered more than anything had ever mattered — and her mouth opened, and what came out was:

*"Sess-ka veth ilun. Sess-ka veth—"*

Petra had never heard the language before. She was certain of that, and equally certain, in some cold cellar of herself, that no one alive had heard it — that it was older than the valley, older than the stones, older than the kingdom whose color the child wore. The sounds were wrong for every tongue she knew. Too many vowels held too long, consonants placed like stones in a streambed, and underneath the words themselves there was a rhythm, a cadence, that made the fire lean sideways in the grate.

Maren dropped the jar of valerian.

It had been in her hand — Petra never saw her pick it up; perhaps she'd grabbed it off the shelf without thinking, valerian being the thing you reached for when a child was shaking — and it left her fingers and hit the stone hearth and burst, and the dried root scattered across the floor among the shards, filling the room with its smell, sweet and dirty and calming, utterly wasted. Maren didn't glance at it. Maren didn't blink. Maren stood holding this mud-caked stranger at arm's length with her face gone the color of the ash in the grate, and Petra, watching her, understood that in forty years of knowing this woman she had never once seen her afraid, and now she was looking at it.

"*Veth ilun,*" the child said again, pleading now, tears cutting clean tracks through the mud on her face. "*Sess-ka veth ilun—*"

"Hush," Maren said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Hush now. Hush. I can't — child, I can't understand you, but I'm not going to hurt you, do you hear me? No one here will hurt you."

Whether the girl understood the words or only the tone, Petra couldn't say. But something in her went quiet — not calm, never calm; she vibrated like a plucked string held still by force — and let Maren lower her into the chair by the fire.

It was then that Petra saw the hand.

The girl's right palm was turned upward where it lay in her lap, and across it, from the heel to the base of the fingers, ran a scar. Not a cut. A burn — an old burn, healed badly, raised and shiny — and the shape of it stopped Petra's breath in her throat.

It was a crown. Five points, small and precise, the work of something pressed into flesh deliberately, evenly, by a metal form heated past bearing. Someone had branded a crown into this child's hand, and they had done it carefully, and they had done it to a hand too small for such a mark.

"Maren," Petra said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Her hand."

"I see it."

"She's wearing—"

"I *see it*, Petra." Maren crouched in front of the chair, unhurried now, deliberate, the way she was when she set a broken bone or lanced a wound — gentleness as a discipline. "Can you eat?" she asked the girl, slowly, loudly, the way people speak to those who don't share their tongue. She mimed it, lifting an imaginary spoon. "Eat?"

The girl stared at her. Then, slowly, she nodded once — and stopped, and frowned, and shook her head, and then nodded again, and Petra realized with a lurch that the child wasn't confused. She was deciding. Weighing something. Choosing, with visible care, whether to accept food from strangers, and what accepting it might cost.

She nodded a third time, final as a signature.

Petra made porridge with honey because honey was what you gave to the dying and the rescued, and while it cooled the girl sat rigid in the chair with her burned hand curled shut and her eyes moving constantly — the door, the window, the chimney, the door again — mapping exits. When Petra set the bowl in her hands she ate the way she did everything: fast, precisely, completely, wasting nothing, and when she finished she set the bowl down and looked at Maren and said, softly, in that impossible language, one more sentence.

It was only four words. Petra counted them without knowing why. Four words, spoken quietly, almost formally, the way you'd state a title or deliver a message entrusted to you — and as the girl spoke them, every hen in the yard outside went silent at once, and the fire stood up straight and tall in the grate, and the wind that had been worrying the eaves all evening stopped as if it had been told to.

Then the ordinary night resumed, and the girl folded forward over her empty bowl and slept, instantly and totally, the sleep of the utterly spent, one burned hand still curled shut beneath her chin.

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Maren began picking up the pieces of the valerian jar. She did it on her knees, slowly, one shard at a time, and her hands were not quite steady, and Petra knelt across from her and helped and waited, because she had learned that Maren spoke when she was ready and not one breath sooner.

"You recognized it," Petra said finally. "The language."

"No."

"Maren."

"I recognized *of* it." Maren swept the last shards into her palm and stood, dumping them in the scrap pail with a clatter. "My grandmother had a word for it. One word, passed down like a heirloom nobody wanted. She said it was the speech from before the stones, before the kingdoms, before the crown—" She stopped. Petra watched her stop, watched the sentence die in her mouth like a moth hitting glass.

"Before the crown," Petra repeated.

Maren looked at the sleeping child. At the ruined purple jacket. At the curled fist with its five-pointed brand. Whatever calculation she was making, it took a long time, and Petra stood in the middle of it feeling the ground shift under everything she knew — the safe, still, spell-less valley, the travelers who always turned back, the almanac with the inked-out name she had found three days ago and told no one about.

"Get the good blanket," Maren said at last. "The wool one. And Petra—" She caught her apprentice's arm, and her grip was strong, and her eyes were grey as the boundary stones. "Tomorrow, when she wakes, we ask her nothing. We feed her, we mend what we can mend, and we ask her *nothing*. And if anyone comes asking — anyone at all, however polite, however wrong their eyes look — you never saw her. Swear it."

"Why?" Petra asked. "Who would come?"

Maren released her arm and turned away toward the fire, and the light caught the lines of her face and deepened every one of them.

"The same ones who branded a crown into a baby's hand," she said. "Now get the blanket. Equinox is tomorrow, and I want the doors barred before dark."

Outside, the wind picked up again from the east, carrying the smell of apples going soft and, beneath it, fainter, something else — woodsmoke, maybe, or something that only smelled like woodsmoke — and in the chair by the dying fire the child slept on with her burned hand open at last, palm up, offering its small crowned scar to the ceiling like a message no one in the valley could read.

* * *

Petra did not sleep.

She lay in her loft bed listening to the house settle and the child breathe — a thin, quick, careful breathing, the breathing of something used to sleeping with one ear open — and she turned the evening over and over like a stone in her hand. Royal purple. A branded crown. A language older than the stones, that made hens go silent and fires stand up straight. And Maren, grey-faced, saying *before the crown* as if the crown were a thing that had existed before crowns, before kings, before anything with a name.

Near midnight she gave up, climbed down, and padded to the window to check the yard one more time — habit, or dread wearing habit's clothing.

The child was sitting up in her chair by the banked fire, watching her.

Not startled. Not guilty. Watching her with those dark, exhausted, unblinking eyes, as if she had known Petra would wake, as if she had been waiting. And when their gazes met, the girl lifted her right hand slowly, opened the burned palm toward the window, toward Petra, and held it there — an offering, or a warning, or a key.

Then she closed her fist, lay back down, and was asleep again before Petra's heart had finished its next beat.

In the loft, staring at the rafters, Petra finally admitted what she had been circling all night: the dread in her chest wasn't for the child at all.

It was the sound of her safe, still, silent valley — the valley where nothing ever crossed and nothing ever changed — beginning, very quietly, to crack.
