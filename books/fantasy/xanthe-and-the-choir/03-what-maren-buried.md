## 3. What Maren Buried

Maren did not sleep.

Petra knew it the way she knew the weather in her knees, the way she could tell a fever coming on a goat before the animal itself had noticed. She had lived under this roof since she was four years old, old enough to remember nothing else and young enough that the valley's stillness had soaked into her like rain into chalk. And in all those years she had never once heard Maren pace at night. Maren was a woman who moved through the world like a decision already made — no wasted steps, no second thoughts, no midnight wandering. If Maren was pacing, then something had come loose in the order of things.

Petra lay in her loft bed and listened to the boards creak below her, counting the turns. Seven. Eleven. Fourteen. The floorboards had their own map, and Petra had memorized it years ago out of boredom: the third board from the hearth groaned, the one by the pantry sighed, the one beneath the window said *hush, hush, hush* with every pass of Maren's feet. By the time grey light began to seep through the shutters, Petra had counted ninety-one circuits of the small downstairs world, and she had made herself a promise.

She went down before Maren could pretend otherwise.

The kitchen smelled of last night's cold ash and the crushed valerian that still glittered in the cracks between the flagstones where the jar had shattered — purple-white flowers ground to dust, their sweetness sharp enough to sting the eyes. Maren sat at the table with both hands wrapped around an untouched cup of nettle tea, and for the first time in Petra's memory, the hedge-witch looked old. Not weathered, not worn smooth like river stone the way she always had, but *old*, as if the night had taken a toll no one had witnessed.

On the table, beside the tea, lay the locked almanac.

Petra stopped halfway down the ladder. Her whole body went quiet, the way prey goes quiet.

"You were awake," Maren said without looking up. It wasn't a question. "You heard me."

"I heard you walking."

"Then you heard me thinking too, I expect." Maren pushed the almanac an inch across the table, toward the empty chair opposite hers. "Sit down, girl."

Petra climbed down the rest of the way. She didn't sit. "You left it unlocked."

"I left it where you'd find it."

The words landed strangely, like a step that meets no stair. Petra looked at the almanac — the cracked leather cover, the brass clasp hanging open now, the book swollen fat with decades of pressed leaves and marginalia and secrets — and felt the ground tilt beneath assumptions she had carried her whole life. Last night she had held this book in her hands and found the clasp stubborn and the lock unyielding, and she had put it back with her heart hammering, certain she'd been caught, certain she'd been denied. Now it sat open on the table like an invitation.

Or a surrender.

"What's in it?" Petra asked, though she knew that was the wrong question. The right question was burning a hole through her chest, and she asked it anyway, because she was her mother's daughter even if she couldn't remember her mother. "Why is my mother's name inked out?"

Maren closed her eyes. In the grey morning light, with the valerian dust still hanging sweet and sad in the air, she looked like a woman standing at the edge of a debt long deferred.

"Sit," she said again, gentler this time. "And I'll tell you what I buried. All of it. Because after last night, keeping it underground would be like trying to bury a bell while it's ringing."

* * *

Petra sat. The tea was bitter and she didn't drink it. Outside, the valley lay wrapped in its usual morning hush — the mill wheel turning its slow eternal circle, smoke rising perfectly straight from chimneys because the wind never dared gust here, the boundary stones on the ridge catching first light like a row of grey teeth. Everything exactly as it had been yesterday. Exactly as it had been every day of her life.

That was the problem, she realized. That was the whole horrible shape of it. Nothing here ever changed.

"Tell me about the stones," Petra said.

Maren turned her cup slowly, once, twice, watching the leaves settle. "You know what every child in the valley knows. No magic crosses the stones. No spell lights, no curse lands, no wild thing of the outer dark comes over the ridge. The valley is safe." She paused. "What no one knows — what only three people living have ever known — is that safety isn't a wall, girl. Safety is a *price*."

"A price paid to whom?"

"To the land itself. To whatever old hungry thing listens when a ward is sung." Maren's mouth twisted. "Wards aren't free. Nothing true is free. You can't just draw a line in the world and say *nothing bad passes here* — the world asks what you'll stake against it. Usually it takes blood. Sometimes it takes years off a life." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "And sometimes, if the singer is strong enough and desperate enough, it takes memory."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere far off, a rooster crowed once and thought better of it.

"My mother," Petra said. Her voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice.

"Your mother, Sefa—" Maren stopped. Stopped so completely that for a moment Petra thought she'd forgotten the name, and then understood that Maren hadn't forgotten anything; she simply hadn't said it aloud in nineteen years, and the word had gone stiff in her throat like a joint that wouldn't bend. "Your mother came here in the spring of the year you were born. Half-dead. Running. She was heavy with you and running from something east of the mountains, something she would never speak of except in fragments, and she came to me because I was known — wrongly, thank the gods — as a woman who could ward a valley."

"Wrongly?"

"I'm a hedge-witch, Petra. I can cure a cow's bloat and set a bone and brew a tea that'll make a dying man comfortable. But a ward over a whole valley? A ward against *that*?" Maren shook her head. "No. What I know of wards would fit in a thimble. Your mother knew wards the way I know weeds. She was — " and here Maren hesitated, choosing words like a woman picking her way across ice — "she was a singer. Of the old kind. The kind they don't make anymore."

"The child's language," Petra whispered. "Last night. The words that made you drop the jar."

"Yes." Maren exhaled. "Yes. That language. Or its cousin. Its echo." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I haven't heard it in nineteen years and hearing it again was like hearing a dead woman clear her throat."

"Tell me," Petra said. "All of it. Don't ration it out to protect me. I'm done being protected."

Something flickered across Maren's face — pain, or pride, or the specific grief of a mentor watching a student grow claws. She set down her cup with a deliberate click.

"All of it, then. Listen well, because I'll only say this once, and afterward we don't speak of it again until we must."

She told it plainly, the way she dosed medicine: exact measures, no sugar on the spoon.

Her mother had arrived knowing she was dying — not in body but in self. Whatever hunted her in the outer world fed on minds, on remembering, and she had fled carrying more than her own life: she carried knowledge of a rite. An unbinding rite, sung in the true grammar, that could seal away a hunger older than kingdoms. She never named the hunger. She called it only *the crown*, and she touched her own brow when she said it, as if she could still feel metal there.

"She was part of a choir," Maren said. "A royal choir. Singers raised from the cradle to hold the old tongue, because the old tongue doesn't describe things, Petra — it *commands* them. Say a true-word over a wound and the wound obeys. Say it over a king and the king obeys. Say it over a hunger, and the hunger sleeps. But every true-word costs the singer something. A memory. A piece of the self. The greater the working, the greater the price. Your mother sang half a rite to seal the crown away from her — from everyone — and the price was everything she had. Every memory. Her name. Her face in the mirror. Her own child's—"

Maren's voice failed on the last word. She took a breath and finished it anyway, because she was Maren, and Maren finished things.

"—her own child's face. She gave it all into the ward. That's what the stones are, girl. They're not rocks. They're your mother's whole remembered life, laid down in a ring, singing *nothing crosses, nothing crosses, nothing crosses* in a voice that will never stop."

Petra discovered she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles. The kitchen seemed very far away, and very close, both at once.

"That's why her name is inked out," she said. "In your almanac."

"Inked out by her own hand, the week before she died. She wrote it, looked at it, and said, *this name belongs to the ward now, not to me.*" Maren's jaw tightened. "She spent her memories buying you a safe valley, and then she forgot she'd done it. She lived her last two years here like a pleasant ghost. Kind. Vague. Always humming songs she couldn't name. She died when you were six, and her last words to me were, *keep the girl inside the stones, and don't let her learn to sing.*"

The words fell into Petra like stones into a well — deep, cold, taking a long time to land. *Don't let her learn to sing.* Nineteen years of Maren's gentle refusals. Nineteen years of *you've no talent for it, girl, and better so*. Nineteen years of a valley where no spell would light, presented as a blessing, as a shelter — when all along it was a scar. A scar shaped like a ring of stones, paid for with a mother Petra had mourned without ever truly knowing she was mourning.

"And the ward," Petra said. Her voice was steady. She was surprised by that. "You said last night it's fraying."

Maren didn't answer at once. She rose, went to the shutter, and opened it. Morning light fell across the table, across the open almanac, across the inked-out name.

"For nineteen years I've walked the ridge each equinox and checked the stones, the way you check a bandage. Each year, a little thinner. Each year, the hum under them a little weaker. Wards are songs, and songs need singers, and the singer has been dead sixteen years." She turned. "But fraying is slow. Fraying is honest. What came to our door last night was not fraying, Petra. Something *found* the boundary. Something is pressing on it, deliberately, from the outside — testing it, thinning it, looking for the weak note in the song. And that child walked through it. Walked *through* your mother's ward like it was morning mist, which means either the ward is nearly dead—" her voice dropped, "—or the thing hunting that child has learned to sing too."

Outside, the valley went on pretending. Smoke rising straight. Wheel turning. Hens muttering their small grievances. A whole valley of people who would wake today and believe, as they believed every day, that the stones kept them safe — never knowing the song in the stones was down to its final verses.

"Maren." Petra made herself say it. "The crown. The thing my mother ran from. Is it the same one? Has it been hunting her ward all this time? Hunting *me*?"

"I don't know." Maren said it like a confession, and it cost her; Petra watched the admission strip another layer of authority off the old woman, leaving her smaller. "I know only what she told me, which was little, and what I've guessed, which may be wrong. But I know this: that child crossed stones no living thing should cross, wearing a crown-shaped burn and carrying a scrap of royal purple, and the crown's servants do not lose track of royal children." Maren's eyes fixed on her, fierce and frightened. "Which brings us to what happens next. Listen to me now, and don't argue.

"You take the child to the root cellar. Not the herb tunnels — the cellar behind the third shelf, the one I dug the winter you were born and told no one about. There's dried food for a month, water from the seep, lamplight enough to read by. You stay down there with her until the equinox riders have come and gone, however long that takes. Then we decide. But Petra — " Maren leaned forward, and for a moment the hedge-witch of the valley, the woman whose word was law from the mill to the eastern ford, filled the kitchen with all her old iron — "you do *not* go to the stones. You do *not* try to see what's pressing on the ward. Whatever happens, whatever you hear, whatever that child says to you in that voice of hers — you hide. Promise me."

Petra looked at her for a long moment.

Then she stood up, crossed the kitchen, and knelt beside the sleeping child on the cot by the fire. The girl was curled small, fists tucked under her chin, mud still dried in the creases of her ears. On her open palm, even in sleep, the burn showed pale against brown skin — a tiny circlet of scar tissue, precise as jewelry. As Petra watched, the child murmured something in her sleep. Three notes of that impossible language, soft as water over stones, and the fire in the hearth — banked to embers hours ago — flared briefly upright, as if answering.

Petra felt it go through her like a struck bell. So it was true. All of it. Words that made things true.

She rose and turned to face Maren.

"No," Petra said.

Maren's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a settling, like snow deciding to slide. "Petra."

"You want me in a hole in the ground. Again." Petra heard her own voice, calm and terribly clear, and marveled at it. "For my whole life you've kept me inside the stones. Inside the fence. Inside the story where nothing changes and nothing's asked of me. You kept me safe the way the ward keeps the valley safe — by paying for it with pieces of me I didn't even know were missing. My mother. My history. Any choice about any of it." She shook her head slowly. "I'm not doing it twice. I won't be warded, Maren. Not by you. Not by anyone."

"The crown eats *memories*, girl. It eats *minds*. You'd be walking out there as food."

"Then I'll walk out there knowing what I am." Petra's heart was hammering, but her voice held. "My mother paid everything she had to buy time. Time for what, Maren? For me to hide in a root cellar until the song runs out? She didn't finish her rite — you said so yourself, half a rite, sealed but not ended. Someone has to finish it. And that child—" she gestured at the small sleeper, at the burned palm, at the language sleeping in her like embers, "—that child is carrying the other half. She crossed the stones. She came to *our door*. Out of every house in this valley, ours. You think that's accident? I think wards answer to their makers' blood. I think my mother's unfinished song pulled that child here like a hook pulls a fish."

Maren stared at her. And Petra watched it happen — watched the moment arrive when the mentor's authority, that great load-bearing beam of her entire childhood, finally cracked. Not shattered. Cracked, the way ice cracks: with a sound like a sigh, showing the black water underneath. Maren sank back onto her bench, and suddenly she was only an old woman in a cold kitchen, holding a cold cup, having kept one secret too long and lost control of it entirely.

"You sound like her," Maren said quietly. "Gods help me. You sound exactly like her."

"Good," Petra said. "Then you know I don't break easy."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The child slept. The fire ticked. Far off on the ridgeline, invisible in the morning haze, the boundary stones held their silence — and Petra thought she could almost hear it now, knowing what it was: the faintest thread of a song, worn thin as old cloth, still saying *nothing crosses, nothing crosses* to a darkness that had begun, at last, to listen.

"If you do this," Maren said at last, and her voice was stripped down to bare wire, "there's no coming back to how things were. You understand that. Once you're outside the stones, once you're part of this — the valley can't be your shelter again. Even if you return. Even if we win. You'll be a woman who knows things, and knowing things doesn't wash out."

"I know."

"Then at least—" Maren's hands flattened on the table, and the iron came back into her spine one last time, the old hedge-witch refusing to be entirely set aside — "at least you'll do it with your eyes open. Come here. Both of you. There are things you must carry that I should have given you years ago."

She opened the almanac all the way to its hollow spine, and from the hidden seam drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord. She set it on the table between them, and did not yet untie it.

"First," Maren said, "the truth of what's coming for us. Because the crown does not send riders for a child unless it intends to be thorough — and I promised you all of it, so you shall have all of it. Your mother didn't flee the crown, Petra. She fled *with* something. The unfinished half of the rite. The words she couldn't pay for. And the crown has spent nineteen years—"

The knock came at the front door.

Three slow knocks, evenly spaced, polite as a tax collector.

Maren went white. The child's eyes snapped open — clear, gold-flecked, utterly awake, and fixed on the door — and her burned palm pressed flat against her breastbone as though holding something in.

Through the door, muffled and courteous, a man's voice said:

"Hedge-wife. We've come about a stray. You'll want to answer."
